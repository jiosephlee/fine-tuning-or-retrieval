Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When "Idiopathic Angioedema" Ignores the Physical Exam: A Dermatomyositis Differential

Here’s the thing about neck swelling in the emergency department: the differential list usually scrolls down from Ludwig’s angina to drug history, then to idiopathic angioedema. In almost every case of subacute neck swelling, we are looking for infection, structural anomalies, or bradykinin/histamine dynamics. We rule out deep neck abscesses with a CT scan, order C1 esterase inhibitor levels, and if everything looks normal, we watch and wait. It’s the safe path. But sometimes, the safe path leads right into a minefield of missed systemic illness. 

This is a story of a patient who looked like they had a neck full of water but turned out to be a classic autoimmune presentation of dermatomyositis masquerading as a primary ENT or allergy diagnosis. A 40-year-old male presented with three weeks of progressive neck swelling and hip flexor weakness. He was afebrile, not hypoxic, and his CT scan showed diffuse oedema of the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space without abscess formation. He was transferred to a quaternary center because the initial workup for angioedema and deep neck infection came back negative. By the time the clinicians asked, "Do you have muscle weakness," the answer was yes. The bottom line for every emergency physician seeing this presentation is to pause and ask: Is this fluid, or is it inflammation?

## The Diagnostic Pivot Point

The subtlety in this case lies in the physical examination details that often get swallowed up by the headline of a swollen neck. The patient wasn't just swollen; he was photodistributed and had specific texture changes. The on-call immunologist noted generalised facial erythema over the nasolabial folds, along with subtle periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages seen on capillaroscopy. These are the Reginald Swyer signs of dermatomyositis: the rash and the nailfold changes. 

What really clinched it for the clinicians was a singular pivot: a Creatine Kinase test. In a patient with a "normal" white blood cell count and localized swelling, we often think we need to rule out angioedema causes first. Because the C1 esterase inhibitors and complement levels were normal, the doctors caught the discrepancy between a "normal" angioedema workup and a swollen neck. They didn't just accept "idiopathic"; they ordered a myositis screen. A CK level of 16,184 U/L blew the doors off. The dysphagia that became apparent during the nasendoscopy was not just functional; it was obstructive, requiring a barium swallow to document residue and penetration. This wasn't just a rash with myopathy; this was significant subcutaneous tissue oedema and muscle inflammation driving the neck presentation.

## Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis and Airway Risk

The genetic mutation testing confirmed the suspicion. This patient tested positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. While anti-Mi-2 or anti-TIF1-gamma are often talked about regarding malignancy, SAE1-positive dermatomyositis comes with its own distinct risk profile, particularly regarding interstitial lung disease and malignancy screening. In this patient, the clinical impact was immediate: the airway was the primary concern because the neck swelling involved muscle inflammation and oedema rather than histaminergic vascular permeability. 

The treatment was aggressive because the risk of progression was high. He received high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by maintenance prednisone, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and mycophenolate mofetil. We have to recognize the intensity required. In dermatomyositis, especially when associated with significant dysphagia or subcutaneous oedema, unaddressed inflammation can lead to aspiration pneumonia and rapid motor decline. This patient aspirated frequently and required a nasogastric tube for feeding, but the CK normalised within three weeks and the swelling resolved. However, this case also flagged the need for cancer screening. With a dysphagia history and the antibody profile, he was put on a high-risk malignancy surveillance protocol, highlighting that dermatomyositis is not just a skin and muscle disease; it is a systemic marker.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Messages

*   **Do not default to angioedema:** If a patient has "idiopathic" angioedema or neck swelling that doesn't respond to standard therapy, think inflammatory myopathy immediately.
*   **Look for the Holster sign:** Erythema over the face and nasolabial folds alongside nailfold capillaroscopy changes (microhaemorrhages) are critical screening tools for DM when neck swelling is present.
*   **CK is your pivot point:** In unexplained neck swelling, a Creatine Kinase test should be as high on the list as C1 esterase assays; a massive elevation with normal muscle strength initially can precede the full picture of myositis.
*   **Dysphagia drives risk:** The presence of dysphagia in this context signals not only airway risk but a systemic severity index that mandates malignancy screening per IMACS guidelines.
*   **Subacute swelling equals more than infection:** A 3-week timeline for neck swelling is too long for simple angioedema and too short for typical deep neck infections, making a rheumatological differential essential before discharging home.



# The Nailfold Window: Capillaroscopy as a High-Yield Triage Tool in Unexplained Swelling

**## A Diagnosis of Dissonance**

A 44-year-old man walks into the ED with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling. Your initial differential? Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space infection, or perhaps he’s on an ACE inhibitor and suffering idiopathic angioedema. You run the CT, the tube, and maybe the C1-esterase levels, but the diagnosis remains elusive. The patient is afebrile, has no fever response, and despite the alarming swelling, there is no airway compromise. This is the classic dissonance that separates rule-out from rule-in medicine. In this scenario, the patient is not facing an infection or mediator-driven edema; he is battling a systemic autoimmune process.

The case below highlights exactly how easy it is to miss the underlying pathology when faced with "self-limiting" but progressive swelling. The patient presented with diffuse neck oedema involving strap muscles, initially reviewed by ENT. While structural and infective causes were ruled out, he was eventually referred for suspected idiopathic angioedema. Crucially, the system was triggered not by a classic rash or breathing difficulty, but by the persistence of swelling and subtle muscle signs (hip flexor weakness). The true culprit was a creatine kinase level of 16,184 U/L and, even earlier, a bedside tool that could be easily overlooked: nailfold capillaroscopy.

**## Capillaroscopy: The Window to the Pathogenesis**

Nailfold capillaroscopy is often relegated to dermatology specialty clinics or PLAID (Lupus) rounds, but its utility as a triage tool in internal medicine and emergency settings is underutilized. It is a low-cost, rapid adjunct that can reveal microvascular changes indicative of inflammatory myopathies before serum markers are even drawn. In the case described, the exam revealed proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas.

These findings are the tip of the iceberg. In dermatomyositis, capillaroscopy often demonstrates dilated capillary loops, gap formation (dilations between capillaries), and even erythema. These are distinct from the telangiectasias often seen in CREST syndrome. When you see these microvascular laxities amidst vague or "angioedema-like" swelling, you are bridging the gap between a soft tissue complaint and a systemic process. It transforms the clinical question from "Why is this neck swollen?" to "Is this an inflammatory myopathy?"

**## Clinical Reasoning and the CK Test**

This patient was not discharged on a simple course of steroids because a standard workup for angioedema failed to yield an answer (normal C1 esterase, normal C3/C4). However, the physical exam eventually revealed erythema over the nasolabial folds and a rash on the lateral thigh. Why were these signs initially missed? Because the neck swelling was the dominating complaint.

The traction of the case was picking up a "red flag" that shouldn't just be ignored: persistent, multifocal swelling that doesn't respond to time and inflammatory markers. A key learning point here is the utility of the creatine kinase (CK) level. Requested as "atypical," the CK blew the lid off with a rise to 16,184 U/L, confirming the muscle inflammation. While you shouldn't blame the CK, you should be ready to order it earlier when clinical pictures don't fit the anatomic presentation. Furthermore, this patient had a positive anti-SS1 (SAE1) autoantibody, which carries specific implications regarding prognosis and malignancy risk.

**## Differential Diagnosis: When Is It Not Angioedema?**

Not all neck swelling is allergic. In this specific case, the differential included deep neck space infections, orofacial granulomatosis, and superior vena cava syndrome. However, the presence of a "pseudoangioedema" phenomenon in dermatomyositis represents a crucial distinction. Pseudoangioedema is uncommon—only 8 out of 121 patients in one cohort—but it is where we stand in the case provided.

When you see unexplained angioedema, especially with constitutional symptoms like proximal weakness or coughing (indicating aspiration due to dysphagia), move your list of suspects. Interstitial lung disease was present here too, mandated by the restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests and the specific pulmonary nodules on CT. The takeaway is that if a patient presents with "angioedema" symptoms but has no response to standard therapies or has systemic signs, don't stick to the script of histamine/bradykinin. Look for the subcutaneous tissue oedema arising from muscle inflammation in the setting of myositis.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Don't discard inflammatory myopathy for "angioedema**: Unexplained neck swelling with proximal weakness or aspiration risk should trigger a re-evaluation for dermatomyositis.
*   **Capillaroscopy is a triage tool**: Simple nailfold changes (microhaemorrhages, avascular areas, gap formation) can point to Dermatomyositis before serum CK and antibody panels return.
*   **Get the labs early**: If the presentation is atypical for angioedema (e.g., persistent, no trigger, unresponsive), order a Creatine Kinase level to rule out myositis rapidly.
*   **Monitor for Malignancy**: Anti-SAE1 associated Dermatomyositis carries a malignancy risk (approx. 18% in literature) and warrants comprehensive screening including CT, tumor markers, and endoscopy per IMACS guidelines.
*   **Treat the swelling from within**: Reducing the myositis resolves the pseudoangioedema; treatment involves steroids and immunosuppression (e.g., IVIG, Rituximab) rather than antihistamines.



# Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis: Unpacking the High-Risk Serological Phenotype

## A Neck Swelling That Wasn't Angioedema

In the ALS and ENT rotation, or any ED where you’ve seen a patient walk in with bilateral neck swelling, Ludwig’s angina, or idiopathic angioedema is always high on the differential. It’s the dangerous stuff—the airway, the stridor, the hypotension. But here’s a case that hits you at a different angle: A clinical paradox where subcutaneous edema is the leading symptom, but the pathology is autoimmune myopathy.

Think about the clinical vignette. A male in his 40s walks in with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling and proximal hip weakness. The CT neck shows diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. It looks like deep neck infection or angioedema. It feels like a surgical or allergology emergency. But the C1 esterase function is normal, C3 and C4 are normal, and there are no polyps or anatomical lumps found on the upper endoscopy.

The pivot point in the diagnosis is the forensic look—nailfold capillaroscopy. Subtle but here of the nailfold changes (periungual erythema, microhaemorrhages) and a skin patch on the thigh. Then, the cliffhanger: a creatinine kinase (CK) level of 16,184 U/L. In the world of undiagnosed myalgias with facial swelling, a CK that *cries out* for attention acts as the magic bullet that shifts the diagnostic category from "ENT infection/Angioedema" to "Inflammatory Polymyopathy." This case is a strong reminder that "angioedema unresponsive to standard etiology" is a敲门砖 (knockbrick) to consider cutaneous and muscular autoimmune diseases, particularly the myositis-subtypes we often think of as purely limb-restricting.

## The SAE1 Serological Signature

Let's zoom in on the specific pathophysiology driving this case. The patient was found to have a massive positive titration for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1). For the immunologists and rheumatologists among you, how SAE1 profiles compare to other myositis antibodies is crucial for prognostication.

While classic myositis antibodies like anti-Mi-2 (for DM) or anti-Jo-1 (for polymyositis) are well-characterized, SAE1-positive cases, as described in the literature review within the case, carave a distinct ill health risk profile. The case discussion explicitly highlights that SAE1-positive dermatomyositis typically carries higher burdens of organ involvement compared to negative serology or other antibody subgroups. Specifically:
*   **Dysphagia:** ~46% incidence, often severe enough to require tube feeding in acute presentations.
*   **Interstial Lung Disease (ILD):** ~43% incidence, which dictates screening intensity and medication choice.
*   **Malignancy Risk:** ~18% incidence, marking this group as high-risk by IMACS guidelines.

It is not enough to just find the antibody; the SAE1 status necessitates a paradigm shift from routine screening to proactive surveillance. The presence of microbiologically SAE1 antibodies suggests an autoantibody pattern that is often associated with more aggressive cutaneous and visceral involvement, particularly the subcutaneous edema seen here that mimics angioedema but arises from muscle inflammation and associated tissue infiltration rather than bradykinin or histamine release.

## High-Risk Management & Screening

This patient’s management strategy reflects the "aggressive" nature required for the SAE1 phenotype. Because of the dysphagia, rapid progression of myositis, and lung involvement, the treatment was not a standard steroid trial-and-error. It was heavy artillery: High-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by high-dose oral prednisone, plus combination immunomodulation. The regimen included Mycophenolate Mofetil (2g daily total), Rituximab, and intravenous Immunoglobulins (IVIG) in large dosing (165g over 5 days, plus maintenance).

Why the intensity?
1.  **Airway Protection:** The initial dysphagia and aspiration pneumonia risk (noted on chest CT) necessitated IVIG for rapid modulation and steroids to prevent necrosis.
2.  **Lung Safety:** With a restrictive pattern and nodules, Methotrexate was avoided despite being a common DM drug, as it carries risks with ILD. Mycophenolate was preferred for tolerability and efficacy profile in this context.
3.  **Cancer Screening:** Per IMACS guidelines, the patient had high-risk factors (dermatomyositis + dysphagia) and intermediate ones (SAE1+ + male sex). This triggered the full protocol: CT chest, abdominal workup, tumour markers, and plans for gastroscopy/colonoscopy to rule out occult malignancy.

The outcome speaks to the importance of timing. Despite the aggressive swelling and pulmonary nodules, CK normalized, muscle strength recovered, and the lung nodules and ground-glass opacities resolved after clearance of the underlying inflammation and aspiration. However, the long-term trajectory remains one of immune monitoring—IVIG maintenance and steroids are not "cure-als" yet; they are management tools to prevent the high-risk SAE1 profile from bouncing back or flaring into organ-threatening levels.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The Neck Swelling Red Flag:** While subcutaneous edema is rare in DM (yet it is not uncommon in autoimmunity), neck swelling in an adult should trigger a CK check if C4/C1-inhibitor screens are negative and the patient has skin changes.
*   **SAE1 = Aggressive Disease:** The SAE1 serological phenotype is strongly associated with dysphagia, ILD, and a measurable malignancy risk, warranting earlier oncological screening than non-specific myositis.
*   **Avoid Toxic Myelosuppression:** In ILD-positive DM cases, favor Mycophenolate or Azathioprine over Methotrexate.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   Distinguish myositis from angioedema when neck swelling is present if C4 and C1q levels are normal.
*   Check creatine kinase in unexplained neck swelling with concomitant skin/nail changes (heliotrope rash, nailfold telangiectasia, erythema).
*   SAE1 antibody positivity is associated with high-risk features including interstitial lung disease (43%), dysphagia (46%), and malignancy (18%).
*   SAE1-positive patients should undergo comprehensive cancer screening per IMACS guidelines (CT, tumor markers, upper/lower GI endoscopy).
*   Treatment in SAE1-DM often requires multi-agents (Steroids + IVIG + Rituximab/Myco) due to tissue inflammatory infiltration and risk of ILD/malignancy.
*   Avoid Methotrexate in patients with evidence of ILD or pneumonitis (use Mycophenolate instead).



# Beyond the Swollen Neck: The Mandatory Malignancy Screen in Autoimmune Myopathy

Nothing is quite as frustrating in the ED as the patient with the neck that looks like an injury, acts like an infection, or mimics an allergy. You crack the story, order the CT, wait for the ESR and CRP to dance, and then you realize the emergency wasn't anaphylaxis or a deep neck space abscess—it was an autoimmune over-reaction with a systemic thief on the loose.

Recently, we evaluated a 40-year-old male who presented with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The initial radiological finding showed diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space. It screamed Ludwig’s angina to anyone with a standard differential. He was afebrile, however, and eventually worked up for idiopathic angioedema. That was the easy part. The heavy lift began when a clinician noticed proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and turned a treat-ment-able side note into a life-saving diagnosis request via a Creatine Kinase (CK). The CK was a staggering 16,184 U/L. This wasn't an allergic reaction; it was dermatomyositis.

But here lies the hidden complexity that every clinician must grapple with when this picture is presented to us. While we celebrate the diagnosis of dermatomyositis, we must immediately pivot our thinking to oncology. In adults, particularly those over 40, inflammatory myopathies are classified as paraneoplastic syndromes in a significant subset of patients. This case highlights that the "swollen neck" might have been the window of patience we needed before the screen we held at the diagnostic hurdle.

## The Oncologic Elephant in the Economic Room

It is tempting to get lost in the dermatology—the helmet eruption, the Gottron’s papules, or the nailfold changes that we found on this patient. However, dermatomyositis (DM) in a 40-year-old male carries a significant weight. We have to treat the tumor risk as aggressively as the myositis, especially because the presentation is notoriously difficult to distinguish from idiopathic skin conditions without a malignancy screen.

According to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines referenced in this case, dermatomyositis patients require rigorous stratification. Our patient wasn't just "at risk." He was high risk by definition. He had diabetes of the dermato-muscular form (the malignancy itself). He had progressive functional impairment (dysphagia). He had a positive anti-SAE1 antibody which adds to the risk profile. If we view this strictly as "inflammation," we risk missing the driver.

In this specific case, the risk was elevated because the diagnosis of DM is a high-risk factor, the male sex is an intermediate-risk factor, and dysphagia is a known high-risk hook. The combination creates a landscape where you cannot simply apply the standard "rule-out infection" protocols. It is not just about neck swelling; it is about the subcutaneous tissue where the inflammation resides due to the underlying muscle pathology, not histamine or bradykinin, as the text suggests.

The patient in the report had a PET scan to rule out active metabolic disease elsewhere and a Chest CT to clear pulmonary pathology. He also had plans for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult GI malignancy, which is particularly crucial in the context of dysphagia and intestinal myositis susceptibility. We cannot leave the room until these risks are mitigated or a clear timeline for surveillance is established, as malignancy surveillance should continue well beyond the initial hospitalization.

## The Critical Takeaways

When you walk into a room with adult dermatomyositis, the skin tells the story, but the cancer risk dictates the choreography. Don't let the angioedema mimic steal this opportunity for early detection. We must ask ourselves:
Which features of myositis and clinical presentation warrant a prompt malignancy screen?
What is the timeline and scope for that screen?
How do specific antibodies alter the baseline risk?

*   **The "Angioedema" Trap:** In adult dermatomyositis, subcutaneous edema and dysphagia can mimic angioedema or deep neck space infections. A high CK level often serves as the differentiator in this scenario.
*   **Risk Stratification is Non-Negotiable:** Active surveillance via IMACS guidelines is warranted for Dermatomyositis, specifically for Dysphagia and Anti-SAE1 positive cases. High-risk features mentioned include male sex, anti-SAE1 antibodies, and dysphagia.
*   **Screening Must Be Aggressive:** The workup should extend beyond the chest. As implied in the case, gastrointestinal screening for occult mucosal malignancy is critical, often necessitating upper and lower endoscopy, especially when dysphagia is present.
*   **Follow the PET/CT:** In unclear cases, a PET scan can help rule out metastatic disease despite localized findings, as referenced by the FDG accumulation in muscle groups without active disease elsewhere in this specific patient.
*   **Treat the Whole Patient:** While we treat the inflammation with steroids and immunosuppression, we cannot deselect the paraneoplastic potential. The mortality risk decreases significantly when associated malignancy is identified and treated concurrently.
*   **Screen Again:** Interval surveillance is not a one-time checkbox. Even without a successful initial screen, patients must be re-evaluated regularly based on disease course and initial risk stratification.

If you see a neck that won't resolve and a CK that climbs to the ceiling, look deeper than the lymph nodes. Look for the cancer, because treating the skin is mandatory, but finding the tumor is what extends the horizon for these patients.



# Airway Safety First: Managing Dysphagia in Acute Inflammatory Myopathy

Sometimes the most dangerous part of a medical mystery isn't the diagnosis itself, but the treatment delay it causes. In a recent case, a man in his 40s presented with severe neck swelling. The front-line teams immediately chased an infectious etiology—Ludwig’s angina. When that failed, they shifted gears to angioedema. Both diagnoses are correct in their own right for the *symptom*, but disastrous for long-term outcomes if they miss the underlying muscle pathology.

What we have here is dermatomyositis presenting with "pseudo-angioedema." But the airway implications go far deeper than a swelling neck. The underlying inflammatory process was actively chewing up the myofibres of the swallow musculature and shoulder girdle. The lesson here is critical for the Emergency Department and the Ward team alike: When you see unexplained neck swelling with high inflammatory markers, before you name an angioedema medication, think about the CK level and the dysphagia trial because malnutrition and aspiration risk can be the coma-equivalent complications of this disease.

## The Radiographic Deception and the Silent Danger

The initial CT of this patient’s neck showed diffuse oedema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. This image screams deep neck infection to the unwary radiologist. But he was afebrile, afebrile room air saturating, and denied fever on history. The ENT team looked for compression and congestion on nasendoscopy. They found increased secretions and coughing, but no structural blockage.

The key to unlocking this clinical picture lies in understanding that muscle hypertrophy and inflammation can mimic soft tissue oedema. In inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis, the neck swelling can be a subacute manifestation of muscle inflammation rather than a histaminergic or bradykininergic reaction. In this case, the C4, C1 esterase function, and C4 levels were all normal, effectively ruling out hereditary angioedema. However, the path to definitive treatment was blocked by the assumption that this was purely a cutaneous or primary airway issue.

Crucially, the dysphagia in this patient was not just a minor annoyance; it was a major airway threat. During the flexible nasendoscopy, the patient had increased secretions and coughing during the exam. The culmination of this finding was a barium swallow study that revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with significant pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration. This is not a patient who can safely eat dinner. It is a patient who is increasingly inhaling their own secretions.

The consequence of this aspiration was seen on admission CT chest: patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation likely representing aspiration pneumonitis. The patient was essentially suffering from the consequences of their muscle wasting and inflammation before they were even aggressive enough to be reclassified.

## Ensuring Nutritional Security and Preventing Complications

In a patient with this degree of proximal muscle involvement and swallowing dysphagia, nutritional status is an independent predictor of morbidity and mortality. Oral intake was impossible to maintain safely given the risk of aspiration pony-mania.

The management strategy involved securing the airway and nutrition early. By Day 1 of admission, it was clear that a nasogastric tube was required. The barium swallow confirmed the severity, and he was admitted with a plan to feed him per-aloogastric route because simply pushing him back to a diet of ice chips wasn't going to fix the aspiration.

This isn't a one-week fix. In the follow-up, he remained on a nasogastric tube for a further two months. This highlights a key teaching point for the emergency clinician: treat the dysphagia aerodynamically. A diet of fluids without protection from aspiration can lead to silent aspiration, pneumonia, and sepsis, which complicates the inflammatory picture further. If the patient isn't nutritional and hydrated, the immune system is stressed.

Furthermore, respiratory muscle involvement. The case report notes an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests and fractional acidosis on a PET scan of multiple muscle groups including the chest wall. This leaves us in a position where we have to balance the need to suppress inflammation with the risk of respiratory muscle fatigue.

## Balancing Immunosuppression with Infection Risk

Once the diagnosis of Anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis was confirmed, the pharmacological path is aggressive. He got methylprednisolone 1000mg, IVIG, Mycophenolate, and Rituximab. That is a heavy hammer.

The mortality risk in inflammatory myopathy comes not only from the disease but from the prophylaxis. This patient has a high-risk profile. He needed PJP prophylaxis because of high-dose steroids and lung involvement. He was started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim but developed hemolytic anaemia, which was a challenge to manage as no G6PD deficiency was found. They switched him to nebulised pentamidine.

This detail is important. Infection management in these patients is precarious. If you suppress the inflammation and give them immunosuppression, you increase their susceptibility to *Pneumocystis*. If you try to get the infection cleared, you might delay the window to prevent muscle necrosis. You have to decide based on the clinical picture.

The resolution of the case is quite remarkable. The patient recovered. His CK normalized. His dysphagia resolved enough to pull the feeding tube. But the transition period was where the risk lay.

## Take-Home Clinical Pearls

*   **The CK is your best friend in neck swelling:** 
    In any patient presenting with unexplained neck swelling, lymphadenopathy, or restrictive symptoms, consider Dermatomyositis if the C-reactive protein is frankly elevated and the CT shows diffuse muscle involvement rather than abscess or pus.
*   **Dysphagia is a medical emergency:**
    Identify subtle signs (coughing on nasendoscopy, thick secretions) early. A Barium swallow can distinguish swallowing dysfunction from structural obstruction.
*   **Watch the airway:** 
    Inflammatory myopathy often involves the muscles of respiration. If your patient is on high-dose steroids or IVIG, they are at risk for PJP pneumonia with the immune system. Monitor for infiltrates that might mimic infectious pneumonia or PJP.
*   **Support via the nose, protect against aspiration:**
    *   **Nasogastric feeding:** Indicated for significant dysphagia to prevent progression to severe deconditioning.
    *   **Anesthesia:** Review the side effects of immunosuppression drugs like Methotrexate with interstitial lung disease.
    *   **Infection monitoring:** Ensure prophylaxis is appropriate.

There is a common adage: "Treat the patient, not the lab result." In this case, it was the neck swelling that brought the patient in, but the muscle enzyme kinetics and the swallow study that saved the patient from malnutrition and treatable further pneumonia. When you see a patient with "angioedema" refilling the hospital floor, remember that it is not always histamine. It could be muscle. And saving the patient's life might just depend on a simple swallow screen.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Red Flag:** Unexplained neck swelling in a patient with no cold lull symptoms matches Dermatomyositis.
*   **Action:** 
*   **Screen:** High-resolution CT and CK levels are required to distinguish pseudo-angioedema from infection.
*   **Therapy:** Aggressive suppression with IVIG and steroids is necessary but requires careful infection prophylaxis balancing.
*   **Monitor:** Periodic CT scans are essential for cancer screening in patients with SAE1 positive myositis.



# Escalating Immunosuppression: A Protocol for Severe Dermatomyositis with Respiratory Involvement

## The Angioedema Mimic
We often get bogged down in the alleyways of the airway algorithm when a patient presents with neck swelling. Our standard mental model screams deep space infection or histaminergic angioedema, and while those are the most common adversaries, there are exceptions that require a different set of keys entirely. This case highlights a patient presenting with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling, initially labeled as probable Ludwig's angina, then reviewed by ENT as a deep neck infection, and finally labeled as idiopathic angioedema. None of those diagnoses fit the picture once you really look at the labs. The patient was afebrile, had a high inflammatory marker (CRP 55.6), and crucially, had a creatine kinase sky-high at 16,184 U/L. That number is the bell noise you can't ignore in the face of paresthesias or unexplained swelling. It is a classic example of subcutaneous tissue edema arising from myositis rather than vasodilation or complement activation, a presentation that essentially forces you to step outside the standard dermatologist-ear nose throat boundaries.

## Recognizing the Subtle Signals
The diagnostic journey here began with a subtle physical sign that was almost under the radar of the initial assessment. Reviewing the case, one notes that this patient lacked the classic textbook features for quite some time. There were no Gottron papules, no established heliotrope eruption, and no visible rash initially. Instead, the exam revealed periungual microhaemorrhages and subtle nailfold changes on capillaroscopy. These are often the "aesthetic" dermatomyositis signs that shock clinicians once they have been fishing for the classic myositis presentation. Even more telling was the presence of the Holster sign, noted on the operative MRI and later confirmed by biopsy, which represents edema of the skin overlying the extensor muscles—a specific marker of the disease process that occurred in the head and neck region.

The diagnostic pivot came when the immunology team identified a positive anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1 (SAE1) antibody. This is a specific marker for a clinical phenotype where dysphagia is particularly prominent (seen in 46% of cases), and malignancy risk is elevated compared to other types of myositis. The patient presented with severe aspiration pneumonitis on CT, confirming the airway involvement, which drove the urgency. The biopsy confirmed perifascicular atrophy and C5b-9 deposition, the definitive histological sign of dermatomyositis, but the clinical management had to account for the subcutaneous swelling which was inflamed muscle tissue, not histologicalangioedema. This distinction is vital because it means antihistamines and potentially epinephrine or methylprednisolone won't treat the swelling as quickly as needed given the inflammatory nature of the myositis.

## When Standard Care Isn't Enough: A Multidisciplinary Approach
When you land a patient in this trap with significant functional compromise—like this patient's severe dysphagia requiring a nasogastric tube—you have to consider an aggressive immunosuppression protocol before waiting too long for a taper. In this case, the team recognized that corticosteroids alone were insufficient to resolve the airway and swallowing risks in the short term. They started with bolus intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days. This is a high-dose regimen often reserved for SIAD/urethral obstruction or severe autoimmune conditions where humoral output is high. It was an upfront decision to treat the inflammatory edema in the retrogenginal space immediately rather than waiting to see if a prednisone taper would work.

Once the patient was stabilized, the intensity ramped up to a combination regimen. They utilized Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2g/kg split over five days, followed by maintenance cycles four weeks apart. For this particular antibody profile (SAE1) and the presence of pulmonary involvement, Rituximab was also added at 1000mg boluses. Crucially, despite the standard consideration of methotrexate for inflammatory myopathies, they explicitly avoided it due to the patient's existing pulmonary nodules and restrictive pattern, suggesting interstitial lung disease (ILD) risk. This is a critical clinical pearl: in patients with clear respiratory compromise or nodular disease, DM therapy must be tailored to spare the lungs from compounds that could exacerbate ILD. They switched prophylaxis to nebulized pentamidine after the patient developed hemolysis on TMP-SMX, demonstrating the rigidity often needed in managing immunocompromised inpatients.

The approach here supports the growing consensus that SAE1-positive patients require more robust immunosuppression than typical DM phenotypes, often sparing methotrexate for steroid carry-over while using mycophenolate mofetil for long-term control. The patient required a taper from 60mg Prednisone but faced long-term airway management requiring a monitoring plan for malignancy per IMACS guidelines. The multi-month follow-up shows close surveillance of CT chest and GI endoscopy is not optional but required given the dysphagia and skin involvement.

## Take Home Messages
*   **Unexplained neck swelling in a patient >40 with proximal weakness is dermatomyositis until proven otherwise.**
*   **Look for the "micro" signs:** Periungual changes, nailfold hemorrhages, and the Holster sign often precede classic heliotrope or Gottron signs.
*   **AB-cr:** Always order a CK level when you see unexplained angioedema or subcutaneous edema without histamine/bradykinin history.
*   **SAE1 phenotype:** Associated with higher risk of dysphagia and malignancy; consider aggressive therapy (IVIG/Rituximab) early.
*   **Lung protection:** Avoid methotrexate if there is evidence of ILD or pulmonary nodules; MMF is a preferred alternative.
*   **Clinical Surveillance:** High-risk DM patients require strict malignancy screening (CT chest, GI endoscopy) per IMACS guidelines.
