Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Pathophysiology] What is the underlying pathophysiological mechanism of the subcutaneous edema observed in this case of dermatomyositis, and how does it differ from the histaminergic or bradykinin pathways underlying typical angioedema?

The subcutaneous edema observed in this case is best characterized as **pseudoangioedema** driven by active **myositis** rather than a specific mediator release pathway. Ultimately, this swelling is a local manifestation of the systemic autoimmune process affecting the skin and muscles.

To understand the pathophysiology, one must distinguish the inflammatory mechanisms involved. In this patient with anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis, the neck swelling results from direct **inflammatory infiltration and tissue reaction** associated with the myositis. As the discussion notes, "the subcutaneous oedema in our patient's case arises from muscle inflammation in the setting of myositis." Inflammatory cytokines and infiltrating cells cause increased vascular permeability and tissue expansion in the neck strap muscles and deep fascia, mimicking the bulk of angioedema without utilizing the specific vascular mediators of true angioedema.

This contrasts sharply with the pathways underlying typical angioedema:
1.  **Histaminergic Angioedema:** Driven by mast cell degranulation and histamine release, typically IgE-mediated. It usually responds to antihistamines and often involves urticaria (whealing), which was absent here.
2.  **Bradykinin-mediated Angioedema:** Occurs via Complement C1 inhibitor deficiency (hereditary) or ACE inhibitor therapy, leading to unopposed bradykinin release.
3.  This Case (Dermatomyositis Pseudoangioedema):** It is **non-histaminergic and non-bradykinin** in the classic sense. Crucially, the workup showed **normal C3 and C4 complement levels**, effectively ruling out hypocomplementemic vasculitis or complement-mediated bradykinin surges often seen in angioedema variants.

Therefore, the key teaching point is that neck swelling in dermatomyositis is a **secondary inflammatory process** due to direct myositis extension, not a primary vascular permeability disorder mediated by kinins or histamines. The presence of elevated CK, characteristic cutaneous signs (periungual erythema, nailfold hemorrhages), and normal complement levels should prompt a workup for dermatomyositis rather than immediate empiric treatment for idiopathic angioedema.
