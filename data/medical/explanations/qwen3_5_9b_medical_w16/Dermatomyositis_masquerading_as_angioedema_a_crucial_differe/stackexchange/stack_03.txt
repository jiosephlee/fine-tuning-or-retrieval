Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient presents with progressive neck swelling, what are the primary differential diagnoses that should be ruled out before considering an autoimmune etiology like dermatomyositis in the absence of classic cutaneous rashes?

When evaluating a patient with progressive neck swelling, the clinician must categorically rule out life-threatening infectious, structural, and vascular etiologies before attributing the presentation to an immune-mediated process like dermatomyositis. In the case we are reviewing, the patient presented with a 3-week history of swelling initially mimicking Ludwig’s angina. Standard workup for this included a CT of the neck, a flexible nasendoscopy by ENT, and a search for bacterial or deep neck space infection. Because infectious causes were excluded, the differential shifted to primary angioedema.

Before concluding an autoimmune etiology, you must exclude hereditary or acquired angioedema (hence C1 esterase inhibitor function and C3/C4 levels) and bradykinin-mediated causes, such as ACE inhibitor use or beta-blocker hypersensitivity. In the reported case, the patient’s C1 esterase and complement levels were normal, and he denied angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor use, making these less likely.

While less common than infection or angioedema, one must also rule out superior vena cava syndrome and orofacial granulomatosis, particularly if swelling is asymmetric or associated with lymphadenopathy and dysphagia. Myasthenia gravis and other neuromuscular disorders should also be considered if weakness is prominent, though they rarely cause massive tissue edema.

The diagnostic pivot point in this specific presentation, and your critical teaching point for the team, is recognizing **pseudoangioedema** secondary to inflammatory myopathy. As demonstrated in this case, subcutaneous tissue oedema can arise from active myositis (muscle inflammation) rather than true angioedema. To secure this diagnosis, a high index of suspicion is required when infectious and angioedemic workups are unrevealing. Specifically, you should inquire about proximal muscle weakness and, crucially, obtain a creatine kinase level. In this patient, a level of 16,184 U/L was obtained during the immune workup phase, triggering the correct referral to immunology.

Therefore, if infection, bradykinin-mediated angioedema, and compressive syndromes are excluded, and the patient has elevated inflammatory markers or muscle enzymes, you must look for the "subtle cutaneous signs" that often accompany the swelling, such as nailfold capillary changes. Dermatomyositis typically involves the pharyngeal muscles, causing dysphagia, which was a key early sign in this patient despite the absence of classic heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules at presentation. This case underscores that autoimmune inflammatory myopathies should be in your differential for unexplained cervical swelling once the common "rule-outs" have been thoroughly excluded.
