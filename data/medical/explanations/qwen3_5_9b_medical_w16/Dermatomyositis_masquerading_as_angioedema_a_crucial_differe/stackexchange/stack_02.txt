Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] During the physical examination, which specific nailfold finding and extra-muscular muscle sign should alert the clinician to a possible autoimmune inflammatory myopathy in a patient presenting with unexplained neck swelling?

During the physical examination of a patient presenting with unexplained neck swelling, the combination of **nailfold capillaroscopy findings showing proximal microhaemorrhages and avascular areas** and **proximal muscle weakness (specifically hip flexor weakness)** should prompt a high-index suspicion for autoimmune inflammatory myopathy, specifically dermatomyositis.

In the case described, a man in his 40s was initially misdiagnosed with idiopathic angioedema or deep neck space infection. The crucial pivot in diagnosis occurred after the clinician recognized subtle cutaneous and musculoskeletal clues inconsistent with typical angioedema. While the patient had diffuse neck edema, a thorough nailfold capillaroscopy revealed **microhaemorrhages and avascular areas**, a hallmark vascular abnormality associated with dermatomyositis. Furthermore, concurrent **proximal muscle weakness**—manifesting as reduced power in his hip flexors—and subsequent **dysphagia** (indicating pharyngeal muscle involvement) were identified on examination.

The clinical reasoning here relies on the recognition that the neck swelling was not mediated by histamine or bradykinin, but rather represented inflammatory myositis involving the neck muscles and deep cervical fascia. The presence of nailfold capillary dropout and hemorrhage in the context of proximal weakness, even in the absence of classic cutaneous signs like Gottron’s papules (which were absent initially), strongly favors an inflammatory myopathy. This presentation underscores that dermatomyositis can mimic angioedema, and the "extra-muscular" manifestation of the disease in this case included both the swallowing difficulty and the inflammatory swelling of the neck muscles. Elevated creatine kinase levels should be investigated when these physical signs are present alongside unexplained neck swelling.
