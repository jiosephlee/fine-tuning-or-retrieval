Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What combination of electroclinical features and serological markers provided the strongest evidence to differentiate this patient's early-onset diabetes from autoantibody-positive Type 1 Diabetes or insulin-resistant Type 2 Diabetes?

In distinguishing this patient's early-onset diabetes as HNF1A-MODY from Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes, the strongest diagnostic evidence was the triad of negative autoimmune serology, preserved beta-cell function, and genetic confirmation.

First, serological evaluation is critical. As described in the case, diabetes-specific autoantibodies, including glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A), were **negative**. This effectively excludes autoimmune Type 1 diabetes, which is marked by beta-cell destruction driven by cell-mediated immunity.

Second, functional assessment of residual beta-cell secretory capacity provided key electroclinical differentiation. A **glucagon stimulation test** yielded a stimulated C-peptide level of **0.83 nmol/L** at 6 minutes. In the context of this case, a level greater than 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion, arguing against the absolute beta-cell failure seen in established Type 1 diabetes. Additionally, the patient presented with no significant symptoms of insulin resistance; specifically, his **BMI was 24.6 kg/m²** and **acanthosis nigricans was absent**, which contrasts with the profile of insulin-resistant Type 2 diabetes. The case report also utilizes electrochemical skin conductance testing (Sudoscan), which demonstrated severely reduced sudomotor function, indicating autonomic neuropathy consistent with the long-standing hyperglycemia, though the primary drivers for the differential diagnosis relied on the C-peptide and serological profile.

Finally, the definitive evidence was molecular genetics. Next-generation sequencing identified a novel heterozygous **HNF1A mutation (p.N270S)**. This confirmed the pathophysiology of Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 1-alpha-MODY, an autosomal dominant disorder explained by the patient’s strong family history (mother, grandmother, and brother diagnosed in their 20s to 30s).

In clinical practice, this profile—negative antibodies, preserved C-peptide, lack of insulin resistance features, and familial autosomal dominant inheritance—should prompt immediate genetic screening. As highlighted in this case, confirming the diagnosis allowed for a shift from intensive insulin therapy to targeted, sulfonylurea-sparing agents (specifically a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin), which significantly improved glycemic control while reducing treatment burden.

### [Pathophysiology] At the molecular level, how does the heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene impair the specific transcriptional functions required for pancreatic beta-cell insulin secretion?

In this case, the heterozygous p.N270S mutation in the *HNF1A* gene impairs pancreatic beta-cell insulin secretion by disrupting the transcriptional regulation of genes essential for glucose metabolism. As detailed in the Discussion, the *HNF1A* gene encodes a transcription factor expressed in the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Functional mutations, such as the novel variant identified here, result in a progressive decline in beta-cell function manifested by the **impaired expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes**, alongside reduced pyruvate kinase activity.

Specifically, the p.N270S mutation is located in exon 4 within the **DNA-binding domain** of the HNF1A protein. This structural localization suggests the mutation compromises the protein's ability to bind to specific promoter sequences, thereby failing to upregulate the transcription of insulin synthesis machinery and the glucose transporter GLUT2. Furthermore, because the expression of the renal sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) is under the transcriptional control of HNF1A, this defect correlates directly with the patient's clinical presentation of glycosuria due to reduced glucose reabsorption.

Understanding this molecular mechanism is vital for clinical precision. The defect described is characteristically heterozygous and results in quantitative transcriptional deficits rather than absolute beta-cell destruction (as seen in Type 1 diabetes). Consequently, the patient presented with preserved—but insufficient—beta-cell function, evidenced by the glucagon stimulation test C-peptide levels. This molecular insight guided the management strategy: recognizing the defect as a transcriptional dysfunction allowed for the transition from initial insulin therapy to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin. As highlighted in the Treatment and Discussion sections, this approach maintained effective glucose control while mitigating the hypoglycemia risks often associated with insulin, underscoring the importance of genetic screening to align therapy with the specific pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiological link between HNF1A mutations and the finding of persistent glycosuria in this patient despite the use of modern sequencing data.

This patient’s persistent glycosuria is a direct phenotypic consequence of the *HNF1A* mutation confirmed by next-generation sequencing. While the genetic data identifies the specific molecular etiology, the pathophysiology relies on the transcriptional role of the HNF1A protein.

As detailed in the case report discussion, the *HNF1A* gene encodes a transcription factor expressed in the renal proximal tubules, where it regulates the expression of the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (*SGLT2*). The novel heterozygous variant identified in this patient (p.N270S) compromises this transcriptional control, leading to reduced *SGLT2* expression. This downregulation lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, resulting in glycosuria at serum glucose concentrations lower than those typically required in Type 2 diabetes.

Therefore, modern sequencing clarifies that this glycosuria is not merely a marker of severe hyperglycemia or insulin deficiency, but a specific renal handling defect driven by the *HNF1A* haploinsufficiency. This mechanism is clinically significant: because SGLT2 is already functionally reduced, the use of pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibitors is contraindicated or risky in this population due to the potential for severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis, a point explicitly highlighted in the case’s discussion.

### [Management] Why was empiric insulin therapy eventually discontinued following genetic confirmation, and what first-line pharmacologic strategy is typically prioritized for HNF1A-MODY?

In this case, empiric insulin therapy was discontinued because the initial diagnosis of type 2 diabetes was revised to monogenic diabetes (MODY) upon genetic confirmation. The patient, initially receiving a basal insulin regimens based on presumed insulin resistance, achieved glycaemic control that allowed for insulin cessation once the specific underlying genotype (*HNF1A*) was identified. Continuing insulin in confirmed *HNF1A*-MODY is generally unnecessary as a first-line strategy because the pathophysiology involves a specific impairment in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion rather than absolute insulin deficiency or severe resistance seen in typical type 2 diabetes.

The standard first-line pharmacologic strategy for *HNF1A*-MODY is low-dose sulfonylureas. These agents work by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels to stimulate insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells, a mechanism that *HNF1A*MODY patients are uniquely sensitive to due to mutations in the transcriptional regulation of beta-cell function. However, as demonstrated in this case report, which highlights the novel p.N270S variant, GLP-1 receptor agonists may serve as a safer and effective alternative, particularly in patients concerned about hypoglycemia. In this specific patient, insulin was replaced with a GLP-1 receptor agonist before eventually settling on metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor for maintenance. Importantly, clinicians should exercise caution with SGLT2 inhibitors in *HNF1A*-MODY, as the gene regulates SGLT2 expression, potentially increasing the risk of dehydration or diabetic ketoacidosis, a nuance worth noting for future management of such genetic diagnoses.

### [Management] In this case, a novel heterozygous substitution (p.N270S) was identified that was not previously documented; what specific genetic and clinical evidence allowed the team to classify this variant as likely pathogenic?

In the absence of prior documentation in established databases such as ClinVar, the classification of a novel genetic variant requires the systematic application of the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. In this case, the team classified the novel *HNF1A* variant (p.N270S) as likely pathogenic based on four converging lines of evidence derived from the report.

First, **functional localization and bioinformatics** supported a pathogenic mechanism; the mutation was located within the DNA-binding domain and adjacent to known pathogenic residues, a computational analysis yielded a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995 indicative of a deleterious effect. Second, **familial segregation** confirmed the inheritance pattern; the variant was consistent with an autosomal dominant transmission, observed across three generations including the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother. Third, the **clinical phenotype** correlated with established HNF1A-MODY features, specifically the presence of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved beta-cell function on the glucagon stimulation test. Together, these genetic, computational, and clinical features satisfied the ACMG criteria for a likely pathogenic designation, allowing for a precise diagnosis despite the lack of prior literature.

### [Clinical Pearls] What specific class of glucose-lowering medications should be utilized with extreme caution or avoided in HNF1A-MODY, and what are the two major safety risks associated with their use in this population?

In patients with HNF1A-MODY, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors should be utilized with extreme caution or avoided. This teaching point is grounded in the pathophysiology described in the discussion of this case: HNF1A mutations lead to reduced transcriptional control of SGLT2 in the renal tubules, resulting in a naturally low renal threshold for glucose and the presence of glycosuria. Consequently, the inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity by these agents exacerbates renal glucose wasting. As highlighted in the case discussion, the two major safety risks associated with this class in this population are severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Because the pharmacologic inhibition of an already compromised transporter may overwhelm compensatory mechanisms, the risks often outweigh the potential glycemic benefits. In this patient, for instance, the clinical team successfully managed glycemia using a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin, aligning with current recommendations to prioritize modalities that do not further stress the renal-SGLT2 axis or beta-cell integrity.

### [Clinical Pearls] How did the patient's ethnicity and geographic background pose a limitation regarding the predictive value of the Exeter MODY probability calculator used for case suspicion?

The primary limitation of the Exeter MODY probability calculator in this case stems from a lack of external validity due to **geographic and ethnic derivation bias**. The discussion explicitly notes that this tool was developed using data from **White European populations**. Consequently, when applied to patients from non-White backgrounds—such as the demographic context largely represented in this team's cohort and supported by references to Asian prevalence studies in the background—the calculator may **overestimate the probability of MODY**.

Clinically, this bias arises because the distinct clinical phenotype separating MODY from Type 2 diabetes (T2D) varies across ethnicities. In White populations, early-onset diabetes is often associated with specific MODY risk factors (e.g., lower BMI, less metabolic syndrome) relative to the higher baseline prevalence of T2D in other populations. In this patient, despite a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² (not severely obese) and a strong family history, the pre-test probability for T2D is inherently higher in diverse ethnic groups where MODY is less frequently diagnosed due to screening bias. By using a Euro-centric algorithm, the calculator generated a 62.4% MODY probability, which risks inflating the likelihood in this context.

Therefore, while the calculator can serve as a useful screening triage tool, its predictive value is attenuated in non-White populations. This discrepancy underscores a critical management principle from the case: **clinical suspicion alone or probability scores cannot substitute for molecular confirmation**. Regardless of the inflated calculator score, the patient required genetic testing to definitively identify the novel *HNF1A* mutation and guide treatment away from insulin-dependent T2D protocols toward sulfonylurea-sparing strategies like GLP-1 receptor agonists.

### [Morning Report] Given that MODY is an autosomal dominant condition, how does this inheritance pattern specifically guide the screening and counseling approach for the patient's mother, brother, and grandmother?

The autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of HNF1A-maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) establishes the framework for cascade screening within the family. In this genetic architecture, each first-degree relative of a confirmed proband has a 50% probability of inheriting the pathogenic HNF1A mutation. Consequently, the patient's mother, who presented with early-onset diabetes in her late 20s, is an obligate carrier having transmitted the disease to her son; she warrants genetic confirmation to optimize her therapy, as patients with confirmed HNF1A-MODY often respond preferentially to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists rather than insulin, which can accelerate beta-cell decline.

The younger brother, who also manifested diabetes by age 20, falls into the 50% risk category for harboring the same p.N270S variant and requires evaluation, including genetic testing and clinical assessment to differentiate MODY from type 2 diabetes. The maternal grandmother, diagnosed in her 30s, represents the prior generation in this transmission chain and should similarly be offered genetic screening to complete the identification of affected family members. Ultimately, this inheritance pattern guides management by identifying carriers who may escape unnecessary insulin treatment and instead receive therapies that align with their monogenic defect, thereby improving long-term glycemic control and preserving beta-cell function across generations.
