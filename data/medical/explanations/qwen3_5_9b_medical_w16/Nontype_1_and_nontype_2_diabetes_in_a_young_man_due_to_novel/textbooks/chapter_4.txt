Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 4: ## Precision Medicine: Treatment Decisions and Pharmacological Management

In the initial phase of the case, the patient’s management followed a common clinical pattern for young adults with significant hyperglycemia: an empiric regimen targeting Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2D). Given the elevated HbA1c of 10.2%, the patient was initially treated with basal insulin degludec, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin. This approach reflected a standard of care driven by the severity of glycemic control and the need for rapid reduction of glucose levels, while accounting for the possibility of residual insulin resistance. However, this empiric strategy, while appropriate for a broad type 2 diabetes presentation, masked the underlying monogenic etiology. The patient's response to this regimen was notable; within one month, glycemic control improved significantly to an HbA1c of 9.1%, yet the onset of genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY prompted a strategic pivot in pharmacotherapy. This highlights a critical principle in clinical reasoning: initial monotherapy for severe hyperglycemia in novel phenotypes often prioritizes glycaemic safety over precision, but confirmation of the specific genetic defect allows for the tailoring of therapy to the underlying pathophysiology.

Once the pathogenic heterozygous variant in the HNF1A gene was identified, the treatment paradigm shifted away from insulin-dependent strategies. In classic Type 1 diabetes, insulin is the absolute first-line therapy because of near-total beta-cell destruction. In HNF1A-MODY, the primary defect is impaired beta-cell function and sensitivity rather than absolute insulin deficiency. Consequently, clamping insulin therapy led to the discontinuation while the patient utilized exogenous insulin, favoring agents that stimulate endogenous insulin secretion to preserve residual beta-cell function. The definitive genetic diagnosis allowed for the cessation of insulin degludec, realizing that the patient did not require physiological replacement but rather functional support. This decision was crucial to prevent unnecessary complications associated with insulin therapy in a patient whose beta-cells remain viable but hypersensitive to glucose, validating the importance of molecular diagnosis in preventing iatrogenic insulin dependence.

The subsequent transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, specifically dulaglutide, underscores the nuances of pharmacological choice in MODY. While sulfonylureas are traditionally the first-line agent for HNF1A-MODY by enhancing insulin secretion via potassium-sensitive ATP channels, the discussion identifies GLP-1 receptor agonists as safer alternatives, particularly in patients where hypoglycemia is a concern or where beta-cell preservation is a priority. GLP-1 receptor agonists stimulate insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner, reducing the risk of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas, and have shown efficacy in maintaining glucose control alongside dopaminergic activity modulation. This therapeutic swap did not compromise glycaemic efficacy; the patient's HbA1c stabilized at 6.0% after 6 months of GLP-1 therapy. This case demonstrates that while sulfonylureas are the historical gold standard, newer classes like GLP-1 agonists offer refined therapeutic options that align with modern goals of preserving beta-cell function and minimizing adverse events such as hypoglycemia.

A critical component of precision medicine in MODY is the exclusion of specific drug classes that may exacerbate the underlying renal pathophysiology. Although SGLT2 inhibitors are effective in T2D by lowering blood glucose through urinary excretion, they are generally contraindicated in HNF1A-MODY. The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor that regulates glucose transport in renal tubules. Patients with HNF1A mutations typically exhibit a lowered renal threshold for glucose, causing glycosuria even at lower blood glucose levels. Administering an SGLT2 inhibitor can exacerbate this defect, heightening the risk of dehydration, volume collapse, and euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis, which the case report explicitly warns against. In the absence of SGLT2 inhibitor use in the patient’s regimen, the reliance on Metformin and DPP-4 inhibitors mirrors a strategy of avoiding interventions that could precipitate metabolic instability, ensuring that glucose-lowering is achieved via hepatic metabolic clearance and GIP/GLP-1 pathways rather than renal excretion mechanisms vulnerable to HNF1A mutation effects.

Long-term maintenance therapy for this patient was simplified to a combination of metformin and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin once glycaemic control stabilized. This outcome reflects the standard goal of maintaining HbA1c within target ranges using the least invasive regimen that preserves beta-cell function. The patient achieved an HbA1c of 6.0% and subsequently maintained levels below 6.5% without insulin or GLP-1 receptor agonists, demonstrating the stability of the patient's beta-cell function under metabolic regulation. The feasibility of reducing pharmacotherapy to oral agents only after initial intensive control serves as a practical lesson for long-term management. It emphasizes that while high-risk genetics require immediate and precise attention, the ultimate goal remains the balancing of efficacy, safety, and quality of life. Through the transition from empiric T2D therapy to targeted HNF1A-modifying treatments, this case illustrates how genetic diagnostics are integral to optimizing therapeutic outcomes and preventing unnecessary exposure to drugs that may not suit the specific pathophysiological context of monogenic diabetes.
