Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Differential Diagnosis of Early-Onset Diabetes

The clinical presentation of this case highlights the diagnostic challenges inherent in identifying monogenic diabetes among young adults. The patient was referred during an annual health examination, where hyperglycemia and glycosuria were detected, rather than presenting with an acute metabolic crisis. Although the patient reported classic symptoms of hyperglycemia, including increased thirst, increased appetite, and recent weight loss—often associated with the rapid decline seen in new-onset Type 1 diabetes—he presented without signs of diabetic ketoacidosis. This lack of ketosis is a critical clinical feature that differentiates this case from a new autoimmune Type 1 presentation, which typically requires immediate insulin initiation and often manifests with ketoacidosis. Instead, the absence of ketosis alongside elevated glycated hemoglobin at 10.2% pointed toward a condition with a slower progression, raising suspicion for a different etiology than autoimmune diabetes.

Physical characteristics played a vital role in narrowing the differential diagnosis specifically regarding metabolic phenotypes. Upon examination, the patient had a body mass index of 24.6 kg/m2, which is within the normal range, and notably, acanthosis nigricans was absent. In the context of early-onset diabetes, these findings are significant red flags for Type 2 diabetes, as severe obesity and cutaneous signs of insulin resistance are common in that disorder, largely driven by the hyperinsulinemia associated with insulin resistance. The absence of acanthosis nigricans argued against classical Type 2 diabetes, despite the patient’s lipid profile suggesting possible metabolic dysregulation. This distinction is crucial because mischaracterizing MODY as Type 2 diabetes often leads to the inappropriate use of antihyperglycemic medications targeted at insulin resistance, which may fail to achieve glycemic control and delay the recognition of the underlying genetic defect.

Family history serves as the most powerful lineage-specific clinical clue for distinguishing MODY from sporadic forms of diabetes. In this case, the family history confirmed early-onset diabetes in three generations, specifically the maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother diagnosed at age 20. This pattern of vertical transmission without skipped generations is the hallmark of autosomal dominant inheritance, strongly suggesting a monogenic cause like MODY. While Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes can occur in families, the recurrence of early-onset disease at such young ages across multiple family members creates a high pre-test probability for MODY. However, clinical reliance on family history alone can be misleading; accurate diagnosis requires integrating these family patterns with biochemical evidence, such as negative autoantibodies, to support a syndrome that fits the criteria for non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes.

The final differentiation relies on ruling out autoimmune causes and confirming the lack of severe metabolic comorbidities typical of Type 2 diabetes. Laboratory investigations revealed negative results for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A), effectively excluding autoimmune Type 1 diabetes, which is often characterized by the presence of pancreatic citizenship autorenvibodies and low C-peptide levels. Conversely, while the patient had risk factors often associated with Type 2 diabetes, such as weight loss and dyslipidemia, his age of onset and lack of severe obesity align with criteria where MODY is more prevalent. Current clinical guidelines suggest that in young adults with diabetes, negative autoantibodies, and lack of severe obesity, genetic screening for MODY should be considered, as accurate diagnosis allows for treatment adjustments, such as utilizing sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, rather than lifelong insulin dependence.

In summary, the patient's presentation combines typical hyperglycemic symptoms with the critical differentiating feature of preserved residual insulin secretion in a non-obese individual with a strong family history. These elements—symptoms of glucotoxicity without ketoacidosis, normal BMI, absence of acanthosis nigricans, and vertical family history of early-onset diabetes—collectively raised the clinical red flags necessary to pivot from a standard evaluation to a specialized MODY workup. Recognizing this specific constellation of clinical findings is essential to prevent misdiagnosis and ensure patients receive the precise pharmacological management that targets their specific pathophysiology.
