Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Workup and Genetic Confirmation of MODY

The establishment of a diagnosis for Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) in this patient required a systematic exclusion of Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes, followed by targeted assessment of beta-cell function and family history. A key initial step in the diagnostic algorithm was the screening for islet autoantibodies to rule out autoimmune diabetes, such as Type 1 diabetes. In this case, the patient tested negative for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A), which is consistent with the non-autoimmune nature of MODY, though clinical awareness remains essential as positive antibody results can occasionally occur in these populations. Alongside autoimmunity screening, assessment of endogenous insulin secretion is critical to distinguish MODY from Type 1 diabetes, which often presents with absolute insulin deficiency early in the disease course. The patient underwent a glucagon stimulation test, which involves measuring C-peptide levels before and after the administration of glucagon. A stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L at six minutes was recorded; generally, a level greater than 0.60 nmol/L suggests preserved endogenous insulin secretion and supports a diagnosis of non-Type 1 diabetes. However, clinical standards dictate that this test should be viewed as supportive rather than definitive, particularly when autoantibody results are inconclusive or the clinical presentation is atypical.

To further characterize the glucose intolerance profile, an Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) was interpreted alongside fasting glucose measurements, revealing a 2-hour post-prandial glucose of 19.8 mmol/L against a fasting level of 12.4 mmol/L. This pattern of significant postprandial hyperglycemia is characteristic of MODY, where postprandial glucose intolerance often emerges first. Furthermore, the patient presented with glycosuria and hyperglycaemia, a phenomenon linked to the pathophysiology of the *HNF1A* gene. The *HNF1A* transcription factor regulates the expression of *SGLT2* in renal tubules; consequently, mutations can lead to a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, resulting in glycosuria even at lower blood glucose levels than typical Type 2 diabetes. Comprehensive assessment also included autonomic function testing using electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan). While not strictly a diagnostic test for MODY itself, the results demonstrated severely reduced sudomotor function in the hands, indicating significant autonomic neuropathy consistent with the duration of significant hyperglycemia. When combined with the negative autoantibodies, preserved beta-cell function, and strong family history of early-onset diabetes, the clinical suspicion for MODY increased sufficiently to warrant molecular genetic testing despite the patient's BMI of 24.6 kg/m², which might otherwise suggest metabolic syndrome and Type 2 diabetes in a traditional algorithm.

Given the clinical probability of MODY, Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) was utilized to analyze the patient's DNA. This approach allowed for the screening of multiple monogenic diabetes-related genes simultaneously, identifying a heterozygous substitution in the *HNF1A* gene, specifically the novel variant p.N270S (c.809A>G) in exon 4. The significance of this finding relied on genetic interpretation guidelines offered by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) because the variant was not previously reported in major databases such as ClinVar or VarSome. Establishing the pathogenicity of a novel variant requires integrating multiple lines of evidence, including mutational location and functional impact. In this instance, the variant was located in the DNA-binding domain of the protein and analysis tools indicated a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995. Furthermore, the family history demonstrated clear segregation of the mutation with diabetes symptoms across three generations, providing strong co-segregation evidence. Although the absence of historical data initially created uncertainty, the convergence of biochemical evidence, conservative ACMG classification criteria for likely pathogenic variants, and familial segregation allowed for the confirmation of HNF1A-MODY. This case underscores that while existing variant databases are valuable resources, the clinical context of segregation and functional prediction scores remain essential for validating novel variants.
