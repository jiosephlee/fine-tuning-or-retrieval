Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Outcomes, Long-Term Prognosis, and Population Implications

The clinical trajectory of this patient underscores the profound impact that accurate genetic diagnosis can have on glycemic control and therapeutic strategy. Upon initial presentation with an HbA1c of 10.2% and symptoms typically associated with Type 2 diabetes, the patient was treated with insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. Once the novel *HNF1A* mutation was identified via next-generation sequencing (NGS), the treatment paradigm shifted to align with the pathophysiology of monogenic diabetes. Insulin therapy was discontinued, and a GLP-1 receptor agonist was introduced to support glycaemic control and potentially preserve remaining pancreatic function, which eventually contributed to a significant reduction in HbA1c to 6.0% within six months. This outcome illustrates the principle that while clinical criteria often point toward obesity or insulin resistance in Type 2 diabetes, patients with MODY require therapies that target specific beta-cell defects rather than purely improving insulin sensitivity. This precision medicine approach avoids the hypoglycaemia risks associated with sulfonylureas in some contexts and allows for effective management with lower HbA1c targets than would have been expected with a Type 2 diabetes diagnosis alone. Despite this improvement, long-term surveillance remains a critical component of the prognosis. As with all forms of diabetes, strict monitoring for microvascular complications is required. Serial assessments in this case revealed no signs of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, supporting the generally lower risk profile of MODY compared to Type 2 diabetes, particularly given the absence of significant structural complications even after prolonged hyperglycaemia. However, the patient’s Sudomotor testing indicated severe autonomic dysfunction, a reminder that end-organ damage can occur in non-insulin-dependent diabetes and necessitates continued vigilance.

The identification of the liver-specific transcription factor gene mutation, specifically the heterozygous variation p.N270S in exon 4, provides a crucial insight into the limitations of current genetic databases and the utility of high-throughput sequencing. This variant was not catalogued in major public repositories such as ClinVar or VarSome at the time of analysis, highlighting a potential gap in the diagnostic records for monogenic diabetes. However, the pathogenicity of this novel variant was validated through the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. Evidence including a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), segregation of the trait within the family (diabetes occurring across three generations), and the variant's location in the known DNA-binding domain of the HNF1A gene met the criteria for a likely pathogenic classification. This case demonstrates that a negative search in public variant databases does not rule out a pathogenic mutation, particularly in diverse populations where many rare alleles may remain unreported. It reinforces that molecular genetic testing should not be restricted solely by the presence of a known mutation in a database but rather by clinical suspicion and ACMG-based interpretation of novel findings.

From a broader epidemiological perspective, this case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by diverse ethnic populations, specifically the underrepresentation of Asian and non-White groups in historical MODY research which has primarily focused on Caucasian cohorts. The patient's normal BMI (24.6 kg/m²) and lack of acanthosis nigricans were consistent with typical MODY presentations but contrast with the high cardiovascular and metabolic syndrome risks often seen in Type 2 diabetes, yet the high BMI observed in some Asian MODY cases is increasingly recognized due to the rising prevalence of Type 2 diabetes antigens in the region. Clinical tools such as the Exeter probability calculator, while useful, have been observed to overestimate MODY probability in non-European populations. Consequently, clinicians must exercise a high index of suspicion regarding family histories of early-onset diabetes regardless of body habitus or ethnic background. Looking forward, the increasing accessibility of NGS panels offers a scalable strategy to improve the diagnostic yield of monogenic diabetes, moving from phenotype-based diagnosis to genotype-directed therapy. Future research must focus on optimizing cost-effective screening algorithms and expanding variant databases to ensure that emerging genetic insights translate into equitable care for all demographic groups, ultimately reducing misdiagnosis and improving patient outcomes globally.
