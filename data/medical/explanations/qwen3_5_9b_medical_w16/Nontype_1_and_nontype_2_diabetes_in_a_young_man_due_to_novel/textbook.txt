Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Clinical Presentation and Differential Diagnosis of Early-Onset Diabetes

The clinical presentation of this case highlights the diagnostic challenges inherent in identifying monogenic diabetes among young adults. The patient was referred during an annual health examination, where hyperglycemia and glycosuria were detected, rather than presenting with an acute metabolic crisis. Although the patient reported classic symptoms of hyperglycemia, including increased thirst, increased appetite, and recent weight loss—often associated with the rapid decline seen in new-onset Type 1 diabetes—he presented without signs of diabetic ketoacidosis. This lack of ketosis is a critical clinical feature that differentiates this case from a new autoimmune Type 1 presentation, which typically requires immediate insulin initiation and often manifests with ketoacidosis. Instead, the absence of ketosis alongside elevated glycated hemoglobin at 10.2% pointed toward a condition with a slower progression, raising suspicion for a different etiology than autoimmune diabetes.

Physical characteristics played a vital role in narrowing the differential diagnosis specifically regarding metabolic phenotypes. Upon examination, the patient had a body mass index of 24.6 kg/m2, which is within the normal range, and notably, acanthosis nigricans was absent. In the context of early-onset diabetes, these findings are significant red flags for Type 2 diabetes, as severe obesity and cutaneous signs of insulin resistance are common in that disorder, largely driven by the hyperinsulinemia associated with insulin resistance. The absence of acanthosis nigricans argued against classical Type 2 diabetes, despite the patient’s lipid profile suggesting possible metabolic dysregulation. This distinction is crucial because mischaracterizing MODY as Type 2 diabetes often leads to the inappropriate use of antihyperglycemic medications targeted at insulin resistance, which may fail to achieve glycemic control and delay the recognition of the underlying genetic defect.

Family history serves as the most powerful lineage-specific clinical clue for distinguishing MODY from sporadic forms of diabetes. In this case, the family history confirmed early-onset diabetes in three generations, specifically the maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother diagnosed at age 20. This pattern of vertical transmission without skipped generations is the hallmark of autosomal dominant inheritance, strongly suggesting a monogenic cause like MODY. While Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes can occur in families, the recurrence of early-onset disease at such young ages across multiple family members creates a high pre-test probability for MODY. However, clinical reliance on family history alone can be misleading; accurate diagnosis requires integrating these family patterns with biochemical evidence, such as negative autoantibodies, to support a syndrome that fits the criteria for non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes.

The final differentiation relies on ruling out autoimmune causes and confirming the lack of severe metabolic comorbidities typical of Type 2 diabetes. Laboratory investigations revealed negative results for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A), effectively excluding autoimmune Type 1 diabetes, which is often characterized by the presence of pancreatic citizenship autorenvibodies and low C-peptide levels. Conversely, while the patient had risk factors often associated with Type 2 diabetes, such as weight loss and dyslipidemia, his age of onset and lack of severe obesity align with criteria where MODY is more prevalent. Current clinical guidelines suggest that in young adults with diabetes, negative autoantibodies, and lack of severe obesity, genetic screening for MODY should be considered, as accurate diagnosis allows for treatment adjustments, such as utilizing sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, rather than lifelong insulin dependence.

In summary, the patient's presentation combines typical hyperglycemic symptoms with the critical differentiating feature of preserved residual insulin secretion in a non-obese individual with a strong family history. These elements—symptoms of glucotoxicity without ketoacidosis, normal BMI, absence of acanthosis nigricans, and vertical family history of early-onset diabetes—collectively raised the clinical red flags necessary to pivot from a standard evaluation to a specialized MODY workup. Recognizing this specific constellation of clinical findings is essential to prevent misdiagnosis and ensure patients receive the precise pharmacological management that targets their specific pathophysiology.

## Diagnostic Workup and Genetic Confirmation of MODY

The establishment of a diagnosis for Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) in this patient required a systematic exclusion of Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes, followed by targeted assessment of beta-cell function and family history. A key initial step in the diagnostic algorithm was the screening for islet autoantibodies to rule out autoimmune diabetes, such as Type 1 diabetes. In this case, the patient tested negative for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A), which is consistent with the non-autoimmune nature of MODY, though clinical awareness remains essential as positive antibody results can occasionally occur in these populations. Alongside autoimmunity screening, assessment of endogenous insulin secretion is critical to distinguish MODY from Type 1 diabetes, which often presents with absolute insulin deficiency early in the disease course. The patient underwent a glucagon stimulation test, which involves measuring C-peptide levels before and after the administration of glucagon. A stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L at six minutes was recorded; generally, a level greater than 0.60 nmol/L suggests preserved endogenous insulin secretion and supports a diagnosis of non-Type 1 diabetes. However, clinical standards dictate that this test should be viewed as supportive rather than definitive, particularly when autoantibody results are inconclusive or the clinical presentation is atypical.

To further characterize the glucose intolerance profile, an Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) was interpreted alongside fasting glucose measurements, revealing a 2-hour post-prandial glucose of 19.8 mmol/L against a fasting level of 12.4 mmol/L. This pattern of significant postprandial hyperglycemia is characteristic of MODY, where postprandial glucose intolerance often emerges first. Furthermore, the patient presented with glycosuria and hyperglycaemia, a phenomenon linked to the pathophysiology of the *HNF1A* gene. The *HNF1A* transcription factor regulates the expression of *SGLT2* in renal tubules; consequently, mutations can lead to a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, resulting in glycosuria even at lower blood glucose levels than typical Type 2 diabetes. Comprehensive assessment also included autonomic function testing using electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan). While not strictly a diagnostic test for MODY itself, the results demonstrated severely reduced sudomotor function in the hands, indicating significant autonomic neuropathy consistent with the duration of significant hyperglycemia. When combined with the negative autoantibodies, preserved beta-cell function, and strong family history of early-onset diabetes, the clinical suspicion for MODY increased sufficiently to warrant molecular genetic testing despite the patient's BMI of 24.6 kg/m², which might otherwise suggest metabolic syndrome and Type 2 diabetes in a traditional algorithm.

Given the clinical probability of MODY, Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) was utilized to analyze the patient's DNA. This approach allowed for the screening of multiple monogenic diabetes-related genes simultaneously, identifying a heterozygous substitution in the *HNF1A* gene, specifically the novel variant p.N270S (c.809A>G) in exon 4. The significance of this finding relied on genetic interpretation guidelines offered by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) because the variant was not previously reported in major databases such as ClinVar or VarSome. Establishing the pathogenicity of a novel variant requires integrating multiple lines of evidence, including mutational location and functional impact. In this instance, the variant was located in the DNA-binding domain of the protein and analysis tools indicated a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995. Furthermore, the family history demonstrated clear segregation of the mutation with diabetes symptoms across three generations, providing strong co-segregation evidence. Although the absence of historical data initially created uncertainty, the convergence of biochemical evidence, conservative ACMG classification criteria for likely pathogenic variants, and familial segregation allowed for the confirmation of HNF1A-MODY. This case underscores that while existing variant databases are valuable resources, the clinical context of segregation and functional prediction scores remain essential for validating novel variants.

## Pathophysiology and Molecular Mechanisms of HNF1A-MODY

The hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene is located on the long arm of chromosome 12 (12q24) and encodes a 631 amino acid transcription factor. As described in the case discussion, this protein plays a critical role in regulating gene expression essential for glucose metabolism in the liver, kidney, and pancreas. In the reported patient, genetic testing revealed a novel heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4. This variant is significant because it adheres to the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern observed in the patient’s family, where the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother were all diagnosed with early-onset diabetes. The presence of a heterozygous mutation confirms that one functional allele is insufficient to maintain normal glucose homeostasis, distinguishing this from recessive disorders and necessitating strategies tailored for monogenic defects rather than heterogeneous polygenic conditions like type 2 diabetes.

In terms of pancreatic pathophysiology, the HNF1A mutation leads to a specific defect in the development and function of beta-cells. The HNF1A protein normally ensures the expression of key genes involved in insulin production, such as insulin itself and GLUT2 (glucose transporter type 2), as well as regulating pyruvate kinase activity. When the transcription factor is compromised, beta-cells fail to respond appropriately to glucose stimuli, resulting in decreased insulin secretion rather than an absolute absence of insulin. Consequently, patients present with progressive glucose intolerance. In this case, the patient’s glucagon stimulation test supported this mechanism; while the stimulated C-peptide level reached 0.83 nmol/L, it remained insufficient to maintain euglycemia under normal dietary stress, explaining the initial initiation of insulin therapy before the correct diagnosis was confirmed. However, once the molecular defect was identified, the requirement for exogenous insulin could be avoided because the remaining beta-cell function was not exhausted, allowing for alternative oral or injectable agents that stimulate secretion without overwhelming the compromised reserve.

Beyond the pancreas, the HNF1A mutation significantly impacts renal physiology through the regulation of SGLT2 (sodium-glucose cotransporter-2) expression. HNF1A normally serves as a transcriptional activator for SGLT2, which reabsorbs glucose in the renal tubules. Deficiency in HNF1A leads to reduced SGLT2 expression, thereby lowering the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. This biological mechanism directly explains the presence of glycosuria in the patient’s initial presentation without significant hyperglycemia, a feature often used to differentiate MODY from other forms of diabetes. Understanding this mechanism is vital for treatment selection; the reported case discussion highlights that SGLT2 inhibitors are generally avoided in HNF1A-MODY because further inhibition of an already downregulated transporter may induce severe dehydration or diabetic ketoacidosis. This risk underscores the importance of targeting beta-cell function with agents like GLP-1 receptor agonists or sulfonylureas, which is reflected in the patient’s successful long-term control with dulaglutide and metformin.

Differentiating HNF1A-MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes relies heavily on the molecular signature of the disease, which manifests as an absence of autoimmune etiology and minimal insulin resistance markers. Approximately 90% of MODY cases, including HNF1A-MODY, are negative for islet autoantibodies such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A). In this case, the patient’s negative antibody profile, combined with the absence of acanthosis nigricans and a BMI within the normal range (24.6 kg/m²), helped clinicians steer away from the initial misdiagnosis of type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, the lack of significant pre-existing insulin resistance allows for therapeutic strategies that target insulin secretion rather than sensitivity. The genetic confirmation of this phenotype is critical because, unlike T2D, there are no standard biomarkers like C-peptide decline alone that definitively diagnose the condition without genetic evidence or, in historical contexts, sulfonylurea responsiveness testing.

Finally, the clinical severity and pathogenicity of the mutation are often correlated with its specific location within the gene structure. In this patient, the novel mutation p.N270S was located in the DNA-binding domain of the HNF1A protein, a region responsible for the transcription factor’s interaction with target gene promoters. Although the variant was not initially present in the ClinVar or VarSome databases, its location adjacent to known pathogenic residues and its high AlphaMissense score met the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria for a likely pathogenic classification. The segregation of the variant with the family’s diabetic phenotype provided the necessary clinical correlation to override the lack of prior database reporting. This case illustrates that even when a mutation is novel and not yet catalogued, its structural location in a critical domain supports the diagnosis of MODY and justifies the shift from standard polygenic diabetes protocols to mechanism-specific management.

## Precision Medicine: Treatment Decisions and Pharmacological Management

In the initial phase of the case, the patient’s management followed a common clinical pattern for young adults with significant hyperglycemia: an empiric regimen targeting Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2D). Given the elevated HbA1c of 10.2%, the patient was initially treated with basal insulin degludec, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin. This approach reflected a standard of care driven by the severity of glycemic control and the need for rapid reduction of glucose levels, while accounting for the possibility of residual insulin resistance. However, this empiric strategy, while appropriate for a broad type 2 diabetes presentation, masked the underlying monogenic etiology. The patient's response to this regimen was notable; within one month, glycemic control improved significantly to an HbA1c of 9.1%, yet the onset of genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY prompted a strategic pivot in pharmacotherapy. This highlights a critical principle in clinical reasoning: initial monotherapy for severe hyperglycemia in novel phenotypes often prioritizes glycaemic safety over precision, but confirmation of the specific genetic defect allows for the tailoring of therapy to the underlying pathophysiology.

Once the pathogenic heterozygous variant in the HNF1A gene was identified, the treatment paradigm shifted away from insulin-dependent strategies. In classic Type 1 diabetes, insulin is the absolute first-line therapy because of near-total beta-cell destruction. In HNF1A-MODY, the primary defect is impaired beta-cell function and sensitivity rather than absolute insulin deficiency. Consequently, clamping insulin therapy led to the discontinuation while the patient utilized exogenous insulin, favoring agents that stimulate endogenous insulin secretion to preserve residual beta-cell function. The definitive genetic diagnosis allowed for the cessation of insulin degludec, realizing that the patient did not require physiological replacement but rather functional support. This decision was crucial to prevent unnecessary complications associated with insulin therapy in a patient whose beta-cells remain viable but hypersensitive to glucose, validating the importance of molecular diagnosis in preventing iatrogenic insulin dependence.

The subsequent transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, specifically dulaglutide, underscores the nuances of pharmacological choice in MODY. While sulfonylureas are traditionally the first-line agent for HNF1A-MODY by enhancing insulin secretion via potassium-sensitive ATP channels, the discussion identifies GLP-1 receptor agonists as safer alternatives, particularly in patients where hypoglycemia is a concern or where beta-cell preservation is a priority. GLP-1 receptor agonists stimulate insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner, reducing the risk of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas, and have shown efficacy in maintaining glucose control alongside dopaminergic activity modulation. This therapeutic swap did not compromise glycaemic efficacy; the patient's HbA1c stabilized at 6.0% after 6 months of GLP-1 therapy. This case demonstrates that while sulfonylureas are the historical gold standard, newer classes like GLP-1 agonists offer refined therapeutic options that align with modern goals of preserving beta-cell function and minimizing adverse events such as hypoglycemia.

A critical component of precision medicine in MODY is the exclusion of specific drug classes that may exacerbate the underlying renal pathophysiology. Although SGLT2 inhibitors are effective in T2D by lowering blood glucose through urinary excretion, they are generally contraindicated in HNF1A-MODY. The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor that regulates glucose transport in renal tubules. Patients with HNF1A mutations typically exhibit a lowered renal threshold for glucose, causing glycosuria even at lower blood glucose levels. Administering an SGLT2 inhibitor can exacerbate this defect, heightening the risk of dehydration, volume collapse, and euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis, which the case report explicitly warns against. In the absence of SGLT2 inhibitor use in the patient’s regimen, the reliance on Metformin and DPP-4 inhibitors mirrors a strategy of avoiding interventions that could precipitate metabolic instability, ensuring that glucose-lowering is achieved via hepatic metabolic clearance and GIP/GLP-1 pathways rather than renal excretion mechanisms vulnerable to HNF1A mutation effects.

Long-term maintenance therapy for this patient was simplified to a combination of metformin and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin once glycaemic control stabilized. This outcome reflects the standard goal of maintaining HbA1c within target ranges using the least invasive regimen that preserves beta-cell function. The patient achieved an HbA1c of 6.0% and subsequently maintained levels below 6.5% without insulin or GLP-1 receptor agonists, demonstrating the stability of the patient's beta-cell function under metabolic regulation. The feasibility of reducing pharmacotherapy to oral agents only after initial intensive control serves as a practical lesson for long-term management. It emphasizes that while high-risk genetics require immediate and precise attention, the ultimate goal remains the balancing of efficacy, safety, and quality of life. Through the transition from empiric T2D therapy to targeted HNF1A-modifying treatments, this case illustrates how genetic diagnostics are integral to optimizing therapeutic outcomes and preventing unnecessary exposure to drugs that may not suit the specific pathophysiological context of monogenic diabetes.

## Clinical Outcomes, Long-Term Prognosis, and Population Implications

The clinical trajectory of this patient underscores the profound impact that accurate genetic diagnosis can have on glycemic control and therapeutic strategy. Upon initial presentation with an HbA1c of 10.2% and symptoms typically associated with Type 2 diabetes, the patient was treated with insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. Once the novel *HNF1A* mutation was identified via next-generation sequencing (NGS), the treatment paradigm shifted to align with the pathophysiology of monogenic diabetes. Insulin therapy was discontinued, and a GLP-1 receptor agonist was introduced to support glycaemic control and potentially preserve remaining pancreatic function, which eventually contributed to a significant reduction in HbA1c to 6.0% within six months. This outcome illustrates the principle that while clinical criteria often point toward obesity or insulin resistance in Type 2 diabetes, patients with MODY require therapies that target specific beta-cell defects rather than purely improving insulin sensitivity. This precision medicine approach avoids the hypoglycaemia risks associated with sulfonylureas in some contexts and allows for effective management with lower HbA1c targets than would have been expected with a Type 2 diabetes diagnosis alone. Despite this improvement, long-term surveillance remains a critical component of the prognosis. As with all forms of diabetes, strict monitoring for microvascular complications is required. Serial assessments in this case revealed no signs of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, supporting the generally lower risk profile of MODY compared to Type 2 diabetes, particularly given the absence of significant structural complications even after prolonged hyperglycaemia. However, the patient’s Sudomotor testing indicated severe autonomic dysfunction, a reminder that end-organ damage can occur in non-insulin-dependent diabetes and necessitates continued vigilance.

The identification of the liver-specific transcription factor gene mutation, specifically the heterozygous variation p.N270S in exon 4, provides a crucial insight into the limitations of current genetic databases and the utility of high-throughput sequencing. This variant was not catalogued in major public repositories such as ClinVar or VarSome at the time of analysis, highlighting a potential gap in the diagnostic records for monogenic diabetes. However, the pathogenicity of this novel variant was validated through the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. Evidence including a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), segregation of the trait within the family (diabetes occurring across three generations), and the variant's location in the known DNA-binding domain of the HNF1A gene met the criteria for a likely pathogenic classification. This case demonstrates that a negative search in public variant databases does not rule out a pathogenic mutation, particularly in diverse populations where many rare alleles may remain unreported. It reinforces that molecular genetic testing should not be restricted solely by the presence of a known mutation in a database but rather by clinical suspicion and ACMG-based interpretation of novel findings.

From a broader epidemiological perspective, this case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by diverse ethnic populations, specifically the underrepresentation of Asian and non-White groups in historical MODY research which has primarily focused on Caucasian cohorts. The patient's normal BMI (24.6 kg/m²) and lack of acanthosis nigricans were consistent with typical MODY presentations but contrast with the high cardiovascular and metabolic syndrome risks often seen in Type 2 diabetes, yet the high BMI observed in some Asian MODY cases is increasingly recognized due to the rising prevalence of Type 2 diabetes antigens in the region. Clinical tools such as the Exeter probability calculator, while useful, have been observed to overestimate MODY probability in non-European populations. Consequently, clinicians must exercise a high index of suspicion regarding family histories of early-onset diabetes regardless of body habitus or ethnic background. Looking forward, the increasing accessibility of NGS panels offers a scalable strategy to improve the diagnostic yield of monogenic diabetes, moving from phenotype-based diagnosis to genotype-directed therapy. Future research must focus on optimizing cost-effective screening algorithms and expanding variant databases to ensure that emerging genetic insights translate into equitable care for all demographic groups, ultimately reducing misdiagnosis and improving patient outcomes globally.
