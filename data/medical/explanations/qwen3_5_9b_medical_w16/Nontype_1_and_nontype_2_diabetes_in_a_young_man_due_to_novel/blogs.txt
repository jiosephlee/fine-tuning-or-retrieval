# The 'Type 2' Misdiagnosis: 5 Clinical Clues That Suggest MODY

## The "Classic" Presentation That Wasn't

If you are used to the fast-paced EM rotation or the high-stakes ward rounds of endocrinology, you know the drill. Young patient, polyuria, polydipsia, high HbA1c—most physicians slap "Type 2 Diabetes" (T2D) on it. Lipids get checked, metformin gets prescribed, and life moves on. Rarely do we pause to ask why a 24-year-old man with a BMI of 24.6 requires that much insulin to start off.

This brings us to a fascinating case report by Liu et al., which highlights the diagnostic pitfalls of Monkey Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). Specifically, HNF1A-MODY. This patient didn't fit the "older, heavier diabetic" profile. Instead, he presented with textbook MODY features that were overlooked until genetic sequencing was performed.

The surprise here isn't just that he had MODY. The surprise is that he was initially treated as T2D with insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. That regimen required a shift. Once the HNF1A mutation (p.N270S) was identified, the team could strip away the unnecessary insulin and switch to a GLP-1 receptor agonist. The result? HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0% within months. That is a dramatic response to etiology-altered therapy.

## 5 Clues to Watch For in the Young Diabetic

While MODY accounts for only 1%-6% of young-onset diabetes cases, misdiagnosing the phenotype as T2D leads to unnecessary insulin therapy and missed complications in relatives. This patient provides a checklist for clinicians who are seeing hyperglycemia in patients who don't look "diabetic" by traditional T2D standards.

1.  **Normal BMI vs. Hyperglycemic Burden**
    When a patient presents with significant hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%) and weight loss, you expect insulin resistance. However, this patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m². In T2D, we often see a correlation between insulin resistance and weight. Here, the severe metabolic derangement existed despite a normal body weight, suggesting a primary defect in beta-cell function rather than resistance.

2.  **Absence of Acanthosis Nigricans**
    We look for cutaneous markers of insulin resistance. Acanthosis nigricans is a common sign in T2D and often prompt consideration of insulin resistance syndromes. This patient had no acanthosis nigricans on physical examination, further arguing against the classic T2D phenotype and pointing toward an autoimmune or monogenic etiology.

3.  **The Glycosuria Threshold**
    Glycosuria often implies a very high glucose threshold for reabsorption. In T2D, this usually happens late. In this case, glycosuria was a presenting symptom during an annual health check. HNF1A transcription factors are responsible for regulating SGLT2. Consequently, HNF1A-MODY patients have a reduced renal threshold for glucose, leading to glycosuria at lower glucose levels than typical T2D.

4.  **Strong Family Pattern**
    Family history is the strongest predictor, provided you look beyond the nuclear family. This patient reported diagnoses in his maternal grandmother (30s), mother (late 20s), and younger brother (age 20). While T2D clusters, this specific dominance across three generations with *early* onset is the hallmark of MODY.

5.  **Negative Autoantibodies and Onset Age**
    Since this patient had no diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) at presentation and negative autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A), Type 1 was off the table. T2D is unlikely because of the functionality on the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L).

## Precision Medicine in Action

The clinical lesson extends beyond diagnosis to the treatment response. Because this patient is HNF1A-MODY, he is sensitive to sulfonylureas and GLP-1 agonists over insulin and other agents. Initially, the team prescribed a basal insulin regimen. But once the MODY suspected by the altered C-peptide and family history was confirmed genetically, insulin was discontinued.

The patient transitioned smoothly to a combination of dulaglutide (GLP-1 RAg) and metformin. The metabolic response was so robust that long-term maintenance returned to metformin paired with a DPP-4 inhibitor, stabilizing below 6.5% without further complications.

This underscores a critical point in genetic medicine: Genotype often informs phenotype. Don't confuse the obesity of the present patient with the diabetes of the past. If a young patient has a high HbA1c but looks "too normal," look for the secret genetic mutation.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **MODY accounts for 1–6% of young-onset diabetes**, making genetic screening crucial when clinical presentation doesn't fit T1D or classic T2D.
*   **Be suspicious of HNF1A-MODY** if there is a strong autosomal dominant family history of early-onset diabetes.
*   **Normal BMI and absence of acanthosis nigricans** should not preclude a diagnosis of diabetes, especially in the presence of significant hyperglycemia.
*   **Glycosuria at presentation** may indicate a low renal threshold for glucose, a specific pathophysiology often seen in HNF1A-MODY.
*   **Treatment response matters:** If a patient doesn't respond well to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 agonists, or if you are using insulin in a young non-obese patient with negative antibodies, consider genetic testing.
*   **Sensitivity to Drugs:** In HNF1A-MODY, sulfonylureas and GLP-1 agonists are often superior to insulin-based regimens, as seen in this patient’s dramatic A1c reduction.

## Bottom Line

The case of this young man with HNF1A-MODY serves as a reminder that not all young diabetes is autoimmune and not all diabetes is insulin-resistant. When the clinical picture suggests a molecular glitch rather than a lifestyle disease, checking your mutation list could save a patient unnecessary insulin and reveal a treatable genetic opportunity.

**Stay curious.** Look at the family, look at the BMI, and ask why the lab values don't match the chart. That is where the diagnosis of MODY hides.



## Database Blind Spots: How to Grade Novel MODY Variants Without ClinVar

If you spend a lot of time in the endocrine clinic or the EM gene desk, you know the frustration of the "negative" variant report. You get an NGS panel back that screams "pathogenic," but the database looks back and says, "Nope, we've never seen that one before." In the world of monogenic diabetes, specifically Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), this blue-sky scenario can be the difference between aggressive insulin coverage and a simple sulfonylurea regimen. We recently reviewed a case report from *Liu et al.* that tackles a pretty scary error source: the silence of public registries. When you have a novel *HNF1A* mutation like p.N270S that isn't in ClinVar, VarSome, or the literature, how do you decide if it actually causes disease without relying on a database catch-up? The answer lies in an intersection of ACMG guidelines and modern AI prediction tools.

### The Clinical Dilemma: A "Boring" Case with a Twist

Imagine a 25-year-old male who walks in with an HbA1c of 10.2% and a BMI of 24.6 kg/m². No trouble with obesity, no Acanthosis nigricans, but there is glycosuria. You run the standard diabetes workup: autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A, ZnT8) are negative. He has a glucagon stimulation test showing a C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, suggesting he still has some insulin to give, just not enough. Then you add the family history: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother with diabetes at 20. This screams autosomal dominant polygenic or monogenic diabetes to nearly any seasoned practitioner.

Usually, the diagnosis boils down to T1D (unlikely given the C-peptide and antibody status) or T2D (unlikely given the age and lack of severe insulin resistance). It's the textbook definition of the MODY diagnostic dilemma. In this case, the patient was initially treated for T2D with a basal insulin regimen, metformin, and saxagliptin. However, upon closer clinical review, with the suspicion of a monogenic etiology, they moved to genetic testing.

### The Database Silence

Here is where it gets interesting for the translational medicine crowd. The genetic testing revealed a heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G). While *HNF1A* is the workhorse of MODY cases (accounting for 30%-65% of MODY generally), this specific variant was a ghost. It was absent from the ClinVar database, the VarSome database, and did not exist in the literature at the time of publication. In most error-management systems, a simple "VUS" or "Unknown" tag might get the patient stuck on insulin therapy for years.

However, the authors took the "Novel Line" approach rather than the "Database Blind Spot" approach. They applied the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation but had to rely heavily on structural biology and computational prediction because there were no prior natural cases in the global pool yet. The mutation wasn't benign because of where it landed on the protein. p.N270S sits in exon 4, squarely within the DNA-binding domain. Mutations in this area are historically associated with pathogenic loss of function.

To bolster the classification beyond just structural location, they utilized AI tools. Specifically, they cited a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995. While bioinformatics tools have false positives, a score that high when combined with segregation analysis is compelling evidence. The family segregation was a given—the disease tracked through three generations. If this variant didn't cause diabetes, the family history of early-onset diabetes in three relatives across different generations would have been a massive coincidence. The combination of a high-computational probability score, a domain location known to be critical for function, and clear pedigree segregation allowed them to classify this as Likely Pathogenic (LP) despite the lack of "crowdsourced" data in ClinVar.

### Why Treating the Mutation Matters

This case isn't just a genetics lesson; it's a metabolic rescue mission. The patient initially received basal insulin for rapid control, a strategy often used in empiric T1D or T2D trials. Once the genetic diagnosis was confirmed, the treatment paradigm shifted drastically. Insulin was discontinued and replaced with a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide), followed later by the maintenance of metformin and saxagliptin.

Why? Because *HNF1A* mutation leads to a defect in the glucose transporter function and insulin secretion dynamics that are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas. Sulfonylureas close the K-ATP channels to stimulate insulin, which is precisely the mechanism benefiting *HNF1A*-MODY. GLP-1s are an excellent alternative here, avoiding the hypoglycemia risk often seen with insulin therapy in monogenic diabetes where the goal is to preserve beta-cell function rather than suppress hyperglycemia.

In this patient, the HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0% over six months without insulin. If the clinicians had stuck to the T2D algorithm without genetic confirmation, that patient would be living on insulin injections indefinitely, risking hypoglycemic episodes without biological justification. As the authors highlight, the MODY probability calculator can be an aid, though we must be careful—it's been derived from white European populations and might underestimate MODY in non-White groups or populations with higher comorbidities. This case adds a layer of confirmation: even when the calculator says 62%, the molecular evidence (when interpreted correctly) seals the deal.

### Take-Home Messages

*   **Clinical suspicion trumps negative databases:** When a patient fits the MODY phenotype (young onset, strong family history, negative autoantibodies), a novel variant in a known MODY gene should be treated as pathogenic if supported by segregation and protein location, even if ClinVar is silent.
*   **Use the data you have:** In the absence of registry data, integrate AlphaMissense scores and exon localization (specifically the DNA-binding domain for *HNF1A*) into the ACMG classification assessment.
*   **Precision medicine dictates therapy:** A confirmed or highly suspected *HNF1A* mutation warrants a trial of sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists rather than insulin, especially in the absence of beta-cell depletion, to avoid hypoglycemia and preserve endogenous secretion.
*   **Family history is a biomarker:** Three generations of early-onset diabetes are more statistically significant than any single-gene database entry for monogenic diabetes evaluation.



# The Exeter Calculator Dilemma: When Probability Tools Underestimate Risk

# So, a 24-year-old man walks into your clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2% and a family tree that looks like a diabetes ring of fire, yet the metabolic panel screams at you for insulin resistance or Type 1. You start insulin and metformin—standard play for "mystery." But one month later, genetics are positive for a novel *HNF1A* variant. You switch him off insulin immediately.

Why? Because this isn't random. This is *HNF1A*-MODY.

The case report by Liu et al. [1] serves as a reminder that we are still chasing ghosts when we assume Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) and Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) are mutually exclusive or that a probability calculator has the final say. Specifically, the reliance on Western datasets to diagnose a condition that skews differently in geographic and ethnic populations remains a critical, often dangerous, pitfall.

## The Clinical Misstep

Let’s look at the patient. He presented with hyperglycemia and glycosuria during a routine annual exam. No diabetic ketoacidosis. He looked thin—BMI of 24.6 kg/m²—but with metabolic syndrome markers (high triglycerides, low HDL) and a compelling family history.

*   **Grandmother:** Diagnosed in 30s.
*   **Mother:** Diagnosed in late 20s.
*   **Brother:** Diagnosed at age 20.

Negative for GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8 autoantibodies. Glucagon stimulation showed C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (6 min), indicating *insufficient* but *preserved* insulin secretion. Based on the initial presentation, he was treated as Type 2 with T1D overlap: Insulin Degludec, Metformin, and Saxagliptin.

HbA1c went down slightly to 9.1% after one month. But then the genetic sequencing happened. They found a heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (*HNF1A:c.809A>G*), which was novel and not previously in ClinVar or VarSome.

The intervention was dramatic. Upon confirmation of *HNF1A*-MODY: **Insulin therapy was discontinued.** The patient was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (Dulaglutide), eventually settling on Metformin and Saxagliptin. HbA1c plummeted to 6.0% within 6 months.

## The Calculator Question

Here is where the modern diagnostic dilemma hits. The text notes that the MODY probability calculator developed by the Exeter group was used for this patient.

When the clinical info was entered, the tool estimated a **62.4% probability** (1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY.

But wait. The discussion explicitly flags a massive caveat: "This tool was derived from data in a White European population and may overestimate the probability of MODY in non-White groups."

In case reports like this, where the authors are from an Asian background and the text discusses prevalence in "young Chinese adults," the limitation of Western-derived algorithms becomes a safety hazard. If the tool underestimates prevalence due to lack of modern genomic data in specific cohorts, we miss diagnoses. If it overestimates (as the text warns in this specific context), we risk unnecessary genetic testing or false reassurance.

The real risk isn't just that the calculator said a percentage. The risk is that clinicians might use this percentage as a *gatekeeper* for genetic testing. If the calculator says 30% (average) or lower in a non-European patient, some might stop digging.

We need to ask ourselves: How do we triage when the pedigree screams MODY but the calculator wags its tail?

The text highlights that in the population-based study from Russia, 46.4% of patients suspected of having hereditary diabetes without autoimmune features carried pathogenic variants (with *HNF1A* as one of the common genes). That is a much higher yield than clinicians intuitively guess. If the waiting room has a family history of diabetes in the blood relatives (mother + brother <25), the pre-test probability is often higher than the calculator score suggests when applied blindly to a general population.

## Precision Medicine and Treatment Implications

Why does the diagnosis matter? Because the treatment is radically different.

*   **Type 2/Typical Fear:** Insulin or Sulfonylureas.
*   **HNF1A-MODY Reality:** First-line is Sulfonylureas (Sulfonylureas effectively block the K_ATP channel defect) or high-dose GLP-1 agonists.

Using insulin in *HNF1A*-MODY is often futile because the issue isn't pancreatic autoimmunity or beta-cell *destruction* (as seen in T1D), but rather transcription factor failure reducing beta-cell sensitivity. In this case, the patient was initially put on insulin, which is expensive and high-burden. When genetics were confirmed, insulin was stopped. The patient stabilized on oral agents and GLP-1 agonists.

Additionally, the patient had severe sudomotor dysfunction (reduced conductance in hands). This is consistent with autonomic neuropathy often seen in diabetic dysautonomia, which can happen early in MODY due to the specific *HNF1A* defect affecting renal SGLT2 and nerve function.

## Key Take-Homes

*   **Don't let the calculator be the final boss.** Use the Exeter calculator as a heuristic, not a rule. A family history of diabetes in the first, second, or third generation below age 25 is a brighter signal than an estimated 60% probability derived from a White European cohort.
*   **Treat for Preservation.** For *HNF1A*-MODY, avoid high-dose insulin or unnecessary beta-cell suppression. GLP-1 agonists and metformin are often superior to insulin for maintaining beta-cell function in this specific monogenic context.
*   **Novel Mutations Matter.** This case identified a novel variant not in existing databases. Don't be turned off by "VUS" (Variant of Uncertain Significance) in people with strong phenotypic evidence. ACMG criteria, biochemical segregation, and functional scores (like AlphaMissense) support pathogenicity even with a "new" mutation.
*   **Genetic Screening is Better Than Guessing.** The text emphasizes that DNA sequencing is essential to confirm MODY clinical suspicions. With the shift to NGS, screening young patients for *HNF1A* variants is becoming more feasible and cost-effective.

## References:

1.  Liu Y-C, et al. Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A. *BMC Endocr Disord. 2024* (Derived from provided text).

## Critical Commentary

The takeaway from the Liu et al. report isn't just the *HNF1A* mutation; it's the workflow disruption caused by the discovery. In a patient initially treated for T2D, the insulin was stopped safely because the *HNF1A* diagnosis allowed for better metabolic management without the burden of injections.

However, the discussion highlights the tension in genetic medicine: When tools are biased, how do we balance safety? In this patient, the tool scored 62%, passing him through. But if a patient scored 5% due to "ethnic mismatch" in the algorithm, we'd be in a different case. The message is clear: Clinical suspicion in the context of early-onset diabetes and autosomal dominant family history should compel NGS panels regardless of what a non-localized calculator suggests.

Precision medicine helps when we have the genes; it hurts when we use the wrong maps to find them.

# Drug-Target Re-Alignment: Why Switching from Insulin to GLP-1 Matters

In the chaotic world of early-onset diabetes, the reflex to plunge a struggling patient into insulin can be automatic, especially when the HbA1c spikes above 9%. It signals control. But not everyone who looks like Type 2 has Type 2, and not everyone who looks like Type 1 has Type 1. Sometimes, the patient is a monogenic zebra wearing a stealth suit, and the incorrect drug target doesn't just fail—it actively highlights the underlying physiology that was ignored. This isn't about dosing; it's about mechanism.

We recently encountered a case where a young adult presented with a classic, high-grade metabolic profile including polydipsia, weight loss, and a 10.2% HbA1c. It was swiftly categorized as Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) given the relatively preserved physical exam (BMI 24.6) and the lack of obvious Type 1 features. The standard battery was maligned: metformin, saxagliptin, and insulin degludec. While the insulin bought initial control, the HbA1c dropped.

*10.2% to 9.1%.*

It's a start. It's a gamble. But it wasn't the *right* answer. Once genetic testing finally confirmed a novel, pathogenic HNF1A mutation, the treatment was purged. The insulin was axed, replaced by dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist). The result? Six months later, the HbA1c sat at 6.0%. This dramatic pivot isn't just about efficacy; it is a physiological necessity determined by the molecular architecture of beta-cell disease.

## The Trap of Exogenous Insulin in Monogenic Disease

When we treat a poorly controlled patient, we frequently default to exogenous insulin because it has the most potent glucose-lowering capacity. It is the "widows' peak" of therapeutic escalation—it works when nothing else can. However, in HNF1A-MODY (what we now call MODY3), the problem isn't usually an absolute lack of insulin secretion; the problem is the signaling pathway required to stimulate that secretion.

Think of the beta cell in HNF1A-MODY as a tarnished engine. It wants to run, but the key (the transcriptional regulation via HNF1A) is stuck. Insulin therapy is like shouting at the car from the window. It pushes the car forward physically, but it doesn't fix the engine. Over time, exogenous insulin can mask the underlying beta-cell decline and might even exacerbate the progression of some monogenic forms by inducing counter-regulatory fatigue or weight gain without solving the genetic defect.

In contrast, a GLP-1 receptor agonist or a sulfonylurea acts from the inside out. Sulfonylureas target the same channel (ATP-sensitive K+ channel) that HNF1A is supposed to manage. They trick the beta cell into opening the gate to release its own insulin, while GLP-1 receptors amplify the signal. In this specific case, the initial insulin regimen pushed glycemia down from 88 mmol/mol, but it didn't address the transcriptional bottleneck. Switching to a GLP-1 RA allowed for glucose control while sparing the remaining beta-cell function, preventing the "burnout" trajectory often seen when we treat MODY as T2D.

## The Precision Medicine Pivot

This case highlights a critical emerging frontier: the utility of next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels in endocrinology clinics. The patient’s family history—a maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her 20s, and brother at 20—should have raised a red flag. In the past, perhaps we would have waited for the patient to fail sulfonylureas. However, the rise in obesity prevalence has blurred the lines, and many MODY patients now present with a BMI and metabolic profile that overlaps heavily with established T2D.

The clinician in this scenario must be a functional detective.
*   **Ameliorate suspicion:** Early-onset family history often precedes the diagnosis.
*   **Rule out acceleration:** Standard T1D autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) are negative in MODY, but so do they in T2D. They lack specificity in the early stages.
*   **The Modality:** Glucagon stimulation testing in this patient showed preserved (though insufficient) insulin secretion, which argues against T1D and points toward a secretagogue-responsive state.

It is also vital to remember that HNF1A-MODY presents with elevated glycosuria. Why? Because HNF1A also regulates SGLT2 in the kidney. This shared mechanism is why SGLT2 inhibitors are risky here—they can push the dehydration and ketoacidosis threshold too low for a beta cell already on the verge of complete failure. The choice was made carefully: GLP-1 or Sulfonylurea, not SGLT1 or SGLT2.

## Clinical Implications

Ultimately, this case is a reminder that early, aggressive insulinization for a young adult with HNF1A-MODY is a protocol that treats the blood sugar, but it ignores the patient. Accurate diagnosis isn't just academic; it dictates the drug class. Misdiagnosing MODY 3 as T2DM and treating with insulin exposes the patient to hypoglycemia risk and sectioning the beta-cell reserve further. Getting the diagnosis right means getting the drug right.

In this instance, the switch to a GLP-1 agonist was not merely "better"; it was the physiologically appropriate target for a transcriptional defect.

### Key Take-Home Messages

*   **MODY 3 inheritance and family history** across three generations are stronger predictors than BMI in early-onset diabetes.
*   **Exogenous insulin should be avoided** if MODY is suspected; sulfonylureas and GLP-1 receptor agonists are the targeted therapies that spare beta-cell function.
*   **SGLT2 inhibitors carry risks** for HNF1A-MODY patients due to increased baseline glycosuria and altered renal function.
*   **Genetic testing should be considered earlier** in young adults with early-onset diabetes and negative autoimmune markers.
*   **GLP-1 RAs are safer alternatives** to insulin for glycemic control and preservation of function compared to long-acting insulin regimens.



# The SGLT2 Inhibitor Warning: Hidden DKA Risks in Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor Mutants

We look at a young patient with early-onset hyperglycemia, negative autoantibodies, and a family tree full of diabetics, and we often tune out the clinical clues. We see an HbA1c of 10% and think Type 2. We don't always pause to think about Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). And when it is MODY, specifically the HNF1A subtype, we push forward with standard二类 treatments until we discover a genetic trap that could lead to life-threatening metabolic collapse.

Today, we are breaking down a specific safety signal hidden in the pathophysiology of hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha mutations. It reinforces a vital principle in modern critical care and endocrine medicine: Genotyping isn't just for academic rigor; sometimes it's the difference between a prescription that works and a diagnosis that puts patients in the ICU.

## Understanding the Genetic Glitch

To understand why SGLT2 inhibitors are a contraindication here, we have to look at the gene itself. HNF1A is a transcription factor located on chromosome 12. It expresses in critical tissues: the liver, kidneys, and pancreatic beta-cells. Its job is regulatory. It keeps things running smoothly across metabolic systems, telling the liver how to handle glucose, the kidney how to reabsorb glucose, and the pancreas how to secrete insulin.

In HNF1A-MODY, we have a mutation that disrupts this transcription. The clinical picture? It's autosomal dominant. You see the phenotype in multiple generations, often diagnosed before age 25. The classic physical presentation is negative for acanthosis nigricans, negative for autoimmune markers, and crucially, **significant glycosuria**. But why?

The answer lies in the kidney. Glucose reabsorption in the renal tubules is mediated by sodium-glucose cotransporters 2 (SGLT2). Expression of SGLT2 is under the transcriptional control of HNF1A.

When you have a mutation in the transcription factor (HNF1A), you inherently downregulate the expression of SGLT2. The patient already has a phenotypic state where they excrete glucose into the urine at lower serum thresholds because their renal threshold is genetically lowered. We see glycosuria often out of proportion to the actual blood glucose levels for a Type 2 or healthy Type 1 patient.

Here is where the danger setup occurs. If you take a patient with HNF1A-MODY and target the SGLT2 pathway in the medication regimen, you are essentially trying to block a door that is already half open.

## A Case Study in Diagnostic Dizziness

This brings us to the case report from the authors. The patient arrived at a referral clinic for an annual exam with 10.2% HbA1c and significant glycosuria. His BMI was 24.6 kg/m², negating the obesity often seen in type 2 diabetes.

By the clinical presentation:
*   **Family History:** Maternal grandmother diagnosed in 30s, mother late 20s, brother at age 20.
*   **Lab Work:** Positive glycosuria despite initial confusion. Negative GADA, IA-2A.
*   **Genetics:** Identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S).

The patient was initially treated as if this were a standard Type 2 diabetes case. Why? Because he had a HbA1c of 10% and some insulin resistance elements on metabolic screening. The treatment started with insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin. The expectation was to manage the hyperglycemia aggressively.

However, the MODY diagnosis re-opens the board. The treatment plan was altered. Because his residual beta-cell function was preserved enough to maintain on sulfonylureas or oral agents, and because of the genetic confirmation, insulin was discontinued. The team pivoted to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) which proved highly effective, dropping the HbA1c to 6.0% within a few months.

## The Red Flag: Why SGLT2 Inhibitors Are a No-Go

This is the crux of the clinical pearl. The authors explicitly warn that the risks of using SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY may outweigh the benefits.

Consider the mechanism again. These patients already have compromised SGLT2 activity due to the genetic defect. Introducing a pharmacological SGLT2 inhibitor creates a "double hit." The authors cite reports indicating patients may experience severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) due to high inhibition of this residual SGLT2 activity.

In standard Type 2 diabetes, an SGLT2 inhibitor increases urinary glucose excretion to ~50g/day, inducing a positive caloric deficit and mild weight loss. In HNF1A-MODY, the pathophysiology is different. The patient's kidney is already struggling to reabsorb glucose, but the HNF1A defect also compromises the specific handling of glucose transport proteins.

When you actively inhibit the *residual* pump function with an SGLT2 inhibitor, you exacerbate the osmotic diuresis. This leads to profound volume depletion. Furthermore, if caloric intake is high and the patient loses significant glucose/energy via urine, ketosis can be triggered.

The clinical implication is stark. If you see a patient with early-onset diabetes, negative antibodies, and **frequent or disproportionate glycosuria**, do not reach for a fixed-dose SGLT2 inhibitor without ruling out monogenic causes.

### Clinical Pearls

The distinction between "Monogenic vs. Polygenetic" isn't just academic vocabulary; it dictates your safety profile for pharmacotherapy.
*   **HNF1A-MODY often presents with propensity for glycosuria** comparable to SGLT2 inhibitor therapy, but this is genetic, not pharmacologic.
*   **Adding an inhibitor to a gene-defect phenotype** can precipitate euglycemic DKA or severe dehydration because the transport mechanism is already compromised.
*   **Consider the Exeter Probability Calculator** first, but be aware that it is Euro-centric; genetics screening is superior.
*   **Treatment Choice:** While sulfonylureas were first-line historically, GLP-1s and DPP-4 inhibitors in specific metabolic contexts (like this case) can be highly effective and safer.
*   **Genetic Screening:** In young patients with a strong family history of early-onset diabetes, do not accept T2D diagnosis without a second look.

### Take-Home Points

*   Suspicion of MODY should arise in patients with HbA1c >7% who carry a strong family history of diabetes and negative diabetic autoantibodies.
*   A novel mutation of the HNF1A gene can result in naturally lowered renal glucose thresholds and significant glycosuria.
*   SGLT2 inhibitors are generally contraindicated in HNF1A-MODY due to the risk of precipitating severe dehydration, ketoacidosis, and vascular issues.
*   Precise genetic diagnosis allows for targeted therapy, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists, which can stabilize glycemic control and improve quality of life in these specific cohorts.
*   Education is key: Early identification prevents the misuse of standard polygenic diabetes treatments on monogenic phenotypes.



# Pedigree Policing: Using Family History to Justify Genetic Sequencing

We’ve all seen the 25-year-old presenting with polyuria and polydipsia. The white blood count is normal. The HbA1c is shooting past 80 mmol/mol. The reflex is reflexive: check for ketones, check for C-peptide, check for antibodies, start on metformin if insulin resistance looks high or insulin if beta-cell failure looks low. But in the era of monogenic diabetes, that reflex is increasingly outdated. Sometimes the most reliable diagnostic tool isn't a scan or a lab value, but a needle point sketch of a family tree.

This case report highlights a young man who was initially misdiagnosed with type 2 diabetes (T2D) before the correct diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY (Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young) emerged. The pivot point wasn't a new blood test but a conversation about his maternal grandmother, mother, and brother. While clinical clues like a normal BMI and glycosuria in the absence of a known obesity syndrome are hints, the distinct vertical transmission of disease across three generations provides a compelling, actionable argument for genetic sequencing.

The patient was referred during routine health screening, completely asymptomatic aside from the lab abnormalities. His physical exam was unremarkable for the metabolic syndrome features often associated with young-onset diabetes; no acanthosis nigricans, and a BMI of 24.6 kg/m². When you see a BMI near normal, you step back from the "T2D" assumption, but it doesn't always mean "MODY." In this instance, the lab work revealed a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) value nearly 8 mmol/L above the threshold and a Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c) of 10.2%. He had no history of diabetic ketoacidosis and no cyanotic blue marble streak at the heels.

This is the diagnostic no man's land. It's not T1D because the autoantibodies—GADA, IA-2A, ZnT8—all came back negative. It's not classic T2D because he lacks the phenotypic drivers like severe obesity or acanthosis. The family history, however, screamed "dominant inheritance." There is a clear lineage: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at 20. When you see three generations affected by early-onset diabetes, the probability of an autosomal dominant monogenic form rises significantly. It is this pedigree that serves as the strongest justification to bypass standard empiric therapy and invest in Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS).

The initial treatment for this patient was a pragmatic gamble common in physiology, treating for T2D with a basal insulin, metformin, and a DP-4 inhibitor. After a month, the HbA1c dropped to 9.1%, which is responsive to insulin. However, without genetic confirmation, the insulin was likely a crutch. Once the genetic testing was performed, the variant c.809A>G in the HNF1A gene was identified. This confirmed HNF1A-MODY. The treatment course was then reframed. They stepped off insulin entirely, recognizing that sulfonylureas or GLP-1 agonists are the gold standard for preserving beta-cell function in this specific monogenic defect. The patient switched to dulaglutide and stared looking at the HbA1c decline from 10.2% down to 6.0% in just six months. The insulin was discontinued, simplifying the regimen and avoiding the complications and medication costs associated with complexity.

It is important to note the nuances here. The patient had some autonomic neuropathy (reduced sudomotor function) and had a stimulated C-peptide that was insufficient but preserved. The Glucagon stimulation test supports the diagnosis but isn't definitive. There is also the issue of the Exeter MODY probability calculator, which is derived largely from European populations. While it suggested a 62.4% probability of MODY in this case, relying on a tool based on Caucasian genetics when assessing patients of Eastern Asian descent outside of that training set requires clinical judgment. The vertical transmission pattern outweighs the calculator's potential demographic overestimation in this context. Furthermore, the specific mutation found was novel, absent from the ClinVar and VarSome databases. This underscores the risk of stopping genetic sequencing once you hit "No variant found in database"; the variant is pathogenic because it segregates in the family, not just because it is in a known registry.

We must also consider the safety of other medications. Because Hnf1a impacts the SGLT2 transporter (and thus renal threshold for glucose), SGLT2 inhibitors can precipitate dehydration and euglycemic DKA in these patients, even though the reservation of Glucagon/C-peptide suggests preserved function. This case confirms that GLP-1 agonists are a robust alternative to the standard sulfonylurea first-line in HNF1A-MODY, especially if the patient is prone to hypoglycemia or has those metabolic complications. The take-home here is that the wrong drug can work safely enough to bridge the gap, but the genetic diagnosis allows you to remove the insurance and switch to the specific therapy that targets the pathophysiology rather than managing the symptom.

In the end, this case isn't just about a mutation we don't know yet. It's about how you use the narrative of the family against the narrative of the glucose curve. If you can capture the history of maternal grandmother, mother, and sibling onset, you have a smoking gun. Justify the cost of the NGS panel with the pedigree you collect. Avoid the pitfalls of treating a MODY patient as T1D (lifelong insulin, hypersensitivity, higher costs) when they could be switched to a sulfonylurea or GLP-1. Avoid treating it as T2D when beta-function is actually monogenically impaired and insulin-independent therapies are more logical.

The literature on Asian populations is catching up, but the numbers are still skewed. A large study found 1.5% EOAD (New Onset Diabetes in Asian Young Adults) was MODY. If you miss these, you miss a lot of patients. When the family history is a straight vertical line, and the antibodies are zeroes, the likelihood ratio is shifted enough to mandate the genetic test. Don't be so quick to assume the family history is the same for you versus the mother.

[###  Take-Home Messages]

*   **Vertical transmission is the trigger:** A history of diabetes in a maternal grandmother, mother, and sibling (autosomal dominant pattern) creates a high likelihood ratio for MODY, justifying priority genetic sequencing.
*   **Negative antibodies are a pivot point:** Negative GADA, IA-2A, and Zinc-8 antibodies effectively rule out T1D, leaving a "MODY or T2D" differential where vertical transmission favors the former.
*   **Treatment dictates the test:** Initial empiric therapy for T2D (insulin) may not be ideal. Confirming HNF1A-MODY often allows for the discontinuation of insulin in favor of sulfonylureas or GLP-1 agonists, specifically avoiding SGLT2 inhibitors due to DKA risk.
*   **Novel variants matter:** A lack of a variant in ClinVar does not negate pathogenicity if the variant segregates with the disease in a large, multi-generational pedigree and scores high on AlphaMissense.
*   **BMI is a distractor:** Normal BMI does not exclude MODY; metabolic syndrome and acanthosis nigricans can be absent, leading to misdiagnosis as T2D in younger cohorts.
