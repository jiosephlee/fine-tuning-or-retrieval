Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The Exeter Calculator Dilemma: When Probability Tools Underestimate Risk

# So, a 24-year-old man walks into your clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2% and a family tree that looks like a diabetes ring of fire, yet the metabolic panel screams at you for insulin resistance or Type 1. You start insulin and metformin—standard play for "mystery." But one month later, genetics are positive for a novel *HNF1A* variant. You switch him off insulin immediately.

Why? Because this isn't random. This is *HNF1A*-MODY.

The case report by Liu et al. [1] serves as a reminder that we are still chasing ghosts when we assume Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) and Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) are mutually exclusive or that a probability calculator has the final say. Specifically, the reliance on Western datasets to diagnose a condition that skews differently in geographic and ethnic populations remains a critical, often dangerous, pitfall.

## The Clinical Misstep

Let’s look at the patient. He presented with hyperglycemia and glycosuria during a routine annual exam. No diabetic ketoacidosis. He looked thin—BMI of 24.6 kg/m²—but with metabolic syndrome markers (high triglycerides, low HDL) and a compelling family history.

*   **Grandmother:** Diagnosed in 30s.
*   **Mother:** Diagnosed in late 20s.
*   **Brother:** Diagnosed at age 20.

Negative for GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8 autoantibodies. Glucagon stimulation showed C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (6 min), indicating *insufficient* but *preserved* insulin secretion. Based on the initial presentation, he was treated as Type 2 with T1D overlap: Insulin Degludec, Metformin, and Saxagliptin.

HbA1c went down slightly to 9.1% after one month. But then the genetic sequencing happened. They found a heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (*HNF1A:c.809A>G*), which was novel and not previously in ClinVar or VarSome.

The intervention was dramatic. Upon confirmation of *HNF1A*-MODY: **Insulin therapy was discontinued.** The patient was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (Dulaglutide), eventually settling on Metformin and Saxagliptin. HbA1c plummeted to 6.0% within 6 months.

## The Calculator Question

Here is where the modern diagnostic dilemma hits. The text notes that the MODY probability calculator developed by the Exeter group was used for this patient.

When the clinical info was entered, the tool estimated a **62.4% probability** (1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY.

But wait. The discussion explicitly flags a massive caveat: "This tool was derived from data in a White European population and may overestimate the probability of MODY in non-White groups."

In case reports like this, where the authors are from an Asian background and the text discusses prevalence in "young Chinese adults," the limitation of Western-derived algorithms becomes a safety hazard. If the tool underestimates prevalence due to lack of modern genomic data in specific cohorts, we miss diagnoses. If it overestimates (as the text warns in this specific context), we risk unnecessary genetic testing or false reassurance.

The real risk isn't just that the calculator said a percentage. The risk is that clinicians might use this percentage as a *gatekeeper* for genetic testing. If the calculator says 30% (average) or lower in a non-European patient, some might stop digging.

We need to ask ourselves: How do we triage when the pedigree screams MODY but the calculator wags its tail?

The text highlights that in the population-based study from Russia, 46.4% of patients suspected of having hereditary diabetes without autoimmune features carried pathogenic variants (with *HNF1A* as one of the common genes). That is a much higher yield than clinicians intuitively guess. If the waiting room has a family history of diabetes in the blood relatives (mother + brother <25), the pre-test probability is often higher than the calculator score suggests when applied blindly to a general population.

## Precision Medicine and Treatment Implications

Why does the diagnosis matter? Because the treatment is radically different.

*   **Type 2/Typical Fear:** Insulin or Sulfonylureas.
*   **HNF1A-MODY Reality:** First-line is Sulfonylureas (Sulfonylureas effectively block the K_ATP channel defect) or high-dose GLP-1 agonists.

Using insulin in *HNF1A*-MODY is often futile because the issue isn't pancreatic autoimmunity or beta-cell *destruction* (as seen in T1D), but rather transcription factor failure reducing beta-cell sensitivity. In this case, the patient was initially put on insulin, which is expensive and high-burden. When genetics were confirmed, insulin was stopped. The patient stabilized on oral agents and GLP-1 agonists.

Additionally, the patient had severe sudomotor dysfunction (reduced conductance in hands). This is consistent with autonomic neuropathy often seen in diabetic dysautonomia, which can happen early in MODY due to the specific *HNF1A* defect affecting renal SGLT2 and nerve function.

## Key Take-Homes

*   **Don't let the calculator be the final boss.** Use the Exeter calculator as a heuristic, not a rule. A family history of diabetes in the first, second, or third generation below age 25 is a brighter signal than an estimated 60% probability derived from a White European cohort.
*   **Treat for Preservation.** For *HNF1A*-MODY, avoid high-dose insulin or unnecessary beta-cell suppression. GLP-1 agonists and metformin are often superior to insulin for maintaining beta-cell function in this specific monogenic context.
*   **Novel Mutations Matter.** This case identified a novel variant not in existing databases. Don't be turned off by "VUS" (Variant of Uncertain Significance) in people with strong phenotypic evidence. ACMG criteria, biochemical segregation, and functional scores (like AlphaMissense) support pathogenicity even with a "new" mutation.
*   **Genetic Screening is Better Than Guessing.** The text emphasizes that DNA sequencing is essential to confirm MODY clinical suspicions. With the shift to NGS, screening young patients for *HNF1A* variants is becoming more feasible and cost-effective.

## Critical Commentary

The takeaway from the Liu et al. report isn't just the *HNF1A* mutation; it's the workflow disruption caused by the discovery. In a patient initially treated for T2D, the insulin was stopped safely because the *HNF1A* diagnosis allowed for better metabolic management without the burden of injections.

However, the discussion highlights the tension in genetic medicine: When tools are biased, how do we balance safety? In this patient, the tool scored 62%, passing him through. But if a patient scored 5% due to "ethnic mismatch" in the algorithm, we'd be in a different case. The message is clear: Clinical suspicion in the context of early-onset diabetes and autosomal dominant family history should compel NGS panels regardless of what a non-localized calculator suggests.

Precision medicine helps when we have the genes; it hurts when we use the wrong maps to find them.
