Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Drug-Target Re-Alignment: Why Switching from Insulin to GLP-1 Matters

In the chaotic world of early-onset diabetes, the reflex to plunge a struggling patient into insulin can be automatic, especially when the HbA1c spikes above 9%. It signals control. But not everyone who looks like Type 2 has Type 2, and not everyone who looks like Type 1 has Type 1. Sometimes, the patient is a monogenic zebra wearing a stealth suit, and the incorrect drug target doesn't just fail—it actively highlights the underlying physiology that was ignored. This isn't about dosing; it's about mechanism.

We recently encountered a case where a young adult presented with a classic, high-grade metabolic profile including polydipsia, weight loss, and a 10.2% HbA1c. It was swiftly categorized as Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) given the relatively preserved physical exam (BMI 24.6) and the lack of obvious Type 1 features. The standard battery was maligned: metformin, saxagliptin, and insulin degludec. While the insulin bought initial control, the HbA1c dropped.

*10.2% to 9.1%.*

It's a start. It's a gamble. But it wasn't the *right* answer. Once genetic testing finally confirmed a novel, pathogenic HNF1A mutation, the treatment was purged. The insulin was axed, replaced by dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist). The result? Six months later, the HbA1c sat at 6.0%. This dramatic pivot isn't just about efficacy; it is a physiological necessity determined by the molecular architecture of beta-cell disease.

## The Trap of Exogenous Insulin in Monogenic Disease

When we treat a poorly controlled patient, we frequently default to exogenous insulin because it has the most potent glucose-lowering capacity. It is the "widows' peak" of therapeutic escalation—it works when nothing else can. However, in HNF1A-MODY (what we now call MODY3), the problem isn't usually an absolute lack of insulin secretion; the problem is the signaling pathway required to stimulate that secretion.

Think of the beta cell in HNF1A-MODY as a tarnished engine. It wants to run, but the key (the transcriptional regulation via HNF1A) is stuck. Insulin therapy is like shouting at the car from the window. It pushes the car forward physically, but it doesn't fix the engine. Over time, exogenous insulin can mask the underlying beta-cell decline and might even exacerbate the progression of some monogenic forms by inducing counter-regulatory fatigue or weight gain without solving the genetic defect.

In contrast, a GLP-1 receptor agonist or a sulfonylurea acts from the inside out. Sulfonylureas target the same channel (ATP-sensitive K+ channel) that HNF1A is supposed to manage. They trick the beta cell into opening the gate to release its own insulin, while GLP-1 receptors amplify the signal. In this specific case, the initial insulin regimen pushed glycemia down from 88 mmol/mol, but it didn't address the transcriptional bottleneck. Switching to a GLP-1 RA allowed for glucose control while sparing the remaining beta-cell function, preventing the "burnout" trajectory often seen when we treat MODY as T2D.

## The Precision Medicine Pivot

This case highlights a critical emerging frontier: the utility of next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels in endocrinology clinics. The patient’s family history—a maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her 20s, and brother at 20—should have raised a red flag. In the past, perhaps we would have waited for the patient to fail sulfonylureas. However, the rise in obesity prevalence has blurred the lines, and many MODY patients now present with a BMI and metabolic profile that overlaps heavily with established T2D.

The clinician in this scenario must be a functional detective.
*   **Ameliorate suspicion:** Early-onset family history often precedes the diagnosis.
*   **Rule out acceleration:** Standard T1D autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) are negative in MODY, but so do they in T2D. They lack specificity in the early stages.
*   **The Modality:** Glucagon stimulation testing in this patient showed preserved (though insufficient) insulin secretion, which argues against T1D and points toward a secretagogue-responsive state.

It is also vital to remember that HNF1A-MODY presents with elevated glycosuria. Why? Because HNF1A also regulates SGLT2 in the kidney. This shared mechanism is why SGLT2 inhibitors are risky here—they can push the dehydration and ketoacidosis threshold too low for a beta cell already on the verge of complete failure. The choice was made carefully: GLP-1 or Sulfonylurea, not SGLT1 or SGLT2.

## Clinical Implications

Ultimately, this case is a reminder that early, aggressive insulinization for a young adult with HNF1A-MODY is a protocol that treats the blood sugar, but it ignores the patient. Accurate diagnosis isn't just academic; it dictates the drug class. Misdiagnosing MODY 3 as T2DM and treating with insulin exposes the patient to hypoglycemia risk and sectioning the beta-cell reserve further. Getting the diagnosis right means getting the drug right.

In this instance, the switch to a GLP-1 agonist was not merely "better"; it was the physiologically appropriate target for a transcriptional defect.

### Key Take-Home Messages

*   **MODY 3 inheritance and family history** across three generations are stronger predictors than BMI in early-onset diabetes.
*   **Exogenous insulin should be avoided** if MODY is suspected; sulfonylureas and GLP-1 receptor agonists are the targeted therapies that spare beta-cell function.
*   **SGLT2 inhibitors carry risks** for HNF1A-MODY patients due to increased baseline glycosuria and altered renal function.
*   **Genetic testing should be considered earlier** in young adults with early-onset diabetes and negative autoimmune markers.
*   **GLP-1 RAs are safer alternatives** to insulin for glycemic control and preservation of function compared to long-acting insulin regimens.
