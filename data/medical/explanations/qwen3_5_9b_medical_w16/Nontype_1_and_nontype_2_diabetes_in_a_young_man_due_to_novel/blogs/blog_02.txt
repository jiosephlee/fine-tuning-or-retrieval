Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Database Blind Spots: How to Grade Novel MODY Variants Without ClinVar

If you spend a lot of time in the endocrine clinic or the EM gene desk, you know the frustration of the "negative" variant report. You get an NGS panel back that screams "pathogenic," but the database looks back and says, "Nope, we've never seen that one before." In the world of monogenic diabetes, specifically Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), this blue-sky scenario can be the difference between aggressive insulin coverage and a simple sulfonylurea regimen. We recently reviewed a case report from *Liu et al.* that tackles a pretty scary error source: the silence of public registries. When you have a novel *HNF1A* mutation like p.N270S that isn't in ClinVar, VarSome, or the literature, how do you decide if it actually causes disease without relying on a database catch-up? The answer lies in an intersection of ACMG guidelines and modern AI prediction tools.

### The Clinical Dilemma: A "Boring" Case with a Twist

Imagine a 25-year-old male who walks in with an HbA1c of 10.2% and a BMI of 24.6 kg/m². No trouble with obesity, no Acanthosis nigricans, but there is glycosuria. You run the standard diabetes workup: autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A, ZnT8) are negative. He has a glucagon stimulation test showing a C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, suggesting he still has some insulin to give, just not enough. Then you add the family history: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother with diabetes at 20. This screams autosomal dominant polygenic or monogenic diabetes to nearly any seasoned practitioner.

Usually, the diagnosis boils down to T1D (unlikely given the C-peptide and antibody status) or T2D (unlikely given the age and lack of severe insulin resistance). It's the textbook definition of the MODY diagnostic dilemma. In this case, the patient was initially treated for T2D with a basal insulin regimen, metformin, and saxagliptin. However, upon closer clinical review, with the suspicion of a monogenic etiology, they moved to genetic testing.

### The Database Silence

Here is where it gets interesting for the translational medicine crowd. The genetic testing revealed a heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G). While *HNF1A* is the workhorse of MODY cases (accounting for 30%-65% of MODY generally), this specific variant was a ghost. It was absent from the ClinVar database, the VarSome database, and did not exist in the literature at the time of publication. In most error-management systems, a simple "VUS" or "Unknown" tag might get the patient stuck on insulin therapy for years.

However, the authors took the "Novel Line" approach rather than the "Database Blind Spot" approach. They applied the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation but had to rely heavily on structural biology and computational prediction because there were no prior natural cases in the global pool yet. The mutation wasn't benign because of where it landed on the protein. p.N270S sits in exon 4, squarely within the DNA-binding domain. Mutations in this area are historically associated with pathogenic loss of function.

To bolster the classification beyond just structural location, they utilized AI tools. Specifically, they cited a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995. While bioinformatics tools have false positives, a score that high when combined with segregation analysis is compelling evidence. The family segregation was a given—the disease tracked through three generations. If this variant didn't cause diabetes, the family history of early-onset diabetes in three relatives across different generations would have been a massive coincidence. The combination of a high-computational probability score, a domain location known to be critical for function, and clear pedigree segregation allowed them to classify this as Likely Pathogenic (LP) despite the lack of "crowdsourced" data in ClinVar.

### Why Treating the Mutation Matters

This case isn't just a genetics lesson; it's a metabolic rescue mission. The patient initially received basal insulin for rapid control, a strategy often used in empiric T1D or T2D trials. Once the genetic diagnosis was confirmed, the treatment paradigm shifted drastically. Insulin was discontinued and replaced with a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide), followed later by the maintenance of metformin and saxagliptin.

Why? Because *HNF1A* mutation leads to a defect in the glucose transporter function and insulin secretion dynamics that are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas. Sulfonylureas close the K-ATP channels to stimulate insulin, which is precisely the mechanism benefiting *HNF1A*-MODY. GLP-1s are an excellent alternative here, avoiding the hypoglycemia risk often seen with insulin therapy in monogenic diabetes where the goal is to preserve beta-cell function rather than suppress hyperglycemia.

In this patient, the HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0% over six months without insulin. If the clinicians had stuck to the T2D algorithm without genetic confirmation, that patient would be living on insulin injections indefinitely, risking hypoglycemic episodes without biological justification. As the authors highlight, the MODY probability calculator can be an aid, though we must be careful—it's been derived from white European populations and might underestimate MODY in non-White groups or populations with higher comorbidities. This case adds a layer of confirmation: even when the calculator says 62%, the molecular evidence (when interpreted correctly) seals the deal.

### Take-Home Messages

*   **Clinical suspicion trumps negative databases:** When a patient fits the MODY phenotype (young onset, strong family history, negative autoantibodies), a novel variant in a known MODY gene should be treated as pathogenic if supported by segregation and protein location, even if ClinVar is silent.
*   **Use the data you have:** In the absence of registry data, integrate AlphaMissense scores and exon localization (specifically the DNA-binding domain for *HNF1A*) into the ACMG classification assessment.
*   **Precision medicine dictates therapy:** A confirmed or highly suspected *HNF1A* mutation warrants a trial of sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists rather than insulin, especially in the absence of beta-cell depletion, to avoid hypoglycemia and preserve endogenous secretion.
*   **Family history is a biomarker:** Three generations of early-onset diabetes are more statistically significant than any single-gene database entry for monogenic diabetes evaluation.
