Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Safe Scan: Leveraging Non-Contrast MRI for Diagnosing Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

Abdominal masses in pregnancy often present a diagnostic Rorschach test. Is it an ovarian cyst, an ectopic pregnancy, or a uterine pathology? When imaging is ambiguous, the stakes are not just diagnostic; they are obstetric. A complex scenario recently described by De Nola et al. highlights how ultrasound alone can mask the true nature of uterine pathology, presenting as adnexal masses or endometriomas, while non-contrast MRI offers the anatomical clarity necessary to guide surgical decision-making without compromising fetal safety.

### The Diagnostic Mimic

Consider a G1P0A1 patient in her 40s presenting in the second trimester at 21 weeks gestation. She presents severe pain in the left upper quadrant and pelvis, along with dyspnoea. Her clinical exam reveals a significantly enlarged abdomen, with a tender, taut-elastic mass occupying the upper left quadrant measuring 18 cm. Bimanual exam detects a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis measuring 9 cm. At this stage, the clinical picture screams pathologic mass, but exactly what?

Initial investigation usually kicks off with the go-to modality: Ultrasound on Colour Doppler. In this case, the US imaging demonstrated two large, unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularisation. The radiological description mimicked two huge decidualised endometriomas. The upper mass was huge, extending from the subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass appeared to be an adnexal cyst. However, there were red flags. The patient developed low-grade fever and signs of inflammation, including neutrophilia and CRP elevation, leading to concerns about torsion or infection. Tumour markers, specifically CA-125, showed a slight elevation (41.8 U/mL) in a second-trimester setting where levels typically normalize.

This is where the diagnosis diverged. While the ultrasound suggested a possibility of endometrioma or ovarian cyst, the clinical reality was different. Laparotomy revealed the cranial mass to be a huge pedunculated, vascularised leiomyoma. Ostensibly, the masses were "uterine," not adnexal, which shifted the management paradigm from gynaecological cystectomy to myomectomy with preservation of uterine viability.

### Imaging: Ultrasound vs. Non-Contrast MRI

The core of this case report is the utility of safety-conscious imaging. Ultrasound is the initial workhorse, particularly for identifying symptomatic uterine leiomyomas on the anterior wall, but it has significant limitations, especially with the "steric bulk" of a gravid uterus and maternal obesity (BMI 33 in this case). The ultrasound failed to adequately define the relationship between the superior mass and the uterine cavity, and the cleavage of the caudal mass was not clear.

This is why the team moved to Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) without contrast. MRI offered superior diagnostic accuracy, allowing for the precise determination of the distance between the masses and the uterine cavity. In this specific scenario, MRI showed inhomogeneous fluid content and focal hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid components, alongside hypertrophied vessels. It clarified that the caudal mass seemed to be in continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. While MRI is powerful, the use of gadolinium is fraught with untoward effects during pregnancy. According to current up-to-date recommendations, gadolinium-enhanced MRI should not be performed during pregnancy unless strictly necessary due to risks of rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin conditions, stillbirth, or neonatal death. In cases of benign masses where gadolinium isn't critical for safety, a non-contrast MRI is the gold standard. By utilizing MRI without contrast, the team gathered necessary anatomical data to distinguish uterine origin from adnexal masses without exposing the fetus to potential contrast risks.

### Management and Safety

The clinical decision to intervene surgically was driven by the deterioration of the patient's condition—worsening abdominal pain and dyspnoea attributed to mechanical compression from the massive lesions. They needed a definitive diagnosis and a plan for the growing uterus. The multidisciplinary team attempted conservative management first but realized that the masses were symptomatic and growing (leiomyomas can increase by 6.90% every week in utero).

Surgical management involved a longitudinal laparotomic approach. The superior mass, initially twisted and leaking, was excised from its uterine implant. The root was closed with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, fixed and adherent to the posterior wall, was left in situ to minimise obstetric risk. The pathologic exam confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. The patient subsequently underwent an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks because the residual pelvic myoma acted as an obstacle to fetal head progression. The outcomes were excellent, with no malignancy found and maternal recovery uncomplicated aside from transfusion requirements for the intraoperative anaemia.

The case serves as a reminder that uterine leiomyomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses in pregnancy, particularly when they have undergone cystic degeneration. Because the management strategy hinges on whether the mass is adnexal (potentially ovarian) or uterine (potentially requiring specific avoidance of the cervix during delivery), accurate anatomical mapping is critical.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Differential Diagnosis is Key:** Large, cystic masses during pregnancy must differentiate between decidualised endometriomas, cystic degenerating leiomyomas, and secondary ovarian neoplasms.
*   **CA-125 Interpretation:** Isolated serum CA-125 measurements are not diagnostic in pregnancy; physiological elevation occurs, particularly in the first and third trimesters, so serial evaluation is preferred over single-point cutoffs.
*   **MRI Safety:** A non-contrast MRI is the safest and most accurate imaging modality for characterizing complex pelvic masses in pregnancy, avoiding the risks associated with gadolinium.
*   **Surgical Timing:** Elective surgery can be considered for complicated masses causing symptoms (pain, dyspnoea) but should be limited to the tailoring procedure (e.g., myomectomy) rather than adnexectomy.
*   **Delivery Planning:** The mode of delivery must account for the anatomical obstruction caused by large posterior wall myomas, often necessitating a planned caesarean section.
*   **Fetal Monitoring:** Fetal heart rate monitoring before, during, and after surgical intervention for abdominal masses is mandatory to minimize maternal and fetal complications.



# CA-125 in Pregnancy: A Diagnostic Trap for Non-Gynecologic Clinicians

## The Cystic Illusion

Walking into the emergency department or a radiology suite with a patient in her 40s presenting with an abdominal mass and an abnormal tumor marker is going to trigger the same alarm bells everywhere. You see the imaging: unilocular cystic masses, thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid. You see the lab work: CA-125 coming back above 35 U/mL. In a standard workup, that’s your green light for an oncologic consult or an aggressive diagnostic laparoscopy.

But what happens when the patient is pregnant? Specifically, what happens when you have an obese woman, 21 weeks out from her last menstrual period, struggling with severe left-quadrant pain and dyspnea, and your tumor markers are behaving like alarmists?

This case report by De Nola et al. illuminates exactly that scenario. The patient presented with what appeared to be two massive decidualized endometriomas or even a primary ovarian malignancy based on the markers and imaging appearance. However, the final diagnosis was benign uterine leiomyomas (fibroids). The key to avoiding unnecessary intervention—and potentially fetal demise—lay in understanding that CA-125 is not static in pregnancy. It’s a physiological roller coaster, and sometimes it feels like an oncologic crisis when in reality, it’s just gestation.

This post is a deep dive into why a single elevated value can mimic malignancy in older patients, emphasizing the necessity of longitudinal trends and the specific median ranges for the second trimester to avoid unnecessary interventions.

## The Physiology of the False Positive

If you are old enough to remember the introduction of CA-125 in the 1980s for epithelial ovarian cancer, you might still hold onto that old equation: High CA-125 = Cancer. While this holds true for postmenopausal women with anecolic masses, the landscape shifts dramatically in the gravid uterus.

In the present case, **the patient was in her 40s (G1P0A1) with a BMI of 33**. These are two demographic features that statistically increase the pre-test probability of gynecologic malignancy (or at least ovarian mass pathology). When you add a CA-125 of **41.8 U/mL** to that picture, the clinical instinct is to act.

However, you have to respect the physiology. The authors note that CA-125 levels exhibit physiological elevation during gestation. There are often described peaks during the first trimester and another closer to the third trimester, but even the baseline during the second trimester is higher than in a standard post-reproductive state.

Here is the critical data from this report:
*   **Median CA-125 in 2nd Trimester:** ~14.0 U/mL.
*   **Median CA-125 in 1st Trimester:** ~23 U/mL.
*   **Median CA-125 in 3rd Trimester:** ~21 U/mL.
*   **Patient Value:** 41.8 U/mL.

While 41.8 is higher than the *median* for the 2nd trimester, it is not exponentially higher. In many fertility-aware tracking models or specific trimester norms, values below 50 U/mL during pregnancy are often viewed with a "thumb on the scale" of benign disease rather than overt malignancy, provided there is a benign imaging phenotype.

The danger for the non-gynecologist here is the **cut-off.** The "standard cut-off" for ovarian cancer (≥35 U/mL) is essentially the definition of abnormal in a non-pregnant adult. If you percieve this patient as a standard 40-year-old with a solid ovarian mass, you act. But she is a 21-week gravid patient with what looked like cystic endometriosis or a leiomyoma. Acting on a single snapshot of a hormone that fluctuates physiologically invites unnecessary surgery.

## The Diagnostic Trap and Decisions

The diagnostic reasoning in this case hinged on the failure to wait or trend the markers in the context of pregnancy norms. The patient had a concurrent history of a urinary tract infection (*Klebsiella pneumoniae*) and iron deficiency anemia, complicating the clinical picture. The fever they developed initially was attributed to the twisted pedunculated myoma (as it turns out), but from the onset, the team saw a "slight increasing trend" in CA-125.

This is where careful imaging becomes your friend. US showed "two large unilocular cystic masses with regular margins," but the MRI was the differentiator.
*   **Without contrast:** You avoid the rheumatological risk, but you still get structural data.
*   **With MRI:** You assess the relationship of the mass to the uterine cavity and the presence of solid components.

The MRI findings here showed **no adenopathy** and disconnected from the ovaries initially, suggesting a uterine origin. The team hypothesized that the upper mass was likely endometriotic due to the marker trend, but the MRI features favored a complex leiomyoma or myoma with cystic degeneration.

They eventually had to operate because the patient was symptomatic:
1.  **Dyspnea:** Due to mass effect on the diaphragm.
2.  **Pain:** From the twisted pedicle of the superior mass.
3.  **Risk:** Torsion or necrosis.

They did not do a hysterectomy. They did a targeted myomectomy. The handicap (BMI 33) and the visibility of the masses (2 x 18cm equivalent) likely dictated the laparotomy. The decision to leave the caudal mass "in situ" is a crucial point. You know why: pregnancy alters myoma size transiently. It can't always be "waxed on the way" to resolution, and the pregnancy must be preserved.

## Takeaways for the Clinician

This isn't just about the uterus; it's about compartmentalizing risk factors. You can have a high pre-test probability of cancer (age, mass, family history) but you must contextualize that risk against the physiological state of pregnancy.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Do not use static cancer cutoffs during pregnancy.** A CA-125 of 35–50 U/mL in a second-trimester patient is frequently benign and often reflects physiologic changes, premature vasculature, or benign peritoneal irritation.
*   **Trend the markers.** An isolated measurement is diagnostic, but a rising trend in the absence of other signs is not malignant. Serial evaluations are more useful for differential diagnosis than a single snapshot.
*   **MRI is safe and essential.** Non-contrast MRI should be the next step when US is equivocal. It avoids the need for gadolinium (unless strictly necessary) and helps define the relationship to the uterine cavity and placenta.
*   **Wait for the diagnosis when safe.** Conservative management with expectant care is usually preferred for asymptomatic fibroids in pregnancy. Surgical intervention (myomectomy) should be reserved for complications (torsion, hemorrhage, obstruction) or high suspicion of malignancy.
*   **Delivery Planning.** Large abdominal masses in pregnancy require careful obstetric planning. Cesarean delivery (especially via uterine incision) may be necessary to prevent rupture or account for the mass during the expulsive phase.

Always keep an eye on the patient, but don't let a lab value drive the driver when the patient has a body that is actively doing a lot of maintenance work—like growing a human.



# The Symptom Threshold: When to Operate on Symptomatic Uterine Leiomyomas

Pregnancy is the ultimate test of diagnostic and surgical restraint. When a patient presents with new growths in the abdomen, the default setting in obstetrics is almost always expectant management. The fear of iatrogenic preterm labor, uterine rupture, or fetal loss often outweighs maternal morbidity, prioritizing the "one healthy baby" outcome over the complications of a large uterus. But what happens when expectant management turns dangerous? When a massive lesion isn't just growing, but actively compromising maternal physiology? This case report challenges the comfort zone of the abdominal mass patient, outlining the difficult "Symptom Threshold" where the risks of continuing the pregnancy outweigh the risks of surgical intervention.

## The Case and the Diagnostic Trap

We are looking at a 40-year-old nulliparous woman presenting at 21 weeks gestation with severe acute abdominal pain and dyspnoea. An obese patient with a BMI of 33, this presentation initially mimicked a typical obstetric pathology like uterine hypertonus or torsion, but the physical exam revealed something distinct: an enormous abdominal mass. The bimanual exam found a fixed mass in the pelvis and a tender swelling of roughly 18 cm in the upper left quadrant, extending to the fundus.

This is where clinical intuition often meets the imaging department. The differential for a 20-week-old uterus with ten-centimeter masses is extensive. While leiomyomas are common, misdiagnosis is the enemy. Ultrasound initially suggested "decidualised endometriomas," a classic pitfall. The upper mass looked cystic on ultrasound, but MRI—the gold standard in fetal safety allowed usage without gadolinium—revealed the truth. It showed thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid, and solid components. It wasn't ovarian cancer; it wasn't an endometrioma. It was a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma that had twisted.

A major confounder in the diagnostic workup was the laboratory biomarker profile. CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL. In board exams, this screams malignancy, but in the obstetrics ward, this numbers are a red herring. Physiological increases occur in pregnancy, with peaks in the first and third trimesters. However, a rise that persists and is higher than the second-trimester median warrants concern, especially for torsion or necrosis. Yet, combined with the mild fever and infection findings, the clinical picture of a necrotizing twisted pedunculated myoma became undeniable.

## Breaking the Breach: When to Cut

The central tension in this case is the decision point. Standard literature usually favors myomectomy only after delivery unless the patient is actively deteriorating or there is high oncologic suspicion. You do not want to recurve at 21 weeks. However, this patient had objective signs of systemic compromise. She had dyspnoea attributed to the "steric bulk" of the abdominal solids pressing on the diaphragm, and the presence of fever and inflammatory markers suggested torsion-induced necrosis of the vascular pedicle.

If you are managing a large mass in pregnancy, you need to define the threshold for intervention. The guiding principle here is the "Symptom Threshold." Was the patient in pain? Yes, severe pain on a 5-point VAS. Was the physiologic reserve threatened? Yes, restrictive lung disease and infection. Was the lesion potentially malignant? The slight CA-125 rise and image features warranted confirmation.

In this scenario, the multidisciplinary team opted for a laparotomy at 21 weeks. This is not routine. It is a break from the standard workup due to the specific anatomy—a 18cm vessel-rich mass that was technically twisted. Leaving it alone risked rupture, severe blood loss, or exacerbation of the respiratory compromise, all of which posed a greater threat to the fetus than the stress of the surgery itself. It is a reminder that maternal stability is the foundation of fetal viability.

## Surgical Nuance and Fetal Safety

The surgical management in this case highlights the specific technical conflicts between gynecological and obstetric goals. The decision was made to excise the upper, pedunculated mass but leave the caudal pelvic mass in situ. Why? The pelvic mass was large, fixed, and adherent to the posterior uterus. Attempting to remove it would likely necessitate a hysterectomy or severe fibroid dehiscence. By excising the acute torsion risk first, they preserved the pregnancy potential while securing the immediate maternal threat.

Fetal monitoring is non-negotiable in this setting. The text references the importance of checking and documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after the procedure, noting work by Zhao et al. regarding the necessity of fetal monitoring. The team administered progesterone prophylaxis immediately post-op to mitigate the stress response on the pregnancy. The microbiology also played a role here; a Klebsiella pneumoniae urine infection was found and treated with specific antibiotics rather than empirical high-dose beta-blockers, showing the need for targeted infection control in the ER admissions for "abdominal pain."

The patient experienced transient anemia and required transfusion, but the cumulative effect of the surgery was positive. The obstetric follow-up showed a reassuring fetal growth rate. The final plan involved an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks because the residual caudal mass was too large to predict for vaginal delivery progress. This second intervention was planned, whereas a post-op rupture during a forceful labor attempt could have been catastrophic. They balanced the variables, opted for the myomectomy early, and avoided a potentially fatal labor dystocia later.

## Take-home messages

*   **Manchester Rule Revisited:** Manage uterine leiomyomas expectantly in pregnancy unless there is dysejaculation, torsion, vascular compromise, or unstable clinical presentation.
*   **Diagnostic Red Flags:** Do not rely on a single CA-125 level in the second trimester to rule out tumors, as physiological levels fluctuate; however, a rising trend with mass necrosis symptoms warrants imaging.
*   **Imaging Safety:** Non-contrast MRI is safe and preferred in the second trimester when US is inconclusive, avoiding gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.
*   **Indication for Surgery:** The principal indications for intervention in the second trimester are torsion, uncontrollable bleeding, or respiratory compromise due to mass effect.
*   **Residual Planning:** Elective delivery at 37 weeks via caesarean section may be necessary for large residual fibroids that impede obstructed labor.
*   **Fetal Safety:** Continuous fetal monitoring during the intraoperative laparotomy reduces stillbirth risk compared to quick, unobserved clamping of large vessels.

In summary, while most women with fibroids get through their pregnancy without a knife, the anatomy dictates the care. This case is a critical lesson in knowing when the uterus becomes a cage rather than a vessel. When the mechanical load of the masses compromises the airway or the blood supply of the growing fetus, the scalpel is the safest delivery method for both the mother and the child.



# Obstetric Roadblocks: How Posterior Intramural Myomas Dictate Delivery Mode

## The Diagnostic Minefield in the Second Trimester

Congratulations if you can spot the complication before the dyspnea sets in. You know the common presentation: lower abdominal pain, uterine growth, maybe a bleed. But what happens when your patient presents at 21 weeks gestation with two massive abdominal tumors, dyspnea from diaphragmatic compression, and an enlarged uterus that bounces off the abdominal wall with a taut-elastic consistency?

This scenario presents a classic diagnostic tightrope. As we walk through a case of simultaneous abdominal masses during pregnancy, we aren't just learning about abdominal imaging. We are learning how the *anatomical location* of a uterine mass—specifically a posterior intramural leiomyoma—can fundamentally dictate the mode of delivery 16 weeks down the road. This case serves as a stark reminder that in obstetrics, "benign" isn't always something you can leave in the body without strategic planning for the day your patient can no longer walk, and you decide you have to push.

## The Clinical Picture

Let’s ground this in the patient data. A 40-year-old G1P0A1 with a BMI of 33 comes to the ER with severe acute pain (VAS 5) and dyspnea. On the bimanual exam, you're fixed; the abdomen feels enlarged for the gestational age, and you can feel two distinct masses. The upper lesion is quite large (18 cm) and extends into the subphrenic space, while the lower lesion is pelvic (9 cm) and fixed.

Initial ultrasound and MRI suggest ovarian pathology. It’s a classic trap. Six co. unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularization mimic decidualised endometriomas perfectly. Even the tumor markers play a dirty trick; CA-125 rises slightly (41.8 U/mL). In a non-pregnant woman, this is an alarm bell for ovarian cancer. In pregnancy? It's the expected physiological fluctuation of a growing uterus, though the increasing trend here warranted attention.

However, the MRI revealed the truth: thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid, and most importantly, continuity with the uterine tissue. The final diagnosis wasn't ovarian cancer—it was complicated uterine leiomyomas. One mass was a pedunculated, twisted myoma causing the acute event (hence the fever, elevated WBC, and anemia at 21 weeks), and the other was a massive cystic degenerating mass adherent to the posterior uterine wall.

## Managing the "Roadblock"

Here is where the clinical decision tree gets interesting. You see a massive mass. Do you operate, or do you wait?

The general dogma is expectant management to protect the pregnancy. Myomas grow with the uterus—roughly 6.9% per week in the first trimester and slower later. But they don't just shrink like lemons; estimates suggest they shrink around 44% six weeks post-delivery.

In this specific case, the decision to operate was driven by immediate complications: worsening pain, signs of torsion in the pedunculated mass, and symptomatic dyspnea from the "steric bulk." The surgical team performed a laparotomy at 21 weeks. They resected the pedunculated mass that was acting up, but they left the second mass behind.

Why? Because the location was critical. It was a massive intramural-subserosal leiomyoma fixed to the posterior wall. Trying to excise it intra-abdominally here carries massive risks of hemorrhage and infection to a term myoma, but if they hadn't operated, the mass would have likely twisted or ruptured anyway. By choosing to remove the pedunculated mass, they mitigated the acute bleeding risk, but they created a permanent, large anatomical constraint for future delivery.

## The Obstructive lesion and the c-section

This brings us to the crux of this blog post: the impact on delivery mode. When you have a leukomyoma in the posterior wall or an intramural location, vaginal birth becomes a theoretical risk rather than a routine option.

In this case, despite the surgical mass removal, the residual pelvic leiomyoma remained. The team opted for an elective Cesarean at 37 weeks. Why? The discussion in the literature attached to this case highlights a very specific danger: a huge myoma adherent to the posterior wall of the isthmic site acts as an obstacle to vaginal birth.

We often see the debate whether to do a myomectomy at the same time as the C-section. The literature here suggests that for large intramural masses, delaying the operation until after birth is often safer, but the immediate risk is that the mass causes obstruction. The residual mass in the pouch of Douglas region (fixed to the posterior uterus) would have prevented the fetal head from progressing normally.

More critically, the risk of uterine rupture. Scarring the uterus is a high-stakes gamble when the placenta might be adjacent or the tumor is central. In this specific patient, the mass created a "roadblock" that dictated the C-section. It wasn't just about the size; it was about the location.

If you look at the evidence referenced in the case report, the hospital stay and duration of surgery were significantly higher when myomectomy was done concurrently with C-section compared to C-section alone. This reinforces the idea that sometimes you must choose between the "myomectomy now" (higher hospital cost/complexity) and the "C-section now" (safety of the maternal and fetal head).

## Take-home Messages

*   **Diagnostic confusion is common:** Posterior intramural myomas with cystic degeneration often mimic ovarian masses on ultrasound and can cause elevated CA-125 due to pelvic hemodynamic changes during pregnancy.
*   **Timing matters:** Surgical management is usually reserved for symptomatic masses, torsion, or infection. In this case, the acute torsion and dyspnea at 21 weeks mandated intervention before mechanical obstruction became permanent.
*   **Location dictates the plan:** Posterior intramural or isthmic masses essentially force a C-section decision because they act as a mechanical barrier to descent and increase the risk of uterine rupture during labor.
*   **Consent is key:** When facing a "twisted clockwork" gyne-pediatic scenario like this, ensure the consent is specific regarding the permanent anatomical change and its impact on labor (e.g., future vaginal delivery risks).
*   **Monitor the growth:** Expect myomas to grow by ~6% per trimester. If a mass is already obstructive in the second trimester, wait until 34-37 weeks for delivery to reduce prematurity risks unless there is decompensation.



# Compressing the Diaphragm: Distinguishing Pregnancy-Related Dyspnea from Mass Effect

## The Classic Puzzle: Dyspnea in the Second Trimester

If a pregnant patient walks into your ED complaining of shortness of breath, the differential usually screams thromboembolism or cardiac decompensation. We are trained to be vigilant about pulmonary embolism (PE), pulmonary edema, and the physiological hypoxemia of pregnancy. But what if the air space is fine, the oxygen saturation is normal, and the heart is beating steadily—yet she still can't catch a breath?

We recently reviewed a case report that brings us a very mechanical perspective to dyspnea in pregnancy: **mass effect.** Specifically, it reminds us that a swollen uterus isn't always the culprit; sometimes, what’s sitting *on* the uterus is the problem. This case illustrates how an atypical abdominal mass in the subphrenic space can mimic respiratory insufficiency, and why a high index of suspicion for ovoid masses is warranted in an obese gravid patient with unexplained dyspnoea.

## The Case: When the Abdomen is Too Big

Here’s where the clinical reality gets messy. We have a 40-year-old, nulliparous woman at 21 weeks gestation. She has a BMI of 33 (obese range). She presents with a classic, albeit atypical, combo: severe acute abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis, plus mild dyspnoea.

Her initial vitals were reassuring. Oxygen saturation was 97%, heart rate was 74, and blood pressure was stable. The ECG was normal. However, her respiratory contribution to the history was significant despite these reassuring numbers.

When the abdominal exam was done, it wasn't just big. It was "more enlarged than expected for the gestational age." A taut-elastic mass occupied the upper left quadrant (18 cm), and a fixed, hard mass occupied the pelvis (9 cm). She was tender, developed a low-grade fever (headed to the Sepsis workup), and had a urinary tract infection with *Klebsiella pneumoniae*. But critically, no pulmonary or cardiac pathology was found.

So, we were left with a differential driven by sterics.

## Chasing the Right Diagnosis in Pregnancy

Obtaining a diagnosis in the second trimester is all about safety vs. accuracy. We ran the usual gauntlet:

*   **Ultrasound:** Showed two large unilocular cystic masses. The upper one was 18x12x7 cm. The lower one was 9.5x8x8 cm. The US actually mimicked "decidualised endometriomas."
*   **Tumor Markers:** This is a critical pearl here. CA-125 was elevated (41.8 U/mL). In normal pregnancy, this hormone usually hits a peak in the first and third trimesters, dropping in the second. An elevation can be alarming to an inexperienced eye, but the differential is massive. It can mean endometrioma, pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, or just benign leiomyoma.
*   **MRI:** Because the risk of gadolinium is real in pregnancy, they wisely ordered a **non-contrast MRI**. This revealed thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content. While at first it mimicked adenexal cysts, the continuity with uterine tissue on the caudal mass pointed toward a myomatous origin.

The key diagnostic differential was **decidualised endometriomas vs. cystic degenerating leiomyomas.** Because no definitive guidelines exist for large masses specifically in pregnancy, the team had to weigh the risk of the mass against the risk of surgery.

## Intervention vs. Expectation

Management here was the crux of the educational moment. Usually, you'd expect these masses to wait and see until postpartum if they aren't clearly malignant or acutely infected. However, this patient had worsening dyspnoea attributed to **mechanical compression from the massive abdominal lesions**, specifically the upper mass compressing the diaphragm.

The risks were deemed too high:
1.  Airway compromise.
2.  Risk of torsion, rupture, or infection (she already had a fever).
3.  Steric hindrance to uterine growth.

They opted for an open laparotomy. The surprise during surgery was that the masses were benign uterine leiomyomas. The upper mass was a pedunculated, vascularized leiomyoma on a stalk (initially twisted), which the surgeon excised. The lower mass was left *in situ* to minimize obstetric risk, as it was fixed and large.

The patient required a C-section at 37 weeks because the pelvic myoma physically obstructed fetal head progression.

## Clinical Takeaways

When you are managing a pregnant patient with "dyspnoea," look beyond the heart and lungs. Here is how we operationalize this case for your ward round:

*   **Mechanical restriction is real.** A 18 cm subphrenic mass can restrict lung expansion just as a massive PE would. If oxygen saturation is normal but symptoms persist, consider steric bulk.
*   **Imaging safety.** Non-contrast MRI is reasonably safe after the first trimester (avoid gadolinium unless strictly necessary) and offers superior accuracy for distinguishing between pelvic masses, endometrioma, and leiomyoma.
*   **Interpret CA-125 with caution.** Do not let an isolated elevated CA-125 in the second trimester trigger a hysterectomy. Levels normalize later in pregnancy, and elevations can be seen in endometriosis, infection, and even benign myomas. Serial trends are more useful than single values.
*   **Obstetric Caesarean planning.** Large pelvic masses (especially those adherent to the posterior isthmic site) can act as a mechanical barrier to vaginal delivery. Plan for C-section delivery concomitantly or electively in these massive myoma cases to avoid obstructed labor.
*   **Surgical timing.** The decision to intervene pre-partum relies on the presumptive risk of complications (torsion/rupture) and symptomatic severity (dyspnoea/anaemia) vs. the risk of preterm labour. If the patient isn't crashing for a 60-second rescue, surgery can be planned safely with multidisciplinary input.
