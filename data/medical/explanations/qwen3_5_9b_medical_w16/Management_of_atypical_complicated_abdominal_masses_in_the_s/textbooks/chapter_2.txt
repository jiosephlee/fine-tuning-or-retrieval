Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Evaluation: Imaging and Laboratory Interpretation

The diagnostic evaluation of abdominal masses in pregnancy requires a stratified approach that prioritizes maternal safety, fetal welfare, and diagnostic accuracy. In this case, the patient’s physical examination presented unique challenges; an obese patient (BMI 33) with a significantly enlarged abdomen made palpation difficult, initially classifying the upper mass as a taut-elastic swelling occupying the left subphrenic region. While a bimanual exam identified a fixed, hard mass in the lesser pelvis bulging at the posterior fornix, the operator had clinical reasons to suspect masses larger than the fetus, prompting advanced imaging. Ultrasound served as the first-line diagnostic modality due to its safety profile and ability to differentiate cystic from solid components. The scan revealed two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularity on Doppler. While the upper mass extended from the left subphrenic region to the fundus, the lower mass appeared potentially adherent to a cystic degenerating myoma. Although ultrasound provided initial localization, its sensitivity is limited by maternal habitus and the presence of gas, making the distinction between adnexal pathology and uterine phenomena less definitive without further confirmation.

Following the ultrasound results, a non-contrast Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) was ordered to clarify the anatomical origin and nature of the lesions. MRI was chosen despite the standard avoidance of gadolinium in pregnancy to resolve specific uncertainties regarding the wall thickness and tissue content. The T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted images demonstrated thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content, and focal hyperintense solid components. Crucially, the study provided high-resolution detail without placental exposure to gadolinium, which is associated with potentially adverse fetal outcomes unless strictly necessary. This imaging modality offered the superior diagnostic accuracy required to evaluate multiple, large, intramural, or posterior-labial conditions, as suggested by the report's background literature. The MRI findings helped differentiate the masses from simple ovarian cysts by highlighting continuous tissue planes between the lower mass and the posterior intramural-myomatous wall of the uterus, which was not as clearly resolved on ultrasound.

Laboratory evaluation in this context requires careful interpretation of tumor markers, specifically CA-125. In the second trimester, serum CA-125 levels physiologically rise and subsequently normalize, reaching medians significantly higher than non-pregnant baseline values. An isolated measurement of 41.8 U/mL in this patient exceeded the laboratory cut-off for malignancy but remained within the physiological elevation range for pregnancy (median 14.0 U/mL, though variable with stress or inflammation). The slight increasing trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior correlated with clinical symptoms rather than providing standalone evidence of a neoplasm. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team interpreted this marker elevation as a supportive, rather than diagnostic, finding. The differential diagnosis remained broad, focusing on decidualized endometriomas—which are known to elevate CA-125—and potentially complicated leiomyomas.

Ultimately, the diagnosis relied on synthesizing imaging morphology, physiological lab trends, and physical findings to orient the surgical strategy. Radiological features of thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid suggested complex cysts, potentially mimicking decidualized endometriomas or cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The continuity of the caudal mass with the uterine tissue was the decisive factor; while the upper mass appeared pedunculated, the lower mass was inferred to originate from the uterine intramural-subserosal wall. This integration of data identified the masses not as separate ovarian adnexal tumors, but as uterine phenomena masquerading as abdominal masses. Recognizing the risk of tumor origin, torsion, or infection, the team prioritized the need for a definitive diagnosis and symptom relief, leading to the laparotomy where the masses were confirmed as benign leiomyomas with cystic degeneration and pedication.
