Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Context and Pathophysiology of Exaggerated Growth

Understanding the natural history of uterine leiomyomas is essential when evaluating women who present with abdominal enlargement during pregnancy. During the second and third trimesters, intramural leiomyomas typically undergo a rapid, non-linear growth phase due to their high sensitivity to estrogen and progesterone. Research indicates that the size of these masses increases by approximately 6.90% per week between the first and second trimesters, driven by proliferative hormonal signaling. This physiological phenomenon explains the clinical dilemma presented in this case: large masses identified at 21 weeks gestation are not static lesions but rapidly expanding entities. The observed "enlargement of the uterus more than expected for gestational age" in the patient's second trimester is a classic sign of exacerbated leiomyomatous growth, which must be distinguished from synchronous uterine fundal height consistent with fetal growth. Without accounting for this rapid expansion, clinicians might misattribute the mass effect to lymphadenopathy or gastrointestinal pathology, delaying life-threatening interventions for respiratory compromise.

Diagnosis and risk stratification in this context are complicated by advanced maternal age and the potential for concurrent pathologies. A patient in her 40s presents a unique differential profile where new-onset pelvic or abdominal masses in the second trimester warrant a heightened index of suspicion for malignancy, specifically ovarian or uterine adenocarcinoma, compared to younger nulliparous women. However, as demonstrated in the current case, the elevated tumor marker CA-125 (41.8 U/mL) must be interpreted within the physiological context of pregnancy, which causes transient, normal elevations of this marker. While a rising trend or levels significantly exceeding the second-trimester median (14.0 U/mL) may raise concern for endometriosis or adenomyosis, the elevated level does not independently confirm malignancy. This necessitates a multidisciplinary approach to differentiate between benign degenerating leiomyomas, as seen in the caudal mass with cystic degeneration, and neoplastic processes, guiding the decision to proceed with surgery or expectant management.

Clinical assessment and diagnostic accuracy are further challenged by patient-specific factors such as obesity, which directly impacts physical examination reliability. In this case, a body mass index of 33 contributed significantly to the difficulty in differentiating the massive abdomen from adhesions or bowel distension on palpation. Physical examination alone revealed a tender swelling and a taut-elastic mass but failed to precisely characterize the depth or origin of the lesions. Consequently, the reliance on non-invasive imaging, specifically transvaginal ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, becomes paramount. The contrast between the clinical impression of a simple abdominal distension and the actual discovery of complex, adherent masses highlights a key learning point: in obese pregnant patients, imaging is not merely adjunctive but diagnostic, as physical examination often lacks the resolution to assess the subphrenic or retroperitoneal extent of large masses.

The development of dyspnea and abdominal pain in this scenario is primarily a function of mechanical compression rather than systemic pulmonary or physiological derangement. Large subphrenic and pelvic masses physically displace the diaphragm upward, reducing functional residual capacity and causing restrictive lung disease. The patient’s specific presentation of acute abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and associated dyspnea at 21 weeks correlates with the upper mass occupying the entire left subphrenic space. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for initial stabilization; analgesic therapies and positional changes were employed to mitigate diaphragmatic compression and pain, yet the underlying pathology required surgical resolution to prevent complications such as torsion, rupture, or infection. Recognizing that these symptoms are often mechanical ensures the surgical team prepares for potential hemodynamic shifts and respiratory restriction upon resection of the extruded mass.

Finally, baseline obstetric considerations for a nulliparous patient in the second trimester dictate the management trajectory of the pregnancy. At 21 weeks of gestation with a high-percentage fetal growth (86th percentile for gestational age), the goal is to preserve the fetus while monitoring for preterm delivery triggers. However, the presence of a large pelvic mass, specifically the caudal lesion found to be fixed and intramural-subserosal, alters the normal partograph for delivery. Nulliparous patients with significant posterior uterine masses often face imminent obstruction during the expulsive phase of labor, rendering vaginal delivery risky if the mass does not regress. This necessitates a primary obstetric plan for elective caesarean section at 37 weeks, balancing the risk of spontaneous preterm labor against the safety of surgical delivery, ensuring optimal neonatal outcome while managing the lingering mass effect.
