Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Morning Report] Summarize the primary diagnostic conflict presented by large abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy and the key factors that informed the decision to intervene surgically.

The primary diagnostic conflict in this clinical scenario centers on distinguishing benign uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration from complex ovarian neoplasms, a distinction that is often obscured by physiological hormonal fluctuations and overlapping imaging features in the second trimester. In this case, ultrasound and MRI demonstrated large, cystic masses with solid components, while a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL—elevated above the physiological median for gestational age while supporting malignancy profiles—created diagnostic ambiguity between endometriosis and degenerating myomas.

The decision to intervene surgically at 21 weeks was driven not by presumptive malignancy, but by acute maternal decompensation and specific exploratory findings. The decision relied on three key factors: the presence of severe, mechanical pain and restrictive dyspnea caused by diaphragmatic compression; signs of infection and fever suggesting possible torsion or necrosis; and the radiological indication of a thick-walled, highly vascularized mass. During the laparotomy, intraoperative assessment confirmed the upper mass was a twisted pedunculated leiomyoma at imminent risk of ischemic damage, validating the procedure as necessary to secure a definitive diagnosis and prevent catastrophic complications. This approach prioritized maternal and fetal safety by removing the compromised tissue while preserving the uterus for a subsequent term delivery.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do the imaging findings on MRI and ultrasound distinguish a uterine leiomyoma from an ovarian endometrioma or primary adnexal cyst in the presence of pregnancy?

Distinguishing uterine leiomyomas from ovarian endometriomas during pregnancy is challenging, as the hematologic environment promotes cystic degeneration in leiomyomas that mimics the fluid-filled nature of decidualised endometriomas. In this case, initial ultrasonography characterized the lesions as two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation, a presentation strongly suggestive of adnexal cysts or endometriomas.

However, non-contrast MRI is the superior modality for differentiation. In this patient, despite the cystic appearance on both US and MRI, the critical diagnostic feature was the **anatomical continuity** of the caudal mass with the posterior uterine wall. On MRI, the caudal lesion appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration, in contrast to a discrete ovarian cyst which would typically lack this direct connection to the uterine parenchyma. Furthermore, the presence of focal hyperintense solid components on weighted MRI sequences helped characterize the degenerative nature of the myoma, whereas solid ovarian masses often present with different vascular patterns or diffusion restriction not seen here.

Clinically, this case underscores that while high-frequency ultrasound is adequate for initial screening, MRI is essential for defining the mass's origin when signs of "steric bulk" or potential torsion arise. Relying solely on the "cystic" appearance without confirming uterine continuity risks misdiagnosis. Accurate imaging guides the decision to operate: as in this case, where the pedunculated upper mass was twisted and necrotic, necessitating intervention, while the lower mass was left in situ due to its connection to the uterine wall and lack of acute complication. Ultimately, the continuity of the lesion with the uterine corpus on MRI is the definitive radiological discriminator separating a degenerating leiomyoma from a primary ovarian cyst in pregnancy.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the physiological fluctuation of serum CA-125 levels across the three trimesters of pregnancy and explain why a second-trimester elevation does not confirm malignancy.

Regarding the fluctuation of CA-125 during pregnancy, serum levels generally follow a biphasic physiological curve rather than a static baseline. As detailed in the background and discussion sections of the case, CA-125 typically rises during the first trimester, reaching a median value of approximately 23 U/mL. It subsequently declines to a nadir in the second trimester, where the median level is approximately 14.0 U/mL, before rising again during the third trimester to a median of roughly 21 U/mL.

In the context of our case, the patient presented in the second trimester (21 weeks) with a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL. While this value exceeded the physiological second-trimester median of 14.0 U/mL and surpassed the standard ovarian cancer threshold of 35 U/mL, it did not indicate malignancy. The pathology report confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration and ischaemic necrosis rather than cancer. This highlights a critical teaching point: while pregnancy itself alters CA-125 dynamics, benign uterine pathology—including leiomyomas, endometriosis, and pelvic inflammation—can cause significant elevation independent of the trimester-specific physiological curve. Therefore, an isolated elevation in the second trimester does not confirm malignancy. Management should prioritize serial evaluation, safe high-accuracy imaging (such as non-contrast MRI), and clinical correlation over reliance on a single tumor marker value, as overtreatment based solely on serology carrying risks to the pregnancy was avoided in this instance.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the typical growth dynamics of uterine leiomyomas during gestation versus the postpartum period, and how does this influence the timing of intervention?

**Clinical Teaching Point: Leiomyoma Growth Dynamics and Surgical Timing**

Leiomyomas exhibit a distinct physiologic growth pattern during pregnancy and puerperium, a phenomenon that significantly informs the timing of surgical intervention. As highlighted in the **Discussion** of this case report, uterine leiomyomas follow a non-linear trend of size change. Specifically, data cited in the report indicate that leiomyoma size increases by an average of 6.90% weekly during gestation, a trend that is significantly higher in the first trimester (6.12% per trimester) compared to the latter stages (3.10% per trimester between the second and third trimesters).

This active hypertrophy implies that masses identified in the second trimester, such as the patient in our case presenting with fibroid-like masses at 21 weeks, may continue to expand and cause increased mechanical compression, vascular compromise, or risk of degeneration. Consequently, the decision for intervention must balance this growth potential against the risks of pregnancy termination or preterm labor. Conservative management is generally preferred to avoid the morbidity of surgery, but surgical management is indicated when complications arise—such as torsion of pedunculated masses (as seen with the patient's superiormass) or severe, intractable symptoms like the restrictive dyspnea and pain experienced by our patient.

The postpartum period offers a unique window for risk modification. The report details that by 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size significantly decreases, with a mean value of a 44.25% reduction. This physiologic involution influences the timing of subsequent procedures by suggesting that immediate postpartum intervention may be unnecessary. In the management of this patient, the team delayed the management of the residual pelvic myoma until after delivery. Post-delivery, the patient was monitored because the residual mass had slightly reduced to 7.5 cm and remained asymptomatic, allowing for deferral of further surgical steps in favor of expectant management.

**Core Takeaway:** Expect significant leiomyoma growth during pregnancy (faster in the first trimester) and regression postpartum. Reserve surgical intervention during pregnancy for complications like torsion or severe symptoms; consider deferring surgery in the postpartum period unless symptoms persist or size prevents vaginal delivery, taking advantage of postpartum involution to minimize intervention.

### [Pathophysiology] What underlying vascular or inflammatory mechanism explains the patient's acute abdominal pain, low-grade fever, and inflammatory markers prior to surgical debulking?

In this case, the combination of acute abdominal pain, low-grade fever, and elevated inflammatory markers prior to surgical debulking is explained by **torsion of a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma**.

**Pathophysiology:**
The critical mechanism is vascular compromise. Following torsion of the mass's pedicle, venous outflow is obstructed first, leading to venous congestion, hemorrhage, and subsequent arterial compromise. This results in ischemic necrosis of the leiomyoma tissue. The necrotic tissue acts as a potent irritant within the peritoneal cavity, triggering a localized sterile peritonitis that releases inflammatory mediators (cytokines) into the systemic circulation. This explains the clinical triad of severe pain, low-grade fever, and a reactive leukocytosis with elevated C-reactive protein, despite the absence of infectious etiologies in the peritoneal fluid cultures.

**Case Evidence:**
The clinical course was consistent with torsion-induced infarction despite the challenging context of the second trimester of pregnancy.
*   **Clinical Presentation:** The patient presented with acute pain, dyspnea from mass effect, and fever during her admission.
*   **Imaging Dynamics:** Ultrasound demonstrated a "large, unilocular cystic mass... with peripheral vascularisation," suggesting a viable but potentially compromised vascular pedicle.
*   **Surgical Confirmation:** Intraoperative exploration revealed that the cranial (upper) mass was "highly vascularised, **initially twisted and leaking**."
*   **Pathologic Confirmation:** Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of a benign uterine leiomyoma with "areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle."

**Clinical Relevance:**
Residents must recognize that large uterine masses in pregnancy can mimic ovarian pathology or infectious processes. While conservative management is often preferred, the development of acute symptoms suggests mechanical complications such as torsion. Unlike infection, where peritoneal fluid is septic, inflammation here is sterile and driven by tissue necrosis. Early recognition and surgical detorsion, as performed here with uterine preservation, prevent further propagation of the inflammatory response and stabilize the patient.

### [Management] What specific clinical indications for surgical intervention during pregnancy take precedence over the risks of preterm labor and spontaneous pregnancy loss?

In the management of abdominal masses during pregnancy, the standard of care generally favors expectant management to avoid procedural stimulation of uterine contractions, hemorrhage, or spontaneous loss. However, surgical intervention becomes the indicated choice when the immediate threat to maternal or fetal viability outweighs these risks.

Based on the case reported by De Nola et al., three specific clinical indications regarding atypical complicated abdominal masses should prioritize surgical intervention:

1.  **Imminent Risk of Catastrophic Complication:** Surgical management is indicated when there is evidence of structural failure or impending torsion. In this case, the upper mass was identified as a pedunculated leiomyoma that was twisted. The torsion, evidenced by severe pain and signs of ischemia/neurosis (necrosis), places the mass at immediate risk of rupture and infection, threatening the pregnancy and maternal health.
2.  **Maternal Physiological Compromise:** When a mass causes significant morbidity that impairs fetal physiology, as seen in the present case, intervention is necessary. The patient developed dyspnea attributed to restrictive lung disease caused by the massive 18-cm subphrenic mass compressing the diaphragm. Without relief, this progressive compression risks fetal hypoxia.
3.  **Diagnostic Uncertainty and Oncologic Suspicion:** While myomas are common, imaging findings suggestive of malignancy warrant excision. This patient presented with elevating CA-125 levels despite benign markers and imaging features suggesting endometrioma or malignancy (solid/cystic components). When imaging and tumor markers create diagnostic ambiguity, the risk of a malignancy progressing to a metastatic state must be weighed against the risk of pregnancy loss.

The management principle derived from this case is that surgical decision-making must be multidisciplinary and grounded in the specific mortality/morbidity balance. In this instance, because the risks of non-intervention (rupture, hypoxia, diagnostic delay for potential malignancy) exceeded the risks of secondary preterm labor (mitigated by 21-week gestation, fetal monitoring, and progesterone prophylaxis), surgery was performed to preserve the pregnancy and ensure maternal safety.

### [Management] Why was a laparotomy associated with a xipho-pubic cut selected over a laparoscopic approach for the resection of the upper pedunculated mass at 21 weeks gestation?

In the management of symptomatic abdominal masses during pregnancy, the surgical approach must balance fetal safety, operative exposure, and maternal tissue preservation. In this case, we opted for a longitudinal xipho-pubic laparotomy over a laparoscopic approach based on three critical factors derived from the patient's presentation and the case description.

First, the sheer volume and location of the pathology dictated the approach. The report notes that "Due to the 'steric bulk' of the pregnant uterus and the huge masses," the initial diagnostic evaluation failed to identify the connective stalk or tumour-feeding vessel pre-operatively. The upper mass was 18 cm, extending into the subphrenic space. Laparoscopic management is generally reserved for "smaller volume of the mass," as noted in the discussion, whereas this case presented two massive lesions that would likely have obscured visualization and limited maneuverability for dissection and hemostasis of the twisted, vascularized pedicle.

Second, the patient's comorbid features increased operative risk with a minimally invasive approach. The patient had a BMIs of 33 (obese), which complicates port placement and intra-abdominal visualization. Furthermore, while the discussion acknowledges that laparoscopy may be feasible in the "early second trimester," the presence of "complicated masses" requiring rapid resolution of torsion, acute pain, and diagnostic uncertainty (distinguishing between pedunculated leiomyoma and endometrioma) favored the security of an open approach.

Finally, the primary clinical priority remained preserving the pregnancy while minimizing surgical time. The open incision allowed immediate, controlled access to the pedunculated mass which was "initially twisted and leaking," ensuring completion of the resection without excessive manipulation that could threaten fetal hemodynamic stability. The discussion highlights that "bias selection in preferring the laparotomy to laparoscopic approach... in the case of bigger... critical locations" is a common and appropriate decision when the risk of complications or difficulty outweighs the theoretical benefits of minimally invasive surgery.

### [Management] How did the size, number, and anatomical localization of the uterine masses influence the final decision regarding the choice of vaginal delivery versus C-section?

In this case, the decision to proceed with an elective cesarean delivery rather than vaginal birth was primarily dictated by the anatomical localization and size of the retained caudal mass. Although the patient presented with two large uterine masses, the clinical team chose to leave the lower lesion in situ to minimize obstetric risk. This caudal mass, measuring approximately 9 to 10 cm, was localized as a fixed, posterior intramural-subserosal myoma occupying the pouch of Douglas.

The final mode of delivery was determined by the mechanical impact of this specific lesion on the birth canal. As noted in the report, this mass "would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour." Its posterior-intramural location and substantial size prevented adequate engagement and descent of the fetus, rendering vaginal delivery unsafe. This outcome underscores a broader management principle: when multiple or large uterine masses are persistently present and fixed in a position that obstructs the pelvic inlet or canal, cesarean delivery is the necessary intervention to prevent dystocia and uterine rupture.

### [Clinical Pearls] According to current embryological and obstetrical safety guidelines, what specific protocols are required for fetal monitoring during non-obstetric abdominal surgery in the second trimester?

Management of non-obstetric abdominal surgery during the second trimester necessitates a strict protocol for fetal monitoring, specifically the mandatory documentation of the fetal heart rate before, during, and after the operative intervention. As referenced in this case report, this requirement is grounded in safety data from a large multicentric study (Zhao et al.) which underscores the imperativeness of verifying fetal cardiac activity throughout the entire surgical window. To operationalize this standard, the surgical team employed serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds to monitor fetal viability continuously during the procedure. These monitoring protocols are integral to a comprehensive safety strategy that includes utilizing non-contrast MRI for preoperative planning to avoid gadolinium teratogenicity and administering intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis to support pregnancy maintenance against surgical stress. Adherence to these guidelines is critical for minimizing the risks of preterm labor, preterm delivery, and fetal loss during second-trimester maternal surgery.
