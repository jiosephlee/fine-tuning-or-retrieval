Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Context and Pathophysiology of Exaggerated Growth

Understanding the natural history of uterine leiomyomas is essential when evaluating women who present with abdominal enlargement during pregnancy. During the second and third trimesters, intramural leiomyomas typically undergo a rapid, non-linear growth phase due to their high sensitivity to estrogen and progesterone. Research indicates that the size of these masses increases by approximately 6.90% per week between the first and second trimesters, driven by proliferative hormonal signaling. This physiological phenomenon explains the clinical dilemma presented in this case: large masses identified at 21 weeks gestation are not static lesions but rapidly expanding entities. The observed "enlargement of the uterus more than expected for gestational age" in the patient's second trimester is a classic sign of exacerbated leiomyomatous growth, which must be distinguished from synchronous uterine fundal height consistent with fetal growth. Without accounting for this rapid expansion, clinicians might misattribute the mass effect to lymphadenopathy or gastrointestinal pathology, delaying life-threatening interventions for respiratory compromise.

Diagnosis and risk stratification in this context are complicated by advanced maternal age and the potential for concurrent pathologies. A patient in her 40s presents a unique differential profile where new-onset pelvic or abdominal masses in the second trimester warrant a heightened index of suspicion for malignancy, specifically ovarian or uterine adenocarcinoma, compared to younger nulliparous women. However, as demonstrated in the current case, the elevated tumor marker CA-125 (41.8 U/mL) must be interpreted within the physiological context of pregnancy, which causes transient, normal elevations of this marker. While a rising trend or levels significantly exceeding the second-trimester median (14.0 U/mL) may raise concern for endometriosis or adenomyosis, the elevated level does not independently confirm malignancy. This necessitates a multidisciplinary approach to differentiate between benign degenerating leiomyomas, as seen in the caudal mass with cystic degeneration, and neoplastic processes, guiding the decision to proceed with surgery or expectant management.

Clinical assessment and diagnostic accuracy are further challenged by patient-specific factors such as obesity, which directly impacts physical examination reliability. In this case, a body mass index of 33 contributed significantly to the difficulty in differentiating the massive abdomen from adhesions or bowel distension on palpation. Physical examination alone revealed a tender swelling and a taut-elastic mass but failed to precisely characterize the depth or origin of the lesions. Consequently, the reliance on non-invasive imaging, specifically transvaginal ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, becomes paramount. The contrast between the clinical impression of a simple abdominal distension and the actual discovery of complex, adherent masses highlights a key learning point: in obese pregnant patients, imaging is not merely adjunctive but diagnostic, as physical examination often lacks the resolution to assess the subphrenic or retroperitoneal extent of large masses.

The development of dyspnea and abdominal pain in this scenario is primarily a function of mechanical compression rather than systemic pulmonary or physiological derangement. Large subphrenic and pelvic masses physically displace the diaphragm upward, reducing functional residual capacity and causing restrictive lung disease. The patient’s specific presentation of acute abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and associated dyspnea at 21 weeks correlates with the upper mass occupying the entire left subphrenic space. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for initial stabilization; analgesic therapies and positional changes were employed to mitigate diaphragmatic compression and pain, yet the underlying pathology required surgical resolution to prevent complications such as torsion, rupture, or infection. Recognizing that these symptoms are often mechanical ensures the surgical team prepares for potential hemodynamic shifts and respiratory restriction upon resection of the extruded mass.

Finally, baseline obstetric considerations for a nulliparous patient in the second trimester dictate the management trajectory of the pregnancy. At 21 weeks of gestation with a high-percentage fetal growth (86th percentile for gestational age), the goal is to preserve the fetus while monitoring for preterm delivery triggers. However, the presence of a large pelvic mass, specifically the caudal lesion found to be fixed and intramural-subserosal, alters the normal partograph for delivery. Nulliparous patients with significant posterior uterine masses often face imminent obstruction during the expulsive phase of labor, rendering vaginal delivery risky if the mass does not regress. This necessitates a primary obstetric plan for elective caesarean section at 37 weeks, balancing the risk of spontaneous preterm labor against the safety of surgical delivery, ensuring optimal neonatal outcome while managing the lingering mass effect.

## Diagnostic Evaluation: Imaging and Laboratory Interpretation

The diagnostic evaluation of abdominal masses in pregnancy requires a stratified approach that prioritizes maternal safety, fetal welfare, and diagnostic accuracy. In this case, the patient’s physical examination presented unique challenges; an obese patient (BMI 33) with a significantly enlarged abdomen made palpation difficult, initially classifying the upper mass as a taut-elastic swelling occupying the left subphrenic region. While a bimanual exam identified a fixed, hard mass in the lesser pelvis bulging at the posterior fornix, the operator had clinical reasons to suspect masses larger than the fetus, prompting advanced imaging. Ultrasound served as the first-line diagnostic modality due to its safety profile and ability to differentiate cystic from solid components. The scan revealed two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularity on Doppler. While the upper mass extended from the left subphrenic region to the fundus, the lower mass appeared potentially adherent to a cystic degenerating myoma. Although ultrasound provided initial localization, its sensitivity is limited by maternal habitus and the presence of gas, making the distinction between adnexal pathology and uterine phenomena less definitive without further confirmation.

Following the ultrasound results, a non-contrast Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) was ordered to clarify the anatomical origin and nature of the lesions. MRI was chosen despite the standard avoidance of gadolinium in pregnancy to resolve specific uncertainties regarding the wall thickness and tissue content. The T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted images demonstrated thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content, and focal hyperintense solid components. Crucially, the study provided high-resolution detail without placental exposure to gadolinium, which is associated with potentially adverse fetal outcomes unless strictly necessary. This imaging modality offered the superior diagnostic accuracy required to evaluate multiple, large, intramural, or posterior-labial conditions, as suggested by the report's background literature. The MRI findings helped differentiate the masses from simple ovarian cysts by highlighting continuous tissue planes between the lower mass and the posterior intramural-myomatous wall of the uterus, which was not as clearly resolved on ultrasound.

Laboratory evaluation in this context requires careful interpretation of tumor markers, specifically CA-125. In the second trimester, serum CA-125 levels physiologically rise and subsequently normalize, reaching medians significantly higher than non-pregnant baseline values. An isolated measurement of 41.8 U/mL in this patient exceeded the laboratory cut-off for malignancy but remained within the physiological elevation range for pregnancy (median 14.0 U/mL, though variable with stress or inflammation). The slight increasing trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior correlated with clinical symptoms rather than providing standalone evidence of a neoplasm. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team interpreted this marker elevation as a supportive, rather than diagnostic, finding. The differential diagnosis remained broad, focusing on decidualized endometriomas—which are known to elevate CA-125—and potentially complicated leiomyomas.

Ultimately, the diagnosis relied on synthesizing imaging morphology, physiological lab trends, and physical findings to orient the surgical strategy. Radiological features of thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid suggested complex cysts, potentially mimicking decidualized endometriomas or cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The continuity of the caudal mass with the uterine tissue was the decisive factor; while the upper mass appeared pedunculated, the lower mass was inferred to originate from the uterine intramural-subserosal wall. This integration of data identified the masses not as separate ovarian adnexal tumors, but as uterine phenomena masquerading as abdominal masses. Recognizing the risk of tumor origin, torsion, or infection, the team prioritized the need for a definitive diagnosis and symptom relief, leading to the laparotomy where the masses were confirmed as benign leiomyomas with cystic degeneration and pedication.

## Risk Stratification and Therapeutic Decision-Making

The decision to intervene surgically during pregnancy represents a critical pivot point in obstetric management, requiring a delicate balance between maternal safety and fetal viability. In the context of atypical abdominal masses, expectant management is often the preferred strategy to avoid disrupting uterine integrity; however, the window for intervention narrows when symptomatic deterioration occurs. In this case, the patient’s initial presentation of severe acute abdominal pain (Visual Analogue Scale=5) and restrictive dyspnoea at 21 weeks gestation shifted the risk stratification from conservative observation toward operative management. This illustrates the general principle that surgery during pregnancy is reserved for cases where the risk of maternal decompensation—such as hemorrhage, perforation, or infection—outweighs the procedural risks of preterm labor or uterine contraction. The necessity for operation was not solely driven by a diagnosis, but by the clinical trajectory: worsening pain, signs of systemic inflammation including low-grade fever, and circulatory compromise indicative of organ pressure or potential torsion.

Evaluating the risk of torsion in pedunculated masses is a decisive factor in this surgical calculus, as mechanical twisting can lead to rapid ischemia and necrosis. While uncomplicated leiomyomas or cystic masses often warrant monitoring, a pedunculated structure with a compromised stalk presents an acute threat. In this scenario, the upper mass was identified as a huge, vascularised, and highly pedunculated leiomyoma. The clinical presentation of fever and acute pain, combined with intraoperative findings of a twisted stalk and leaking tissue, confirmed the diagnosis of torsion with subsequent ischemic changes. This finding justified the immediate excision of the cranial mass to prevent the catastrophic release of necrotic tissue contents and potential sepsis, while sparing the uterus from further manipulation. It underscores the importance of imaging characterization; the identification of a stalk and its relationship to the uterine wall via MRI was essential in distinguishing between a benign intramural component and a high-risk pedunculated ligament.

The timing of surgery further reflects the obstetric constraints inherent in the second trimester of gestation. This period offers a "therapeutic window" where the risk of inducing spontaneous miscarriage or preterm labor is lower than in the first trimester, yet fetal size does not yet present the severe mechanical constraints seen in the third trimester. The patient’s gestational age of 21+6 weeks supported the decision for an elective ap-proach rather than immediate rescue surgery; waiting for the third trimester would have increased the risk of spontaneous preterm delivery due to uterine crowding. Conversely, delaying beyond the second trimester could have resulted in invasive molding of the fetal head against the mass, complicating future delivery. The multidisciplinary team recognized that the masses were occupying critical space (occupying the left subphrenic space and pouch of Douglas), which limited uterine growth and caused dyspnoea. Thus, the timing was chosen to address the respiratory and obstructive pathology before fetal growth exacerbated the steric bulk, preserving the conditions for a viable pregnancy duration.

Effective risk stratification relies heavily on a coordinated Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) approach. The management of this patient involved close collaboration between Obstetricians, Gynecologists, and Surgeons, as evidenced by the transfer to the Gynaecological Division and the execution of a complex laparotomy. Surgery in pregnancy is not purely an obstetrical procedure; it requires surgical precision to avoid accidental rupture of decidua or intra-abdominal structures while maintaining fetal heart rate monitoring. In this case, the team utilized serial transvaginal ultrasounds to monitor fetal viability during the intervention and administered progesterone prophylaxis. The MDT assessment allowed for a "demolition" strategy limited to the problematic upper mass, while leaving the fixed caudal mass in situ to minimize iterative uterine incision risks. This selective intervention highlights the goal of preserving pregnancy and avoiding overtreatment, adhering to the broader principle that surgical management should aim to render the pregnancy safer rather than extract it, provided the immediate threat is neutralizable.

Finally, the Informed Consent process in such involves high-risk scenarios must comprehensively address the spectrum of potential adverse outcomes. While the case report documents that informed consent was obtained before the laparotomy, the foundation of such a discussion must inherently cover risks specific to uterine surgery, including uterine rupture, severe postoperative bleeding requiring transfusion or hysterectomy, and the increased likelihood of preterm delivery. In the postoperative period, the residual caudal mass necessitated an elective caesarean delivery at 37 weeks to prevent obstructed labour or placental abruption during a vaginal attempt. This progression demonstrates that the consent given at the time of the initial emergency—watching for complications—was followed by a revised plan based on anatomical constraints. The patient and team weighed the long-term obstetric implications of the residual scar against the immediate relief gained from surgery. Ultimately, the successful neonatal outcome and maternal recovery validate that the risks of surgical intervention were appropriately weighed against the prolonged risks of expectant management in a case of massive symptomatic burden.

## Surgical Technique and Intraoperative Obstetric Safety

The surgical management of atypical abdominal masses during pregnancy requires a balance between diagnostic necessity, maternal safety, and fetal preservation. In the context of this case, the clinical team selected a laparotomy for the intervention rather than the minimally invasive laparoscopic approach. While the discussion section notes that laparoscopic myomectomy is often preferred in the first trimester or for smaller, non-intramural masses to reduce maternal morbidity, the decision for a laparotomy was dictated by the extreme size, complex intramural location, and critical anatomical proximity of the masses to the uterine isthmic site in this specific instance. The procedure utilized a longitudinal xiphos-pubic incision, chosen to provide the wide exposure necessary for safe resection of the pedunculated tumors and repair of large vascular structures while accommodating the gravid uterus. Throughout the procedure, obstetric safety protocols were central to the team’s actions. The multidisciplinary group mandated the verification of fetal cardiac activity before, during, and after surgery. This high level of surveillance was supported by the administration of intravenous progesterone prophylaxis, initiated intraoperatively and continued for the postoperative period, to mitigate the risk of uterine contractions triggered by surgical stress or manipulation.

The specific technique for resecting the pedunculated leiomyoma required meticulous vascular control to manage the significant blood loss potential associated with these highly vascularized growths. Upon exploration, the cranial mass was identified as a large, twisted, and leaking pedunculated leiomyoma, a presentation that explained the patient’s acute onset of fever and severe pain. The surgical team excised the mass from its uterine implant gently using a double clamp to secure the stalk, ensuring hemostasis immediately prior to division. Once the stalk was cut, the exposed root of the uterine myoma was carefully closed using a single absorbable suture. This method of manual clamping and ligation avoided the need for complex stapling devices in this setting. However, the vascularity of the lesion remained a significant intraoperative and postoperative challenge; despite the clamping technique, the patient’s hemoglobin levels deteriorated significantly post-operatively, necessitating blood transfusions and intravenous iron supplementation. This highlights that mechanical vascular control, while essential, does not guarantee the preservation of blood volume in degenerating, necrotic myomas.

Crucial to the success of this case was the strategic decision regarding which masses to resect versus which to observe. While the upper pedunculated mass presented an immediate risk of torsion, rupture, or obstruction due to its twisting nature, the caudal mass was left in situ. The surgical team determined that operating on the fixed pelvic mass would not outweigh the risks of worsening intrauterine stress, potential adhesions, or uterine damage. The goal was to minimize obstetric complications and avoid overtreatment, particularly since the caudal mass was identified as a uterine leiomyoma with cystic degeneration. Had the caudal mass been removed, extensive adhesiolysis could have been performed; however, leaving it in place maintained the integrity of the remaining uterine wall. This was an interventional decision based on the understanding that leaving the mass would not impede recovery and could be managed through a planned mode of delivery, which was subsequently planned as an elective caesarean section.

Final intraoperative and immediate postoperative safety was ensured through careful hemodynamic monitoring and prophylactic antibiotic and pain management protocols. The patient received antibiotic prophylaxis regarding *Klebsiella pneumoniae* identified in urine cultures pre-operatively, minimizing the risk of septic complications during the procedure. Additionally, progesterone prophylaxis was continued until discharge to maintain fetal stability during the critical healing phase. The placement of the caesarean section on the same surgical scar in the third trimester at 37 weeks allowed for a controlled delivery without attempting vaginal birth, anticipating that the residual fibroids would obstruct the fetal head. This comprehensive approach—combining prudent surgical selection, precise vascular management of the resected mass, conservative management of the residual mass, and rigorous fetal monitoring—resulted in an excellent neonatal outcome and preserved the maternal pregnancy until term.

## Perinatal Outcomes, Delivery Planning, and Long-Term Resolution

The decision for the mode of delivery in this case was dictated by the presence of the large, caudal residual myoma remaining in the pelvic cavity following the initial 21-week laparotomy. Although the patient underwent a successful myomectomy for the symptomatic superior pedunculated mass, the inferior mass was deliberately left in situ to mitigate immediate risks such as uterine rupture and pregnancy loss. Consequently, the retained caudal mass, characterized as a large cystic intramural-subserosal lesion, presented a significant mechanical obstacle for fetal descent. As the uterus enlarges during the third trimester, a mass fixed to the posterior wall or located near the contractile ring acts as a ball-valve obstruction, preventing the engagement of the fetal head and posing a high risk of uterine rupture during the second stage of labor. Therefore, vaginal delivery was contraindicated despite the absence of fetal distress, necessitating an elective cesarean section. This approach prioritized maternal safety and fetal well-being by avoiding the unpredictable dynamics of an obstructed labor associated with such significant anatomical distortion.

Timing of the antepartum cesarean section required a careful balance between fetal lung maturity and uterine stabilization to prevent acute decompensation. In this scenario, the multidisciplinary team elected to perform the delivery at 37 weeks of gestation. While term delivery often occurs at 39 weeks, the 37-week mark offered a.window where the risk of spontaneous preterm labor—and the potential hemorrhagic complications associated with large, vascular masses—could be controlled in a surgical setting. Additionally, deferring delivery beyond this point increases the physiological stress on the mother as the uterine muscles stretch around the mass, raising the likelihood of necrosis or torsion. By scheduling the delivery at 37 weeks, the team ensured fetal viability and pulmonary maturity while capitalizing on the physiological improvements in myoma size that typically accompany the transition from pregnancy to the postpartum puerperium.

Following the initial laparotomy and leading up to the term delivery, the patient’s maternal recovery was complicated by significant anemia and a secondary urinary tract infection, both of which were critical to managing the pregnancy trajectory. During hospitalization, the patient experienced a drop in hemoglobin to 7.5 g/L, necessitating intravenous iron supplementation and two blood transfusions to restore oxygen-carrying capacity prior to discharge. These transfusions were vital to support fetal development during the subsequent 16-week interval. Simultaneously, the detection of *Klebsiella pneumoniae* in urine samples prompted the administration of specific antibiotic prophylaxis with cefazolin and clarithromycin, ensuring that the surgical site remained free of infection without compromising the pregnancy. This meticoulus recovery protocol allowed for the successful progression to a 37-week gestational age without obstetric complications despite the presence of large, retained abdominal masses.

In the long-term resolution, the postpartum management focused on the natural regression of uterine leiomyomas and the patient’s reproductive goals. Uterine leiomyomas typically undergo significant involution following delivery, with studies indicating a mean reduction in size of approximately 44% by six weeks postpartum, driven by the decrease in hormonal stimulation that maintained the mass during the third trimester. In this case, the residual myoma measured 7.5 cm postpartum, reflecting this physiological regression and a favorable outcome without symptoms. Given the patient's advanced maternal age and successful lactation, the decision was made to delay any future intervention. Conservative management is preferred in the postpartum period for symptomatic masses to preserve endometrial function and allow for spontaneous resolution of hormonal-driven growth, provided the mass remains asymptomatic. Future surgical steps remain unscheduled, demonstrating a multidisciplinary consultation that prioritized maternal recovery and breastfeeding success over immediate definitive control of benign pathology.
