Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Section 1: The Clinical Mask – Recognizing Atypical Thoracic Presentations

In many clinical scenarios, the origin of respiratory distress is assumed to be primary pulmonary. However, Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF) presents a significant diagnostic challenge by masquerading as common thoracic pathologies. This case illustrates the "pulmonary mask" phenomenon, where a pediatric patient presents with recurrent pleural effusions, chest pain, and respiratory distress that, due to their location and nature, initially direct the clinician toward thoracic etiologies. The patient presented with a progressive escalation of dyspnea and right-sided chest pain over a week, accompanied by objective signs of significant fluid accumulation in the pleural space. Physical examination revealed tachypnea with mild respiratory distress, right-sided stony dull percussion, and decreased vocal fremitus and breath sounds extending from the subscapular to the axillary regions. These findings are indistinguishable from community-acquired pneumonia, tuberculous pleuritis, or empyema, prompting initial concern for primary lung pathology rather than an abdominal source.

The diagnostic complexity is often heightened by epidemiological and social factors. In this instance, the mother reported a history of Tuberculosis contact with the patient's grandfather, leading to a strong initial suspicion of pulmonary Koch's. Upon the second admission for recurrent effusions, the Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid was slightly elevated, further reinforcing the diagnostic distraction toward tuberculosis despite the patient's lack of fever or chronic weight loss. Consequently, the child was empirically started on antitubercular therapy without suspecting a pancreatic origin. This highlights a crucial learning point: while pleural fluid ADA is a diagnostic marker for tuberculosis, significantly elevated pleural fluid amylase—specifically pancreatic-type amylase—is the definitive indicator for PPF, even when other features suggest infection. The presence of elevated amylase in the pleural fluid should always prompt a search for an intra-abdominal cause, particularly when the patient is an otherwise healthy child who is not responding to standard pulmonary therapies.

The definitive clue to the diagnosis lay not solely in the initial imaging but in a comprehensive review of the history, specifically regarding trauma. The patient had previously suffered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle strike one month prior, a detail he concealed due to school bullying. This concealment delayed the correct diagnosis for over a month, during which time the child underwent multiple thoracenteses. These procedures provided temporary relief; the first thoracocentesis drained approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid which resulted in immediate improvement of oxygen saturation and resolution of chest pain. However, the recurrence of the effusion six days later signaled that the pleural tap had addressed the symptom, not the underlying pathology, necessitating a shift in diagnostic reasoning from infectious to structural etiology.

Finally, the initial therapeutic response serves as a heuristic for further investigation while awaiting definitive imaging. The transient improvement following thoracocentesis in a patient with recurrent "blood" fluid should raise a red flag for an exudative cause with high enzyme content, rather than a simple serous effusion. Furthermore, the consistency of the fluid—dark red blood-stained and consistent across admissions—should distinguish it from the serosanguinous exudate typical of empyema. The realization that the pathology was linked to the abdominal trauma, rather than respiratory infection, compelled the medical team to order a contrast-enhanced CT and MRCP. This case demonstrates that unrecognized abdominal trauma can manifest as a thoracic emergency, and maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when pulmonary workup fails is essential for avoiding unnecessary invasive pulmonary interventions and preventing the morbidity associated with missed PPF diagnoses.

## Section 2: Diagnostic Reasoning and Imaging – Tracing the Fluid to its Source

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the necessity of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when respiratory distress presents with atypical findings. The patient’s initial presentation of recurrent right-sided pleural effusions and respiratory distress prompted a standard thoracic workup, including chest radiography and ultrasound. While fluid was aspirated three times to relieve distress, the dark red color and severity of the fluid suggested a more complex etiology than simple atelectasis or pneumonia. In a pediatric patient with recurrent hemorrhagic effusions, the clinical reasoning must be susceptible to the possibility that the primary pathology may not be pulmonary, despite the thoracic symptoms. In this specific instance, the consistent appearance of dark, bloody fluid alongside the temporary improvement after aspiration established a pattern of a thoraco-abdominal communication, requiring an expansion of the diagnostic scope beyond the chest.

The investigation moved forward through pleural fluid analysis, which serves as the critical pivot point in establishing pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) compared to other causes of exudative pleural effusion. While the dark, bloody nature of the fluid initially raised concern for malignancy or tuberculosis, the biochemistry provided a definitive clue. In this patient, pleural fluid amylase was markedly elevated at 6118 U/L, and lipase was elevated at 7211 U/L. Diagnostic standards generally consider pleural fluid amylase levels exceeding 1000 IU/L to be pathognomonic for PPF. This finding effectively shifted the clinical suspicion from primary lung pathology to pancreatic disease, even though the patient had no initial signs of abdominal pain. To further solidify the diagnosis, the pleural fluid amylase typing should be considered. Although specifically reported in this case that elevated amylase indicates pancreatic leakage, pleural fluid amylase typing distinguishes pancreatic-type amylase seen in PPF from salivary-type amylase found in other conditions such as tuberculosis or pancreatic-cancer metastasis that might present with high, non-specific amylase levels.

However, a definitive diagnosis requires ruling out common mimics before confirming PPF. This patient initially received antitubercular therapy due to family contact with "Koch's" disease and slightly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels. The decision to stop these medications and pivot to a surgical diagnosis relied on a systematic exclusion of alternatives. Malignancy was investigated through pleural fluid cytology and peripheral blood smears, both of which were negative, correlating with the absence of weight loss or fever. Empyema and epihoria were ruled out due to the lack of neutrophilic exudate and the absence of fever. Furthermore, the history of recurrent effusions was consistent with chronic fluid leakage rather than acute infection. While Gleis’s report and clinical guidelines suggest that malignancies or Boerhaave’s syndrome can also present with amylase-rich fluid, others were excluded based on normal renal/liver imaging and the failure to visualize abdominal organ migration into the chest, consistent with the patient's normal bowel movements and lack of vomiting.

Once thoracic differentials were excluded, advanced cross-sectional imaging was required to map the trajectory of the fistula. A contrast-enhanced CT scan served as the first modality, revealing a hydropneumothorax and, crucially, a multiloculated hypodense collection. The CT scan visualized the collection extending from the right paraesophageal region tracking down toward the tail of the pancreas. This finding provided the macroscopic evidence needed to hypothesize a pancreatic origin prior to the specific ductal visualization. While CT is excellent at detecting serous collections or pseudocysts, with high sensitivity in the retroperitoneum, it was less specific in defining the acute communication channel without the use of contrast flow dynamics. To precisely locate the anatomical connection between the pancreatic duct and the pleural cavity, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was the preferred modality.

MRCP provided the definitive diagnostic confirmation by visualizing the exact anatomy of the lesion. It confirmed the presence of tubular cystic collections in the distal body and tail of the pancreas and explicitly demonstrated a narrow communication with the left pleural cavity as well as the right, with the right communication being more significant. The imaging revealed that the communication extended through the oesophageal hiatus, tracking superiorly from the pancreatic duct into the pleural space. This finding confirmed the "leak" of pancreatic juice through the diaphragm rather than direct lung or pleural pathology. By integrating the high pleural fluid enzymatic markers with the specific radiological tracking of fluid from the pancreas to the pleura, the clinicians were able to distinguish PPF from other rare causes of hemorrhagic effusion. Ultimately, the integration of fluid biochemistry and advanced trajectory imaging allowed for the cessation of unnecessary pulmonary interventions and the initiation of targeted medical management.

## Section 3: Pathophysiology and Etiology – Mechanisms of Fistulization in Pediatrics

A pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a pathological communication between the pancreatic ductal system and the pleural cavity, effectively turning a gastrointestinal leak into a thoracic problem. Diagnostically, this entity presents a significant challenge because the clinical manifestations are predominantly thoracic, often misleading clinicians like the initial treating team of this case who suspected pulmonary tuberculosis or malignancy. In the reported child, the presentation of recurrent, massive, hemoptoic pleural effusions mimicked common pediatric respiratory infections or infections, delaying the abdominal foreign trauma history as the primary suspect. Emergent thoracocentesis in such cases reveals inflammatory, often blood-stained fluid, yet the underlying pathology arises from the disruption of the pancreatic duct. To understand the consequences of this rupture, one must visualize the anatomical pathways involved. The pancreas is retroperitoneal, but its posterior surface lies in close proximity to the diaphragm. When the pancreatic parenchyma or duct is injured, the released fluid tracks through the path of least resistance. Typically, a posterior leakage allows pancreatic juices to enter the retroperitoneal space, from whence they migrate superiorly through the natural diaphragmatic hiatuses—most commonly the aortic or esophageal hiatus—into the mediastinum and ultimately breach the pleural space to form an effusion. As seen in the magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography of this case, the fluid collection was noted tracking from the right paraesophageal level through the right pleural space to the tail of the pancreas, confirming the anatomical route from the retroperitoneum to the pleural cavity.

The etiology of PPF differs significantly between pediatric and adult populations, which is crucial for case formulation and history taking. In adults, the vast majority of cases are attributed to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and ductal hypertension resulting from alcohol consumption. In contrast, pediatric cases are predominantly non-alcoholic, arising primarily from two mechanisms: blunt abdominal trauma and genetic predispositions to pancreatitis. As illustrated in this specific report, the patient suffered a bicycle handle injury one month prior to presentation, establishing the baseline trauma which precipitated the acute pancreatitis and subsequent ductal rupture. Trauma complicates pediatric pancreatitis significantly, as the healing tissues in the pediatrics population may be more prone to spontaneous disruption or pseudocyst formation following a hematoma. Furthermore, pediatric chronic pancreatitis, when not traumatic, is often linked to genetic mutations. Approximately 30% of pediatric cases involve mutations in genes such as *SPINK1* (serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1) or *PRSS1* (cationic trypsinogen), which predispose younger patients to recurrent pancreatitis. While adult cases are managed with an eye toward alcohol cessation, diagnosing a child with PPF requires a high index of suspicion for blunt trauma history—often uncovered only after repeated inquiry—and exclusion of familial genetic causes in cases of chronic recurrence.

Understanding the pathophysiology of fistula closure informs the conservative management strategy employed in this case. The fistula acts as a "leaky pipe," continuously allowing high-pressure pancreatic fluid to escape into the pleural cavity. The driving force behind the effusion is the intraductal pressure. Conservative management aims to reduce this pressure to allow spontaneous sealing of the fistula tract. Initiating bowel rest, as seen with the patient on nil per oral and total parenteral nutrition, eliminates the intestinal stimulation that triggers pancreatic enzyme release. Concurrently, the administration of somatostatin analogues, such as Octreotide, directly inhibits exocrine pancreatic secretion, lowering the volume and pressure of the fluid accumulating in the duct. Over the ten-day course in this case, the combination of drainage via intercostal catheter and medical suppression allowed the positive pressure to dissipate, enabling the fistula closure. This physiological understanding underscores why thoracocentesis alone is insufficient for long-term resolution, as continued secretion prevents the tract from healing. Furthermore, the diaphragm serves as the anatomical gateway; because the esophageal hiatus is a natural opening that accommodates the esophagus, it is a frequent route for the fistula without requiring direct diaphragmatic erosion unless the fluid volume is extremely high. Recognizing these mechanisms helps the clinician differentiate PPF from other causes of amylase-rich pleural fluid and guides the transition from aggressive thoracic workups to targeted abdominal imaging and medical support.

## Section 4: Management Strategies – Conservative, Endoscopic, and Surgical Approaches

The initial management of a Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF) focuses on stabilizing respiratory status and controlling the pleural leak through a stepwise approach, prioritizing conservative medical therapy. In the immediate setting, the priority is correcting hypoxia and reducing the volume of pleural fluid to restore lung expansion. This patient presented with significant tachypnea and hypoxia, necessitating admission to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and oxygen supplementation via mask. Given the recurrent nature of the effusions and the restrictive nature of the accumulated fluid, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted to facilitate continuous, sterile decompression of the right pleural space. The dark red nature of the drained fluid and the stabilization of oxygen saturation post-aspiration highlight the immediate benefit of mechanical drainage, but long-term control depends on cessation of the pancreatic fistula output.

Conservative medical management forms the foundation of therapy, with the primary goal being the induction of pancreatic rest to allow the disrupted ductal connection to heal. This protocol mandates a strict nil per os (NPO) status to prevent any enteral stimulation that could drive further pancreatic secretion. To maintain the patient’s metabolic requirements without stimulating the gut, Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN) is instituted, as demonstrated in this case where feeding was bypassed entirely to reduce exocrine output. Additionally, Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy (PERT) is often utilized to aid digestion during the fasting period, ensuring close nutritional surveillance prevents catabolism while the fistula closes.

Pharmacological intervention plays a critical adjunctive role in suppressing the volume of pancreatic fluid production. Somatostatin analogues, specifically Octreotide, are used to inhibit the exocrine function of the pancreas, thereby reducing fistulous flow. In this clinical scenario, Octreotide was administered at an initial dose of 30 µg/kg/day, a dosage consistent with the preferred therapeutic range of 10–40 µg/kg/day found in pediatric management literature. This pharmacotherapy acts synergistically with bowel rest to lower intraductal pressure and minimize the volume of leakage entering the pleural cavity.

The decision to escalate to endoscopic or surgical intervention, such as ERCP stenting or distal pancreatectomy, is dictated by the patient's response to conservative therapy. Ideally, drainage output from the intercostal drain serves as the primary biomarker for success; a progressive decrease in fluid volume over time indicates effective closure of the fistula. While case series suggest conservative measures may be required for a standard duration of 2–3 weeks, successful outcomes can occur sooner with correct management. In this case, pleural drainage ceased completely within ten days, allowing for the removal of the intercostal drain and hospital discharge. However, if drain output remains high or respiratory collapse persists after the initial trial period, endoscopic stenting to divert flow or surgical intervention becomes necessary. This case illustrates that when conservative measures are applied rigorously, including nutritional support, bowel rest, and somatostatin analogues, less invasive management is often sufficient, avoiding the higher morbidity associated with surgical resection.

## Section 5: Clinical Significance and Long-term Prognosis

Diagnostic delays in the context of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) typically arise when clinicians fixate on primary thoracic pathology due to the prominence of respiratory distress and pleural symptoms. This case underscores the critical importance of identifying the "pulmonary mask" phenomenon, where abdominal origins of disease masquerade as thoracic emergencies. The child presented with classic signs of pleural effusion, including tachypnea and dullness to percussion, leading to initial workups for tuberculosis and malignancy. In a high-quality pediatric thoracic workup, the accumulation of pleural fluid amylase and lipase is the pivotal differentiator for PPF. As demonstrated in this report, serum pleural fluid amylase levels exceeding 1000 U/L in the presence of a traumatic history should immediately shift the diagnostic focus from the chest to the abdomen. Clinicians must remain vigilant for a history of blunt abdominal trauma, even when presented as a minor accident or when the history is obscured by patient non-disclosure, as seen in this case where the child withheld information regarding a bicycle handle injury. Failure to obtain a detailed social history and thorough physichial examination may perpetuate misdiagnosis, leading to unnecessary procedures and increased morbidity.

Regarding long-term prognosis and clinical management, the successful outcome of this case highlights the potential for conservative resolution in pediatric PPF when the pancreatic ductal anatomy allows. While surgical intervention remains an option for refractory cases, the priority in the pediatric population is to preserve pancreatic function and minimize operative sepsis if possible. The application of total parenteral nutrition, nil per os status, and the use of somatostatin analogues (Octreotide) facilitated the closure of the fistula tract over a period of ten days, eventually allowing the removal of the intercostal drain. The child’s discharge marked a return to baseline activity, successfully attending school and engaging in strenuous exercise without recurrence. Long-term follow-up should therefore involve periodic monitoring for recurrent pleural effusions, but it must also address the psychosocial implications of the injury. In this instance, the diagnosis was delayed partly due to social factors, including bullying that prevented the patient from disclosing the trauma. Consequently, follow-up care for pediatric PPF should encompass a multidisciplinary approach that includes psychological support to encourage future disclosure of abdominal injuries and to ensure the child adapts to a balanced diet and lifestyle modifications that reduce pancreatic stress.
