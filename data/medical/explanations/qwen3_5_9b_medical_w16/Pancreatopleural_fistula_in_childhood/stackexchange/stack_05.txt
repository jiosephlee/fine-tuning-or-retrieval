Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Management] If conservative medical management is unsuccessful in closing the fistula, what is the preferred second-line endoscopic intervention and what is the subsequent surgical backup?

If conservative medical management—comprising bowel rest, nutritional support, and somatostatin analogues like octreotide—is unsuccessful in closing the pancreatopleural fistula, the preferred second-line intervention is endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with trans-papillary stenting. As detailed in our discussion, conservative therapy succeeds in only roughly 12% of pediatric cases. ERCP serves a therapeutic rather than purely diagnostic role here; by placing a stent across the breach or at the leak site, the procedure significantly reduces intraductal pressure, effectively sealing the fistula. In the literature cited within our case review, this endoscopic approach yielded a 100% success rate in pediatric cohorts, with stents typically required for 4 to 6 months and fistulas resolving within 2 to 3 weeks.

Should endoscopic stenting be anatomically impossible due to severe ductal obstruction or left-sided or distally located fistulas, surgery becomes the essential backup. The surgical options described include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure), or cystojejunostomy. This surgical armamentarium demonstrated a 93% success rate in pediatric surgical series with less than 5% operative mortality. The clinical teaching point is to recognize the "leaky pipe" pathophysiology: try to control the leak via pressure reduction (medical/endoscopic) before removing the source (surgical). While our patient resolved with first-line medical management alone, this tiered approach—Medical → Endoscopic → Surgical—remains the standard management hierarchy for pancreatopleural fistulas.
