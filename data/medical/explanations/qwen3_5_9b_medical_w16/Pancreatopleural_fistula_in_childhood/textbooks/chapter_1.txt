Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 1: ## Section 1: The Clinical Mask – Recognizing Atypical Thoracic Presentations

In many clinical scenarios, the origin of respiratory distress is assumed to be primary pulmonary. However, Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF) presents a significant diagnostic challenge by masquerading as common thoracic pathologies. This case illustrates the "pulmonary mask" phenomenon, where a pediatric patient presents with recurrent pleural effusions, chest pain, and respiratory distress that, due to their location and nature, initially direct the clinician toward thoracic etiologies. The patient presented with a progressive escalation of dyspnea and right-sided chest pain over a week, accompanied by objective signs of significant fluid accumulation in the pleural space. Physical examination revealed tachypnea with mild respiratory distress, right-sided stony dull percussion, and decreased vocal fremitus and breath sounds extending from the subscapular to the axillary regions. These findings are indistinguishable from community-acquired pneumonia, tuberculous pleuritis, or empyema, prompting initial concern for primary lung pathology rather than an abdominal source.

The diagnostic complexity is often heightened by epidemiological and social factors. In this instance, the mother reported a history of Tuberculosis contact with the patient's grandfather, leading to a strong initial suspicion of pulmonary Koch's. Upon the second admission for recurrent effusions, the Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid was slightly elevated, further reinforcing the diagnostic distraction toward tuberculosis despite the patient's lack of fever or chronic weight loss. Consequently, the child was empirically started on antitubercular therapy without suspecting a pancreatic origin. This highlights a crucial learning point: while pleural fluid ADA is a diagnostic marker for tuberculosis, significantly elevated pleural fluid amylase—specifically pancreatic-type amylase—is the definitive indicator for PPF, even when other features suggest infection. The presence of elevated amylase in the pleural fluid should always prompt a search for an intra-abdominal cause, particularly when the patient is an otherwise healthy child who is not responding to standard pulmonary therapies.

The definitive clue to the diagnosis lay not solely in the initial imaging but in a comprehensive review of the history, specifically regarding trauma. The patient had previously suffered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle strike one month prior, a detail he concealed due to school bullying. This concealment delayed the correct diagnosis for over a month, during which time the child underwent multiple thoracenteses. These procedures provided temporary relief; the first thoracocentesis drained approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid which resulted in immediate improvement of oxygen saturation and resolution of chest pain. However, the recurrence of the effusion six days later signaled that the pleural tap had addressed the symptom, not the underlying pathology, necessitating a shift in diagnostic reasoning from infectious to structural etiology.

Finally, the initial therapeutic response serves as a heuristic for further investigation while awaiting definitive imaging. The transient improvement following thoracocentesis in a patient with recurrent "blood" fluid should raise a red flag for an exudative cause with high enzyme content, rather than a simple serous effusion. Furthermore, the consistency of the fluid—dark red blood-stained and consistent across admissions—should distinguish it from the serosanguinous exudate typical of empyema. The realization that the pathology was linked to the abdominal trauma, rather than respiratory infection, compelled the medical team to order a contrast-enhanced CT and MRCP. This case demonstrates that unrecognized abdominal trauma can manifest as a thoracic emergency, and maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when pulmonary workup fails is essential for avoiding unnecessary invasive pulmonary interventions and preventing the morbidity associated with missed PPF diagnoses.
