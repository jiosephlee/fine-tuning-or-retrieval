Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# The Pulmonary Mask: Don't Distrust the History in Recurrent Effusions

Recurrent pleural effusion is a clinical riddle that often tempts us to look under the chest. We come up against tuberculosis, heart failure, and malignancy again and again. It is easy to tunnel-vision on the thorax. But as this pediatric case illustrates, the key to solving the mystery often lies in a conversation about the past that you never got to have on the first admission.

This case highlights a rare entity known as Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF). Think of PPF as the body’s plumbing system gone wrong—and it’s a lesson in why we must never stop asking about the abdomen when the breath gets short.

## The Case

A child presented to the outpatient department with progressive right-sided chest pain and respiratory distress for a week. The physical exam told a story of stony dullness and decreased breath sounds on the right. We thoracentised, and out came one litre of dark red, bloody fluid. The symptoms improved temporarily, and the patient was discharged. They returned six days later. Same pain, worse air hunger, more air leaking.

By the third admission, we were losing our minds. The fluid was consistently dark, bloody, and recurrent. The initial admission of thoracentesis raised suspicion for pulmonary TB, a common differential in this demographic with a contact in the family. The Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) in the fluid was slightly elevated. We empirically started antitubercular therapy. It hasn’t worked for the fluid.

Then, the child—and his reluctant memory—talked. There was a history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle bar collision one month prior. The patient had been hiding it. That conversation, coupled with a contrast-enhanced CT scan, changed the game. We saw a hypodense collection tracking from the retroperitoneum to the tail of the pancreas, extending superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus. MRCP confirmed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas. The effusion was tracking from the pancreatic duct into the chest.

## The Diagnostic Trap

This case is a classic example of the "Pulmonary Mask." In PPF, the pathology is abdominal, but the presentation is thoracic. Because the effusion is massive and recurrent, the respiratory distress can be profound, mimicking primary lung disease.

In this specific instance, the history-taking was thwarted. We saw the presentation; we kept trying to fit it into chest-scar patterns. The pleural fluid analysis needs to be the tie-breaker in these scenarios.

If you have high amylase in the fluid, your differential for PPF grows. In this case, the pleural fluid amylase was a staggering 6118 U/L (and lipase 7211 U/L). As the text notes, pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is often pathognomonic. Importantly, **pleural fluid amylase typing** can distinguish this from high results due to maceration in pancreatic injury or other causes. High amylase with normal pancreatic duct imaging usually warrants looking for another cause, but in trauma or pancreatitis contexts, look for fistula.

Prior to PPF, differential diagnoses included:
*   **Tuberculosis:** Often the first thought in pediatric effusions with family contact. (ADA was slightly elevated, but GeneXpert was negative).
*   **Emphysema/Empyema:** No fever, no neutrophilic exudate.
*   **Malignancy:** Absent weight loss, normal blood picture, negative cytology.
*   **Boerhaave's Syndrome:** Normal bowel habits, specific history of vomiting (required).
*   **Hepatic Hydrothorax:** Normal LFTs.

The pathophysiology here is key for the board exams: An abdominal injury causes pancreatic duct disruption. Pancreatic fluid follows the path of least resistance, which isn't the local inflammatory zone, but upward through natural diaphragmatic hiatuses (aortic or oesophageal) into the mediastinum and pleural space.

## Management and Outcome

Once the diagnosis was secured via MRCP, we abandoned the anti-TB drugs. We opted for conservative management, as surgical options are high risk for children.

*   **Nil Per Os (NPO)** status to rest the pancreas.
*   **Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN)** to ensure nutrition without oral intake.
*   **Octreotide** infusion (30 µg/kg/day) to suppress exocrine pancreatic secretion.
*   **Percutaneous Drainage (ICD)** to manage the fluid collection.

Over 10 days, the output from the drain decreased, eventually stopping. The chest tube was removed, and the child was discharged.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Dark red fluid in an effusion isn't just cardiac or inflammatory.** Always rule out hemorrhagic or enzymatic sources like PPF.
*   **The "Pulmonary Mask" hides abdominal sources.** If you are seeing recurrent, bloody, or massive effusions in a child or adult, a thoracic workup is easy, but the pathology might be retroperitoneal or abdominal.
*   **Pleural Amylase ≥1000 IU/L is a major red flag.** This finding should drive you toward pancreatic imaging (CT or MRCP) rather than treating for TB empirically.
*   **Don't let the history slide.** This patient was hiding his trauma due to school bullying. In pediatrics and unconscious adults, hesitate before dismissing a trauma history, even if it seems remote or unconnected to the current chief complaint.
*   **Conservative treatment often works.** NPO, TPN, and Somatostatin agonists (Octreotide) are the first line if the duct anatomy allows. ERCP and surgery are reserved for failures of this approach.
*   **Cost of misdiagnosis:** Every day spent chasing pulmonary malnutrition and anti-tubercular drugs delays the resolution of a fistula and exposes the patient to unnecessary sensitivities and costs. If in doubt, if the amylase is high, check the pancreas.

***

**Pearl of the Week:** When a young patient is diligent about hiding a history of trauma—whether due to bullying, fear of parental punishment, or simply cognitive delay—find the trust. The answer is likely in a broken duct, not a broken lung.
