Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# The Hepatopulmonary Fibrosis Nexus: Keeping Your Finger on the Telomere Pulse

When a 27-year-old woman presents with progressive dyspnea, pancytopenia, and signs of organ fibrosis, the impulse in a modern clinical setting is often to check for autoimmune interstitial lung disease, organizing pneumonia, or early-onset liver metastasis. However, as the case report below illustrates, when even a rigorous exclusion of infectious, autoimmune, and nutritional causes leaves you staring at unexplained pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis alongside bone marrow failure, the conversation must shift. Diagnosis for what is likely a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD), specifically linked to the *PARN* gene, reveals a critical diagnostic blind spot: young adulthood fibrosis is not "idiopathic" until proven otherwise.

## The Case: A "Symptom Cluster" That Resists Standard Therapy

This patient presented with a 5-year history of fatigue and weakness that descended into an inability to perform self-care within weeks. On physical exam, the clues to the true etiology were subtle but specific: premature greying of hair, pallor with icterus, and knuckle hyperpigmentation. Perhaps the most striking finding was the "bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae," a hallmark often described in DC (Dyskeratosis Congenita) but frequently overlooked in routine physical assessments.

Her laboratory profile was a confusing mosaic that initially pointed toward nutritional depletion rather than primary marrow failure. We saw severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency alongside a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT). While she had a positive DAT, the reticulocyte count was critically low (0.39%), and the marrow did not show peripheral destruction, but rather ineffective erythropoiesis. Iron studies revealed elevated ferritin, and the smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes and Cabot rings. After months of aggressive B12 replacement and immune workup, the fatigue persisted. The clinical picture included thoracic scoliosis, hepatosplenomegaly (on examination), ground-glass haziness in the lungs on PET-CT, and a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, indicating cirrhosis. The constellation of cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and lung fibrosis in a young adult with a lack of classic autoimmune serologies demanded a departure from standard differential diagnoses.

## When "Idiopathic" Isn't Idiopathic

The pivot to a genetic etiology was triggered by the disproportionate severity of her systemic dysfunction relative to her nutritional deficiencies. While severe B12 deficiency can cause anemia, it does not typically cause lung fibrosis or cirrhosis. Following the clinical-grid approach of tracing the symptoms to the organ systems involved, the presence of pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure in a young patient without the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation) is the key trigger.

The genetics, as detailed in this report, identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While classified as a "Variant of Uncertain Significance" (VUS) due to the complex nature of monoallelic inheritance, the location of the variant within the R3H domain—a highly conserved region essential for telomerase maturation—strongly supports a pathogenic role. This case fills a gap in the literature regarding monoallelic *PARN* mutations. While biallelic variants typically cause Autosomal Recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita (Type 6), monoallelic variants are more commonly associated with Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure (TB-PFBMFT4).

It is crucial to recognize that TBDs can present as "non-IDPF" (histopathologically unrecognizable Pulmonary Fibrosis) or unexplained liver disease in young adults. The absence of classic DC signs should not preclude testing. In our patient, the genetic diagnosis unified the findings: the pulmonary disease was the result of telomere attrition impairing lung epithelial repair, the liver stiffness was due to progressive hepatic fibrosis on the telomere scale, and the pancytopenia was a product of hematopoietic stem cell exhaustion—fractured and exacerbated by the concurrent nutritional stressors.

## Clinical Implications and Variants

The educational value here extends beyond this single case report. In evaluating young adults with multisystem "aging" phenotypes or unexplained organ fibrosis, we must maintain a low threshold for telomere biology assessment. Flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays to measure telomere length remain a gold standard, but germline sequencing is becoming increasingly accessible and often more definitive for variant identification.

Understanding the spectrum of TBDs is also vital for clinical risk stratification. While this patient harbored a monoallelic *PARN* variant which classically favors pulmonary fibrosis (PFBMFT4), she exhibited significant bone marrow failure and hepatic involvement. This highlights that the "phenotypic penetrance" of single-allele variants can be variable. Focusing on the range of organ involvement (lung, liver, bone marrow) rather than just cancer risk or the classic DC triad leads to earlier diagnosis.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Consider TBD in Young Adults:** Unexplained pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or cirrhosis in a patient under 40 should trigger a refusal to accept "idiopathic" diagnoses without telomere assessment.
*   **Look for the Clues:** Rapid-onset greying hair, bald tongue, brittle skin/nails, and premature osteoporosis are key physical exam findings that link organ systems.
*   **Nutrition Doesn't Fix Everything:** Correcting B12 deficiency or autoimmune markers is the first step in suspected cases, but disproportionate clinical improvement often reveals a genetic etiology.
*   **PARN Variants:** A monoallelic *PARN* variant (specifically non-biallelic) is a significant predictor of Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and Bone Marrow Failure.
*   **Hepatic Involvement:** Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are under-recognized features of TBDs and should be surveilled with elastography (like the 18.1 kPa measurement seen here) in these patients.

In the end, the necessity of this diagnosis was clear. While genetic counseling and genetic testing confirmed the VUS status of the *PARN* variant in our case, the emerging literature on haploinsufficiency in telomere maintenance supports the clinical diagnosis. Early recognition of this syndrome isn't just about assigning a code; it's about informing surveillance, managing expectations for stem cell transplantation potential, and understanding that the aging clock is ticking faster than it should in these patients.
