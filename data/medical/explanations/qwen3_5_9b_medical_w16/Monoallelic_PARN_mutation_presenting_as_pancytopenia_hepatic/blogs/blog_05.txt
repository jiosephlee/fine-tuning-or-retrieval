# Beyond Supportive Care: The Role of Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplant in TBD

When you see pancytopenia, premature greying, and pulmonary fibrosis in a woman in her 20s, your immediate doubt should be: “Wait, isn’t this Dyskeratosis Congenita?” and your second doubt should be: “Can this happen without the classic triad?” We recently encountered a patient who forced us to confront the spectrum of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs).

Here’s the kicker: she had a diagnosis of severe Vitamin B12 deficiency. We corrected it to the bone (intravenous cyanocobalamin, daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks). ## Recognizing the Subtle Phenotype

In the realm of TBDs, phenotypic variability is not just a nuisance; it’s the primary reason many adult patients end up in the mist of undiagnosed syndromes. Dissolving the classic Dyskeratosis Congenita mucocutaneous triad (reticular pigmentation, nail dystrophy, leukoplakia) into a constellation of marrow failure and organ fibrosis. Herded in this case report, our patient presented with a 5-year history of generalized weakness, fatigue, and dyspnea. She had what looked like a classic nutritional crisis: severe B12 deficiency, anergy, and a positive direct antiglobulin test.

However, the bone marrow was dry but ineffective (erythroid hyperplasia) with no dysplasia, and the B12 deficiency, while contributing, failed to explain the cirrhosis, the osteoporosis, or the severe neuropathy. The clinical exome sequencing eventually flagged a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This is a crucial distinction. While biallelic variants cause Autosomal Recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita 6, monoallelic variants, like this one, are increasingly implicated in Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and Bone Marrow Failure (TBD4). The absence of a family history initially masked the hereditary nature, emphasizing that *de novo* mutations or variable penetrance are real risks.

## The Treatment Dilemma: Supportive vs. Curative

This patient was managed supportively. We transfused, supplemented, and monitored. She showed improvement, and at a 2-week follow-up, her blood counts were stable with no new deterioration. However, is that the end of the path for the TBD patient who is bleeding out?

The treatment landscape for TBDs is often summarized as supportive, but the discussion paper correctly recognizes the role of Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT). The authors note that haematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be potentially curative in patients with haematological manifestations. In this specific case, however, the patient accepted that curative options were not feasible, likely reflecting the multidisciplinary decision-making required when pulmonary fibrosis and advanced hepatic fibrosis are present alongside marrow failure. This dilemma underscores that while blood counts might stabilize with vitamin support, the underlying telomere shortening in the marrow continues the clock toward failure without HSCT.

Therefore, the role of HSCT shifts from a last resort to a timed intervention. If a TBD patient with pulmonary fibrosis or hepatic steatosis remains young, fit enough for transplant, and demonstrates progressive cytopenia that responds only transiently to nutritional correction, HSCT becomes a viable conversation to prevent bone marrow failure.

## Clinical Take-Home Messages

Here are the core takeaways to keep at your treatment algorithm next time you chase pancytopenia:

*   **Don't rule out TBD just because the mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita is absent.** Adults can present with pancytopenia, osteoporosis, and organ fibrosis without the classic rash or nail changes.
*   **Suspect a genetic disorder in patients with disproportionate symptoms.** If B12 deficiency explains the anemia but not the hepatosplenomegaly or osteoporosis, look deeper. Clinical exome sequencing is becoming accessible and is a diagnostic game-changer.
*   **Consider HSCT for progressive hematological TBDs.** While supportive care is standard, HSCT remains potentially curative for patients with haematological manifestations, distinguishing this from a purely supportive management track for non-hematological fibrotic courses.
*   **Monitor for organ involvement.** This patient had *PARN* mutations but developed significant hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary disease. Because TBDs affect all rapidly dividing tissues, even after correcting a nutritional deficiency, you must surveil the marrow and fibrotic organs regularly.
