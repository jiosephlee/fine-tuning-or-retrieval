Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Look Past the Triad: The Adult Presentation of Telomere Biology Disorders

## The Old Dogma vs. New Reality

For decades, the classic teaching on Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) and Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) painted a specific portrait: a childhood-onset syndrome characterized by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. When that triad is present alongside bone marrow failure, the diagnosis is straightforward. But what about the young adult? What about the patient presenting with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cirrhosis, and bone marrow suppression who has *no* family history and looks absolutely normal aside from early fatigability?

The patient we discuss herein challenges that paradigm. We are looking at a woman in her late 20s presenting with a five-year history of progressive fatigue, weight loss, and dyspnea. She had no history of childhood organ failure, and critically, she lacked the classic mucocutaneous features of DC. Yet, she showed manifestations of thinning telomeres across multiple organ systems. This case forces us to expand our differential for premature ageing syndromes beyond the pediatric realm and beyond the skin, hair, and nails. It serves as a reminder that genetic disorders can assert themselves much later in life, often mimicking acquired inflammatory or toxic conditions.

## A Case of Multisystem Failure in Youth

The patient presented with insidious signs of multisystem decline. Examination revealed pallor, icterus, and premature greying of hair. Less commonly appreciated were specific mucocutaneous signs like knuckle hyperpigmentation, skin thickening, and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae. On a gentler physical exam, the findings were more subtle but equally alarming: hepatosplenomegaly with the liver extending 5 cm below the right costal margin and thoracic scoliosis. The respiratory exam showed reduced breath sounds bilaterally, consistent with underlying fibrotic lung disease, while neurologic evaluation revealed bilateral thenar atrophy and signs of neuropathy.

Before we reached the genetic verdict, we were dealing with a young woman with pancytopenia, a low reticulocyte count, and evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis. The peripheral smear revealed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and Cabot rings. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies were confirmed via serum studies.

The clinical trap here lies in the moth-eaten marrow hyperplasia and severe nutritional deficiency. When B12 deficiency accounts for a bone marrow investigation, it can explain dimorphic red cells and hypersegmented neutrophils. In this case, vitamin B12 deficiency was severe enough to compound the marrow failure phenotype. The patient was started on high-dose intravenous cyanocobalamin and folic acid, which provided symptomatic improvement. However, despite this correction, the systemic dysfunction persisted. Why? Because if the marrow stem cells have a shortened replicative lifespan, supplementing a nutrient that supports maturation doesn't fix the underlying telomere attrition accelerating the senescence of those stem cells.

## The Diagnostic Red Herrings

It is easy to see where this case could easily derail into acquired autoimmune or infectious workups. The patient had hepatosplenomegaly and elevated liver stiffness. She had ground-glass haziness on PET-CT and ultrasound, pointing toward pulmonary fibrosis. The positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG initially suggests immune-mediated destruction. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative autoimmune serologies, and the low reticulocyte count pointed firmly toward ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral clearance.

Even without the DAT confusion, the combination of cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult should trigger an alarm. Transient elastography showed liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa. To linearize that to a probabilistic cirrhosis diagnosis. In an adult with no history of alcohol or hepatitis, that is an outlier. CT scans showed diffuse ground-glass haziness in both lung fields. The constellation of pancytopenia, weight loss, premature greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic organ involvement finally shifted the suspicion toward a cryptic inherited disorder.

## The Genetic Clue

The pivot point was clinical exome sequencing. While initially bi-allelic variants in *PARN* are associated with Autosomal Recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6), the value of this case lies in identifying the patient's monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene. Specifically, the c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg missense variant was identified, classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance at first.

The variant alters a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain and fits the profile of harmless benign variation in populations. It predicts a damaging functional effect based on multiple in silico tools. The clinical-genetic correlation here supported a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) despite the monoallelic state. This specific genetic profile is distinct from biallelic mutations, often presenting with a phenotype dominated by pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure rather than the full DC triad.

The discussion in the literature following this case underscores a critical point: TBDs are not just "Dyskeratosis Congenita." They encompass a spectrum including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson, Revesz syndrome, and telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis/bone marrow failure. The *PARN* gene encodes an exoribonuclease required for the maturation of TERC RNA (the telomerase RNA component). Disruption here shortens telomeres. In the case of adults, this often presents later, often without the "triad."

## Key Takeaways

Here is what you need to carry forward from this case into your next clinic rotation:

*   **Consider TBDs in Adult Onset:** Unexplained pancytopenia in a young adult should prompt consideration of telomere biology disorders, even without the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita.
*   **Nutritional Red Flags:** Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can mask or worsen the clinical picture of marrow failure due to underlying telomere attrition; supplementing B12 may help acutely but does not correct the underlying genetic defect.
*   **Organ Fibrosis = Alarm:** In a young person, unexplained pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis (stiffness >12-15 kPa) are "red flags" for a functional gene mutation rather than environmental toxicity or viral etiology.
*   **VUS + Clinical Picture = Diagnosis:** A Variant of Uncertain Significance in a gene with a strong Mendelian association (like *PARN*) becomes actionable when the clinical phenotype aligns with the expected natural history of the gene.
*   **Prognostic Monitoring:** While support may stabilize symptoms initially, monitoring for progression of marrow failure is crucial, as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may be curative in progressive cases.

## Conclusion

This case is a textbook example of why we need to move beyond the "melk-mol coffee" syndrome of Grey's in the textbooks and embrace the complex reality of genomics. It highlights how genetic defects in telomere maintenance—which regulate the replicative lifespan of cells—can manifest as multisystem fibrosis, marrow failure, and premature aging in late adolescence. By maintaining a high index of suspicion for telomere biology disorders in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease, we can offer earlier genetic counseling, appropriate organ surveillance, and access to therapies like hematopoietic stem cell transplantation before irreversible deconditioning sets in. Remember: if a patient looks too old for their lab results, telomeres might be the culprit, not the struggles of life.
