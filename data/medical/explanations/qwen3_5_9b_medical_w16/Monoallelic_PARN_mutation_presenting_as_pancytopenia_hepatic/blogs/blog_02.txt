Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Nutritional Deficiencies as Clues, Not Culprits: The B12 Excuse for Dyskeratosis Congenita

**Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla**

When you see pancytopenia and a low reticulocyte count, what is the first thing you do? You chase the vitamin B12. If you correct it and the patient’s counts don't normalize completely, you perhaps look harder at the marrow. But what if the vitamin B12 deficiency isn't the cause of the marrow failure? It seems tempting, especially when you have a patient with morphological features of megaloblastic change—dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and hypersegmented neutrophils. In this case, we had a patient where the nutritional deficiency was real, treated, and corrected. And yet, the chronic fatigue, organ fibrosis, and premature greying refused to go away. This creates a classic "diagnostic capture" scenario: we successfully treated a nutrient problem and assumed we did the job, leaving a genetic disorder to wind up its clock in the background.

In the case of a woman in her late 20s presenting with a five-year history of progressive fatigue, the initial presentation appeared straightforward. She had generalised weakness, early fatigability, and reduced functional capacity. The exam revealed pallor, a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae, and knuckle hyperpigmentation. The bilirubin was raised, and the LDH was elevated, suggesting some degree of hemolysis or ineffective erythropoiesis. The DAT was positive for IgG, but the low reticulocyte count argued against an immune-mediated destruction. The marrow showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than frank hypoplasia.

The laboratory image screamed B12 deficiency. Serum B12 was low, as was folate. Iron studies showed elevated ferritin, confirming ineffective erythropoiesis rather than iron deficiency. An upper GI endoscopy ruled out obvious malignancy, but a duodenal biopsy showed mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells. This implied the patient couldn't make enough intrinsic factor. The treatment plan seemed clear. Intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for five days, then weekly to monthly maintenance. Folic acid, and propping up iron stores.

But here is where the clinical rigour stops the narrative shortcut. The patient’s symptoms did not resolve. She continued to have progressive hepatomegaly and weight loss. Imaging revealed grade II hepatic steatosis and cirrhotic liver stiffness measured at 18.1 kPa. PET-CT showed diffuse ground-glass haziness in both lung fields, consistent with pulmonary fibrosis. While the B12 deficiency was real and likely exacerbated the ineffective erythropoiesis, the constellation of findings was disproportionate to a simple nutritional deficit. She had premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, and neuropathy. The B12 explained the macrocytosis and hypersegmented neutrophils, but it did not explain the rampant fibrosis or the neurodegeneration.

This is the danger zone in adult medicine, especially in young adults. We tend to treat the most common presentation first. We rule out pernicious anemia or celiac disease. We see the dimorphic smear and the low reticulocyte count. We fix the vitamin. But we often miss the underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD). While the B12 deficiency was a major contributor to her cytopenia, it was superimposed on a fragile hematopoietic reserve caused by a shortened telomere length. Once the telomeres shorten below a functional threshold, the replicative capacity of the bone marrow is compromised. Correcting the nutritional substrate makes the cell healthy for as long as the genetic capacity allows, but it cannot fix the attrition of the endoplasmic telomere length itself.

Ultimately, the clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While initially classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the correlation with the patient's phenotype—pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and premature greying—supported a diagnosis of a Telomere Biology Disorder. Compounding this complexity, it is worth noting that the patient lacked significant family history. In many TBDs, a de novo mutation or incomplete penetrance can mask the inheritance pattern. The *PARN* gene belongs to the family of exoribonucleases, essential for maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DKC)—leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticulate pigmentation—this diagnosis is frequently delayed. Most clinical teams might not suspect a TBD without the classic DC features, yet the "cryptic" signs were lying in plain sight: the fatigue, the grey hair, and the fibrosis.

### Key Takeaways

*   **Don't stop at the correctable deficiency:** Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can mimic marrow failure syndromes, but resolution of the deficiency often unmasks the persistence of a co-existing pathology like a telomere biology disorder.
*   **Look for the "red flags":** In young adults with unexplained pancytopenia, consider the presence of systemic fibrosis (pulmonary or hepatic), premature greying, or skeletal findings like osteoporosis, which are classic markers of accelerated ageing associated with TBDs.
*   **Beware of the "DC Triad":** Dyskeratosis congenita and related disorders often present without the classic oral, nail, and skin triad. Monoallelic variants in *PARN* specifically can present with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4) without the cutaneous signs.
*   **Genetics can reveal the teaspoon:** When the clinical phenotype (pancytopenia, cirrhosis, lung fibrosis) is disproportionate to the acquired etiology (B12 deficiency), genetic testing for telomere biology disorders is warranted even in the absence of a known family history.
