Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] What is the physiological role of telomeres in preserving genomic integrity, and why are rapidly dividing tissues like bone marrow and liver disproportionately affected in telomere biology disorders?

Telomeres function as repetitive DNA caps at the ends of chromosomes, preserving genomic integrity by shielding the chromosome ends from degradation and preventing fusion with neighboring chromosomes. Under physiological conditions, the enzyme telomerase maintains telomere length through elongation during DNA replication. However, with each cell division, telomeres naturally shorten. When they reach a critical threshold, the cell is unable to sustain further replication and enters a state of cellular senescence or undergoes apoptosis.

In telomere biology disorders (TBDs), pathogenic variants in genes encoding components of the telomerase complex or its regulators impair this maintenance mechanism, leading to premature telomere attrition. This renders cells highly susceptible to replication stress. Rapidly dividing tissues, such as bone marrow (hematopoietic cells), liver (hepatocytes), and mucosal surfaces, are disproportionately affected because their high turnover rates accelerate the consumption of telomeric DNA. These tissues exhaust their replicative potential sooner than slowly dividing tissues, leading to the functional failure observed in our patient.

In the reported case, this pathophysiology manifests as the classic presentation of telomere dysfunction. The patient’s germline mutation in the *PARN* gene impairs the maturation of the telomerase RNA component (*TERC*), thereby reducing telomerase activity and accelerating telomere shortening. Consequently, the patient developed pancytopenia due to defective hematopoiesis and hepatic fibrosis due to hepatocyte exhaustion. This case illustrates that when telomere maintenance fails, high-turnover organs manifest the earliest clinical sequelae, often mimicking idiopathic marrow failure or pulmonary fibrosis in adults where the underlying genetic etiology remains uncoupled from the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita.

### [Pathophysiology] Specifically, how does a mutation in the PARN gene impair the processing of telomerase RNA component (TERC) and subsequent telomere maintenance?

The fundamental mechanism by which a *PARN* mutation impairs telomere maintenance lies in the essential role PARN plays in the RNA processing of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). PARN encodes a highly conserved exoribonuclease that regulates gene expression by shortening mRNA poly(A) tail length through deadenylation.

Specifically, as noted in the discussion of this case, TERC undergoes post-transcriptional modification where it acquires oligo(A) tails that target the nuclear RNA for degradation. Functional PARN is required to remove these tails, a process necessary for the proper 3'-prime-end maturation of the TERC. When PARN function is compromised—whether through biallelic mutations causing autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita or the monoallelic variant in this patient (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg)—TERC cannot be correctly processed. This results in an insufficiency of functional telomerase complex (TERT and TERC), impeding the enzyme’s ability to maintain telomere length.

The consequence of this specific biochemical defect is the progressive attrition of telomeres in dividing cells. As described in the background and case presentation, this premature telomere shortening triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis in tissues with high turnover, such as the bone marrow, lung, and liver. This pathophysiology explains the patient's multisystem presentation of pancytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis, distinguishing the genetic mechanism from her acquired vitamin B12 deficiency and highlighting the clinical imperative to identify telomere biology disorders in young adults with multisystem organ failure or premature aging.

### [Clinical Pearls] Beyond hematologic failure, which anatomical or systemic features in this patient (e.g., premature greying, skeletal abnormalities) serve as critical 'clues' for a diagnosis of telomere biology disorder in an adult?

In adults presenting with multisystem failure, the absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad (reticular pigmentation, leukoplakia, nail dystrophy) often delays the diagnosis of telomere biology disorder (TBD). Based on this case report, specific non-hematologic clues that should raise suspicion for TBD include a constellation of features predisposing high-turnover tissues to premature exhaustion.

First, **mucocutaneous signs of premature aging** are frequently overlooked. This patient presented with **premature greying of hair** and a **bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae**, alongside **knuckle hyperpigmentation**. These findings reflect the clonal exhaustion of stem cells in the cutaneous and mucosal lineages, which are typically the earliest targets of telomere attrition.

Second, **unexplained organ fibrosis in a young adult** is a critical red flag. While idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and hepatic cirrhosis commonly present in older age groups, this patient demonstrated diffuse pulmonary fibrosis and grade II hepatic steatosis progressing to cirrhosis (measured by elastography) in her late 20s. This suggests telomere shortening has compromised the replicative reserve of lung epithelial and hepatic stem cells.

Third, **skeletal markers of accelerated aging**, specifically **osteoporosis** with secondary hyperparathyroidism and thoracic scoliosis, indicate systemic metabolic stress. Finally, **neuropathy** manifesting as sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy and thenar atrophy represents involvement of the neural tissue.

**Clinical Reasoning:** Rapidly dividing cell populations rely on telomeres to maintain genomic integrity. When telomeres critically shorten, stem cell niches fail, leading to the clinical triad of marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and aging.

**Take-home Point:** In young adults with unexplained pancytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, or liver disease, actively screening for mucocutaneous signs of aging (hair, skin, tongue) and skeletal fragility can unmask TBDs. Genetic exome sequencing is indicated when the constellation of cytopenia, organ fibrosis, and premature aging features is present despite normal nutritional status or absence of a family history.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should clinicians interpret the 'absence' of the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita when evaluating an adult with progressive organ fibrosis and bone marrow failure?

When evaluating an adult with progressive organ fibrosis and bone marrow failure, clinicians should not anchor their diagnosis on the classic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) mucocutaneous triad, as the patient's absence does not rule out a telomere biology disorder (TBD). As demonstrated in this report of a woman in her late 20s, TBDs—particularly those associated with monoallelic mutations like the *PARN* variant identified here—encompass a broader phenotypic spectrum where the classic triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation may be absent or replaced by features of accelerated aging and multisystem fibrosis.

The underlying pathophysiology involves telomere attrition in high-turnover tissues such as the bone marrow, lung, and liver. In contrast to biallelic variants that often cause classical DC, monoallelic variants (haploinsufficiency) frequently present as isolated pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes without the full dermatologic profile. In this case, the patient presented with pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, and neuropathy despite a lack of the full DC triad; her findings were ultimately explained by a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant (c.613T>C).

Therefore, the diagnostic standard must shift from a reliance on mucocutaneous inspection to a systematic evaluation of genetic and functional markers when marroal failure or organ fibrosis presents outside standard etiologies in young adults. Clinicians should utilize next-generation sequencing for *PARN*, *TERT*, and *TERC* and consider telomere length assays. As seen in this case, early recognition of the TBD phenotype—even in the absence of the classical triad—is crucial for appropriate genetic counseling, specific organ surveillance, and timely consideration of bone marrow transplantation for progressive marrow failure.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In this patient, how does the presence of severe vitamin B12 deficiency and ineffective erythropoiesis complicate the diagnosis of an inherited telomere biology disorder, and what evidence supported the decision to pursue genetic testing instead of solely nutritional management?

The presence of severe vitamin B12 deficiency complicates the diagnosis by inducing acquired hematologic features—specifically megaloblastic anemia with ineffective erythropoiesis—that mimic the bone marrow failure intrinsic to inherited telomere biology disorders (TBDs). In this case, the patient presented with pancytopenia, basophilic stippling, and hypersegmented neutrophils, findings initially suggestive of nutritional etiology. However, the decision to pursue genetic testing rather than solely nutritional management was driven by the disproportionate severity of extrahematologic manifestations and their persistence despite metabolic correction.

Specifically, clinical features such as pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis are not attributable to B12 deficiency and pointed toward a multisystem genetic disorder. Despite aggressive treatment with intravenous cyanocobalamin and scheduled maintenance, the patient’s fibrotic and aging features remained unexplained by the nutritional workup alone. Furthermore, the bone marrow examination revealed erythroid hyperplasia despite a low reticulocyte count, indicating marrow exhaustion that is characteristic of telomere attrition rather than simple deficiency. This constellation of findings raised suspicion for a genetic underlying cause, which was confirmed by the identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant via exome sequencing. This case highlights that while correcting acquired B12 deficiency is critical for symptomatic relief, it does not resolve the underlying genetic pathology or fibrosis associated with TBDs, necessitating long-term surveillance and guidance for prognostication.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What features in the laboratory and bone marrow evaluation (such as a positive DAT with a low reticulocyte count) argued against autoimmune cytopenia and in favor of marrow failure syndromes associated with telomere biology?

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) in the setting of anemia and elevated LDH often initially suggests autoimmune hemolytic anemia; however, the concurrent low reticulocyte count of 0.39% is the critical feature that argued against a primary immune-mediated etiology. In typical autoimmune hemolysis, even in the presence of significant peripheral destruction, the bone marrow responds with a robust reticulocytosis. By contrast, the absence of a reticulocytic response indicates marrow failure or intrinsic ineffective erythropoiesis.

The bone marrow examination provided the necessary evidence to support this hematology: it demonstrated erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores without frank dysplasia or aplasia, a pattern consistent with "ineffective erythropoiesis." As noted in the case report, despite the positive DAT and markers of hemolysis, the "low reticulocyte response and marrow findings were more consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral immune-mediated destruction."

This constellation points toward a telomere biology disorder (TBD). The pathophysiology of TBDs involves progressive telomere attrition that impairs the replicative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, leading to marrow failure syndromes that can mimic nutritional deficiencies (like the concurrent B12 deficiency seen here) or autoimmune disease. In this context, the DAT positivity may be secondary to marrow turnover issues or a concurrent immune phenomenon that is overwhelmed by the marrow’s inability to compensate due to stem cell exhaustion, ultimately localizing the defect to the marrow’s production capacity rather than peripheral destruction.

### [Management] In the context of this patient's confirmed diagnosis, when is hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) considered a definitive management option compared to supportive care?

In the context of this patient’s telomere biology disorder (TBD), hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is considered the definitive management option specifically for **progressive bone marrow failure (BMF)**, whereas supportive care remains the standard of care for non-hematologic manifestations such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis. As outlined in the discussion of the report, "haematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be potentially curative in patients with haematologic manifestations," but there is no curative therapy for the underlying pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis associated with this genetic defect.

Critically, the indication for HSCT depends on the etiology and progression of the cytopenia. In this patient, the pancytopenia was predominantly driven by secondary vitamin B12 deficiency and ineffective erythropoiesis, which we were able to partially correct with replacement and supportive monitoring; therefore, HSCT was not immediately indicated. However, the underlying *PARN* variant indicates an inherent predisposition to BMF due to telomere attrition in hematopoietic stem cells. Consequently, the decision to proceed with HSCT should be reserved for cases where BMF becomes "progressive," negative to nutritional or medical optimization, and refractory to persistent transfusion support.

**Clinical Takeaway:** For young adults presenting with multisystem failure and unexplained fibrosis or cytopenias, one must distinguish between acquired deficiencies and primary TBD marrow failure. Early recognition via genetic testing is vital because while supportive care stabilizes the non-hematologic features, HSCT offers the only potential cure for the hematologic sequela of TBD progression.

### [Management] For a patient with a telomere biology disorder and advanced hepatic fibrosis (cirrhosis), what specific longitudinal surveillance tools are recommended to monitor disease progression?

In the management of a patient with a confirmed telomere biology disorder (TBD) and advanced hepatic fibrosis, longitudinal surveillance must be comprehensive, targeting the specific organ systems compromised by premature telomere attrition. While the primary hope is supportively managing complications, surveillance is critical to detect malignant transformation or progression to end-stage organ failure early, as unifying genomic defects drive dysfunction in all high-turnover tissues.

Based on the approach described in this case, the recommended tools include **serial transient elastography**, **pulmonary imaging**, and **hematologic monitoring**. In this patient, we initially utilized transient elastography to assess liver stiffness, which correlated with cirrhosis on mechanical imaging, showing a stiffness of 18.1 kPa. Establishing baseline liver stiffness is essential; subsequent measurements should track progression of fibrosis, which is a direct consequence of limited hepatocyte replicative capacity due to telomere shortening. Furthermore, given the patient’s presentation with pulmonary fibrosis (confirmed by PET-CT showing ground-glass haziness and fibrotic changes), regular imaging surveillance is required to monitor for evolving interstitial lung disease, which is a known, lethal complication of TBD. Finally, hematologic surveillance is vital. Although her pancytopenia was initially overshadowed by Vitamin B12 deficiency, the underlying telomere biology defect places her at risk for bone marrow failure. Therefore, regular complete blood counts and reticulocyte monitoring are non-negotiable to detect marrow exhaustion before it becomes clinically catastrophic.

In summary, for patients with TBD and hepatic cirrhosis as seen in this report, surveillance should be "regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement." This multidimensional approach addresses the systemic nature of telomere attrition, ensuring that progression in one organ—such as the liver—does not obscure underlying failure in the bone marrow or lungs. We must remain vigilant for malignancies, as the background of this disorder includes an increased risk of hematological and solid tumors, necessitating a proactive rather than reactive strategy.

### [Morning Report] How do the ACMG/AMP guidelines and the specific clinical phenotype guide the reclassification of a PARN variant currently labeled as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in a case like this?

When we encounter a Vari ant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in a gene recognized to cause Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD), such as *PARN*, reclassification relies on integrating ACMG/AMP classification criteria with a precise clinical phenotype. This case illustrates that decision process. We identified a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) initially labeled as a VUS, yet our reasoning to upgrade this toward a pathogenic status was driven by four specific ACMG criteria and the clinical context:

First, we evaluated structural and population information. The variant meets **PM1** because it occurs within the highly conserved R3H domain of the protein, a critical region with no known benign variants, and it meets **PM2** as it is absent in population databases. Second, computational evidence supports this via **PP3**, as multiple prediction tools flagged the variant as damaging. Third, the gene itself is validated for classification via **PP2**, given *PARN*'s known intolerance to missense variation.

However, meeting these molecular criteria alone does not always constitute a definitive reclassification in an isolated setting; the clinical phenotype acts as the critical tie-breaker. In this patient, ACMG-derived suspicion was substantiated by a multisystem presentation consistent with the *PARN*-associated TBD spectrum (specifically telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure syndrome). Despite the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, the patient exhibited bone marrow failure (pancytopenia), hepatic fibrosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis alongside premature aging.

Therefore, the reclassification is guided not merely by molecular metrics, but by the congruence of ACMG evidence with a phenotypic presentation that mirrors established pathogenic variants (such as p.Lys59Arg) within the literature. This approach ensures that genetic data does not treat *PARN* variants in isolation, but is weighed as part of a syndrome analysis before finalizing the variant's classification.
