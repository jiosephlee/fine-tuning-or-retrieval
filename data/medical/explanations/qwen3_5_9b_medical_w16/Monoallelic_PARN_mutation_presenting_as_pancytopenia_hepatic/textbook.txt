Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Multisystemic Phenotype

This case involves a woman in her late 20s who presented with a five-year history of progressive fatigue and generalized weakness, symptoms that significantly impair her functional capacity and self-care. Her clinical course was chronic, marked by worsening exertional dyspnea, intermittent chest discomfort, and weight loss over the most recent three months. This prolonged decline in functional status serves as the primary complaint and signals a progressive, systemic pathology rather than an acute or localized illness. On physical examination, the patient exhibited signs consistent with a multisystem degenerative process, including marked pallor and icterus indicative of hemolysis or marrow imbalance. Notably, findings of premature aging were evident, such as premature greying of the hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae. Additionally, the abdominal examination revealed significant hepatosplenomegaly (hepatomegaly 5 cm below the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm), and neurologic assessment identified thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, and findings suggestive of neuropathy, all contributing to a picture of cumulative organ and tissue failure.

The hematologic evaluation was central to understanding the patient’s multisystem involvement. Laboratory investigations revealed pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count of 0.39%, indicating a failure of production rather than peripheral destruction, despite an erythrocyte sedimentation rate that was likely elevated due to other processes. The peripheral blood smear provided critical morphological clues, demonstrating dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—findings characteristic of ineffective erythropoiesis often seen in megaloblastic anemia. However, serum studies confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, and initial assessment implicated parietal cell reduction from gastritis as a likely cause of absorption failure. While the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG, the combination of a markedly low reticulocyte response and marrow findings without overt peripheral hemolysis supported a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis over immune-mediated destruction. Bone marrow aspiration confirmed trilineage hyperplasia with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, and cytogenetic analysis was normal, arguing against malignancy or primary chromosomal anomalies.

Beyond the hematologic triad, imaging and specialized testing highlighted fibrotic involvement of the liver and lungs, a rare presentation for this age group. Abdominal ultrasound confirmed hepatosplenomegaly, and transient elastography demonstrated grade II hepatic steatosis alongside hepatic cirrhosis, indicated by a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. In the absence of positive autoimmune serologies or viral hepatitis, this finding suggested intrinsic parenchymal damage. Similarly, respiratory examination revealed reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scantular regions, corroborated by PET-CT imaging which showed diffuse ground-glass haziness with multiple fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes in both lung fields. The presence of pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic cirrhosis in a patient under 30 years is highly atypical and raises strong suspicion for an underlying inherited etiology driving premature organ failure.

The systemic assessment further reinforced the diagnosis of accelerated aging through associated skeletal and neuromuscular findings. The patient had osteoporosis confirmed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, accompanied by secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte disturbances including hypocalcemia and hypokalaemia, and vitamin D deficiency, which explained her recurrent bone pain and fractures. Electromyography and nerve conduction studies identified asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes in proximal and distal muscle groups. While the patient had a confirmed vitamin B12 deficiency responsive to treatment, the constellation of premature greying, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy represented a phenotype disproportionate to nutritional deficiency alone. This clinical spectrum, involving the bone marrow, liver, and lungs with signs of accelerated aging in a young adult, prompted the consideration of a telomere biology disorder, where telomere shortening and organ fibrosis can manifest as cryptic multisystem degeneration even in the absence of a classic family history or the full mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Exclusion of Common Etiologies

The initial diagnostic challenge in this case centered on a young adult woman presenting with multisystem organ failure, requiring a systematic exclusion of common, acquired etiologies to justify a genetic investigation. The patient’s primary complaints of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, and weight loss traditionally prompt an evaluation for hematologic malignancy, autoimmune cytopenia, or severe nutritional deficits. The diagnostic team prioritized ruling out the most prevalent and treatable causes before attempting to address the rare genetic possibilities. This exclusionary process was critical in distinguishing a treatable metabolic issue from a syndromic genetic disorder.

The evaluation of nutritional deficiencies formed the cornerstone of the initial laboratory workup. Investigations revealed severe vitamin B12 deficiency and mild folate deficiency, supported by peripheral blood smear findings characteristic of ineffective erythropoiesis, including dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and hypersegmented neutrophils. Bone marrow examination confirmed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and significant iron stores, consistent with sequestration of iron in ineffective erythropoiesis rather than destruction. Intraventricularly, the patient also suffered from atrophic gastritis with reduced parietal cells, which provided an anatomical basis for impaired intrinsic factor production and subsequent B12 malabsorption. Following the administration of high-dose intravenous cyanocobalamin and folic acid, the patient demonstrated some correction in blood counts. However, a crucial diagnostic pivot occurred upon recognizing that this hemologic response was incomplete regarding the patient's systemic burden; the resolution of macrocytosis and pancytopenia did not address the underlying hepatic and pulmonary pathology.

Concurrently, the extensive workup for autoimmune and inflammatory liver and lung disease yielded negative results, further narrowing the differential away from acquired dermatologic or rheumatologic syndromes. While the patient exhibited elevated inflammatory markers and a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG, the peripheral smear lacked evidence of significant hemolysis (e.g., schistocytes, spherocytes), and the bone marrow did not show features of autoimmune marrow suppression. Furthermore, autoimmune serologies including antinuclear antibodies were negative, and an infectious panel for viral hepatitis, HIV, and common opportunistic infections was largely unremarkable. In the respiratory domain, imaging demonstrated diffuse ground-glass haziness and subpleural atelectasis consistent with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). However, IPF is exceptionally rare in a patient in her late twenties, and the absence of granulomatous disease or connective tissue disease markers arguing against conditions like rheumatoid arthritis or systemic sclerosis reinforced the likelihood of a primary pulmonary pathology distinct from acquired fibrosis.

A significant discrepancy emerged between the laboratory response to nutritional correction and the patient's enduring clinical severity. Although the bone marrow failure indices and reticulocyte response improved with B12 replacement, the constellation of premature greying, balding tongue, thoracic scoliosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy persisted. These features signaled a systemic machinery failure rather than localized nutritional deprivation. Specifically, the presence of hepatic fibrosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa) alongside bone marrow failure and pulmonary fibrosis created a phenotypic pattern inconsistent with simple pernicious anemia or autoimmune gastritis. The weak reticulocyte response despite erythroid hyperplasia suggested an intrinsic marrow replicative defect, which is often a hallmark of telomere biology disorders, even when triggered or exacerbated by comorbidities.

Despite the absence of a reported family history, which would typically suggest a recessive inheritance pattern or variable penetrance, the clinician recognized the syndromic nature of the presentation. The combination of bone marrow failure, multi-organ fibrosis, and features of accelerated aging in a young adult fits the spectrum of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). The absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad (reticulate pigmentation, nail dystrophy, leukoplakia) is a classic diagnostic pitfall, as severe or atypical presentations can mimic acquired conditions. The persistence of systemic failure despite the correction of nutritional parameters raised the suspicion that the hematologic and organ dysfunction were manifestations of a root genetic defect that rendered cellular systems more vulnerable to stress or that mimicked acquired catabolic states. This phenotypic incongruence served as the primary justification for escalating from standard clinical care to advanced genomic diagnostics.

The decision point for clinical exome sequencing was reached based on this failure of standard diagnostic algorithms in a patient with progressive multi-system organ failure. In cases of undiagnosed dysmorphic syndromes, young-onset organ failure, or unexplained multisystem fibrosis and marrow failure, exome sequencing offers a high yield for identifying pathogenic variants when genetic testing targets specific organ systems. The patient’s PARN variant identification underscored the necessity of broad calling for rare Mendelian disorders. Clinicians must be prepared to interpret "Variants of Uncertain Significance" (VUS) within the context of the specific phenotype; in this context, the monoallelic PARN variant, while initially ambiguous, aligns with the clinical features of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. This step confirmed that standard exclusionary workups for common etiologies were insufficient, reinforcing the need for genetic evaluation when patients present with unexplained multisystem failure and premature aging features.

## Pathophysiology: Telomere Biology and the PARN Gene

To understand the clinical phenotype of this patient, one must first grasp the fundamental mechanism of telomere maintenance in replicative cells. Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences located at the ends of chromosomes that function as protective caps, shielding genetic material from degradation and preventing end-to-end fusion. With every cell division, telomeres naturally shorten due to the "end-replication problem." Under physiological conditions, the enzyme telomerase serves as a critical counterbalance; it is a ribonucleoprotein complex composed primarily of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Telomerase adds these repetitive sequences back onto the chromosome ends, thereby preserving genomic integrity. When telomeres reach a critical length, cells enter a state of senescence or undergo apoptosis to prevent genomic instability. In Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs), germline defects compromise this maintenance system, leading to premature telomere attrition, cellular exhaustion, and dysfunction in tissues that rely on high turnover.

While many TBDs arise from direct mutations in structural components like TERC or TERT, the PARN gene plays a distinct upstream regulatory role. PARN (Polypeptide Nucleotide Phosphorylase, Poly(A) Polymerase Ribonucleoside 5'-Hydroxylase and Poly(A)-Specific Phosphatase) is an exoribonuclease responsible for deadenyation, a process that shortens mRNA poly(A) tails to regulate gene expression. Specifically, PARN is required for the removal of post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails from nuclear RNAs, a step essential for the 3-prime end maturation of the TERC RNA component. Deep sequencing analysis confirms that proper TERC maturation is dependent on PARN activity. Without functional PARN, TERC RNA cannot be properly processed, impairing the assembly of functional telomerase complexes. In the context of this case, the patient harbored a monoallelic missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) affecting a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain. Although classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance due to limited evidence, the location within a critical functional domain suggests a deleterious effect that likely results in hypomorphic function or haploinsufficiency, disrupting the telomerase machinery indirectly by hindering TERC maturation.

The mechanism of disease progression is defined by this molecular inefficiency leading to functional telomere shortening. As telomeres shorten, the proliferative capacity of stem cell progenitors declines, eventually triggering premature aging of specific tissues. This biological vulnerability primarily impacts organ systems with high cellular turnover, such as the hematopoietic stem cells of the bone marrow, the fibroblasts in the lung parenchyma, and peripheral liver cells. In these tissues, the inability to sustain cell division leads to the clinical hallmarks of TBDs: cytopenia due to marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis from alveolar stem cell exhaustion, and hepatic fibrosis from hepatocyte turnover deficits. This explains the patient's concurrent presentation of pancytopenia, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis despite the absence of the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita.

Furthermore, the pathophysiology of telomere dysfunction creates a diathesis where the affected organs are significantly more susceptible to environmental stressors and comorbidities. While the primary driver of the patient's severe anemia was severe vitamin B12 deficiency leading to ineffective erythropoiesis, the presence of telomere attrition likely compromised the reserve capacity of her hematopoietic stem cells. Consequently, the bone marrow became less able to compensate for the sterilizing stress of the nutritional deficiency. This interaction highlights a crucial teaching point in TBDs: genetic predisposition can magnify the pathological impact of common acquired conditions. Even in cases where a nutritional deficiency is the immediate cause of symptoms, the underlying telomere shortening limits the body's resilience, potentially leading to more severe organ failure or accelerated phenotypic expression in reproductive tissues, as seen with the premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy observed in this patient.

## Genetic Identification and Variant Classification

The definitive etiology for the patient’s multisystem presentation was established through clinical exome sequencing, which identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene. Specifically, the analysis revealed a nucleotide substitution c.613T>C resulting in a protein alteration designated p.Cys205Arg. While initially classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) due to the limitation of functional data at the time of analysis, the biological significance of this change became more apparent upon detailed structural and population-based evaluation. This specific variant resides in exon 8 and affects the pCys205 residue, which is located within the highly conserved R3H domain (residues 178–245) of the PARN protein. Variants in domain regions known to be critical for function and lacking benign historical variation are significant indicators of pathogenicity, as the substitution alters a highly conserved amino acid essential for the exoribonuclease function of PARN.

To further evaluate the potential impact of the amino acid change, bioinformatic analysis was employed to predict the deleterious effect of the mutation. In silico tools provided consistent evidence of damage to the protein function, with four of five prediction algorithms—including LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT—classifying the p.Cys205Arg change as damaging. These computational scores suggest that the variant disrupts the normal folding or enzymatic activity of the PARN protein. When applying the ACMG/AMP standards for variant interpretation, the case met several supportive criteria. Specifically, criterion PM1 applies because the substitution occurs within a well-established functional domain (the R3H domain). Additionally, PM2 supports the classification because the variant is absent or extremely rare in population databases. The predictive power of the bioinformatics tools fulfills criterion PP3, as multiple independent programs predict a deleterious effect. Furthermore, PP2 is met because *PARN* is a gene with a low rate of benign missense variation, and missense variants are a common mechanism of disease in this locus.

The interpretation of the *PARN* mutation must also be contextualized by its inheritance pattern and the resulting clinical phenotype. Distinguishing between monoallelic and biallelic variants is crucial, as *PARN* pathogenic variants follow different spectrums. Germline double, or biallelic, variants in *PARN* typically cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), characterized by the classical mucocutaneous triad. In contrast, the phenotype associated with monoallelic germline variants aligns more closely with the telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure phenotype (PFBMFT4). This patient presented with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure without the classic mucocutaneous features, fitting the PFBMFT4 spectrum described for monoallelic carriers. Although the diagnosis was initially complicated by a concurrent severe vitamin B12 deficiency that contributed to pancytopenia, the persistence of systemic fibrosis and premature aging signs, alongside the specific genetic finding, supported a telomere biology disorder diagnosis. This case underscores that genetic diagnosis in adult-presenting TBDs often requires linking phenotypic features of organ fibrosis and marrow failure with variant data that meets ACMG criteria, even in the absence of classical dyskeratosis congenita stigmata.

## Management Strategies, Prognosis, and Clinical Implications

Management of telomere biology disorders (TBD) requires a tiered approach that prioritizes the correction of acquired, reversible comorbidities before addressing the underlying genetic dysregulation. In the case presented, the patient’s profound vitamin B12 deficiency represented an acute, correctable insult that significantly compounded her constitutional telomere shortcomings. The management hierarchy began with aggressive nutritional rehabilitation, specifically high-dose intravenous cyanocobalamin and weekly folate supplementation, to reverse the ineffective erythropoiesis. Once nutritional deficiencies were addressed, symptomatic management was initiated for comorbidities such as severe osteoporosis and neuromuscular weakness. This included elemental calcium and vitamin D supplementation to support bone mineral density and physiotherapy to address thenar atrophy. Transfusion support was reserved for symptomatic anemia or pancytopenia that persisted despite nutritional correction, reflecting the principle that severe marrow failure within a TBD context requires hemodynamic stabilization while long-term genetic risks are managed.

Long-term surveillance protocols are critical due to the progressive nature of telomere attrition in high-turnover tissues. Patients must be monitored for the development of thrombocytopenia or anemia, as telomere shortening predisposes bone marrow progenitors to accelerated senescence, potentially leading to aplastic anemia even in the absence of overt cytopenia at presentation. Concurrently, the clinical team must track hepatic and pulmonary function. In this case, non-invasive liver stiffness measurement (transient elastography) served as a surrogate marker for fibrosis progression. Pulmonary surveillance involves monitoring for radiographic fibrosis and respiratory decline, as the small size of the lungs allows fibrotic changes to manifest without the systemic respiratory compromise often seen in hepatobiliary TBDs. Regular clinical review facilitates the early detection of recurrence of acquired conditions or the emergence of malignancies, which are significant risks in TBD cohorts due to genomic instability associated with short telomeres.

Genetic counseling plays a pivotal role in the management plan, as family history is often negative due to de novo mutations or variable penetrance in heterozygous carriers. This patient’s monoallelic PARN variant highlights the complexity of inheritance in TBDs; while biallelic mutations typically result in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita, monoallelic variants are increasingly recognized to cause a dominant-like phenotype of pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. Risk assessment for family members is complicated by this variability, necessitating education on clinical signs to watch for, such as fragility or premature greying. Furthermore, reproductive counseling should address the heritability risks for the offspring of carriers, as the variant represents a loss of function that can be inherited if the father is a carrier or if recombination occurs.

Regarding refractory hematopoietic manifestations, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only potentially curative intervention for TBD-associated bone marrow failure. While the patient in this case did not immediately require HSCT due to the predominant reason for cytopenia being nutritional deficiency, the potential exists for marrow failure if telomere attrition worsens in the face of transplants. Clinicians should keep HSCT as a contingency for progressive pancytopenia or symptoms refractory to supportive care, as early transplantation may yield better outcomes than waiting for advanced marrow collapse. Although pharmacological agents like androgens have shown promise in stabilizing telomerase activity, their role remains investigational.

The principal teaching point from this case is the recognition of occult telomere biology disorders in young adults where the classical triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticulate skin pigmentation is absent. Clinicians evaluating multisystemic disease with unexplained pancytopenia, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis should maintain a high suspicion for TBDs, even without a clear family history. Because conditions like severe vitamin B12 deficiency can mask the underlying telomere defect, it is essential to treat reversible causes while simultaneously investigating for underlying genetic etiologies through next-generation sequencing. Early identification allows for targeted surveillance, genetic counseling, and informed decision-making regarding transplant eligibility, ultimately preventing unnecessary interventions such as chronic immunosuppression if an autoimmune mimic is misdiagnosed.
