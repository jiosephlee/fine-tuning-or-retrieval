# Look Past the Triad: The Adult Presentation of Telomere Biology Disorders

## The Old Dogma vs. New Reality

For decades, the classic teaching on Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) and Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) painted a specific portrait: a childhood-onset syndrome characterized by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. When that triad is present alongside bone marrow failure, the diagnosis is straightforward. But what about the young adult? What about the patient presenting with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cirrhosis, and bone marrow suppression who has *no* family history and looks absolutely normal aside from early fatigability?

The patient we discuss herein challenges that paradigm. We are looking at a woman in her late 20s presenting with a five-year history of progressive fatigue, weight loss, and dyspnea. She had no history of childhood organ failure, and critically, she lacked the classic mucocutaneous features of DC. Yet, she showed manifestations of thinning telomeres across multiple organ systems. This case forces us to expand our differential for premature ageing syndromes beyond the pediatric realm and beyond the skin, hair, and nails. It serves as a reminder that genetic disorders can assert themselves much later in life, often mimicking acquired inflammatory or toxic conditions.

## A Case of Multisystem Failure in Youth

The patient presented with insidious signs of multisystem decline. Examination revealed pallor, icterus, and premature greying of hair. Less commonly appreciated were specific mucocutaneous signs like knuckle hyperpigmentation, skin thickening, and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae. On a gentler physical exam, the findings were more subtle but equally alarming: hepatosplenomegaly with the liver extending 5 cm below the right costal margin and thoracic scoliosis. The respiratory exam showed reduced breath sounds bilaterally, consistent with underlying fibrotic lung disease, while neurologic evaluation revealed bilateral thenar atrophy and signs of neuropathy.

Before we reached the genetic verdict, we were dealing with a young woman with pancytopenia, a low reticulocyte count, and evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis. The peripheral smear revealed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and Cabot rings. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies were confirmed via serum studies.

The clinical trap here lies in the moth-eaten marrow hyperplasia and severe nutritional deficiency. When B12 deficiency accounts for a bone marrow investigation, it can explain dimorphic red cells and hypersegmented neutrophils. In this case, vitamin B12 deficiency was severe enough to compound the marrow failure phenotype. The patient was started on high-dose intravenous cyanocobalamin and folic acid, which provided symptomatic improvement. However, despite this correction, the systemic dysfunction persisted. Why? Because if the marrow stem cells have a shortened replicative lifespan, supplementing a nutrient that supports maturation doesn't fix the underlying telomere attrition accelerating the senescence of those stem cells.

## The Diagnostic Red Herrings

It is easy to see where this case could easily derail into acquired autoimmune or infectious workups. The patient had hepatosplenomegaly and elevated liver stiffness. She had ground-glass haziness on PET-CT and ultrasound, pointing toward pulmonary fibrosis. The positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG initially suggests immune-mediated destruction. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative autoimmune serologies, and the low reticulocyte count pointed firmly toward ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral clearance.

Even without the DAT confusion, the combination of cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult should trigger an alarm. Transient elastography showed liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa. To linearize that to a probabilistic cirrhosis diagnosis. In an adult with no history of alcohol or hepatitis, that is an outlier. CT scans showed diffuse ground-glass haziness in both lung fields. The constellation of pancytopenia, weight loss, premature greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic organ involvement finally shifted the suspicion toward a cryptic inherited disorder.

## The Genetic Clue

The pivot point was clinical exome sequencing. While initially bi-allelic variants in *PARN* are associated with Autosomal Recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6), the value of this case lies in identifying the patient's monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene. Specifically, the c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg missense variant was identified, classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance at first.

The variant alters a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain and fits the profile of harmless benign variation in populations. It predicts a damaging functional effect based on multiple in silico tools. The clinical-genetic correlation here supported a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) despite the monoallelic state. This specific genetic profile is distinct from biallelic mutations, often presenting with a phenotype dominated by pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure rather than the full DC triad.

The discussion in the literature following this case underscores a critical point: TBDs are not just "Dyskeratosis Congenita." They encompass a spectrum including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson, Revesz syndrome, and telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis/bone marrow failure. The *PARN* gene encodes an exoribonuclease required for the maturation of TERC RNA (the telomerase RNA component). Disruption here shortens telomeres. In the case of adults, this often presents later, often without the "triad."

## Key Takeaways

Here is what you need to carry forward from this case into your next clinic rotation:

*   **Consider TBDs in Adult Onset:** Unexplained pancytopenia in a young adult should prompt consideration of telomere biology disorders, even without the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita.
*   **Nutritional Red Flags:** Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can mask or worsen the clinical picture of marrow failure due to underlying telomere attrition; supplementing B12 may help acutely but does not correct the underlying genetic defect.
*   **Organ Fibrosis = Alarm:** In a young person, unexplained pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis (stiffness >12-15 kPa) are "red flags" for a functional gene mutation rather than environmental toxicity or viral etiology.
*   **VUS + Clinical Picture = Diagnosis:** A Variant of Uncertain Significance in a gene with a strong Mendelian association (like *PARN*) becomes actionable when the clinical phenotype aligns with the expected natural history of the gene.
*   **Prognostic Monitoring:** While support may stabilize symptoms initially, monitoring for progression of marrow failure is crucial, as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may be curative in progressive cases.

## Conclusion

This case is a textbook example of why we need to move beyond the "melk-mol coffee" syndrome of Grey's in the textbooks and embrace the complex reality of genomics. It highlights how genetic defects in telomere maintenance—which regulate the replicative lifespan of cells—can manifest as multisystem fibrosis, marrow failure, and premature aging in late adolescence. By maintaining a high index of suspicion for telomere biology disorders in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease, we can offer earlier genetic counseling, appropriate organ surveillance, and access to therapies like hematopoietic stem cell transplantation before irreversible deconditioning sets in. Remember: if a patient looks too old for their lab results, telomeres might be the culprit, not the struggles of life.



# Nutritional Deficiencies as Clues, Not Culprits: The B12 Excuse for Dyskeratosis Congenita

**Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla**

When you see pancytopenia and a low reticulocyte count, what is the first thing you do? You chase the vitamin B12. If you correct it and the patient’s counts don't normalize completely, you perhaps look harder at the marrow. But what if the vitamin B12 deficiency isn't the cause of the marrow failure? It seems tempting, especially when you have a patient with morphological features of megaloblastic change—dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and hypersegmented neutrophils. In this case, we had a patient where the nutritional deficiency was real, treated, and corrected. And yet, the chronic fatigue, organ fibrosis, and premature greying refused to go away. This creates a classic "diagnostic capture" scenario: we successfully treated a nutrient problem and assumed we did the job, leaving a genetic disorder to wind up its clock in the background.

In the case of a woman in her late 20s presenting with a five-year history of progressive fatigue, the initial presentation appeared straightforward. She had generalised weakness, early fatigability, and reduced functional capacity. The exam revealed pallor, a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae, and knuckle hyperpigmentation. The bilirubin was raised, and the LDH was elevated, suggesting some degree of hemolysis or ineffective erythropoiesis. The DAT was positive for IgG, but the low reticulocyte count argued against an immune-mediated destruction. The marrow showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than frank hypoplasia.

The laboratory image screamed B12 deficiency. Serum B12 was low, as was folate. Iron studies showed elevated ferritin, confirming ineffective erythropoiesis rather than iron deficiency. An upper GI endoscopy ruled out obvious malignancy, but a duodenal biopsy showed mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells. This implied the patient couldn't make enough intrinsic factor. The treatment plan seemed clear. Intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for five days, then weekly to monthly maintenance. Folic acid, and propping up iron stores.

But here is where the clinical rigour stops the narrative shortcut. The patient’s symptoms did not resolve. She continued to have progressive hepatomegaly and weight loss. Imaging revealed grade II hepatic steatosis and cirrhotic liver stiffness measured at 18.1 kPa. PET-CT showed diffuse ground-glass haziness in both lung fields, consistent with pulmonary fibrosis. While the B12 deficiency was real and likely exacerbated the ineffective erythropoiesis, the constellation of findings was disproportionate to a simple nutritional deficit. She had premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, and neuropathy. The B12 explained the macrocytosis and hypersegmented neutrophils, but it did not explain the rampant fibrosis or the neurodegeneration.

This is the danger zone in adult medicine, especially in young adults. We tend to treat the most common presentation first. We rule out pernicious anemia or celiac disease. We see the dimorphic smear and the low reticulocyte count. We fix the vitamin. But we often miss the underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD). While the B12 deficiency was a major contributor to her cytopenia, it was superimposed on a fragile hematopoietic reserve caused by a shortened telomere length. Once the telomeres shorten below a functional threshold, the replicative capacity of the bone marrow is compromised. Correcting the nutritional substrate makes the cell healthy for as long as the genetic capacity allows, but it cannot fix the attrition of the endoplasmic telomere length itself.

Ultimately, the clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While initially classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the correlation with the patient's phenotype—pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and premature greying—supported a diagnosis of a Telomere Biology Disorder. Compounding this complexity, it is worth noting that the patient lacked significant family history. In many TBDs, a de novo mutation or incomplete penetrance can mask the inheritance pattern. The *PARN* gene belongs to the family of exoribonucleases, essential for maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DKC)—leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticulate pigmentation—this diagnosis is frequently delayed. Most clinical teams might not suspect a TBD without the classic DC features, yet the "cryptic" signs were lying in plain sight: the fatigue, the grey hair, and the fibrosis.

### Key Takeaways

*   **Don't stop at the correctable deficiency:** Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can mimic marrow failure syndromes, but resolution of the deficiency often unmasks the persistence of a co-existing pathology like a telomere biology disorder.
*   **Look for the "red flags":** In young adults with unexplained pancytopenia, consider the presence of systemic fibrosis (pulmonary or hepatic), premature greying, or skeletal findings like osteoporosis, which are classic markers of accelerated ageing associated with TBDs.
*   **Beware of the "DC Triad":** Dyskeratosis congenita and related disorders often present without the classic oral, nail, and skin triad. Monoallelic variants in *PARN* specifically can present with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4) without the cutaneous signs.
*   **Genetics can reveal the teaspoon:** When the clinical phenotype (pancytopenia, cirrhosis, lung fibrosis) is disproportionate to the acquired etiology (B12 deficiency), genetic testing for telomere biology disorders is warranted even in the absence of a known family history.



# The Hepatopulmonary Fibrosis Nexus: Keeping Your Finger on the Telomere Pulse

When a 27-year-old woman presents with progressive dyspnea, pancytopenia, and signs of organ fibrosis, the impulse in a modern clinical setting is often to check for autoimmune interstitial lung disease, organizing pneumonia, or early-onset liver metastasis. However, as the case report below illustrates, when even a rigorous exclusion of infectious, autoimmune, and nutritional causes leaves you staring at unexplained pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis alongside bone marrow failure, the conversation must shift. Diagnosis for what is likely a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD), specifically linked to the *PARN* gene, reveals a critical diagnostic blind spot: young adulthood fibrosis is not "idiopathic" until proven otherwise.

## The Case: A "Symptom Cluster" That Resists Standard Therapy

This patient presented with a 5-year history of fatigue and weakness that descended into an inability to perform self-care within weeks. On physical exam, the clues to the true etiology were subtle but specific: premature greying of hair, pallor with icterus, and knuckle hyperpigmentation. Perhaps the most striking finding was the "bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae," a hallmark often described in DC (Dyskeratosis Congenita) but frequently overlooked in routine physical assessments.

Her laboratory profile was a confusing mosaic that initially pointed toward nutritional depletion rather than primary marrow failure. We saw severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency alongside a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT). While she had a positive DAT, the reticulocyte count was critically low (0.39%), and the marrow did not show peripheral destruction, but rather ineffective erythropoiesis. Iron studies revealed elevated ferritin, and the smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes and Cabot rings. After months of aggressive B12 replacement and immune workup, the fatigue persisted. The clinical picture included thoracic scoliosis, hepatosplenomegaly (on examination), ground-glass haziness in the lungs on PET-CT, and a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, indicating cirrhosis. The constellation of cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and lung fibrosis in a young adult with a lack of classic autoimmune serologies demanded a departure from standard differential diagnoses.

## When "Idiopathic" Isn't Idiopathic

The pivot to a genetic etiology was triggered by the disproportionate severity of her systemic dysfunction relative to her nutritional deficiencies. While severe B12 deficiency can cause anemia, it does not typically cause lung fibrosis or cirrhosis. Following the clinical-grid approach of tracing the symptoms to the organ systems involved, the presence of pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure in a young patient without the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation) is the key trigger.

The genetics, as detailed in this report, identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While classified as a "Variant of Uncertain Significance" (VUS) due to the complex nature of monoallelic inheritance, the location of the variant within the R3H domain—a highly conserved region essential for telomerase maturation—strongly supports a pathogenic role. This case fills a gap in the literature regarding monoallelic *PARN* mutations. While biallelic variants typically cause Autosomal Recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita (Type 6), monoallelic variants are more commonly associated with Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure (TB-PFBMFT4).

It is crucial to recognize that TBDs can present as "non-IDPF" (histopathologically unrecognizable Pulmonary Fibrosis) or unexplained liver disease in young adults. The absence of classic DC signs should not preclude testing. In our patient, the genetic diagnosis unified the findings: the pulmonary disease was the result of telomere attrition impairing lung epithelial repair, the liver stiffness was due to progressive hepatic fibrosis on the telomere scale, and the pancytopenia was a product of hematopoietic stem cell exhaustion—fractured and exacerbated by the concurrent nutritional stressors.

## Clinical Implications and Variants

The educational value here extends beyond this single case report. In evaluating young adults with multisystem "aging" phenotypes or unexplained organ fibrosis, we must maintain a low threshold for telomere biology assessment. Flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays to measure telomere length remain a gold standard, but germline sequencing is becoming increasingly accessible and often more definitive for variant identification.

Understanding the spectrum of TBDs is also vital for clinical risk stratification. While this patient harbored a monoallelic *PARN* variant which classically favors pulmonary fibrosis (PFBMFT4), she exhibited significant bone marrow failure and hepatic involvement. This highlights that the "phenotypic penetrance" of single-allele variants can be variable. Focusing on the range of organ involvement (lung, liver, bone marrow) rather than just cancer risk or the classic DC triad leads to earlier diagnosis.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Consider TBD in Young Adults:** Unexplained pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or cirrhosis in a patient under 40 should trigger a refusal to accept "idiopathic" diagnoses without telomere assessment.
*   **Look for the Clues:** Rapid-onset greying hair, bald tongue, brittle skin/nails, and premature osteoporosis are key physical exam findings that link organ systems.
*   **Nutrition Doesn't Fix Everything:** Correcting B12 deficiency or autoimmune markers is the first step in suspected cases, but disproportionate clinical improvement often reveals a genetic etiology.
*   **PARN Variants:** A monoallelic *PARN* variant (specifically non-biallelic) is a significant predictor of Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and Bone Marrow Failure.
*   **Hepatic Involvement:** Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are under-recognized features of TBDs and should be surveilled with elastography (like the 18.1 kPa measurement seen here) in these patients.

In the end, the necessity of this diagnosis was clear. While genetic counseling and genetic testing confirmed the VUS status of the *PARN* variant in our case, the emerging literature on haploinsufficiency in telomere maintenance supports the clinical diagnosis. Early recognition of this syndrome isn't just about assigning a code; it's about informing surveillance, managing expectations for stem cell transplantation potential, and understanding that the aging clock is ticking faster than it should in these patients.



## When the Variant is "Unknown": Integrating Phenotype to Interpret Genetic Variants of Uncertain Significance

Finding a pathogenic variant in a patient with an unexplained multisystem disease is exciting. It gives the answer to the mystery. But the opposite scenario is where most of us land in real life: we find a genetic variant, the bioinformatics pipeline flags it, but its significance is a flat "Variant of Uncertain Significance" (VUS). This is the bureaucratic dead end of genetic interpretation. How do you handle a patient with telomere biology disorder (TBD) features when the gene sequence refuses to confirm the diagnosis?

This case challenge is more than theoretical; it highlights a critical skill set for the clinician who sees the chaos before the clarity arrives. We present a woman in her late 20s with pancytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, and advanced liver cirrhosis. By the skin of her teeth, the clinical picture screamed "Telomere Biology Disorder," yet the only genetic finding was a monoallelic PARN missense variant labeled as a VUS. This creates a disconnect between our phenotypic certainty and our genetic statistics. In the hands of a pragmatic clinician, the phenotype acts as an anchor, pulling the interpretation weight of the variant downstream.

## The Clinical Phenotype: A Multisystem Scream for Genetic Help

It starts with the fatigue. Over a five-year span, this patient experienced progressive weakness, exertional dyspnea, and weight loss. By the time she reached our center, her physical exam revealed a constellation of findings that are rarely isolated. She presented with pallor, icterus, and premature greying of her hair. This was accompanied by knuckle hyperpigmentation and atrophy of the lingual papillae, leading to a bald tongue. These are not standard dermatological or hematological findings; they are signals of cellular stress and premature aging.

When you dive into the labs, the picture becomes messier. She has pancytopenia with a dangerously low reticulocyte count. The peripheral smear shows dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and Cabot rings. You see the classic signs of ineffective erythropoiesis. The inflammatory markers were high, and autoimmune serologies were negative. You might jump to pernicious anemia or immune-mediated cytopenias immediately. However, she also had grade II hepatic steatosis, liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa indicating cirrhosis, and lungs filled with diffuse ground-glass haziness consistent with pulmonary fibrosis.

There is a nuance that complicates this specific case. She had severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. The upper GI endoscopy and biopsy confirmed reduced parietal cells, a common cause of malabsorption. Initially, the doctors treated her with high-dose intravenous cyanocobalamin. While this corrected the nutritional parameters and improved her counts temporarily, the underlying systemic dysfunction remained disproportionate to the nutritional deficit alone. The constellation of cytopenia, premature greying, osteoporosis, and organ fibrosis in a young adult is the hallmark of a syndrome that has nothing to do with a vitamin deficiency by default.

## The Genetic Dilemma: A Variant of Uncertain Significance

Here is where the story diverges from a textbook lecture. While the phenotype points toward a telomere biology disorder, the genetic exome sequencing found a specific foot injury: a monoallelic missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg).

Usually, telomere biology disorders are autosomal recessive, often requiring biallelic hits in genes like TERT or TERC. Yet, this patient had only one variant. Furthermore, the specific variant found was classified as a VUS. This classification is not a judgment that the variant is harmless; it is a statement that there is insufficient data to prove it is harmful. PARN variants are known to cause Dyskeratosis Congenita and TD-related pulmonary fibrosis, but this specific missense mutation in the R3H domain was flagged as "uncertain" because the database lacked enough patient cases to confirm its pathogenicity definitively.

The clinician must then overcome the hurdle of "is this genetic test information?" It is common to discard a VUS immediately if the inheritance pattern doesn't match. However, miplation biology disorders often present with broad phenotypic variability and variable penetrance, especially with monoallelic variants acting in a haploinsufficient manner. The critical question becomes: does the patient's phenotype fit the genetic risk profile associated with this gene, even if the variant itself is undervalued by current classification standards?

## Using Phenotype to Drive Interpretation

The resolution of this case lies in the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria, specifically the newly recognized emphasis on clinical phenotype. In this case, the team did not ignore the VUS status. Instead, they applied the gene-specific evidence to push the classification toward "Likely Pathogenic."

The PARN gene itself has incredibly strict criteria for variants to be benign. It is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare variants. The variant in question, p.Cys205Arg, sits within the R3H domain, a critically conserved functional region. There are no known benign variations in this specific spot. This alignment supports the PM1 criterion, which gives evidence based on the residue's domain and its functional importance.

In a report like Life in the Fast Lane or a typical EM clinic chat, we warn against relying on the initial VUS call. The ACMG/AMP standards also point to PM2, noting the variant is absent or extremely rare in population databases. They utilized PP3 to indicate that multiple in silico tools predicted the variant to be damaging. But the strongest point comes back to the clinical reality. The patient had the disease phenotype: pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure in a young adult.

The genetic data in older literature supports that monoallelic PARN variants are more often associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. Even with only one hit, the haploinsufficiency is enough to set up a telomere crisis. The decision to treat the genetic aspect as relevant, despite the "VUS" label, allows for appropriate surveillance. Without this phenotypic integration, the genetic finding would have been ignored, and the family would receive no genetic counseling.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages

*   **When phenotype trumps genetics:** In rare multisystem diseases like telomere biology disorders, a strong constellation of symptoms should not be abandoned just because a genetic finding is a VUS. The clinical picture may justify consideration of the variant's pathogenicity.
*   **ACMG criteria matters for VUS:** Don't just accept the database label. Review the ACMG criteria applied to the variant. Features like location in a critical domain (PM1) and absence in population databases (PM2) can elevate the significance of a variant when supported by the phenotype.
*   **Variable penetrance is rule number one:** Patients with Monoallelic mutations in telomere genes may not fit the classic Dyskeratosis Congenita triad. They may present with isolated organ fibrosis or bone marrow failure in adulthood.
*   **Haploinsufficiency is a driver:** PARN is a haploinsufficient gene; a single defective allele can disrupt the mRNA processing necessary for telomerase maturation.
*   **Correctable vs. Genetic:** Treat reversible causes like Vitamin B12 deficiency, but do not stop searching for the genetic substrate. Correcting a deficiency does not stop the underlying telomere decay, and the patient remains at risk for progression of marrow failure or pulmonary disease.

## Conclusion

The "Variant of Uncertain Significance" label is a placeholder for a clinical decision. It should not be treated as a wall, but as a door that requires further key from the bedside. This case reinforces that genomic interpretation is not a computer algorithm but a tripartite conversation between the lab data, the patient's history, and the disease context. When a patient presents with the somatic burden of cellular aging in a young adult, the VUS status cannot blind us to the probable genetic path.

This patient was managed supportively. We transfused, supplemented, and monitored. She showed improvement, and at a 2-week follow-up, her blood counts were stable with no new deterioration. However, is that the end of the path for the TBD patient who is bleeding out?

Therefore, the role of HSCT shifts from a last resort to a timed intervention. If a TBD patient with pulmonary fibrosis or hepatic steatosis remains young, fit enough for transplant, and demonstrates progressive cytopenia that responds only transiently to nutritional correction, HSCT becomes a viable conversation to prevent bone marrow failure.

## Clinical Take-Home Messages

Here are the core takeaways to keep at your treatment algorithm next time you chase pancytopenia:

*   **Don't rule out TBD just because the mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita is absent.** Adults can present with pancytopenia, osteoporosis, and organ fibrosis without the classic rash or nail changes.
*   **Suspect a genetic disorder in patients with disproportionate symptoms.** If B12 deficiency explains the anemia but not the hepatosplenomegaly or osteoporosis, look deeper. Clinical exome sequencing is becoming accessible and is a diagnostic game-changer.
*   **Consider HSCT for progressive hematological TBDs.** While supportive care is standard, HSCT remains potentially curative for patients with haematological manifestations, distinguishing this from a purely supportive management track for non-hematological fibrotic courses.
*   **Monitor for organ involvement.** This patient had *PARN* mutations but developed significant hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary disease. Because TBDs affect all rapidly dividing tissues, even after correcting a nutritional deficiency, you must surveil the marrow and fibrotic organs regularly.
