Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Dysphagia Without Dyspepsia: Neurogenic Choking When GI Workup Is Normal

As emergency physicians and generalists, we are trained to treat swallowing dysphagia with a specific funnel: consider aoesophageal structural causes, rule out motility disorders, and treat for reflux. The paradigm assumes that when a patient sits up, speaks, but struggles to swallow, the pathology resides in the esophagus. When a patient presents with recurrent choking on solids and liquids, a frantic search for GERD, stricture, or motility starts almost immediately. Flexure scopes, barium studies, and pH probes become the bread and butter. But what happens when the laryngoscopy is normal, the endoscopy finds smooth mucosa, and the patient still collapses on a bite of solid food?

This case report serves as a stark reminder that the swallowing center does not live in your throat. It lives in your pons. We are covering a fascinating diagnostic journey where the culprit was not in the gut, but in the posterior fossa. By ignoring the neurological context within what is often a gastroenterology-heavy workup, we can miss structural brain pathology that presents with subtle but life-threatening respiratory consequences.

## The Clinical Dilemma: Negative GI, Positive Symptoms

The presentation described in this report is a classic diagnostic challenge: a woman in her 40s with a seven-year history of unpredictable choking episodes. These weren’t just occasional gasps; they were nocturnal events occurring multiple times a week that woke her from sleep with coughing and gasping. She avoided restaurants because of the fear of choking in public. It was the kind of social isolation we all know too well.

The initial diagnostic path followed the textbook GI approach. Three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies were performed. All returned "normal." The probability of a hiatal hernia or laryngeal mass was zero. The clinical team assumed the issue was functional or psychological until the patient began documenting oxygen saturation at home. This never happened to be entirely random home monitoring; it was the turning point. During the pandemic, she noticed that her oxygen would plummet to 70%–80% during these episodes. Alarmingly, she realized she never felt short of breath. She didn't gasp for air consciously until she checked her device.

This lack of conscious sensation during desaturation is the most alarming aspect of the clinical picture. Breathlessness is an interoceptive sensation mediated by afferent neural pathways. If that signal is interrupted before it reaches the cortex, a patient can suffer hypoxemia without the drive to breathe. In this patient, the workup for pulmonary pathology was negative, but the pattern screamed central respiratory dysfunction.

## The Discovery: Old Scans, New Findings

Why did it take fourteen years to diagnose the root cause? The answer lies in the history, specifically the timeline of her headaches. Fourteen years prior to the onset of choking, the patient was involved in a minor motor vehicle accident with a loss of consciousness. Post-accident, she developed persistent, pressure-like occipital headaches. These headaches were initially dismissed as nummular headaches and treated with scalp injections with little success.

The pivotal moment in this case was the re-evaluation of historical imaging. Records revealed a non-contrast CT of the head performed immediately following that trauma, reported at the time as normal. However, a careful re-review of that 14-year-old scan uncovered the true culprit: a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion. This was a previously missed sign of Chiari malformation type I (CMI).

A subsequent MRI confirmed 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent. This anatomical deviation had been sitting in her file for a decade, waiting for a symptom that actually forced us to look at the brain rather than the esophagus.

## Pathophysiology: The Bridge Between Bulbar and Breathing

Understanding why this patient choked and desaturated without distress requires understanding the anatomy of the brainstem. CMI presents a volumetric mismatch between neural structures and the available posterior fossa space. As the cerebellar tonsils herniate, they can compress the brainstem and obstruct cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow.

In our patient, this compression had two major effects. First, it affected lower cranial nerve nuclei, leading to oropharyngeal dysfunction (choking on solids and liquids). Second, it impacted the respiratory centers within the brainstem. Sleep studies demonstrated predominantly central sleep apnoea events (72%), indicating that the automatic drive to breathe was suppressed during sleep and sleep, even though voluntary breathing remained intact. This is an acquired form of central hypoventilation, sometimes referred to as 'Ondine’s curse'.

The distinction between voluntary and automatic breathing is critical here. The cortex controls voluntary respiration, which is why the patient remained conscious throughout her desaturation. However, the automatic respiratory drive involves pathways mediated by brainstem regions that were likely compromised by the tonsillar descent. This disconnect explains why the patient failed to wake up gasping, even when her oxygen saturation dropped below 90%. Without the sensation of air hunger, she continued breathing at a rate insufficient to oxygenate her blood.

Furthermore, the association between CMI and occipital/vertex headaches is well-known, particularly those precipitated by Valsalva maneuvers. However, this patient had headaches that were often constant, though occipital. The resolution of her symptoms (specificall headache) with hair cutting suggests scalp allodynia, a known but often overlooked variant in CMI patients.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Messages

This case highlights that thrombosis, not just structures, can lead to missing. It provides a specific framework for when a swallowed patient does not get a reaction. Here is the summary of what to remember when dysphagnosis in the rooting system is normal:

*   **When Standard Dysphagia Workup is Negative:** If laryngoscopy and endoscopy are normal in a patient with persistent choking, raise your suspicion for a brainstem or structural neurological origin, specifically Chiari Malformation Type I.
*   **Review Old Imaging:** Do not leave any imaging unanalyzed. A historical CT head can offer crucial hints of a crowded foramen magnum that might otherwise be overlooked amidst a decade of clinical noise.
*   **Watch for "Ondine’s Curse" Patterns:** Be vigilant for patients who have significant oxygen desaturations but lack respiratory distress or subjective sensation of dyspnea. This dissociation is a significant marker of automated central drive failure.
*   **Underestimate of Sleep Studies:** Polysonmography may underestimate the severity of sleep-disordered breathing in CMI if EEG is not utilized (Type III only). A predominance of central apnoeas alongside bulbar symptoms is a major red flag for brainstem compression.
*   **Unify the Headache History:** Chronic occipital headaches, especially those unresponsive to typical trigeminal or other primary headache treatments, occurring after trauma or long-standing, should trigger an imaging assessment of the foramen magnum.

Chiari malformation presents with a challenge to the clinician because it often mimics more common, less immediately life-threatening conditions. But as we seen in this case, the respiratory consequences of brainstem compression are profound. Recognizing this structural connection is essential for the safety of patients who present with unexplained bulbar and central respiratory dysfunction. If the throat is clean, look up.
