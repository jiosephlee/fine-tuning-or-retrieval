Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Acquired or Unmasked? Separating Trauma Sequelae from Primary Chiari Malformation

When a patient presents with unexplained choking and profound oxygen desaturation, most of our respiratory algorithms point immediately to aspiration, cardiac ischemia, or obstructive airway disease. It is easy, almost automatic, to send the patient home with a clarithromycin and a pulse oxygen advice sheet. But what happens when the laryngoscopy is normal, the pulse oximeter shows a patient dropping to 80% saturation without dyspnea, and the esophagus is patent? 

In this case, we are looking at a woman in her early 40s with a 7-year history of recurrent choking that affected both solids and liquids, accompanied by nocturnal episodes occurring three to four times weekly. The social consequence was severe; she avoided public gatherings, and the family physicians spent years chasing gastroenterological and otolaryngological causes. The real kicker? She couldn’t be distressed about the hypoxia. Home monitoring during the pandemic incidentally revealed oxygensaturation dropping to the 70%–80% range without respiratory distress. She literally forgot to breathe. This case report details how structural brainstem pathology caused central hypoventilation mimicking an acquired form of Andy Hardy’s “Ondine’s curse.”

## The Neural Mimicry of Central Hypoventilation

The most disarming feature of this presentation is the dissociation between physiological distress and subjective awareness. The patient experienced central apneae where the automatic respiratory drive during sleep failed, yet voluntary breathing remained intact while she was awake. In a prospective study of adults with Chiari malformation type I (CMI), 50% had sleep-related breathing disorders, but pure central sleep apnoea is rare. This patient had a sleep study with predominantly central events at 72%, driving the oxygen desaturation.

However, the AHI was only 6.0. We must examine this number with skepticism. That low index was derived from a type III sleep study which lacks EEG monitoring. Without accurate sleep staging, the AHI calculation overestimates true sleep time, likely leading to a significant underestimation of the severity of the respiratory events. If the patient breathes voluntarily when distracted but their automatic drive fails when sleeping, you are seeing a phenotype of acquired central hypoventilation. Pulmonary function tests, including pulmonary diffusing capacity, were normal, pointing away from primary lung pathology and solidly toward a neurogenic etiology.

## The Diagnostic Lag: A Lesson in Image Reconstruction

The 14-year diagnostic delay highlights a sedative yet critical lesson in image review. For much of this patient’s life, standard imaging was normal or the findings were missed. A non-contrast CT scan from 14 years ago, performed for a minor motor vehicle accident, revealed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. This was reported as normal at the time but, upon retrospective review, showed an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent. Current MRI confirmed Chiari type I. 

This history raises a specific question for the emergency clinician and the physician managing chronic headaches. If a patient has headaches that look like a migraine or tension-type disorder, but there is a history of head trauma followed by new onset symptoms, we must ask: Was the trauma the cause, or did it unmask a congenital variant? Chiari I is defined by caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils. In the general population, it is found in 1% of adults. This diagnosis is often symptomatic of a volumetric mismatch between neural structures and the posterior fossa.

The mechanism here involves cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow obstruction and direct compression of the brainstem. In adults, lower cranial nerve involvement is often present (15–26%), which explains the choking episodes associated with dysfunction of the oropharyngeal or laryngeal muscles. This is why the videofluoroscopic swallowing study was likely crucial despite the normal scopes. The neurological exam in this patient showed allodynia at the vertex, where the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. Cutting her hair reduced this baseline pain, suggesting a sensitized trigeminocervical complex often seen in CMI patients.

## Trauma as Catalyst or Unmasker?

The contemporary approach to CMI distinguishes between primary (congenital) malformations and acquired forms resulting from trauma or increased intracranial pressure. In this case, the trauma preceded the symptom onset by 14 years. The immediate onset of occipital headaches post-accident suggests a potential traumatic hemodynamic shift. However, without pre-trauma imaging, we cannot determine if the tonsillar herniation happened during the trauma or was merely subclinical until provoked by the insult. 

The literature suggests a spectrum rather than a binary path. While childhood trauma can blunt the posterior fossa or cause scarring, in this patient, the temporal relationship between the accident and the headaches could reflect either traumatic pressure causing tonsillar descent or a congenital variant exacerbated by trauma. The distinction matters for counseling and follow-up, though the immediate clinical management focuses on the unmasking of symptoms rather than the origin of the anatomy. The patient chose conservative management over posterior fossa decompression, requiring structured monitoring with monthly assessments and an annual MRI.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Choking with normal laryngoscopy:** If choking affects solids and liquids equally and scopes are normal, consider central dysphagia from brainstem pathology rather than mechanical obstruction.
*   **"Silent" Hypoxia:** Unexplained oxygen desaturation on home monitoring without symptoms of distress is a potential indicator of central hypoventilation due to brainstem compression.
*   **Historical Imaging:** Re-evaluating old imaging, especially CT scans from trauma, is vital for Chiari malformation diagnosis when the clinical picture does not fit the timeline or standard workup.
*   **Non-EEG Sleep Studies:** A-type III sleep study may underestimate sleep apnoea events; persistently large central apneas with anatomic crowding warrant EEG monitoring.
*   **Surgical vs. Conservative:** Posterior fossa decompression is often recommended for symptomatic relief, but multidisciplinary decision-making should respect patient autonomy, with strict surveillance protocols for ongoing clinical improvement.
