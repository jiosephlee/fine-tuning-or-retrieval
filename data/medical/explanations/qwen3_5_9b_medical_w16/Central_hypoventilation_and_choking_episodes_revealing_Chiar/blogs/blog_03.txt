Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# The Power of Stewardship: Uncovering Chiari Through Historical CT Review

## The Aircraft Gate Oxygen Drop

We've all seen the patient who comes in with a "hair shirt" symptom profile—dyspnea without a lung issue, cardiac arrest without a heart issue, choking that won't quit. This week, I want to share a case report that highlights a crucial diagnostic pivot often overlooked in the metric-driven world of modern medicine. A woman in her early 40s presented with a 14-year diagnostic odyssey defined by recurrent choking, nocturnal desaturation, and unexplained headaches. By the time we reached her console, she had endured 14 years of testing. She had normal laryngoscopy, normal endoscopy, normal lung function, and negative autoimmune workups. It was paid for by the healthcare system and the patient's emotional well-being.

The most telling clue wasn't found in the latest MRI. It was found in a non-contrast CT scan taken 14 years prior—a red herrish CAT scan from a motor vehicle accident. When the team re-reviewed that archived data, the lights came on. A crowded foramen magnum. The finding confirmed an under-recognized Chiari malformation type I (CMI), explaining why she was forgetting to breathe, why she was choking, and why her dyspnea was essentially a neurological blind spot. This case is a masterclass in the clinical stewardship of our archives.

## The Diagnostic Dolio-Augury

This patient’s journey typifies the frustration encountered when we treat based on organ systems rather than the whole person. She arrived with vocal dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally—a classic red flag for a neurological rather than mechanical issue—but the reflex was GI. Three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies were performed. Everything looked perfect. The distress, however, didn't stop.

The graph that eventually broke the case was the home pulse oximetry data acquired during the pandemic. She wasn't coughing until she checked the number. She wasn't gasping until the device blinked her oxygen down to 72%–80%. There was no respiratory distress, no tachycardia yet no apnea—just asymptomatic hypoxia.

This lack of respiratory distress is the hallmark of brainstem pathology affecting the ascending afferent pathways. The circuit is intact enough to force air in when you try, but the automatic drive (medullary chemoreflexes) is lost during sleep or distraction. When she tripped in the airport and her O2 index dropped to 78%, she wasn't *aware* of the danger. The brainstem, compressed by the tonsillar descent, interrupting the interoceptive signals to the cortex. A lack of "air hunger" makes the condition dangerously insidious.

## The Radiographic Clue: Looking Backwards

The narrative twist is the re-evaluation of the 14-year-old CT. It is frustrating that the malformation wasn't caught then. With a non-contrast CT, you are limited to bony landmarks and basic soft tissue density, yet the anatomy speaks for itself if you know where to look.

We need to be disciplined in the review of historical imaging. When a workup is negative but symptoms persist, prior studies are not "junk" data; they are the most important clinical history on the planet. For the radiographic signs of a crowded foramen in this context, look for:

*   **Crowded Foramen Magnum:** The space is patent, but the neural tissue is breaching the boundaries.
*   **Loss of CSF Space:** On the CT, look for the venous sinus and the bony arch. There should be a flash of CSF space (the cisterna magna is usually better seen on MRI, but the flow dynamics are often hinted at by bone scalloping). In this case, there was a distinct loss of space at the craniocervical junction.
*   **Tonsillar Extension:** You can identify the cerebellar tonsils pushing through the foramen if the resolution is sufficient, even on CT.

The subsequent MRI confirmed 11 mm of tonsillar descent. While CMI is defined as at least 5 mm, the debate persists on what constitutes pathology. However, this patient was symptomatic. The mechanical compression of the lower brainstem nuclei leads to the clinical triad we see: bulbar dysfunction (choking/dysphagia), headache (often occipital or vertex), and respiratory instability (central hypopnea).

## The Stewardship Strategy

Why does this case matter beyond the wireframes and lumbar scallops? It brings the value of stewardship into clinical decision-making.

*   **Cost Efficiency:** This patient was proposed for posterior fossa decompression. However, before the intervention was discussed, we utilized existing data to make a decision. The hospital saved resources; the patient avoided repeated invasive diagnostic CTs.
*   **Diagnostic Precocity:** If the answer has been sitting in the EMR for 14 years, we are the ones tasked with finding it. Delay is not the job service; patience is. We must ask, "What have we already imaged?"
*   **Clinical Horizons:** In any adult patient with refractory dysphagia, central sleep apnea, or headaches exacerbated by positional effects or trauma history, a fossa examination is mandatory. If the endoscopy fails, go to the spine.

This is not just a case of a rarely seen anomaly. It is a case of the brainstem failing its job to tell the chest to "go." The waking mind tries to breathe, but the sleep drive vanishes. And the asymptotic oxygen desaturation confirms that our reliance on subjective panic is misplaced physiology.

## Take-Home Messages

*   Suspect Chiari Malformation Type I in patients with refractory choking, central sleep apnea, or occipital headaches that persist despite negative GI/ENT workups.
*   Check archiving systems. Re-evaluating historical imaging (even old non-contrast CTs) can reveal previously overlooked structural causes.
*   Anatomic variations (e.g., tonsillar descent > 5 mm) are not pathological in and of themselves; symptoms dictate the threshold for intervention.
*   Asymptomatic hypoxia documented via home oximetry or cuff checks is a powerful sentinel sign of brainstem respiratory instability.
*   Central hypopnea with loss of air hunger indicates a disconnect in ascending cerebral pathways rather than lung or heart failure.

## Conclusion

This case is a reminder that the most expensive diagnostic test is a diagnostic delay. We see a modern culture where we order more to be safe, but the smartest clinicians use what is in their hands. A simple decision to scroll back to a scan from 14 years ago led to a unifying diagnosis for a decade-long mystery. When a patient presents with a symptom constellation that medical convention cannot explain, look for the anatomy before you order the function. Sometimes the cure is hidden in the past.
