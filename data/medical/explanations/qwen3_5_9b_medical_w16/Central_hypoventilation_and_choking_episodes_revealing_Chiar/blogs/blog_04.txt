Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Scalp Allodynia and Vertex Pain: A Neurological Clue Missed for Decades

There are certain phrases that stand out in a patient’s history more than any diagnostic code or vital sign trend. In emergency medicine and neurology, we often chase the big red flags: aphasia, hemiparesis, or seizures. But we frequently miss the subtle, chronic complaints that sit at the periphery of our diagnostic gaze. Consider the patient who tells you that cutting their hair relieves their headache. In a case report detailing a 14-year diagnostic odyssey for Chiari malformation type I, this was the smoking gun. A woman in her early 40s presented with what we should recognize as end-stage central hypoventilation and significant respiratory failure, masked for years by what she perceived as anxiety or heartburn. However, the most striking clinical feature was her vertex pain and scalp allodynia, which responded drastically to altering her hairstyle. This post explores how vertex pain and scalp hypersensitivity can be a missed neurological clue for posterior fossa cerebellar displacement.

## The Diagnostic Odyssey

We are often taught to associate Chiari malformation type I with a paroxysmal occipital headache, but this case illustrates the heterogeneity of the condition. The patient presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes that affected both solids and liquids equally. These were nocturnal in frequency, occurring three to four times weekly, and profound enough to cause significant social withdrawal. Despite multiple flexible laryngoscopies and upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, no anatomical abnormalities were found, leading clinicians down a path of gastroenterology and laryngology rather than neurology. The situation became more sinister during the COVID-19 pandemic when home monitoring revealed she was experiencing severe oxygen desaturation, dropping to 70%–80%, without any reported respiratory distress or air hunger. A similar incident at an airport led to denied boarding due to saturation of 78% that resolved within minutes of supplemental oxygen administration.

The physical examination was deceptively normal by conventional standards, with normal heart, blood pressure, and respiratory rates. However, the history is where the story began to unfold. The symptoms were reportedly precipitated 14 years prior by a minor motor vehicle accident involving a brief loss of consciousness. This timeline suggests either that the trauma triggered the symptoms in a previously silent condition or that the trauma unmasked a congenital anatomical variant. A review of a historical non-contrast CT scan from 14 years earlier, originally reported as normal, accidentally revealed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Current MRI confirmed a 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent. Despite the severity of the findings, the patient had a symptomatic presentation so diverse that it fooled multiple specialties for over a decade.

## Beyond Occipital Pain

The most unique component of this case report, and the focus of this post, is the nature of the headache itself. The patient described her headaches as constant, pressure-like, and localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex. This was accompanied by marked allodynia, where innocuous touch, such as the weight of her hair, triggered significant discomfort. Over the years, this scalp pain was misdiagnosed as nummular headache, and the patient underwent multiple local scalp injections with only transient relief. It wasn't until she cut her hair short that she reported a reduction in baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10. While this decision created emotional distress and conflicted with cultural expectations, it provided the retrospective clinical evidence necessary to link her cutaneous symptoms to her central nervous system findings.

The pathophysiology supporting this connection is rooted in the mechanics of tonsillar descent. A developmentally small posterior fossa creates a volumetric mismatch between the cerebellum and available space. As the tonsils descend 5 mm or more, they compress the brainstem at the craniocervical junction. While card and thro cardinal signs include occipital headaches precipitated by Valsalva, the literature is expanding to include scalp hypersensitivity. In this patient, the pain sensation from the scalp is transmitted via sensory afferents that bypass higher cortical processing to reach the brainstem. By disconnecting or compressing these pathways, the automatic regulation of breathing is disrupted, while the sensation of breathlessness itself is lost. This explains why the patient had profound hypoxia without air hunger, likely disconnecting the afferent signals that must ascend to the cortex to generate the feeling of air starvation.

## Clinical Implications and Next Steps

This case serves as a critical reminder that unexplained respiratory dysfunction and bulbar symptoms should prompt a neurological workup. Standard pulmonary function tests and normal chest imaging excluded lung pathology, but the central apnoea index was significant in the sleep study. The study demonstrated a predominance of central events at 72%, supporting the concept of brainstem compression affecting the respiratory control centers. However, the sleep study was a Type III, which lacks EEG monitoring, leading to an underestimation of the apnoea–hypopnoea index due to inclusion of wakefulness time in the total recording period. Despite this limitation, the presence of severe desaturation without dyspnoea and the anatomical compression warrants attention.

When evaluating patients with chronic headaches and swallowing difficulties, we must keep the posterior fossa in our differential, even without the classic cough-precipitated occipital pain. If a patient presents with refractory choking, vomiting vertigo, or headaches that respond to mechanical pressure reduction of the scalp, consider imaging that focuses on the foramen magnum. We cannot rely on previous imaging if the skull position or review was inconsistent. The decision for posterior fossa decompression surgery in this case was debated, and she opted for conservative management initially with structured monitoring. The takeaway remains that unexplained symptoms of oropharyngeal dysfunction are neurogenic in origin a significant percentage of the time, and missing that diagnosis can delay life-saving intervention.

## Take-Home Messages
*   **Scalp allodynia is an under-recognized symptom:** In patients with Chiari malformation type I, cutaneous hypersensitivity at the vertex and scalp may manifest as non-painful pressure that converts to pain, often leading to misdiagnosis as primary headache disorders.
*   **Mechanical relief is a red flag:** A history where hair cutting, collars, or Valsalva changes headache severity warrants neuroimaging to rule out tonsillar herniation and brainstem compression.
*   **Differentiate voluntary from automatic breathing:** Patients with Chiari should be evaluated for central sleep apnoea where voluntary respiration remains intact (no air hunger during hypoxia), distinguishing brainstem dysfunction from primary cardiac or pulmonary causes.
*   **Review historical imaging carefully:** Old CT scans may contain overlooked signs of a crowded foramen magnum that were missed during initial interpretation due to different clinical contexts.
*   **Bulbar dysfunction precedes the diagnosis:** Dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally without mechanical obstruction should trigger a search for neurogenic causes, specifically brainstem compression, as endoscopy will be unrevealing.
