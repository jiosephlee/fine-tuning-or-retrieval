Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenges

The clinical presentation of this case involves a complex triad of central hypoventilation, oropharyngeal dysphagia, and persistent cephalalgia that spanned over a decade prior to final identification. The patient, a woman in her early 40s, initially sought medical attention for a seven-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. Distinct from mechanical obstruction seen in structural airway pathology, the equal difficulty with all consistencies suggests an underlying neuromuscular or central etiology. Concurrently, she reported a history of nocturnal hypoxemia and chronic headaches that predated the choking symptoms, having originated fourteen years prior to the clinic visit following a motor vehicle accident. This prolonged duration and the combination of seemingly disconnected neurological and respiratory symptoms created a formidable diagnostic puzzle that initially led clinicians down non-neurological pathways.

A key pedagogical feature of this case is the nature of the dysphagia and the subsequent negative gastrointestinal workup. The patient underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all of which were reported as normal. In standard medical practice, choking is frequently attributed to gastro-oesophageal reflux disease or structural esophageal pathology; however, negative pH studies and normal endoscopy in the setting of persistent choking should trigger a broader neurological evaluation. The lack of a structural cause on direct visualization points toward neurogenic dysphagia, particularly when the patient can swallow voluntarily but experiences distress, coughing, or gagging without clear laryngeal exam findings. This disconnect highlights that bulbar dysfunction can arise from CNS pathology rather than peripheral airway or esophageal stenosis.

The respiratory manifestations of this case provide a critical lesson in recognizing central hypoventilation patterns. While sleep-disordered breathing is well recognized, it is frequently associated with observed apneas or significant daytime symptoms. In contrast, this patient documented asymptomatic oxygen saturations between 70% and 80% using home pulse oximetry during the pandemic, often during wakefulness. This phenomenon is characteristic of brainstem compromise, where the automatic respiratory drive—controlled by medullary centers—is disrupted, while the voluntary drive mediated by cortical pathways remains intact. The airport incident further illustrated this safety risk: the patient experienced acute desaturation without the sensation of dyspnea or air hunger, relying instead on rescue oxygen to prevent life-threatening hypoxia. The absence of conscious distress during significant hypoxia is a specific "red flag" that necessitates a neurological workup rather than a purely pulmonary one.

The chronic headache profile adds another layer to the clinical challenge, serving as an early marker for foramen magnum pathology despite atypical characteristics. The patient described constant, pressure-like occipital and vertex headaches that were notably unexacerbated by Valsalva maneuvers, coughing, or straining, which can contradict the classic teaching of positional Chiari headaches. However, the presence of marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of hair triggered pain, supported a diagnosis of neuralgic origin. The resolution of baseline headache pain with hair cutting, alongside the temporal onset following trauma, provided clues to the underlying structural etiology. While Valsalva headaches are commonly reinforced as a cardinal sign, clinicians must recognize variants where chronically altered CSF dynamics manifest as allodynia and constant pressure without provocation by increased intrathoracic pressure.

The impact of these unexplained symptoms on the patient’s quality of life underscores the severity of missed diagnostic findings. The inability to safely eat in public led to significant social withdrawal and anxiety, highlighting that "negative" test results do not necessarily equate to symptom resolution for the patient. Initial investigations, including normal chest imaging, pulmonary function tests, metabolic panels, and thyroid function tests, effectively excluded pulmonary, metabolic, and thyroid causes. This systematic elimination of common respiratory and metabolic disorders is a necessary step in the diagnostic reasoning process, yet it also emphasizes the need for a higher index of suspicion regarding intracranial anatomy in patients with the specific combination of unexplained dysphagia, desaturation, and occipital headache patterns. The eventual identification of a previously overlooked CT scan from fourteen years earlier served as the pivotal link, reinforcing the importance of reviewing historical imaging in chronic, multisystem presentations.
