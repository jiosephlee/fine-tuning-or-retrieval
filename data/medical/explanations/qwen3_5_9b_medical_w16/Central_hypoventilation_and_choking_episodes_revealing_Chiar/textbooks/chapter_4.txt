Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Strategies and Patient-Centered Management

The management of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is distinct for its potential to present with reversible symptomatic dysfunction once the mechanical obstruction is identified in symptomatic patients. In the context of this case, the decision-making framework centered on weighing the indications for surgical intervention against the benefits of conservative observation. Posterior fossa decompression is generally the curative treatment for symptomatic patients, as it addresses the root cause—cranio-cervical junction compression and obstructive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow—thereby potentially reversing respiratory and cranial nerve deficits. Specifically, posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF dynamics and alleviate symptoms such as central hypoventilation or dysphagia. However, the decision to proceed is not solely dictated by anatomical findings. In this patient, while surgical intervention was recommended due to the severity of her central hypoventilation and life-threatening risk of hypoxaemia, the complexity of her presentation required a careful discussion of risks versus benefits. The potential for neurological deficit associated with any surgery must be balanced against the risks of prolonged untreated dysfunction, requiring a tailored approach rather than a universal mandate for operating.

When surgery is deferred, conservative management serves topreserve function and mitigate symptom burden while awaiting definitive intervention or further diagnostic clarification. In this case, the management plan included dietary modifications to facilitate safe swallowing during choking episodes and strategies to reduce anxiety surrounding public social interactions that were previously avoided. Additionally, the patient recognized that managing her unique headaches involved cutting her hair short, a form of conservative care that addressed the cutaneous allodynia associated with her occipital neuralgia symptoms. While cough suppression is a standard adjunct in the conservative management of bulbar symptoms to prevent asphyxiation during shortness of breath episodes, this patient prioritized non-pharmacologic coping strategies. This highlights the importance of addressing the psychosocial impact of chronic symptoms, where anxiety and the fear of choking can exacerbate the patient's experience, even in the absence of frank airway obstruction.

A critical component of the conservative approach in CMI is the establishment of a rigorous monitoring protocol to detect clinical deterioration early. The case report outlines a structured surveillance plan that prioritizes the safety of the patient while respecting the patient's current choice to observe. This plan includes monthly clinical assessments to evaluate headache and dysphagia severity, quarterly overnight oximetry to track oxygen saturation trends, and annual MRI surveillance to monitor cerebellar tonsillar descent and foramen magnum dynamics. This triad of monitoring allows for timely intervention if the patient’s status changes; specifically, any clinical deterioration would necessitate revisiting the recommendation for surgical decompression. This protocol also acknowledges the limitations of previous diagnostic tools in this case, such as the Type III sleep study, by planning for future, more comprehensive evaluations at specialized centers once the patient is ready.

Patient autonomy remains a central tenet in managing structural neurological conditions where the threshold for surgery is high and symptoms can be episodic or atypical. In this scenario, the patient elected to delay surgery to allow for the coordination of a definitive Type I polysomnography to confirm the nature of her central events further and to align with her cultural and personal preferences regarding body modification for pain management. This shared decision-making process underscores the principle that while guidelines may recommend surgery for equivalent symptomatic severity, the patient’s values regarding risk, timing, and diagnostic certainty must guide the final management plan. The clinical team’s role is to provide a structured safety net—through the defined monitoring protocols—rather than imposing a timeline for intervention, ensuring that the patient maintains control over her care trajectory while remaining protected against life-threatening complications.
