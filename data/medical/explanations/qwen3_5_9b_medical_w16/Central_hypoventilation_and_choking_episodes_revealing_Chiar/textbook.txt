Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenges

The clinical presentation of this case involves a complex triad of central hypoventilation, oropharyngeal dysphagia, and persistent cephalalgia that spanned over a decade prior to final identification. The patient, a woman in her early 40s, initially sought medical attention for a seven-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. Distinct from mechanical obstruction seen in structural airway pathology, the equal difficulty with all consistencies suggests an underlying neuromuscular or central etiology. Concurrently, she reported a history of nocturnal hypoxemia and chronic headaches that predated the choking symptoms, having originated fourteen years prior to the clinic visit following a motor vehicle accident. This prolonged duration and the combination of seemingly disconnected neurological and respiratory symptoms created a formidable diagnostic puzzle that initially led clinicians down non-neurological pathways.

A key pedagogical feature of this case is the nature of the dysphagia and the subsequent negative gastrointestinal workup. The patient underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all of which were reported as normal. In standard medical practice, choking is frequently attributed to gastro-oesophageal reflux disease or structural esophageal pathology; however, negative pH studies and normal endoscopy in the setting of persistent choking should trigger a broader neurological evaluation. The lack of a structural cause on direct visualization points toward neurogenic dysphagia, particularly when the patient can swallow voluntarily but experiences distress, coughing, or gagging without clear laryngeal exam findings. This disconnect highlights that bulbar dysfunction can arise from CNS pathology rather than peripheral airway or esophageal stenosis.

The respiratory manifestations of this case provide a critical lesson in recognizing central hypoventilation patterns. While sleep-disordered breathing is well recognized, it is frequently associated with observed apneas or significant daytime symptoms. In contrast, this patient documented asymptomatic oxygen saturations between 70% and 80% using home pulse oximetry during the pandemic, often during wakefulness. This phenomenon is characteristic of brainstem compromise, where the automatic respiratory drive—controlled by medullary centers—is disrupted, while the voluntary drive mediated by cortical pathways remains intact. The airport incident further illustrated this safety risk: the patient experienced acute desaturation without the sensation of dyspnea or air hunger, relying instead on rescue oxygen to prevent life-threatening hypoxia. The absence of conscious distress during significant hypoxia is a specific "red flag" that necessitates a neurological workup rather than a purely pulmonary one.

The chronic headache profile adds another layer to the clinical challenge, serving as an early marker for foramen magnum pathology despite atypical characteristics. The patient described constant, pressure-like occipital and vertex headaches that were notably unexacerbated by Valsalva maneuvers, coughing, or straining, which can contradict the classic teaching of positional Chiari headaches. However, the presence of marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of hair triggered pain, supported a diagnosis of neuralgic origin. The resolution of baseline headache pain with hair cutting, alongside the temporal onset following trauma, provided clues to the underlying structural etiology. While Valsalva headaches are commonly reinforced as a cardinal sign, clinicians must recognize variants where chronically altered CSF dynamics manifest as allodynia and constant pressure without provocation by increased intrathoracic pressure.

The impact of these unexplained symptoms on the patient’s quality of life underscores the severity of missed diagnostic findings. The inability to safely eat in public led to significant social withdrawal and anxiety, highlighting that "negative" test results do not necessarily equate to symptom resolution for the patient. Initial investigations, including normal chest imaging, pulmonary function tests, metabolic panels, and thyroid function tests, effectively excluded pulmonary, metabolic, and thyroid causes. This systematic elimination of common respiratory and metabolic disorders is a necessary step in the diagnostic reasoning process, yet it also emphasizes the need for a higher index of suspicion regarding intracranial anatomy in patients with the specific combination of unexplained dysphagia, desaturation, and occipital headache patterns. The eventual identification of a previously overlooked CT scan from fourteen years earlier served as the pivotal link, reinforcing the importance of reviewing historical imaging in chronic, multisystem presentations.

## Pathophysiology: Chiari Malformation and Central Respiratory Dysfunction

The anatomical foundation of this patient’s presentation begins with the definition and measurement of Chiari malformation type I (CMI). Formally, CMI is defined by the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal magnetic resonance imaging. While this displacement exceeds the normal variant threshold, the clinical significance is not determined by the degree of herniation alone but by the resulting functional consequences. In this case, imaging confirmed an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils, accompanied by documented cerebrospial fluid (CSF) dynamics obstruction. Contemporary imaging studies highlight a "volumetric mismatch" caused by a developmentally small posterior fossa, which creates a restrictive environment for neural structures. This anatomical constraint is essential to understand regarding the patient’s history; a review of a 14-year-old CT scan from a motor vehicle accident revealed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. Although the 11 mm tonsillar descent appears chronic on current imaging, it may represent a pre-existing variant unmasked by trauma, a phenomenon noted in clinical literature where compression exacerbates or reveals latent pathology.

As the anatomical defect involves the foramen magnum, it inevitably affects the brainstem, particularly the medulla and pons, which house the primary respiratory control centers. This anatomical compression disrupts the automatic drive required for respiration, particularly during sleep or when the patient is not actively focusing on breathing. The polysomnography in this case demonstrated a predominance of central events (72%), distinguishing the respiratory abnormality from obstructive sleep apnea where anatomical airway collapse is the primary mechanism. Instead, the breathing events here reflect a failure of neural signaling to trigger breath initiation despite the absence of airway obstruction, as evidenced by normalized pulmonary function tests and chest imaging. Consequently, the patient manifests respiratory failure that closely mimics acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, or Ondine’s curse. This distinction is critical for the medical learner: in CMI, the lungs and airways are structurally patent, but the cortical command to breathe autonomously is intermittently suppressed due to pressure on the respiratory nuclei. This explains the nocturnal choking and recurrent episodes occurring during sleep, where the reliance on automatic brainstem drive is maximal.

A further layer of complexity in this pathophysiology regarding respiratory function is the dissociation between physiological need and subjective perception. Clinically, the patient experienced severe oxygen desaturation dropping to 70%–80% levels down to unconsciousness, yet reported no sensation of air hunger or dyspnea during these events. This phenomenon is attributed to the vertebral body and foramen magnum location, where the afferent neural pathways that ascend from the brainstem to the cortex to process interoceptive signals may be disrupted. Because these ascending afferent fibers carry the "danger signal" of hypoxia, their interruption by pressure from the herniated tonsils means the cortex fails to receive the warning of "air hunger." This is why manual distraction or momentary loss of cortical awareness, such as waiting at an airport, precipitated acute desaturation without the patient realizing their physiological distress until intervention occurred. This specific mechanism differentiates compressive brainstem pathology from metabolic causes of hypoxia, where the sensation of breathlessness would remain intact until failure occurred.

Finally, the pathophysiology of bulbar symptoms, specifically the choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids equally, reflects compression of lower cranial nerve nuclei more broadly. While the patient had normal laryngoscopies, the equal difficulty with both consistencies suggests a neurogenic dysphagia rather than a mechanical obstruction. This aligns with the involvement of the medullary swallowing centers situated within the posterior fossa space that is compromised by the tonsillar descent. Together, the volumetric mismatch, the specific compression of the medullary respiratory centers, and the disruption of interoceptive pathways create a unique clinical phenotype. Therefore, patients presenting with unexplained respiratory dysfunction, particularly those with combined central apnea and bulbar symptoms, require targeted neurological evaluation rather than just pulmonary or gastrointestinal workups. The diagnosis is established not merely by the presence of tonsillar descent, but by the symptom-complex that correlates with the anatomical compression at the craniocervical junction.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Extraneous Findings to Anatomical Confirmation

The diagnostic trajectory in this case illustrates the clinical utility of archival imaging review in identifying long-overlooked anatomical variants. While the patient underwent extensive evaluation for choking and headaches over a 14-year period, these investigations largely failed to identify an intracranial cause until the re-examination of a non-contrast CT scan obtained 14 years prior during a motor vehicle accident. This historical CT, originally reported as normal for the acute trauma, revealed on close scrutiny a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion. This finding, which represents a reduction of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction, serves as a screening indicator for Chiari malformation type I (CMI). The process underscores a critical learning point for clinical practice: the re-evaluation of historical imaging can retrospectively unify chronic, multi-system symptoms, particularly when current investigations lack sufficient sensitivity to detect subtle posterior fossa anomalies compared to retrospective radiological review.

Following the re-examination of the CT, definitive neuroimaging via MRI of the brain and cervical spine was performed to confirm the anatomical diagnosis. The MRI standardized the assessment by measuring the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils, which are displaced by 11 mm below the foramen magnum. According to contemporary diagnostic criteria, CMI is defined by tonsillar descent of at least 5 mm; thus, this patient's 11 mm descent represents a clinically significant herniation. In addition to the tonsillar displacement, the MRI confirmed the loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction observed on the archived CT. This anatomical confirmation validated the CT findings and provided a structural explanation for the patient’s neurological deficits, distinguishing the underlying pathology from functional causes that might have been suggested by the longitudinal clinical history alone.

The integration of sleep study data was pivotal in linking the structural findings to the patient’s respiratory complaints. A Type III sleep study (which lacks electroencephalogram monitoring but records airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and heart rate) demonstrated a mild apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) of 6.0 per hour. However, the distribution of these events was highly significant, with 72% classified as central rather than obstructive. In a typical Type III study without EEG, the AHI is calculated against total recording time, which can include periods of wakefulness and lead to an underestimation of the true sleep-disordered breathing burden. Despite this limitation, the predominance of central apnoea, coupled with documented asymptomatic oxygen desaturation to 70%–80% events, strongly indicated brainstem compression approaching the respiratory center. This pattern aligned with the case of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), where the automatic respiratory drive is compromised while voluntary breathing remains intact, providing a physiological correlation to the structural MRI findings.

To establish CMI as the unifying diagnosis, a systematic exclusion of mimicking conditions was required, as the patient’s symptoms of choking, dysphagia, and hypoxia initially suggested primary visceral or neuromuscular pathology. Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease was ruled out via normal upper endoscopies and pH monitoring, as the patient experienced choking affecting both solids and liquids equally—a hallmark of neurogenic dysphagia rather than mechanical obstruction or aspiration. Myasthenia gravis was excluded through normal acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibodies and the absence of fatigability or diurnal variation in symptoms. Furthermore, primary pulmonary causes were eliminated via normal chest imaging and pulmonary function testing. This process of systematic exclusion is a fundamental diagnostic principle; dismissing common etiologies without sufficient evidence prompted the necessary shift toward rarer neurological diagnoses, thereby narrowing the differential diagnosis to structural brainstem pathology consistent with CMI.

Finally, the diagnostic synthesis required reconciling the patient's symptom timeline with the physical finding of posterior fossa crowding. The patient’s history noted the onset of occipital headaches and the subacute progression of choking symptoms following a motor vehicle accident 14 years prior. The correlation between head trauma and CMI is a area of evolving understanding, where trauma may either induce herniation in the setting of a decompensated posterior fossa or unmask a previously asymptomatic congenital variant. Given the immediate onset of headaches post-injury and the subsequent 14-year progression involving bulbar signs and respiratory compromise, the clinical reasoning favored a diagnosis where trauma exacerbated a developmental anomaly rather than causing primary CMI de novo. Recognizing this link allowed the medical team to understand the trajectory of the disease, moving from a fragmented working diagnosis of cardiac or metabolic issues to a unified neurological assessment that prioritized structural decompression as a potential therapeutic option.

## Therapeutic Strategies and Patient-Centered Management

The management of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is distinct for its potential to present with reversible symptomatic dysfunction once the mechanical obstruction is identified in symptomatic patients. In the context of this case, the decision-making framework centered on weighing the indications for surgical intervention against the benefits of conservative observation. Posterior fossa decompression is generally the curative treatment for symptomatic patients, as it addresses the root cause—cranio-cervical junction compression and obstructive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow—thereby potentially reversing respiratory and cranial nerve deficits. Specifically, posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF dynamics and alleviate symptoms such as central hypoventilation or dysphagia. However, the decision to proceed is not solely dictated by anatomical findings. In this patient, while surgical intervention was recommended due to the severity of her central hypoventilation and life-threatening risk of hypoxaemia, the complexity of her presentation required a careful discussion of risks versus benefits. The potential for neurological deficit associated with any surgery must be balanced against the risks of prolonged untreated dysfunction, requiring a tailored approach rather than a universal mandate for operating.

When surgery is deferred, conservative management serves topreserve function and mitigate symptom burden while awaiting definitive intervention or further diagnostic clarification. In this case, the management plan included dietary modifications to facilitate safe swallowing during choking episodes and strategies to reduce anxiety surrounding public social interactions that were previously avoided. Additionally, the patient recognized that managing her unique headaches involved cutting her hair short, a form of conservative care that addressed the cutaneous allodynia associated with her occipital neuralgia symptoms. While cough suppression is a standard adjunct in the conservative management of bulbar symptoms to prevent asphyxiation during shortness of breath episodes, this patient prioritized non-pharmacologic coping strategies. This highlights the importance of addressing the psychosocial impact of chronic symptoms, where anxiety and the fear of choking can exacerbate the patient's experience, even in the absence of frank airway obstruction.

A critical component of the conservative approach in CMI is the establishment of a rigorous monitoring protocol to detect clinical deterioration early. The case report outlines a structured surveillance plan that prioritizes the safety of the patient while respecting the patient's current choice to observe. This plan includes monthly clinical assessments to evaluate headache and dysphagia severity, quarterly overnight oximetry to track oxygen saturation trends, and annual MRI surveillance to monitor cerebellar tonsillar descent and foramen magnum dynamics. This triad of monitoring allows for timely intervention if the patient’s status changes; specifically, any clinical deterioration would necessitate revisiting the recommendation for surgical decompression. This protocol also acknowledges the limitations of previous diagnostic tools in this case, such as the Type III sleep study, by planning for future, more comprehensive evaluations at specialized centers once the patient is ready.

Patient autonomy remains a central tenet in managing structural neurological conditions where the threshold for surgery is high and symptoms can be episodic or atypical. In this scenario, the patient elected to delay surgery to allow for the coordination of a definitive Type I polysomnography to confirm the nature of her central events further and to align with her cultural and personal preferences regarding body modification for pain management. This shared decision-making process underscores the principle that while guidelines may recommend surgery for equivalent symptomatic severity, the patient’s values regarding risk, timing, and diagnostic certainty must guide the final management plan. The clinical team’s role is to provide a structured safety net—through the defined monitoring protocols—rather than imposing a timeline for intervention, ensuring that the patient maintains control over her care trajectory while remaining protected against life-threatening complications.

## Clinical Significance: Diagnostic Delays and Neuro-Respiratory Intersection

Challenging the conventional association between respiratory distress and primary pulmonary or gastroesophageal disease, this case illustrates the critical importance of considering structural neurological pathology in patients with unexplained chronic dyspnea and dysphagia. The patient experienced a diagnostic delay spanning 14 years, during which multiple evaluations by otolaryngology and gastroenterology failed to yield a diagnosis. This trajectory underscores a broader clinical principle: normal endoscopic and laryngoscopic findings in the setting of persistent bulbar symptoms should not preclude neurological evaluation. In the context of this case, the repeated choking episodes unaffected by diet consistency were eventually linked to motor vehicle accident-induced brainstem disruption. For the practicing clinician, this reinforces the necessity of a low threshold for neuroimaging when coughing, choking, or "air hunger" persists despite normal respiratory and gastrointestinal workups. A history of minor head trauma combined with progressive headaches and bulbar dysfunction serves as a vital clinical marker to trigger a review of craniovertebral junction anatomy.

A specific red flag identified in this presentation is the nature of dysphagia itself. This patient demonstrated difficulty swallowing equal amounts of solids and liquids, a phenotype that distinguishes neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical obstruction or strictures. Mechanical causes are more consistent between food textures due to lumen narrowing, whereas neurogenic dysfunction affects the coordination of the entire swallowing mechanism (oropharynx and larynx). The extensive clinical history of swallowing dysfunction affecting all consistencies in the absence of structural anomalies on endoscopy should prompt immediate consideration of Chiari malformation type I (CMI). This case highlights that upper aerodigestive symptoms can be the primary manifestation of posterior fossa pathology, mimicking acquired central hypoventilation syndrome or "Ondine's curse." The pathophysiology involves brainstem compression and cerebrospinal fluid flow obstruction, which disrupts the sensory and motor pathways necessary for swallowing and respiratory drive. The distinct presentation of biting off liquid or solids without actual airway collateral obstruction suggests high-level cortical pathways remain intact while lower brainstem nuclei are compromised.

Respiratory dysfunction in CMI presents a unique diagnostic challenge due to the "acquired central hypoventilation" phenotype. Unlike primary lung disease, the patient experienced severe oxygen desaturation ranging from 70% to 80% without subjective dyspnea or distress. This paroxysm of "dissociated" breathing—where the automatic drive to breathe fails during sleep or distraction, but voluntary breathing remains preserved—mimics congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (PHOX2B mutation). The case report attributes this lack of air hunger to brainstem pathology interrupting afferent signals that ascend to the cortex to generate the sensation of breathlessness. Clinically, this renders patients at higher risk for unobserved apnoea, particularly during sleep when cortical vigilance decreases. Consequently, the reliance on home pulse oximetry alone was insufficient, as only one in three such episodes was noticed by the patient themselves. Comprehensive polysomnography, specifically Type I monitoring with EEG, capnography, and video recording, is required to differentiate central sleep apnoea from obstructive events and accurately calculate the apnoea–hypopnoea index.

The diagnostic standard for CMI emphasizes the shift from purely anatomical to symptomatic classification. While the current case reported an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils against the definition of CMI (≥5 mm), the significance lies in correlating this anatomical finding with the patient's symptomatology. Modern imaging studies suggest CMI exists on a spectrum, and many adults harbor asymptomatic anatomical variants. Therefore, surgical intervention is contingent upon the patient's clinical burden rather than imaging thresholds alone. In this instance, the decision for potential posterior fossa decompression was weighed against the patient's preference for conservative management, highlighting the utility of structured monitoring plans. A multidisciplinary team involving neurology, sleep medicine, and otolaryngology is essential to synthesize these complex findings. Consensus allows for the integration of sleep study data, swallowing assessments, and radiological imaging, preventing the repetitive delays seen in the literature where cases like this are attributed to non-pulmonary anxiety or GERD. Ultimately, recognizing the intersection of neurology and pulmonology prevents malignant misdiagnosis, as respiratory decompensation in CMI can lead to life-threatening hypoxaemia if the underlying structural cause is ignored.
