Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Pathophysiology] What are the two primary autoantibodies associated with Immune-mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM), and which one has a stronger historical association with statin therapy?

The two primary autoantibodies associated with Immune-mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) are anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) and anti–hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR). As detailed in the Background section of this case report, the use of statins is strongly associated with anti-HMGCR autoantibody positivity. This serologic marker distinguishes a subset of IMNM patients from those with classic polymyositis or other inflammatory myopathies.

It is clinically relevant to note, as supported by this case report, that approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for both anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR. This patient, for example, tested negative for the myositis panel including both HMGCR and SRP, which required reliance on muscle biopsy findings for diagnosis. Therefore, while anti-HMGCR has the stronger historical association with statin therapy, a high index of suspicion should remain even in seronegative patients.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Clinically, how does the trajectory of Creatine Kinase (CK) levels over time in chronic IMNM differ from that seen in acute drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, and why is this distinction important for diagnosis?

**Clinical Teaching Cognition**

It is essential to recognize that the trajectory of creatine kinase (CK) elevation differentiates immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from acute drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, guiding critical management decisions regarding immunosuppression versus supportive care.

In the provided case of semaglutide-associated IMNM, the CK trajectory was subacute and progressive. Following a single dose of semaglutide, the patient initially presented with a modest CK elevation (337 U/L). However, over a 4-month interval—a period during which the drug had effectively ceased—the CK rose to a peak of 3992 U/L (a 12-fold increase), accompanied by progressive proximal weakness. This delayed, chronic escalation indicates an ongoing pathogenic process rather than direct acute toxicity.

By contrast, the discussion notes a comparative case of tirzepatide-induced rhabdomyolysis where the patient presented with a massive acute CK elevation that normalized within days after drug discontinuation and hydration, without the requirement for immunosuppression. While rhabdomyolysis is characterized by a rapid, acute spike in myoglobin and CK leading to renal injury, IMNM is an autoimmune disorder driven by individual myofibrillar necrosis that persists despite withdrawal of the product.

This distinction is diagnostic and therapeuticly vital. Misidentifying IMNM as simple drug toxicity or rhabdomyolysis risks withholding necessary corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin, leading to irreversible muscle damage and persistent weakness. Conversely, over-intensifying immunosuppression in acute rhabdomyolysis carries unnecessary toxicity. A rising CK trend over weeks, particularly in the presence of fatigue or dysphagia, should prompt investigation into an autoimmune etiology and consideration for myositis rather than toxicity alone.

### [Management] This patient presented with severe proximal weakness and dysphagia, features often requiring aggressive immunosuppression. Why was Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG) utilized as an adjunctive therapy alongside corticosteroids in this specific case?

In this specific case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), the decision to utilize intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) alongside corticosteroids was driven by the severity of the patient's presentation and the critical need for rapid immunomodulation. While high-dose corticosteroids are the standard first-line therapy for IMNM, their onset of action is often delayed, which poses a significant risk to patients with rapid clinical deterioration or life-threatening complications, such as this patient's profound exhaustion and progressive dysphagia. 

The case report explicitly notes that IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg "due to her persistent weakness and severe features." In the setting of severe, rapidly progressive autoimmune myopathy, there is a therapeutic window where the aggressive inflammatory response can outpace the damage-repair capacity of steroid monotherapy. The addition of IVIG aims to rapidly mitigate immune activation and provide a bridge toward remission. In this patient, the combination regimen yielded a marked reduction in creatine kinase levels and functional recovery, allowing for a rapid taper and discontinuation of prednisone within two months. This outcome is clinically significant because idiopathic IMNM typically requires prolonged intensive immunosuppression for remission; by utilizing aggressive combination therapy, the team achieved steroid-sparing efficacy and reduced the long-term toxicity associated with chronic corticosteroid use.

### [Morning Report] Applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality assessment criteria, which specific factor in this patient's history supports classifying the association between semaglutide and IMNM as 'probable' despite the untested nature of a formal re-challenge?

The specific factor supporting a classification of 'probable' causality is the **clinically reasonable response to drug withdrawal**, or positive dechallenge. While a rechallenge is often the definitive test for causality, it was explicitly deemed unethical in this case due to the severity of the reaction and the risk of recurrence. Consequently, the causality assessment relied on the patient’s sustained improvement and normalization of creatine kinase levels following the cessation of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppression. The authors note in the Discussion that this withdrawal response, combined with a reasonable time relationship and the exclusion of alternative diagnoses, fulfills the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria for probable association in the absence of a formal rechallenge.

### [Pathophysiology] The muscle biopsy revealed necrotic fibers with CD68+ macrophage staining and Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) positivity. What specific immunological mechanism does the MAC positivity reflect in the context of muscle necrosis?

The presence of the membrane attack complex (MAC) positivity in this muscle biopsy reflects **terminal complement activation leading to direct sarcolemmal cytolytic injury**.

To elaborate, the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) is the final terminal component of the complement cascade. In this case, its deposition on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres indicates that antibody-mediated or immune-triggered complement activation is occurring directly against muscle cell membranes. This results in the formation of transmembrane pores that cause osmotic lysis and necrosis of the fiber.

As described in this report, the patient demonstrated features consistent with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The biopsy revealed dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers with myophagocytosis (evidenced by CD68+ macrophages) and rare CD3+ T cells. The MAC staining is particularly significant here because it distinguishes this process from permissive myopathies or polymyositis, where necrosis is often driven primarily by T-cell cytotoxicity with less complement deposition. In this context, the MAC positivity confirms that complement-mediated cytotoxicity is a driver of the muscle destruction, corroborating the diagnosis of IMNM and supporting the potential association with semaglutide use identified in the case report. Immunotherapy was subsequently employed, which successfully extinguished this immune-mediated lysis and normalized CK levels.

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient with metabolic comorbidities such as NAFLD and concurrent skeletal muscle injury (elevated CK), how should one interpret concurrent transaminitis to avoid attributing muscle enzymes to liver disease and vice versa?

Interpretation of transaminitis in the setting of elevated creatine kinase requires a clear understanding of enzyme distribution, as neither AST nor ALT are liver-specific. In this specific case, the patient had biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with mild transaminitis (ALT > AST) established three years prior to the acute decline. When skeletal muscle injury developed, both muscle enzymes and hepatic enzymes were elevated concurrently.

To distinguish between these sources and avoid diagnostic attribution errors, one must adhere to the following physiological and clinical principles grounded in this case:

1.  **Establish Baseline Etiology:** As seen in this patient, a history of metabolic comorbidities like obesity and a prior biopsy diagnosing NAFLD strongly suggest that mild, stable transaminitis is hepatic in origin rather than secondary to muscle breakdown. Skeletal muscle is a poor source for ALT but contributes significantly to AST levels; however, persistent mild elevation distinct from acute muscle injury patterns suggests chronic liver pathology.

2.  **Analyze Temporal Trends of Enzymes:** This is the most critical differentiator in this case report. Waning suspicion of the liver as the source of ongoing derangement is possible because "her borderline elevation in AST/ALT persisted after CK levels normalised." If transaminases normalize concurrently with a decrease in CK (implying muscle recovery), the primary source was myopathic. However, in this patient, the chronic liver enzymes persisted despite the resolution of the acute muscle injury, confirming the hepatic source of the AST/ALT was independent of the immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

3.  **Utilize Magnitude and Ratio:** While AST elevations can occur with muscle injury, the ratio of CK to transaminases is essential. In this case, the CK elevation was profound (up to 3992 U/L) relative to the transaminases (AST 191 U/L, ALT 165 U/L). In pure liver injury, you would rarely see CK in the thousands. Conversely, do not dismiss a concurrent rise in transaminases as "just muscle" in a patient with known hepatic vulnerability; in this case, it was likely a summation of chronic liver disease and acute muscle injury.

**Clinical Teaching Summary**
Always measure CK when evaluating transaminitis. Conversely, do not attribute all AST/ALT elevation to liver disease in myopathic presentations. Look for a history of liver biopsy, the magnitude of muscle enzymes, and—most importantly—the distinction in trajectories: CK should respond to immunosuppression and cessation of offending agents (e.g., semaglutide), while chronic NAFLD markers may persist. This patient resolved the muscle crisis but maintained the hepatic history, proving they are distinct pathologies often concurrent in metabolic syndrome patients.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Although approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative for common markers like HMGCR and SRP, what definitive diagnostic finding in the biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of necrotizing myopathy in this patient despite negative serology?

In patients with suspected immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), the absence of HMGCR or SRP autoantibodies does not preclude the diagnosis. In this case, the definitive diagnostic finding lay in the muscle biopsy histomorphology, which exhibited the hallmark features of the disease despite seronegativity.

The biopsy revealed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a distinct paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates, distinguishing the pathology from polymyositis where perifascicular or endomysial T-cell cuffs are prominent. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis through CD68 and acid phosphatase staining. Furthermore, the presence of the membrane attack complex on myofibres supported the complement-mediated mechanism of muscle destruction. Together, this pattern of focal necrosis, regeneration, and myophagocytosis with minimal inflammation provided the pathological confirmation of IMNM in the absence of antibody testing support.

### [Management] The patient achieved complete resolution of CK elevation and functional status off immunosuppression after only 2 months of tapering. How does this recovery profile compare to the typical long-term prognosis of idiopathic IMNM regarding immunosuppressive therapy requirements?

Good morning. Regarding the recovery profile observed in this case, it is crucial to distinguish this presentation from the natural history of idiopathic immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

In typical idiopathic IMNM, characterized by chronic antigen-driven inflammation often associated with autoantibodies such as anti–HMGCR or anti–SRP, prognosis regarding immunosuppressive therapy is generally poor in terms of treatment-free intervals. Idiopathic cases often require prolonged, intensive maintenance immunosuppression, sometimes indefinitely, to prevent relapse and manage persistent muscle weakness or CK burden.

In contrast, this patient’s clinical course—complete resolution with a rapid tapering schedule and return to baseline functional status off immunosuppression—closely aligns with a drug-induced etiology. As the authors note, "drug-induced IMNM, which can improve with brief immunosuppression after withdrawal of the offending agent, rather than idiopathic IMNM, which often requires prolonged and intensive immunosuppressive therapy."

Therefore, the short duration of therapy required here serves as a distinguishing clinical feature. While idiopathic IMNM typically necessitates a long-term treatment course to maintain negative serology and normalized muscle function, drug-triggered IMNM suggests that early cessation of the offending agent (semaglutide in this instance) may satisfy the primary driver of inflammation, allowing for resolution with minimal therapy. This distinction highlights the importance of pharmacovigilance and prompting clinicians to consider withdrawal of suspected agents before committing to chronic immunosuppression.

### [Clinical Pearls] Cardiac evaluation revealed elevated troponin and high BNP levels. Based on the cardiac MRI findings mentioned, how were active myocarditis and stress-induced cardiomyopathy differentiated in the absence of late gadolinium enhancement?

In this case, direct differentiation between active myocarditis and stress-induced cardiomyopathy was challenging, as the authors explicitly noted the initial cardiac findings were inconclusive. While the cardiac MRI demonstrated myocardial edema and elevated biomarkers consistent with acute injury, the absence of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) argued against established fibrosis or specific necrosis patterns typically seen in chronic myocarditis.

The clinical reasoning for differentiating these entities, when imaging is equivocal, relies heavily on the trajectory of biomarkers and imaging resolution. In this patient, the presence of edema confirmed active myocardial injury, but the lack of LGE indicated no permanent scarring. The definitive differentiation occurred over time: the repeat echocardiogram and cardiac MRI revealed normalization of abnormalities, and biomarkers resolved. This rapid clinical and radiographic recovery supports a diagnosis of a transient process, such as focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy or reversible inflammation associated with her immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), rather than active myocarditis which typically requires prolonged immunosuppression and often demonstrates persistent LGE.

The key teaching point is that in the absence of provocative LGE patterns, the resolution of cardiac edema and normalization of biomarkers are the most critical factors in distinguishing transient stress/reactionary injury from active inflammatory myocarditis.
