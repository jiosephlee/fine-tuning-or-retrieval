Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Differential Diagnosis

The patient was a 60-year-old female presenting with a complex history of metabolic and hepatic disease, which established the context for her medication initiation. She had a known history of obesity, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and remote venous thromboembolism. Three years prior to her decline, she underwent extensive evaluation for persistent transaminitis revealing ALT elevations approximately twice the upper limit of normal with ALT exceeding AST. Abdominal imaging and biopsy confirmed hepatosteatosis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), reinforcing the severity of her metabolic burden. This specific history is clinically relevant because NAFLD is a frequent indication for weight-loss pharmacotherapy, directly leading to the commencement of semaglutide. Understanding this baseline is essential, as the thigh biopsy would later distinguish between hepatic steatosis and potential myopathic causes of transaminase fluctuations.

Initial clinical deterioration occurred immediately following the first dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously). Within hours, the patient developed severe gastrointestinal intolerance, manifesting as nausea and vomiting that persisted to the point of hospitalization. The acute presentation was complicated by acute renal failure and severe metabolic acidosis requiring intravenous fluids and bicarbonate. While the acute hospitalization involved hospitalization details regarding IVIG and subsequent course, the critical teaching point regarding the substance use occurred during this initial phase. Notably, creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L during this acute admission, which did not suggest massive muscle breakdown or typical rhabdomyolysis. Concurrently, troponin levels were normal, and the patient did not present with muscle pain or cola-colored urine, leaving the acute failure initially attributed to dehydration and hypovolemia rather than direct myotoxicity. This initial presentation often lands the clinician in a diagnostic trap of acute drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, yet the lack of severe CPK elevation and subsequent resolution of renal function suggested a complex mechanism beyond simple toxicity.

Following discharge, the clinical picture shifted from acute intolerance to a progressive, subacute decline spanning four months. Despite the cessation of semaglutide and resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms, the patient developed progressive proximal muscle weakness that eventually prevented her from standing or sitting upright. She additionally reported dysphagia and dysphonia. This evolution distinguishes the case from typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, where normalization of muscle enzymes usually occurs within days of discontinuation. Instead, the 12-fold increase in CK levels at the tertiary referral suggests a subacute inflammatory process allowing time for further diagnostic consideration. The presence of bulbar symptoms is a critical red flag that warrants investigation for systemic myopathies, hypoglycemia, or motor neuron disease, given the distribution of weakness extending beyond the hips to the throat.

Physical examination upon admission to the tertiary center revealed significant weakness localized to the proximal muscle groups, without evidence of dermatomyositis features such as malar rash or heliotrope rash. There was no synovitis, sicca symptoms, or skin changes, which initially narrowed the differential diagnosis away from classic Cutaneous Rheumatologic Myopopathies and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus. However, the absence of specific cutaneous signs does not exclude immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), particularly in the subset of patients with seronegative status. Elevated inflammatory markers, including ESR and C-reactive protein, despite the absence of a constitutional fever, supported an inflammatory etiology rather than functional deconditioning alone. By this stage, the clinical team had to navigate a specific window of diagnosis where the combination of subacute progressive weakness, bulbar involvement, and unexplained transaminitis prompted the final suspicion of IMNM rather than a continuation of the acute tolerability issue with semaglutide.

Evaluating this timeline is crucial for distinguishing between drug-induced toxic myopathy and drug-triggered immune-mediated disease. In cases of direct toxic myopathy, such as the documented rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide, CK levels typically peak acutely and resolve rapidly with hydration and drug withdrawal, as seen in the patient's initial 337 U/L reading. Conversely, the subacute exacerbation and 4-month lag before significant weakness demonstrated the likely autoimmune pathophysiology. While the patient’s pro-BNP and troponin levels were initially normal or mildly elevated, satisfying the need to rule out primary cardiac etiologies or stress cardiomyopathy, the persistence of weakness without cardiac decompensation favored a primary myopathic process. Establishing this window of opportunity is vital, as early recognition of IMNM allows for timely immunosuppressive intervention, whereas attributing the symptoms solely to metabolic side effects or drug toxicity might lead to delayed treatment and prolonged functional decline.

## Pathophysiology of Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct clinical entity within the spectrum of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, characterized primarily by severe proximal muscle weakness and myal pain, accompanied by necrotising muscle fibers with a paucity of endomysial inflammatory infiltrates. This histological profile differentiates IMNM from classic polymyositis, which typically demonstrates a dense CD8+ T-cell infiltrate invading non-necrotic muscle fibers, or dermatomyositis, which is defined by perifascicular atrophy and muscle fiber necrosis at the periphery. In the case of IMNM, the muscle biopsy findings of necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration, as observed in this patient’s right vastus lateralis biopsy, highlight the central role of muscle cell death rather than lymphocytic invasion. The muscle biopsy stained for CD68 (a macrophage marker) and demonstrated acid phosphatase activity, confirming myophagocytosis where macrophages engulf and clean up necrotic muscle debris, a hallmark that should prompt clinicians to consider IMNM in patients with severe muscle breakdown meeting specific exclusion criteria.

The pathophysiology of most classic cases of IMNM is strongly linked to the presence of autoantibodies, specifically anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). The association with HMGCR, the rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis, is particularly well-established in patients exposed to statin therapy. Statin-induced myopathy facilitates the presentation of HMGCR peptides by muscle cells, generating T-cell responses and leading to antibody production, though the text notes that HMGCR antibodies are often the most prevalent driver of necrotizing myopathy in the statin population. While statin-associated IMNM typically follows the administration of lipid-lowering agents, the underlying immune mechanism involves HMGCR autoantibodies that trigger a cascade of cytotoxic T-cell responses and complement activation. Understanding this standard pathophysiology is critical for the differential diagnosis of this patient, as she served as the proband for a potential drug-induced variation of this syndrome absent the classic serological marker.

Significant diagnostic challenges arise in cases of seronegative IMNM, which constitute approximately one-third of all patients with this condition. The absence of anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies, as found in this patient’s myositis panel, renders the diagnosis more difficult and suggests a broader spectrum of potential triggers beyond the known autoantibody-associated mechanisms. In this clinical context, the patient tested negative for SRP and HMGCR antibodies despite meeting the clinical and histological criteria for IMNM. This seronegative presentation underscores the necessity of integrating clinical history, such as the temporal relationship to semaglutide use, with histopathological findings when autoantibody testing does not immediately clarify the diagnosis. While the lack of autoantibodies implies myopies may exist yet to be identified, clinically, the course remains consistent with an immune-mediated process requiring immunosuppression regardless of serostatus.

The mechanism driving the specific pathology of IMNM involves overexpression of MHC class I on the membrane of skeletal muscle fibers in the presence of complement system activation. This leads to the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), which causes direct lysis of the muscle fiber membrane and subsequent necrosis. Muscle biopsy findings in severely affected areas, such as the necrotic fibers membrane attack complex positive stain seen in this patient, serve as additional evidence for this mechanism. The resolution of muscle weakness and normalization of creatine kinase in this patient following the cessation of the implicated agent and a course of immunosuppression, including corticosteroids and IVIG, supports the immunopathogenic nature of the disease. The rapid clearance of the necrotic debris and cessation of the complement-driven lysis lead to muscle regeneration and recovery, illustrating the dynamic nature of the muscle pathology in response to immunomodulatory therapy.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Biopsy, Serology, and Drug Causality

In the workup of this patient, interpreting laboratory trends was the first step toward differentiating between metabolic liver disease and emergent muscle pathology. The patient had a preexisting history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with transaminitis, manifesting as elevated AST and ALT levels prior to semaglutide use. While aspartate aminotransferase (AST) can be derived from muscle mitochondria, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is predominantly hepatic, making the isolated persistence of these enzymes indicative of her underlying hepatosteatosis rather than muscle breakdown. However, the dramatic subsequent elevation of creatine kinase (CK) followed a distinct trajectory, rising from a modest 337 U/L during the initial hospitalization to a peak of 3992 U/L approximately four months later. This 12-fold increase is disproportionate to the stable hepatic profile and specific for a severe myopathy. For the clinician, this pattern highlights that while transaminases can occasionally coexist with myopathy, the magnitude and persistence of CK elevation in the context of progressive symmetrical weakness should trigger a myositis-specific evaluation rather than assuming a progression of fatty liver disease.

The definitive diagnostic features were identified through muscle histology, which distinguished this patient’s condition from inflammatory myopathies like polymyositis. Histological analysis of the vastus lateralis muscle revealed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, which are hallmarks of necrotising myopathy. Immunohistochemical staining confirmed an inflammatory response mediated primarily by macrophages, evidenced by acid phosphatase staining and robust CD68 (macrophage marker) positivity with myophagocytosis. Notably, the biopsy showed only rare CD3-positive T-cells and perivascular inflammatory cells. This paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates compared to the density of necrotic tissue aligns with the pathological definition of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), where muscle fibers are destroyed by the membrane attack complex rather than by a dense T-cell invasion. The presence of the membrane attack complex positive stain on necrotising myofibres rather than a dense inflammatory infiltrate is a key discriminator from classic polymyositis, necessitating specific historical context regarding the patient's medication exposure.

Serological limitations posed a significant diagnostic challenge, as this patient presented with seronegative IMNM. Her myositis panel was negative for high-risk autoantibodies, including anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) and anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), which are classically associated with the two major subtypes of IMNM. Furthermore, the ANA was negative, and only a low-titre SS-B antibody was present, a finding that lacked clear correlation with her immunologic phenotype given the absence of other Sjogren’s syndrome manifestations. This scenario underscores a critical clinical lesson: while these antibodies are biomarkers of disease, a negative panel does not rule out IMNM, particularly when histopathology is consistent. Approximately one-third of patients with IMNM lack detectable autoantibodies, necessitating reliance on clinical presentation and, in ambiguous cases, muscle biopsy to confirm diagnosis and exclude overlap syndromes.

Differentiating drug-induced toxic myopathy from immunologic IMNM driven by a drug is central to understanding the required treatment duration. Drug-induced toxic myopathy is typically characterized by rapid resolution upon cessation of the offending agent without the need for immunosuppression. In contrast, this patient’s clinical course showed that CK levels did not normalize quickly after stopping semaglutide but rather progressed to severe weakness requiring immunosuppression. The patient required high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) to achieve sustained improvement, evidenced by normal CK levels and functional recovery while off medication. This delay in improvement—occurring over months rather than days—indicates that the drug likely acted as a trigger for an autoimmune process rather than exerting direct skeletal muscle toxicity. Clinicians must appreciate that the severity and persistence of weakness relative to the discontinuation of the suspected agent are vital indicators of the underlying mechanism.

Finally, establishing the causal link between semaglutide and this specific autoimmune reaction relied on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) criteria for causality assessment. In this case, there was a reasonable time relationship between the initial exposure to semaglutide and the onset of severe myopathy, four months after a single dose, despite the initial production of symptoms shortly thereafter. Since the patient denied other risk factors such as statin use, alcohol, or endocrine disorders, it was unlikely that her IMNM was due to causes other than the medication. A clinician must also observe that the condition responded clinically to removal of the agent and brief immunosuppression, fulfilling the dechallenge component of the criteria. Although future studies will likely clarify the exact pathophysiology, this case demonstrates how case-specific evidence, combined with pharmacovigilance frameworks like the UMC criteria, can identify probable adverse drug reactions in the absence of prior seropositive markers.

## Management Strategies and Immunosuppression Protocols

In the management of severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), particularly when presenting with life-threatening or severely disabling features, the primary goal is to rapidly suppress the inflammatory process to prevent irreversible muscle damage and complications such as aspiration or respiratory failure. In this case, the patient presented with significant proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia, alongside a dramatic elevation in creatine kinase (CK) levels peaking at 3992 U/L. To address this acute inflammatory crisis, the patient was administered high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for five days. This pulse steroid therapy is a standard first-line intervention for severe myopathic crises, designed to rapidly inhibit the immune attack on muscle fibers. The decision to initiate this aggressive regimen was driven by the inability to stand or sit upright, the involvement of bulbar function affecting swallowing, and the risk of respiratory compromise, necessitating immediate and potent anti-inflammatory control before transitioning to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily.

While corticosteroids are the cornerstone of IMNM treatment, this patient required adjunctive therapy due to the persistence of weakness and the severity of her clinical presentation. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was indicated following the pulse steroid course because she maintained severe features and persistent weakness despite initial treatment. In this context, IVIG 2 g/kg split over four days was administered to modulate the immune response through Fc receptor blockade and neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies. The addition of IVIG supports a broader clinical principle wherein immunosuppressive therapy is escalated in refractory or severe cases. The patient demonstrated a marked reduction in CK levels over two weeks, supporting the efficacy of this combined approach. Importantly, the management plan acknowledged the quality of the response; since the CK normalized significantly, no further doses of IVIG were pursued after the initial course, balancing individual responder risk with cost and infection exposure.

Long-term immunosuppression strategies in IMNM typically involve a slow taper of oral corticosteroids over 12 to 24 months to prevent recurrence, given the chronic autoimmune nature of the disease. However, the treatment trajectory in this case deviated from the norm due to the suspected drug-induced etiology. Clinical reasoning centered on the temporal relationship between semaglutide use and symptom onset, leading to the hypothesis that the condition would resolve with removal of the trigger. Consequently, the tapering schedule was aggressive but monitored closely, resulting in a successful weaning from prednisone over a period of two months. This approach was reinforced by her sustained clinical improvement and returning CK levels to normal ranges, allowing for discontinuation of immunosuppression entirely by the eight-month mark. This illustrates that in probable drug-induced IMNM, shorter courses of immunosuppression may be sufficient if the offending agent is removed and the patient exhibits robust functional recovery.

Concurrent management of comorbidities must be considered when treating severe autoimmune conditions with potent steroids and immunosuppressants. At 12 days post-discharge, this patient was readmitted for septic shock secondary to a *Pseudomonas* urinary tract infection. Notably, during this admission her CK levels were found to be normal, indicating that the muscle-specific inflammation had not recurred at the time of infection. While immunosuppression increases susceptibility to opportunistic infections, the management of the septic shock itself was driven by the clinical necessity of controlling the infection. The resolution of the urinary tract infection and the presence of normal inflammatory markers allowed for a return to outpatient follow-up, highlighting the importance of vigilant monitoring for infectious complications in patients receiving high-dose steroids.

Finally, a critical component of the management strategy involved the discontinuation of the offending agent, semaglutide. Although the patient had received only a single dose prior to symptom onset, the persistence of symptoms and the severe nature of the injury following that dose were used to establish probable causality. In the absence of a proven target autoantibody (the myositis panel was negative for HMGCR and SRP), pharmacovigilance relies heavily on dechallenge procedures. Removing semaglutide was essential to halt the potential ongoing antigenic drive or metabolic trigger that may have precipitated the autoimmune response. The discussion of causality emphasized the lack of rechallenge (ethically impossible) but noted that the persistent improvement and drug-free recovery supported the suspicion of a drug-induced etiology. This case underscores that in IMNM, identifying and removing the triggering factor can be as impactful as pharmacologic immunosuppression for determining long-term prognosis and management duration.

## Clinical Outcomes and Pharmacovigilance Implications

The patient's clinical trajectory in this case serves as a critical teaching point regarding the natural history of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and the implications of causality in drug-related adverse events. Following a comprehensive treatment course involving intravenous methylprednisolone, high-dose oral prednisone, and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), the patient experienced marked improvement within two weeks. The definitive outcome was observed ten months after initial evaluation, at which time she had regained her baseline functional status and remained free of significant muscle enzyme elevation. Crucially, she remained off immunosuppressive therapy for eight months without relapse or recurrence of symptoms. This ability to be successfully weaned off corticosteroids rapidly contrasts with idiopathic IMNM, which frequently requires prolonged, often indefinite, maintenance therapy. However, the patient’s severity and the presence of a distinct trigger allowed for initial aggressive intervention with a short course of immunosuppression after the offending agent was removed, paralleling the treatment course of many confirmed drug-induced myopathies. This clinical course reinforces the utility of recognizing a temporal relationship between medication exposure and disease onset to guide immunosuppressive therapy in the acute setting.

Understanding this case requires distinguishing it from other reported adverse events in the class of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, specifically those involving direct muscle toxicity. A previously reported case detailing tirzepatide use demonstrated rhabdomyolysis characterized by massive creatine kinase (CK) elevation and rapid resolution following drug discontinuation and hydration, consistent with a direct toxic mechanism. In contrast, this patient presented with a more insidious progression of proximal weakness and dysphagia over a four-month period following a single dose of semaglutide, necessitating immunosuppression rather than just hydration. This distinction highlights a spectrum of adverse neuromuscular events associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists, ranging from acute rhabdomyolysis to probable immune-mediated necrotising myopathy. These divergent presentations suggest that clinicians must broaden their diagnostic differential beyond simple toxicity, considering a complex immune etiology when symptoms persist or progress over weeks rather than resolving immediately upon cessation.

The emergence of this case expands the known safety profile of GLP-1 agonists, which will likely become increasingly utilized for obesity and metabolic disorders. While gastrointestinal side effects are well-documented, and some evidence suggests a general loss of skeletal muscle mass, evidence of a specific autoimmune mechanism has remained limited. This report provides the first patient-level data supporting an association between semaglutide and IMNM, categorized as probable causality using WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria. Because the specific combination of symptoms, liver history, and muscle biopsy findings points toward a mechanistic trigger, this case underscores the necessity for enhanced pharmacovigilance. The lack of a definitive serological marker (HMGCR or SRP antibodies) in this patient further supports the need to report variation in clinical phenotypes, as not all cases of drug-induced IMNM will present with the typical antibody profile. Accurate reporting of such cases is essential to establish antecedent relationships for a growing population of patients at risk.

For residents and clinicians managing patients on GLP-1 receptor agonists, awareness of this potential adverse effect warrants specific monitoring recommendations. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold for evaluating new onset proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, or muscle pain in this population, even if the medication is well-tolerated regarding gastrointestinal symptoms. Given the temporal lag seen in this case prior to biopsy confirmation, preventing severe disability may rely on early recognition and the temporary discontinuation of the agent while workup proceeds. Although rechallenge is unethical in confirmed cases of severe toxicity, the clinical logic applied here—cessation followed by resolution—is the gold standard for establishing causality. Future monitoring strategies may involve more frequent CK and liver enzyme surveillance in patients initiating or escalating therapy, particularly those with pre-existing conditions such as fatty liver disease, as seen in this patient, which may theoretically modulate susceptibility to immune-mediated injury.
