Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Beyond Weight Loss: Pharmacovigilance for Emerging GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

In the world of metabolic medicine, Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become cornerstone therapies for obesity and type 2 diabetes. We all know the stats: dramatic weight loss, improved glycemic control, and vasodilation benefits for cardiovascular outcomes. But what about the flip side? While gastrointestinal intolerance is the headline side effect, we are only beginning to map the wider safety profile of these agents. Rare reports of rhabdomyolysis have circulated, but this case pushes the differential further into immunology. It is a stark reminder that when we prescribe weight loss medications, we must stay vigilant for neuromuscular complications that can mimic muscle trauma but are actually autoimmune.

## Case Presentation
We are presenting a case of a 60-year-old female with a background of significant obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Three years prior to her deterioration, she had persistent transaminase elevation consistent with steatosis, but her liver function remained stable beyond that. Seeking further metabolic management for persistent NAFLD and obesity, she was initiated on semaglutide. The reaction was immediate and severe. Following her first 0.25 mg injection, she suffered a cascade of symptoms: nausea, vomiting, dyspnea requiring non-invasive ventilation, and acute kidney injury. While this initial admission resolved with supportive care, her CK was only mildly elevated on presentation at that time (337 U/L).

However, the true story unfolded over the subsequent four months. Instead of improving, she progressively declined. She lost the ability to sit or stand unaided and developed severe dysphagia and dysphonia. When she eventually presented to tertiary care, her Creatine Kinase (CK) had skyrocketed to nearly 4000 U/L, a 12-fold increase. Physical exam confirmed profound proximal weakness without evidence of joint inflammation or rash. Serial labs showed ongoing inflammation, and a muscle biopsy was the only way to nail the diagnosis.

The biopsy was the key differentiator. It revealed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a macrophage infiltrate and membrane attack complex positivity, consistent with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Unlike classic rhabdomyolysis where CK normalizes after stress and hydration, this patient required immunosuppression. She was treated with a burst of intravenous methylprednisolone followed by daily oral prednisone and a course of IVIG. The response was dramatic. Within weeks, the CK normalized, and she was eventually weaned off immunosuppression entirely while avoiding the offending agent.

## Why This Case Hits the Emergency Clinician
This case raises a red flag for anyone managing patients on emerging metabolic weight loss therapies. We usually categorize myopathy in the ED setting as either toxic metabolic (statins, alcohol), traumatic (compartment syndrome), or inflammatory (polymyositis, dermatomyositis). IMNM is often linked to statins and the anti-HMGCR antibody, but this patient was seronegative for HMGCR and SRP. The temporal relationship here is critical: the decline was not immediate toxicity that resolved with stopping the drug, but a subacute autoimmune process triggered by the drug.

The distinction between toxic rhabdomyolysis and drug-induced autoimmune myopathy is often subtle, yet the management is vastly different. A case involving tirzepatide noted rapid CK improvement with hydration alone. In this semaglutide case, the patient had a 4-month course of progressive decline. If you see a patient on GLP-1 agonists complaining of fatigue or muscle weakness, and their CK is rising rather than falling, do not assume it is merely dehydration-related rhabdomyolysis. You must consider an immune-mediated etiology. The fact that the CK peaked in the thousands and took a prolonged course to resolve supports the immune-mediated hypothesis over direct chemical toxicity.

Furthermore, the literature is catching up on this rarity. The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria likely classify this as "probable causality" due to the robust response to stopping semaglutide and initiating immunosuppression. It is not just a hunch; it is a signal that warrants investigation. The presence of an isolated Anti-SS-B antibody, without ANA or SS-A positivity, suggests a distinct autoimmune derangement, however unpredictable that may be for a muscle specialist. This uncertainty often confuses the surgeon or internist, reinforcing the need for high-quality clinical correlation.

## Clinical Pearls
GLP-1 receptor agonists are high-volume medications, and while large-scale singular outcomes are expected, pharmacovigilance cannot afford to catch these rarer signals. Immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG) was required to resolve the myopathy, distinguishing this from simple drug-induced central pump failure or rhabdomyolysis where hydration is the primary cure. Always think of autoimmune etiologies when CK remains elevated despite cessation of the offending agent and clinical symptoms are progressive.

Just because a patient is on "weight loss meds" does not mean the GI symptoms are the only systemic threat. A lay history of "feeling weak" can quickly lead to physical disability in the context of IMNM, where dysphagia poses an immediate airway risk. The need to avoid the drug long-term is non-negotiable in confirmed cases, and rechallenge is unethical if avoidance of glucagon-like peptide-1 agonists restores functional baseline. Finally, this adds to the growing list of potential steroid-sparing or non-steroidal triggers to watch for in the era of GLP-1s across the patient population.

## Take-home Messages
*   **Distinguish Toxicity from Autoimmunity:** Rapid CK normalization after hydration supports toxic rhabdomyolysis; progressive weakness with a delayed peak in CK and need for immunosuppression supports immune-mediated necrotising myopathy.
*   **Broaden the Differential:** When prescribing GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide, monitor for proximal muscle weakness and dysphagia, not just GI symptoms.
*   **Early Intervention:** High CK with proximal weakness may require muscle biopsy and early rheumatology consultation to confirm IMNM and prevent permanent disability.
*   **Avoid Rechallenge:** Upon diagnosis of a semaglutide-associated IMNM, permanent discontinuation of the class agent is indicated upon confirmation of resolution.



# When Antibodies Fail: Managing Seronegative Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy

You know that feeling when you run the myositis panel, call the numbers to zero, and the patient right there in front of you has a CK in the thousands and looks like they are falling over? It happens more often than you'd think, and yet we still frequently get stuck in a diagnostic ethical paradox. Do you treat? Do you biopsy? Or do you attribute the weakness to deconditioning and watch her CK normalize on its own?

This is the classic "seronegative" dilemma. While the medical world knows that Statin-associated necrotising myopathy is often HMGCR positive and antibody-mediated, what do you tell a patient when the panel comes back clean? The recent case report by Lam et al.* (published in *Rheumatology*, this report serves as a real-world lesson: with the right combination of clinical suspicion and histological evidence, you can manage the "dark diagnosis" where standard immunology fails.

## The 60-Year-Old Woman Who Wouldn't Sit Ups

Our patient was a 60-something female with a heavy burden of metabolic disease—obesity, NAFLD, hypertension, hypothyroidism. She had been doing well until 2.5 years prior when steatohepatitis was diagnosed. Two and a half years later, persistent fatty liver and obesity led to a trial of semaglutide.

Here is where it gets interesting. Within hours of that *first dose* of semaglutide, she developed vomiting, hypovolemic shock, and a CK bump to 337 U/L. She was discharged, expecting to be back to normal. She was not.

Over the subsequent four months, the patient descended into progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphonia, and dysphagia. She couldn't stand without assistance. She presented to an outside hospital with a CK of 3,992 U/L. A 12-fold rise in CK? That’s not typical rhabdomyolysis, which usually clears within days of stopping the toxin. This is a slow burn.

## The Serology Trap

This is the point where most of us hit the brick wall. The workup was exhaustive. They were negative for everything the board wants to check:
*   **ANCA, SS-A, SSA/SSB, Sjogren's:** Negative (or low titer SS-B).
*   **Myositis Panel:** Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, and all the others were negative.
*   **HMGCR and SRP:** The hallmark antibodies for IMNM were **negative**.

If you order a test and it comes back negative, you often assume the patient doesn't have the disease. With a negative HMGCR and SRP, IMNM becomes a "working diagnosis of exclusion." In this case, the patient had an elevated troponin and negative HGHCR/SRP. Her findings were "inconclusive" on heart MRI. The liver was fatty (as history expected), and the kidney and autoimmune markers were clean.

It was a dark diagnosis: The picture screams autoimmune, but the test results say it doesn't exist.

## The Biopsy That Speaks

When the autoantibodies are silent, the muscle fiber has to shout. In this case, a thigh muscle biopsy (vastus lateralis) was performed. And it was critical. The biopsy showed:
*   Dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers.
*   Macrophage infiltration on immunohistochemistry.
*   Myophagocytosis (macrophage eating dead muscle fibers).
*   Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) positive stain on myofibres.

This histology was consistent with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy despite the negative antibodies. It reminded us that IMNM is defined by necrosis and low lymphocytic infiltrates, not just the serology. The histology served as the anchor for the clinical decision when the antibodies provided no signal.

## Treatment and The "Dark" Outcome

The patient received a massive hit of treatment right away: 5 days of IV methylprednisolone followed by high-dose oral prednisone and a 2g/kg course of IVIG.

Usually, idiopathic IMNM requires prolonged, intensive immunosuppression. However, because this case appeared to be "drug-induced" (likely the semaglutide despite being 4 months off), she responded incredibly well.
*   **CK normalized** within days of steroids and improved further over 2 weeks.
*   She was weaned off prednisone in 2 months.
*   She recovered baseline functional status by 10 months.

Would she have done better if she were seropositive? Likely. But she proved that you don't need the perfect antibody to treat the right syndrome. The clinical response confirmed the diagnosis.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Don't auto-discharge** on the "negative" seropane results if the clinical phenotype fits. Proximal weakness + high CK + respiratory symptoms in an older patient = IMNM until proven otherwise.
*   **Biopsy is the safety net:** When an antibody panel is negative, muscle biopsy can be the definitive diagnostic tool. Look specifically for necrotising features and myophagocytosis.
*   **Timing matters:** This patient had weakness for 4 months post-semaglutide excursion. While drug-induced myopathy usually resolves immediately upon cessation, the autoimmune component (IMNM) takes time. The long lag period suggests the drug triggered the autoimmune process, not just a direct toxic effect.
*   **Treat the patient, not the lab:** In patients presenting with classic myopathic features and high CK, empiric immunosuppression (if contraindications allow) combined with biopsy can salvage function. Don't let normal creatinine levels during a septic shock admission fool you into thinking the myopathy has resound. In this case, the CK normalized during a UTI/shock admission, reinforcing the immune-mediated nature.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Seronegative IMNM is real:** A negative HMGCR/SRP panel does not rule out immune-mediated necrotising myopathy. Up to one-third of IMNM patients lack these specific markers.
*   **Biopsy is mandatory for seronegative cases:** Histopathology showing necrotic fibers, myophagocytosis, and macrophage infiltrate can confirm the diagnosis when autoantibodies fail.
*   **Watch for GLP-1 triggers:** Be vigilant for muscle symptoms (weakness, high CK) in patients starting GLP-1 receptor agonists, particularly if they have risk factors (history of autoimmune disease or family history).
*   **Response to therapy is the ultimate confirmatory test:** Significant improvement in strength and normalization of CK with immunosuppression can validate the diagnosis of suspected immunologic myopathy even in seronegative individuals.

### ### Time Course Over Peak Levels: Differentiating IMNM from Acute Rhabdomyolysis

**EMCRIT meets the Weight Loss Clinic: When muscle enzymes mislead.**

We’ve all been there. You see a creatine kinase (CK) in the thousands on admission, your brain screams "Rhabdo/Rhabdo/Rhabdo," you think trauma, you think ischemia, you think toxin. You start hydration. But what if the story isn't about the acute injury? What if the injury is chronic, immune-mediated, and trending up over months rather than dropping down over days?

We recently encountered a case report that challenges the simplistic view of CK elevation in the structural pain vs. obese patient population. While GLP-1 agonists like tirzepatide have been associated with rhabdomyolysis in rare case reports, we must look at *kinetics* rather than just absolute numbers to differentiate a toxic acute rhabdomyolysis from an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

## The GLP-1 Controversy and the Wrong Turn

You don’t need a textbook to know GLP-1s are hot work. Semaglutide, tirzepatide, weekly injections, thin people, BP meds (high, low, high, ...). And now muscles.

Usually, when we see CK elevate in the setting of a new medication, we stop the medication and wait. If it’s toxic-gastric muscle cell necrosis (like the tirzepatide case referenced in the background), you see a massive spike (CK over 50,000 in that specific case) and you see a rapid normalization within days of discontinuation and rehydration. The pathophysiology is direct toxicity: the cell dies, the muscle liberates its markers, the body clears it, and we are done within a week or two.

But in this case, a 60s female patient with obesity, NAFLD, and a remote DVT showered us with red flags for inflammatory myopathy, which turned out to be a *seronegative* IMNM triggered by semaglutide after just one single 0.25mg dose.

Here’s the timeline that makes this case a breach in clinical dogma:

*   **Day 0:** Administered 0.25mg Semaglutide. Immediate severe adverse reaction (GI, renal failure, acidosis).
*   **Hospital Day 1:** CK was 337 U/L (normal 12–191). *Not* a rhabdomyolysis range.
*   **Hospital Discharge (5 days later):** Deconditioned.
*   **1 Month Post-Dose:** Patient still had GI symptoms, no muscle weakness.
*   **4 Months Post-Dose:** Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia. CK spiked to 3,992 U/L.
*   **Treatment Response:** No improvement with hydration alone. Dramatic response to Pulse Steroids + IVIG.
*   **Outcome:** Full resolution only after 8 months of stopping immunosuppression.

The devil is in the gap between Hospital Day 1 and Hospital Discharge. That initial visit was toxicile, but the *CK trajectory* over the next four months told us the story wasn’t toxic anymore. The CK didn’t spike to 100k and drop; it crawled up over months, suggestive of ongoing tissue destruction driven by inflammation rather than acute toxin accumulation.

## Why CK Trends Matter More Than Single Values

The classic teaching for rhabdomyolysis is that the enzyme level peaks early and falls after the insult is removed. If you stop the offending drug (Statins, Cobicistat, harmful supplements, electrolyte issues, etc.), the CK should trend down.

If you are treating with hydration but the CK keeps climbing for months, or if the weakness persists week-over-week, you are no longer treating a toxic myopathy. You are treating an autoimmune process.

In this case report, the authors highlight a critical distinction based on a recent literature review. The tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis resolved quickly after drug withdrawal. This patient had an antibody-negative IMNM that resolved *only* after immunosuppersion and withdrawal of the trigger. The CK did not normalize until 8 months later, off the steroids.

The clinical teaching here is simple but often missed in the EM half-life mindset: Serial CK is diagnostic. The initial CK of 337 U/L looked "normal" compared to the later 3,992. But the factor of 12 increase over 4 months was the red flag that screamed "Destruction persists" rather than "Acute change."

## Diagnostic Tools in the EM

In the field, we often miss this nuance. We focus on the 50,000 U/L on admission. But how do we distinguish IMNM from viral myositis vs. polymyositis?

1.  **The Biopsy:** The gold standard remains histology. You need necrotic myofibres with myophagocytosis. In this patient, the muscle biopsy showed disseminated necrosis and regeneration, plus myophagocytosis. Autoantibodies (HMGCR, SRP) were negative, but the histology was definitive.
2.  **The Clinical Course:** Does the patient improve with steroids? If a patient has "rhabdo" symptoms but fails to improve over weeks after stopping the drug, think autoimmune myocarditis or IMNM.

This highlights the diagnostic gap. We have the serology for HMGCR, SRP, and various myositis antibodies, but about one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. Don't assume just because the labs are negative, this is just "exercise induced" or "metabolic."

## Key Takeaways

*   **Differentiate Trajectory:** Rhabdomyolysis from toxins resolves within days; IMNM persists and worsens over weeks to months without immunosuppression.
*   **Serial Labs > Peak Labs:** A CK of 337 or 4,000 is meaningless clinically without understanding the rise/fall slope over weeks.
*   **Response to Immunosuppression:** If steroids/IVIG are required for CK to normalize, you aren't dealing with toxic rhabdo, even if the initial trigger was a drug.
*   **Seronegative Reality:** IMNM occurs without anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP in significant numbers. Rely on histology and clinical presentation.
*   **Look for the Hidden Triggers:** GLP-1s are not just for the stomach and fat anymore; monitor for muscle symptoms in obese/diabetic patients on metabolic therapy.



# Occult Dysphagia: A Red Flag for Myopathy in Patients with Metabolic Disease

In a clinical routine often blinded by metabolic risk factors, a common patient presentation can easily be misread. In patients with obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), we are conditioned to expect dysphagia from gastroesophageal reflux, pill oesophagitis, or simply "aging." But when you layer in proximal muscle weakness and a rapid, unexplained rise in creatine kinase (CK), the differential diagnosis sharpens. 

We recently reviewed a case that challenges the status quo: a 60-year-old female with severe dysphagia and progressive weakness following the initiation of semaglutide. This patient didn't just have "metabolic syndrome"; she developed Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM). In this post, we will dissect why you shouldn’t dismiss dysphagia as functional in the setting of a new weight-loss medication, and how a muscle biopsy can pivot you from conservative management to immunosuppression.

## The Diagnostic Trap

When a patient walks into a clinic complaining of trouble swallowing, your head often goes to the ENT or upper GI. For a patient with obesity and fatty liver, the reflex is often to diagnose GERD or functional dysphagia. It’s a comfortable diagnosis. It feels safer than ordering EMG, MRI, or muscle biopsies.

But consider the following: 
*   Mechanical causes don't typically present with such profound proximal limb weakness.
*   The "metabolic" patient is often on multiple medications, increasing the risk for toxins and side effects.
*   When dysphagia co-occurs with significant proximal weakness and elevated muscle enzymes, you are looking at a specific neuromuscular phenotype.

This case illustrates the classic triad that got missed initially: **Dysphagia, Dysphonia, and Proximal Weakness**. The patient's dysphagia was documented as difficulty swallowing solids and liquids for several days, not just food bolus. She was in her activities of daily living just months prior, fully independent. The metabolic context (obesity, hepatosteatosis) provided a plausible explanation for her initial deconditioning or even her gastric symptoms, masking the true pathology.

## The Clinical Narrative

The patient initiated semaglutide for refractory fatty liver 2.5 years after her initial metabolic workup. She tolerated the drug poorly at first—nausea and vomiting long enough to require hospitalization—but eventually stabilized. Four months after that initial dose, the decline began. She presented unable to sit upright, with a CK of nearly 4,000 U/L.

At this stage, the patient had no rash, no skin changes, and no history of statin use (which is the most common trigger for IMNM due to anti-HMGCR antibodies). She was seronegative for the classic myositis panel, including SRP and HMGCR antibodies.

The key to this patient’s survival is the decision to biopsy. Advanced imaging (MRI muscle) showed muscle edema and necrosis. ECMG or biopsy confirmed the immunohistochemistry: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, macrophage infiltration (CD68 stains), and a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. This histologic pattern screams IMNM.

The treatment protocol was aggressive but effective. She received high-dose methylprednisolone followed by IVIG. Within 12 days, her CK normalized. More importantly, her functional status returned to baseline after months of careful weaning off steroids. She was maintained off immunosuppression and off the semaglutide after this episode, with no recurrence over a 10-month follow-up.

## The Semaglutide Nexus

This brings us to the most controversial aspect of the report: the temporal association with GLP-1 receptor agonists. While IMNM is classically associated with statin therapy, reports of necrotizing features following certain antihypertensives, chemotherapeutics, and recently oral agents (like aluclidine) suggest our need to expand our differential.

Semaglutide is now ubiquitous in the obesity and T2DM landscape. While CK elevations are commonly attributed to the energy deficit from dieting or rhabdomyolysis from any weight loss medication that induces hypermetabolic states, this case argues for an immune-mediated mechanism.

*   The patient was CK normal or only mildly elevated immediately post-injection (337 U/L), yet developed a 12-fold rise (to 3992 U/L) over the subsequent 4 months while off the drug. 
*   Standard rhabdomyolysis usually resolves with hydration and withdrawal; this progressed and worsened over months until immunosuppression was started.
*   The WHO-Uppsala criteria classify the association as "probable" causality because the interval fits, other causes were reasonably excluded, and the clinical course resolved with withdrawal and immunosuppression.

While a definitive causal link requires rechallenge (ethically impossible here), the exponential rise in usage requires us to consider drug-induced triggers that mimic autoimmune disease. The temporal relationship here is not the short-term rhabdomyolysis seen with some other agents, but a delayed immune presentation.

## Key Takeaways

*   **Do not ignore dysphagia in the context of proximal weakness:** In patients with metabolic comorbidities, treat a dysphagia-IWHM (Ischemic Weakness Hypothesis) triad as a red flag for IMNM, not just GERD.
*   **Statins are not the only trigger:** While anti-HMGCR is strongly linked to statin use, other substances, including GLP-1 agonists, may trigger immune-mediated necrotising myopathy.
*   **Seronegative status doesn't rule out IMNM:** A significant subset of patients are negative for HMGCR or SRP antibodies. Clinical presentation and muscle biopsy often drive the diagnosis in this subset.
*   **Response to immunosuppression predicts pathophysiology:** If withdrawal of the drug and treatment with steroids/IVIG yields rapid functional recovery, it supports an immune-mediated process over direct toxic myopathy.
*   **Muscle Biopsy is worth the cost:** MRI findings of muscle edema in the proximal thighs and the diagnosis of necrotizing myopathy led to the correct identification. Muscle biopsy can differentiate between necrotising myopathy and inflammatory muscle disease.

## Conclusion

As our tools for managing weight and metabolic health expand, so do the side-effect profiles. This case serves as a reminder that dysphagia is a symptom, not always the etiology. When you see it in a rheumatic or metabolic patient, listen closely to the complaint of "weakness" in the shoulders and hips. In the absence of the expected autoimmune markers, trust the biopsy. The patient in this report had a preventable-appearing disability that resolved well with early recognition, but the cost of that delay could have been permanent dependence on assistive care or total dysphagia. Keep your eyes on the dysphagia in the metabolic patient; the myopathy might be hiding in plain sight.



# The Critical Role of Drug Cessation in Managing Drug-Induced Myositis

It seems in our modern EM world, when a patient presents with new proximal weakness and elevated CK, we are too busy checking ANA titers and venting on potential myositis panels to pause and think about what they just ingested or injected. Usually, we think of statins as the big bad wolf triggering necrotising myopathy (NMG). But the world of myositis is shifting. With the meteoric rise of GLP-1 receptor agonists for metabolic disease, we now have a potential new culprit that isn't just causing direct muscle toxicity, but potentially triggering an autoimmune storm.

I'm looking at a case report by Lam et al., detailing a 60-year-old woman who developed a staggering autoimmune necrotising myopathy shortly after receiving her first dose of semaglutide. This isn't just a simple case of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis that resolves with hydration and rest; this was immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). While the literature is scarce on this specific link, the clinical implications for how we manage drug-induced immune myositis are significant.

The real lesson here isn't just recognizing the new drug, but understanding the specific management trajectory that follows. If you identify a medication association, how does it change your immunosuppression plan? Let's break down the evidence in this case.

## The Invisible Culprit: Semaglutide and IMNM

This patient had no family history of myopathy, no unexplained exertions, and no statin use. Her medical history was chronic obesity and hepatosteatosis. She was initiated on semaglutide 2.5 years after her initial liver diagnosis. During that window, she had a substantial event involving nausea and vomiting, acute renal failure, and mild CK elevation (337 U/L). But that wasn't the main event.

Over the following four months, the picture deteriorated. She developed progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, and profound proximal weakness that halostasis to the point where she couldn't stand upright. Her Creatine Kinase (CK) skyrocketed to nearly 4000 U/L.

The key distinction to make here is between direct toxicity and immune-mediated destruction. The background in this report contrasts this with a previous case of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis. In that previous rhabdomyolysis case, the CK fell rapidly within days of stopping the drug. In this IMNM case, the 12-fold increase in CK occurred over months *after* a single dose, and the recovery required active immunosuppression, not just stopping the drug.

This patient was seronegative for the classic myositis antibodies. She lacked the hallmark HMGCR and SRP antibodies often seen in autoimmune necrotising myopathy. She had a low-titre SS-B positivity in the setting of negative ANA, which adds another layer of diagnostic complexity. Ultimately, the muscle biopsy was the truth-teller. It showed necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltration, and phagocytosis consistent with IMNM, but without the specific lymphocytic infiltrates seen in polymyositis.

## The "Off Switch": Cessation as Primary Therapy

Where many myositis treatments focus solely on the downstream effects (the inflammation), the most critical intervention in this case was stopping the upstream trigger: semaglutide.

We often treat idiopathic inflammatory myopathies with high-dose steroids and, if refractory, IVIG or biologics, assuming the disease is self-perpetuating. But in drug-induced scenarios, the offending agent often serves as a continuous "fuel" for the immune system to attack.

In this case, the patient did not receive additional IVIG after the initial course. She also saw a remarkably rapid improvement in functional status without long-term maintenance therapy. Why? Because the trigger was removed. The removal of the drug effectively lowered the antigenic load driving the autoimmunity, allowing the baseline immunosuppression to work without the need for escalation.

If this patient had continued semaglutide while on prednisone, one can imagine the therapeutic effect would have been blunted or the steroid burden would need to be significantly higher to counteract the ongoing inflammatory drive.

## Steroid Burden and Duration: Drug-Induced vs. Idiopathic

This brings us to the pivotal management principle described in the title. The duration and intensity of steroid therapy can change dramatically based on whether the IMNM is idiopathic or drug-induced.

Typical idiopathic IMNM requires indefinite therapy. Even HMGCR-positive statin-associated IMNM often requires months to years of immunosuppression before tapering can begin slowly, with high risks of relapse.

Here, the steroid wean was aggressive. The patient received pulse methylprednisolone, followed by oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg, and was successfully weaned off her prednisone rapidly over 2 months. She remained off immunosuppression for 7-8 months at the time of follow-up with normal CK and PHP function.

This suggests a fundamental difference in the course of action. Drug-induced IMNM, once the agent is removed, behaves more like a self-limiting condition supported by a short immunomodulatory shadow. Idiopathic IMNM requires the long war.
*   **The Shield:** If you can identify and remove the trigger, you might salvage the need for lifelong immunosuppression in certain patients with IMNM.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Check the Labs and the Drug List:** If you see IMNM (necrosis on biopsy, elevated CK, proximal weakness) and the patient is on semaglutide or tirzepatide, including these in your differential, especially if the onset follows initiation of therapy.
*   **The Importance of Biopsy:** Serology can be useless, especially in cases negative for SRP and HMGCR. Muscle biopsy showing necrosis without inflammation, or macrophage-dominated phagocytosis, is the diagnostic anchor for necrotising myopathy.
*   **The Cessation Benefit:** Drug cessation is the most effective treatment. In drug-associated cases, removal of the offending agent leads to a lower steroid burden in the acute phase compared to idiopathic cases, which rarely respond to cessation alone.
*   **Beware of Seronegativity:** Not all IMNM patients have HMGCR or SRP antibodies. Clinical presentation and biopsy support are required when antibody panels come back negative.
*   **Monitor the Course:** If the CK stays elevated despite stopping a new medication, this may cross into autoimmune territory. If it improves rapidly after stopping, consider direct toxicity or toxicity-induced myositis.

## Take-Home Messages

*   Drug-discontinuation must be the immediate first step in suspected drug-induced IMNM, prior to escalating immunosuppression, as the offending agent drives the inflammation.
*   Drug-induced IMNM may allow for a more rapid steroid wean compared to idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, which typically require prolonged high-dose therapy.
*   GLP-1 receptor agonists (specifically semaglutide and tirzepatide) have emerging associations with necrotising myopathy and should trigger a full myositis workup if weakness arises.
*   Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic standard for necrotising myopathy, particularly in seronegative populations where HMGCR and SRP antibodies are absent.
*   Patient outcomes in drug-induced IMNM correlate strongly with timely trigger identification and withdrawal, rather than aggressive long-term immunotherapy alone.
