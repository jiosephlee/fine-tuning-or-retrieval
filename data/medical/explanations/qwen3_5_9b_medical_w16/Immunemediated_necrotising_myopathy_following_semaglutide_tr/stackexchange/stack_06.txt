Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient with metabolic comorbidities such as NAFLD and concurrent skeletal muscle injury (elevated CK), how should one interpret concurrent transaminitis to avoid attributing muscle enzymes to liver disease and vice versa?

Interpretation of transaminitis in the setting of elevated creatine kinase requires a clear understanding of enzyme distribution, as neither AST nor ALT are liver-specific. In this specific case, the patient had biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with mild transaminitis (ALT > AST) established three years prior to the acute decline. When skeletal muscle injury developed, both muscle enzymes and hepatic enzymes were elevated concurrently.

To distinguish between these sources and avoid diagnostic attribution errors, one must adhere to the following physiological and clinical principles grounded in this case:

1.  **Establish Baseline Etiology:** As seen in this patient, a history of metabolic comorbidities like obesity and a prior biopsy diagnosing NAFLD strongly suggest that mild, stable transaminitis is hepatic in origin rather than secondary to muscle breakdown. Skeletal muscle is a poor source for ALT but contributes significantly to AST levels; however, persistent mild elevation distinct from acute muscle injury patterns suggests chronic liver pathology.

2.  **Analyze Temporal Trends of Enzymes:** This is the most critical differentiator in this case report. Waning suspicion of the liver as the source of ongoing derangement is possible because "her borderline elevation in AST/ALT persisted after CK levels normalised." If transaminases normalize concurrently with a decrease in CK (implying muscle recovery), the primary source was myopathic. However, in this patient, the chronic liver enzymes persisted despite the resolution of the acute muscle injury, confirming the hepatic source of the AST/ALT was independent of the immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

3.  **Utilize Magnitude and Ratio:** While AST elevations can occur with muscle injury, the ratio of CK to transaminases is essential. In this case, the CK elevation was profound (up to 3992 U/L) relative to the transaminases (AST 191 U/L, ALT 165 U/L). In pure liver injury, you would rarely see CK in the thousands. Conversely, do not dismiss a concurrent rise in transaminases as "just muscle" in a patient with known hepatic vulnerability; in this case, it was likely a summation of chronic liver disease and acute muscle injury.

**Clinical Teaching Summary**
Always measure CK when evaluating transaminitis. Conversely, do not attribute all AST/ALT elevation to liver disease in myopathic presentations. Look for a history of liver biopsy, the magnitude of muscle enzymes, and—most importantly—the distinction in trajectories: CK should respond to immunosuppression and cessation of offending agents (e.g., semaglutide), while chronic NAFLD markers may persist. This patient resolved the muscle crisis but maintained the hepatic history, proving they are distinct pathologies often concurrent in metabolic syndrome patients.
