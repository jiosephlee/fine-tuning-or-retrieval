Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct clinical entity within the spectrum of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, characterized primarily by severe proximal muscle weakness and myal pain, accompanied by necrotising muscle fibers with a paucity of endomysial inflammatory infiltrates. This histological profile differentiates IMNM from classic polymyositis, which typically demonstrates a dense CD8+ T-cell infiltrate invading non-necrotic muscle fibers, or dermatomyositis, which is defined by perifascicular atrophy and muscle fiber necrosis at the periphery. In the case of IMNM, the muscle biopsy findings of necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration, as observed in this patient’s right vastus lateralis biopsy, highlight the central role of muscle cell death rather than lymphocytic invasion. The muscle biopsy stained for CD68 (a macrophage marker) and demonstrated acid phosphatase activity, confirming myophagocytosis where macrophages engulf and clean up necrotic muscle debris, a hallmark that should prompt clinicians to consider IMNM in patients with severe muscle breakdown meeting specific exclusion criteria.

The pathophysiology of most classic cases of IMNM is strongly linked to the presence of autoantibodies, specifically anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). The association with HMGCR, the rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis, is particularly well-established in patients exposed to statin therapy. Statin-induced myopathy facilitates the presentation of HMGCR peptides by muscle cells, generating T-cell responses and leading to antibody production, though the text notes that HMGCR antibodies are often the most prevalent driver of necrotizing myopathy in the statin population. While statin-associated IMNM typically follows the administration of lipid-lowering agents, the underlying immune mechanism involves HMGCR autoantibodies that trigger a cascade of cytotoxic T-cell responses and complement activation. Understanding this standard pathophysiology is critical for the differential diagnosis of this patient, as she served as the proband for a potential drug-induced variation of this syndrome absent the classic serological marker.

Significant diagnostic challenges arise in cases of seronegative IMNM, which constitute approximately one-third of all patients with this condition. The absence of anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies, as found in this patient’s myositis panel, renders the diagnosis more difficult and suggests a broader spectrum of potential triggers beyond the known autoantibody-associated mechanisms. In this clinical context, the patient tested negative for SRP and HMGCR antibodies despite meeting the clinical and histological criteria for IMNM. This seronegative presentation underscores the necessity of integrating clinical history, such as the temporal relationship to semaglutide use, with histopathological findings when autoantibody testing does not immediately clarify the diagnosis. While the lack of autoantibodies implies myopies may exist yet to be identified, clinically, the course remains consistent with an immune-mediated process requiring immunosuppression regardless of serostatus.

The mechanism driving the specific pathology of IMNM involves overexpression of MHC class I on the membrane of skeletal muscle fibers in the presence of complement system activation. This leads to the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), which causes direct lysis of the muscle fiber membrane and subsequent necrosis. Muscle biopsy findings in severely affected areas, such as the necrotic fibers membrane attack complex positive stain seen in this patient, serve as additional evidence for this mechanism. The resolution of muscle weakness and normalization of creatine kinase in this patient following the cessation of the implicated agent and a course of immunosuppression, including corticosteroids and IVIG, supports the immunopathogenic nature of the disease. The rapid clearance of the necrotic debris and cessation of the complement-driven lysis lead to muscle regeneration and recovery, illustrating the dynamic nature of the muscle pathology in response to immunomodulatory therapy.
