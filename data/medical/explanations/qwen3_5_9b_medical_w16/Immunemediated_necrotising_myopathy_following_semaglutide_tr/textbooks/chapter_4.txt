Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 4: ## Management Strategies and Immunosuppression Protocols

In the management of severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), particularly when presenting with life-threatening or severely disabling features, the primary goal is to rapidly suppress the inflammatory process to prevent irreversible muscle damage and complications such as aspiration or respiratory failure. In this case, the patient presented with significant proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia, alongside a dramatic elevation in creatine kinase (CK) levels peaking at 3992 U/L. To address this acute inflammatory crisis, the patient was administered high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for five days. This pulse steroid therapy is a standard first-line intervention for severe myopathic crises, designed to rapidly inhibit the immune attack on muscle fibers. The decision to initiate this aggressive regimen was driven by the inability to stand or sit upright, the involvement of bulbar function affecting swallowing, and the risk of respiratory compromise, necessitating immediate and potent anti-inflammatory control before transitioning to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily.

While corticosteroids are the cornerstone of IMNM treatment, this patient required adjunctive therapy due to the persistence of weakness and the severity of her clinical presentation. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was indicated following the pulse steroid course because she maintained severe features and persistent weakness despite initial treatment. In this context, IVIG 2 g/kg split over four days was administered to modulate the immune response through Fc receptor blockade and neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies. The addition of IVIG supports a broader clinical principle wherein immunosuppressive therapy is escalated in refractory or severe cases. The patient demonstrated a marked reduction in CK levels over two weeks, supporting the efficacy of this combined approach. Importantly, the management plan acknowledged the quality of the response; since the CK normalized significantly, no further doses of IVIG were pursued after the initial course, balancing individual responder risk with cost and infection exposure.

Long-term immunosuppression strategies in IMNM typically involve a slow taper of oral corticosteroids over 12 to 24 months to prevent recurrence, given the chronic autoimmune nature of the disease. However, the treatment trajectory in this case deviated from the norm due to the suspected drug-induced etiology. Clinical reasoning centered on the temporal relationship between semaglutide use and symptom onset, leading to the hypothesis that the condition would resolve with removal of the trigger. Consequently, the tapering schedule was aggressive but monitored closely, resulting in a successful weaning from prednisone over a period of two months. This approach was reinforced by her sustained clinical improvement and returning CK levels to normal ranges, allowing for discontinuation of immunosuppression entirely by the eight-month mark. This illustrates that in probable drug-induced IMNM, shorter courses of immunosuppression may be sufficient if the offending agent is removed and the patient exhibits robust functional recovery.

Concurrent management of comorbidities must be considered when treating severe autoimmune conditions with potent steroids and immunosuppressants. At 12 days post-discharge, this patient was readmitted for septic shock secondary to a *Pseudomonas* urinary tract infection. Notably, during this admission her CK levels were found to be normal, indicating that the muscle-specific inflammation had not recurred at the time of infection. While immunosuppression increases susceptibility to opportunistic infections, the management of the septic shock itself was driven by the clinical necessity of controlling the infection. The resolution of the urinary tract infection and the presence of normal inflammatory markers allowed for a return to outpatient follow-up, highlighting the importance of vigilant monitoring for infectious complications in patients receiving high-dose steroids.

Finally, a critical component of the management strategy involved the discontinuation of the offending agent, semaglutide. Although the patient had received only a single dose prior to symptom onset, the persistence of symptoms and the severe nature of the injury following that dose were used to establish probable causality. In the absence of a proven target autoantibody (the myositis panel was negative for HMGCR and SRP), pharmacovigilance relies heavily on dechallenge procedures. Removing semaglutide was essential to halt the potential ongoing antigenic drive or metabolic trigger that may have precipitated the autoimmune response. The discussion of causality emphasized the lack of rechallenge (ethically impossible) but noted that the persistent improvement and drug-free recovery supported the suspicion of a drug-induced etiology. This case underscores that in IMNM, identifying and removing the triggering factor can be as impactful as pharmacologic immunosuppression for determining long-term prognosis and management duration.
