Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning: Biopsy, Serology, and Drug Causality

In the workup of this patient, interpreting laboratory trends was the first step toward differentiating between metabolic liver disease and emergent muscle pathology. The patient had a preexisting history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with transaminitis, manifesting as elevated AST and ALT levels prior to semaglutide use. While aspartate aminotransferase (AST) can be derived from muscle mitochondria, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is predominantly hepatic, making the isolated persistence of these enzymes indicative of her underlying hepatosteatosis rather than muscle breakdown. However, the dramatic subsequent elevation of creatine kinase (CK) followed a distinct trajectory, rising from a modest 337 U/L during the initial hospitalization to a peak of 3992 U/L approximately four months later. This 12-fold increase is disproportionate to the stable hepatic profile and specific for a severe myopathy. For the clinician, this pattern highlights that while transaminases can occasionally coexist with myopathy, the magnitude and persistence of CK elevation in the context of progressive symmetrical weakness should trigger a myositis-specific evaluation rather than assuming a progression of fatty liver disease.

The definitive diagnostic features were identified through muscle histology, which distinguished this patient’s condition from inflammatory myopathies like polymyositis. Histological analysis of the vastus lateralis muscle revealed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, which are hallmarks of necrotising myopathy. Immunohistochemical staining confirmed an inflammatory response mediated primarily by macrophages, evidenced by acid phosphatase staining and robust CD68 (macrophage marker) positivity with myophagocytosis. Notably, the biopsy showed only rare CD3-positive T-cells and perivascular inflammatory cells. This paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates compared to the density of necrotic tissue aligns with the pathological definition of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), where muscle fibers are destroyed by the membrane attack complex rather than by a dense T-cell invasion. The presence of the membrane attack complex positive stain on necrotising myofibres rather than a dense inflammatory infiltrate is a key discriminator from classic polymyositis, necessitating specific historical context regarding the patient's medication exposure.

Serological limitations posed a significant diagnostic challenge, as this patient presented with seronegative IMNM. Her myositis panel was negative for high-risk autoantibodies, including anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) and anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), which are classically associated with the two major subtypes of IMNM. Furthermore, the ANA was negative, and only a low-titre SS-B antibody was present, a finding that lacked clear correlation with her immunologic phenotype given the absence of other Sjogren’s syndrome manifestations. This scenario underscores a critical clinical lesson: while these antibodies are biomarkers of disease, a negative panel does not rule out IMNM, particularly when histopathology is consistent. Approximately one-third of patients with IMNM lack detectable autoantibodies, necessitating reliance on clinical presentation and, in ambiguous cases, muscle biopsy to confirm diagnosis and exclude overlap syndromes.

Differentiating drug-induced toxic myopathy from immunologic IMNM driven by a drug is central to understanding the required treatment duration. Drug-induced toxic myopathy is typically characterized by rapid resolution upon cessation of the offending agent without the need for immunosuppression. In contrast, this patient’s clinical course showed that CK levels did not normalize quickly after stopping semaglutide but rather progressed to severe weakness requiring immunosuppression. The patient required high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) to achieve sustained improvement, evidenced by normal CK levels and functional recovery while off medication. This delay in improvement—occurring over months rather than days—indicates that the drug likely acted as a trigger for an autoimmune process rather than exerting direct skeletal muscle toxicity. Clinicians must appreciate that the severity and persistence of weakness relative to the discontinuation of the suspected agent are vital indicators of the underlying mechanism.

Finally, establishing the causal link between semaglutide and this specific autoimmune reaction relied on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) criteria for causality assessment. In this case, there was a reasonable time relationship between the initial exposure to semaglutide and the onset of severe myopathy, four months after a single dose, despite the initial production of symptoms shortly thereafter. Since the patient denied other risk factors such as statin use, alcohol, or endocrine disorders, it was unlikely that her IMNM was due to causes other than the medication. A clinician must also observe that the condition responded clinically to removal of the agent and brief immunosuppression, fulfilling the dechallenge component of the criteria. Although future studies will likely clarify the exact pathophysiology, this case demonstrates how case-specific evidence, combined with pharmacovigilance frameworks like the UMC criteria, can identify probable adverse drug reactions in the absence of prior seropositive markers.
