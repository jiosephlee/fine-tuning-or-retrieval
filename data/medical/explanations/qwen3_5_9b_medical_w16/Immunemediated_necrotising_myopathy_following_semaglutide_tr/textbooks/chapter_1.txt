Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Differential Diagnosis

The patient was a 60-year-old female presenting with a complex history of metabolic and hepatic disease, which established the context for her medication initiation. She had a known history of obesity, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and remote venous thromboembolism. Three years prior to her decline, she underwent extensive evaluation for persistent transaminitis revealing ALT elevations approximately twice the upper limit of normal with ALT exceeding AST. Abdominal imaging and biopsy confirmed hepatosteatosis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), reinforcing the severity of her metabolic burden. This specific history is clinically relevant because NAFLD is a frequent indication for weight-loss pharmacotherapy, directly leading to the commencement of semaglutide. Understanding this baseline is essential, as the thigh biopsy would later distinguish between hepatic steatosis and potential myopathic causes of transaminase fluctuations.

Initial clinical deterioration occurred immediately following the first dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously). Within hours, the patient developed severe gastrointestinal intolerance, manifesting as nausea and vomiting that persisted to the point of hospitalization. The acute presentation was complicated by acute renal failure and severe metabolic acidosis requiring intravenous fluids and bicarbonate. While the acute hospitalization involved hospitalization details regarding IVIG and subsequent course, the critical teaching point regarding the substance use occurred during this initial phase. Notably, creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L during this acute admission, which did not suggest massive muscle breakdown or typical rhabdomyolysis. Concurrently, troponin levels were normal, and the patient did not present with muscle pain or cola-colored urine, leaving the acute failure initially attributed to dehydration and hypovolemia rather than direct myotoxicity. This initial presentation often lands the clinician in a diagnostic trap of acute drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, yet the lack of severe CPK elevation and subsequent resolution of renal function suggested a complex mechanism beyond simple toxicity.

Following discharge, the clinical picture shifted from acute intolerance to a progressive, subacute decline spanning four months. Despite the cessation of semaglutide and resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms, the patient developed progressive proximal muscle weakness that eventually prevented her from standing or sitting upright. She additionally reported dysphagia and dysphonia. This evolution distinguishes the case from typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, where normalization of muscle enzymes usually occurs within days of discontinuation. Instead, the 12-fold increase in CK levels at the tertiary referral suggests a subacute inflammatory process allowing time for further diagnostic consideration. The presence of bulbar symptoms is a critical red flag that warrants investigation for systemic myopathies, hypoglycemia, or motor neuron disease, given the distribution of weakness extending beyond the hips to the throat.

Physical examination upon admission to the tertiary center revealed significant weakness localized to the proximal muscle groups, without evidence of dermatomyositis features such as malar rash or heliotrope rash. There was no synovitis, sicca symptoms, or skin changes, which initially narrowed the differential diagnosis away from classic Cutaneous Rheumatologic Myopopathies and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus. However, the absence of specific cutaneous signs does not exclude immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), particularly in the subset of patients with seronegative status. Elevated inflammatory markers, including ESR and C-reactive protein, despite the absence of a constitutional fever, supported an inflammatory etiology rather than functional deconditioning alone. By this stage, the clinical team had to navigate a specific window of diagnosis where the combination of subacute progressive weakness, bulbar involvement, and unexplained transaminitis prompted the final suspicion of IMNM rather than a continuation of the acute tolerability issue with semaglutide.

Evaluating this timeline is crucial for distinguishing between drug-induced toxic myopathy and drug-triggered immune-mediated disease. In cases of direct toxic myopathy, such as the documented rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide, CK levels typically peak acutely and resolve rapidly with hydration and drug withdrawal, as seen in the patient's initial 337 U/L reading. Conversely, the subacute exacerbation and 4-month lag before significant weakness demonstrated the likely autoimmune pathophysiology. While the patient’s pro-BNP and troponin levels were initially normal or mildly elevated, satisfying the need to rule out primary cardiac etiologies or stress cardiomyopathy, the persistence of weakness without cardiac decompensation favored a primary myopathic process. Establishing this window of opportunity is vital, as early recognition of IMNM allows for timely immunosuppressive intervention, whereas attributing the symptoms solely to metabolic side effects or drug toxicity might lead to delayed treatment and prolonged functional decline.
