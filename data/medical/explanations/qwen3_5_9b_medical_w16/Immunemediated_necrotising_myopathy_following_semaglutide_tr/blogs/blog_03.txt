Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### ### Time Course Over Peak Levels: Differentiating IMNM from Acute Rhabdomyolysis

**EMCRIT meets the Weight Loss Clinic: When muscle enzymes mislead.**

We’ve all been there. You see a creatine kinase (CK) in the thousands on admission, your brain screams "Rhabdo/Rhabdo/Rhabdo," you think trauma, you think ischemia, you think toxin. You start hydration. But what if the story isn't about the acute injury? What if the injury is chronic, immune-mediated, and trending up over months rather than dropping down over days?

We recently encountered a case report that challenges the simplistic view of CK elevation in the structural pain vs. obese patient population. While GLP-1 agonists like tirzepatide have been associated with rhabdomyolysis in rare case reports, we must look at *kinetics* rather than just absolute numbers to differentiate a toxic acute rhabdomyolysis from an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

## The GLP-1 Controversy and the Wrong Turn

You don’t need a textbook to know GLP-1s are hot work. Semaglutide, tirzepatide, weekly injections, thin people, BP meds (high, low, high, ...). And now muscles.

Usually, when we see CK elevate in the setting of a new medication, we stop the medication and wait. If it’s toxic-gastric muscle cell necrosis (like the tirzepatide case referenced in the background), you see a massive spike (CK over 50,000 in that specific case) and you see a rapid normalization within days of discontinuation and rehydration. The pathophysiology is direct toxicity: the cell dies, the muscle liberates its markers, the body clears it, and we are done within a week or two.

But in this case, a 60s female patient with obesity, NAFLD, and a remote DVT showered us with red flags for inflammatory myopathy, which turned out to be a *seronegative* IMNM triggered by semaglutide after just one single 0.25mg dose.

Here’s the timeline that makes this case a breach in clinical dogma:

*   **Day 0:** Administered 0.25mg Semaglutide. Immediate severe adverse reaction (GI, renal failure, acidosis).
*   **Hospital Day 1:** CK was 337 U/L (normal 12–191). *Not* a rhabdomyolysis range.
*   **Hospital Discharge (5 days later):** Deconditioned.
*   **1 Month Post-Dose:** Patient still had GI symptoms, no muscle weakness.
*   **4 Months Post-Dose:** Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia. CK spiked to 3,992 U/L.
*   **Treatment Response:** No improvement with hydration alone. Dramatic response to Pulse Steroids + IVIG.
*   **Outcome:** Full resolution only after 8 months of stopping immunosuppression.

The devil is in the gap between Hospital Day 1 and Hospital Discharge. That initial visit was toxicile, but the *CK trajectory* over the next four months told us the story wasn’t toxic anymore. The CK didn’t spike to 100k and drop; it crawled up over months, suggestive of ongoing tissue destruction driven by inflammation rather than acute toxin accumulation.

## Why CK Trends Matter More Than Single Values

The classic teaching for rhabdomyolysis is that the enzyme level peaks early and falls after the insult is removed. If you stop the offending drug (Statins, Cobicistat, harmful supplements, electrolyte issues, etc.), the CK should trend down.

If you are treating with hydration but the CK keeps climbing for months, or if the weakness persists week-over-week, you are no longer treating a toxic myopathy. You are treating an autoimmune process.

In this case report, the authors highlight a critical distinction based on a recent literature review. The tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis resolved quickly after drug withdrawal. This patient had an antibody-negative IMNM that resolved *only* after immunosuppersion and withdrawal of the trigger. The CK did not normalize until 8 months later, off the steroids.

The clinical teaching here is simple but often missed in the EM half-life mindset: Serial CK is diagnostic. The initial CK of 337 U/L looked "normal" compared to the later 3,992. But the factor of 12 increase over 4 months was the red flag that screamed "Destruction persists" rather than "Acute change."

## Diagnostic Tools in the EM

In the field, we often miss this nuance. We focus on the 50,000 U/L on admission. But how do we distinguish IMNM from viral myositis vs. polymyositis?

1.  **The Biopsy:** The gold standard remains histology. You need necrotic myofibres with myophagocytosis. In this patient, the muscle biopsy showed disseminated necrosis and regeneration, plus myophagocytosis. Autoantibodies (HMGCR, SRP) were negative, but the histology was definitive.
2.  **The Clinical Course:** Does the patient improve with steroids? If a patient has "rhabdo" symptoms but fails to improve over weeks after stopping the drug, think autoimmune myocarditis or IMNM.

This highlights the diagnostic gap. We have the serology for HMGCR, SRP, and various myositis antibodies, but about one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. Don't assume just because the labs are negative, this is just "exercise induced" or "metabolic."

## Key Takeaways

*   **Differentiate Trajectory:** Rhabdomyolysis from toxins resolves within days; IMNM persists and worsens over weeks to months without immunosuppression.
*   **Serial Labs > Peak Labs:** A CK of 337 or 4,000 is meaningless clinically without understanding the rise/fall slope over weeks.
*   **Response to Immunosuppression:** If steroids/IVIG are required for CK to normalize, you aren't dealing with toxic rhabdo, even if the initial trigger was a drug.
*   **Seronegative Reality:** IMNM occurs without anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP in significant numbers. Rely on histology and clinical presentation.
*   **Look for the Hidden Triggers:** GLP-1s are not just for the stomach and fat anymore; monitor for muscle symptoms in obese/diabetic patients on metabolic therapy.
