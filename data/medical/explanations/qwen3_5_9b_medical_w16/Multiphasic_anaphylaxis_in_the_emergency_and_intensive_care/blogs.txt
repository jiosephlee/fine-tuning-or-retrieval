# The 0.3 mg Trap: Why 'Adult' Autoinjectors May Be Underdosing

Anaphylaxis management is often taught like a muscle-memory reflex: Draw up the IM glucocorticoid, give the biphasic steroid, watch for the BP. You know this well. But when resistance meets relapse, that memory can't carry you all the way. We always think we’ve made the call on our initial round of epinephrine, but recent case reports suggest that in the setting of a multiphasic or recurrent shock, our "adult" auto-injector might actually be an underdose.

Consider this: A woman in her 60s, weighing 104 kilograms, walks into the ER with a Clarithromycin-induced anaphylactic shock. She is given the standard 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. Her BP comes back up, her rash clears, and she seems stable. Then, two hours later, she goes dark again. And two hours after that, she goes down again. Over the course of 12 hours, she experiences *four* distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock requiring episodic intervention, escalating from auto-injector to IV prepared doses. She isn't just biphasic; she’s multiphasic.

The question echoing through the ED isn't just "did you get the airway secured?" it should be looking like "was the complement receptor system overloaded, engaged the downregulation of beta receptors, or did you actually just give too little vasopressor?"

## The Case: Clarity Amidst Chaos
The patient presented initially with hypotension (74/50) and hypoxia (88% SpO2) shortly after taking a single 500 mg dose of Clarithromycin. Her history was spotless relevant to the anaphylaxis up to that point; no previous systemic anaphylaxis, just a mild skin reaction to beta-lactams in adolescence. She was admitted to the ward for monitoring. Standard protocol usually kicks in: saline bolus, antihistamines, and ensure observation for at least 4-6 hours to catch biphasic recurrence.

This patient checked the box. She recovered. But she came back within the observation window. The intubation wasn't needed, but she was transferred to the ICU. Here’s where the clinical details get thorny. During the first two episodes of relapse, the team utilized the standard 0.3 mg auto-injector. By the third and fourth episodes, due to the clear underdosing in that 104 kg body weight, they switched to 0.5 mg IV prepared epinephrine.

Interestingly, the severity of the attacks decreased each time. The initial presentation featured deep cyanosis and profound hypotension. By the final episode, it was manageable dyspnea and pruritus. But the fix came not just from increasing the vasopressor to 0.5 mg, but recognizing the pattern of recurrence. The patient was stabilized, transferred back to standard care after 24 hours, and discharged to Levocetirizine and an auto-injector taught to the patient.

However, the underlying etiology changed the calculus. Tryptase levels were elevated during the third episode and stayed high in follow-up, along with hepatomegaly and minor diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis. This suggests we aren't just dealing with a simple food/drug allergy in a normal immune background; mast cell density is higher.

## Why The 0.3 mg Auto-Injector is a Trap
We are in a world where epinephrine auto-injectors are housed conveniently for patients. But when we talk to ER directors, ponders the logistics, and read retelling the story in guidelines, we see a glaring discrepancy.

Guideline recommendations for epinephrine dosing in adults are calculated at 0.01 mg/kg, with a cap often recommended at 0.5 mg for adults over 50 kg. Why? Because 0.5 mg is roughly 0.01 mg/kg for a 50 kg person. But we know no adult weighs exactly 50 kg.

The case mentioned above? 104 kg. A 0.3 mg dose at that weight is equivalent to a 30 mg/kg dose. If you look at the calculation, it is actually a massive underdose. A 0.01 mg/kg dose for that patient should approach 1.04 mg cap, but arguably 0.5 mg is a safer cap for safety. Given the limitations of autoinjectors, the 0.3 mg device is the sole commercially available option in many systems for adults, yet for a 100+ kg patient, it is a fraction of the recommendation.

A 2025 retrospective study cited in the discussion of this case analyzed this exact dilemma. The data indicated that patients who initially received 0.3 mg required significantly more care, more escalation, and subsequent hospital resources compared to those who received the full 0.5 mg initially. The 0.3 mg auto-injector is the default "adult" dose, but physiologically, it treats the smallest adult not the heaviest.

In the case presented, the team admitted they hesitated between two strategies for the subsequent relapses: maintain continuous epinephrine through a pump or wait and hope for the voluntariness of spontaneous resolution. In an ideal world, there is evidence-driven guidance. But the lack of defined guidance for multiphasic courses creates oscillating risk. The patient received prednisone and dexamethasone as well, acknowledging the rise of old-school thinking on steroid prevention of biphasic reactions, despite recent systematic reviews suggesting they do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis.

## Clinical Pearls
*   **0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg:** The standard auto-injector (EpiPen) contains 0.3 mg. While many guidelines suggest 0.3 mg for adults, for patients >50 kg or in massive anaphylaxis, 0.5 mg is the calculated standard cap.
*   **Auto-injector in the ED:** Don't be afraid to use the auto-injector in the emergency department. It can be faster, less hesitation, and "no needle" delay. But recognize the rapidity of failure if that device is underdosing the patient. Switch to 0.5 mg options as soon as feasible.
*   **Multiphasic > Biphasic:** Multiphasic anaphylaxis has *four* or more distinct episodes. Biphasic has two. Multiphasic patients are not simple biphasic courses, and they are at higher risk of fatality. Treatment requires vigilance beyond the initial 4-6 hours.
*   **Mastocytosis Consideration:** Systemic mastocytosis (high baseline tryptase, multiorgan involvement) predisposes patients to severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis. If your anaphylaxis is weirdly recurrent, keep mastocytosis and baseline tryptase monitoring in your differential.
*   **No Reassurance Assumption:** Don't trust the clinical response as predicting the final course until you’ve had at least a 4-6 hour quiet window. If that pan-fry is multiphasic, watch closely.

## Take-Home Messages
*   A 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector is a known underdose for patients weighing >50 kg; consider switching to non-auto-injector preparations of 0.5 mg in the ED.
*   Multiphasic anaphylaxis requires extended monitoring beyond standard biphasic protocols, ideally into the 12-hour window.
*   Suspected mastocytosis or elevated tryptase should be investigated in recurrent anaphylaxis, especially when symptoms fail to resolve with standard therapy.



# Steroids Save Who? Re-evaluating the Role of Corticosteroids in Anaphylaxis

## The Paradox of Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

It starts in the ED, always does. You have a patient walking in with hypotension, hypoxia, and skin that looks like bronzed meat. You know the drill: epinephrine, airway, fluids, antihistamines. In this specific case, a 60-year-old woman presenting after taking clarithromycin, things looked under control when the numbers normalized. Blood pressure back to baseline? Check. Facial swelling subsiding? Check. You transition to the acute ward. But then the classic horror of biphasic anaphylaxis strikes.

She didn't get out of the hospital the night she arrived. Despite the initial response to 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector, she redeemed herself in the ICU with four separate anaphylactic episodes. Each time requiring a specific bolus of epinephrine. It is a textbook example of "recurrence of symptoms despite adequate care." The pathology here was likely clarithromycin, but the substrate was a high-risk biology. Tryptase levels were elevated during the episodes and remained high after resolution. Further analysis suggested a systemic mastocytosis phenotype, even if full diagnostic criteria weren't met.

In the middle of this chaos, the medical team added the "ancient remedy": corticosteroids. They were given both prednisone and dexamethasone. Why? Because that is what we do. We fear the biphasic recurrence, so we drench the patient in anti-inflammatory medication. The case report candidly admits that while they supplemented the treatment, the role of corticosteroids remains a subject of intense debate and rather poor evidence.

## The Evidence Against the Holy Grail

For decades, we fell into the trap of prophylactic corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis. The logic was seductive: inflammation takes time to build the cytokine cascade that causes the relapse. If we stop it sooner (within 4 to 6 hours), we prevent the second wave. But as the literature accumulates, this dogma is becoming untenable.

Recent systematic reviews have been sobering. One 2017 review examined twenty two studies, finding that as many as twenty one of them showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Only one study actually supported the use of corticosteroids. This is not to say they have no utility elsewhere, but using them specifically to prevent relapse of anaphylaxis is the equivalent of throwing cactus glue on a snake bite; it's technically "medical" but offers no proven survival benefit.

It is getting worse. A 2019 study reported an increased likelihood of ICU or ward admission if corticosteroids were used prehospital. When you look at the side effect profile—hyperglycemia was noted in this very patient with a glucose of 12.7 mmol/L—the risk is becoming clearer. We are trading a manageable drug reaction for a prolonged hospital stay without a clear safety net. The case presentation highlights this tension: at primary care level, prednisone was used, but at the ICU level, dexamethasone was used, reflecting conflicting protocols within the same hospital.

## Navigating the Uncertainty

So, where does this leave us in a practice environment where guidelines haven't caught up (or at all)? The case authors suggest that optimal observation duration is debated. The Dutch and World Allergy Organization notes that half the biphasic reactions occur within six to 12 hours, yet there is no consensus monitoring recommendation that balances safety with resource allocation.

This case is important because the patient had *multiphasic* anaphylaxis. She relapsed not once, but three times within the first 12 hours of admission (making four total). This is the extreme end of the phenotypic spectrum. She required 0.3 mg auto-injector doses initially, then 0.5 mg intramuscular doses later. This highlights a separate but related issue in emergency medicine: sticky but critical underdosing of auto-injectors. For an adult weighing 104 kg, a 0.3 mg dose is sub-therapeutic. The fact that symptoms ultimately decreased in intensity over time is comforting, but it was achieved through serial epinephrine, not steroids. Epinephrine is the mechanism we should trust for relapse termination with its receptor activation. If we have a patient who is crashing, they need Epi, not a steroid injection.

This brings us back to the mastocytosis question. While she didn't meet formal criteria, the elevated baseline tryptase and clinical presentation make her biology scream mast cell instability. In mastocytosis, the baseline mediator burden is different. Does that change the steroid equation? The authors admit that research specifically addressing the role of systemic mastocytosis in biphasic anaphylaxis is virtually non-existent. This highlights the desperate need for better guidelines, or perhaps a registry, to track what actually works when the first line is exhausted.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Epinephrine is the game changer:** Serial administration of epinephrine led to this patient's resolution, whereas corticosteroids showed no clear benefit in preventing the recurrence.
*   **Check your auto-injector:** For adults over 50 kg, the standard 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector is likely underdosing; ensure you are using 0.5 mg doses in critical settings.
*   **Question the steroids:** Current systematic reviews demonstrate no proven benefit for corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, and potential for increased hospital stay or hypokalemia/hyperglycemia.
*   **Monitor the vulnerable:** Multiphasic anaphylaxis, while rare, presents with significant risk factors like elevated baseline tryptase. If mastocytosis is suspected, a more aggressive osmotic might be justified, but steroids should not be the only variable adjusted.
*   **Need new data:** The lack of response-based guidelines for multiphasic cases means it is time for experts to develop consensus recommendations or for a dedicated registry to capture outcomes.



# Tryptase Timing: Crucial Lessons from Missed Peaks

Anaphylaxis is the big boss of allergic reactions, but we often treat the acute management with the care it demands and leave the diagnostic follow-up to happenstance. This is particularly true when things don't resolve with one shot of epinephrine. When a patient presents not just once, but multiphasically, you are looking at the worst-case scenario in allergy work. You want to catch the bug in the system. But how do you diagnose the underlying etiology if the timing of your blood work is off by a few hours?

In a recent case report examining multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting, we see a textbook example of clinical brilliance during stabilization overshadowed by a significant diagnostic oversight regarding serum tryptase timing. The patient, a woman in her 60s, presented to the ED with profound anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin. It was a high-stakes presentation involving hypotension, hypoxemia, and widespread urticaria. Her team responded appropriately with auto-injector epinephrine, IV fluids, and antihistamines, achieving initial stability. However, she was admitted to the ICU for what turned out to be four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours, each requiring further dosing of epinephrine. While the clinical team managed the hemodynamic collapse effectively, the laboratory workflow failed to capture the crucial temporal markers of mast cell mediator release needed to investigate underlying pathology.

The story illustrates a critical tension between the urgency of resuscitation and the precision of diagnostic sampling. In anaphylaxis management, we often prioritize airway and breathing, but studies suggest that the diagnostic utility of serum tryptase—one of the few objective markers for mast cell activation—is entirely dependent on a strict temporal window. Tryptase is released from mast cells rapidly and peaks usually one to two hours after symptom onset, returning toward baseline within 24 hours. To diagnose mastocytosis, a condition often associated with refractory or biphasic reactions, clinicians need two distinct readings. You need a baseline sample taken at least 24 hours after resolution, and you need an acute peak sample taken during the hyperacute phase of the reaction.

In this specific case, the laboratory team missed the boat on both fronts. The first tryptase sample was not drawn until one hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode. By that time, the initial surge had likely stabilized sufficiently to miss the full magnitude of the peak release. The second sample was even more problematic, being drawn 27 hours after the last recurrence of symptoms. While this captured a potential baseline, the lack of an acute measuring during the optimal peak window made establishing a true delta impossible. The authors note that while baseline tryptase was elevated and remained so during follow-up, the inability to measure the peak during the acute episodes left significant ambiguity. The patient was subsequently diagnosed with a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, with elevated tryptase levels and supporting physical findings like hepatomegaly, but the diagnostic criteria could not be fully met due to the specific sampling gaps that occurred during the initial 24-hour admission.

This case serves as a stark reminder that in the aftermath of severe anaphylaxis, the "stable" patient still requires precise triage in the form of blood collection. The clinical team is undoubtedly trained to rush in, tap an arm, and crank the med cart, but the phlebotomy bag often gets overlooked by withdrawal from the simulation. The guidelines are clear: take a sample immediately on presentation without delaying resuscitation, take a second sample within one to two hours to catch the peak, and take a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to rule out inflammation. In reality, adhering to these three samples is often logistically challenging. Yet, missing all the peak data, as happened in this case, hinders the ability to stratify risk for future episodes or identify a predisposition like systemic mastocytosis that might make the same patient more vulnerable to the slightest exposure to a trigger in the future.

The broader implication of this case extends beyond the tryptase itself, touching on the complexity of managing multiphasic reactions where standard guidance—such as the role of corticosteroids or the duration of observation—is murky. Tryptase data bridges the gap between standard allergy and visceral pathology. Without that data, clinicians are left treating the crisis and hoping the next one doesn't come along so destructive, rather than identifying a root cause that could allow for targeted prophylaxis. Understanding the kinetics of the lab test is just as important as understanding the physiology of the reaction.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Timing is everything for mastocytosis workup:** Serum tryptase levels must be captured at three specific stages to be clinically useful: immediately upon presentation (without delaying treatment), 1–2 hours post-onset for peak levels, and a minimum of 24 hours post-resolution for true baseline levels.
*   **Do not delay resuscitation for phlebotomy:** The initial draw should happen prior to therapy; however, if deferrable, do not delay the epinephrine injection in favor of drawing blood. If the patient stabilizes, the acute phase is temporary.
*   **Missed peaks obscure diagnoses:** In cases of recurrence or multiphasic anaphylaxis, missing the peak trying level window can prevent the identification of underlying systemic mastocytosis, a dangerous condition that predisposes patients to severe reactions.
*   **Elevated baseline matters:** Even if the spike was missed, an elevated baseline tryptase during follow-up warrants further investigation for mast cell disorders, particularly in patients with complex or recurrent anaphylactic history.



## Hidden in Plain Sight: Recognizing Systemic Mastocytosis in Recurrent Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis that resolves, only to crash your patient again hours later, is a nightmare scenario. Imagine treating a patient for anaphylactic shock, watching the blood pressure come back up, clearing the monitor for a few hours, and then having to do it all again three times in a row within 12 hours. It’s a clinical and logistical whiplash that leaves most of us scrambling. But while we are often focused on the "multiphasic" label, sometimes there is a bigger issue lurking underneath the mask of a typical allergy reaction. This case report serves as a stark reminder that when anaphylaxis refuses to behave, we need to look beneath the skin for a diagnosis of Systemic Mastocytosis (SM).

In this case, we are presented with a 60-year-old woman, weighing in at 104 kg, who presented to the emergency department with an apparent drug allergy to clarithromycin. Her history was relevant in its own right: a prior mild skin reaction to amoxicillin and a significant local reaction to a wasp sting without systemic signs. Despite her best efforts, therapy failed to keep the reaction at bay. She experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes from the initial presentation through her ICU stay. Each relapse required aggressive treatment with intramuscular epinephrine and the symptoms, while severe at first with cyanosis and hypotension, reportedly diminished in intensity with subsequent relapses.

The clinical management highlights the friction between theory and the life-and-death reality. The team treated with corticosteroids immediately upon the first recurrence, a practice rooted in tradition rather than modern evidence, which increasingly questions the utility of steroids in preventing biphasic reactions. Furthermore, the medication logistics reveal a critical safety failure often overlooked in high-acuity settings. The patient received two doses of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector before being switched to 0.5 mg individual doses. For a 104 kg patient, the 0.3 mg dose is significantly under the recommended 0.01 mg/kg (maxing at 0.5 mg). In a high-risk case like recurrent anaphylaxis, underdosing can mean the difference between resolving the reaction or watching the patient decompensate while waiting for the next dose to arrive.

While the acute management was reactive and frustrating, the long-term resolution points us toward the underlying etiology. The authors in the case report identified a high likelihood of underlying systemic mastocytosis. The prompt asks us to recognize these hidden clues. Standard workups for anaphylaxis often treat it as a singular event with a discrete trigger. But when the reaction smashes through the door, the phenomenology of mastocytosis starts to bleed into the background. In this specific patient, we see the classic triad of clinical red flags that should trigger an allergy-refractory workup: hepatomegaly identified on ultrasound, a history of neuropsychiatric symptoms including depression and memory problems, and a tryptase analysis that defies the natural course of an allergy reaction.

It is also instructive to note the timing of the biomarkers. The report admits that tryptase levels were not drawn during the initial onset, violating the guideline recommendation of drawing samples immediately, at the peak (1-2 hours), and upon resolution. Despite this deviation from the gold standard, the baseline and subsequent tryptase levels remained elevated, providing the data point necessary to raise the suspicion of a mast cell disorder. This underscores that while we should strive for perfect protocol, in the real world where we must act first and test later, the persistence of elevation is a vital clue when the clinical picture doesn't fit a standard IgE-dependent model.

Systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe, multiphasic, and sometimes refractory anaphylactic courses. In this patient, the reaction to clarithromycin was severe, but the persistence of symptoms over days and the residual mediator markers suggest a pre-existing mast cell burden. The intensity of symptoms waning over time might reflect the depletion of a finite media store in a massive mast cell burden or simply a titration of the systemic load, but either way, it demands a different diagnostic approach than a standard drug allergy.



# Don't Just Wait: Continuous Infusion vs. Expectant Observation in Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a terrible storm to weather. Typically, once the drip of epinephrine twitches off the monitor and the blood pressure climbs, the work feels done. But what do you do when the storm doesn’t just pass; it returns? While biphasic anaphylaxis is a known entity, the multiphasic course—multiple relapses in the absence of re-exposure—is a rare beast that terrifies both patients and intensivists. I’m digging into a recent case report by Schneider et al., which beautifully dissects this nightmare scenario. The patient was an older woman who was supposed to be safe after the first EPINEPHrine dose, only to find herself rebounding into shock for a fourth time. It’s a vivid reminder that while guidelines offer answers for single-episode reactions, the ICU floor often sits in a testing ground of unvalidated strategies.

## The Clinical Vignette

Meet a woman in her 60s with a heavy medication load and a history of beta-lactam sensitivity. She took clarithromycin for erysipelas and didn’t think twice, anything else she would have. One hour after that dose, the anaphylaxis struck. She came into the ED hypotensive, hypoxemic, and visibly raving with flushing and generalized erythema. The ED team acted quickly with epinephrine and fluids, and the initial picture looked like the textbook presentation of a controlled shock. But once she moved into the ward, the real test began. Despite the initial "good response," the patient developed recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission. She required epinephrine for the ED event and subsequently a second, third, and fourth time in varying dosages while in the hospital. When the initial shock resolves, you diagnose uncontrolled mast cell degranulation. However, when the patient does not adapt to progressively smaller intensities of anaphylactic shock, it raises the question of whether the mast cells are truly resetting or if a different pathology is at play.

In this specific scenario, the patient faced three distinct events in the ED, requiring a cumulative dose of epinephrine during the acute phase, and a fourth while hospitalized. The first three doses were standard 0.3 mg auto-injectors, but the fourth effective dose used a prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular administration. This trend highlights that since the initial epinephrine dose was relatively lower, the escalation of care is critical. After each recurrence, the severity lessened, ranging from deep hypoxaemia with cyanosis to dyspnoea and pruritus during the final episode. So, when the medical team couldn't find a "smoking gun" allergen and the patient stopped reacting, what was the next move?

## The Therapeutic Dilemma

The registration of the patient for intensive care was a direct result of her recurrent instability. Here is the crux of the dilemma: The ICU team had to choose between two very different management philosophies. The first option was continuous administration of epinephrine via an intravenous pump. This approach would have pre-emptively treated anaphylaxis, aiming to stabilize mast cells and prevent future degranulation-disastrous consequences. The second approach was an expectant observation strategy. Given the rapidly decreasing intensity of the symptoms with each subsequent episode, the team hypothesised that the system was contrail and would resolve spontaneously once the mast cells quieted down.

In the absence of consistent literature, both approaches came with serious risks. Continuous epinephrine infusion carries the weight of pharmacokinetic unknowns, potential hypotension upon sudden cessation, and the potential for adrenergic complications like arrhythmias or stress to the hypertensive tissues. Expectant management carries the risk of a sudden, unexpected, catastrophic relapse that could have occurred between pulse checks. The team noted that a definitive rule for this was lacking, yet they had to manage a patient who was essentially unpredictable. Fortunately, neither aggressive infusion nor constant adrenaline was deemed necessary as the patient did not show further symptom relapse after approximately 24 hours without a recurrence.

## Unmasking the Root Cause

While the management of multiphasic anaphylaxis remains frustratingly vague, understanding the underlying cause can change the game in the long run. For this patient, further analysis suggested a high likelihood of underlying systemic mastocytosis. Even though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria due to limited bone marrow biopsy material, the clinical picture was screaming this possibility. Tryptase levels were elevated during the third episode and remained elevated through follow-up, which is a stark indicator of mastocytic burden. In the past, some look to use baseline tryptase levels early in the workup, yet guidelines recommend specific sampling intervals including a peak sample at 1-2 hours and a baseline 24 hours later. In this case, the first tryptase sample was taken 1 hour after the third episode and the second 27 hours after the last recurrence. This lag meant the data did not strictly follow the standard protocol. The presence of hepatomegaly on sonography, combined with potential neuropsychiatric symptoms like depression and memory issues, supports a systemic inflammatory profile that doesn't just present as a simple allergy.

Furthermore, consider the medication usage during these acute events. Standard auto-injectors often contain 0.3 mg of epinephrine; in patients weighing over 50 kg, this is historically considered underdosing compared to the recommended regimen of 0.01 mg/kg. In a multiphasic course, where you are fighting to stay stable, that underdosing can translate to inadequate stabilization. A recent retrospective study cited in the discussion suggests that patients who initially received 0.3 mg require escalation significantly more often than those who start with a standard 0.5 mg dose. While the use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors is a barrier that often becomes a precedent in standard practice, in the ICU setting, the immediate availability of intravenous prepared doses of 0.5 mg might be necessary to avoid skeletal muscle metabolic fatigue and ensure rapid hemodynamic stability.

## Take-Home Messages

*   Multiphasic anaphylaxis presents a unique challenge with no established consensus for continuous epinephrine infusion versus expectant observation.
*   Clinical easing should be monitored carefully; escalation of care protocols should adapt to symptom intensity and patient response.
*   Suspect systemic mastocytosis in patients with recurrent reactions, elevated tryptase levels, and neuropsychiatric symptoms.
*   Ensure tryptase sampling adheres to guidelines (peak and baseline) to accurately diagnose underlying mastocytic disorders.
*   Recognize that standard 0.3 mg auto-injectors may be underdosed for adults weighing more than 50 kg, potentially leading to repeat administration.



# Four Hours Isn’t Enough: Re-thinking Observation Durations for Biphasic Reactions

Anaphylaxis management textbooks have a sacred section: the protocol for biphasic relaxation. The standard dogma is concise and reassuring: treat, stabilize, observe for four to six hours, and discharge if asymptomatic. We teach this mantra to residents, stick it on the hospital doors, and rely on it for resource allocation. But what happens when the “relaxation” is a lie? What if your patient stabilizes miraculously for three hours only for the ICU tremors to start again?

This is the world of the 64-year-old woman presented in a recent case report on multiphasic anaphylaxis. Her story is a stark reminder that our observation windows might be woefully short for the subset of patients who don't fit the standard curve of resolution. While the typical incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis hovers around 4-6%, the trajectory of this specific patient was terrifyingly non-linear. She didn't just relapse once; she suffered four distinct episodes of shock within the first 12 hours of admission despite receiving appropriate care and epinephrine with each recurrence.

## Clinical Profile of a Multiphasic Case

The patient was a woman in her 60s with significant comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes and hypertension, presenting with anaphylaxis following a single dose of clarithromycin. Her initial presentation was classic and severe: hypotension, hypoxia, generalized erythema, and agitation. After an initial response to epinephrine and supportive care, she was transferred to the acute ward. Standard logic would suggest that if she had responded to the initial dose, her hospital course could have been tapered.

However, the patient manifested recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock. Treatment was escalated to include continuous monitoring in the ICU after the first relapse. She required a total of four epinephrine doses during her admission to manage these showers of symptoms. Notably, the intensity of the episodes actually lessened over time, shifting from deep hypoxia and cyanosis on the first return to simple dyspnea and pruritus on the last. This "waxing and waning" pattern is characteristic of multiphasic courses but remains incredibly difficult to predict before the second crisis strikes.

## The 78-Hour Window

The crux of the clinical dilemma lies in the definition of biphasic versus multiphasic anaphylaxis, which currently lacks consensus. Most guidelines are anchored to a 6-hour post-resolution window, with some extending to 12 hours. However, the background data of our case highlights that symptom recurrence is documented up to 78 hours after the initial allergen exposure.

The strategy adopted in this case was pragmatic but fraught with difficulty because it was based on observation without specific biological data to time it. When the patient appeared stable for approximately 24-33 hours after the last recurrence (totaling roughly 3 days of care before discharge), no further episodes occurred. This 24-hour window without relapse was deemed safe for discharge. The question remains: for a standard patient, is 6 hours sufficient? Or does a titre-based clinical picture—like the hepatomegaly and high tryptase seen in this patient—mandate a longer observational horizon?

## Pathology and Pharmacology Nuances

Beyond the observation clock, there were two critical pharmacological and pathological findings in this case that influence future emergency management. First, the patient had an elevated baseline tryptase level and repeated elevations following each anaphylactic episode, suggesting a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria. Conditions like this predispose patients to mast cell mediator release that is less responsive to standard triggers and more prone to recurrence.

Second, there is the issue of dosing resilience. The patient initially received epinephrine via auto-injectors containing 0.3 mg. While often preferred for time sensitivity or protocol familiarity, this dose is lower than the recommended 0.5 mg max dose for adults. Given her weight of 104 kg, this patient was effectively double the recommended threshold for severe anaphylaxis to rely on standard auto-injectors alone without a backup backup dose. She relapsed despite this escalation, eventually requiring manually prepared 0.5 mg doses in the hospital setting. Additionally, corticosteroids were administered with some controversy in the unit; while often used to prevent relapse, modern evidence increasingly questions their utility, yet in this unstable patient, they were part of the ICU protocol until discharge.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Standard 4-6 hour observation may be unsafe for high-risk features:** Cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis can recur up to 78 hours post-exposure, challenging traditional discharge windows.
*   **Trialling for Mastocytosis:** Elevated tryptase levels and persistent symptoms despite treatment should prompt a search for mast cell disorders (e.g., systemic mastocytosis).
*   **Epinephrine Dosing:** Auto-injectors (0.3mg) may be underdosed for adults >50kg (or >30kg for some weight-based calculations); ensure backup vials (0.5mg) are immediately available in the ED.
*   **Corticosteroids remain controversial:** While used for relapse prevention, evidence does not consistently support their efficacy in preventing biphasic reactions.
*   **Risk Stratification is currently lacking:** There are no validated reliable predictors for biphasic anaphylaxis; clinical judgment must weigh severity of initial presentation and response to epinephrine against resource availability.

## Bottom Line

Anaphylaxis in the absence of a clear trigger often assumes a benign post-resolution phase. However, the data presented suggests that for select patients—particularly those with features suggestive of mast cell disorders—the window of vulnerability extends far beyond the standard ICU discharge threshold. Until further research solidifies risk stratification, a conservative approach to monitoring remains the safest path.
