Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Initial Presentation and Emergency Stabilization

The patient’s arrival at the emergency department marked the critical initial phase of management for suspected anaphylaxis, necessitating immediate diagnostic recognition and therapeutic intervention. Upon presentation, the clinical picture was defined by the rapid aggregation of symptoms across multiple organ systems, which is the hallmark of a systemic allergic reaction. The patient exhibited cutaneous manifestations including pruritus, flushing, and generalized urticaria, alongside cardiovascular collapse characterized by hypotension, with a blood pressure reading of 74/50 mm Hg. Her respiratory status was compromised by hypoxia, with peripheral oxygen saturation recorded at 88% on room air, and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute consistent with compensatory tachypnea. Additionally, the patient displayed signs of hemodynamic distress through increased transpiration and agitation. While urticaria alone does not diagnose shock, the combination of cutaneous signs with hypotension and hypoxaemia fulfills the diagnostic criteria for anaphylactic shock. The temporal relationship between these findings and the intake of oral clarithromycin one hour prior to arrival provided strong evidence for a drug-induced etiology, prompting the activation of acute resuscitation protocols despite the patient's lack of prior history with anaphylactic shock.

Immediate stabilization protocols were enacted to reverse the life-threatening features identified during the initial assessment. The cornerstone of therapy, intramuscular epinephrine, was administered via an auto-injector containing 0.3 mg, which is often the standard dose for preclinical settings and specific hospital protocols, though guidelines generally recommend 0.5 mg for adults weighing over 50 kg. The choice of an auto-injector was prioritized to minimize treatment delays in time-sensitive situations where personnel may be unfamiliar with the handling of vasoactive agents. To manage the hypoxia and respiratory distress, supplemental oxygen was delivered at 15 liters via a non-rebreather mask, a high-flow source required to maintain adequate saturation during the vasodilation and increased demand of the acute reaction. Concurrently, volume resuscitation was initiated with 500 mL of normal saline intravenously to counteract hypotension caused by increased vascular permeability and third-spacing of fluid. Adjunctive pharmacotherapy included the administration of 2 mg of clemastine intravenously to target the histamine-mediated cutaneous symptoms. Early administration of epinephrine is emphasized in medical literature as vital to prevent the rapid progression of shock; in this instance, the rapid recognition and prompt injection allowed for the reversal of the primary hemodynamic decline. This immediate success with epinephrine supports the clinical principle that circulatory collapse in anaphylaxis requires immediate vasoconstrictive support to restore blood pressure and perfusion to critical organs.

Following the initial intervention, the assessment of the patient’s response indicated a positive acute clinical reaction to the therapy. The blood pressure normalized, and facial swelling along with erythema decreased significantly, allowing for a transition from the emergency stabilization phase to ongoing acute ward monitoring. This initial response demonstrates the efficacy of standard anaphylaxis management, yet it also highlighted the necessity for continued observation, as patients with severe shock-like presentations are at risk for recurrence or biphasic reactions. Consequently, the patient was admitted to the acute internal ward for further monitoring without telemetry control initially. The decision to admit, rather than discharge, was driven by the severity of the hemodynamic instability observed at arrival, which serves as a risk factor for relapse. This stabilization phase underscored the importance of early epinephrine use relative to the onset of symptoms, ensuring that the initial responders could mitigate the immediate threat to life while preparing for the possibility of subsequent clinical deterioration that characterized her prolonged hospital course.

## Clinical Phenomenon: The Multiphasic Anaphylactic Course

To understand the trajectory of this patient, one must first distinguish multiphasic anaphylaxis from standardized definitions of allergic reactions. While biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as a recurrence of symptoms at least seven hours after an initial response, typically resolving within 24 hours, this case represents a multiphasic course characterized by multiple relapses. This distinction is critical, as while biphasic reactions occur in an estimated 4% to 6% of anaphylactic events, multiphasic presentations are relatively rare and complicate management due to the unpredictability of subsequent episodes. In the background analysis, the definition requires an asymptomatic phase between the reaggravation and the initial reaction, which is a key feature present in this case where the patient experienced symptom-free intervals between four distinct episodes requiring intervention.

Differentiating multiphasic anaphylaxis from other diagnostic possibilities presents a specific clinical challenge. Clinicians must differentiate these true recurrences from refractory anaphylaxis, where symptoms persist despite epinephrine, or ongoing allergen absorption from residual exposure. In this case, clarithromycin was administered as a single initial dose and discontinued immediately, ruling out prolonged absorption from continued medication intake. Furthermore, the clinical picture did not match a refractory course, as the patient demonstrated a clear, albeit transient, physiological response to epinephrine that temporarily restored stability before the next recurrence. This differentiation is vital to ensure that clinicians recognize the anaphylaxis is not responsive enough to stop treatment, but rather that the patient is proned to recurrent mast cell mediator release independent of ongoing exposure.

The management of this specific multiphasic presentation involved significant decision-making regarding the patient's location and monitoring intensity. Following the first recurrence, despite the initial response to emergency care at the emergency department, the decision was made to transfer the patient to the intensive care unit (ICU). This escalation was necessitated by the risk of shock recurrence and the need for continuous support, even as the medical team debated whether to employ continuous intravenous epinephrine or adopt an expectant approach. A notable clinical phenomenon observed in this specific case was the trajectory of symptom intensity over time. While the initial presentation involved hypotension, hypoxia, and severe dyspnea, subsequent recurrences showed lessening severity, ranging eventually to only rash and pruritus, suggesting a potential stabilization of the underlying mast cell activation despite the ongoing clinical risk.

Finally, the management of this case highlights the significant challenges in risk stratifying patients for prolonged observation. Current guidelines acknowledge that reliable predictors for biphasic or multiphasic recurrence do not yet exist, though suggested risk factors include severe initial presentation, delayed epinephrine administration, and multiple doses required. In this instance, the lack of defined observation protocols forced clinicians to rely on expert consensus, such as the hospital protocol transfer to the ICU, rather than evidence-based thresholds. While symptoms ultimately resolved and the patient was discharged, the absence of a clear predictive algorithm complicates resource allocation and safety. Consequently, clinicians must remain prepared to manage unexpected recurrences and recognize that symptom resolution may not equate to complete remission of the underlying anaphylactic drive in multiphasic courses.

## Diagnostic Evaluation and Suspected Systemic Mastocytosis

In cases of severe or multiphasic anaphylaxis, standard allergen avoidance and monitoring are complicated by the possibility of underlying mast cell neoplasms or activation disorders. This patient’s presentation of four distinct anaphylactic episodes despite adequate response to initial epinephrine prompted an investigation into systemic mastocytosis (SM). While SM is rare, it predisposes patients to dangerous mediator release because basal mast cell numbers are significantly elevated, and mast cells may be hypersensitive to triggers. When anaphylaxis is recurrent or refractory to standard therapy, clinicians must consider whether a disease process rather than a simple acute drug reaction is driving the instability. The consultation with allergology in this case was necessary to distinguish between true biphasic relapse and a systemic disorder affecting mast cell kinetics, recognizing that standard allergic workup might miss an underlying malignancy if only the immediate trigger is addressed.

The diagnostic cornerstone for suspected mast cell disorders is the assessment of serum tryptase, a highly specific enzyme released during mast cell degranulation. Pathophysiologically, an elevated baseline or acute tryptase level correlates with mast cell burden and the likelihood of recurrent release. Current guidelines recommend a specific sampling strategy: an initial sample during the acute phase (before treatment), followed by a sample 1 to 4 hours later when levels typically peak, and a final baseline sample taken at least 24 hours after clinical resolution to ensure true stabilization. In this case, the initial workup failed to adhere to these standards; no tryptase was drawn during the first two episodes, and the subsequent sampling occurred one hour after the third episode and 27 hours after resolution. Despite this lack of optimal timing, the subsequent samples demonstrated elevated tryptase levels that remained so during follow-up monitoring. This persistent elevation was clinically significant, as true baseline elevation suggests a high population of autonomous mast cells rather than transient degranulation alone, supporting the suspicion of systemic pathology.

Beyond laboratory confirmation, clinicians must evaluate ancillary clinical findings that align with systemic mastocytosis. These disorders often present with organ involvement and nonspecific systemic signs. In this patient, abdominal sonography revealed hepatomegaly, a known finding in SM caused by liver infiltration. Furthermore, neuropsychiatric symptoms are frequently associated with mastocytosis due to the involvement of enteric nerves or neuro-leukin release; this patient had a history of depression and memory problems, which were noted as relevant findings even if her depression was a pre-existing comorbidity. Identifying these signs helps stratify the risk of life-threatening anaphylaxis and prompts a lower threshold for genetic or histological testing. However, the utility of imaging and history is limited by the rarity of the condition and the overlap of symptoms with common medical issues, necessitating confirmation through diagnostic criteria.

Formal diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis relies on the World Health Organization (WHO) classification criteria, which generally require the presence of major criteria (such as bone marrow mast cells) or a combination of minor criteria (including plasma tryptase elevation, multidirectional cytogenetics, or specific PDGFRA mutations). The patient in this case presented with a high likelihood of SM but did not meet the requirements for a formal diagnosis. Specifically, she fulfilled only two WHO minor criteria, and the major criteria could not be assessed because the bone marrow biopsy yielded insufficient material. This limitation highlights a practical reality in emergency medicine: while bone marrow is the gold standard for diagnosis, it is often invasive and technically limited in urgent or acute settings. Consequently, a "high probability" diagnosis without biopsy confirmation leaves significant clinical ambiguity, requiring careful long-term management rather than a binary cured or uncured status.

Once the suspicion is raised, the implications for future anaphylaxis management are profound, even if the biopsy remains inconclusive. An elevated baseline tryptase indicates a higher mast cell burden, which may translate to a heightened risk of future episodes or more severe reactions upon new exposure. For this patient, the trend toward a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis reinforced the need for strict trigger avoidance, education on auto-injector use, and ready access to epinephrine rather than reliance on antihistamines alone as a monotherapy. The patient was subsequently discharged with epinephrine training and antihistamine prophylaxis (levocetirizine), with no further relapses observed. This outcome demonstrates that even in the absence of a confirmed WHO diagnosis, the management principles for suspected systemic mastocytosis—vigilant monitoring, prompt epinephrine administration during relapse, and identification of underlying triggers—align with the safety protocols required for recurrent anaphylaxis.

## Therapeutic Dilemmas and Guideline Discrepancies

The management of the recurrent anaphylactic episodes in this case presented a significant therapeutic dilemma, as the patient experienced successive relapses despite appropriate initial treatment and a standard period of observation. While the initial presentation with hypotension and hypoxia was managed with standard resuscitative measures including oxygen and normal saline fluid boluses, the transition into the intensive care unit for a multiphasic course required more nuanced decision-making absent of specific consensus guidelines. Fundamentally, the case highlights the clinical tension between maintaining standard pharmacological protocols for single-episode anaphylaxis and adapting to the unpredictable trajectory of a multiphasic reaction.

A critical point of contention in this case involved epinephrine dosing during the relapse phase. The patient, weighing 104 kg, initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector. Although her initial stabilization was sufficient, subsequent relapses were treated with individually prepared doses of 0.5 mg to address potential underdosing. Current resuscitation guidelines typically recommend a dose of 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults, acknowledging that 0.3 mg auto-injectors, often standard in preclinical settings, may constitute an underdose in larger adults. The use of 0.3 mg devices has been critically examined in recent literature presented in the case report, where a 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine needed escalation of care, such as additional injections, significantly more often than those treated with the full 0.5 mg dose. In this scenario, the preference for auto-injectors during the initial ED stabilization was justified by speed and minimizing delay in time-sensitive settings, yet the subsequent switch to IV-prepared doses acknowledges their limitation in delivering the guideline-recommended threshold for a patient of this size. This discrepancy illustrates the practical friction between guideline-adherent dosing and logistical constraints within emergency protocols.

The administration of corticosteroids during the relapse phase further exemplifies the gap between established practice and current evidence. Clinicians supplemented the epinephrine and antihistamine therapy with intravenous prednisone and dexamethasone. Historically, corticosteroids were utilized pre-emptively to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions, particularly in hospital settings where observation periods extend beyond the initial 4 to 6 hours. However, the analysis of this case critiques this practice, noting that a 2017 systematic review found no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Conversely, contemporary data emphasizes potential risks, with one 2019 study showing an increased likelihood of ICU/hospital ward admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Despite these concerns, their use persisted in this case per institutional protocols for both general ward and intensive care settings, highlighting the difficulty in abandoning established practices even when evidence trends toward obsolescence.

Regarding the specific management strategy for the relapse phase, the consultation with the allergology department revealed a difficult choice between pre-emptive stabilization and conservative monitoring. Clinicians considered continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to treat the patient proactively against further relapsing episodes. The alternative was an expectant management approach, predicated on the specific clinical observation that the intensity of symptoms lessened with each successive attack. These risk assessments require balancing the high stakes of untreated anaphylaxis against the risks of medication toxicity or cardiovascular instability from continuous infusion. The decision to transition back to a standard ward after 24 hours without recurrence supports the utility of clinical tracking, yet the lack of data on optimal infusion titration versus conservative monitoring leaves such decisions largely expert-dependent.

Fluid resuscitation was integral to managing the hypotensive episodes described, with 500 mL normal saline administered initially and during the second shock episode. While fluid management in anaphylactic shock is generally standard to maintain perfusion, the case underscores the need for careful titration in patients with comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension, where fluid overload could complicate the clinical picture. The broader challenge highlighted by this multiphasic presentation remains the lack of evidence-based literature defining optimal management for multi-relapse scenarios. Unlike standard anaphylaxis, where algorithms are robust, the multiphasic course lacks reliable predictors for relapse timing or severity. Consequently, reliance on expert consensus is necessary, but the absence of a registry for such cases impedes the development of data-driven recommendations. This case ultimately serves as an example of the clinical reality where treatment decisions are often made in the empiric void, necessitating a vigilance that goes beyond established guidelines without compromising patient safety.

## Long-term Management, Patient Education, and Clinical Significance

Following the resolution of a multiphasic anaphylactic course, discharge planning must provide a safety net that extends beyond hospital confinement to address potential re-exposure and management at home. In this specific case, the patient was discharged after a three-day admission, standard of care involving the prescription of a daily antihistamine, levocetirizine, to proactively manage cutaneous symptoms, and the obligation of a patient-owned epinephrine auto-injector. Patient education was a pivotal component of the post-acute phase, focusing on the recognition of differing diagnostic gradients between mild allergic reactions and anaphylactic shock. The patient was instructed to monitor for early warning signs such as pruritus, flushing, and throat swelling, establishing a clear protocol for when to utilize the auto-injector to prevent progression to hypotension or respiratory compromise. This education also highlights a practical nuance found in standard administration: while clinical guidelines often recommend an adult dose of 0.5 mg, the patient was fitted with a 0.3 mg auto-injector, a discrepancy that underscores the necessity of counseling patients regarding dosage adequacy, particularly in adults over 50 kg where auto-injectors may subtherapeutically dose the patient.

Long-term management also necessitates a vigilant look toward underlying systemic etiologies, particularly when clinical presentation suggests mastocytosis. Subsequent analysis of this patient revealed elevated baseline and post-event tryptase levels, combined with physical findings such as hepatomegaly and possible neuropsychiatric symptoms like depression and memory problems. Although these findings did not meet the formal diagnostic criteria of the World Health Organization due to unavailable major criteria, the profile indicated a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. Clinicians should recognize that this underlying condition predisposes the patient to excessive mast cell mediator release, increasing the likelihood of severe or multiphasic reactions in the future. Therefore, long-term follow-up should include monitoring for systemic progression and mast cell burden, rather than treating the anaphylaxis as an isolated event, as undiagnosed systemic disease significantly alters the risk stratification and prognosis for subsequent exposures.

Finally, the complexity of managing multiphasic anaphylaxis illustrates the critical need for optimized resource allocation and specialized data collection methods. The hospital management of this patient involved balancing the availability of intensive care resources against the imperative of patient safety, debating between expectant approaches versus pre-emptive intravenous epinephrine administration. This lack of a definitive protocol highlights that current observation windows, often ranging from four to six hours, may be insufficient for multiphasic cases, where recurrence has been shown to occur up to 78 hours later. To address this gap, there is a strong advocacy for the establishment of specialized allergy registries. By aggregating data from complex cases similar to this one, the medical community can move beyond expert consensus toward evidence-based recommendations regarding monitoring duration, advanced therapies, and observational safety, ultimately refining treatment protocols for patients presenting with recurrent and refractory anaphylactic reactions.
