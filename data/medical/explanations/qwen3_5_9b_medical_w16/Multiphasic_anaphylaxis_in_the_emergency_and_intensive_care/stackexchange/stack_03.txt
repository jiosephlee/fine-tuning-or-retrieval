Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] How does systemic mastocytosis predispose a patient to a multiphasic or severe clinical course of anaphylaxis compared to typical allergic reactions?

This case illustrates why an undiagnosed underlying mast cell disorder can transform a standard allergic reaction into a protracted, life-threatening cascade. While typical IgE-mediated anaphylaxis is driven by acute, localized mast cell degranulation, systemic mastocytosis (SM) involves a neoplastic proliferation of mast cells infiltrating tissues. As noted in the report, this pathophysiology results in an "abnormal increase in mast cells" and a kinetic environment of "excessive mediator release."

In this patient, the presence of SM predisposed her to a multiphasic course through a high systemic reservoir of activated mast cells. Unlike typical reactions where mediators are cleared upon hemodynamic stabilization with epinephrine, the extensive mast cell burden associated with SM creates a sustained inflammatory trigger. This is evidenced by the patient’s recurrent episodes requiring multiple doses of epinephrine within 12 hours, despite initial clinical resolution. Furthermore, the analysis revealing elevated and persistent baseline tryptase levels, along with physical findings of hepatomegaly, supports the diagnosis of a mast cell storm rather than simple isolated allergy.

Clinically, this teaches us to consider SM in the differential for severe, recurrent anaphylaxis that does not resolve predictably with standard first-line therapies. Recognizing these features—persistent mediator markers like tryptase and signs of multiorgan involvement—allows for more aggressive observation and early investigation of mast cell disorders, as the sheer volume of cells driving the reaction mimics a refractory or biphasic pattern distinct from typical allergic hypersensitivity.
