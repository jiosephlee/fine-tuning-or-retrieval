Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] What long-term management and discharge criteria should be established for a patient with a confirmed or suspected underpinning diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis?

### Clinical Teaching Point: Management of Suspected Systemic Mastocytosis with Anaphylaxis

In the setting of anaphylaxis where systemic mastocytosis (SM) is suspected, particularly with a multiphasic course, management must account for the high risk of recurrent mediator release and the potential for prolonged hospitalization. Based on the case presented, three key principles guide the discharge and long-term care of these patients.

**Discharge Criteria**
Given the risk of biphasic recurrence, which can occur up to 78 hours post-exposure though most frequently within 24 hours, immediate discharge upon initial resolution is unsafe. In the specific context of this case, the patient required approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylactic symptoms after stabilization before transfer to a standard care ward. Final discharge occurred after 3 days of admission. Clinicians must consider a minimum 24-hour observation period post-symptom resolution to ensure the diphasic window has passed, particularly in patients presenting with multiple relapses, as seen in this multiphasic presentation.

**Long-term Management**
Once the acute instability resolves, management focuses on baseline symptom control and emergency preparedness. This patient was started on daily levocetirizine, an H1-antihistamine used for prophylaxis and cutaneous symptom control. Furthermore, emergency self-care is paramount; the patient was educated and equipped with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) for immediate administration in future reactions. While formal diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis were not fully met in this instance due to biopsy limitations, the clinical suspicion remained high. Long-term follow-up with an allergologist is essential to monitor tryptase levels, which in this case remained elevated during follow-up and served as a surrogate marker for mast cell burden and disease activity.

**Diagnostic Vigilance**
Finally, suspected SM requires rigorous workup to distinguish between true SM and other causes of chronic mast cell activation. Although the case report noted a failure to adhere to ideal tryptase sampling intervals during the initial workup—specifically a baseline sample at full clinical resolution—delayed tryptase evaluation was noted to have elevated levels. Clinicians should prioritize obtaining a baseline tryptase at least 24 hours after full symptom resolution to assess for SM. Persistent elevation, combined with a history of multidomin, such as hepatomegaly or neuropsychiatric symptoms, should prompt sustained anticoagulation or mucosal studies appropriate for formal SM diagnosis. Ultimately, strict allergen avoidance (such as the discontinuation of clarithromycin) and vigilant monitoring for mediator release symptoms remain the cornerstones of care for patients with high pretest probability of mastocytosis.
