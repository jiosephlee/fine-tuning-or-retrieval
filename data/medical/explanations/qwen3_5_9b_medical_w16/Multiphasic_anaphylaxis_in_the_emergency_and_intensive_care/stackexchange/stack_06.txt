Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Morning Report] In a patient with recurrent anaphylaxis under the supervision of emergency medicine, what are the risk factors necessitating admission to the ICU versus standard ward monitoring?

In managing patients with recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis, the distinction between intensive care unit (ICU) admission and standard ward monitoring remains a pragmatic decision rather than one driven by strictly validated predictive algorithms. As we saw in our patient, there are no universally reliable predictors for biphasic reactions, though literature suggests that risk stratification should lean heavily on the severity of the initial presentation and the response to first-line therapy.

In this case, the decision to admit this patient to the ICU was triggered specifically by the recurrence of anaphylactic shock on the internal ward. Although she initially responded to intramuscular epinephrine and was admitted to the acute ward, she experienced her first relapse of shock within 12 hours. This clinical milestone necessitated the escalation to ICU for closer surveillance and management of hemodynamic instability. The literature referenced in the report notes that suggested risk factors for such complex courses include a severe initial presentation, the need for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of prior biphasic reactions. However, because no definitive screening tool exists, the presence of hemodynamic instability or respiratory compromise requiring vasoactive escalation remains our primary indication for fallhazard ICU admission.

Furthermore, the underlying pathophysiology in this patient—highly suggestive of systemic mastocytosis despite not meeting formal diagnostic criteria—underscores why these patients are refractory to standard care. Patients with mastocytosis or suspected mast cell disorders present with exaggerated mediator release and multiorgan inflammation, predisposing them to multiphasic courses that standard allergic etiology would not predict. The case required intravenous corticosteroids and escalating epinephrine dosages (from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to 0.5 mg individual preparations) due to the weight of the patient (104 kg) and the severity of the relapse.

Therefore, the principle emerging from this case is that admission to a standard unit may be appropriate only after the resolution of the initial shock and the assured asymptomatic phase with no further relapses within the first 24 hours on observation. Conversely, if a patient presents with hypotension, hypoxia, or requires repeated epinephrine doses to regain stability, the threshold for ICU admission is met to ensure immediate availability of continuous airway and circulatory support.
