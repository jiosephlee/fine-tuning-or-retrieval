Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] What pathophysiological mechanisms might explain the observed attenuation of symptom severity in subsequent relapses of multiphasic anaphylaxis?

In this case of multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin, the observed attenuation of symptom severity in subsequent relapses likely reflects a combination of mast cell mediator kinetics and the cumulative hemodynamic stabilization provided by therapeutic interventions.

First, pathophysiologically, the initial episode represents a "perfect storm" of mediator release, depleting the labile stores of histamine, tryptase, and other vasoactive amines from the mast cell granules that were clipped during the first massive degranulation event. While the patient’s elevated baseline and episodic tryptase levels suggest a predisposition to severe release—likely consistent with the high suspicion of systemic mastocytosis noted in the case—the capacity for massive mediator release often diminishes in subsequent waves as granule stores deplete or exocytotic receptors become transiently downregulated.

Second, the patient received cumulative intramuscular epinephrine, with dosing escalating from 0.3 mg (auto-injectors) to 0.5 mg (IV doses) upon admission to the ICU. Epinephrine is the primary mechanism for halting anaphylaxis by reducing vascular permeability and stabilizing membranes; its repeated administration in this case suggests that each subsequent dose effectively countered the residual vasodilatory effects of the prior relapse. The "attenuation" may thus be partly a result of failing the patient's vasoconstrictive reserve rather than a change in the underlying allergen.

Finally, the presence of suspected systemic mastocytosis implies a hyper-proliferative mast cell population. While this predisposes the patient to the initial severe presentation (indicated by the deep shock and hypoxia), the clinical course demonstrates that once systemic vascular tone is re-established—via fluid resuscitation and catecholamines—the relative severity of recurrent mediator release can be lower. This case underscores that while elevated tryptase levels predict a higher risk of anaphylaxis, the trajectory of subsequent relapses is significantly modulated by the adequacy of epinephrine therapy and the physiological exhaustion of the initial mediator wave.

**Clinical Takeaway:** In multiphasic anaphylaxis, clinicians should anticipate that recurrent relapses will often be less hemodynamically compromising than the initial event, particularly following adequate epinephrine stabilization and hydration, despite the underlying threat of mastocytosis. However, vigilance remains mandatory, as the attenuation does not guarantee resolution, underscoring the need for prolonged monitoring.
