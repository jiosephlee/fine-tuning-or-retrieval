### [Clinical Pearls] What is the recommended protocol for serial tryptase level sampling to accurately distinguish an elevated baseline from anaphylactic release?

To accurately distinguish between an acute anaphylactic release of tryptase and a chronically elevated baseline suggestive of systemic mastocytosis or other mast cell disorders, strict adherence to a temporal sampling protocol is required.

As detailed in the report by Schneider et al., guidelines recommend obtaining a minimum of two, and ideally three, serial tryptase samples. The protocol relies on kinetics: tryptase levels rise rapidly with degranulation and peak approximately one to two hours after symptom onset. Therefore, an initial sample should be drawn as soon as feasible without delaying required life-saving treatment. A second sample must be obtained 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset (but not exceeding 4 hours) to capture the peak level.

However, to establish the true baseline, a third sample is critical. This must be drawn a minimum of 24 hours after the patient has achieved full clinical resolution. This interval allows endogenous clearance of the acute tryptase release, rendering persistent elevation a marker of underlying mast cell burden.

In the case presented, the clinical team failed to adhere to these intervals during the initial emergency workup; instead of optimal peak capture, the first tryptase was measured one hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and the second sample was delayed 27 hours after symptom resolution. While this delayed timing was suboptimal for acute kinetics, the subsequent analysis revealed that tryptase levels remained elevated in follow-up. This persistent elevation raised a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. This case underscores that in multiphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, a delayed-resolution tryptase sample is often the only reliable method to identify underlying mast cell pathologies that complicate clinical course and management.

**Teaching Point:** Never interpret a single post-anaphylaxis tryptase result in isolation. Always secure a follow-up sample at the 24-hour mark post-resolution to differentiate acute mediator release from baseline mast cell activation.

### [Management] Based on current evidence, what is the role of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic or recurrent anaphylaxis?

**Corticosteroids Versus Epinephrine in Biphasic Anaphylaxis**

The cornerstone of anaphylaxis management remains the prompt administration of epinephrine and clinical observation; corticosteroids are not indicated for the prevention of biphasic or recurrent reactions.

While steroid administration is historically ingrained in our practice—our patient in this report received both IV prednisone and dexamethasone following the first recurrence of anaphylaxis based on this traditional belief—the evidence does not support this approach. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies examining this specific interaction found that 21 studies demonstrated no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis, with only one study providing tentative support.

Furthermore, there are potential harms to consider. Recent data suggest a risk of adverse events associated with steroid prophylaxis; specifically, a 2019 study indicated an increased likelihood of ICU or ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Consequently, most contemporary guidelines advise against routine corticosteroid administration in this setting.

Despite this, practice remains variable. Our patient was treated per unit protocols that still reflect older practices, yet she ultimately avoided further relapse, likely the result of continued epinephrine availability and monitoring rather than steroid prophylaxis. In summary, do not rely on corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions; the risk-benefit ratio favors observation and resuscitative support with epinephrine alone.

### [Pathophysiology] How does systemic mastocytosis predispose a patient to a multiphasic or severe clinical course of anaphylaxis compared to typical allergic reactions?

This case illustrates why an undiagnosed underlying mast cell disorder can transform a standard allergic reaction into a protracted, life-threatening cascade. While typical IgE-mediated anaphylaxis is driven by acute, localized mast cell degranulation, systemic mastocytosis (SM) involves a neoplastic proliferation of mast cells infiltrating tissues. As noted in the report, this pathophysiology results in an "abnormal increase in mast cells" and a kinetic environment of "excessive mediator release."

In this patient, the presence of SM predisposed her to a multiphasic course through a high systemic reservoir of activated mast cells. Unlike typical reactions where mediators are cleared upon hemodynamic stabilization with epinephrine, the extensive mast cell burden associated with SM creates a sustained inflammatory trigger. This is evidenced by the patient’s recurrent episodes requiring multiple doses of epinephrine within 12 hours, despite initial clinical resolution. Furthermore, the analysis revealing elevated and persistent baseline tryptase levels, along with physical findings of hepatomegaly, supports the diagnosis of a mast cell storm rather than simple isolated allergy.

Clinically, this teaches us to consider SM in the differential for severe, recurrent anaphylaxis that does not resolve predictably with standard first-line therapies. Recognizing these features—persistent mediator markers like tryptase and signs of multiorgan involvement—allows for more aggressive observation and early investigation of mast cell disorders, as the sheer volume of cells driving the reaction mimics a refractory or biphasic pattern distinct from typical allergic hypersensitivity.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the key clinical criteria used to differentiate true biphasic anaphylaxis from sustained anaphylaxis with transient response to treatment?

The differentiation of true biphasic anaphylaxis from sustained anaphylaxis with a transient response to epinephrine relies primarily on the identification of a distinct period of complete clinical resolution prior to symptom recurrence.

According to the definition utilized in the framework discussed by Schneider et al., true biphasic anaphylaxis is characterized by the return of symptoms several hours after apparent symptom resolution, provided there has been no re-exposure to the allergen. In contrast, sustained anaphylaxis is distinguished by the cessation of transient improvements; the patient may rally with treatment, only for symptoms to progressively worsen or remain distinct without an intervening asymptomatic phase. Clinicians must also rule out ongoing allergen absorption (e.g., from residual food or slow-release medications) to confirm the reaction is independent of continued exposure.

As analyzed in the case report by Schneider et al., this distinction is critical for management but clinically challenging to establish with certainty. In that patient, a good initial response to epinephrine was achieved, followed by a recurrence of symptoms fitting anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission. While these episodes were treated as biphasic deviation in the monitoring plan, subsequent literature cited in this work emphasizes that predicting whether a response represents true recurrence versus a breakdown in sustained hemodynamic stability is often distinguished only retrospectively. Consequently, because these two conditions cannot be reliably differentiated by clinical signs alone during the asymptomatic window, current evidence supports prolonged inpatient observation—often extending beyond the traditional 4 to 6 hours—to ensure the exclusion of biphasic relapses up to 24 hours or, in rare documented instances, up to 78 hours post-initial resolution.

### [Management] In an adult patient weighing over 50 kg, how should the choice between 0.3 mg and 0.5 mg epinephrine auto-injectors be managed to avoid underdosing?

In adults weighing over 50 kg, clinicians should prioritize the use of 0.5 mg epinephrine auto-injectors over 0.3 mg devices to prevent underdosing and recurrence of anaphylaxis. Guidelines recommend a dose of 0.01 mg/kg (capped at 0.5 mg), making the 0.5 mg dose the standard for patients above this weight threshold. This recommendation is critical, as underdosing with a 0.3 mg device is a common source of treatable therapeutic failure.

The case presented highlights this management principle: a patient weighing 104 kg initially received 0.3 mg auto-injectors, which provided transient symptom relief but was associated with recurrent anaphylactic episodes. While a good initial response was observed, the patient required four total intramuscular doses, with the latter two doses converted to individually prepared 0.5 mg injections to resolve symptoms effectively. The discussion underscores that while 0.3 mg devices remain widely available, they lead to critical underdosing in adults over 50 kg. Notably, a 2025 retrospective study cited in this report demonstrated that patients receiving 0.3 mg epinephrine required significantly more frequent escalation of care compared to those receiving 0.5 mg. Therefore, despite logistical barriers or historical reliance on 0.3 mg devices, adherence to the 0.5 mg standard for patients exceeding 50 kg is essential to maximize first-dose effectiveness and reduce the risk of biphasic or multiphasic relapse.

### [Morning Report] In a patient with recurrent anaphylaxis under the supervision of emergency medicine, what are the risk factors necessitating admission to the ICU versus standard ward monitoring?

In managing patients with recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis, the distinction between intensive care unit (ICU) admission and standard ward monitoring remains a pragmatic decision rather than one driven by strictly validated predictive algorithms. As we saw in our patient, there are no universally reliable predictors for biphasic reactions, though literature suggests that risk stratification should lean heavily on the severity of the initial presentation and the response to first-line therapy.

In this case, the decision to admit this patient to the ICU was triggered specifically by the recurrence of anaphylactic shock on the internal ward. Although she initially responded to intramuscular epinephrine and was admitted to the acute ward, she experienced her first relapse of shock within 12 hours. This clinical milestone necessitated the escalation to ICU for closer surveillance and management of hemodynamic instability. The literature referenced in the report notes that suggested risk factors for such complex courses include a severe initial presentation, the need for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of prior biphasic reactions. However, because no definitive screening tool exists, the presence of hemodynamic instability or respiratory compromise requiring vasoactive escalation remains our primary indication for fallhazard ICU admission.

Furthermore, the underlying pathophysiology in this patient—highly suggestive of systemic mastocytosis despite not meeting formal diagnostic criteria—underscores why these patients are refractory to standard care. Patients with mastocytosis or suspected mast cell disorders present with exaggerated mediator release and multiorgan inflammation, predisposing them to multiphasic courses that standard allergic etiology would not predict. The case required intravenous corticosteroids and escalating epinephrine dosages (from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to 0.5 mg individual preparations) due to the weight of the patient (104 kg) and the severity of the relapse.

Therefore, the principle emerging from this case is that admission to a standard unit may be appropriate only after the resolution of the initial shock and the assured asymptomatic phase with no further relapses within the first 24 hours on observation. Conversely, if a patient presents with hypotension, hypoxia, or requires repeated epinephrine doses to regain stability, the threshold for ICU admission is met to ensure immediate availability of continuous airway and circulatory support.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the clinical guidelines for the duration of observation required after the resolution of anaphylaxis symptoms to anticipate biphasic recurrence?

Current clinical counseling on post-anaphylaxis observation remains a challenge, as there is no universally agreed-upon consensus for the duration of admission required after symptom resolution. Traditional guidelines and common clinical practice recommend a minimum observation period of 4 to 6 hours following complete clinical response to epinephrine. However, the pathophysiology of biphasic recurrence—defined as a recurrence of symptoms within 24 hours of resolution without re-exposure to the allergen—warrants a more nuanced approach, particularly given that approximately half of biphasic reactions can occur between 6 and 12 hours post-event.

In the case presented by Schneider et al., the limited utility of the standard 4- to 6-hour window is illustrated by the patient’s multiphasic course. This patient presented with hypotension and recurrent anaphylactic shock requiring multiple intramuscular epinephrine doses prior to stabilization. Although the initial presentation and response to treatment initially suggested resolution, the administration of four separate epinephrine doses and the development of recurrent shock during the first 12 hours complicated the risk profile. In this scenario, the medical team extended the monitoring period beyond the traditional window; the patient was safely transferred back to a standard care ward only after approximately 24 hours without recurrent symptoms.

Consequently, while a risk-stratified approach formally recommended by emerging guidelines (such as the 2021 UK guideline) is advocated, the practical application must rely on identifying high-risk features. Patients with severe initial presentations, hypotension, or those requiring more than one dose of epinephrine are at significantly higher risk for biphasic or multiphasic recurrence. In these cases, clinical judgment should support prolonged observation periods, often extending up to 24 hours, to ensure patient safety while balancing healthcare resources. Corticosteroid administration remains controversial and is not recommended for preventing recurrence, further placing the burden of monitoring on the duration of observation and the acuity of the clinical sequelae.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why is allergen-specific skin testing contraindicated during the acute recovery phase of a severe anaphylactic reaction?

Allergen-specific skin testing is contraindicated during the acute recovery phase of severe anaphylaxis because it risks precipitating a recurrence of the systemic reaction through re-exposure. In the case report by Schneider et al., this contraindication was a principal factor in management. Given the patient's multiphasic course with four recurrent episodes of shock, the authors noted that skin testing was avoided "as risking renewed serious anaphylaxis... was not deemed proportional."

The clinical rationale relies on the heightened mast cell reactivity observed in the immediate post-reactive period. In this specific case, the difficulty was compounded by a suspected underlying systemic mastocytosis, which predisposes patients to excessive mediator release upon exposure. The management strategy prioritizes safety over diagnostic certainty during this window, recognizing that strict allergen avoidance is safely achievable during the observation period without the procedural risk of skin testing. Diagnostic confirmation can be deferred until the patient has remained asymptomatic for a sufficient period to confirm resolution of the multiphasic instability.

### [Pathophysiology] What pathophysiological mechanisms might explain the observed attenuation of symptom severity in subsequent relapses of multiphasic anaphylaxis?

In this case of multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin, the observed attenuation of symptom severity in subsequent relapses likely reflects a combination of mast cell mediator kinetics and the cumulative hemodynamic stabilization provided by therapeutic interventions.

First, pathophysiologically, the initial episode represents a "perfect storm" of mediator release, depleting the labile stores of histamine, tryptase, and other vasoactive amines from the mast cell granules that were clipped during the first massive degranulation event. While the patient’s elevated baseline and episodic tryptase levels suggest a predisposition to severe release—likely consistent with the high suspicion of systemic mastocytosis noted in the case—the capacity for massive mediator release often diminishes in subsequent waves as granule stores deplete or exocytotic receptors become transiently downregulated.

Second, the patient received cumulative intramuscular epinephrine, with dosing escalating from 0.3 mg (auto-injectors) to 0.5 mg (IV doses) upon admission to the ICU. Epinephrine is the primary mechanism for halting anaphylaxis by reducing vascular permeability and stabilizing membranes; its repeated administration in this case suggests that each subsequent dose effectively countered the residual vasodilatory effects of the prior relapse. The "attenuation" may thus be partly a result of failing the patient's vasoconstrictive reserve rather than a change in the underlying allergen.

Finally, the presence of suspected systemic mastocytosis implies a hyper-proliferative mast cell population. While this predisposes the patient to the initial severe presentation (indicated by the deep shock and hypoxia), the clinical course demonstrates that once systemic vascular tone is re-established—via fluid resuscitation and catecholamines—the relative severity of recurrent mediator release can be lower. This case underscores that while elevated tryptase levels predict a higher risk of anaphylaxis, the trajectory of subsequent relapses is significantly modulated by the adequacy of epinephrine therapy and the physiological exhaustion of the initial mediator wave.

**Clinical Takeaway:** In multiphasic anaphylaxis, clinicians should anticipate that recurrent relapses will often be less hemodynamically compromising than the initial event, particularly following adequate epinephrine stabilization and hydration, despite the underlying threat of mastocytosis. However, vigilance remains mandatory, as the attenuation does not guarantee resolution, underscoring the need for prolonged monitoring.

### [Management] What long-term management and discharge criteria should be established for a patient with a confirmed or suspected underpinning diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis?

### Clinical Teaching Point: Management of Suspected Systemic Mastocytosis with Anaphylaxis

In the setting of anaphylaxis where systemic mastocytosis (SM) is suspected, particularly with a multiphasic course, management must account for the high risk of recurrent mediator release and the potential for prolonged hospitalization. Based on the case presented, three key principles guide the discharge and long-term care of these patients.

**Discharge Criteria**
Given the risk of biphasic recurrence, which can occur up to 78 hours post-exposure though most frequently within 24 hours, immediate discharge upon initial resolution is unsafe. In the specific context of this case, the patient required approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylactic symptoms after stabilization before transfer to a standard care ward. Final discharge occurred after 3 days of admission. Clinicians must consider a minimum 24-hour observation period post-symptom resolution to ensure the diphasic window has passed, particularly in patients presenting with multiple relapses, as seen in this multiphasic presentation.

**Long-term Management**
Once the acute instability resolves, management focuses on baseline symptom control and emergency preparedness. This patient was started on daily levocetirizine, an H1-antihistamine used for prophylaxis and cutaneous symptom control. Furthermore, emergency self-care is paramount; the patient was educated and equipped with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) for immediate administration in future reactions. While formal diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis were not fully met in this instance due to biopsy limitations, the clinical suspicion remained high. Long-term follow-up with an allergologist is essential to monitor tryptase levels, which in this case remained elevated during follow-up and served as a surrogate marker for mast cell burden and disease activity.

**Diagnostic Vigilance**
Finally, suspected SM requires rigorous workup to distinguish between true SM and other causes of chronic mast cell activation. Although the case report noted a failure to adhere to ideal tryptase sampling intervals during the initial workup—specifically a baseline sample at full clinical resolution—delayed tryptase evaluation was noted to have elevated levels. Clinicians should prioritize obtaining a baseline tryptase at least 24 hours after full symptom resolution to assess for SM. Persistent elevation, combined with a history of multidomin, such as hepatomegaly or neuropsychiatric symptoms, should prompt sustained anticoagulation or mucosal studies appropriate for formal SM diagnosis. Ultimately, strict allergen avoidance (such as the discontinuation of clarithromycin) and vigilant monitoring for mediator release symptoms remain the cornerstones of care for patients with high pretest probability of mastocytosis.
