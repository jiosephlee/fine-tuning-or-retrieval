Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Initial Presentation and Emergency Stabilization

The patient’s arrival at the emergency department marked the critical initial phase of management for suspected anaphylaxis, necessitating immediate diagnostic recognition and therapeutic intervention. Upon presentation, the clinical picture was defined by the rapid aggregation of symptoms across multiple organ systems, which is the hallmark of a systemic allergic reaction. The patient exhibited cutaneous manifestations including pruritus, flushing, and generalized urticaria, alongside cardiovascular collapse characterized by hypotension, with a blood pressure reading of 74/50 mm Hg. Her respiratory status was compromised by hypoxia, with peripheral oxygen saturation recorded at 88% on room air, and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute consistent with compensatory tachypnea. Additionally, the patient displayed signs of hemodynamic distress through increased transpiration and agitation. While urticaria alone does not diagnose shock, the combination of cutaneous signs with hypotension and hypoxaemia fulfills the diagnostic criteria for anaphylactic shock. The temporal relationship between these findings and the intake of oral clarithromycin one hour prior to arrival provided strong evidence for a drug-induced etiology, prompting the activation of acute resuscitation protocols despite the patient's lack of prior history with anaphylactic shock.

Immediate stabilization protocols were enacted to reverse the life-threatening features identified during the initial assessment. The cornerstone of therapy, intramuscular epinephrine, was administered via an auto-injector containing 0.3 mg, which is often the standard dose for preclinical settings and specific hospital protocols, though guidelines generally recommend 0.5 mg for adults weighing over 50 kg. The choice of an auto-injector was prioritized to minimize treatment delays in time-sensitive situations where personnel may be unfamiliar with the handling of vasoactive agents. To manage the hypoxia and respiratory distress, supplemental oxygen was delivered at 15 liters via a non-rebreather mask, a high-flow source required to maintain adequate saturation during the vasodilation and increased demand of the acute reaction. Concurrently, volume resuscitation was initiated with 500 mL of normal saline intravenously to counteract hypotension caused by increased vascular permeability and third-spacing of fluid. Adjunctive pharmacotherapy included the administration of 2 mg of clemastine intravenously to target the histamine-mediated cutaneous symptoms. Early administration of epinephrine is emphasized in medical literature as vital to prevent the rapid progression of shock; in this instance, the rapid recognition and prompt injection allowed for the reversal of the primary hemodynamic decline. This immediate success with epinephrine supports the clinical principle that circulatory collapse in anaphylaxis requires immediate vasoconstrictive support to restore blood pressure and perfusion to critical organs.

Following the initial intervention, the assessment of the patient’s response indicated a positive acute clinical reaction to the therapy. The blood pressure normalized, and facial swelling along with erythema decreased significantly, allowing for a transition from the emergency stabilization phase to ongoing acute ward monitoring. This initial response demonstrates the efficacy of standard anaphylaxis management, yet it also highlighted the necessity for continued observation, as patients with severe shock-like presentations are at risk for recurrence or biphasic reactions. Consequently, the patient was admitted to the acute internal ward for further monitoring without telemetry control initially. The decision to admit, rather than discharge, was driven by the severity of the hemodynamic instability observed at arrival, which serves as a risk factor for relapse. This stabilization phase underscored the importance of early epinephrine use relative to the onset of symptoms, ensuring that the initial responders could mitigate the immediate threat to life while preparing for the possibility of subsequent clinical deterioration that characterized her prolonged hospital course.
