Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Dilemmas and Guideline Discrepancies

The management of the recurrent anaphylactic episodes in this case presented a significant therapeutic dilemma, as the patient experienced successive relapses despite appropriate initial treatment and a standard period of observation. While the initial presentation with hypotension and hypoxia was managed with standard resuscitative measures including oxygen and normal saline fluid boluses, the transition into the intensive care unit for a multiphasic course required more nuanced decision-making absent of specific consensus guidelines. Fundamentally, the case highlights the clinical tension between maintaining standard pharmacological protocols for single-episode anaphylaxis and adapting to the unpredictable trajectory of a multiphasic reaction.

A critical point of contention in this case involved epinephrine dosing during the relapse phase. The patient, weighing 104 kg, initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector. Although her initial stabilization was sufficient, subsequent relapses were treated with individually prepared doses of 0.5 mg to address potential underdosing. Current resuscitation guidelines typically recommend a dose of 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults, acknowledging that 0.3 mg auto-injectors, often standard in preclinical settings, may constitute an underdose in larger adults. The use of 0.3 mg devices has been critically examined in recent literature presented in the case report, where a 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine needed escalation of care, such as additional injections, significantly more often than those treated with the full 0.5 mg dose. In this scenario, the preference for auto-injectors during the initial ED stabilization was justified by speed and minimizing delay in time-sensitive settings, yet the subsequent switch to IV-prepared doses acknowledges their limitation in delivering the guideline-recommended threshold for a patient of this size. This discrepancy illustrates the practical friction between guideline-adherent dosing and logistical constraints within emergency protocols.

The administration of corticosteroids during the relapse phase further exemplifies the gap between established practice and current evidence. Clinicians supplemented the epinephrine and antihistamine therapy with intravenous prednisone and dexamethasone. Historically, corticosteroids were utilized pre-emptively to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions, particularly in hospital settings where observation periods extend beyond the initial 4 to 6 hours. However, the analysis of this case critiques this practice, noting that a 2017 systematic review found no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Conversely, contemporary data emphasizes potential risks, with one 2019 study showing an increased likelihood of ICU/hospital ward admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Despite these concerns, their use persisted in this case per institutional protocols for both general ward and intensive care settings, highlighting the difficulty in abandoning established practices even when evidence trends toward obsolescence.

Regarding the specific management strategy for the relapse phase, the consultation with the allergology department revealed a difficult choice between pre-emptive stabilization and conservative monitoring. Clinicians considered continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to treat the patient proactively against further relapsing episodes. The alternative was an expectant management approach, predicated on the specific clinical observation that the intensity of symptoms lessened with each successive attack. These risk assessments require balancing the high stakes of untreated anaphylaxis against the risks of medication toxicity or cardiovascular instability from continuous infusion. The decision to transition back to a standard ward after 24 hours without recurrence supports the utility of clinical tracking, yet the lack of data on optimal infusion titration versus conservative monitoring leaves such decisions largely expert-dependent.

Fluid resuscitation was integral to managing the hypotensive episodes described, with 500 mL normal saline administered initially and during the second shock episode. While fluid management in anaphylactic shock is generally standard to maintain perfusion, the case underscores the need for careful titration in patients with comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension, where fluid overload could complicate the clinical picture. The broader challenge highlighted by this multiphasic presentation remains the lack of evidence-based literature defining optimal management for multi-relapse scenarios. Unlike standard anaphylaxis, where algorithms are robust, the multiphasic course lacks reliable predictors for relapse timing or severity. Consequently, reliance on expert consensus is necessary, but the absence of a registry for such cases impedes the development of data-driven recommendations. This case ultimately serves as an example of the clinical reality where treatment decisions are often made in the empiric void, necessitating a vigilance that goes beyond established guidelines without compromising patient safety.
