Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The 0.3 mg Trap: Why 'Adult' Autoinjectors May Be Underdosing

Anaphylaxis management is often taught like a muscle-memory reflex: Draw up the IM glucocorticoid, give the biphasic steroid, watch for the BP. You know this well. But when resistance meets relapse, that memory can't carry you all the way. We always think we’ve made the call on our initial round of epinephrine, but recent case reports suggest that in the setting of a multiphasic or recurrent shock, our "adult" auto-injector might actually be an underdose.

Consider this: A woman in her 60s, weighing 104 kilograms, walks into the ER with a Clarithromycin-induced anaphylactic shock. She is given the standard 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. Her BP comes back up, her rash clears, and she seems stable. Then, two hours later, she goes dark again. And two hours after that, she goes down again. Over the course of 12 hours, she experiences *four* distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock requiring episodic intervention, escalating from auto-injector to IV prepared doses. She isn't just biphasic; she’s multiphasic.

The question echoing through the ED isn't just "did you get the airway secured?" it should be looking like "was the complement receptor system overloaded, engaged the downregulation of beta receptors, or did you actually just give too little vasopressor?"

## The Case: Clarity Amidst Chaos
The patient presented initially with hypotension (74/50) and hypoxia (88% SpO2) shortly after taking a single 500 mg dose of Clarithromycin. Her history was spotless relevant to the anaphylaxis up to that point; no previous systemic anaphylaxis, just a mild skin reaction to beta-lactams in adolescence. She was admitted to the ward for monitoring. Standard protocol usually kicks in: saline bolus, antihistamines, and ensure observation for at least 4-6 hours to catch biphasic recurrence.

This patient checked the box. She recovered. But she came back within the observation window. The intubation wasn't needed, but she was transferred to the ICU. Here’s where the clinical details get thorny. During the first two episodes of relapse, the team utilized the standard 0.3 mg auto-injector. By the third and fourth episodes, due to the clear underdosing in that 104 kg body weight, they switched to 0.5 mg IV prepared epinephrine.

Interestingly, the severity of the attacks decreased each time. The initial presentation featured deep cyanosis and profound hypotension. By the final episode, it was manageable dyspnea and pruritus. But the fix came not just from increasing the vasopressor to 0.5 mg, but recognizing the pattern of recurrence. The patient was stabilized, transferred back to standard care after 24 hours, and discharged to Levocetirizine and an auto-injector taught to the patient.

However, the underlying etiology changed the calculus. Tryptase levels were elevated during the third episode and stayed high in follow-up, along with hepatomegaly and minor diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis. This suggests we aren't just dealing with a simple food/drug allergy in a normal immune background; mast cell density is higher.

## Why The 0.3 mg Auto-Injector is a Trap
We are in a world where epinephrine auto-injectors are housed conveniently for patients. But when we talk to ER directors, ponders the logistics, and read retelling the story in guidelines, we see a glaring discrepancy.

Guideline recommendations for epinephrine dosing in adults are calculated at 0.01 mg/kg, with a cap often recommended at 0.5 mg for adults over 50 kg. Why? Because 0.5 mg is roughly 0.01 mg/kg for a 50 kg person. But we know no adult weighs exactly 50 kg.

The case mentioned above? 104 kg. A 0.3 mg dose at that weight is equivalent to a 30 mg/kg dose. If you look at the calculation, it is actually a massive underdose. A 0.01 mg/kg dose for that patient should approach 1.04 mg cap, but arguably 0.5 mg is a safer cap for safety. Given the limitations of autoinjectors, the 0.3 mg device is the sole commercially available option in many systems for adults, yet for a 100+ kg patient, it is a fraction of the recommendation.

A 2025 retrospective study cited in the discussion of this case analyzed this exact dilemma. The data indicated that patients who initially received 0.3 mg required significantly more care, more escalation, and subsequent hospital resources compared to those who received the full 0.5 mg initially. The 0.3 mg auto-injector is the default "adult" dose, but physiologically, it treats the smallest adult not the heaviest.

In the case presented, the team admitted they hesitated between two strategies for the subsequent relapses: maintain continuous epinephrine through a pump or wait and hope for the voluntariness of spontaneous resolution. In an ideal world, there is evidence-driven guidance. But the lack of defined guidance for multiphasic courses creates oscillating risk. The patient received prednisone and dexamethasone as well, acknowledging the rise of old-school thinking on steroid prevention of biphasic reactions, despite recent systematic reviews suggesting they do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis.

## Clinical Pearls
*   **0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg:** The standard auto-injector (EpiPen) contains 0.3 mg. While many guidelines suggest 0.3 mg for adults, for patients >50 kg or in massive anaphylaxis, 0.5 mg is the calculated standard cap.
*   **Auto-injector in the ED:** Don't be afraid to use the auto-injector in the emergency department. It can be faster, less hesitation, and "no needle" delay. But recognize the rapidity of failure if that device is underdosing the patient. Switch to 0.5 mg options as soon as feasible.
*   **Multiphasic > Biphasic:** Multiphasic anaphylaxis has *four* or more distinct episodes. Biphasic has two. Multiphasic patients are not simple biphasic courses, and they are at higher risk of fatality. Treatment requires vigilance beyond the initial 4-6 hours.
*   **Mastocytosis Consideration:** Systemic mastocytosis (high baseline tryptase, multiorgan involvement) predisposes patients to severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis. If your anaphylaxis is weirdly recurrent, keep mastocytosis and baseline tryptase monitoring in your differential.
*   **No Reassurance Assumption:** Don't trust the clinical response as predicting the final course until you’ve had at least a 4-6 hour quiet window. If that pan-fry is multiphasic, watch closely.

## Take-Home Messages
*   A 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector is a known underdose for patients weighing >50 kg; consider switching to non-auto-injector preparations of 0.5 mg in the ED.
*   Multiphasic anaphylaxis requires extended monitoring beyond standard biphasic protocols, ideally into the 12-hour window.
*   Suspected mastocytosis or elevated tryptase should be investigated in recurrent anaphylaxis, especially when symptoms fail to resolve with standard therapy.
