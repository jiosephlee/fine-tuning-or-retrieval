Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Four Hours Isn’t Enough: Re-thinking Observation Durations for Biphasic Reactions

Anaphylaxis management textbooks have a sacred section: the protocol for biphasic relaxation. The standard dogma is concise and reassuring: treat, stabilize, observe for four to six hours, and discharge if asymptomatic. We teach this mantra to residents, stick it on the hospital doors, and rely on it for resource allocation. But what happens when the “relaxation” is a lie? What if your patient stabilizes miraculously for three hours only for the ICU tremors to start again?

This is the world of the 64-year-old woman presented in a recent case report on multiphasic anaphylaxis. Her story is a stark reminder that our observation windows might be woefully short for the subset of patients who don't fit the standard curve of resolution. While the typical incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis hovers around 4-6%, the trajectory of this specific patient was terrifyingly non-linear. She didn't just relapse once; she suffered four distinct episodes of shock within the first 12 hours of admission despite receiving appropriate care and epinephrine with each recurrence.

## Clinical Profile of a Multiphasic Case

The patient was a woman in her 60s with significant comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes and hypertension, presenting with anaphylaxis following a single dose of clarithromycin. Her initial presentation was classic and severe: hypotension, hypoxia, generalized erythema, and agitation. After an initial response to epinephrine and supportive care, she was transferred to the acute ward. Standard logic would suggest that if she had responded to the initial dose, her hospital course could have been tapered.

However, the patient manifested recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock. Treatment was escalated to include continuous monitoring in the ICU after the first relapse. She required a total of four epinephrine doses during her admission to manage these showers of symptoms. Notably, the intensity of the episodes actually lessened over time, shifting from deep hypoxia and cyanosis on the first return to simple dyspnea and pruritus on the last. This "waxing and waning" pattern is characteristic of multiphasic courses but remains incredibly difficult to predict before the second crisis strikes.

## The 78-Hour Window

The crux of the clinical dilemma lies in the definition of biphasic versus multiphasic anaphylaxis, which currently lacks consensus. Most guidelines are anchored to a 6-hour post-resolution window, with some extending to 12 hours. However, the background data of our case highlights that symptom recurrence is documented up to 78 hours after the initial allergen exposure.

The strategy adopted in this case was pragmatic but fraught with difficulty because it was based on observation without specific biological data to time it. When the patient appeared stable for approximately 24-33 hours after the last recurrence (totaling roughly 3 days of care before discharge), no further episodes occurred. This 24-hour window without relapse was deemed safe for discharge. The question remains: for a standard patient, is 6 hours sufficient? Or does a titre-based clinical picture—like the hepatomegaly and high tryptase seen in this patient—mandate a longer observational horizon?

## Pathology and Pharmacology Nuances

Beyond the observation clock, there were two critical pharmacological and pathological findings in this case that influence future emergency management. First, the patient had an elevated baseline tryptase level and repeated elevations following each anaphylactic episode, suggesting a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria. Conditions like this predispose patients to mast cell mediator release that is less responsive to standard triggers and more prone to recurrence.

Second, there is the issue of dosing resilience. The patient initially received epinephrine via auto-injectors containing 0.3 mg. While often preferred for time sensitivity or protocol familiarity, this dose is lower than the recommended 0.5 mg max dose for adults. Given her weight of 104 kg, this patient was effectively double the recommended threshold for severe anaphylaxis to rely on standard auto-injectors alone without a backup backup dose. She relapsed despite this escalation, eventually requiring manually prepared 0.5 mg doses in the hospital setting. Additionally, corticosteroids were administered with some controversy in the unit; while often used to prevent relapse, modern evidence increasingly questions their utility, yet in this unstable patient, they were part of the ICU protocol until discharge.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Standard 4-6 hour observation may be unsafe for high-risk features:** Cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis can recur up to 78 hours post-exposure, challenging traditional discharge windows.
*   **Trialling for Mastocytosis:** Elevated tryptase levels and persistent symptoms despite treatment should prompt a search for mast cell disorders (e.g., systemic mastocytosis).
*   **Epinephrine Dosing:** Auto-injectors (0.3mg) may be underdosed for adults >50kg (or >30kg for some weight-based calculations); ensure backup vials (0.5mg) are immediately available in the ED.
*   **Corticosteroids remain controversial:** While used for relapse prevention, evidence does not consistently support their efficacy in preventing biphasic reactions.
*   **Risk Stratification is currently lacking:** There are no validated reliable predictors for biphasic anaphylaxis; clinical judgment must weigh severity of initial presentation and response to epinephrine against resource availability.

## Bottom Line

Anaphylaxis in the absence of a clear trigger often assumes a benign post-resolution phase. However, the data presented suggests that for select patients—particularly those with features suggestive of mast cell disorders—the window of vulnerability extends far beyond the standard ICU discharge threshold. Until further research solidifies risk stratification, a conservative approach to monitoring remains the safest path.
