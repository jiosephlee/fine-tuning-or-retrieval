Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Tryptase Timing: Crucial Lessons from Missed Peaks

Anaphylaxis is the big boss of allergic reactions, but we often treat the acute management with the care it demands and leave the diagnostic follow-up to happenstance. This is particularly true when things don't resolve with one shot of epinephrine. When a patient presents not just once, but multiphasically, you are looking at the worst-case scenario in allergy work. You want to catch the bug in the system. But how do you diagnose the underlying etiology if the timing of your blood work is off by a few hours?

In a recent case report examining multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting, we see a textbook example of clinical brilliance during stabilization overshadowed by a significant diagnostic oversight regarding serum tryptase timing. The patient, a woman in her 60s, presented to the ED with profound anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin. It was a high-stakes presentation involving hypotension, hypoxemia, and widespread urticaria. Her team responded appropriately with auto-injector epinephrine, IV fluids, and antihistamines, achieving initial stability. However, she was admitted to the ICU for what turned out to be four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours, each requiring further dosing of epinephrine. While the clinical team managed the hemodynamic collapse effectively, the laboratory workflow failed to capture the crucial temporal markers of mast cell mediator release needed to investigate underlying pathology.

The story illustrates a critical tension between the urgency of resuscitation and the precision of diagnostic sampling. In anaphylaxis management, we often prioritize airway and breathing, but studies suggest that the diagnostic utility of serum tryptase—one of the few objective markers for mast cell activation—is entirely dependent on a strict temporal window. Tryptase is released from mast cells rapidly and peaks usually one to two hours after symptom onset, returning toward baseline within 24 hours. To diagnose mastocytosis, a condition often associated with refractory or biphasic reactions, clinicians need two distinct readings. You need a baseline sample taken at least 24 hours after resolution, and you need an acute peak sample taken during the hyperacute phase of the reaction.

In this specific case, the laboratory team missed the boat on both fronts. The first tryptase sample was not drawn until one hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode. By that time, the initial surge had likely stabilized sufficiently to miss the full magnitude of the peak release. The second sample was even more problematic, being drawn 27 hours after the last recurrence of symptoms. While this captured a potential baseline, the lack of an acute measuring during the optimal peak window made establishing a true delta impossible. The authors note that while baseline tryptase was elevated and remained so during follow-up, the inability to measure the peak during the acute episodes left significant ambiguity. The patient was subsequently diagnosed with a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, with elevated tryptase levels and supporting physical findings like hepatomegaly, but the diagnostic criteria could not be fully met due to the specific sampling gaps that occurred during the initial 24-hour admission.

This case serves as a stark reminder that in the aftermath of severe anaphylaxis, the "stable" patient still requires precise triage in the form of blood collection. The clinical team is undoubtedly trained to rush in, tap an arm, and crank the med cart, but the phlebotomy bag often gets overlooked by withdrawal from the simulation. The guidelines are clear: take a sample immediately on presentation without delaying resuscitation, take a second sample within one to two hours to catch the peak, and take a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to rule out inflammation. In reality, adhering to these three samples is often logistically challenging. Yet, missing all the peak data, as happened in this case, hinders the ability to stratify risk for future episodes or identify a predisposition like systemic mastocytosis that might make the same patient more vulnerable to the slightest exposure to a trigger in the future.

The broader implication of this case extends beyond the tryptase itself, touching on the complexity of managing multiphasic reactions where standard guidance—such as the role of corticosteroids or the duration of observation—is murky. Tryptase data bridges the gap between standard allergy and visceral pathology. Without that data, clinicians are left treating the crisis and hoping the next one doesn't come along so destructive, rather than identifying a root cause that could allow for targeted prophylaxis. Understanding the kinetics of the lab test is just as important as understanding the physiology of the reaction.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Timing is everything for mastocytosis workup:** Serum tryptase levels must be captured at three specific stages to be clinically useful: immediately upon presentation (without delaying treatment), 1–2 hours post-onset for peak levels, and a minimum of 24 hours post-resolution for true baseline levels.
*   **Do not delay resuscitation for phlebotomy:** The initial draw should happen prior to therapy; however, if deferrable, do not delay the epinephrine injection in favor of drawing blood. If the patient stabilizes, the acute phase is temporary.
*   **Missed peaks obscure diagnoses:** In cases of recurrence or multiphasic anaphylaxis, missing the peak trying level window can prevent the identification of underlying systemic mastocytosis, a dangerous condition that predisposes patients to severe reactions.
*   **Elevated baseline matters:** Even if the spike was missed, an elevated baseline tryptase during follow-up warrants further investigation for mast cell disorders, particularly in patients with complex or recurrent anaphylactic history.
