Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Steroids Save Who? Re-evaluating the Role of Corticosteroids in Anaphylaxis

## The Paradox of Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

It starts in the ED, always does. You have a patient walking in with hypotension, hypoxia, and skin that looks like bronzed meat. You know the drill: epinephrine, airway, fluids, antihistamines. In this specific case, a 60-year-old woman presenting after taking clarithromycin, things looked under control when the numbers normalized. Blood pressure back to baseline? Check. Facial swelling subsiding? Check. You transition to the acute ward. But then the classic horror of biphasic anaphylaxis strikes.

She didn't get out of the hospital the night she arrived. Despite the initial response to 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector, she redeemed herself in the ICU with four separate anaphylactic episodes. Each time requiring a specific bolus of epinephrine. It is a textbook example of "recurrence of symptoms despite adequate care." The pathology here was likely clarithromycin, but the substrate was a high-risk biology. Tryptase levels were elevated during the episodes and remained high after resolution. Further analysis suggested a systemic mastocytosis phenotype, even if full diagnostic criteria weren't met.

In the middle of this chaos, the medical team added the "ancient remedy": corticosteroids. They were given both prednisone and dexamethasone. Why? Because that is what we do. We fear the biphasic recurrence, so we drench the patient in anti-inflammatory medication. The case report candidly admits that while they supplemented the treatment, the role of corticosteroids remains a subject of intense debate and rather poor evidence.

## The Evidence Against the Holy Grail

For decades, we fell into the trap of prophylactic corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis. The logic was seductive: inflammation takes time to build the cytokine cascade that causes the relapse. If we stop it sooner (within 4 to 6 hours), we prevent the second wave. But as the literature accumulates, this dogma is becoming untenable.

Recent systematic reviews have been sobering. One 2017 review examined twenty two studies, finding that as many as twenty one of them showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Only one study actually supported the use of corticosteroids. This is not to say they have no utility elsewhere, but using them specifically to prevent relapse of anaphylaxis is the equivalent of throwing cactus glue on a snake bite; it's technically "medical" but offers no proven survival benefit.

It is getting worse. A 2019 study reported an increased likelihood of ICU or ward admission if corticosteroids were used prehospital. When you look at the side effect profile—hyperglycemia was noted in this very patient with a glucose of 12.7 mmol/L—the risk is becoming clearer. We are trading a manageable drug reaction for a prolonged hospital stay without a clear safety net. The case presentation highlights this tension: at primary care level, prednisone was used, but at the ICU level, dexamethasone was used, reflecting conflicting protocols within the same hospital.

## Navigating the Uncertainty

So, where does this leave us in a practice environment where guidelines haven't caught up (or at all)? The case authors suggest that optimal observation duration is debated. The Dutch and World Allergy Organization notes that half the biphasic reactions occur within six to 12 hours, yet there is no consensus monitoring recommendation that balances safety with resource allocation.

This case is important because the patient had *multiphasic* anaphylaxis. She relapsed not once, but three times within the first 12 hours of admission (making four total). This is the extreme end of the phenotypic spectrum. She required 0.3 mg auto-injector doses initially, then 0.5 mg intramuscular doses later. This highlights a separate but related issue in emergency medicine: sticky but critical underdosing of auto-injectors. For an adult weighing 104 kg, a 0.3 mg dose is sub-therapeutic. The fact that symptoms ultimately decreased in intensity over time is comforting, but it was achieved through serial epinephrine, not steroids. Epinephrine is the mechanism we should trust for relapse termination with its receptor activation. If we have a patient who is crashing, they need Epi, not a steroid injection.

This brings us back to the mastocytosis question. While she didn't meet formal criteria, the elevated baseline tryptase and clinical presentation make her biology scream mast cell instability. In mastocytosis, the baseline mediator burden is different. Does that change the steroid equation? The authors admit that research specifically addressing the role of systemic mastocytosis in biphasic anaphylaxis is virtually non-existent. This highlights the desperate need for better guidelines, or perhaps a registry, to track what actually works when the first line is exhausted.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Epinephrine is the game changer:** Serial administration of epinephrine led to this patient's resolution, whereas corticosteroids showed no clear benefit in preventing the recurrence.
*   **Check your auto-injector:** For adults over 50 kg, the standard 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector is likely underdosing; ensure you are using 0.5 mg doses in critical settings.
*   **Question the steroids:** Current systematic reviews demonstrate no proven benefit for corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, and potential for increased hospital stay or hypokalemia/hyperglycemia.
*   **Monitor the vulnerable:** Multiphasic anaphylaxis, while rare, presents with significant risk factors like elevated baseline tryptase. If mastocytosis is suspected, a more aggressive osmotic might be justified, but steroids should not be the only variable adjusted.
*   **Need new data:** The lack of response-based guidelines for multiphasic cases means it is time for experts to develop consensus recommendations or for a dedicated registry to capture outcomes.
