Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Don't Just Wait: Continuous Infusion vs. Expectant Observation in Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a terrible storm to weather. Typically, once the drip of epinephrine twitches off the monitor and the blood pressure climbs, the work feels done. But what do you do when the storm doesn’t just pass; it returns? While biphasic anaphylaxis is a known entity, the multiphasic course—multiple relapses in the absence of re-exposure—is a rare beast that terrifies both patients and intensivists. I’m digging into a recent case report by Schneider et al., which beautifully dissects this nightmare scenario. The patient was an older woman who was supposed to be safe after the first EPINEPHrine dose, only to find herself rebounding into shock for a fourth time. It’s a vivid reminder that while guidelines offer answers for single-episode reactions, the ICU floor often sits in a testing ground of unvalidated strategies.

## The Clinical Vignette

Meet a woman in her 60s with a heavy medication load and a history of beta-lactam sensitivity. She took clarithromycin for erysipelas and didn’t think twice, anything else she would have. One hour after that dose, the anaphylaxis struck. She came into the ED hypotensive, hypoxemic, and visibly raving with flushing and generalized erythema. The ED team acted quickly with epinephrine and fluids, and the initial picture looked like the textbook presentation of a controlled shock. But once she moved into the ward, the real test began. Despite the initial "good response," the patient developed recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission. She required epinephrine for the ED event and subsequently a second, third, and fourth time in varying dosages while in the hospital. When the initial shock resolves, you diagnose uncontrolled mast cell degranulation. However, when the patient does not adapt to progressively smaller intensities of anaphylactic shock, it raises the question of whether the mast cells are truly resetting or if a different pathology is at play.

In this specific scenario, the patient faced three distinct events in the ED, requiring a cumulative dose of epinephrine during the acute phase, and a fourth while hospitalized. The first three doses were standard 0.3 mg auto-injectors, but the fourth effective dose used a prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular administration. This trend highlights that since the initial epinephrine dose was relatively lower, the escalation of care is critical. After each recurrence, the severity lessened, ranging from deep hypoxaemia with cyanosis to dyspnoea and pruritus during the final episode. So, when the medical team couldn't find a "smoking gun" allergen and the patient stopped reacting, what was the next move?

## The Therapeutic Dilemma

The registration of the patient for intensive care was a direct result of her recurrent instability. Here is the crux of the dilemma: The ICU team had to choose between two very different management philosophies. The first option was continuous administration of epinephrine via an intravenous pump. This approach would have pre-emptively treated anaphylaxis, aiming to stabilize mast cells and prevent future degranulation-disastrous consequences. The second approach was an expectant observation strategy. Given the rapidly decreasing intensity of the symptoms with each subsequent episode, the team hypothesised that the system was contrail and would resolve spontaneously once the mast cells quieted down.

In the absence of consistent literature, both approaches came with serious risks. Continuous epinephrine infusion carries the weight of pharmacokinetic unknowns, potential hypotension upon sudden cessation, and the potential for adrenergic complications like arrhythmias or stress to the hypertensive tissues. Expectant management carries the risk of a sudden, unexpected, catastrophic relapse that could have occurred between pulse checks. The team noted that a definitive rule for this was lacking, yet they had to manage a patient who was essentially unpredictable. Fortunately, neither aggressive infusion nor constant adrenaline was deemed necessary as the patient did not show further symptom relapse after approximately 24 hours without a recurrence.

## Unmasking the Root Cause

While the management of multiphasic anaphylaxis remains frustratingly vague, understanding the underlying cause can change the game in the long run. For this patient, further analysis suggested a high likelihood of underlying systemic mastocytosis. Even though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria due to limited bone marrow biopsy material, the clinical picture was screaming this possibility. Tryptase levels were elevated during the third episode and remained elevated through follow-up, which is a stark indicator of mastocytic burden. In the past, some look to use baseline tryptase levels early in the workup, yet guidelines recommend specific sampling intervals including a peak sample at 1-2 hours and a baseline 24 hours later. In this case, the first tryptase sample was taken 1 hour after the third episode and the second 27 hours after the last recurrence. This lag meant the data did not strictly follow the standard protocol. The presence of hepatomegaly on sonography, combined with potential neuropsychiatric symptoms like depression and memory issues, supports a systemic inflammatory profile that doesn't just present as a simple allergy.

Furthermore, consider the medication usage during these acute events. Standard auto-injectors often contain 0.3 mg of epinephrine; in patients weighing over 50 kg, this is historically considered underdosing compared to the recommended regimen of 0.01 mg/kg. In a multiphasic course, where you are fighting to stay stable, that underdosing can translate to inadequate stabilization. A recent retrospective study cited in the discussion suggests that patients who initially received 0.3 mg require escalation significantly more often than those who start with a standard 0.5 mg dose. While the use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors is a barrier that often becomes a precedent in standard practice, in the ICU setting, the immediate availability of intravenous prepared doses of 0.5 mg might be necessary to avoid skeletal muscle metabolic fatigue and ensure rapid hemodynamic stability.

## Take-Home Messages

*   Multiphasic anaphylaxis presents a unique challenge with no established consensus for continuous epinephrine infusion versus expectant observation.
*   Clinical easing should be monitored carefully; escalation of care protocols should adapt to symptom intensity and patient response.
*   Suspect systemic mastocytosis in patients with recurrent reactions, elevated tryptase levels, and neuropsychiatric symptoms.
*   Ensure tryptase sampling adheres to guidelines (peak and baseline) to accurately diagnose underlying mastocytic disorders.
*   Recognize that standard 0.3 mg auto-injectors may be underdosed for adults weighing more than 50 kg, potentially leading to repeat administration.
