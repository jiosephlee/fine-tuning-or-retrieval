Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 3: ## Pathophysiological Mechanisms of Infection-Triggered CM-HUS

Understanding the pathophysiology of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) requires a clear appreciation of the alternative complement pathway and its dysregulation. The alternative pathway is a crucial component of the innate immune system that continuously monitors for pathogens and damaged cells. Under healthy conditions, it is tightly regulated by surface proteins such as factor H and C4b-binding protein. In CM-HUS, this regulatory system is overwhelmed or ineffective, leading to unbridled activation of the alternative pathway. This results in a massive generation of C3 convertase, C3b deposition, and the formation of membrane attack complexes. Without careful restraint, the complement system cleaves complement component 3 into C3b perpetually, creating a destructive environment that damages endothelial cells throughout the vasculature. This endothelial injury precipitates platelet aggregation and fibrin deposition, forming the characteristic microthrombi of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) seen in this patient’s renal biopsy and peripheral smear.

A critical concept in interpreting this diagnosis is the distinction between inherited and acquired triggers. While 50% to 70% of CM-HUS patients harbour genetic variants in complement regulatory proteins such as factors H, I, and B, clinical manifestation often requires an external "second hit" to breach the threshold of clinical disease. In this case, extended genetic testing revealed no pathogenic variants, identifying a subset of patients with a functional defect often termed infection-triggered CM-HUS. This patient’s highly relevant medical history, characterized by no prior complement predisposition or family history of similar conditions, supports the theory of "acquired" CM-HUS where the infection itself acted as the sufficient trigger following the dog bite. This highlights that the absence of a genetic diagnosis does not rule out the diagnosis of CM-HUS, particularly when the clinical presentation and treatment response perfectly align with complement blockade.

The specific interaction between *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* and the host immune system presents a unique pathophysiological paradox that explains the causality in this patient. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated, gram-negative rod with variable serum susceptibility to complement-mediated lysis. While the complement system is essential for clearing encapsulated bacteria like *C. canimorsus* and *Neisseria* species, the activation required to achieve clearance can inadvertently drive the very pathogenesis causing TMA. In instances of host complement deficiency or dysregulation, the activation cascade becomes unregulated rather than contained. This case illustrates how a virulence factor—the bacterial capsule—normally protecting the pathogen from phagocytosis and complement lysis may, in a susceptible host, drive a runaway inflammatory response. The report notes that *C. canimorsus* can activate complement pathways, particularly in susceptible hosts, creating a clinical scenario where the body's defense mechanism becomes the primary driver of organ damage.

Diagnostic interpretation in cm-HUS often relies heavily on a negative search for alternative causes and the exclusion of standard markers. A common pitfall for learners is assuming that low complement levels are a prerequisite for diagnosis. However, as observed in this case, standard complement assays C3 and C4 are frequently within normal limits during the acute phase of CM-HUS. The case documentation explicitly noted that local C3 and C4 levels were normal, and standard functional assays for the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways remained within reference ranges until extended testing was performed post-treatment initiation. This occurs because the alternative pathway consumes C3 rapidly, but under conditions where the pathway is not primarily depleted but rather dysregulated (as is the case with acquired triggers), total complement levels may remain stable due to compensatory liver synthesis. Consequently, the absence of hypocomplementemia should not preclude the diagnosis of CM-HUS, which is ultimately a clinical and histopathological diagnosis of exclusion.

The therapeutic strategy necessitates balancing the benefits of complement blockade against the risks of impaired bacterial defense. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component C5, effectively suppresses the formation of the membrane attack complex, halting the hemolysis and thrombosis responsible for the TMA syndrome. However, the use of terminal complement inhibitors leaves the host vulnerable to infections from encapsulated bacteria. This is clinically relevant in the management of a patient bitten by a dog, as *C. canimorsus* specifically relies on the alternative pathway for killing. The case team navigated this dilemma by initiating eculizumab only after blood cultures became negative, minimizing the risk of uncontrolled bacteraemia. Additionally, antimicrobial prophylaxis and vaccination were implemented to mitigate the risk of *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga* infection post-treatment, illustrating the complex interplay between immunologic suppression and host defense mechanics.
