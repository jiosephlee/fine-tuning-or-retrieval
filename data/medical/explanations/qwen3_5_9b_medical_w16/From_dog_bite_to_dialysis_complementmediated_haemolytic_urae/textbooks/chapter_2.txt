Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Pivot: Recognizing and Defining Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA)

The initial management of this patient centered on the recovery from severe sepsis, Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), and fluid resuscitation, all of which are treatable with standardized antimicrobial and supportive measures. However, a critical diagnostic pivot occurred when the patient, despite improved inflammatory markers, developed isolated, worsening haemolysis and persistent renal failure. In patients presenting with sepsis, the clinical picture is often dominated by systemic inflammatory response, which can mask the specific microvascular pathology of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The distinction here lies in the trajectory of organ dysfunction: while the patient’s fever and leukocytosis improved with antibiotics, her kidney function deteriorated into anuria, and she developed microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). Recognizing this dissociation—specifically the return of oedema and haematological instability while sepsis resolved—is key. Clinicians must monitor for sudden drops in haemoglobin, rising reticulocytes, and unexplained thrombocytopenia persisting after the initial septic phase, as these are red flags that suggest a superimposed TMA rather than sepsis alone.

Establishing the diagnosis of TMA requires demonstrating MAHA, defined by the presence of schistocytes, which represent red blood cells fragmented within the microvasculature. In this case, the laboratory provided compelling evidence of MAHA through a peripheral smear showing 15% to 20% schistocytes concurrent with a rapid, isolated drop in haemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over six days. Supporting markers included undetectable haptoglobin and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), confirming ongoing intravascular hemolysis. Importantly, the initial direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG, which initially suggested autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. However, recognizing that DATs can yield false positives in the setting of penicillin treatment or transfusion was crucial. Repeat testing confirmed these as false positives, leaving MAHA as the primary haematological etiology. The persistence of thrombocytopenia despite the clearance of the initial septic drive further solidified the suspicion of a microvascular occlusive process requiring targeted intervention.

Before committing to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), clinicians must rigorously exclude its major mimics, specifically thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin-producing *Escherichia coli* (STEC)-associated HUS. This diagnostic exclusion is standard practice in TMA workups to ensure the most reversible or fatal causes are not missed. TTP is distinguished by severe deficiency of the ADAMTS13 enzyme, which must be below 10%. In this patient, ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, rendering TTP highly unlikely. Conversely, STEC-HUS is defined by a prodrome of diarrheal illness and a positive stool PCR for Shiga toxin. The patient’s history lacked prodromal enteric symptoms, and the PCR returned negative, effectively ruling out STEC-HUS. By systematically excluding these two conditions, the clinical team narrowed the differential diagnosis to a secondary or idiopathic TMA, where infection-triggered dysregulation became the primary consideration.

The definitive pathological confirmation of the diagnosis came through renal biopsy and complement analysis, both of which presented educational nuances regarding TMA diagnosis. The kidney biopsy revealed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli, fibrin thrombi, and crescent formation, hallmarks of thrombotic microangiopathy that mimics the findings of typical TTP or HUS. However, immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, which distinguished this case from post-infectious glomerulonephritis. Complement level analysis provided another teaching point: basic C3 and C4 levels were within normal limits both before and after initial therapy. Clinicians must understand that normal C3/C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS, as complement levels may remain stable during the acute phase or due to consumption rates varying in inflammatory states. This was further reinforced by extended functional assays, which showed reduced alternative pathway activity that likely reflected the treatment response with eculizumab and plasma exchange rather than a pre-existing hereditary defect in Factor H, B, or I.

Ultimately, this diagnostic sequence illustrates that TMA in the context of sepsis requires a dynamic reassessment rather than a static diagnosis. The initial confusion between DIC and TMA is common, but the hallmark of this case was the persistence of haemolysis and thrombocytopenia despite sepsis control. The diagnostic process prioritized safety by systematically ruling out TTP and STEC-HUS before targeting the complement system. This approach ensures that expensive, specific therapies like eculizumab are utilized appropriately. For the learner, the key takeaway is that renal biopsy remains the gold standard for pathological confirmation of TMA, and complement inhibitor therapy should be initiated based on a strong clinical suspicion of infection-triggered complement dysregulation, with full awareness that a negative genetic or C3/C4 test does not exclude the diagnosis when the clinical and histological picture is consistent.
