Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 1: ## Initial Clinical Syndrome: Bacterial Sepsis and Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation

The clinical presentation of this case provides a critical teaching model for understanding the epidemiology and acute management of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection. This fastidious, gram-negative rod is a normal inhabitant of the oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats, yet it is rarely pathogenic in humans. Invasive infections, which can lead to fulminant sepsis, typically occur following a bite, a scratch, or close contact with the animal. In this instance, the patient’s history of being bitten by her dog four days prior to admission establishes the vector and timeframe. Clinically, the risk of infection is heightened if appropriate prophylaxis is not administered; here, the patient received a tetanus vaccination on the day of the incident but failed to collect her prescribed antibiotic. This delay in treatment removal allowed the organism to proliferate, precipitating the overwhelming septic response observed on admission. It is a fundamental teaching point that while such infections are rare in immunocompetent adults, they carry a high mortality rate, emphasizing the need for a high suspicion of zoonotic pathogens in the context of animal exposure.

The patient’s initial admission reveals the hallmarks of severe bacterial sepsis, specifically a rapid progression to septic shock with significant metabolic derangement. Upon presentation, the patient was alert but clinically septic, exhibiting mottled skin and cool extremities, which are signs of poor peripheral perfusion and vasodilation. A unique and ominous feature of this case was severe hypoglycemia, with a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L that was refractory to standard dosing of oral glucose and glucagon. While hypoglycemia in critically ill patients can be multifactorial, such a profound and resistant level suggests a severe state of systemic stress and exhaustion of metabolic reserves in the context of sepsis. Concurrently, the patient presented with lactic acidosis, characterized by a lactate level of 5.7 mmol/L and a pH of 7.27. These findings, alongside the systemic inflammation and acute organ dysfunction, confirmed a diagnosis of septic shock, which was further supported by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1/3, indicating clinical deterioration despite potential compensatory mechanisms.

Laboratory analysis on admission uncovered a profound constellation of hematologic and renal derangements that initially pointed toward Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia. The workup revealed marked pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and acute kidney injury, alongside elevated liver enzymes. In the context of severe sepsis, the observed thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy are consistent with the consumption of clotting factors and platelets driven by an uncontrolled inflammatory cascade. The initial clinical interpretation was that these abnormalities represented early DIC driven by unknown pathogens, though *C. canimorsus* was suspected due to the clinical history. This highlights a common diagnostic challenge: the sluggish growth of *Capnocytophaga* often delays culture confirmation, potentially delaying targeted therapy. Consequently, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was initiated immediately to cover zoonotic pathogens, a standard management principle when etiology is unclear but the clinical suspicion is high.

Understanding the distinction between sepsis-induced DIC and Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) is vital for interpreting this phase of the clinical course. While DIC involves systemic consumption of coagulation pathways and platelets due to a widespread clotting response, TMA is characterized by the formation of microthrombi within the endothelium that predominantly causes microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and end-organ injury. In the initial phase of this patient’s care, the overwhelming signs of infection and systemic coagulopathy masked the developing microvascular pathology. The pancytopenia and coagulopathy were attributed to DIC and sepsis. However, the pathophysiology of DIC and TMA are distinct yet overlapping processes; early infection can trigger endothelial stress. It is in this initial syndrome that the sepsis masked the complement-mediated pathology. Clinically, the patient was stabilized with transfusions and broad-spectrum antibiotics in parallel with source control for the dog bite and septic shock, setting the stage for the subsequent differentiation of evolving microthrombi from systemic clotting consumption as her inflammatory metrics improved.
