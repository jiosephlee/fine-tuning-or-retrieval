Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 5: ## Prognostic Factors and Clinical Implications

The clinical trajectory of this patient, as well as the broader population of patients with complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), underscores a dissociation between hematological and renal recovery. In this specific case, the patient achieved hematological remission and adequate urine output relatively quickly following the initiation of eculizumab therapy, a crucial distinction from her renal prognosis. While her hematological parameters stabilized with immunological blocking of C5, her serum creatinine remained elevated at 175 µmol/L, indicating that she remains at risk for end-stage kidney disease. This pattern illustrates a fundamental principle in TMA management: hematological remission often precedes renal recovery, and the persistence of renal impairment despite hematological clearance is not uncommon. Consequently, clinicians must anticipate a slow and often incomplete recovery of renal function even when hematological metrics suggest a favorable response to treatment.

The timing of eculizumab therapy proved to be the most significant prognostic factor in this case, aligning with evidence that delayed initiation correlates with higher risks of end-stage kidney disease. The decision to administer complement blockade was strategic; while plasma exchange provided transient stabilization and returned diuresis, sustained recovery only occurred after the first doses of eculizumab. Crucially, the therapy was initiated after blood cultures became negative, which mitigated the risk of overwhelming infection associated with terminal complement inhibition against encapsulated bacteria like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. This emphasizes that successful management requires balancing the urgent need to halt endothelial damage in TMA with the safety imperative of maintaining host defense against infection.

Long-term management of patients on complement inhibitors centers on infection risk mitigation and personalized therapy duration. In this patient, blood prevention measures were rigorous, including vaccination with meningococcal serogroups A, C, Y, W, and B, and the administration of prophylactic penicillin during the induction phase. While the absence of confirmed pathogenic complement gene variants complicates the decision-making process for discontinuing therapy, the severity of the clinical presentation—with initial anuria and dialysis dependence—warrants extended monitoring. Clinical guidelines suggest that while the absence of genetic defects in CFH, CFI, or CFB may support a trial of discontinuation, careful reassessment of renal function and signs of relapse over 6 to 12 months is essential before stopping maintenance therapy indefinitely.

A critical lesson from this case is the necessity of identifying rare infectious triggers in undifferentiated thrombotic microangiopathy. The initial septic shock presentation masked the underlying CM-HUS, likely due to the dominance of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and sepsis-mediated organ dysfunction. This case highlights that when TMA occurs in the context of an infection, clinicians should remain vigilant for the superimposition of secondary HUS, even if the initial workup focuses primarily on the sepsis. Delay in recognizing the complement-mediated component can lead to missed opportunities for early intervention, resulting in the irreversible renal fibrosis observed in many delayed presentations.

Finally, accurate diagnostic workup serves as the foundation for prognostic stratification, utilizing specific clinical and laboratory criteria to rule out differentials. In this patient’s workup, the PLASMIC score was used to identify a low probability of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which was confirmed by an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%. Screening for Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* via stool PCR was also negative, satisfying diagnostic criteria for STEC-HUS exclusion. The integration of these tools—ADAMTS13 quantification, serological testing for Shiga toxin, and clinical scoring systems—ensures that TMA is correctly attributed to complement dysregulation. While this patient did not present with the classical triad of HUS at onset, the application of these tools allowed for the precise classification of infection-triggered CM-HUS, facilitating the targeted use of eculizumab.
