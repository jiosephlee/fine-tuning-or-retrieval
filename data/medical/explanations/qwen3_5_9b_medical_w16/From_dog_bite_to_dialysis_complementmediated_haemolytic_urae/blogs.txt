Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# From Bite to Death: Preventing Fatal *Capnocytophaga* Sepsis in the Immunocompetent

Dog bites often get waved off as minor nuisance injuries in the emergency department, especially in healthcare workers who see them daily. When the victim is a healthy adult, the instinct is usually to irrigate the wound and refer for sutures. But take a moment to read the case report by Fredriksen et al., a grotesque story where a snap of a jaw resulted in renal failure, dialysis dependence, and a trail of schistocytes. This highlights a devastating, hypoglycaemic physiology seen in inoculation of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. While often reserved for discuss in immunocompromised hosts or splenectomized patients, fastidious Gram-negative rods like *C. canimorsus* can strike the healthy woman hard, with case fatality rates approaching 30% in severe sepsis.

The lush presentation is deceptive. A woman in her late 40s had a minor bite to her lip four days prior, received the tetanus shot from a dog, but delayed seeking antibiotics. The initial workup for septic shock looked generic: pancytopenia, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and acute kidney injury (AKI). The clinical focus was dominated by the shock and the rapid progression to complete anuria. The diagnostic odyssey here wasn't just about identifying the bacteria, but untangling the dual pathology of sepsis-induced DIC out of which a mushroom cap of Complement-Mediated HUS sprang.

This case adds a subtle but vital layer to the *Capnocytophaga* algorithm: refractory hypoglycemia. The patient presented with a severe, life-threatening hypoglycemia nadir of 0.6 mmol/L that was refractory to oral glucose and glucagon. In septic adults, while lactic acidosis is expected, profound hypoglycemia in the absence of insulinoma or pancreatitis should immediately trigger a blood culture labeled with zoonotic coverage. Often, *C. canimorsus* is missed because it grows slowly in blood cultures. In our patient, the organism wasn't confirmed until seven days later. While broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam covered it eventually, the delay allowed the organism to consume host factors and trigger complement dysregulation.

Even after the initial sepsis stabilized, the case took a nasty turn with sudden isolated haemolysis. This is the classic diagnostic crossfire of TMA. As the inflammatory markers normalized, the patient developed severe microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), with schistocytes appearing on the smear and platelets drifting low. Standard OMARI tools were deployed to rule out TTP, confirming a Normal 43.5% ADAMTS13 activity, and the infusion of steroids for suspected autoimmune haemolytic anaemia failed to halt the bleeding and renal decline. This forced a biopsy, which was devastatingly clear of post-streptococcal HUS or TTP markers, driving the diagnosis toward infection-induced CM-HUS.

The therapeutic dilemma in this case highlights the complexity of managing TMA in the context of active or recent infection. Eculizumab is the salvage therapy for atypical HUS, yet it blocks terminal complement components that are vital for fighting encapsulated bacteria like *C. canimorsus* and *Neisseria*. The team utilized plasma exchange as a bridge before starting the monoclonal antibody. This strategy allowed them to stabilize hematology first, then initiate the eculizumab once blood cultures were negative, balancing the risk of fatal meningococcal/septic progression against the progressive renal loss.

The takeaway here is not just about treating the dog bite, but recognizing the hidden sequelae of polymicrobial shock in the immunocompetent. While death is not the inevitable fate for every *Capnocytophaga* bacteremia survivor, the window for intervention is narrow. The noise of septic shock and DIC often drowns out the alarm of microthrombosis, but the progression to renal failure or persistent schistocytes indicates a failure to activate. Early recognition, broad coverage, and planning for thrombotic microangiopathy are the only ways to tip the scale in favor of survival.

## Key Take-Home Messages
- Refractory hypoglycemia in a patient with a recent animal bite is a specific red flag for *Capnocytophaga* sepsis.
- *C. canimorsus* remains a fastidious organism; negative cultures or lack of rapid growth on first draw does not rule out infection.
- Differentiation between DIC sepsis and CM-HUS requires careful monitoring for the emergence of MAHA and thrombocytopenia post-sepsis stabilization.
- Eculizumab is highly effective for CM-HUS but requires careful timing regarding infectious status and prior vaccination.
- ADAMTS13 activity >10% effectively rules out TTP in the context of TMA, guiding treatment toward complement blockade.
- Immunocompetent adults are not immune to fatal *C. canimorsus* sepsis; broad-spectrum coverage is mandatory for deep-tissue trauma or bites.



# The Sepsis-TMA Lag: Why Thrombotic Microangiopathy Reveals Itself After Initial Recovery

There is a specific kind of diagnostic ghost in the emergency department, one that haunts us when a patient stabilizes but then suddenly deteriorates in an organ-specific way. We often associate with the triad of fever, septic shock, and multiorgan failure. But the timeline matters. When these elements separate, the geriatrician in the room looks for a second hit, but the internist looks for a new bug.

In a fascinating case report by Evason Fredriksen and colleagues, we see exactly this phenomenon. A woman in her late 40s was bitten by a dog, developed fulminant sepsis due to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, crashed into septic shock, and then, almost miraculously, began to recover. But she didn’t fully recover. She developed isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia days later. It wasn't sepsis anymore; it was Complementary-Mediated Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

This case teaches us that TMA isn't always a straight line diagnosis, but one that can hide behind the curtain of DIC and sepsis. It also highlights a terrifying diagnostic window where the inflammation settles, but the complement system goes haywire.

## The Bug, The Bite, and The Blink

Let's back up to the trauma. The patient was bitten on the lip, presumed minor. She didn’t pick up the prescription for penicillin immediately, the incubation for *Capnocytophaga* was 4-day incubation silent, and then *boom*, she was in the ER with a glucose of 0.6 mmol/L and a lactate of 5.7 mmol/L.

This is classic *Capnocytophaga* fulminant sepsis. It’s an encapsulated, gram-negative rod from the dog’s mouth that loves to cause DIC. The initial picture was textbook:
*   Fever > 40 °C
*   Pancytopenia
*   DIC (coagulopathy)
*   AKI
*   Hypoglycaemia (resistant to glucose)

She received broad-spectrum antibiotics (Pip/Tazocin) and was moved to the ICU. It seems she responded *beautifully* to the consortium. Within a week, the cultures were cleared, the inflammatory markers were trending down, and the labs were stabilizing. By day 8 on the ward, she was looking like the patient she was five days prior.

Then, the lights started blinking. Her haemoglobin tanked from 111 to 68 g/L. There was no bleeding. No trauma. No obvious dissection. She developed microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA).

## The Diagnostic Hurdles

Here is where it gets interesting. We think we know TMA. It’s the triad of thrombocytopenia, MAHA, and AKI. But how did we miss it in the first week?

When I see MAHA and thrombocytopenia, I immediately run ADAMTS13 to rule out TTP. In this case, ADAMTS13 was 43.5%. Not 10%. This effectively ruled out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP).

We also had to look for Shiga toxin. STEC-HUS is the most common in kids. In adults, it’s rare. Stool PCR was negative.

Then came the confusing Coombs test (DAT). It came back positive. We assumed autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. We gave prednisone. The haemolysis didn't stop. Then we saw the blood smear. There were 15%–20% schistocytes. This was the TMA confirmation. But the DAT? It was likely a reactive result due to prior transfusions or the penicillin.

The biopsy was the last piece. It showed severe acute tubular necrosis but fibrin thrombi and crescent formation. No immune complex deposition. This was TMA. Since STEC was out and ADAMTS13 was normal, we were left with the diagnosis of CM-HUS.

This case highlights a crucial rule: Normal C3 and C4 levels do *not* rule out complement-mediated disease. The functional assays showed reduced alternative pathway activity later on, but during the acute phase, standard complement levels were normal. It is a diagnosis of exclusion that demands vigilance.

## The Therapeutic Dilemma

This is where the management gets tricky. A patient with active TMA needs immunosuppression or complement blockade. A patient with meningococcal or *Capnocytophaga* bacteremia needs antibiotics and immune surveillance.

Eculizumab (a complement C5 inhibitor) is the standard for CM-HUS. However, it is also the standard paradox. It protects healthy endothelium but may leave open the cell membrane to bacterial invasion. If your patient has *Capnocytophaga*, giving them an antibody that blocks the terminal complement makes them seem *more* infections.

In this case, the team used a bridging strategy. They started Plasmapheresis (PEX) for symptom control. Then, after cultures turned negative and with prophylactic Apocillin, they started Eculizumab (900 mg weekly).

Wait, why did we know the bacterial load was gone? Because the blood cultures were negative. If we had given Eculizumab *during* positive cultures in this case, the balance skews towards disaster.

Despite the risk, the patient started improving. Haematological remission was achieved. The kidney function only partially recovered (creatinine stuck at 175 µmol/L). But the bleeding stopped.

## Key Clinical Pearls

*   **No Sepsis Lag in Infection-Triggered TMA:** While DIC is the immediate threat of *Capnocytophaga* sepsis, a secondary phase of CM-HUS can emerge days later, even when the primary septic shock is resolved.
*   **Ruling Out TTP:** ADAMTS13 > 10% helps you pivot to TMA/HUS workup rather than treating for TTP vertically.
*   **False DATs:** A positive DAT in the context of transfusion and infection may be reactive, not indicative of Evans syndrome.
*   **Normal Complement Levels:** C3/C4 can be normal in disease-active CM-HUS. Do not rule it out based on standard chemistries alone.
*   **PEX Before Eculizumab:** Bridging can be used for stabilization in high-risk patients before starting immunomodulation, provided infection control is in place.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Don't close the book on sepsis too early** when looking at AKI and thrombocytopenia; a second wave of TMA (CM-HUS) is possible days or weeks later.
*   **MAHA + Thrombocytopenia + AKI** after sepsis requires a broad differential that includes infection-triggered HUS, not just DIC or TTP.
*   **Standard C3/C4 levels are not diagnostic** for complement-mediated disease; functional assays and clinical response to therapy are crucial.
*   **For *Capnocytophaga*, treat Eculizumab cautiously:** Ensure bacteremia is controlled with antibiotics and prophylaxis before blocking complement to avoid uncontrolled infection.
*   **Delayed diagnosis is common:** Renal biopsy can help in equivocal cases where immunosuppression fails to halt haemolysis.

This case is a reminder that in medicine, the ghost isn't always the patient dying; sometimes, it's the patient walking you into a second diagnosis. Keep the scope on the patient, the lab, and the biology. Sometimes the second act is the one that keeps the patient alive.



# Triaging TMA: Using PLASMIC Scores and ADAMTS13 to Avoid TTP Misdiagnosis

## The Patient: A Dog Bite That Kept On Giving

Imagine this scenario: You present a sudden fever, hypoglycemia, and septic shock after a dog bite. You treat it aggressively with piperacillin-tazobactam, her fever breaks, her inflammatory markers start to fall, and you start to feel like the worst case of the weekend is behind you. Then, three days later, you get a call. Her hemoglobin has plummeted, her platelets are stuck at 30, and her urine output has halted completely. You order a peripheral smear. You are screaming. There are schistocytes. Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) has just sprung up out of nowhere.

This is exactly the trajectory of the case by Fredriksen et al, published in *BMJ Case Reports*. The patient was a previously healthy 40-something woman who, four days post-bite, was initially fighting a *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis. Her course was terrifying at first—DIC, acute kidney injury, anuric renal failure. You would think this is the end of recovery, yet she stumbled on her feet (metaphorically). But then, the clinical picture pivoted. After initial improvement, she developed isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. It wasn't the sepsis anymore; it was a Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) masquerading as a complication of recovery.

For the ED and ICU physician, TMA is a high-stakes triage. Is it Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP)? Is it Hemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (HUS)? Is it drug-induced or just a complicated sepsis? In this case, distinguishing TTP from Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS) was critical, as the treatments diverge significantly, and the risks of therapy (like eculizumab) are non-trivial, especially in the setting of active bacterial infection.

## The Waiting Game: Waiting for ADAMTS13 is Not Staring into the Dark

The gold standard to rule out TTP is testing ADAMTS13 activity. In primary TTP, this enzyme is usually severely deficient, <10% of normal. Unfortunately, you can't get this back immediately. It often takes 5 to 7 days for results. In the interim, you have a patient with MAHA (Schistocytes), low platelets, and potential renal failure. Do you proceed to full-dose PEX (Plasma Exchange) immediately? Do you start eculizumab? Do you wait?

This is where the PLASMIC score comes in. It's not just a buzzword; it's a decision tool to stratify the risk of TTP when you are waiting for confirmatory testing. The PLASMIC score (Platelets, Lactate, Attending, Mortality, Infection, Creatinine) assigns points based on clinical and laboratory data.

In our case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7. The authors noted this as a "low pretest probability of TTP." While the formal risk calculation requires a complex formula, the takeaway for the clinician is vital: a low PLASMIC score coupled with normal inflammatory markers in the right context shifts the diagnosis away from primary TTP. In this specific patient, the score was 4/7, and the ADAMTS13 activity result eventually came back at 43.5%. That 43.5% is completely above the level of suspicion for TTP (<10%).

However, the PLASMIC score doesn't just say "Do you have TTP."; it says "Is TTP the leading cause?" If the score is low and ADAMTS13 is preserved, you must broaden your differential. Is this STEC-HUS? CM-HUS? Or TMA from an infection? In this report, the diagnosis shifted once Shiga toxin was ruled out. The TMA was secondary to the *Capnocytophaga*.

## The Therapeutic Tightrope: PEX, Eculizumab, and *C. canimorsus*

When you diagnose TMA, the treatment paradigm depends on the cause. If it is TTP, PEX is the life-saving intervention. If it is HUS (especially infection-triggered CM-HUS), the management is controversial.

In our patient, PEX was initiated as a "bridging therapy" before the diagnosis of CM-HUS was cemented. This is standard practice when you have a TMA but the etiology isn't fully clear. However, the report highlights a specific danger that ED and phenicologists need to remember: Eculizumab. Eculizumab blocks terminal complement. While it is the treatment for CM-HUS, it blocks opsonization of encapsulated bacteria. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is encapsulated.

The authors walked a fine line. They started Eculizumab 8 days after the final PEX session and 4 days after the first dose, but they had ensured the blood cultures were negative. If you start complement blockade before the sepsis is cleared, you are playing with fire. This patient had antibacterial therapy broad-spectrum antibiotics (Tazocin, then Apocillin) covering *Capnocytophaga*, but the key was that cultures were negative before the eculizumab induction. This is a crucial risk stratification point.

Furthermore, you have to watch out for immunological interference. In this case, an initial Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) or Coombs test was positive for IgG. This led the team to suspect autoimmune hemolytic anemia initially, prompting steroids. The positive Coombs was actually a false positive, likely due to penicillin or the presence of foreign proteins from blood products. This is a classic "pearl": do not let a positive DAT distract you from the smear if schistocytes are present.

The definitive diagnosis was ultimately made via kidney biopsy and follow-up complement testing. The initial C3/C4 levels were normal, which underscores the point that standard complement levels during a TMA crisis don't always reflect the complement dysregulation (in this case, a functional alternative pathway test <60% eventually confirmed the diagnosis). The reduction was noted post-treatment, but the absence of normal complement levels does not rule out CM-HUS, and normal complement levels don't rule it in.

## The Diagnostic Trap of "Other"

One of the most valuable lessons here is the timing. The initial workup focused on DIC and sepsis. The TMA signs appeared *after* the sepsis was improving ("On day 8... clinical picture changed when she developed a sudden isolated drop in haemoglobin"). This dissociation between clinical stability and hematologic collapse is a red flag that the initial sepsis might have been the "second hit" on a triggerable genetic or acquired pathway.

Another trap is the ADAMTS13. It is tempting to see 43.5% and pat yourself on the back for ruling out TTP. But be careful. ADAMTS13 can be mildly reduced in other severe TMAs (maladie auto-immune). However, <10% is the hard cutoff for TTP. What we see here is how easy it is to misdiagnose CM-HUS as TTP or vice versa without the triage tools in hand. If you had treated this as TTP on ADAMTS13 results alone, over 10% would likely allow for observation or different workup, but the severity of the schistocytes usually forces your hand. The key was that ADAMTS13 ruled TTP completely (clearing it for PEX-dependence), while the clinical course confirmed CM-HUS.

# Eculizumab vs. Antibiotics: Navigating the Therapeutic Paradox of CM-HUS During Bacteraemia

TMA is the emergency doctor’s version of a puzzle where every piece seems to contradict the next. You’ve got microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, you’ve got thrombocytopenia, and you’ve got acute kidney injury. The instinct is immediately to treat the thrombotic microangiopathy. But what if the treatment you choose—the very thing that would save their marrow and kidneys—is the thing that precipitates them into another form of medical disaster?

This is the exact paradox faced in a recent case of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by a dog bite. It’s a fascinating picture because it forces us to weigh the immediate life-and-death battle of tissue thrombosis against the subtle, dangerous vulnerability of an encapsulated bacterial species. It’s a reminder that the most rational molecular therapy can sometimes be the most lethal if the context isn’t understood.

## The Shadow of the Dog Bite

Most of us associate dog bites with flesh wounds or prophylactic tetanus shots. In this case, it was the harbinger of a rapid fire collapse. A previously healthy woman in her late 40s was bitten on the lip four days prior and prescribed penicillin, but she never picked it up. Less than a week later, she arrived at the emergency department in septic shock with a mottled coolness to her extremities, severe hypoglycaemia, and refractory to glucose. Her clinical picture screamed sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), complicated by deep acute kidney injury.

The bacteria eventually grew in culture: *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. It’s a slow-growing, encapsulated gram-negative rod normally found in dog mouths. It fits the profile perfectly, but it presents a unique wrinkle in management. While it often causes severe sepsis in asplenic patients, it can invade healthy tissue if the natural complement barriers are compromised or if the pathogen simply gains the upper hand.

After stabilising the sepsis and *C. canimorsus* infection, the patient seemed to do okay for a week. But then the clinical picture shifted. She developed a sudden, isolated drop in haemoglobin, underlying persistent thrombocytopaenia, and worsening renal failure. The lights went on. This wasn't just sepsis anymore; the clinical soundbites of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) were becoming loud and clear. However, it had peeled off the original diagnosis of septic DIC. The diagnosis was narrowed down to CM-HUS, but the path to that tag was paved with diagnostic landmines.

## The Diagnostic Minefield

Distinguishing TMA in the acute setting is often a battle against the clock and the laboratory. In this case, the team was right to consider the differential early. TTP is the first thing you must rule out. In this patient, ADAMTS13 activity was safely above 10% at 43.5%, which made TTP highly unlikely. They also ruled out the classic STEC-HUS by negative PCR for Shiga toxin strains, and the absence of rashes or prodromal diarrhoea made diarrhoeagenic bacterial causes less probable. The immunofluorescence of the kidney biopsy showed no immune complex deposition, ruling out post-infectious glomerulonephritis.

What remains is the hallmark atypical HUS, driven by complement dysregulation. In many cases, you get a reduced C3 or C4, but in functional complement studies, the standard C3/C4 levels remained normal. This is a critical teaching point from the literature. Normal complement levels on a standard panel do not exclude CM-HUS, especially during the active phase. The diagnosis leaned heavily on the clinical picture and the kidney biopsy, showing fibrin thrombi and acute tubular necrosis. It only looked like a classic HUS because the first hit was so evident: schistocytes, low complement activity on functional assays (like AH50), and an anuric kidney despite lack of typical triggers.

## The Red Line: Treating CM-HUS in Sepsis

Here is where the clinical decision-making gets heavy. The standard of care for CM-HUS that is refractory to hydralazine is eculizumab. It is a monoclonal antibody that binds the terminal complement component 5, inhibiting the formation of the membrane attack complex. It effectively stops the immune system from driving endothelial damage. But it also effectively leaves the patient naked against encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria meningitidis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*.

It is now known that *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is also complement-sensitive. The capsule ensures survival in the bloodstream, and once the complement attack is neutralised by the pathogen’s own complement-resistance factors, the bacterium is taken care of by the immune system only when the terminal pathway is intact. Using eculizumab in a septic patient with *Capnocytophaga* is admitting to a medical contradiction. It is like giving a blanket to a patient suffering from cold while standing in a flood. The blanket stops the cold but invites drowning.

In this case report, the clinicians navigated this trap with precision. They resisted the urge to rush eculizumab during the active bacteraemia. While the patient had signs of TMA superimposed on sepsis, they did not want to compromise the terminal immunity that was still trying to clear the *Capnocytophaga*. They did initiate plasma exchange (PEX) as a temporising measure to bridge the clotting issues and reduce the circulating immune burden. This was a temporary fix, providing partial diuresis but failing to stop the microthrombi from forming.

The turn came when the blood cultures showed negativity. Only then did they feel confident that the bacterial burden had decreased. They cleared antibiotics and, ensuring the patient remained protected by prophylactic penicillin and vaccinating against Meningococcus post-recovery, they started the eculizumab. The logic was elegant. By delaying the inhibitor until the infection was suppressed, they protected the patient from a fatal bacteremia while rescuing the kidneys from uncontrolled complement activation. The patient achieved haematological remission and spontaneous diuresis, avoiding the progression to end-stage renal disease.

## Clinical Takeaways

The management of TMA is almost always a race. In TTP, we give plasma immediately. In STEC-HUS, we give supportive care. In atypical HUS, we gather our facts to start complements inhibitors. But this case highlights that context matters more than the trajectory. Here are the key pearls from this complex case.

*   **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS.** Functional assays (like AH50) and clinical exclusion of other causes are often better guides than routine complement panels, especially in the early or acute phase.
*   **Capnocytophaga needs antibiotics.** If you suspect a zoonotic transmission, ensure coverage includes the appropriate gram-negative rod. Do not assume a dog bite is non-infectious.
*   **Timing of eculizumab can be a balancing act.** In cases of bacterial sepsis with overlying TMA, delaying eculizumab until major bacterial sources are controlled may be safer to avoid fulminant bacterial overwhelming sepsis.
*   **TMA can emerge late.** A patient can present with sepsis and DIC and then transition into isolated TMA later in the course once the acute inflammatory markers start waning.
*   **Vaccination is key.** If you block complement, you must ensure rapid protection against encapsulated bacteria. Prophylaxis and vaccination are non-negotiable parts of the post-eculizumab plan.



# Biopsy or Blindly? Determining the Utility of Renal Biopsy in Acquired TMA Diagnoses

From a silly dog bite to full-on dialysis and monoclonal antibody therapy. It sounds like a classic textbook derailment, and in many ways, this case is exactly that. But the workup is where things get spicy. We’re talking normal complement levels, negative ADAMTS13, and a microbiological diagnosis of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a rare trigger for infection-associated Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA).

If your TMA patient has normal C3/C4, are you going blind treat running with Eculizumab, or are you digging in with a renal biopsy? This case of Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS) triggered by *C. canimorsus* forces us to rethink when invasive pathology is actually the tie-breaker in an adult with Microangiopathic Haemolytic Anaemia (MAHA).

## The Clinical Conundrum: When the Serology Stutts

Let’s set the scene. A previously healthy woman, late 40s, bites the dog, the dog bites her. Four days later, she’s a septic endpoint: fever, DIC, pancytopenia, kidney failure. She looks like she needs broad-spectrum antibiotics and/OR vascular support. She’s given Picolin (apixaban? No, Apocillin – penicillin) and Piperacillin/Tazobactam.

By day 8, she’s anuric. She’s in the ICU. Then, things flip. Her sepsis markers start improving. But suddenly, she drops a massive chunk of hemoglobin. No bleeding. Just falling Hb. High LDH, undetectable haptoglobin. Her platelets stay low. The peripheral smear shows 15–20% schistocytes.

Okay, the board call: TMA has struck. Now, you run the numbers.
*   **ADAMTS13 Activity:** 43.5%. Rule out TTP (severe deficiency is <10%). Done.
*   **Streptococcal/Pneumococcal PCR:** SHiga toxin negative. Rule out STEC-HUS. Done.
*   **Autoimmunity:** ANA/ANCA negative.
*   **Complement Profile:** C3 and C4 normal. Functional assays normal.

Here is the kicker: **Normal levels of C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS.** It can easily be normal during the acute phase, especially early in the onset or post-infectious. So, you are left with a TMA in the presence of negative serology and a confirmed but rare pathogen (*C. canimorsus*). The diagnosis hangs on the tissue diagnosis.

## The Renal Biopsy Decision

In the EMERGENCY department, we rarely biopsy TMA. We usually start Eculizumab if the clinical picture screams TTP/C3 glomerulopathy (though often we treat a high score TMA empirically if we can't wait). But this patient was transferred to the nephrology ward. She was stable hemodynamically. She had no indication of TTP, STEC-HUS, or malignant hypertension.

The renal biopsy was the pivot point here. Why was it done?

The case report highlights a critical finding on the lights microscope: **Fibrin thrombi in the glomeruli and arterioles with no immune complex deposition.**

This biopsy served two specific, vital purposes:
1.  **Confirmation of TMA:** It proved that the renal failure was due to microvascular thrombosis, not just ATN or obstruction.
2.  **Exclusion of Primary Glomerulopathy:** Immunofluorescence showed no deposition (likely ruling out immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritis), and electron microscopy showed no primary podocyte disease (like typical FSGS/intrinsic nephritis).

This ruled out several "look-alikes." If there were immune deposits, we'd be looking at Lupus or Pauci-immune Vasculitis. If there were immune complex or complement depositions at high levels, it might suggest a genetic predisposition confirmed by genetics later.

The fact that the biopsy showed TMA (thrombi, crescents, ischemia) without immune complexes in a patient with normal C3/C4 solidified the "Complement-Mediated" hypothesis—specifically acquired, triggered by the infection—rather than an immune-complex disease.

## The Therapeutic Tightrope

Diagnosing the trigger is less urgent than treating the pathology, but it affects you safety. Here we have a classic contradiction.

CM-HUS requires **Eculizumab** (anti-C5).
However, Eculizumab inhibits terminal complement.
In this specific case, *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative rod protected by complement. Without it, increase risk?

But the *also* risk is renal function death.

In this case, the team waited. Antibiotics were effective, blood cultures were negative, then Eculizumab was started. This emphasizes a vital point for trainees:
*   In a septic patient, wait for culture clearance or high clinical stability before complement blockade.
*   If you blast Eculizumab early in active vagocultural sepsis *without* antibiotics, you risk overwhelming bacteremia.

They started Eculizumab in conjunction with vaccination and prophylaxis (Apocillin) and close monitoring. The result? Remission. Remission of hematology and kidney function.

## When to Biopsy in TMA: Some Pearls

So, did we have to biopsy? In this CM-HUS case, the biopsy helped exclude other primary glomerular causes. In TTP (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura), biopsy is rarely helpful as the main presentation is TMA and treatment is PEC. In STEC-HUS, it is often avoided. In acute leaves, it may be needed if atypical diagnoses of GN are needed to check.

Here are the key takeaways from this case report.

*   **Normal complement levels do not rule out complement-mediated TMA.** A negative C3/C4 does not exonerate CM-HUS in the acute setting.
*   **Renal biopsy is diagnostic when serology is silent.** It confirms TMA and specifically excludes primary glomerular nephropathies (like FSGS or immune-complex GN).
*   **Timing differentiates triggers.** Hemolysis appearing *after* sepsis resolves (rather than during the active height of inflammait) is suggestive of an infection-triggered CM-HUS.
*   **Defence mechanisms have trade-offs.** *C. canimorsus* defenses involve complement; removing complement (Eculizumab) requires strict antibiotic coverage and monitoring for encapsulated pathogens.
*   **TMA remains a diagnosis of exclusion.** Always check ADAMTS13 to exclude TTP first (target <10%), rule out Shiga toxin, and confirm ruling out immune-complex GN via biopsy if uncertainty persists.

In a world of high-tech serology, the renal biopsy isn't always dead weight. It’s the only thing that can definitively prove the "fibrin thrombi" are there and nothing else. Don't let "normal blood work" distract you while the kidney does the heavy lifting.

# Hereditary or Triggered? Distinguishing Inherited Complement Defects from Infection-Induced TMA

There is something undeniably terrifying about a healthy 49-year-old presenting with a dog bite and turning into dialysis cases within a week. More importantly for us, there is something intellectually brutal about trying to distinguish between a hereditary autoimmune storm and a triggered inflammatory cascade when the genetic testing comes back negative.

This week, we’re looking at a fascinating case of Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (CM-HUS) that was triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, but notable specifically because the patient lacked the classic germline mutations usually associated with "Atypical HUS" (aHUS). It forces us to confront a frustrating reality in nephrology and hematology: diagnosis of complement-mediated TMA often relies on a process of exclusion, and a negative genetic panel doesn't always rule out the need for eculizumab. But it does force us to ask: If there’s no gene, why are we blocking complement?

The literature has long established that 50–70% of CM-HUS cases harbor genetic variants in complement regulatory proteins like Factor H, I, or B. However, the remaining cases are often termed "acquired" or "triggered." In this specific scenario, does the absence of a variant mean it's not CM-HUS, or does it mean we're looking at the rare, obscure non-idiopathic variants of the disease? Let's break down the diagnostic and therapeutic puzzle using this case report.

## The Initial Hunt: Sepsis vs. TMA vs. TTP

The patient’s initial presentation was indistinguishable from many other forms of severe sepsis. She had DIC, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury following a dog bite. The team wisely covered for *Capnocytophaga* early on with piperacillin/tazobactam, cultures eventually confirmed the pathogen. However, the trajectory shifted.

Initially, the TMA features were obscured by the overwhelming sepsis and DIC. It was only after day 3 in the ICU that the specific markers of TMA emerged: MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening renal failure, with a sudden isolated drop in hemoglobin (111 → 68 g/L).

Before we make a diagnosis of CM-HUS, we must rigorously exclude the big hitters:

1.  **Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP):** Investigate ADAMTS13 activity first. If it's below 10%, treat for TTP immediately. In this patient, it was 43.5%, effectively ruling out classic TTP.
2.  **STEC-HUS:** Enteric pathogens are the most common pediatric cause but rare in adults. A negative Shiga toxin PCR was the green light here.
3.  **Autoimmune Hemolysis:** The patient had a positive DAT (Coombs test) for IgG initially. However, this resolved on repeat testing, suggesting it was likely a false positive from penicillin or prior transfusions, respectively.

The "smoking gun" for CM-HUS was the renal biopsy. It showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and crescent formation, but crucially, **no immune complex deposition.** This pattern distinguishes CM-HUS from immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritis.

## The Genetic Dilemma: Hereditary vs. Acquired

Here is the crux of the clinical education we need to impart. As practitioners, we are trained to think of CM-HUS as a genetic lottery. A patient presents, we assume a germline defect (Factor H, I, or B, HD, Factor B, MCP), and we prescribe eculizumab. But what happens when the panel is clean?

In this patient, extended complement genetic testing revealed **no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants.** This is a "non-carrier" state in the context of the C1, C2, C3, C4, Factor H, Factor I, Factor B, Membrane Cofactor Protein (CD46) panels checked.

This creates a diagnostic gray zone. The authors note that the absence of a mutation weakens the hypothesis of a pre-existing complement defect requiring a "second hit." Instead, this supports the theory of "infection-triggered CM-HUS."

**Why does this matter for the workup?**
1.  **Timing is everything:** The case details a snag. Because of logistics and safety, genetic testing was delayed until *after* treatment (three PEX sessions and one dose of eculizumab). This makes interpreting the functional complement levels risky.
2.  **Functional Blind Spots:** The team measured the alternative pathway activity (AH50). This was markedly reduced (6%). However, the authors rightly suggest this is most consistent with the treatment effects of eculizumab/PEX, not an intrinsic complement depletion or activation. Standard C3/C4 levels often remain normal during the disease course, making reliance on them a trap.
3.  **The Clinical Response:** Because the patient had no genetic variant, the burden of proof shifted. The decision to continue eculizumab was driven by the dramatic clinical improvement (hematologic remission and partial renal recovery) post-blockade.

If we find ourselves in a situation with acute infection, no genetic variants, and classic TMA, the diagnosis becomes one of "**phenocopy by exclusion**." We treat the TMA with eculizumab and observe the response. If hemoptysis, hemolysis, and platelets improve rapidly, we have our answer.

## The Therapeutic Paradox: Blocking Complement in Sepsis

This case highlights a philosophical and safety dilemma that every nephrologist and critical care physician needs to understand. We treat CM-HUS with eculizumab. Eculizumab blocks C5, effectively shutting down the terminal complement cascade.

For an encapsulated organism like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* or *Neisseria meningitidis*, this is risky. The terminal complement system is a weapon WE need to use to kill these bugs. If you block it, you might give them the green light to grow in the blood.

*   **The Safety Protocol:** In this case, the team initiated eculizumab (and vaccines) only **after multiple blood cultures were negative (and cultures were no longer active growth).** They also proceeded with meningococcal vaccination (Menveo/Bexsero) before or alongside the therapy.
*   **The Prophylaxis:** Because of the inherent risk of *Capnocytophaga* resistance without the terminal complement activation, the patient received persistent prophylactic antibiotics (Ampicillin/Apocillin) during therapy.

This reinforces a critical takeaway: If you suspect infection-triggered CM-HUS, you must ensure active bacteremia is cleared before starting definitive complement blockade, rather than switching back and forth between sepsis and TMA management.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Homes

Here is what we can take from this case to keep in our POCKETS next time we see MAHA with AKI:

*   **TMA is a diagnosis of exclusion in adults.** You must rule out TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC-HUS before confirming CM-HUS.
*   **A negative genetic panel does not rule out CM-HUS.** While most aHUS cases have germline mutations (50–70%), a significant subset is acquired. In healthy patients with no family history, think "infection-triggered."
*   **Renal Biopsy is the tie-breaker.** Look for fibrin thrombi and endothelial damage. The absence of immune complex deposition helps differentiate CM-HUS from post-infectious glomerulonephritis.
*   **Don't rely on C3/C4 levels.** Patients with active alternative pathway activation often have normal serum C3/C4. If you missed the TMA signs based on "normal" complement levels, you missed the window.
*   **Bridge, but don't wait.** If the safety profile allows, bridge with PEX while waiting for ADAMTS13 and genetic results, but consider that PEX is transient. Eculizumab provides the sustained blockade needed for CM-HUS.
*   **Timing of Genetic Testing:** Ideally, apply for genetic testing *before* starting eculizumab, as treatment suppresses functional markers. Clinical response can sometimes substitute for a final genetic confirmation in the acute setting.

## Conclusion

This patient’s journey from a dog bite to dialysis dependence is a grim reminder of how quickly a zoonotic infection can spiral into multi-system failure. However, the recovery offers a silver lining. It underscores the power of eculizumab even when the molecular culprit (a genetic mutation) remains unidentified.

In the end, the diagnosis of acquired CM-HUS rests on the clinical response to therapy. If the hemolysis stops and the platelets clean up, the treatment was right, regardless of whether the patient has a genetic Variant. When in doubt about the genetic status, treat the conflict, manage the sepsis, and monitor the hemolysis.
