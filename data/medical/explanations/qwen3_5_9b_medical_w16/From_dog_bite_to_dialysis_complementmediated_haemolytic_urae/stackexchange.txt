Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the immediate post-bite presentation of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection typically evolve, and what is the initial metabolic complication that demands urgent recognition?

In the context of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection following an animal bite, the clinical course often evolves rapidly from localized trauma to fulminant septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulopathy (DIC). As illustrated in this case, significant systemic derangement can manifest approximately four days post-injury. A critical early metabolic warning sign that demands urgent recognition is severe, refractory hypoglycemia. In this patient, glucose failed to respond to bolus administration (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), and this profound hypoglycemia—coupled with lactic acidosis—signaled the overwhelming metabolic burden of sepsis rather than a diabetic emergency or simple starvation.

Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion when encountering Gram-negative sepsis and coagulopathy of unknown origin in a patient with recent animal contact, as the organism is fastidious and blood cultures may remain negative for several days. Furthermore, this case underscores a potentially biphasic evolution: while the initial phase is defined by septic shock and DIC, patients may develop a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (CM-HUS) later in the course, even after initial clinical improvement with antibiotics. Therefore, early broad-spectrum empiric coverage and sustained monitoring for hematologic and renal complications are essential to mitigate high mortality and the risk of end-stage chronic kidney disease associated with developing TMA.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In the context of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), why was the negative detection of Shiga toxin in stool PCR insufficient to confirm a diagnosis of CM-HUS, and what other primary exclusion criteria were reviewed?

In the context of this case, a negative Shiga toxin PCR is insufficient to confirm a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) because it serves a specific exclusionary function: it rules out typical Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC)-HUS. However, ruling out one etiology does not positively establish another; CM-HUS remains primarily a diagnosis of exclusion underpinned by the absence of other known TMAs and clinical response to complement blockade. In this patient, the negative PCR eliminated the infectious trigger most commonly associated with pediatric HUS, but the diagnosis required a broader differential exclusion to account for the presence of an infection-triggered complement dysregulation rather than STEC or TTP.

To confirm the diagnosis, we reviewed three primary exclusion criteria that distinguished CM-HUS from other causes of TMA. First, we must definitively exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which presents with overlapping MAHA and thrombocytopenia. In this case, the patient's ADAMTS13 activity was preserved at 43.5% (normal >10%), effectively ruling out TTP. Second, we excluded autoimmune aetiologies; the patient tested negative for ANA and ANCA, and subsequent renal biopsy immunofluorescence revealed no immune complex deposition. Finally, we distinguished this TMA from sepsis-induced disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). While the initial presentation included DIC features associated with *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia, the emergence of isolated microangiopathic haemolysis after the inflammatory profile and coagulopathy improved supported a superimposed TMA triggered by the infection. Thus, while the negative stool PCR clears the field of STEC, the diagnosis of CM-HUS relies on the preservation of ADAMTS13 activity, exclusion of autoimmune glomerulonephritis, and a biopsy-confirmed TMA phenotype distinct from primary glomerular disease.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the definitive laboratory threshold used to rule out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) in a patient presenting with MaHA and thrombocytopenia?

In the evaluation of a patient with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and thrombocytopenia, the definitive laboratory threshold to rule out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) is an ADAMTS13 activity level greater than 10%.

In the case of **Capnocytophaga canimorsus**-associated complement-mediated HUS described here, the distinction between TTP and other forms of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was critical for management. TTP is a medical emergency requiring urgent plasma exchange, whereas other TMA etiologies, such as this patient's CM-HUS, are typically driven by complement dysregulation and managed with eculizumab or supportive care. As noted in the case report, while the patient initially presented with a constellation of symptoms suggesting sepsis and DIC, the subsequent development of MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia prompted a workup for TTP.

Although clinical triage tools like the PLASMIC score were used to estimate pretest probability during the initial phase, laboratory confirmation is required. In this patient, the ADAMTS13 activity was found to be 43.5%. Because TTP typically presents with ADAMTS13 activity below 10%, a level of 43.5% effectively ruled out TTP and guided the clinical team toward a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS. This distinction was vital, as the patient progressed to dialysis dependence and was treated with eculizumab after the engine was confirmed negative for ADAMTS13 deficiency.

### [Pathophysiology] Why are standard serological complement assays (C3 and C4) frequently normal in patients with complement-mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS), despite underlying alternative pathway dysregulation?

**Answer**

This is an excellent question regarding a critical diagnostic pitfall in thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). In complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), standard serological assays for C3 and C4 are frequently normal, despite underlying dysregulation, primarily because total serum levels do not reliably reflect functional complement pathway activation or localized consumption.

As illustrated in this case, the patient presented with overt CM-HUS physiology—microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection. Despite the clinical diagnosis of alternative pathway dysregulation, initial testing revealed that both C3 and C4 concentrations were within the reference range. This is because the pathogenesis involves unregulated amplification of the alternative pathway rather than a pan-complement depletion detectable by standard immunoassays. In many cases, particularly sporadic infection-triggered CM-HUS as seen here, the consumption of terminal complement components can occur without proportionally lowering the baseline total levels of C3 or C4 in circulation, or homeostatic production may compensate for the loss.

Importantly, the case report emphasizes that standard complement levels should not be used to exclude the diagnosis. Functional assays provide the necessary sensitivity; in this patient, the quantitative alternative pathway activity (AH50), while measuring a different aspect of the pathway than C3/C4 concentration, was markedly reduced (6%). Furthermore, the patient’s clinical outcome upon initiation of eculizumab serves as a therapeutic diagnostic tool, confirming the diagnosis in the context of alternative pathway-dysregulation.

The clinical lesson is precise: do not rule out CM-HUS based on normal C3/C4 levels. In the setting of a TMA following infection, particularly when ADAMTS13 activity is preserved and STEC is excluded, one must assume complement-mediated etiology until proven otherwise and consider functional testing or therapeutic eculizumab even in the presence of normal baseline C3 and C4.

### [Management] Discuss the specific risks of initiating terminal complement inhibition with eculizumab in the setting of active bacteraemia with *Capnocytophaga* species, and what criteria guided the timing of administration in this case?

Initiating terminal complement inhibition with eculizumab in the setting of active *Capnocytophaga* bacteraemia carries a profound risk of invasive infection and overwhelming sepsis. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative bacterium that relies on the terminal complement cascade for effective opsonization and phagocytic clearance. By administering eculizumab without adequate antimicrobial coverage or immunologic protection, you remove the host's primary mechanism for eliminating encapsulated organisms. This can allow the pathogen to proliferate unchecked, potentially transforming controlled sepsis into a fatal, disseminated infection.

In this case, the timing of eculizumab therapy was strictly guided by criteria designed to ensure the bacterial burden was controlled and host defenses were bolstered before pharmacologic blockade was instituted. As documented in the case report, administration was delayed until blood cultures had become negative, confirming that concurrent antibiotic therapy (initially piperacillin/tazobactam, later de-escalated to Apocillin) had successfully cleared detectable bacteraemia. Crucially, eculizumab induction was initiated only following the administration of meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero). Because acquired immunity from vaccination requires time to develop effectively, the clinician prescribed prophylactic Apocillin, explicitly noting that the blockade would be maintained only because vaccines had not been administered for at least two weeks prior to complement inhibition.

This management strategy underscores a vital principle in treating complement-mediated HUS: while early recognition of hemolysis and thrombocytopenia is key for morbidity reduction, clinicians must ensure that the specific infectious trigger is resolved and that complementary prophylactic measures (vaccination and terminal antibiotics) are in place before inhibiting the terminal complement component.

### [Management] What role should plasma exchange (PEX) serve in this specific therapeutic algorithm compared to its definitive role in TTP, and how was its efficacy monitored in this patient?

This case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-induced complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) highlights a critical divergence in therapeutic strategy: while plasma exchange (PEX) serves as the definitive cure in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), its role in CM-HUS is that of a temporizing bridge to initiation of eculizumab.

In TTP, the underlying pathology is the formation of autoantibodies against the ADAMTS13 enzyme, leading to upstream defects in vWF cleavage and massive microthrombi. PEX mechanically clears these autoantibodies, thereby restoring enzyme activity and halting thrombosis. In contrast, CM-HUS is driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically triggered by infection (as with this dog bite). Here, PEX does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation; rather, it provides temporary clearance of circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators to stabilize the hemostatic environment while waiting for complement blockade therapy. Our patient illustrates this clearly; while ADAMTS13 activity was preserved at 43.5%—effectively ruling out TTP—PEX was initiated as bridging therapy prior to confirmed diagnosis and eculizumab induction.

Efficacy of PEX in this specific patient was monitored through a triad of hematology recovery and renal stabilization. Although the patient presented with complete anuria requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, efficacy was explicitly tracked by the return of spontaneous diuresis. Specifically, following the second and third PEX sessions, the patient achieved spontaneous diuresis before the first dose of eculizumab was administered, indicating a transient response to exchange. This was corroborated by the resolution of the microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (rise in platelets to >77×10⁹/L and stabilization of hemoglobin without transfusion) and the clearance of schistocytes on peripheral smear. However, the report emphasizes that sustained remission of both haematological and renal parameters occurred only after the initiation of complement blockade with eculizumab.

The algorithmic lesson here is that in suspected TMA of unknown etiology where ADAMTS13 results are pending or TTP is excluded, PEX acts as a stabilizing measure. Its efficacy is measured by the rapid improvement in renal output (from anuria to diuresis) and correction of cytopenias, but clinicians must recognize this as a temporary bridge to definitive therapy rather than a curative intervention for complement-mediated pathology.

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient presents with hemolytic anemia and a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) but does not respond to corticosteroids. What environmental factors associated with *Capnocytophaga* treatment or transfusion could cause a false-positive DAT result?

In patients presenting with hemolytic anemia and a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) who fail to respond to corticosteroids, clinicians suspect drug-induced interference, specifically from **penicillin antibiotics** or **recent blood transfusions**. Penicillin derivatives can adsorb to red blood cell surfaces, binding IgG and producing a false-positive DAT that mimics autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). Similarly, transfusions can lead to transient reactivity due to alloantibodies or passive transfer of immunoglobulins.

This dynamic is illustrated in the provided case report of a patient with *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia. The patient received phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) for initial prophylaxis and therapeutic treatment. As she progressed to acute kidney injury and developed microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), an initial DAT was positive for IgG. However, hemolysis persisted despite high-dose prednisolone therapy. The clinical team noted that "the initial DAT was likely a false positive from penicillin or prior transfusions," and repeat testing became negative. This finding underscores a critical principle: in the setting of severe sepsis and suspected thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), a steroid-refractory positive DAT should not default to a diagnosis of AIHA. Recognizing penicillin-induced or transfusion-related DAT artifacts allows for the correct identification of the underlying pathology—such as complement-mediated HUS—and appropriate initiation of therapies like eculizumab without unnecessary steroid immunosuppression.

### [Morning Report] Summarize the clinical reasoning behind the delayed diagnosis of CM-HUS, considering that overt microangiopathic hemolysis developed after the initial phase of septic shock and DIC.

The delayed diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) in this case serves as a critical teaching point regarding the phenotypic evolution of infection-triggered thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The core reason for the delay was the **masking of TMA features by the initial overwhelming phase of sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)**.

Initially, the patient’s presentation of shock, massive inflammatory response, and coagulopathy was correctly attributed to bacterial sepsis and DIC. This clinical dominance obscured the early signs of TMA, which often evolve paradoxically *after* the initial septic insult begins to abate. Specifically, overt microangiopathic hemolytic anaemia (MAHA) did not manifest until the patient was clinically stable from the septic shock; this lag likely reflects the activation of the alternative complement pathway, which may peak or cause endothelial damage once the acute inflammatory storm stabilizes.

Furthermore, several diagnostic pitfalls contributed to the delay. First, standard serologic markers (C3 and C4) were normal. In acute infection-triggered CM-HUS, classical complement levels may remain within reference ranges despite active alternative pathway dysregulation, leading clinicians to doubt complement-mediated pathology. Second, a false-positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) led to an erroneous work-up for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA), which was treated with corticosteroids without addressing the underlying TMA seen on peripheral smear (schistocytes).

The definitive breakthrough occurred only when the inflammatory profile normalized. The persistence of thrombocytopenia combined with the sudden, isolated drop in haemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, and confirming schistocytosis—amid a background of renal failure—truly distinguished the TMA from resolving DIC. Ultimately, the diagnosis was secured by a high index of suspicion when infectious resolution was accompanied by *recursive* deterioration of renal function and hemolysis. Clinicians must remain vigilant for late-onset MAHA in patients recovering from severe sepsis, as early recognition of even poorly defined TMA is the strongest predictor for renal recovery and survival in CM-HUS.

### [Management] Given that this patient lacked inherited complement gene variants, what factors support the use of indefinite or long-term eculizumab therapy, and what would potentially allow for discontinuation?

In the context of this case of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, the decision to continue eculizumab long-term, despite the absence of inherited complement gene variants, rests primarily on the severity of the initial renal injury. As noted in the outcome and follow-up, the authors explicitly state that "complement blockade is being continued due to the severity of her disease." The patient presented with dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and anuria. In CM-HUS, the severity of the initial presentation—specifically any requirement for renal replacement therapy—is a potent predictor of long-term renal outcomes and a significant risk factor for TMA relapse. Therefore, even without a genetic predisposition, the initial damage to the glomeruli and endothelium necessitates prolonged surveillance to ensure the vasculopathy does not recur once complement complement blockade is withdrawn.

Conversely, factors that would support discontinuation are centered on the stability of renal function and the absence of active thrombotic microangiopathy. The case report outlines a plan for "reassessment for discontinuation if renal function stabilises." This implies that normalization of serum creatinine and glomerular filtration, alongside sustained hematological remission (absence of schistocytes, normalization of hemoglobin, and platelet counts), are prerequisites for tapering therapy. While the presence of pathogenic complement gene abnormalities would strongly favor indefinite therapy to prevent relapse driven by dysregulated complement activation, their absence in this patient suggests that the trigger (the bacterial infection) may have resolved once the sepsis and TMA screen clear. However, without a genetic driver, the "idiopathic" nature of the trigger still carries a risk of recurrence; thus, discontinuation is not immediate upon withdrawal of the pathogen but requires objective documentation of sustained remission, typically over a defined period (historically 6 to 12 months in guidelines) to ensure the complement system has returned to a state of homeostasis without the need for blockade.
