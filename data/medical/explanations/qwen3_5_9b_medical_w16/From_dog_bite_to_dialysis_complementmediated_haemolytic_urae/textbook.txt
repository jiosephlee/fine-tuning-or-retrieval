## Initial Clinical Syndrome: Bacterial Sepsis and Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation

The clinical presentation of this case provides a critical teaching model for understanding the epidemiology and acute management of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection. This fastidious, gram-negative rod is a normal inhabitant of the oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats, yet it is rarely pathogenic in humans. Invasive infections, which can lead to fulminant sepsis, typically occur following a bite, a scratch, or close contact with the animal. In this instance, the patient’s history of being bitten by her dog four days prior to admission establishes the vector and timeframe. Clinically, the risk of infection is heightened if appropriate prophylaxis is not administered; here, the patient received a tetanus vaccination on the day of the incident but failed to collect her prescribed antibiotic. This delay in treatment removal allowed the organism to proliferate, precipitating the overwhelming septic response observed on admission. It is a fundamental teaching point that while such infections are rare in immunocompetent adults, they carry a high mortality rate, emphasizing the need for a high suspicion of zoonotic pathogens in the context of animal exposure.

The patient’s initial admission reveals the hallmarks of severe bacterial sepsis, specifically a rapid progression to septic shock with significant metabolic derangement. Upon presentation, the patient was alert but clinically septic, exhibiting mottled skin and cool extremities, which are signs of poor peripheral perfusion and vasodilation. A unique and ominous feature of this case was severe hypoglycemia, with a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L that was refractory to standard dosing of oral glucose and glucagon. While hypoglycemia in critically ill patients can be multifactorial, such a profound and resistant level suggests a severe state of systemic stress and exhaustion of metabolic reserves in the context of sepsis. Concurrently, the patient presented with lactic acidosis, characterized by a lactate level of 5.7 mmol/L and a pH of 7.27. These findings, alongside the systemic inflammation and acute organ dysfunction, confirmed a diagnosis of septic shock, which was further supported by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1/3, indicating clinical deterioration despite potential compensatory mechanisms.

Laboratory analysis on admission uncovered a profound constellation of hematologic and renal derangements that initially pointed toward Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia. The workup revealed marked pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and acute kidney injury, alongside elevated liver enzymes. In the context of severe sepsis, the observed thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy are consistent with the consumption of clotting factors and platelets driven by an uncontrolled inflammatory cascade. The initial clinical interpretation was that these abnormalities represented early DIC driven by unknown pathogens, though *C. canimorsus* was suspected due to the clinical history. This highlights a common diagnostic challenge: the sluggish growth of *Capnocytophaga* often delays culture confirmation, potentially delaying targeted therapy. Consequently, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was initiated immediately to cover zoonotic pathogens, a standard management principle when etiology is unclear but the clinical suspicion is high.

Understanding the distinction between sepsis-induced DIC and Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) is vital for interpreting this phase of the clinical course. While DIC involves systemic consumption of coagulation pathways and platelets due to a widespread clotting response, TMA is characterized by the formation of microthrombi within the endothelium that predominantly causes microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and end-organ injury. In the initial phase of this patient’s care, the overwhelming signs of infection and systemic coagulopathy masked the developing microvascular pathology. The pancytopenia and coagulopathy were attributed to DIC and sepsis. However, the pathophysiology of DIC and TMA are distinct yet overlapping processes; early infection can trigger endothelial stress. It is in this initial syndrome that the sepsis masked the complement-mediated pathology. Clinically, the patient was stabilized with transfusions and broad-spectrum antibiotics in parallel with source control for the dog bite and septic shock, setting the stage for the subsequent differentiation of evolving microthrombi from systemic clotting consumption as her inflammatory metrics improved.

## Diagnostic Pivot: Recognizing and Defining Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA)

The initial management of this patient centered on the recovery from severe sepsis, Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), and fluid resuscitation, all of which are treatable with standardized antimicrobial and supportive measures. However, a critical diagnostic pivot occurred when the patient, despite improved inflammatory markers, developed isolated, worsening haemolysis and persistent renal failure. In patients presenting with sepsis, the clinical picture is often dominated by systemic inflammatory response, which can mask the specific microvascular pathology of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The distinction here lies in the trajectory of organ dysfunction: while the patient’s fever and leukocytosis improved with antibiotics, her kidney function deteriorated into anuria, and she developed microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). Recognizing this dissociation—specifically the return of oedema and haematological instability while sepsis resolved—is key. Clinicians must monitor for sudden drops in haemoglobin, rising reticulocytes, and unexplained thrombocytopenia persisting after the initial septic phase, as these are red flags that suggest a superimposed TMA rather than sepsis alone.

Establishing the diagnosis of TMA requires demonstrating MAHA, defined by the presence of schistocytes, which represent red blood cells fragmented within the microvasculature. In this case, the laboratory provided compelling evidence of MAHA through a peripheral smear showing 15% to 20% schistocytes concurrent with a rapid, isolated drop in haemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over six days. Supporting markers included undetectable haptoglobin and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), confirming ongoing intravascular hemolysis. Importantly, the initial direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG, which initially suggested autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. However, recognizing that DATs can yield false positives in the setting of penicillin treatment or transfusion was crucial. Repeat testing confirmed these as false positives, leaving MAHA as the primary haematological etiology. The persistence of thrombocytopenia despite the clearance of the initial septic drive further solidified the suspicion of a microvascular occlusive process requiring targeted intervention.

Before committing to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), clinicians must rigorously exclude its major mimics, specifically thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin-producing *Escherichia coli* (STEC)-associated HUS. This diagnostic exclusion is standard practice in TMA workups to ensure the most reversible or fatal causes are not missed. TTP is distinguished by severe deficiency of the ADAMTS13 enzyme, which must be below 10%. In this patient, ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, rendering TTP highly unlikely. Conversely, STEC-HUS is defined by a prodrome of diarrheal illness and a positive stool PCR for Shiga toxin. The patient’s history lacked prodromal enteric symptoms, and the PCR returned negative, effectively ruling out STEC-HUS. By systematically excluding these two conditions, the clinical team narrowed the differential diagnosis to a secondary or idiopathic TMA, where infection-triggered dysregulation became the primary consideration.

The definitive pathological confirmation of the diagnosis came through renal biopsy and complement analysis, both of which presented educational nuances regarding TMA diagnosis. The kidney biopsy revealed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli, fibrin thrombi, and crescent formation, hallmarks of thrombotic microangiopathy that mimics the findings of typical TTP or HUS. However, immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, which distinguished this case from post-infectious glomerulonephritis. Complement level analysis provided another teaching point: basic C3 and C4 levels were within normal limits both before and after initial therapy. Clinicians must understand that normal C3/C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS, as complement levels may remain stable during the acute phase or due to consumption rates varying in inflammatory states. This was further reinforced by extended functional assays, which showed reduced alternative pathway activity that likely reflected the treatment response with eculizumab and plasma exchange rather than a pre-existing hereditary defect in Factor H, B, or I.

Ultimately, this diagnostic sequence illustrates that TMA in the context of sepsis requires a dynamic reassessment rather than a static diagnosis. The initial confusion between DIC and TMA is common, but the hallmark of this case was the persistence of haemolysis and thrombocytopenia despite sepsis control. The diagnostic process prioritized safety by systematically ruling out TTP and STEC-HUS before targeting the complement system. This approach ensures that expensive, specific therapies like eculizumab are utilized appropriately. For the learner, the key takeaway is that renal biopsy remains the gold standard for pathological confirmation of TMA, and complement inhibitor therapy should be initiated based on a strong clinical suspicion of infection-triggered complement dysregulation, with full awareness that a negative genetic or C3/C4 test does not exclude the diagnosis when the clinical and histological picture is consistent.

## Pathophysiological Mechanisms of Infection-Triggered CM-HUS

Understanding the pathophysiology of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) requires a clear appreciation of the alternative complement pathway and its dysregulation. The alternative pathway is a crucial component of the innate immune system that continuously monitors for pathogens and damaged cells. Under healthy conditions, it is tightly regulated by surface proteins such as factor H and C4b-binding protein. In CM-HUS, this regulatory system is overwhelmed or ineffective, leading to unbridled activation of the alternative pathway. This results in a massive generation of C3 convertase, C3b deposition, and the formation of membrane attack complexes. Without careful restraint, the complement system cleaves complement component 3 into C3b perpetually, creating a destructive environment that damages endothelial cells throughout the vasculature. This endothelial injury precipitates platelet aggregation and fibrin deposition, forming the characteristic microthrombi of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) seen in this patient’s renal biopsy and peripheral smear.

A critical concept in interpreting this diagnosis is the distinction between inherited and acquired triggers. While 50% to 70% of CM-HUS patients harbour genetic variants in complement regulatory proteins such as factors H, I, and B, clinical manifestation often requires an external "second hit" to breach the threshold of clinical disease. In this case, extended genetic testing revealed no pathogenic variants, identifying a subset of patients with a functional defect often termed infection-triggered CM-HUS. This patient’s highly relevant medical history, characterized by no prior complement predisposition or family history of similar conditions, supports the theory of "acquired" CM-HUS where the infection itself acted as the sufficient trigger following the dog bite. This highlights that the absence of a genetic diagnosis does not rule out the diagnosis of CM-HUS, particularly when the clinical presentation and treatment response perfectly align with complement blockade.

The specific interaction between *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* and the host immune system presents a unique pathophysiological paradox that explains the causality in this patient. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated, gram-negative rod with variable serum susceptibility to complement-mediated lysis. While the complement system is essential for clearing encapsulated bacteria like *C. canimorsus* and *Neisseria* species, the activation required to achieve clearance can inadvertently drive the very pathogenesis causing TMA. In instances of host complement deficiency or dysregulation, the activation cascade becomes unregulated rather than contained. This case illustrates how a virulence factor—the bacterial capsule—normally protecting the pathogen from phagocytosis and complement lysis may, in a susceptible host, drive a runaway inflammatory response. The report notes that *C. canimorsus* can activate complement pathways, particularly in susceptible hosts, creating a clinical scenario where the body's defense mechanism becomes the primary driver of organ damage.

Diagnostic interpretation in cm-HUS often relies heavily on a negative search for alternative causes and the exclusion of standard markers. A common pitfall for learners is assuming that low complement levels are a prerequisite for diagnosis. However, as observed in this case, standard complement assays C3 and C4 are frequently within normal limits during the acute phase of CM-HUS. The case documentation explicitly noted that local C3 and C4 levels were normal, and standard functional assays for the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways remained within reference ranges until extended testing was performed post-treatment initiation. This occurs because the alternative pathway consumes C3 rapidly, but under conditions where the pathway is not primarily depleted but rather dysregulated (as is the case with acquired triggers), total complement levels may remain stable due to compensatory liver synthesis. Consequently, the absence of hypocomplementemia should not preclude the diagnosis of CM-HUS, which is ultimately a clinical and histopathological diagnosis of exclusion.

The therapeutic strategy necessitates balancing the benefits of complement blockade against the risks of impaired bacterial defense. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component C5, effectively suppresses the formation of the membrane attack complex, halting the hemolysis and thrombosis responsible for the TMA syndrome. However, the use of terminal complement inhibitors leaves the host vulnerable to infections from encapsulated bacteria. This is clinically relevant in the management of a patient bitten by a dog, as *C. canimorsus* specifically relies on the alternative pathway for killing. The case team navigated this dilemma by initiating eculizumab only after blood cultures became negative, minimizing the risk of uncontrolled bacteraemia. Additionally, antimicrobial prophylaxis and vaccination were implemented to mitigate the risk of *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga* infection post-treatment, illustrating the complex interplay between immunologic suppression and host defense mechanics.

## Therapeutic Dilemma: Complement Blockade During Active Bacteraemia

The central therapeutic challenge in this case involves navigating the paradox of simultaneously protecting host immunity while blocking complement-mediated tissue damage. In patients with complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), the prompt administration of eculizumab is generally indicated to halt the progression of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and renal failure. However, in the context of an active or recent invasive infection with an encapsulated organism, such as *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, the risk profile shifts significantly. Eculizumab targets terminal complement component C5, which is essential for the formation of the membrane attack complex required to lyse bacteria. Prior to the stability of blood cultures in this patient, the clinical team had to balance the dangers of fulminant complement-dependent bacteremia against the irreversible nature of untreated TMA. The decision to delay complement blockade until blood cultures were negative and pancytopenia resolved demonstrates a prioritization of definitive bacterial clearance before initiating immunodeficiency-prone therapy, reflecting a cautious risk-benefit analysis common in high-stakes TMA cases.

Accurately assessing the infectious risk requires an understanding of the specific pathogen’s virulence mechanisms in the context of complement blockade. *C. canimorsus* is a Gram-negative, encapsulated rod that typically evades the immune system through complement resistance mechanisms, relying heavily on terminal complement activity for phagocyte protection. While *Neisseria meningitidis* is the organism most famously associated with eculizumab-associated rapid sepsis, *C. canimorsus* carries a comparable risk profile for meningococcal-like fulminant sepsis, particularly in the absence of adequate terminal complement activity. In the absence of asplenia or profound immunosuppression, the diagnosis remains difficult, but prior case reports, including the discussion of patients with pre-existing genetic complement variants, suggest that the risk of death from bacterial infection may be mitigated if antimicrobial coverage is robust while bridging therapy is initiated. In this instance, the patient was already considered stable enough for de-escalation and prophylaxis once bacteremia resolved, which was confirmed by negative cultures.

The management of TMA during this acute phase necessitates a bridging strategy, as plasma exchange (PEX) offers temporary physiological stabilization without correcting the underlying dysregulation. In this patient, PEX was initiated prior to the initiation of eculizumab while waiting for diagnostic clarification, likely to reduce complement fragment burden or inflammatory mediators. Consistent with the pharmacological limitations of PEX in CM-HUS, the patient’s renal function improved spontaneously after the final PEX sessions, suggesting that while PEX provided a window of fluid balance, it was not sufficient to halt the haemolysis long-term. The definitive resolution of thrombocytopenia and haemolysis occurred only with the introduction of eculizumab, highlighting that while exchange therapy can serve as a valuable bridge to prevent death from microthrombi during the diagnostic workup, it does not provide the durable haematological remission offered by complement blockade.

To sustain eculizumab therapy safely, meticulous attention must be paid to passive and active immunity against encapsulated bacteria. Vaccination protocols dictate that meningococcal conjugate and serogroup B vaccines should ideally be administered at least two weeks prior to starting complement blockade to ensure adequate antibody response; however, the case illustrates a pragmatic approach where vaccination was administered shortly before induction due to urgency. To compensate for the suboptimal timing of vaccine administration, the patient received prophylactic penicillin (Apocillin) alongside eculizumab to cover the risk of contamination or breakthrough infection. This strategy, alongside the continued use of specific antibiotics (ampicillin) based on culture sensitivities, underscores the necessity of concomitant antibacterial coverage. The patient remained on maintenance eculizumab for four months with strict surveillance, demonstrating that aggressive infection control and vaccine planning are non-negotiable prerequisites for the safe long-term use of complement inhibitors in clinical practice.

## Prognostic Factors and Clinical Implications

The clinical trajectory of this patient, as well as the broader population of patients with complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), underscores a dissociation between hematological and renal recovery. In this specific case, the patient achieved hematological remission and adequate urine output relatively quickly following the initiation of eculizumab therapy, a crucial distinction from her renal prognosis. While her hematological parameters stabilized with immunological blocking of C5, her serum creatinine remained elevated at 175 µmol/L, indicating that she remains at risk for end-stage kidney disease. This pattern illustrates a fundamental principle in TMA management: hematological remission often precedes renal recovery, and the persistence of renal impairment despite hematological clearance is not uncommon. Consequently, clinicians must anticipate a slow and often incomplete recovery of renal function even when hematological metrics suggest a favorable response to treatment.

The timing of eculizumab therapy proved to be the most significant prognostic factor in this case, aligning with evidence that delayed initiation correlates with higher risks of end-stage kidney disease. The decision to administer complement blockade was strategic; while plasma exchange provided transient stabilization and returned diuresis, sustained recovery only occurred after the first doses of eculizumab. Crucially, the therapy was initiated after blood cultures became negative, which mitigated the risk of overwhelming infection associated with terminal complement inhibition against encapsulated bacteria like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. This emphasizes that successful management requires balancing the urgent need to halt endothelial damage in TMA with the safety imperative of maintaining host defense against infection.

Long-term management of patients on complement inhibitors centers on infection risk mitigation and personalized therapy duration. In this patient, blood prevention measures were rigorous, including vaccination with meningococcal serogroups A, C, Y, W, and B, and the administration of prophylactic penicillin during the induction phase. While the absence of confirmed pathogenic complement gene variants complicates the decision-making process for discontinuing therapy, the severity of the clinical presentation—with initial anuria and dialysis dependence—warrants extended monitoring. Clinical guidelines suggest that while the absence of genetic defects in CFH, CFI, or CFB may support a trial of discontinuation, careful reassessment of renal function and signs of relapse over 6 to 12 months is essential before stopping maintenance therapy indefinitely.

A critical lesson from this case is the necessity of identifying rare infectious triggers in undifferentiated thrombotic microangiopathy. The initial septic shock presentation masked the underlying CM-HUS, likely due to the dominance of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and sepsis-mediated organ dysfunction. This case highlights that when TMA occurs in the context of an infection, clinicians should remain vigilant for the superimposition of secondary HUS, even if the initial workup focuses primarily on the sepsis. Delay in recognizing the complement-mediated component can lead to missed opportunities for early intervention, resulting in the irreversible renal fibrosis observed in many delayed presentations.

Finally, accurate diagnostic workup serves as the foundation for prognostic stratification, utilizing specific clinical and laboratory criteria to rule out differentials. In this patient’s workup, the PLASMIC score was used to identify a low probability of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which was confirmed by an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%. Screening for Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* via stool PCR was also negative, satisfying diagnostic criteria for STEC-HUS exclusion. The integration of these tools—ADAMTS13 quantification, serological testing for Shiga toxin, and clinical scoring systems—ensures that TMA is correctly attributed to complement dysregulation. While this patient did not present with the classical triad of HUS at onset, the application of these tools allowed for the precise classification of infection-triggered CM-HUS, facilitating the targeted use of eculizumab.
