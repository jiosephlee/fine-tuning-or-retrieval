Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Eculizumab vs. Antibiotics: Navigating the Therapeutic Paradox of CM-HUS During Bacteraemia

TMA is the emergency doctor’s version of a puzzle where every piece seems to contradict the next. You’ve got microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, you’ve got thrombocytopenia, and you’ve got acute kidney injury. The instinct is immediately to treat the thrombotic microangiopathy. But what if the treatment you choose—the very thing that would save their marrow and kidneys—is the thing that precipitates them into another form of medical disaster?

This is the exact paradox faced in a recent case of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by a dog bite. It’s a fascinating picture because it forces us to weigh the immediate life-and-death battle of tissue thrombosis against the subtle, dangerous vulnerability of an encapsulated bacterial species. It’s a reminder that the most rational molecular therapy can sometimes be the most lethal if the context isn’t understood.

## The Shadow of the Dog Bite

Most of us associate dog bites with flesh wounds or prophylactic tetanus shots. In this case, it was the harbinger of a rapid fire collapse. A previously healthy woman in her late 40s was bitten on the lip four days prior and prescribed penicillin, but she never picked it up. Less than a week later, she arrived at the emergency department in septic shock with a mottled coolness to her extremities, severe hypoglycaemia, and refractory to glucose. Her clinical picture screamed sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), complicated by deep acute kidney injury.

The bacteria eventually grew in culture: *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. It’s a slow-growing, encapsulated gram-negative rod normally found in dog mouths. It fits the profile perfectly, but it presents a unique wrinkle in management. While it often causes severe sepsis in asplenic patients, it can invade healthy tissue if the natural complement barriers are compromised or if the pathogen simply gains the upper hand.

After stabilising the sepsis and *C. canimorsus* infection, the patient seemed to do okay for a week. But then the clinical picture shifted. She developed a sudden, isolated drop in haemoglobin, underlying persistent thrombocytopaenia, and worsening renal failure. The lights went on. This wasn't just sepsis anymore; the clinical soundbites of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) were becoming loud and clear. However, it had peeled off the original diagnosis of septic DIC. The diagnosis was narrowed down to CM-HUS, but the path to that tag was paved with diagnostic landmines.

## The Diagnostic Minefield

Distinguishing TMA in the acute setting is often a battle against the clock and the laboratory. In this case, the team was right to consider the differential early. TTP is the first thing you must rule out. In this patient, ADAMTS13 activity was safely above 10% at 43.5%, which made TTP highly unlikely. They also ruled out the classic STEC-HUS by negative PCR for Shiga toxin strains, and the absence of rashes or prodromal diarrhoea made diarrhoeagenic bacterial causes less probable. The immunofluorescence of the kidney biopsy showed no immune complex deposition, ruling out post-infectious glomerulonephritis.

What remains is the hallmark atypical HUS, driven by complement dysregulation. In many cases, you get a reduced C3 or C4, but in functional complement studies, the standard C3/C4 levels remained normal. This is a critical teaching point from the literature. Normal complement levels on a standard panel do not exclude CM-HUS, especially during the active phase. The diagnosis leaned heavily on the clinical picture and the kidney biopsy, showing fibrin thrombi and acute tubular necrosis. It only looked like a classic HUS because the first hit was so evident: schistocytes, low complement activity on functional assays (like AH50), and an anuric kidney despite lack of typical triggers.

## The Red Line: Treating CM-HUS in Sepsis

Here is where the clinical decision-making gets heavy. The standard of care for CM-HUS that is refractory to hydralazine is eculizumab. It is a monoclonal antibody that binds the terminal complement component 5, inhibiting the formation of the membrane attack complex. It effectively stops the immune system from driving endothelial damage. But it also effectively leaves the patient naked against encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria meningitidis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*.

It is now known that *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is also complement-sensitive. The capsule ensures survival in the bloodstream, and once the complement attack is neutralised by the pathogen’s own complement-resistance factors, the bacterium is taken care of by the immune system only when the terminal pathway is intact. Using eculizumab in a septic patient with *Capnocytophaga* is admitting to a medical contradiction. It is like giving a blanket to a patient suffering from cold while standing in a flood. The blanket stops the cold but invites drowning.

In this case report, the clinicians navigated this trap with precision. They resisted the urge to rush eculizumab during the active bacteraemia. While the patient had signs of TMA superimposed on sepsis, they did not want to compromise the terminal immunity that was still trying to clear the *Capnocytophaga*. They did initiate plasma exchange (PEX) as a temporising measure to bridge the clotting issues and reduce the circulating immune burden. This was a temporary fix, providing partial diuresis but failing to stop the microthrombi from forming.

The turn came when the blood cultures showed negativity. Only then did they feel confident that the bacterial burden had decreased. They cleared antibiotics and, ensuring the patient remained protected by prophylactic penicillin and vaccinating against Meningococcus post-recovery, they started the eculizumab. The logic was elegant. By delaying the inhibitor until the infection was suppressed, they protected the patient from a fatal bacteremia while rescuing the kidneys from uncontrolled complement activation. The patient achieved haematological remission and spontaneous diuresis, avoiding the progression to end-stage renal disease.

## Clinical Takeaways

The management of TMA is almost always a race. In TTP, we give plasma immediately. In STEC-HUS, we give supportive care. In atypical HUS, we gather our facts to start complements inhibitors. But this case highlights that context matters more than the trajectory. Here are the key pearls from this complex case.

*   **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS.** Functional assays (like AH50) and clinical exclusion of other causes are often better guides than routine complement panels, especially in the early or acute phase.
*   **Capnocytophaga needs antibiotics.** If you suspect a zoonotic transmission, ensure coverage includes the appropriate gram-negative rod. Do not assume a dog bite is non-infectious.
*   **Timing of eculizumab can be a balancing act.** In cases of bacterial sepsis with overlying TMA, delaying eculizumab until major bacterial sources are controlled may be safer to avoid fulminant bacterial overwhelming sepsis.
*   **TMA can emerge late.** A patient can present with sepsis and DIC and then transition into isolated TMA later in the course once the acute inflammatory markers start waning.
*   **Vaccination is key.** If you block complement, you must ensure rapid protection against encapsulated bacteria. Prophylaxis and vaccination are non-negotiable parts of the post-eculizumab plan.
