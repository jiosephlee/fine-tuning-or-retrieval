Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Triaging TMA: Using PLASMIC Scores and ADAMTS13 to Avoid TTP Misdiagnosis

## The Patient: A Dog Bite That Kept On Giving

Imagine this scenario: You present a sudden fever, hypoglycemia, and septic shock after a dog bite. You treat it aggressively with piperacillin-tazobactam, her fever breaks, her inflammatory markers start to fall, and you start to feel like the worst case of the weekend is behind you. Then, three days later, you get a call. Her hemoglobin has plummeted, her platelets are stuck at 30, and her urine output has halted completely. You order a peripheral smear. You are screaming. There are schistocytes. Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) has just sprung up out of nowhere.

This is exactly the trajectory of the case by Fredriksen et al, published in *BMJ Case Reports*. The patient was a previously healthy 40-something woman who, four days post-bite, was initially fighting a *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis. Her course was terrifying at first—DIC, acute kidney injury, anuric renal failure. You would think this is the end of recovery, yet she stumbled on her feet (metaphorically). But then, the clinical picture pivoted. After initial improvement, she developed isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. It wasn't the sepsis anymore; it was a Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) masquerading as a complication of recovery.

For the ED and ICU physician, TMA is a high-stakes triage. Is it Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP)? Is it Hemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (HUS)? Is it drug-induced or just a complicated sepsis? In this case, distinguishing TTP from Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS) was critical, as the treatments diverge significantly, and the risks of therapy (like eculizumab) are non-trivial, especially in the setting of active bacterial infection.

## The Waiting Game: Waiting for ADAMTS13 is Not Staring into the Dark

The gold standard to rule out TTP is testing ADAMTS13 activity. In primary TTP, this enzyme is usually severely deficient, <10% of normal. Unfortunately, you can't get this back immediately. It often takes 5 to 7 days for results. In the interim, you have a patient with MAHA (Schistocytes), low platelets, and potential renal failure. Do you proceed to full-dose PEX (Plasma Exchange) immediately? Do you start eculizumab? Do you wait?

This is where the PLASMIC score comes in. It's not just a buzzword; it's a decision tool to stratify the risk of TTP when you are waiting for confirmatory testing. The PLASMIC score (Platelets, Lactate, Attending, Mortality, Infection, Creatinine) assigns points based on clinical and laboratory data.

In our case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7. The authors noted this as a "low pretest probability of TTP." While the formal risk calculation requires a complex formula, the takeaway for the clinician is vital: a low PLASMIC score coupled with normal inflammatory markers in the right context shifts the diagnosis away from primary TTP. In this specific patient, the score was 4/7, and the ADAMTS13 activity result eventually came back at 43.5%. That 43.5% is completely above the level of suspicion for TTP (<10%).

However, the PLASMIC score doesn't just say "Do you have TTP."; it says "Is TTP the leading cause?" If the score is low and ADAMTS13 is preserved, you must broaden your differential. Is this STEC-HUS? CM-HUS? Or TMA from an infection? In this report, the diagnosis shifted once Shiga toxin was ruled out. The TMA was secondary to the *Capnocytophaga*.

## The Therapeutic Tightrope: PEX, Eculizumab, and *C. canimorsus*

When you diagnose TMA, the treatment paradigm depends on the cause. If it is TTP, PEX is the life-saving intervention. If it is HUS (especially infection-triggered CM-HUS), the management is controversial.

In our patient, PEX was initiated as a "bridging therapy" before the diagnosis of CM-HUS was cemented. This is standard practice when you have a TMA but the etiology isn't fully clear. However, the report highlights a specific danger that ED and phenicologists need to remember: Eculizumab. Eculizumab blocks terminal complement. While it is the treatment for CM-HUS, it blocks opsonization of encapsulated bacteria. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is encapsulated.

The authors walked a fine line. They started Eculizumab 8 days after the final PEX session and 4 days after the first dose, but they had ensured the blood cultures were negative. If you start complement blockade before the sepsis is cleared, you are playing with fire. This patient had antibacterial therapy broad-spectrum antibiotics (Tazocin, then Apocillin) covering *Capnocytophaga*, but the key was that cultures were negative before the eculizumab induction. This is a crucial risk stratification point.

Furthermore, you have to watch out for immunological interference. In this case, an initial Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) or Coombs test was positive for IgG. This led the team to suspect autoimmune hemolytic anemia initially, prompting steroids. The positive Coombs was actually a false positive, likely due to penicillin or the presence of foreign proteins from blood products. This is a classic "pearl": do not let a positive DAT distract you from the smear if schistocytes are present.

The definitive diagnosis was ultimately made via kidney biopsy and follow-up complement testing. The initial C3/C4 levels were normal, which underscores the point that standard complement levels during a TMA crisis don't always reflect the complement dysregulation (in this case, a functional alternative pathway test <60% eventually confirmed the diagnosis). The reduction was noted post-treatment, but the absence of normal complement levels does not rule out CM-HUS, and normal complement levels don't rule it in.

## The Diagnostic Trap of "Other"

One of the most valuable lessons here is the timing. The initial workup focused on DIC and sepsis. The TMA signs appeared *after* the sepsis was improving ("On day 8... clinical picture changed when she developed a sudden isolated drop in haemoglobin"). This dissociation between clinical stability and hematologic collapse is a red flag that the initial sepsis might have been the "second hit" on a triggerable genetic or acquired pathway.

Another trap is the ADAMTS13. It is tempting to see 43.5% and pat yourself on the back for ruling out TTP. But be careful. ADAMTS13 can be mildly reduced in other severe TMAs (maladie auto-immune). However, <10% is the hard cutoff for TTP. What we see here is how easy it is to misdiagnose CM-HUS as TTP or vice versa without the triage tools in hand. If you had treated this as TTP on ADAMTS13 results alone, over 10% would likely allow for observation or different workup, but the severity of the schistocytes usually forces your hand. The key was that ADAMTS13 ruled TTP completely (clearing it for PEX-dependence), while the clinical course confirmed CM-HUS.
