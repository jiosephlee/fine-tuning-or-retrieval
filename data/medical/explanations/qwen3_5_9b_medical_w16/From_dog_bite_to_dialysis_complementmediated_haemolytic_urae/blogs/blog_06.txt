Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Hereditary or Triggered? Distinguishing Inherited Complement Defects from Infection-Induced TMA

There is something undeniably terrifying about a healthy 49-year-old presenting with a dog bite and turning into dialysis cases within a week. More importantly for us, there is something intellectually brutal about trying to distinguish between a hereditary autoimmune storm and a triggered inflammatory cascade when the genetic testing comes back negative.

This week, we’re looking at a fascinating case of Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (CM-HUS) that was triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, but notable specifically because the patient lacked the classic germline mutations usually associated with "Atypical HUS" (aHUS). It forces us to confront a frustrating reality in nephrology and hematology: diagnosis of complement-mediated TMA often relies on a process of exclusion, and a negative genetic panel doesn't always rule out the need for eculizumab. But it does force us to ask: If there’s no gene, why are we blocking complement?

The literature has long established that 50–70% of CM-HUS cases harbor genetic variants in complement regulatory proteins like Factor H, I, or B. However, the remaining cases are often termed "acquired" or "triggered." In this specific scenario, does the absence of a variant mean it's not CM-HUS, or does it mean we're looking at the rare, obscure non-idiopathic variants of the disease? Let's break down the diagnostic and therapeutic puzzle using this case report.

## The Initial Hunt: Sepsis vs. TMA vs. TTP

The patient’s initial presentation was indistinguishable from many other forms of severe sepsis. She had DIC, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury following a dog bite. The team wisely covered for *Capnocytophaga* early on with piperacillin/tazobactam, cultures eventually confirmed the pathogen. However, the trajectory shifted.

Initially, the TMA features were obscured by the overwhelming sepsis and DIC. It was only after day 3 in the ICU that the specific markers of TMA emerged: MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening renal failure, with a sudden isolated drop in hemoglobin (111 → 68 g/L).

Before we make a diagnosis of CM-HUS, we must rigorously exclude the big hitters:

1.  **Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP):** Investigate ADAMTS13 activity first. If it's below 10%, treat for TTP immediately. In this patient, it was 43.5%, effectively ruling out classic TTP.
2.  **STEC-HUS:** Enteric pathogens are the most common pediatric cause but rare in adults. A negative Shiga toxin PCR was the green light here.
3.  **Autoimmune Hemolysis:** The patient had a positive DAT (Coombs test) for IgG initially. However, this resolved on repeat testing, suggesting it was likely a false positive from penicillin or prior transfusions, respectively.

The "smoking gun" for CM-HUS was the renal biopsy. It showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and crescent formation, but crucially, **no immune complex deposition.** This pattern distinguishes CM-HUS from immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritis.

## The Genetic Dilemma: Hereditary vs. Acquired

Here is the crux of the clinical education we need to impart. As practitioners, we are trained to think of CM-HUS as a genetic lottery. A patient presents, we assume a germline defect (Factor H, I, or B, HD, Factor B, MCP), and we prescribe eculizumab. But what happens when the panel is clean?

In this patient, extended complement genetic testing revealed **no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants.** This is a "non-carrier" state in the context of the C1, C2, C3, C4, Factor H, Factor I, Factor B, Membrane Cofactor Protein (CD46) panels checked.

This creates a diagnostic gray zone. The authors note that the absence of a mutation weakens the hypothesis of a pre-existing complement defect requiring a "second hit." Instead, this supports the theory of "infection-triggered CM-HUS."

**Why does this matter for the workup?**
1.  **Timing is everything:** The case details a snag. Because of logistics and safety, genetic testing was delayed until *after* treatment (three PEX sessions and one dose of eculizumab). This makes interpreting the functional complement levels risky.
2.  **Functional Blind Spots:** The team measured the alternative pathway activity (AH50). This was markedly reduced (6%). However, the authors rightly suggest this is most consistent with the treatment effects of eculizumab/PEX, not an intrinsic complement depletion or activation. Standard C3/C4 levels often remain normal during the disease course, making reliance on them a trap.
3.  **The Clinical Response:** Because the patient had no genetic variant, the burden of proof shifted. The decision to continue eculizumab was driven by the dramatic clinical improvement (hematologic remission and partial renal recovery) post-blockade.

If we find ourselves in a situation with acute infection, no genetic variants, and classic TMA, the diagnosis becomes one of "**phenocopy by exclusion**." We treat the TMA with eculizumab and observe the response. If hemoptysis, hemolysis, and platelets improve rapidly, we have our answer.

## The Therapeutic Paradox: Blocking Complement in Sepsis

This case highlights a philosophical and safety dilemma that every nephrologist and critical care physician needs to understand. We treat CM-HUS with eculizumab. Eculizumab blocks C5, effectively shutting down the terminal complement cascade.

For an encapsulated organism like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* or *Neisseria meningitidis*, this is risky. The terminal complement system is a weapon WE need to use to kill these bugs. If you block it, you might give them the green light to grow in the blood.

*   **The Safety Protocol:** In this case, the team initiated eculizumab (and vaccines) only **after multiple blood cultures were negative (and cultures were no longer active growth).** They also proceeded with meningococcal vaccination (Menveo/Bexsero) before or alongside the therapy.
*   **The Prophylaxis:** Because of the inherent risk of *Capnocytophaga* resistance without the terminal complement activation, the patient received persistent prophylactic antibiotics (Ampicillin/Apocillin) during therapy.

This reinforces a critical takeaway: If you suspect infection-triggered CM-HUS, you must ensure active bacteremia is cleared before starting definitive complement blockade, rather than switching back and forth between sepsis and TMA management.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Homes

Here is what we can take from this case to keep in our POCKETS next time we see MAHA with AKI:

*   **TMA is a diagnosis of exclusion in adults.** You must rule out TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC-HUS before confirming CM-HUS.
*   **A negative genetic panel does not rule out CM-HUS.** While most aHUS cases have germline mutations (50–70%), a significant subset is acquired. In healthy patients with no family history, think "infection-triggered."
*   **Renal Biopsy is the tie-breaker.** Look for fibrin thrombi and endothelial damage. The absence of immune complex deposition helps differentiate CM-HUS from post-infectious glomerulonephritis.
*   **Don't rely on C3/C4 levels.** Patients with active alternative pathway activation often have normal serum C3/C4. If you missed the TMA signs based on "normal" complement levels, you missed the window.
*   **Bridge, but don't wait.** If the safety profile allows, bridge with PEX while waiting for ADAMTS13 and genetic results, but consider that PEX is transient. Eculizumab provides the sustained blockade needed for CM-HUS.
*   **Timing of Genetic Testing:** Ideally, apply for genetic testing *before* starting eculizumab, as treatment suppresses functional markers. Clinical response can sometimes substitute for a final genetic confirmation in the acute setting.

## Conclusion

This patient’s journey from a dog bite to dialysis dependence is a grim reminder of how quickly a zoonotic infection can spiral into multi-system failure. However, the recovery offers a silver lining. It underscores the power of eculizumab even when the molecular culprit (a genetic mutation) remains unidentified.

In the end, the diagnosis of acquired CM-HUS rests on the clinical response to therapy. If the hemolysis stops and the platelets clean up, the treatment was right, regardless of whether the patient has a genetic Variant. When in doubt about the genetic status, treat the conflict, manage the sepsis, and monitor the hemolysis.
