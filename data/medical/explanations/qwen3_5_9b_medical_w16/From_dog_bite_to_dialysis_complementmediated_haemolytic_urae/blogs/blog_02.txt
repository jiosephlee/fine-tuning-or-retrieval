Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# The Sepsis-TMA Lag: Why Thrombotic Microangiopathy Reveals Itself After Initial Recovery

There is a specific kind of diagnostic ghost in the emergency department, one that haunts us when a patient stabilizes but then suddenly deteriorates in an organ-specific way. We often associate with the triad of fever, septic shock, and multiorgan failure. But the timeline matters. When these elements separate, the geriatrician in the room looks for a second hit, but the internist looks for a new bug.

In a fascinating case report by Evason Fredriksen and colleagues, we see exactly this phenomenon. A woman in her late 40s was bitten by a dog, developed fulminant sepsis due to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, crashed into septic shock, and then, almost miraculously, began to recover. But she didn’t fully recover. She developed isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia days later. It wasn't sepsis anymore; it was Complementary-Mediated Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

This case teaches us that TMA isn't always a straight line diagnosis, but one that can hide behind the curtain of DIC and sepsis. It also highlights a terrifying diagnostic window where the inflammation settles, but the complement system goes haywire.

## The Bug, The Bite, and The Blink

Let's back up to the trauma. The patient was bitten on the lip, presumed minor. She didn’t pick up the prescription for penicillin immediately, the incubation for *Capnocytophaga* was 4-day incubation silent, and then *boom*, she was in the ER with a glucose of 0.6 mmol/L and a lactate of 5.7 mmol/L.

This is classic *Capnocytophaga* fulminant sepsis. It’s an encapsulated, gram-negative rod from the dog’s mouth that loves to cause DIC. The initial picture was textbook:
*   Fever > 40 °C
*   Pancytopenia
*   DIC (coagulopathy)
*   AKI
*   Hypoglycaemia (resistant to glucose)

She received broad-spectrum antibiotics (Pip/Tazocin) and was moved to the ICU. It seems she responded *beautifully* to the consortium. Within a week, the cultures were cleared, the inflammatory markers were trending down, and the labs were stabilizing. By day 8 on the ward, she was looking like the patient she was five days prior.

Then, the lights started blinking. Her haemoglobin tanked from 111 to 68 g/L. There was no bleeding. No trauma. No obvious dissection. She developed microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA).

## The Diagnostic Hurdles

Here is where it gets interesting. We think we know TMA. It’s the triad of thrombocytopenia, MAHA, and AKI. But how did we miss it in the first week?

When I see MAHA and thrombocytopenia, I immediately run ADAMTS13 to rule out TTP. In this case, ADAMTS13 was 43.5%. Not 10%. This effectively ruled out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP).

We also had to look for Shiga toxin. STEC-HUS is the most common in kids. In adults, it’s rare. Stool PCR was negative.

Then came the confusing Coombs test (DAT). It came back positive. We assumed autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. We gave prednisone. The haemolysis didn't stop. Then we saw the blood smear. There were 15%–20% schistocytes. This was the TMA confirmation. But the DAT? It was likely a reactive result due to prior transfusions or the penicillin.

The biopsy was the last piece. It showed severe acute tubular necrosis but fibrin thrombi and crescent formation. No immune complex deposition. This was TMA. Since STEC was out and ADAMTS13 was normal, we were left with the diagnosis of CM-HUS.

This case highlights a crucial rule: Normal C3 and C4 levels do *not* rule out complement-mediated disease. The functional assays showed reduced alternative pathway activity later on, but during the acute phase, standard complement levels were normal. It is a diagnosis of exclusion that demands vigilance.

## The Therapeutic Dilemma

This is where the management gets tricky. A patient with active TMA needs immunosuppression or complement blockade. A patient with meningococcal or *Capnocytophaga* bacteremia needs antibiotics and immune surveillance.

Eculizumab (a complement C5 inhibitor) is the standard for CM-HUS. However, it is also the standard paradox. It protects healthy endothelium but may leave open the cell membrane to bacterial invasion. If your patient has *Capnocytophaga*, giving them an antibody that blocks the terminal complement makes them seem *more* infections.

In this case, the team used a bridging strategy. They started Plasmapheresis (PEX) for symptom control. Then, after cultures turned negative and with prophylactic Apocillin, they started Eculizumab (900 mg weekly).

Wait, why did we know the bacterial load was gone? Because the blood cultures were negative. If we had given Eculizumab *during* positive cultures in this case, the balance skews towards disaster.

Despite the risk, the patient started improving. Haematological remission was achieved. The kidney function only partially recovered (creatinine stuck at 175 µmol/L). But the bleeding stopped.

## Key Clinical Pearls

*   **No Sepsis Lag in Infection-Triggered TMA:** While DIC is the immediate threat of *Capnocytophaga* sepsis, a secondary phase of CM-HUS can emerge days later, even when the primary septic shock is resolved.
*   **Ruling Out TTP:** ADAMTS13 > 10% helps you pivot to TMA/HUS workup rather than treating for TTP vertically.
*   **False DATs:** A positive DAT in the context of transfusion and infection may be reactive, not indicative of Evans syndrome.
*   **Normal Complement Levels:** C3/C4 can be normal in disease-active CM-HUS. Do not rule it out based on standard chemistries alone.
*   **PEX Before Eculizumab:** Bridging can be used for stabilization in high-risk patients before starting immunomodulation, provided infection control is in place.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Don't close the book on sepsis too early** when looking at AKI and thrombocytopenia; a second wave of TMA (CM-HUS) is possible days or weeks later.
*   **MAHA + Thrombocytopenia + AKI** after sepsis requires a broad differential that includes infection-triggered HUS, not just DIC or TTP.
*   **Standard C3/C4 levels are not diagnostic** for complement-mediated disease; functional assays and clinical response to therapy are crucial.
*   **For *Capnocytophaga*, treat Eculizumab cautiously:** Ensure bacteremia is controlled with antibiotics and prophylaxis before blocking complement to avoid uncontrolled infection.
*   **Delayed diagnosis is common:** Renal biopsy can help in equivocal cases where immunosuppression fails to halt haemolysis.

This case is a reminder that in medicine, the ghost isn't always the patient dying; sometimes, it's the patient walking you into a second diagnosis. Keep the scope on the patient, the lab, and the biology. Sometimes the second act is the one that keeps the patient alive.
