Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Biopsy or Blindly? Determining the Utility of Renal Biopsy in Acquired TMA Diagnoses

From a silly dog bite to full-on dialysis and monoclonal antibody therapy. It sounds like a classic textbook derailment, and in many ways, this case is exactly that. But the workup is where things get spicy. We’re talking normal complement levels, negative ADAMTS13, and a microbiological diagnosis of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a rare trigger for infection-associated Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA).

If your TMA patient has normal C3/C4, are you going blind treat running with Eculizumab, or are you digging in with a renal biopsy? This case of Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS) triggered by *C. canimorsus* forces us to rethink when invasive pathology is actually the tie-breaker in an adult with Microangiopathic Haemolytic Anaemia (MAHA).

## The Clinical Conundrum: When the Serology Stutts

Let’s set the scene. A previously healthy woman, late 40s, bites the dog, the dog bites her. Four days later, she’s a septic endpoint: fever, DIC, pancytopenia, kidney failure. She looks like she needs broad-spectrum antibiotics and/OR vascular support. She’s given Picolin (apixaban? No, Apocillin – penicillin) and Piperacillin/Tazobactam.

By day 8, she’s anuric. She’s in the ICU. Then, things flip. Her sepsis markers start improving. But suddenly, she drops a massive chunk of hemoglobin. No bleeding. Just falling Hb. High LDH, undetectable haptoglobin. Her platelets stay low. The peripheral smear shows 15–20% schistocytes.

Okay, the board call: TMA has struck. Now, you run the numbers.
*   **ADAMTS13 Activity:** 43.5%. Rule out TTP (severe deficiency is <10%). Done.
*   **Streptococcal/Pneumococcal PCR:** SHiga toxin negative. Rule out STEC-HUS. Done.
*   **Autoimmunity:** ANA/ANCA negative.
*   **Complement Profile:** C3 and C4 normal. Functional assays normal.

Here is the kicker: **Normal levels of C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS.** It can easily be normal during the acute phase, especially early in the onset or post-infectious. So, you are left with a TMA in the presence of negative serology and a confirmed but rare pathogen (*C. canimorsus*). The diagnosis hangs on the tissue diagnosis.

## The Renal Biopsy Decision

In the EMERGENCY department, we rarely biopsy TMA. We usually start Eculizumab if the clinical picture screams TTP/C3 glomerulopathy (though often we treat a high score TMA empirically if we can't wait). But this patient was transferred to the nephrology ward. She was stable hemodynamically. She had no indication of TTP, STEC-HUS, or malignant hypertension.

The renal biopsy was the pivot point here. Why was it done?

The case report highlights a critical finding on the lights microscope: **Fibrin thrombi in the glomeruli and arterioles with no immune complex deposition.**

This biopsy served two specific, vital purposes:
1.  **Confirmation of TMA:** It proved that the renal failure was due to microvascular thrombosis, not just ATN or obstruction.
2.  **Exclusion of Primary Glomerulopathy:** Immunofluorescence showed no deposition (likely ruling out immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritis), and electron microscopy showed no primary podocyte disease (like typical FSGS/intrinsic nephritis).

This ruled out several "look-alikes." If there were immune deposits, we'd be looking at Lupus or Pauci-immune Vasculitis. If there were immune complex or complement depositions at high levels, it might suggest a genetic predisposition confirmed by genetics later.

The fact that the biopsy showed TMA (thrombi, crescents, ischemia) without immune complexes in a patient with normal C3/C4 solidified the "Complement-Mediated" hypothesis—specifically acquired, triggered by the infection—rather than an immune-complex disease.

## The Therapeutic Tightrope

Diagnosing the trigger is less urgent than treating the pathology, but it affects you safety. Here we have a classic contradiction.

CM-HUS requires **Eculizumab** (anti-C5).
However, Eculizumab inhibits terminal complement.
In this specific case, *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative rod protected by complement. Without it, increase risk?

But the *also* risk is renal function death.

In this case, the team waited. Antibiotics were effective, blood cultures were negative, then Eculizumab was started. This emphasizes a vital point for trainees:
*   In a septic patient, wait for culture clearance or high clinical stability before complement blockade.
*   If you blast Eculizumab early in active vagocultural sepsis *without* antibiotics, you risk overwhelming bacteremia.

They started Eculizumab in conjunction with vaccination and prophylaxis (Apocillin) and close monitoring. The result? Remission. Remission of hematology and kidney function.

## When to Biopsy in TMA: Some Pearls

So, did we have to biopsy? In this CM-HUS case, the biopsy helped exclude other primary glomerular causes. In TTP (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura), biopsy is rarely helpful as the main presentation is TMA and treatment is PEC. In STEC-HUS, it is often avoided. In acute leaves, it may be needed if atypical diagnoses of GN are needed to check.

Here are the key takeaways from this case report.

*   **Normal complement levels do not rule out complement-mediated TMA.** A negative C3/C4 does not exonerate CM-HUS in the acute setting.
*   **Renal biopsy is diagnostic when serology is silent.** It confirms TMA and specifically excludes primary glomerular nephropathies (like FSGS or immune-complex GN).
*   **Timing differentiates triggers.** Hemolysis appearing *after* sepsis resolves (rather than during the active height of inflammait) is suggestive of an infection-triggered CM-HUS.
*   **Defence mechanisms have trade-offs.** *C. canimorsus* defenses involve complement; removing complement (Eculizumab) requires strict antibiotic coverage and monitoring for encapsulated pathogens.
*   **TMA remains a diagnosis of exclusion.** Always check ADAMTS13 to exclude TTP first (target <10%), rule out Shiga toxin, and confirm ruling out immune-complex GN via biopsy if uncertainty persists.

In a world of high-tech serology, the renal biopsy isn't always dead weight. It’s the only thing that can definitively prove the "fibrin thrombi" are there and nothing else. Don't let "normal blood work" distract you while the kidney does the heavy lifting.
