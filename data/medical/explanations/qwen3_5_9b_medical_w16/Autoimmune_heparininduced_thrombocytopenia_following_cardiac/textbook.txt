Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Initial Clinical Evaluation and Differential Diagnosis

The clinical history of this patient provides the essential context for understanding the temporal window of his thrombocytopenia. In his 70s, the patient presented with exertional dyspnea and a known history of atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban. His initial evaluation revealed a systolic murmur and moderate aortic stenosis, leading to diagnostic cardiology intervention one week prior to scheduled cardiac surgery. Notably, this preoperative catheterization required anticoagulation with 2000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH) via a transradial approach with an unheld apixaban schedule. Subsequent to this procedure, the patient underwent comprehensive cardiac surgery involving aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta reconstruction under cardiopulmonary bypass, utilizing 45,000 units of UFH for intraoperative anticoagulation with Activated Clotting Time monitoring. This dual exposure to UFH is clinically significant because the standard seroconversion timeline for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) requires a minimum of 5 to 10 days of prior heparin exposure to synthesize a primary immune response. Consequently, the preoperative catheterization exposure allowed for an early onset thrombocytopenia without waiting for the typical postoperative immunoglobulin G antibody formation, distinguishing this case from the standard syndrome of cardiac surgery-associated HIT.

Following the operation, the patient’s platelet kinetics deviated significantly from the expected postoperative nadir. His platelet count decreased from a normal preoperative baseline of 210×10⁹/L to 155×10⁹/L by postoperative day 1 (POD1). Consistent with standard of care, therapeutic prophylaxis was initiated with low-molecular-weight heparin (dalteparin), escalated from 5000 U on POD1 to 7500 U on POD3 through POD4. While a minor decline in platelets (to 155×10⁹/L) is a common non-prognostic finding following cardiac surgery, a progressive decline is not. In this patient, despite holding LMWH on POD5 and initiating fondaparinux, the platelet count fell further to 50×10⁹/L by POD5. Clinically, this steady decline exceeding 50% to 50% of baseline after 48 hours of full prophylaxis should raise immediate suspicion for an immune-mediated process rather than consumptive coagulopathy or therapeutic failure alone. The persistence and severity of this drop, extending to a nadir of 26×10⁹/L by POD6, necessitated a shift from monitoring to active investigation for thrombocytopenic syndromes.

The trajectory of the case was altered by the emergence of neurological and hemodynamic comorbidities, hinting towards a hypercoagulable state rather than simple refractory thrombocytopenia. While the patient initially presented with postoperative delirium, this worsened concurrently with the nadirizing platelet count on POD6. An emergency CT brain scan was performed to assess for other causes of deterioration, such as intracranial hemorrhage or fluid overload. The imaging revealed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe. The presence of a stroke in the setting of profound thrombocytopenia is a hallmark feature of HIT, which is paradoxically associated with a high risk of thrombosis—including venous thromboembolism, stroke, and coronary or peripheral limb ischemia—rather than bleeding alone. This presentation shifts the diagnostic weight from bleeding diathesis or drug toxicity to HIT-associated thrombosis, aligning with the principle that thrombosis is the ultimate clinical consequence of antibody-mediated platelet activation.

Laboratory evaluation aimed to exclude common confounders before confirming an antibody diagnosis. A comprehensive blood count showed no evidence of hemolysis, indicated by normal reticulocyte counts and elevated haptoglobin, which also initially made thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) less likely. However, given the intense platelet consumption, the peripheral smear was a critical step; an absence of schistocytes and "toxic" white blood cell morphologies suggested TTP or disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) were unlikely precipitants at this stage. Furthermore, the patient remained hemodynamically stable without obvious signs of sepsis or organ failure, and blood/urine cultures were negative, making infectious sepsis-induced immunothrombocytopenia improbable. Drug-induced thrombocytopenia from recently administered antibiotics (vancomycin, cefazolin) or cardiovascular agents (furosemide, metoprolol) was excluded because the onset of thrombocytopenia occurred too early for drug-specific antibody maturation. By synthesizing the platelet kinetics, the thrombotic stroke, and the lack of alternative etiologies, the clinical team concluded that HIT was the most probable diagnosis, frequently triggered by the patient’s documented preoperative UFH exposure.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and the Immunopathology of aHIT

The diagnostic process for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) relies heavily on timing, clinical presentation, and specific serological evidence, all of which were critical in distinguishing Classic HIT from the autoimmune variant in this patient. In standard cardiac surgery populations, a significant platelet decline typically occurs between postoperative days 5 and 10, reflecting the time needed to generate a sufficient number of heparin-specific antibodies. However, this patient’s thrombocytopenia began unexpectedly early, with counts dropping from 210×109/L preoperatively to 50×109/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5). While an early nadir of POD5 is rare, it was logically linked to preoperative heparin exposure identified during a diagnostic catheterization one week prior to surgery. The intraprocedural use of 2000 units of unfractionated heparin during the catheterization primed the immune system before the cardiac surgery, shortening the window to develop detectable antibodies. This temporal relationship, combined with thrombotic stroke and an isolated negative for other causes such as transfusion or infection, elevated the suspicion of HIT as the primary driver of the coagulopathy.

Initial serological testing utilized a screening immunoassay for HIT antibodies, specifically the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), which revealed a strong-positive result with an optical density of 2.2 units (reference <0.4). While this finding confirms the presence of antibodies capable of binding platelet factor 4 (PF4) in the context of polyanionic surfaces, ELISA lacks the specificity to distinguish between functional, platelet-activating antibodies and non-activating complexes that yield false-positive results. Consequently, the confirmatory gold standard, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), was required to determine the pathogenic nature of the antibodies. In classic HIT, the SRA measures platelet activation in the presence of increasing concentrations of heparin, typically showing significant serotonin release only at higher heparin concentrations that facilitate immune complex formation. However, this patient’s SRA demonstrated a distinct immunological phenotype: strong serotonin release occurred even in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL), reaching 96% of the maximal release. This heparin-independent activation is the hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), indicating that the circulating antibodies bound to PF4 are potent enough to cross-link platelet FcγRIIa receptors without the need for heparin-dependent epitope unfolding or immune complex bridging.

Further characterization of the antibody mechanism involved a specific quality control profile using a monoclonal antibody that blocks Fc receptors on platelets (IV.3). In classic HIT, this blocking agent typically inhibits platelet activation in the presence of patient serum because the platelets are activated primarily through Fc-receptor clustering by heparin-PF4 complexes. In this case, despite the addition of the IV.3 blocker, 76% serotonin release remained at 0.1 U/mL heparin, indicating incomplete inhibition. This partial resistance to Fc-receptor blockade is a novel and unique serological feature observed in this patient. It suggests a hyper-reactive antibody profile where the Fc domain may be directly involved in membrane binding or where other non-Fc-dependent activation pathways are functional, contributing to the profound refractory thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×109/L) and consumptive features. This distinct profile highlights that aHIT antibodies can exist as a spectrum of severity, where the resistance to standard Fc-blocking strategies serves as an indicator of a more aggressive clinical course.

Ultimately, the diagnosis of aHIT was validated by the patient’s serological evolution alongside their clinical improvement. While the SRA profile remained atypical for several months, subsequent testing following platelet recovery demonstrated a transition toward a more classic heparin-dependent hinge, consistent with the eventual waning of the autoimmune antibodies. This longitudinal data, combined with the severity of the thrombocytopenia and the early temporal onset linked to preoperative exposure, confirms the role of pathogenic heparin-independent antibodies. Understanding this immunopathology is vital for clinical management, as misidentifying aHIT as classic HIT could lead to insufficient anticoagulation or delayed recognition of associated complications like immune thrombotic strokes. The atypical SRA results in this case provide a critical teaching point for acknowledging that functional assays must be interpreted within the full clinical context, especially when encountering preoperative heparin exposure and severe thrombocytopenia that defies standard temporal windows.

## Therapeutic Strategy: Anticoagulation and Immunomodulation

The immediate therapeutic priority in this case was the cessation of all heparin-containing anticoagulants to prevent further cross-linking of heparin-dependent platelet factor 4 antibodies, which were driving the thrombocytopenia and thrombotic risk. Standard care for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) requires replacement of heparin with a non-heparin anticoagulant, typically a direct thrombin inhibitor (DTI) or a factor Xa inhibitor, but the selection in this instance was heavily influenced by the patient's concurrent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia. Although prophylactic dose fondaparinux had been initiated on postoperative day 5, it was discontinued when the clinical team initiated therapeutic management due to safety concerns regarding potential bleeding in a recently infarcted brain. The treating hematologist chose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, over other alternatives like fondaparinux specifically for its short half-life and the ability to precisely titrate efficacy via partial thromboplastin time (PTT) monitoring. This choice prioritized the ability to rapidly reduce anticoagulation in the event of hemorrhage, a critical consideration given the patient's neurological deficits. The absolute avoidance of low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) and unfractionated heparin (UFH) was essential, as continued exposure would reliably re-stimulate the pathogenic antibodies and perpetuate the immune-mediated thrombocytopenia.

Beyond simple anticoagulation, the specific diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) necessitated immunomodulatory intervention to dampen the underlying immune dysregulation. Given the patient's profound nadir platelet count of 13×10⁹/L and signs of consumptive coagulopathy, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was administered to mitigate the autoimmune platelet activation. The rationale was based on the unique pathophysiology of aHIT, where the antibody subtypes possess heparin-independent activating properties and demonstrated partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade in vitro. Treatment with 100 g of IVIg over three days was intended to suppress this refractory antibody response. However, the clinical response to immunotherapy was surprisingly modest; the platelet count rose only slightly to 54×10⁹/L rather than normalizing, reflecting the severity of the autoimmune phenotype. This atypical serological profile, where Fc receptor blocking was only partially effective, likely contributed to the limited efficacy of IVIg in rapidly reversing the cytopenia, highlighting that in severe aHIT, the immune storm may require sustained suppression despite standard immunomodulatory doses.

As the patient's clinical status stabilized, the anticoagulation strategy evolved from acute control to long-term thromboprophylaxis. Persistent markers of hypercoagulability during argatroban therapy, specifically falling fibrinogen levels and rising d-dimer levels accompanied by the appearance of schistocytes, indicated that the DTI alone was not sufficiently controlling the systemic activation. Consequently, the patient was switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on postoperative day 14. The transition addressed the inefficacy of argatroban in controlling the coagulopathy while offering the convenience of subcutaneous once-daily dosing. Notably, fondaparinux is an indirect factor Xa inhibitor reliant on antithrombin (AT) for activity; because the patient had a reduced baseline AT level of 0.70 U/mL, supplemental AT concentrate was administered prior to the first dose to ensure the medication functioned effectively. This adjustment underscores the importance of monitoring both the thrombotic burden (via fibrinogen and d-dimer) and the functional capacity of the administered anticoagulant pathway. The patient remained on therapeutic fondaparinux until day 65, at which point platelet recovery allowed for the resumption of oral apixaban, illustrating a tailored approach where aggressive management is maintained until specific recovery milestones are achieved in the setting of autoimmune thrombocytopenia.

## Monitoring the Coagulopathy and Treatment Response

In severe cases of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT), serial platelet monitoring alone is often insufficient to gauge disease severity or the immediate efficacy of intervention. In this case, the patient’s platelet count plummeted to a nadir of 13×109/L before undergoing cardiac surgery-related inflammatory changes were fully resolved. The administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was initiated to suppress the immune-mediated platelet destruction, yet the clinical response was notably muted; the platelet count rose only modestly from 13×109/L to 54×109/L. This lack of a brisk response contrasted with the typical pattern for aHIT, where patients often experience rapid platelet recovery upon immune suppression, and served as an early clinical indicator for an unusually severe subtype. In conjunction with this platelet trajectory, the team relied on fibrinogen and d-dimer levels to assess the underlying coagulation status. Since fibrinogen is a protein consumed during clot formation and an acute-phase reactant, successful control of the hypercoagulable state typically results in rising fibrinogen levels and falling d-dimer levels over time. However, during the initial treatment with argatroban, the patient demonstrated a progressive decrease in fibrinogen from a postoperative elevation to a nadir of 2.49 g/L, occurring simultaneously with a peak in D-dimer levels around postoperative day 12. These opposing trends indicated that consumption of clotting factors was outpacing hepatic synthesis or acute-phase elevation, suggesting that the hypercoagulability was persisting despite direct thrombin inhibitor therapy.

Peripheral blood examination provided further objective evidence of severe thrombotic consumption. While a blood film typically shows platelets to quantify thrombocytopenia, the presence of red cell fragments, or schistocytes, signals hemolysis driven by shearing forces within the microcirculation. In this case, schistocytes appeared on the peripheral blood film between postoperative days 9 and 16. The absence of toxic white blood cell abnormalities, normal haptoglobin, and negative cultures ruled out other causes such as thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura or bacterial infection; instead, the presence of schistocytes in the setting of HIT antibodies confirmed widespread microvascular thrombosis. The convergence of falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimers, and the appearance of schistocytes throughout the period of argatroban treatment provided a robust rationale for escalating therapy. Consequently, the medical team switched from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on postoperative day 14, prioritizing aggressive anticoagulation over the convenience of the earlier regimen to address the persistent consumptive coagulopathy.

The ultimate assessment of treatment response tied the hemodynamic stability of the coagulation system to the clinical resolution of symptoms and the recovery of platelet counts. Following the switch to fondaparinux and the concurrent use of herpeticosuppressive therapy, the platelet count gradually normalized, reaching 155×109/L by postoperative day 62, and the neurological status improved without sequelae. Consequently, long-term anticoagulation was resumed with oral apixaban. Finally, serological monitoring demonstrated that while the platelet count recovered, immune monitoring lagged behind clinical improvement. Serial serotonin-release assays (SRA) confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies for several months post-recovery; however, the atypical profile shifted over time, showing reduced heparin-independent activation by postoperative day 124. This evolution underscores that serological findings often persist after the physiological risk has resolved, emphasizing that weeks of hematological normalization should not be dismissed as transient given the persistence of potentially auto-reactive antibodies.

## Resolution of the Case and Clinical Takeaways

The patient’s clinical trajectory concluded with a favorable outcome, demonstrating the potential for recovery despite the severity of the autoimmune response. By postoperative day 62, the platelet count had normalized to 155×10⁹/L and was maintained at 202×10⁹/L at follow-up, coinciding with the complete resolution of neurological abnormalities associated with the parietal stroke. This recovery was achieved through a prolonged period of anticoagulation with fondaparinux (administered until day 65) before the reintroduction of oral apixaban for long-term management of atrial fibrillation. The durability of this recovery, without evidence of recurrent thrombosis or bleeding complications, highlights that while the initial aHIT presentation was severe, the condition did not mandate indefinite indefinite treatment provided the immune response eventually waned. The normalization of platelet counts also serves as a marker that the immune system has cleared the heparin-antibody interaction, allowing for the reintroduction of long-term oral anticoagulation previously suspended due to stroke risk.

Following clinical recovery, the patient’s serological profile underwent a distinct evolution, providing critical evidence for the dynamic nature of HIT antibodies over time. While earlier SRA results during the nadir phase displayed partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade consistent with severe aHIT, the last blood sample obtained at 124 days post-injury showed a profile characteristic of predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, with significantly reduced platelet-activating properties in the absence of heparin. This seroconversion underscores a key diagnostic principle: a patient’s initial serology does not necessarily predetermine the long-term serological phenotype, and repeating antibody testing during recovery may be required if the clinical picture shifts or if new surgical exposure to heparin occurs. This evolution confirms that the initial "atypical" profile was likely a transient state of heightened immune activation that normalized over the course of months, rather than a permanent autoimmune defect.

From a management perspective, the switch from argatroban to fondaparinux illustrates the necessity of monitoring indirect markers of hypercoagulability in aHIT cases. Although the patient’s platelet count began to rise modestly, the rising d-dimer levels and the complicated fibrinogen decline indicated ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite the direct thrombin inhibitor. Consequently, the treating team escalated to fondaparinux, which ultimately provided better control, evidenced by the normalization of coagulation parameters and the cessation of schistocytosis. This clinical decision reinforces that the resolution of thrombocytopenia alone is an inadequate marker of therapeutic success in aHIT; clinicians must monitor for evidence of volthrombosis through fibrinogen and d-dimer trends, particularly in patients with profound thrombocytopenia.

The final lesson from this case centers on the nuances of preoperative heparin exposure and the recognition of atypical HIT phenotypes in high-risk surgical populations. The unusually early onset of thrombocytopenia, preceding day 5 post-cardiac surgery, was directly traced to the intra-procedural heparin exposure during the preoperative catheterization. This demonstrates that the "5-day" window rule for HIT is a guideline rather than an absolute cutoff, and clinicians must account for heparin exposure within days prior to major surgery, including catheterizations. Furthermore, the patient’s phenotype—marked by a platelet nadir of 13×10⁹/L and resistance to rapid platelet recovery with IVIg—aligns with the spectrum of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). For board evaluations or clinical reasoning, this case teaches that severe thrombocytopenia unremittent despite standard IVIg therapy, coupled with stroke or thrombosis, should prompt a re-evaluation for aHIT rather than classic HIT. Finally, the case highlights a need for careful planning in cardiac surgery protocols, advising that preoperative heparin exposure necessitates heightened vigilance for thrombocytopenia in the immediate early postoperative period, potentially influencing the choice of prophylactic agents or the timing of immunosuppressive interventions.
