Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Inhibition or Inevitable Activation? Interpreting Atypical Serotonin-Release Assay Profiles in HIT

## The Diagnosis That Got Messy

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is one of those rare things that looks simple in the algorithm but often proves to be a diagnostic minefield in practice. You stop the heparin, you start a direct thrombin inhibitor, you wait to see the platelets bounce back. Clinically, it usually fits. But what do you do when the serology screams "HIT," the antibody test looks like a paradox, and the patient is already throwing a hurricane sized storm of thrombocytopenia and thrombosis?

Let’s talk about a case where the serum told a story that turned on its head. You have a complex cardiac surgery patient with a parietal stroke and fibrinogen levels climbing into the single digits. The initial HIT panel comes back strong positive. Then comes the serotonin-release assay (SRA). To most readers, a positive SRA confirms the diagnosis and changes management. In this specific scenario, however, the SRA profile was so atypical that it defied standard interpretation of heparin-dependency. But the clinical picture demanded action. This case report by St Bernard and colleagues highlights a milder subtype of HIT (actually a severe one) where standard heparin-dependent HIT dogma doesn't always apply.

To understand why this mattered, we have to look at the timeline. The patient had preoperative catheterization with a lower-than-usual UFH dose (2000 U), followed by four days of LMWH post-op. His platelets dropped steadily reaching 50×10⁹/L on Day 5. We know that HIT platelet falls usually happen between Day 5 and 10 post-op. But this happened early because of that catheterization. That early onset, combined with a massive drop to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L and a paradoxical clinical course, pointed toward autoimmune HIT (aHIT). The defining feature here isn't just the drop in platelets; it's the serology that looks like it’s double-handled.

## Decoding the Serotonin-Release Assay

Here is where the clinical education gets granular. We all know that the gold standard for HIT serology is the PF4 polyanion ELISA followed by the SRA. The ELISA detects the antibodies, the SRA proves they function. Standard HIT antibodies are heparin-dependent. They need the heparin to bind PF4, form a complex, and then trigger platelets via the Fcγ receptor.

In this case, the SRA results were complicated. The lab looked for two specific things to rule in *autoimmune* HIT versus *classic* HIT.
1.  **Heparin-Independence:** Does the serum activate platelets even without the heparin? The text notes a massive 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin. In classic HIT, you should see 0% serotonin release at 0 U/mL. This 96% indicates the antibodies were activating platelets of their own volition, independent of the heparin targeting the Fcγ receptor. That flags autoimmune HIT.
2.  **Fc-Receptor Blockade:** Usually, we block the Fcγ receptor with a monoclonal antibody (IV.3). If the platelets stop releasing serotonin, we know the antibody was Fc-dependent (classic HIT). The SF/HIT guidelines suggest we expect inhibition below 20% release.

Here is the kicker that makes the case report novel. When they added the Fc receptor-blocking antibody, they still saw 76% serotonin release. This suggests the antibodies had partial resistance to the Fc blockade. It is a unique, unreferenced profile in the literature. It challenges the idea that aHIT antibodies are completely independent of Fc blocks, or at least in some severe subtypes, the pairing is more resistant to inhibition in vitro. This likely explains the extreme thrombocytopenia and persistent hypercoagulability in the patient.

When I interpreted this report, my initial thought was the classic dilemma. Screening was strong positive (OD 2.2), so it was HIT, but the SRA was indeterminate or off-protocol. We have to treat HIT if the 4Ts score is high and the ELISA is positive, regardless of the SRA nuances. However, recognizing these atypical nuances is vital because they explain why the patient had such a severe course. The clotting wasn't just high; the antibody profile suggested a specific subset of aHIT that didn't respond well to the initial standard treatments, or rather, responded poorly until the mechanism was fully understood.

## Management and Clinical Outcomes

Regardless of the weird lab profile, the clinical management requires heavy lifting. The patient had a stroke on Day 6. Immediate anticoagulation with argatroban was started to avoid UFH/LMWH entirely. They also implemented high-dose IV Ig.

The patient had a good outcome, which is reassuring given the severity. Platelets recovered to 155×10⁹/L by Day 62. However, we saw some nuances in the response. High-dose Ig didn't work the way we expect. In classic aHIT, we see a brisk rise in platelets within days. This patient crept from a nadir of 13×10⁹/L up to 54×10⁹/L by Day 14. It was a slow rise, suggesting the antibody burden was massive or the Fc block resistance was playing a role in recalcitrance.

Eventually, the patient switched from argatroban to therapeutic fondaparinux. This is unusual since fondaparinux is not approved for HIT, but it's an indirect factor Xa inhibitor that bypasses the Fc receptors. They supplemented with AT concentrate to ensure efficacy. The patient's coagulation profile eventually stabilized. D-dimers peaked and fell. Anti-platelet clumping was absent on peripheral smear. The last SRA test at Day 124 showed a return to a heparin-dependent profile, suggesting the antibody nature evolved or the autoimmune component waned.

The key lesson here is not about the exact mechanism of the antibody blocking the Fc receptor. It's about clinical suspicion. When you have early postoperative thrombocytopenia, a stroke, and a strong ELISA, you treat HIT. If the SRA profile comes back weird or indeterminate, that is confirmation of a complex phenotype, not a reason to withhold treatment.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   Early thrombocytopenia post-cardiac surgery can be aHIT, triggered by preoperative UFH exposure (catheterization), not just CPB.
*   PF4 ELISA positivity with an atypical SRA profile indicating platelet activation without heparin should raise suspicion for Autoimmune HIT.
*   Partial resistance to Fc-receptor blockade in SRA does not rule out HIT; treat with anticoagulation if clinical suspicion is high.
*   Monitor fibrinogen and D-dimer trends to gauge hypercoagulability resolution.
*   Severe thrombocytopenia with high-dose Ig resistance may indicate a stubborn phenotype, but aggressive management allows for recovery.
