Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# The Silent Threat: Autoimmune HIT and the High-Risk of Thrombotic Stroke

## Case Snapshot: When Heparin Isn't the Target

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is one of those classic clinical diagnoses where the answer seems obvious once you have the timing and the platelet history. But what happens when the timing is wrong, the strokes are abrupt, and the platelets crash to dangerous levels faster than we expect?

We are looking at a case of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a rare but high-stakes subset of HIT that behaves very differently from the classic immune complex-driven disorder. This patient presented with a paradox: severe thrombocytopenia, a new arterial thrombosis (stroke), and a laboratory profile that looked like HIT but wasn't dependent on heparin to activate. It serves as a stark reminder that in the ICU or post-op ward, the "board exam" answer isn't always the whole story.

## The Clinical Timeline: Overtaking Classic Expectations

Let's look at the trajectory. We have an elderly male undergoing significant cardiac surgery—valve replacement, aortic graft, CABG. The hypothermic circulatory arrest isn't even the story; the story is the platelets.

Pre-op, he had a cath with low-dose UFH. Surgery proceeded with high-dose UFH during CPB. Classically, we expect the "classic window" of HIT to be between POD 5 and POD 10, occurring after the first heparin dose triggers the IgG anti-PF4 antibody. Here's where this case gets tricky.

The platelet count started dropping early.
*   **Preroute:** 210 × 10⁹/L
*   **POD1:** 155 × 10⁹/L (Minor drop, often seen with CPB or lab fluctuation)
*   **POD5:** 50 × 10⁹/L
*   **POD6:** 26 × 10⁹/L

By POD6, the patient got a new stroke. The thrombocytopenia wasn't just bleeding risk; it was a violent prothrombotic state. The clotting workup showed elevated fibrinogen and an elevated D-dimer later on, confirming the thrombotic storm. The cerebral infarct was left parietal.

Why was the onset so early? Usually, this rules out HIT and points to other differentials like DIC, TTP, or simple dilution. The authors flagged the **preoperative heart catheterization** and the UFH given there. That exposure primed the antibodies days before the surgery even started. This is the "silent threat"—the clock started ticking before you even scooped the first heparin out of the bag.

## The Lab Reality: SRA Tells a Deeper Story

The standard workflow triggered suspicion because of the low count and stroke. High-sensitivity PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive. But ELISA has too many false positives to stand alone. We need activation data. The Serotonin-Release Assay (SRA) is the gold standard.

However, the SRA here showed something the standard HIT profile doesn't usually show.
1.  **Heparin Independence:** In classic HIT, platelets need heparin bound to PF4 to trigger the antibody. In aHIT, the antibodies activate platelets *without* heparin. In this patient, we saw 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin. Classic HIT typically shows nothing at 0.
2.  **The Blockade Failure:** The Fc receptor-blocking antibody (IV.3) is used to verify HIT. In classic HIT, adding this inhibitor should stop the activation. Here, it was only *partially* effective. The SRA at 0.1 U/mL still showed 76% serotonin release. This partial resistance suggests a distinct pathophysiology.

This is the "forgotten antibody phenotype." If you see severe thrombocytopenia and an SRA positive for aHIT antibodies, the prognosis can be worse, and you need to think about thrombosis more urgently.

## Management Battles: Arg vs. Fondaparinux

Treatment is the tricky part. When you have a patient on CPB, the anticoagulation is critical to prevent extension of the stroke, but you also have a risk of bleeding.

The team started with **Argatroban** (direct thrombin inhibitor). Why? Classic HIT is usually treated with LMWH or Fondaparinux, but with a recent stroke and platelets < 30, the safety profile of IV drips matters. They targeted PTT 55-85.

Complication of care 1: Early on, they considered Fondaparinux, a pentasaccharide. But the patient had a stroke immediately after starting LMWH (Dalteparin). This is classic "danger signal."
Complication of care 2: The platelets didn't bounce back fast enough. Platelets went to a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L.
Complication of care 3: Mild response to IVIG. In classic aHIT, IVIG can work miracles. This patient had a modest increase (13 to 54) because the antibody activity was so severe.

Eventually, they switched to therapeutic-dose Fondaparinux with concentrate supplementation. It worked. By POD62, the platelets were 155 × 10⁹/L.

The key lesson here: Do not stick with standard heparins. But if Fondaparinux or a Direct Thrombin Inhibitor (DTI) fails to control the hypercoagulability, escalate. The presence of schistocytes during the illness course confirmed ongoing microangiopathic consumption, proving the thrombocytopenia was active.

## What We Need to Remember

This case highlights that aHIT is not just a "late version" of HIT. It is a fulminant process. The laboratory findings (0 U/mL SRA activity + partial blockade resistance) are the only way to distinguish this from classic HIT definitively. If you have the stroke and the platelet crash, don't miss this.

### Key Take-Home Messages

*   **aHIT can occur before POD5:** Preoperative heparin exposure (like cath before surgery) is a major risk factor for initiating the immune timeline early.
*   **SRA is crucial:** Use the Serotonin-Release Assay to differentiate aHIT (high activation at 0 U/mL) from typical HIT.
*   **Fulminant coagulation:** Watch for schistocytes and high consumption (fibrinogen, D-dimer) during treatment; B-HIT with normal platelets is common, but aHIT often progresses to consumptive coagulopathy (similar to TTP/DIC).
*   **Treatment remains aggressive:** DTIs or Fondaparinux are mandatory. Do not wait for test results if the clinical picture suggests HIT.
*   **Stroke risk is real:** Thrombosis in HIT is not just an anticoagulation failure; it is the nature of the disease process.

Be aggressive in your anticoagulation. This case proves that the best treatment is to catch the "silent" aHIT early and stop the heparin, or it will stop your patient's platelets from recovering.
