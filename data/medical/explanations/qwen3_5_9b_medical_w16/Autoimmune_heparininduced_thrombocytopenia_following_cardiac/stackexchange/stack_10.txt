Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] What long-term management principles regarding oral anticoagulation (e.g., apixaban) apply to a patient with a history of autoimmune HIT once thrombocytopenia has resolved, and how does the specific liability of the condition influence this decision?

In the management of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT), the paramount long-term principle involves the permanent avoidance of heparin and its direct derivatives to prevent recurrent immune-mediated thrombocytopenia and thrombosis. Once the acute phase is resolved and platelet counts have normalized, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) such as apixaban represent the preferred standard of care for long-term venous thromboembolism prophylaxis or atrial fibrillation management. This decision is driven by the pathophysiology of HIT, where antibodies targeting the platelet factor 4 (PF4)-heparin complex activate platelets via the Fc-gamma receptor; since DOACs do not interact with this mechanism, they provide effective anticoagulation without triggering the PF4-Fc receptor cascade.

This patient’s case underscores the feasibility of such a transition. Although the patient’s thrombocytopenia was severe (nadir of 13×109/L) and complications included a thrombotic stroke, fondaparinux (a parenteral agent derived from what is functionally similar to heparin in this immune context relative to factor Xa) was discontinued at post-operative Day 65 (POD65) upon platelet recovery. Apixaban was then resumed for long-term anticoagulation. The case highlights that the specific liability of aHIT—the presence of heparin-independent antibodies—necessitates strict avoidance of all heparins, including those used for catheterization that might trigger the response. However, the long-term safety of DOACs is supported here by the clinical and serological evolution; notably, by POD124, laboratory evaluation confirmed the patient’s antibodies had shifted toward a predominantly profile consistent with heparin-dependent HIT. This resolution of the auto-reactive component, combined with normal platelet recovery, provided the biochemical and hematologic foundation to safely reintroduce the non-heparin based DOAC therapy apixaban (5 mg twice daily) for this patient’s atrial fibrillation and stroke history. Thus, the management principle is to treat the thrombotic risk with DOACs while the immune liability to heparin imposes a lifelong treatment-free zone regarding parenteral heparins.
