Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Seronegative IMNM: When the Panel Is Negative, the Biopsy Is the Biomarker

Why this case matters: a woman in her 60s developed rapidly progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a 12-fold rise in CK over 4 months after a single dose of semaglutide. Her myositis serologies — including the two antibodies most associated with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP — were negative. The muscle biopsy, not the panel, provided the diagnosis: dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition and only rare T‑cells. Treatment with high‑dose steroids and IVIG led to recovery and sustained remission off immunosuppression while she avoided GLP‑1 receptor agonists. This is a neat reminder: up to one‑third of IMNM is seronegative — and in those patients tissue is the test that matters.

## What is “seronegative” IMNM?

IMNM is an aggressive, predominantly proximal necrotising myopathy classically associated with anti‑HMGCR (statin‑linked) or anti‑SRP antibodies. Histologically it stands apart from polymyositis and dermatomyositis: prominent fibre necrosis/regeneration with a relative paucity of lymphocytes. But serology is not omniscient — roughly 20–30% of IMNM patients have no detectable HMGCR or SRP antibodies. In those patients, clinical suspicion + targeted diagnostics (imaging, biopsy) must drive diagnosis and management.

Key features from this case that scream IMNM despite a negative panel:
- Rapidly progressive proximal weakness with bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia).
- Marked CK elevation (peak 3992 U/L).
- MRI showing symmetric proximal muscle edema (vastus lateralis involvement).
- Muscle biopsy: necrotic and regenerating myofibres, CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis, MAC deposition, scarce CD3+ T‑cells.

## Why the biopsy “wins” here

When serologies are negative, the biopsy is the biomarker. Characteristic IMNM histopathology to look for:
- Scattered/patchy necrotic and regenerating fibres rather than a uniform inflammatory infiltrate.
- Macrophage‑predominant infiltrate with myophagocytosis (CD68+).
- Deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9/MAC) on necrotic and non‑necrotic fibres.
- Sparse lymphocytes (occasional CD3+ T cells) — i.e., not the dense endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates of polymyositis.

In this patient the biopsy showed exactly that pattern. That pattern, paired with the clinical picture, is diagnostic of IMNM even without HMGCR/SRP antibodies.

## Use MRI to target the biopsy (and the diagnosis)

MRI of muscles is a practical, non‑invasive adjunct and, crucially, helps select the best biopsy site. In this case MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema in the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps — and the biopsy from the vastus lateralis confirmed IMNM.

Practical MRI tips:
- Look for symmetric proximal thigh and pelvic girdle edema.
- Fatty replacement and chronic changes suggest chronic disease; early inflammatory edema favors an active, potentially treatable process.
- Target biopsy to an edematous but not end‑stage fatty replaced area for the best yield.

## How to think about a negative myositis panel — a practical approach

Start with a clear clinical pattern: subacute → rapidly progressive proximal weakness, CK often in the thousands, bulbar symptoms, and constitutional signs or cardiopulmonary involvement. If that pattern exists, negative antibodies do not rule out IMNM.

Suggested sequence in a suspected IMNM when serology is negative or equivocal:
1. Thorough history — meds (statins, supplements like red yeast rice, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and increasingly GLP‑1 RAs), recent infections, exposures.
2. Baseline labs — CK, AST/ALT (remember hepatic vs muscular source), TSH, ESR/CRP, renal function.
3. Myositis panel (SRP, HMGCR, Jo‑1 and others) — perform, but don’t stop there if clinical suspicion remains.
4. Muscle MRI to map edema and guide biopsy.
5. Consider EMG as adjunct (can show myopathic units and irritability) — but a normal EMG doesn’t exclude IMNM.
6. Proceed to muscle biopsy early when: progressive weakness, bulbar involvement/dysphagia, CK markedly elevated, MRI/EMG consistent or unclear — especially if serologies are negative.

In short: treat the patient, not the panel. If the clinical picture and imaging point to IMNM, get tissue.

## Red herrings and pitfalls — what tripped up this case (and how to avoid it)

- Isolated low‑titer antibodies: this patient had a low‑titer SS‑B/La with negative ANA and SS‑A. Isolated, low‑titer SS‑B without supportive clinical features is unlikely to explain the picture and was a red herring. Always interpret autoantibodies in clinical context.
- Prior mild transaminase elevation: she had hepatosteatosis documented years earlier. Mild chronic AST/ALT elevation can be hepatic; recall that AST/ALT can also rise from muscle — but biopsy confirmed NAFLD in this patient and CK normalized after treatment, arguing the transaminitis was chronic hepatic disease rather than muscle breakdown.
- Attributing everything to a single exposure: this patient had a striking immediate intolerance to semaglutide and temporal relationship to onset of symptoms supports a probable association, but causality is not proven. Nonetheless, withdrawal of the offending agent (and reporting adverse events) is reasonable.

## Management learning points from the case

IMNM frequently requires prompt immunosuppression. In this case the team used high‑dose IV methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a single course of IVIG (2 g/kg split over 4 days). The patient improved clinically and biochemically and remained off immunosuppression for months after a short course — a pattern consistent with drug‑triggered autoimmune myopathy in which removal of the trigger can shorten the required immunotherapy.

Practical takeaways:
- Consider early aggressive therapy (steroids ± IVIG) in severe IMNM (bulbar involvement, very high CK, respiratory compromise).
- HMGCR/SRP negativity does not obviate immunotherapy if clinical and histologic findings support IMNM.
- Search for and stop potential triggers (statins, supplements, potentially GLP‑1 RAs in this case).
- Report suspected drug‑induced cases to pharmacovigilance bodies.

## Bottom line

Seronegative IMNM is real and not rare. Don’t be reassured by a “negative myositis panel” when the clinical picture and imaging suggest an aggressive, necrotising myopathy. Muscle biopsy remains the decisive diagnostic tool — it is both the biomarker and the guide to therapy in seronegative cases. This semaglutide‑associated case illustrates that timely recognition, tissue diagnosis and prompt immunotherapy (with withdrawal of suspected offending agent) can restore patients to baseline.

Key take‑home messages
- Up to ~1/3 of IMNM patients are seronegative for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP — a negative panel does not rule out IMNM.
- Muscle biopsy is diagnostic: look for necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage myophagocytosis (CD68+), MAC (C5b‑9) deposition and sparse lymphocytes.
- Use MRI to identify active edema and to target the biopsy to the most informative site.
- Proceed early to biopsy when there is rapidly progressive proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, CK in the thousands, or discordant/negative serology.
- Interpret isolated, low‑titer autoantibodies (eg, SS‑B alone) in clinical context — they may be incidental.
- Treat the disease pattern: if biopsy and clinical picture indicate IMNM, start immunosuppression (steroids ± IVIG) and withdraw suspected offending agents; report suspected drug associations.
