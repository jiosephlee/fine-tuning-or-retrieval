Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Clinical Presentation and Early Clues to Inflammatory Myopathy

This patient’s course is instructive because the initial drug exposure and acute illness could have pointed clinicians toward a toxic or volume‑depletion–related myopathy, yet the subsequent evolution of symptoms and tests favored an immune‑mediated process. The first important time point is the index exposure: within hours of receiving a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide she developed severe nausea and vomiting, became hypovolaemic, and was hospitalized with acute kidney injury and metabolic acidosis. At that admission her CK was only modestly elevated (~337 U/L), a level that can be seen with mild muscle injury or with systemic illness and would be more consistent with dehydration‑related muscle stress than frank rhabdomyolysis.

What distinguished the subsequent course was the subacute, progressive pattern over the next 4 months. Rather than recovering after the acute event, she developed gradually worsening symmetric proximal limb weakness, and importantly new bulbar symptoms—dysphagia and dysphonia—eventually becoming unable to sit or stand without assistance. She did not have a cutaneous eruption, synovitis, oedema or prominent myalgias. On bedside neurologic exam the pattern was classic for a myopathic process: symmetric proximal > distal weakness without focal neurologic signs. The absence of skin findings argues against dermatomyositis, and the lack of joint or systemic features made an overlap connective‑tissue myositis less likely at presentation.

Laboratory trends reinforced concern for an inflammatory myopathy. Her CK rose progressively to a peak of ~4000 U/L, a magnitude typical of necrotising muscle disease but — crucially — rising over months rather than spiking acutely and then falling. AST and ALT were mildly elevated, but she had a prior history and biopsy‑proven hepatosteatosis, so the transaminase elevations were not specific for muscle origin. Inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) were mildly elevated and TSH was normal, supporting a non‑thyroid, inflammatory myopathic process. The timing and trajectory of CK are important: toxic rhabdomyolysis (including direct drug toxicity) typically causes a rapid, large CK rise with prompt improvement after withdrawal of the offending agent and rehydration; a slowly progressive CK increase despite discontinuation of the drug should raise suspicion for an immunologic myopathy.

Ancillary testing added objective evidence of inflammatory muscle involvement. MRI of the thighs showed symmetric muscular edema most prominent in proximal muscles — a pattern typical of active myositis or necrotising myopathy. Cardiac testing revealed mild troponin elevation and cardiac MRI changes compatible with transient myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement, suggesting possible inflammatory involvement of the heart (seen in some inflammatory myopathies) rather than an ischemic scar. Taken together, these imaging findings supported a diffuse inflammatory process rather than isolated toxin‑mediated muscle breakdown.

Several “red flags” in this case favored immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) over a purely toxic rhabdomyolysis: the subacute progressive course over months, development of bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia), symmetric proximal muscle edema on MRI, a CK trajectory that increased and remained elevated after the single semaglutide exposure, and the lack of rapid CK normalization that would be expected after correction of hypovolaemia if the initial injury had been exclusively toxic. These early clinical and paraclinical clues prompted the treating team to pursue a myositis workup and muscle biopsy, and to treat aggressively when the clinical picture and later histology pointed toward IMNM.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Broad Differential to Confirmed IMNM

When confronted with a patient who has subacute progressive proximal weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), a structured differential and stepwise diagnostic approach is essential. The working differential should include acute toxic or metabolic rhabdomyolysis, drug‑associated immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM, most classically statin‑associated via anti‑HMGCR), other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis), inclusion body myositis, endocrine or metabolic myopathies (thyroid, adrenal, electrolyte disturbances), motor neuron disease, and primary neuromuscular junction disorders. The information available in the history, the temporal pattern of CK change, the distribution of weakness, targeted serologies, neuroimaging/EMG, and—when required—muscle biopsy allow the clinician to narrow this list and reach a confident diagnosis.

Time course and CK kinetics are powerful early discriminators. Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically produces a rapid, often massive rise in CK with early symptoms (myalgias, dark urine) and resolution with removal of the offending agent and supportive care over days to weeks. In contrast, immune‑mediated myopathies commonly show a more indolent or progressive rise in CK over weeks to months. In this case the patient had a modest CK (337 U/L) at the index hospitalization immediately after semaglutide exposure but then developed progressive weakness and a delayed peak CK of 3,992 U/L four months later, a pattern more consistent with an evolving autoimmune myopathy than with an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis that would be expected to improve shortly after the insult was removed.

Clinical features further narrow the possibilities. The absence of a characteristic skin rash argues against classic dermatomyositis, and the prominent, symmetric proximal weakness with bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) fits more with IMNM or polymyositis than with inclusion body myositis, which is typically asymmetric, affects distal and finger flexor muscles, and progresses more slowly in older men. Lack of myalgia, pigmenturia, or cola‑coloured urine during the later decline also weighs against ongoing frank rhabdomyolysis. Normal TSH makes overt thyroid myopathy unlikely, and the degree of CK elevation generally exceeds what is seen in neuromuscular junction disorders or motor neuron disease, which typically do not cause large CK increases.

Electrophysiology and imaging provide objective support for inflammatory myopathy when available. EMG (not described in detail in this report) can show myopathic motor unit changes and irritability, but muscle MRI demonstrating symmetric myofascial or intramuscular oedema—“myoedema”—is a sensitive marker of active inflammatory myositis. In this patient, bilateral femur MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle oedema, reinforcing an active inflammatory process rather than a focal toxic injury.

Autoantibody testing helps classify inflammatory myopathies and identify IMNM subtypes. Myositis‑specific panels include anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP (strongly associated with IMNM), antisynthetase antibodies (eg, Jo‑1) associated with the antisynthetase syndrome, Mi‑2 and MDA5 linked to dermatomyositis phenotypes, and others (TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, cN1A). A positive anti‑HMGCR in the right clinical context supports statin‑associated IMNM; anti‑SRP is another IMNM marker. However, approximately one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative, so a negative panel does not exclude the disorder. In this case the full myositis panel was negative (including HMGCR and SRP), meaning the diagnosis could not rest on serology alone and that seronegative IMNM needed to remain on the differential.

Transaminase elevations are common in both liver disease and muscle injury, so it is important to distinguish the source. AST is present in muscle as well as liver, whereas ALT is more liver‑specific. A rising CK concurrent with AST/ALT elevations suggests myocyte origin; persistent transaminase elevation out of proportion to CK, or that persists after CK normalization, suggests hepatic disease. This patient had a history of NAFLD with prior liver biopsy showing steatohepatitis before semaglutide use and later repeat biopsy confirming progressive hepatic steatosis. Her AST/ALT remained modestly elevated even after CK normalized, supporting a hepatic, not muscular, source for the chronic transaminase abnormality.

Cardiac involvement must be assessed in inflammatory myopathies because myocarditis or cardiomyopathy can coexist. Troponin and natriuretic peptides can be elevated with cardiac injury but are not specific for primary myocarditis in the setting of severe systemic illness. Echocardiography and cardiac MRI are useful to detect functional impairment, wall motion abnormalities, oedema, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) indicative of inflammation or fibrosis. This patient had a mildly elevated high‑sensitivity troponin and markedly elevated pro‑BNP, but preserved left ventricular ejection fraction and cardiac MRI that showed only mild focal hypokinesis with oedema and no definite LGE—findings that could reflect focal myocardial inflammation or stress‑related changes but were not diagnostic of fulminant myocarditis. The subsequent normalization of cardiac studies in follow‑up supports a reversible process.

When noninvasive testing and serologies are inconclusive—or when the diagnosis will change management—muscle biopsy is indicated. Biopsy differentiates IMNM from polymyositis, dermatomyositis, inclusion body myositis, and metabolic or dystrophic processes. The hallmark of IMNM on histology is prominent myofibre necrosis and regeneration with relatively scant lymphocytic infiltrate, frequent macrophage (CD68+) myophagocytosis, and complement activation evidenced by membrane attack complex deposition on muscle fibers. In contrast, polymyositis shows more endomysial T‑cell infiltrates, dermatomyositis has perimysial/perivascular inflammation and perifascicular atrophy, and inclusion body myositis has rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates. In this case the right vastus lateralis biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres, CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis, deposition of membrane attack complex, and only rare CD3+ T cells—classic features supporting IMNM.

Bringing these elements together, the preponderance of evidence in this patient favors seronegative IMNM. The delayed and progressive CK rise after the index event, the distribution of symmetric proximal weakness with bulbar involvement, MRI evidence of active myositis, and pathognomonic biopsy findings establish the diagnosis despite a negative myositis antibody panel. Alternative explanations were less consistent: acute toxic rhabdomyolysis would be expected to peak and resolve more rapidly, the patient denied statin exposure making statin‑associated HMGCR IMNM unlikely, and other inflammatory myopathies were not supported by skin findings or biopsy morphology. Finally, the clinical response to corticosteroids and IVIG with durable recovery after cessation of semaglutide further aligns with a drug‑triggered immune myopathy, although causation cannot be proven from a single case.

For learners, the key lessons are to use the temporal pattern of CK and symptoms, focused clinical features, targeted serologies, MRI, and ultimately muscle biopsy in sequence to discriminate inflammatory myopathies from toxic or metabolic causes. Muscle biopsy remains the definitive test when serology is negative or when histologic pattern will directly influence treatment, as it did here by confirming IMNM and guiding immunotherapy.

## IMNM Essentials: Pathophysiology, Serologies, and Histopathology

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct subtype of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies that classically presents with relatively rapid onset, severe proximal muscle weakness, markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK), and frequent bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia). It is an uncommon disorder, most often seen in middle‑aged or older adults, and is distinguished from other myopathies by its histopathologic pattern: prominent myofibre necrosis and regeneration with only sparse lymphocytic infiltrates on muscle biopsy.

Serologic classification of IMNM is clinically useful because defined autoantibody subsets associate with different clinical contexts and prognosis. Two well‑recognized antibodies are anti‑HMGCR (anti‑3‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑CoA reductase) and anti‑SRP (signal recognition particle). Anti‑HMGCR IMNM is strongly linked to prior statin exposure in many patients and often presents with subacute severe weakness and high CK; anti‑SRP IMNM typically associates with very severe weakness, more frequent treatment‑resistance, and a higher risk of extramuscular involvement (including cardiac involvement) in some series. Importantly, about one‑third of IMNM cases are seronegative for these defined antibodies; seronegativity does not exclude the diagnosis and may reflect as‑yet unidentified autoantibodies or non‑antibody–mediated triggers.

Pathophysiologically, IMNM is characterized by immune‑mediated myofibre injury that is not primarily driven by dense endomysial T‑cell infiltration (as in polymyositis). A key feature is complement activation with deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b‑9) on the sarcolemma of affected fibres, implicating antibody‑ or complement‑dependent cytotoxic mechanisms. Macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis is also a hallmark: activated macrophages (CD68+) clear necrotic fibres and show increased lysosomal activity on special stains such as acid phosphatase. By contrast, only limited numbers of CD3+ T lymphocytes are typically present, often scattered and perivascular rather than forming dense endomysial infiltrates.

The muscle biopsy in this patient demonstrates the canonical features of IMNM and helps explain why clinicians considered an immune cause rather than pure toxic rhabdomyolysis. The biopsy showed dispersed necrotic and regenerating myofibres, prominent CD68+ macrophages with evidence of myophagocytosis, and increased acid phosphatase activity consistent with phagolysosomal activation. Complement activation was documented by membrane attack complex deposition on necrotic and some non‑necrotic fibres, supporting a complement‑mediated myocytotoxic process. Only rare CD3+ T cells and some perivascular inflammation were present, which fits the expected paucity of lymphocytes in IMNM. Taken together, these histologic findings are characteristic of necrotising autoimmune myopathy and help distinguish it from other entities.

Distinguishing IMNM from polymyositis, dermatomyositis, and toxic rhabdomyolysis depends on clinicopathologic correlation. Polymyositis typically shows prominent endomysial CD8+ T‑cell infiltrates invading non‑necrotic fibres; dermatomyositis shows perifascicular atrophy, perimysial/perivascular inflammation, and complement deposition in capillaries. Toxic rhabdomyolysis or purely metabolic muscle injury can produce myofibre necrosis and high CK, but generally lacks the immune signatures seen in IMNM — namely MAC deposition on fibre membranes and macrophage‑mediated myophagocytosis with persistent weakness that improves with immunotherapy rather than simply with withdrawal of the offending agent and supportive care.

Clinically relevant triggers for IMNM have been identified, most notably statin exposure in association with anti‑HMGCR positivity. Other drugs, infections, and malignancy have been implicated in subsets of patients. The existence of a seronegative IMNM subgroup underscores that additional autoantibodies and triggers likely remain to be described. In the present case, the patient was seronegative for HMGCR and SRP but had biopsy‑proven IMNM and a temporal relationship to semaglutide exposure; this constellation fits a seronegative, likely drug‑associated IMNM phenotype—one in which muscle biopsy, not serology alone, is decisive for diagnosis and for guiding immunosuppressive therapy.

## GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and Muscle Injury: Association, Mechanisms, and Causality

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) such as semaglutide are widely prescribed for type 2 diabetes and, increasingly, for obesity. Their therapeutic actions—glucose-dependent insulin secretion, slowed gastric emptying and appetite suppression—produce tolerability issues that are most commonly gastrointestinal (nausea, vomiting). Weight loss with GLP-1RAs often includes loss of lean mass as well as fat, and a small number of case reports have raised concerns about more severe muscle injury, including isolated reports of rhabdomyolysis with drugs in this class.

Published neuromuscular events linked to incretin-based peptides are rare but instructive. A recent report of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis described an acute presentation with massive CK elevation (>50 000 U/L) while the patient was actively receiving the drug; CK and symptoms improved rapidly after drug cessation and supportive care without immunosuppression. That pattern is typical for direct toxic rhabdomyolysis: abrupt onset, very high CK, and rapid decline after withdrawal and hydration.

The clinical course in the present case differs in several important ways that favour immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis. First, the CK evolution was delayed and progressive: only a modest CK elevation at the index hospitalisation after the first semaglutide dose, then a progressive decline in strength and a 12-fold CK rise over the following four months. Second, histopathology demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition—features characteristic of IMNM and of an immune-mediated process more than a purely toxic insult. Third, the clinical course required immunotherapy (high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG) for recovery rather than simple supportive care, and she achieved durable remission off immunosuppression after a relatively short treatment course and avoidance of further GLP-1RA exposure. These elements—temporal progression, biopsy with immune signatures (CD68+ myophagocytosis, MAC deposition, rare CD3 cells), and response to immune-directed therapy—support classification as IMNM in this patient.

Assessing causality between semaglutide and IMNM benefits from a structured approach. Using the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) framework, several points are relevant. There is a temporal relationship: symptoms began immediately after the first dose (severe GI illness with AKI) and progressive myopathy unfolded over the subsequent months. Alternative explanations are less likely: she had no antecedent statin exposure (a well-known trigger of HMGCR-positive IMNM), myositis autoantibody panels were negative (although ~30% of IMNM can be seronegative), and liver disease predated the event and persisted after CK normalization, arguing against muscle breakdown as the sole cause of transaminase elevation. There was a clinically reasonable response on withdrawal plus immunotherapy (positive dechallenge), and rechallenge would be unethical given the severity—so that key criterion cannot be addressed. Taking these elements together, the relationship is best described as “probable/likely” rather than certain. Important caveats remain: a smouldering pre-existing autoimmune myopathy cannot be definitively excluded in the absence of pre-drug CK or muscle testing, and a single case cannot establish causation at the population level.

Mechanistically, the link between semaglutide and IMNM is plausible but speculative. IMNM pathogenesis commonly involves autoantibody-associated immune attack on myofibres with complement activation (explaining MAC staining) and macrophage-mediated necrosis. Potential mechanisms by which a GLP-1RA could trigger or unmask such a process include: (1) drug‑induced immune dysregulation that breaks tolerance and permits autoimmunity; (2) an initial non‑immune muscle injury (for example, hypovolaemia-induced ischemic stress or minor rhabdomyolysis during the initial hospitalization) that exposes normally sequestered muscle antigens and leads to epitope spreading and adaptive immune activation; and (3) molecular mimicry between a drug-related peptide or drug–protein adduct and a muscle antigen. It is also conceivable that systemic metabolic stress (dehydration/AKI, electrolyte shifts) primed antigen presentation or inflammatory pathways. Conversely, many GLP-1RA effects are reported to be anti‑inflammatory in other contexts, so the net immunologic effect may depend on host susceptibility factors that are not yet defined. The biopsy’s complement deposition and macrophage-predominant inflammation support an immune-mediated effector mechanism rather than a purely toxic, self-limited myonecrosis.

In clinical practice these considerations lead to several pragmatic recommendations grounded in this case and current knowledge. If a patient develops progressive proximal weakness or new systemic muscle symptoms after a GLP-1RA, clinicians should promptly check CK and consider neuromuscular referral; an isolated, acute CK spike with prompt recovery after stopping the drug is more consistent with toxic rhabdomyolysis, while delayed progressive weakness, very high or rising CK over weeks–months, or inflammatory features on imaging/biopsy should raise concern for IMNM and prompt immunologic evaluation and early consideration of immunosuppression. Rechallenge with the suspected GLP-1RA after an episode suggestive of IMNM should be avoided because of the risk of recurrence and because it would be unethical in an individual patient. All suspected serious adverse reactions should be reported to local pharmacovigilance systems to allow case aggregation and signal detection. For the research community, systematic pharmacovigilance, case registries, controlled pharmacoepidemiologic studies, and mechanistic work—especially studies of autoantibody responses, HLA associations, and the effect of GLP-1RAs on innate/adaptive immunity—are needed to determine whether there is a true causal relationship and which patients are at risk.

In summary, this case presents a plausible, temporally associated IMNM after semaglutide exposure distinct from the acute toxic rhabdomyolysis reported with other agents. The pathology and clinical response favour an immune-mediated mechanism. While the evidence supports a probable association by established causality criteria, further cases and mechanistic studies are required before a definitive causal link can be drawn or routine screening recommendations made.

## Management, Follow-up, and Practical Lessons

Initial management of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is driven by severity of weakness (especially bulbar or respiratory involvement), rapidity of progression, and objective markers such as creatine kinase (CK). In this case the patient had progressive proximal weakness with bulbar symptoms and a CK that rose into the thousands, which appropriately prompted aggressive initial immunotherapy. High‑dose corticosteroids remain the first‑line immediate therapy: an intravenous pulse (methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days) is commonly used when rapid control is needed, followed by oral prednisone at approximately 1 mg/kg/day. This approach treats ongoing immune‑mediated muscle injury and stabilises patients while additional therapies take effect.

IVIG has an established role in IMNM, particularly when there are severe features (bulbar dysfunction, marked weakness, rapidly rising CK) or when a rapid clinical response is required. Typical dosing is 2 g/kg given over 2–5 days (often split over 3–5 infusions). In the presented patient, IVIG was added because of persistent severe weakness and bulbar involvement; the combination of pulse steroids and IVIG frequently produces a relatively fast biochemical and clinical improvement in drug‑triggered or antibody‑positive IMNM.

Decisions about adding steroid‑sparing agents depend on the presumed cause and clinical trajectory. Idiopathic or antibody‑positive IMNM (for example anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP) often requires prolonged multi‑agent immunosuppression: methotrexate or mycophenolate are common steroid‑sparing choices started early to allow steroid tapering, and rituximab is used for refractory disease or certain antibody profiles. By contrast, when a drug trigger is suspected and the patient shows a clear clinical and CK response after withdrawal and a short course of immunotherapy (as in this case), prolonged immunosuppression may not be necessary. Practically, reserve addition of steroid‑sparing agents for patients who fail to improve within several weeks, whose CK remains elevated, who relapse during steroid taper, or who have steroid‑related toxicity.

Monitoring during treatment and follow‑up should be structured and multidisciplinary. Serial CK measurements are the most accessible biochemical marker of disease activity; interpret CK alongside AST/ALT (which may be elevated from muscle as well as liver). Early and repeated quantitative strength assessments (e.g., manual muscle testing or timed functional measures) document recovery. Bulbar symptoms warrant formal swallow evaluation and otolaryngology or speech–language pathology input; respiratory muscle involvement should prompt baseline and serial spirometry with supine and upright forced vital capacity and, if available, maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures or overnight oximetry. MRI of affected muscle groups is useful to document active inflammatory edema and to guide biopsy when needed; MRI changes also help track response. Cardiac surveillance is indicated when troponin/pro‑BNP are abnormal or when symptoms/signs suggest myocarditis: ECG, transthoracic echocardiography, and cardiac MRI are the preferred modalities and were appropriately obtained in this patient because of elevated cardiac biomarkers and MRI evidence of myocardial edema. Repeat imaging or biomarker reassessment should follow until findings stabilise or normalise.

Tapering corticosteroids is individualized. In drug‑associated IMNM with a robust early response (clinical improvement and CK normalisation), an accelerated taper can be reasonable to minimise steroid toxicity—many clinicians begin taper after clear improvement and aim to stop or reach a low maintenance dose over weeks to a few months while monitoring closely. In idiopathic or antibody‑positive IMNM, a slower taper over many months is generally required to avoid relapse. Relapse vigilance is essential after discontinuation: new or recurrent weakness, worsening dysphagia, or any CK rise should prompt re‑evaluation. Practical follow‑up frequency is typically every 1–4 weeks initially (with CK and clinical exam), then lengthened to every 2–3 months once stable.

Preventing and managing complications of treatment and of the disease is a critical part of care. High‑dose steroids and adjunctive immunotherapy increase infection risk; inpatient periods of high‑dose therapy warrant attention to catheter care, urine monitoring, and early treatment of infections (this patient developed a pseudomonal UTI and septic shock). Consider Pneumocystis prophylaxis (trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole) when prolonged corticosteroid therapy (>20 mg prednisone daily for >4 weeks) or additional immunosuppression is anticipated. Vaccinate against influenza, pneumococcus, COVID‑19 and other indicated non‑live vaccines before starting immunosuppression when feasible; live vaccines should be avoided during significant immunosuppression. IVIG carries specific risks including volume overload, renal dysfunction, infusion reactions, and thromboembolic events; assess renal function and thrombotic risk before and during infusions and adjust infusion rate as needed. Bone health should be addressed early with calcium, vitamin D, lifestyle measures, and consideration of bisphosphonate therapy for those at sustained high steroid exposure. Immobility, inflammation and steroids increase venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk — prophylactic anticoagulation during hospitalisation and active disease should be considered, especially in patients with prior VTE.

Rehabilitation and nutrition are central to recovery. Early, supervised physical and occupational therapy focused on graded strengthening and prevention of contractures improves functional outcomes; avoid overly aggressive exercise in the inflammatory phase. For bulbar dysfunction, swallow therapy and dietary modification reduce aspiration risk; temporary enteral feeding should be considered for severe dysphagia. Nutritional strategies include adequate protein intake and caloric support to preserve or rebuild lean muscle mass, and attention to vitamin and micronutrient status.

Long‑term planning after an episode of presumed drug‑triggered IMNM includes avoidance of the suspected offending agent(s) — in this case, GLP‑1 receptor agonists — and careful documentation of the adverse drug reaction in the medical record. Counsel the patient about alternative weight‑management strategies (lifestyle modification, referral to obesity medicine or bariatric care, and discussion of other pharmacologic agents with a different mechanism) using shared decision‑making that weighs metabolic benefits against potential immune risks. Report the adverse event to pharmacovigilance authorities to contribute to post‑marketing safety data. Follow‑up visits should continue at regular intervals (initially frequent, then spacing as stability is demonstrated) with CK checks and clinical assessment; re‑evaluate sooner for any new symptoms suggesting relapse.

Key practical lessons from this case
- IMNM can be seronegative; absence of myositis‑specific antibodies does not exclude the disease and muscle biopsy remains diagnostic when clinical suspicion is high.  
- Differentiate immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy from toxic rhabdomyolysis by time course (persistently rising CK and progressive weakness over weeks–months suggest immune mediation) and by biopsy showing necrosis with inflammatory markers rather than solely acute necrosis.  
- Consider recently introduced drugs as potential triggers for IMNM; prompt withdrawal plus early immunotherapy (steroids ± IVIG) can be effective when a drug etiology is suspected.  
- Tailor immunosuppression intensity and duration to response: drug‑associated cases may remit after a short early course, whereas idiopathic/antibody‑positive IMNM often requires prolonged and multi‑agent therapy.  
- Monitor for and proactively mitigate complications of disease and therapy (infection, VTE, bone loss, swallowing/respiratory compromise), and coordinate rehabilitation and nutrition to optimise functional recovery.

In summary, aggressive early treatment (as used for this patient: pulse steroids followed by high‑dose prednisone and a one‑time course of IVIG) is appropriate for severe IMNM with bulbar involvement. Close monitoring, prevention of treatment complications, dedicated rehabilitation, and avoidance of the suspected trigger are the pillars of management and of achieving durable recovery.
