Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] Using the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality framework, which elements in this case support a “probable” relationship between semaglutide and the myopathy (time relationship, alternative causes unlikely, response to withdrawal), and why is rechallenge generally inappropriate?

Short answer: The case meets the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre “probable” category because (1) the temporal sequence is compatible with a drug‑triggered immune myopathy, (2) competing explanations are unlikely based on history, serology and biopsy, and (3) there was a clinically meaningful response after drug withdrawal and immunotherapy. Rechallenge is generally inappropriate because of the high risk of provoking a recurrent — potentially more severe or life‑threatening — immune reaction and because a deliberate re‑exposure would be unethical when safer alternatives exist.

Why this is “probable” (WHO‑UMC reasoning tied to the case)
- Time relationship: the single semaglutide dose preceded the patient’s immediate intolerance and systemic illness (nausea, vomiting, AKI) within hours to days, and the progressive proximal weakness with a marked CK rise evolved over the expected interval for an immune‑mediated adverse event (weeks to months). The chronology (exposure → symptom onset → progressive myopathy) fulfills the required reasonable temporal association.
- Alternative causes unlikely: the patient denied statin or other known myotoxic exposures; myositis autoantibody panel (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) was negative; there was no clinical or laboratory evidence of a systemic connective‑tissue disease to explain the picture; no family history or other obvious metabolic/toxic cause was identified. Muscle biopsy demonstrated necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage infiltration, membrane attack complex deposition and scant lymphocytes — histology consistent with IMNM and not simply metabolic myopathy or pure toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- Response to withdrawal (dechallenge) and treatment: semaglutide was not continued, and after high‑dose steroids + IVIG the patient’s CK fell rapidly and her strength and function recovered to baseline. Importantly, she remained off immunosuppression and off GLP‑1 receptor agonists for many months with sustained clinical and biochemical remission — a pattern more typical of a drug‑triggered IMNM that resolves after removal of the trigger and a finite course of immunotherapy than of idiopathic IMNM, which frequently requires prolonged immunosuppression.

Important caveats that keep the label at “probable” rather than “certain”
- No pre‑exposure baseline CK is available, so a pre‑existing smouldering myopathy cannot be excluded definitively.
- Immunosuppression (steroids + IVIG) is a confounder for dechallenge interpretation — the improvement may reflect effective treatment rather than withdrawal alone. The durable remission off therapy and drug does, however, support a trigger relationship.
- Lack of a disease‑specific autoantibody (seronegative IMNM) limits mechanistic proof; about one‑third of IMNM cases are seronegative.

Why rechallenge is generally inappropriate in this context
- Safety/ethics: deliberate re‑exposure risks recurrence of the same immune process with unpredictable severity — dysphagia/respiratory muscle involvement, myocarditis, or fulminant necrotising myopathy can be life‑threatening. Exposing a recovered patient to that risk solely to prove causality is not ethically justified.
- Immunologic principle: immune‑mediated adverse reactions often become more rapid and severe on re‑exposure because of immunologic memory; a positive rechallenge therefore may cause a worse outcome than the index event.
- Clinical utility: a positive rechallenge would provide stronger causal proof but confers unacceptable patient risk, and a negative rechallenge could be misleading if immunomodulatory treatment or waning immune responsiveness confounds results.
- Alternatives to rechallenge: careful reporting to pharmacovigilance systems, case aggregation, and mechanistic studies (for example, searching for shared HLA associations or autoantibody discovery) are safer ways to establish causality at the population level.

Bottom line: The case fulfills the WHO‑UMC criteria for a “probable” adverse‑drug reaction — compatible timing, lack of plausible alternative explanations, and sustained clinical improvement after stopping semaglutide and treating the immune process — but rechallenge to prove causality would be inappropriate because it risks provoking a recurrent, potentially severe immune myopathy.
