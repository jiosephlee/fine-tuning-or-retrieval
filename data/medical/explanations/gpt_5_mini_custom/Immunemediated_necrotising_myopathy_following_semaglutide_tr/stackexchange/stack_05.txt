Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Management] What is your initial treatment strategy for severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy with dysphagia, including steroid dosing (pulse methylprednisolone and transition to oral prednisone), indications for IVIG, and early monitoring for respiratory, swallowing, and cardiac complications?

Bottom line
For severe immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with bulbar involvement (dysphagia) treat promptly with high‑dose IV corticosteroids, add IVIG early for severe/bulbar or rapidly progressive disease, and institute immediate focused monitoring for respiratory failure, aspiration risk and cardiac involvement. In the presented case this approach (methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily ×5 → oral prednisone 1 mg/kg and IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 4 days) resulted in rapid CK fall and clinical recovery — a practical template for initial management.

Initial immunotherapy
- Pulse steroids: give IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily (typical range 500–1000 mg) for 3–5 days in life‑ or function‑threatening myositis (marked proximal weakness, bulbar dysfunction, rapidly rising CK). The team in this case used 1 g daily ×5.
- Transition to oral steroids: after pulses, convert to oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day (usual practical max ~60–80 mg) and reassess frequently. Plan a taper guided by clinical strength, swallowing, and CK trend rather than fixed calendar once improving.
- IVIG indications and dosing: add IVIG early (total 2 g/kg, commonly given over 2–5 days) when there is bulbar involvement/dysphagia, severe weakness, respiratory compromise, very high CK, or when rapid effect is desired or when antibodies (anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR) predict IVIG responsiveness. In this case IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days was given because of persistent weakness and severe features and likely hastened improvement.
- When to add steroid‑sparing agents: if incomplete response after 2–4 weeks, steroid dependence, or relapsing disease, add a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant (eg, methotrexate, azathioprine) or consider rituximab/cyclophosphamide in refractory or life‑threatening cases. (The reported patient improved and did not require prolonged agents.)

Early and ongoing monitoring (prioritize)
1) Respiratory
- Baseline and serial measurements: bedside forced vital capacity (FVC) sitting and supine, negative inspiratory force (NIF/MIP), and pulse oximetry. Obtain arterial blood gas if hypercapnia suspected.
- Thresholds for escalation: FVC <50% predicted, a fall in FVC >20% when supine, or NIF less negative than about −30 cm H2O warrant ICU monitoring and low threshold for elective intubation to protect ventilation/airway.
- Monitor frequently (daily to several times daily) in the acute phase if bulbar weakness or declining FVC.

2) Swallowing and nutrition
- Otolaryngology/speech–language pathology bedside swallow evaluation before oral intake. Keep patient NPO until swallowing is judged safe if there is dysphagia or aspiration concern.
- Early enteral access (nasogastric or percutaneous gastrostomy if prolonged) should be considered to maintain nutrition and prevent aspiration. In practice, bulbar IMNM is an accepted indication to add IVIG early because of high aspiration risk.

3) Cardiac
- Baseline ECG, troponin (high‑sensitivity), BNP, and transthoracic echocardiography if any abnormalities or symptoms. Consider cardiology consult and cardiac MRI if myocarditis suspected.
- Continuous telemetry if troponin/BNP elevated, arrhythmia history, or cardiac MRI/echo abnormalities. In this case the patient had mild hs‑troponin elevation, markedly elevated proBNP, and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema — all arguing for close cardiac monitoring and cardiology input.

4) Laboratory and infection surveillance
- Daily or near‑daily CK and basic metabolic panel early (to track muscle injury, renal function, and steroid side effects). Monitor glucose, CBC, electrolytes, and liver tests.
- VTE prophylaxis while immobilized.
- Be vigilant for infection: high‑dose steroids ± IVIG increase infection risk (the case patient subsequently developed pseudomonal urosepsis). If steroids are to be continued at immunosuppressive doses >3–4 weeks, consider Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis per institutional practice.

Supportive care and disposition
- Admit to monitored bed (step‑down/ICU) if bulbar dysfunction, declining FVC, or cardiac involvement.
- Early physical and occupational therapy when safe; swallow therapy.
- Coordinate rheumatology/neuromuscular, neurology, cardiology, speech–language pathology, and nutrition services.

How this case supports the approach
- The team used 1 g IV methylprednisolone daily ×5 then oral prednisone 1 mg/kg with IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 4 days because of severe proximal weakness and dysphagia. CK fell markedly over 2 weeks and the patient regained baseline function off immunosuppression over months. Cardiac markers and MRI abnormalities prompted cardiac imaging and monitoring, and the patient later developed bacterial sepsis — underscoring infection risk with aggressive immunosuppression and the need for vigilance.

Clinical pearl
Treat severe IMNM early and aggressively when bulbar or respiratory or cardiac involvement is present: pulse steroids → transition to prednisone 1 mg/kg, add IVIG (2 g/kg) promptly for bulbar disease or rapidly progressive weakness, and institute frequent respiratory, swallow and cardiac monitoring to anticipate airway, ventilatory, or cardiac complications.
