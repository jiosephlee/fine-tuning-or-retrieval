Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients with elevated transaminases and concomitant hyperCKemia, what clinical and laboratory clues help distinguish hepatic transaminitis from muscle-derived enzyme elevations (e.g., AST/ALT pattern, gamma-glutamyl transferase, bilirubin, imaging, and timing relative to CK), and how would you apply them in a patient with known NAFLD?

Takeaway: when AST and ALT are both elevated in a patient with concurrent CK elevation, ask first “liver or muscle?” Key discriminators are the pattern of AST vs ALT, liver-specific tests (GGT, bilirubin, synthetic function), temporal relationship to CK, prior baseline liver tests and imaging, and targeted muscle testing. Applying these systematically separates true hepatic transaminitis from muscle-derived transaminase release — a distinction that mattered in this patient with known NAFLD.

Why this works (pathophysiology, briefly)
- AST is present in both liver and skeletal muscle (cytosolic and mitochondrial isoforms); ALT is far more liver‑enriched. Significant muscle injury therefore raises AST proportionally more than ALT.  
- GGT and bilirubin are produced in the liver/biliary system and are not elevated from skeletal muscle injury. Synthetic markers (albumin, INR, platelets) reflect chronic hepatic function, not muscle breakdown.  
- Enzyme timing: CK rises rapidly with muscle injury and falls over days (CK half‑life ≈ 36 h). AST and ALT have their own half‑lives (AST shorter than ALT), so when transaminases are muscle‑derived they should move in parallel with CK; persistent transaminase elevation after CK normalizes points to hepatic origin.

Practical clinical and laboratory clues
- AST/ALT pattern
  - Muscle source: AST often ≥ ALT (AST≫ALT or AST>ALT), and transaminase elevations generally correlate with CK magnitude.  
  - Hepatic source (including NAFLD): ALT commonly > AST (especially early/mild NAFLD); AST>ALT can occur with fibrosis/cirrhosis or alcoholic injury.
- GGT and alkaline phosphatase
  - GGT elevated → supports hepatic/biliary origin. GGT normal → favors muscle source when CK is high. Alkaline phosphatase is less specific but helps when cholestasis is suspected.
- Bilirubin and synthetic markers
  - Elevated bilirubin, low albumin, prolonged INR, thrombocytopenia → support hepatic dysfunction, not muscle injury.
- CK and timing
  - If CK is high and AST/ALT rise at the same time, suspect muscle contribution. Repeat tests after CK normalizes: if transaminases fall with CK, muscle was the major source; if transaminases persist, liver is likely responsible.
- Imaging and prior tests
  - Ultrasound/MRI showing steatosis or biopsy-proven NAFLD supports chronic hepatic source. Prior baseline transaminases are invaluable.
- Targeted muscle testing if suspicion remains
  - Aldolase, LDH, myoglobin, myositis serologies, muscle MRI, EMG, and ultimately muscle biopsy for definitive myopathy diagnosis.

Applying this to the presented case
- Baseline data: the patient had chronic mild transaminase elevation 3 years before semaglutide, with ALT>AST, normal GGT, normal bilirubin and synthetic indices, ultrasound showing hepatosteatosis and liver biopsy consistent with NASH. Those data argue for a chronic hepatic source (NAFLD) for her baseline transaminase abnormalities.  
- At myopathy presentation: CK became markedly elevated (peak ~3992 U/L) and AST (191) slightly exceeded ALT (165). That change (AST rising with CK and AST≥ALT) is compatible with a muscle contribution to the transaminase elevation during the acute myopathy.  
- Follow‑up behavior: after immunosuppression and recovery the CK normalized and the patient’s transaminases returned to the same borderline elevations seen previously (ALT>AST), consistent with chronic hepatic (NAFLD) rather than ongoing muscle injury. This temporal pattern — transaminases rising with CK during active myopathy but returning to baseline hepatic pattern once CK normalized — is the classic discriminator.

Bottom line practical approach at the bedside
1. Measure CK whenever transaminases are elevated without clear liver syndrome.  
2. Check GGT, bilirubin and basic synthetic function (INR, albumin, platelets).  
3. Compare AST vs ALT pattern and prior liver tests; review imaging/biopsy if available.  
4. Reassess after CK normalizes: a fall of AST/ALT with CK → muscle source; persistence → liver.  
5. If muscle source is suspected, pursue myositis workup (aldolase, myositis serologies, MRI/EMG, biopsy) rather than escalating hepatic workup.  
6. In patients with known NAFLD, don’t assume all transaminase elevations are hepatic — use the CK/GGT/timing strategy to avoid misattribution.

In this patient the preexisting NAFLD explained the chronic ALT>AST and the persistent mild transaminitis after CK normalized, while the transient AST predominance and marked CK elevation during her illness signaled a superimposed muscle source (IMNM) during the acute episode.
