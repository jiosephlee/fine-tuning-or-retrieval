Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Imaging and Biopsy Strategy in Suspected Inflammatory Myopathy

What a case to wake up a team. A previously well man in his 40s arrives with subacute neck “angioedema,” dysphagia and subtle proximal weakness. CT shows marked soft‑tissue swelling in the neck but no abscess. Flexible nasendoscopy is unrevealing. It’s only after noting periungual changes and ordering a CK that the picture snaps into focus: CK >16,000 U/L, FDG‑PET with diffuse muscle uptake (head and neck conspicuous), thigh MRI with active myositis, and a muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis with C5b‑9 capillary deposition. Final diagnosis: anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis presenting as pseudo‑angioedema with significant bulbar/airway muscle involvement.

This case is a neat microcosm of modern myositis work‑up: use MRI and FDG‑PET not as redundant tests but as complementary tools — MRI to map muscle edema and phenotype, PET to survey for occult malignancy and to highlight metabolically active disease in anatomically difficult regions (eg, head/neck). Use those maps to target biopsy and avoid a useless sample.

Below I’ll walk through practical imaging protocols, how to interpret MRI edema patterns (and why they matter), FDG‑PET strengths and pitfalls, the pathology signatures that distinguish dermatomyositis, IMNM and polymyositis, and tactical tips to maximize diagnostic yield from a muscle biopsy.

## Start with the clinical context — imaging is to answer specific questions
Before ordering MRI or PET ask:
- Do I need to confirm active inflammatory myopathy vs chronic/degenerative change?
- Do I need to map the best biopsy site?
- Am I screening for malignancy as a trigger of myositis?
- Are there airway/bulbar muscles involved that MRI of the thighs will miss?

In our patient the neck swelling and dysphagia made mapping head/neck disease crucial — MRI thighs alone would have missed the most clinically threatening sites. FDG‑PET illuminated extensive head/neck muscle activity and showed no occult malignancy.

## MRI: how to tailor sequences and how to read the patterns
Protocol essentials
- Axial and coronal T1, axial and coronal fluid‑sensitive sequences (T2 FS or STIR), and post‑contrast T1 FS if available.
- Slice thickness ~4–5 mm; include pelvis and proximal thighs for routine proximal myopathy. For bulbar/neck symptoms, extend to neck and masticator spaces.
- Compare contralateral sides and include fat‑sensitive T1 to assess chronic fatty replacement.

What to look for (and what it suggests)
- Perifascicular/ perifascicular edema (signal hyperintensity at the muscle periphery on STIR/T2 FS): classic for dermatomyositis. Often patchy, near fascial planes.
- Subcutaneous and fascial edema: more common in dermatomyositis and antisynthetase syndromes; also explains “pseudo‑angioedema.”
- Symmetric proximal muscle edema with diffuse involvement: can be seen in IMNM and polymyositis; IMNM often shows more widespread, confluent edema reflecting necrosis.
- Endomysial involvement with fatty change and atrophy (T1 hyperintensity): suggests chronic disease or inclusion body myositis if distal/patchy and asymmetric.
- Enhancement after gadolinium correlates with active inflammation and may guide biopsy.
Practical MRI cues to pick a biopsy site
- Target areas with maximal STIR/T2 FS hyperintensity but avoid regions of advanced fatty replacement (high T1 signal) — fatty tissue yields little diagnostic inflammatory signal.
- Choose a muscle large enough to yield multiple cores or an open specimen (eg, vastus lateralis, rectus femoris, deltoid depending on imaging).
- If edema is patchy, pick the most hyperintense region and document exact location (level and relation to anatomical landmarks) for the surgeon.

In the case: thigh MRI showed active perifascicular changes in obturator internus/externus and distal iliopsoas — imaging helped document the extent and select a representative thigh site for biopsy even though the most life‑threatening activity lay in the neck.

## FDG‑PET: use it as a combined disease map and malignancy screen — but know its limits
What PET is good for
- Whole‑body survey for occult malignancy in myositis patients (high yield in dermatomyositis).
- Identifying metabolically active muscles in regions outside routine MRI coverage — e.g., bulbar and neck muscles where biopsy is impractical but recognition of involvement alters management.
- Highlighting multifocal active disease in patchy myositis.

Key PET pitfalls
- Muscle FDG uptake is nonspecific: recent exercise, corticosteroids, insulin, inflammation, infection and even physiologic uptake can produce diffuse or focal signals.
- PET picks up inflammation — not specific for autoimmune myositis versus infection/injury.
- Small mucosal or early cancers (eg, some GI neoplasms) can be PET‑occult; PET complements but does not replace directed endoscopy and CT as indicated.
- Timing matters: recent strenuous activity before PET can dramatically increase muscle uptake. Ensure patients avoid exercise 24–48 hours pre‑scan.
Practical PET tips
- Ask about recent exertion, steroid timing, or recent IVIG — these can alter uptake.
- Use PET to decide if additional imaging (MRI targeted to PET‑positive region) or urgent interventions (eg, protect airway when bulbar muscles light up) are needed.
- If PET shows focal uptake suspicious for malignancy, correlate with CT or targeted imaging and proceed to directed biopsy as indicated.

In our patient PET made two big contributions: it documented head/neck metabolic activity (explaining dysphagia and soft‑tissue swelling) and it showed no hypermetabolic malignancy elsewhere, which guided the initial cancer screen strategy.

## Pathology: what to expect and why it matters clinically
Dermatomyositis (DM)
- Histology: perifascicular atrophy/necrosis, perivascular/perimysial inflammation (often B cells and plasmacytoid dendritic cells), capillary loss.
- Immunopathology: deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) in endomysial/perifascicular capillaries is a supportive marker.
- Clinical correlation: skin findings, subcutaneous/fascial edema, higher association with ILD and malignancy in some subtypes (eg, anti‑TIF1‑γ).

Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)
- Histology: prominent myofibre necrosis with minimal lymphocytic inflammation; macrophage‑predominant cleanup; necrosis out of proportion to inflammatory infiltrate.
- Often associated antibodies: anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP.
- Clinical correlation: very high CKs, severe weakness, treatment may require aggressive immunosuppression.

Polymyositis (PM) — increasingly uncommon as a pure entity
- Histology: endomysial CD8+ T‑cell infiltrates with invasion of non‑necrotic fibers expressing MHC‑I.
- Consider alternative diagnoses (eg, inclusion body myositis) if there is distal, asymmetric weakness or older age.

How pathology feeds back into treatment
- Finding perifascicular necrosis with C5b‑9 deposition strongly supports dermatomyositis and prompts cancer screening, consideration of ILD evaluation, and early combined immunosuppression.
- IMNM pathology pushes toward rapid escalation (IVIG + steroid‑sparing agent, sometimes rituximab, earlier) because of necrotic dominance and potential refractoriness.

The muscle biopsy in our patient demonstrated classic dermatomyositis features including perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition — clinching the diagnosis when combined with CK, antibody profile and imaging.

## Avoiding a nondiagnostic biopsy — a checklist
1. Use imaging to map active disease and avoid fat‑replaced muscle. MRI STIR/T2 FS hyperintensity + PET hypermetabolism = best target.
2. Time biopsy before or as early as possible relative to immunosuppression. If high‑dose steroids or IVIG cannot be delayed (airway compromise, severe dysphagia), proceed with treatment but recognize sensitivity of biopsy may decrease.
3. Choose the right muscle and technique:
   - For patchy disease, open biopsy gives larger tissue and more diagnostic yield.
   - Core needle biopsy under ultrasound or MRI guidance is acceptable if you can accurately target the active area and obtain multiple cores.
4. Take sufficient tissue: aim for multiple cores or an open specimen including fascia; include skin if cutaneous signs are present and may help with diagnosis.
5. Communicate with pathology: indicate clinical suspicion, antibody results, recent treatments (steroids/IVIG), and imaging findings so special stains (eg, C5b‑9, MHC‑I, immune markers) are applied.
6. Avoid sampling chronically fatty muscles or atrophic ends of a muscle — these often show non‑specific findings.
7. Document exact biopsy location and orientation to correlate with imaging.

## Workflow example — a coordinated pathway
1. Clinical suspicion (CK elevated, weakness, skin signs): order targeted MRI (include symptomatic regions) and myositis antibody panel.
2. If malignancy is a concern or disease extends beyond MRI field (eg, head and neck), add whole‑body FDG‑PET.
3. Correlate MRI STIR/T2 hyperintense areas with PET hypermetabolism; choose the best accessible site for biopsy (active on MRI, hypermetabolic on PET).
4. Schedule biopsy prior to initiation of high‑dose immunosuppression if clinically safe. If not, proceed with therapy and still obtain biopsy when feasible — document timing.
5. Send tissue with a clear clinical note; include request for routine H&E, immunohistochemistry for complement (C5b‑9), MHC‑I, inflammatory cell markers and electron microscopy if IBM is in differential.
6. Parallel malignancy screening based on risk (age, male sex, dysphagia, myositis subtype/antibodies) — CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET, and directed endoscopy as indicated.

## Pitfalls to watch for
- Relying on a single imaging modality: MRI and PET answer different questions; use them synergistically.
- Misinterpreting PET muscle uptake without checking for recent exercise or steroid effects.
- Biopsying the “closest” weak muscle rather than the “most active” muscle on imaging — convenience leads to nondiagnostic samples.
- Forgetting to screen for malignancy in high‑risk myositis subtypes (eg, dermatomyositis with dysphagia).
- Ignoring skin biopsy when cutaneous signs are present — skin can aid diagnosis in dermatomyositis.

## Closing — keep the team talking
Imaging is not a ritual — it’s a map. MRI defines local anatomy and disease phenotype; FDG‑PET surveys the body and highlights metabolically active areas you might otherwise miss (head/neck, paraspinal, or multifocal disease). Use them together to guide biopsy selection, minimize nondiagnostic specimens, and prioritize malignancy screening. Communicate imaging, serology and clinical urgency with the surgeon and pathologist so the specimen is taken from the right place, at the right time, and processed with the right stains.

In our case, that coordinated approach transformed a confusing presentation masquerading as angioedema into a targeted diagnosis of SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis — and allowed rapid, appropriate immunosuppression that ultimately led to clinical recovery.

Key take‑home points
- Use MRI STIR/T2 FS to map active muscle edema and avoid fatty‑replaced or atrophic muscle when selecting biopsy sites.
- FDG‑PET is valuable both for malignancy screening and for detecting active disease in regions not covered by standard MRI (eg, head/neck, bulbar muscles), but interpret uptake in the context of recent exercise, steroids or IVIG.
- Dermatomyositis on biopsy: perifascicular atrophy/necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition; IMNM: prominent necrosis with scant inflammation; polymyositis: endomysial CD8+ T‑cell invasion.
- Coordinate timing: biopsy ideally before high‑dose immunosuppression; if treatment is urgent, document timing and proceed — pathology may still be informative.
- Multidisciplinary communication (clinician → radiologist → surgeon → pathologist) is the single most important step to avoid nondiagnostic biopsies and to get a rapid, accurate diagnosis.
