Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When ‘Hepatitis’ Is Muscle: Interpreting AST/ALT in Myositis

What makes this case interesting: a man in his 40s presents to the ED with progressive neck “angioedema” and dysphagia, is worked up for infection and angioedema, and then—because someone asked for a creatine kinase (CK)—turns out to have CK 16,184 U/L with AST 396 U/L, ALT 143 U/L and LDH 692 U/L. The diagnosis was anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with significant muscle inflammation around the head/neck causing soft‑tissue oedema and dysphagia. Liver enzymes that look “hepatic” were actually muscle-derived.

If you see unexplained transaminase elevations in a weak or dyspnoeic patient, don’t reflex into an exhaustive hepatic workup first. Muscle disease can produce very high CK and an AST>ALT pattern with raised LDH, and this pattern should redirect you to look for myositis—or other muscle injury—early.

## The trap
AST and ALT are often framed as “liver enzymes,” but AST is abundant in skeletal muscle (and heart, kidney, RBCs) while ALT is more liver‑predominant. In substantial muscle injury (trauma, rhabdomyolysis, inflammatory myopathy) CK skyrockets, AST typically rises higher than ALT, and LDH goes up. That pattern can mimic hepatocellular injury and prompt unnecessary imaging, hepatology referrals, or delays in immunosuppression when the real problem is inflammatory myositis.

In our case:
- CK = 16,184 U/L (massive)
- AST = 396 U/L, ALT = 143 U/L (AST > ALT)
- LDH = 692 U/L
- Urine myoglobin screen was negative, and renal function remained normal
- The patient’s CK normalized (37 U/L) within weeks of targeted immunosuppression

## Quick physiology primer
- CK is the most specific and sensitive marker of skeletal‑muscle necrosis in the acute/subacute setting. Very high CKs (often >1,000 U/L, sometimes tens of thousands) point to muscle injury.
- AST is present in both liver and muscle; ALT is more liver‑specific. So muscle disease more commonly gives an AST‑predominant picture.
- LDH rises in both liver and muscle injury and tends to parallel the CK/AST pattern.
- Urine myoglobin may be absent even with huge CK elevations; absence of myoglobinuria does not exclude severe muscle injury.
- Markers that support true hepatic injury: high bilirubin, elevated alkaline phosphatase/GGT consistent with cholestasis, synthetic dysfunction (INR ↑), hepatomegaly on exam/US, or ALT >> AST.

## Practical, bedside approach when you see elevated transaminases in a weak/dyspnoeic patient
1. Look at the clinical context first
   - Any proximal muscle weakness, myalgias, rashes (heliotrope, Gottron’s, shawl/V‑sign), dysphagia, or facial/neck swelling? These point to myositis.
2. Order CK (and repeat if clinical suspicion is high)
   - A very high CK should move muscle higher on your differential than isolated hepatic injury.
3. Check LDH, AST, ALT together and compare patterns
   - AST > ALT with markedly elevated CK suggests muscle source.
4. Check renal function and urine for myoglobin/urinalysis
   - Rhabdomyolysis → risk of AKI; manage with fluids and monitor closely even if urine myoglobin is negative.
5. Basic hepatic safety labs before immunosuppression
   - Viral hepatitis screen (HBsAg, anti‑HBc, HCV), HIV, and other infection screens as local guidance/oncologic risk dictate. (In the case above these screens were negative and informed safe initiation of immunosuppression.)
6. Image and subspecialty input as indicated
   - MRI of affected muscles, chest imaging (for ILD risk in myositis), myositis antibody panel and rheumatology/immunology input if dermatomyositis suspected. Muscle biopsy if diagnosis remains uncertain.
7. Use serial trends to monitor disease and treatment response
   - CK is typically the most responsive lab to treatment. In the reported case CK fell from 16k to normal within weeks after steroids, IVIG, MMF and rituximab—supporting muscle origin of the transaminase elevations.
8. Don’t forget other causes of AST>ALT
   - Alcoholic hepatitis, ischemic hepatitis, hemolysis, cardiac injury—consider the whole clinical picture.

## Case in point — what saved time here
- Careful bedside exam found periungual changes, a rash and proximal weakness. That prompted a CK, which flipped the workup from ENT/angioedema/infection to inflammatory myopathy.
- Appropriate infection screens were performed prior to aggressive immunosuppression.
- Treatment targeted the underlying immune myositis and the biochemical abnormalities (CK, AST/ALT, LDH) normalized as the myositis improved.

## Clinical pearls
- Always ask: does this patient have muscle signs? If yes, check CK.
- AST>ALT with markedly elevated CK almost always suggests muscle injury rather than primary liver disease.
- A normal urine myoglobin does not rule out severe muscle injury—don’t be reassured solely by a negative myoglobin test.
- Before starting high‑dose steroids or other immunosuppression, screen for chronic viral infections (HBV, HCV, HIV) and consider latent TB per local protocols—this was done in the reported case.
- Use serial CK (and AST/ALT/LDH) to monitor response to therapy; CK usually normalizes faster than clinical recovery of function.

## Take‑home messages
- In a weak or dyspnoeic patient, unexplained transaminase elevations warrant a CK. Very high CK with AST>ALT and raised LDH = think muscle, not liver.
- Don’t chase liver imaging or invasive hepatic workup first when the clinical picture fits myositis; pivot to muscle testing and rheumatology/immunology input.
- Screen for viral hepatitis and other infections before starting immunosuppression.
- Monitor serial CK/AST/ALT/LDH to follow disease activity and treatment response—the case above illustrates dramatic biochemical recovery after appropriate myositis therapy.

If you want a one‑line algorithm to tape to your chart: unexplained transaminase rise + weakness/myalgias → CHECK CK.
