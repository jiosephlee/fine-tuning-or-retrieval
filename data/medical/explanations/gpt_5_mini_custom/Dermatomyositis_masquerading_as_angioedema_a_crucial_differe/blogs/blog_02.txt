Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Airway at Risk: Dysphagia and Extrathoracic Restriction in Dermatomyositis

What makes this case grab your attention? A previously well man in his 40s turns up to the ED with progressive neck swelling that looks—and even gets worked up as—angioedema or a deep neck infection. ENT nasendoscopy shows no obstructing lesion, but there are copious secretions and coughing. A careful exam finds subtle periungual changes and proximal weakness; CK is 16,184 U/L. Imaging shows myositis involving head/neck muscles and peribronchial consolidation consistent with microaspiration. Video‑fluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) demonstrates severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform residue and penetration. Final diagnosis: SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with marked upper aerodigestive muscle involvement. He required pulse steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing immunosuppression, and temporary nasogastric feeding.

Why this matters to ED, ENT, ICU and ward teams
- Dermatomyositis can present with “pseudoangioedema” from inflammatory soft‑tissue/muscle edema rather than histamine/bradykinin pathways. Neck swelling that doesn’t behave like typical angioedema should trigger a broader differential.
- Pharyngeal and laryngeal muscle involvement can produce severe dysphagia, pooling secretions, microaspiration and progressive extrathoracic restrictive physiology—putting the airway and lungs at real risk even without classic limb‑dominant weakness.
- Early recognition changes management: immediate swallowing assessment, aspiration mitigation, close airway surveillance and early aggressive immunotherapy when airway musculature is threatened.

Clinical pathway distilled from the case

Initial red flags
- Subacute progressive neck swelling not responding to usual angioedema therapies or without typical triggers (no ACE‑inhibitor, normal complements, no urticaria, non‑tender).
- New or subtle bulbar signs: increased secretions, cough with bolus, dysphonia, nasal regurgitation.
- Proximal muscle weakness or myalgias on exam.
- Very high CK (this case 16,184 U/L) and raised transaminases/LDH should prompt thinking beyond ENT/infectious causes.

Early investigations that matter
- ENT nasendoscopy: excludes structural obstruction and documents secretion burden and vocal cord function. In the case, it showed normal anatomy but increased secretions and coughing—more consistent with weakness than obstruction.
- Swallow study: VFSS or FEES (fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing). VFSS in this case confirmed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform residue and penetration.
- CK and basic myositis workup early if neuromuscular cause suspected.
- Cross‑sectional imaging: neck CT can show strap muscle edema; chest CT may show peribronchial consolidation from aspiration. PET/MRI can localize active myositis.
- Pulmonary function tests: extrathoracic restrictive pattern may be present when upper airway/neck muscles are involved (this patient had such a pattern without fibrotic ILD on imaging).
- Complement and C1 esterase function if angioedema is being considered—normal in this patient, helping de‑prioritize bradykinin/histamine mechanisms.
- Early multi‑disciplinary serology/biopsy: myositis antibody panel and muscle biopsy confirmed SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis here.

Aspiration mitigation and nutritional support — practical steps
- Low threshold to NBM (nil by mouth) when aspiration is suspected on bedside screening or when secretions/pooling are evident.
- Early speech pathology input for bedside assessment and planning for VFSS/FEES. In the case the VFSS guided the decision for NG feeding.
- Start enteral feeding via nasogastric tube when oral intake is unsafe. The patient required NG feeding for about 2 months.
- Plan for longer‑term enteral access (PEG) only if prolonged dysphagia persists despite therapy; avoid premature PEG insertion when recovery is likely.
- Airway hygiene: suctioning, chest physiotherapy, consider nebulized mucolytics; early antibiotics if aspiration pneumonitis evolves to pneumonia.
- Document diet and feeding precautions clearly in the chart and escalate to ICU if airway protection is marginal.

When to escalate immunotherapy for airway/ swallowing muscle compromise
- Bulbar/airway muscle involvement with:
  - Severe dysphagia with aspiration on VFSS/FEES
  - Inability to manage secretions, recurrent aspiration events, or progressive respiratory compromise
  - Very high CK supporting active inflammatory myopathy
- In this case the team gave 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily, followed by an oral taper, with early IVIG (2 g/kg loading then monthly cycles), mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab—an aggressive approach targeted at rapid control of limb and bulbar myositis.
- “Pulse” steroids + IVIG should be considered earlier when bulbar/airway muscles are threatened; they tend to produce faster disease control than oral steroids alone.
- Coordinate infectious prophylaxis (PJP prophylaxis was attempted with TMP‑SMX in this patient but stopped due to hemolysis; nebulized pentamidine was used thereafter).

Airway monitoring and thresholds for ICU/airway intervention
- Admit to a higher level of care if:
  - Rapidly progressive neck swelling affecting respiration
  - Increasing secretions and ineffective cough, recurrent aspiration, or respiratory distress
  - Worsening gas exchange, rising pCO2, hypoxia, or inability to protect airway
- Consider early elective intubation if bulbar weakness is severe and airway protection is uncertain—late emergency airway is riskier.
- Tracheostomy is an option for prolonged ventilatory/airway support or secretion management if recovery is delayed.

Practical coordination — who should do what, early
- ED/ward team: suspect non‑infective causes if presentation atypical; send CK early; NBM and involve speech pathology; start aspiration precautions; notify ENT and rheumatology/immunology.
- ENT: nasendoscopy to exclude obstruction and document secretions/vocal fold motion.
- Speech pathology: bedside screen, arrange VFSS or FEES, recommend NG/PEG and rehab plan.
- Radiology: targeted neck CT, chest CT, MRI/PET if myositis suspected.
- Rheumatology/Immunology: initiate myositis serology, consider early escalation to pulse steroids/IVIG and steroid‑sparing agents in coordination with infection screening.
- ICU: when ventilatory/airway risk is present; coordinate airway protection and respiratory support.

A few pathophysiologic reminders that help decision‑making
- Neck/face “angioedema” in dermatomyositis is usually inflammatory edema from muscle/soft tissue inflammation, not mast cell or bradykinin driven. That explains lack of response to antihistamines and normal complement/C1 esterase in many cases.
- Bulbar muscle inflammation predisposes to aspiration and can produce radiographic peribronchial consolidation and lower‑zone airspace change from microaspiration—the CT chest in this patient showed exactly that.
- Extrathoracic restrictive PFT patterns can reflect weakness of extrathoracic (upper airway) musculature without intrinsic lung fibrosis; correlate with imaging and clinical picture.

Clinical pearls
- Think myositis when neck “angioedema” is atypical: look for skin/nailfold changes and check CK early.
- Nasendoscopy can be normal anatomically but highly informative: pooling secretions and cough during the exam point to bulbar weakness.
- VFSS/FEES are essential to define aspiration risk and guide decisions about NBM and enteral feeding.
- Severe bulbar/airway involvement is an indication for urgent aggressive immunotherapy (pulse steroids ± IVIG) and close airway monitoring.
- Coordinate ENT, speech pathology, rheumatology/immunology and ICU early—multidisciplinary care is what prevents bad outcomes.

Take‑home messages
- Neck swelling that doesn’t fit classic angioedema or infection: consider inflammatory myopathy/dermatomyositis.
- If bulbar signs or secretions are present, get nasendoscopy and prompt VFSS/FEES; err on the side of NBM and early NG feeding to prevent aspiration.
- Severe pharyngeal dysphagia or threatened airway from myositis warrants early escalation to pulse steroids and IVIG in consultation with rheumatology/immunology.
- Multidisciplinary coordination (ENT, speech pathology, radiology, rheum/immunology, ICU) is essential to secure the airway, mitigate aspiration, and deliver timely immunosuppression.

This case is a great reminder: not all neck swellings are ENT problems—sometimes the airway is at risk because the muscles that protect it are on fire. Catch it early, protect the airway and the lungs, and treat the inflammation aggressively.
