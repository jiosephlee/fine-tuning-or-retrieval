Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Not All Swollen Necks Are Angioedema: Spotting Dermatomyositis in the ED

What makes this case interesting: a previously well man in his 40s presented with 3 weeks of progressive, atraumatic neck swelling that looked like Ludwig’s angina or angioedema but proved to be SAE1-positive dermatomyositis with massive CK elevation, dysphagia and inflammatory strap‑muscle oedema. He had normal C4 and normal C1‑esterase inhibitor function (arguing against bradykinin angioedema), CT neck with diffuse strap‑muscle oedema but no abscess, subtle periungual nailfold changes, and a CK of 16,184 U/L. Prompt recognition and targeted immunosuppression (pulse steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab) led to recovery of strength and resolution of neck swelling and dysphagia over weeks–months.

Why this matters to EM/ENT teams
- Atraumatic neck swelling commonly triggers an algorithm that focuses on infection, airway compromise and histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema. Inflammatory myopathies with subcutaneous/soft‑tissue oedema (pseudoangioedema) are uncommon but important mimics. Missing them delays definitive therapy and risks aspiration/airway dysfunction from pharyngeal and bulbar muscle involvement.
- Dermatomyositis can present with predominant head/neck swelling, dysphagia and prominent systemic muscle inflammation, even when classic rashes like Gottron’s or heliotrope are subtle or absent.

A practical ED approach to atraumatic neck swelling (quick bedside framework)
1. Airway first: look for stridor, muffled voice, drooling, progressive dysphagia, oxygenation/ventilation compromise. Early ENT/airway team involvement if concern.
2. Infection vs angioedema vs other
   - Infection: fever, leukocytosis, focal pain, fluctuant mass, CT with rim‑enhancing collection → ENT/surgical drainage.
   - Histaminergic angioedema: urticaria, pruritus, rapid onset, response to antihistamines/steroids/epinephrine.
   - Bradykinin‑mediated angioedema: ACEi history, family history, low C4/C1‑INH abnormality, delayed response to antihistamines/steroids.
   - Pseudoangioedema from myositis/inflammatory disease: subacute onset, inflammatory markers may be only mildly raised, muscle weakness or dysphagia, subtle skin/nail changes, imaging showing diffuse muscle/strap involvement without abscess.
3. Immediate useful tests (if safe and available)
   - Point-of-care: vitals, airway exam, bedside swallow, oxygenation.
   - Bloods: CBC, CRP/ESR, renal function, LFTs, CK (if any myalgia/weakness/skin changes or atypical angioedema), troponin if cardiac concern.
   - Complement testing and C1‑esterase function if you suspect bradykinin angioedema.
4. Imaging: contrast CT neck is excellent to exclude abscess and to show distribution of oedema — diffuse strap muscle oedema/retropharyngeal inflammation without collection is a red flag for non‑infectious causes.
5. Specialist tests/imaging in suspicious cases: myositis antibody panel, ANA, nailfold capillaroscopy, MRI thighs/neck, FDG‑PET if malignancy is a concern, and early ENT flexible nasendoscopy to assess bulbar/airway involvement.

Clues that should make you think dermatomyositis / inflammatory myopathy rather than angioedema
- Subacute progressive neck swelling over days–weeks rather than minutes–hours.
- Associated proximal muscle weakness (even mild): note hip flexor weakness in this case.
- Dysphagia or coughing with secretions on nasendoscopy — think bulbar muscle involvement and aspiration risk.
- Subtle cutaneous findings: periungual erythema/telangiectasias, nailfold microhaemorrhages/avascular areas on capillaroscopy, Holster or shawl sign, violaceous facial erythema — classic rashes may be absent.
- Very high CK and transaminases (AST often higher than ALT) with normal renal function and negative urine myoglobin → inflammatory myositis rather than primary rhabdomyolysis from trauma.
- Normal C4 and normal C1‑INH function: argues against bradykinin‑mediated angioedema (but does not exclude histaminergic causes).
- CT neck: diffuse inflammation of strap muscles and retropharyngeal soft tissue without focal fluid collection/abscess.

Lab & imaging pearls from the case
- CK can be massive (this case 16,184 U/L). If neck swelling seems "atypical" for angioedema, add CK early.
- Transaminases are often elevated in myositis (AST>ALT common) — don’t assume hepatic disease without thinking muscle source.
- CT neck: diffuse strap‑muscle oedema and retropharyngeal inflammation without rim‑enhancing collection — think inflammatory myositis/pseudoangioedema.
- Complement levels (C3/C4) and C1‑esterase quant/function were normal in this patient — makes hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency unlikely.
- Consider a myositis antibody panel; anti‑SAE1 antibodies (as in this patient) are associated with cutaneous involvement, dysphagia and risk of ILD and malignancy — helpful for prognostication and specialist planning.

When to order CK (practical trigger)
- Any atraumatic soft‑tissue neck swelling that is subacute/chronic or not explained by infection/abscess AND any of:
  - proximal weakness or myalgias
  - dysphagia or voice changes with normal airway anatomy
  - subtle rash or periungual/nailfold abnormalities
  - CT neck showing strap muscle oedema without abscess
- In short: low threshold in atypical neck swelling — a single CK is cheap and high‑yield.

Who to involve and when (consult triggers)
- Call ENT urgently for: airway compromise, evidence of deep neck space abscess, worsening airway obstruction, or need for operative drainage.
- Call immunology/allergy if: suspected angioedema (to interpret C4/C1‑INH results) or to discuss non‑histaminergic angioedema management.
- Call rheumatology/myositis specialists when: CK is markedly elevated, myositis suspected clinically or on imaging, dysphagia/aspiration present, or positive myositis antibodies — early specialist input guides immunosuppression and malignancy screening.
- Admit for airway monitoring and for initiation of immunosuppression if bulbar/airway muscles involved or CK/clinical course severe.

Management considerations illustrated by the case
- Airway protection and aspiration risk assessment are critical — this patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia requiring nasogastric feeding and had aspiration pneumonitis on CT.
- Immunotherapy may need to be aggressive when bulbar/airway muscles are involved: this patient received 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily, high‑dose IVIG loading and monthly maintenance, mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab — multidisciplinary decisions depend on disease severity and comorbidities.
- Malignancy screening is important in adult dermatomyositis (this patient had CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET and tumor markers; follow‑up endoscopy planned).

The bottom line — clinical takeaways
- Atraumatic neck swelling is not always angioedema or infection. Keep inflammatory myopathy (pseudoangioedema) on your differential, especially with subacute progression, weakness, dysphagia, and subtle skin/nailfold signs.
- Order a CK early in atypical neck swelling — it’s inexpensive and can be immediately diagnostic.
- Normal C4 and normal C1‑INH function argue against bradykinin‑mediated angioedema; consider inflammatory causes when those tests are normal.
- CT neck showing diffuse strap‑muscle oedema without abscess should prompt consideration of myositis and early rheumatology/immunology input.
- Protect the airway, screen for aspiration, and escalate to specialist care early — timely diagnosis and immunosuppression can reverse weakness and resolve pseudoangioedema.

Key take‑home points
- Think "pseudoangioedema" when neck swelling is subacute and atypical for histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema.
- Low threshold to check CK when neck swelling is unexplained OR when there are any neuromuscular or cutaneous clues.
- Normal complement and C1‑INH testing point away from bradykinin angioedema.
- CT: diffuse strap muscle oedema without collection = red flag for inflammatory myopathy.
- Early ENT for airway/abscess concerns; early rheumatology/immunology for suspected myositis/myositis‑specific antibodies and initiation of immunotherapy.

If you see an atraumatic swollen neck that doesn’t behave like classic angioedema — stop, look for weakness and subtle rashes/nailfold changes, get a CK, and call your rheumatology/immunology colleagues early.



# Airway at Risk: Dysphagia and Extrathoracic Restriction in Dermatomyositis

What makes this case grab your attention? A previously well man in his 40s turns up to the ED with progressive neck swelling that looks—and even gets worked up as—angioedema or a deep neck infection. ENT nasendoscopy shows no obstructing lesion, but there are copious secretions and coughing. A careful exam finds subtle periungual changes and proximal weakness; CK is 16,184 U/L. Imaging shows myositis involving head/neck muscles and peribronchial consolidation consistent with microaspiration. Video‑fluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) demonstrates severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform residue and penetration. Final diagnosis: SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with marked upper aerodigestive muscle involvement. He required pulse steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing immunosuppression, and temporary nasogastric feeding.

Why this matters to ED, ENT, ICU and ward teams
- Dermatomyositis can present with “pseudoangioedema” from inflammatory soft‑tissue/muscle edema rather than histamine/bradykinin pathways. Neck swelling that doesn’t behave like typical angioedema should trigger a broader differential.
- Pharyngeal and laryngeal muscle involvement can produce severe dysphagia, pooling secretions, microaspiration and progressive extrathoracic restrictive physiology—putting the airway and lungs at real risk even without classic limb‑dominant weakness.
- Early recognition changes management: immediate swallowing assessment, aspiration mitigation, close airway surveillance and early aggressive immunotherapy when airway musculature is threatened.

Clinical pathway distilled from the case

Initial red flags
- Subacute progressive neck swelling not responding to usual angioedema therapies or without typical triggers (no ACE‑inhibitor, normal complements, no urticaria, non‑tender).
- New or subtle bulbar signs: increased secretions, cough with bolus, dysphonia, nasal regurgitation.
- Proximal muscle weakness or myalgias on exam.
- Very high CK (this case 16,184 U/L) and raised transaminases/LDH should prompt thinking beyond ENT/infectious causes.

Early investigations that matter
- ENT nasendoscopy: excludes structural obstruction and documents secretion burden and vocal cord function. In the case, it showed normal anatomy but increased secretions and coughing—more consistent with weakness than obstruction.
- Swallow study: VFSS or FEES (fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing). VFSS in this case confirmed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform residue and penetration.
- CK and basic myositis workup early if neuromuscular cause suspected.
- Cross‑sectional imaging: neck CT can show strap muscle edema; chest CT may show peribronchial consolidation from aspiration. PET/MRI can localize active myositis.
- Pulmonary function tests: extrathoracic restrictive pattern may be present when upper airway/neck muscles are involved (this patient had such a pattern without fibrotic ILD on imaging).
- Complement and C1 esterase function if angioedema is being considered—normal in this patient, helping de‑prioritize bradykinin/histamine mechanisms.
- Early multi‑disciplinary serology/biopsy: myositis antibody panel and muscle biopsy confirmed SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis here.

Aspiration mitigation and nutritional support — practical steps
- Low threshold to NBM (nil by mouth) when aspiration is suspected on bedside screening or when secretions/pooling are evident.
- Early speech pathology input for bedside assessment and planning for VFSS/FEES. In the case the VFSS guided the decision for NG feeding.
- Start enteral feeding via nasogastric tube when oral intake is unsafe. The patient required NG feeding for about 2 months.
- Plan for longer‑term enteral access (PEG) only if prolonged dysphagia persists despite therapy; avoid premature PEG insertion when recovery is likely.
- Airway hygiene: suctioning, chest physiotherapy, consider nebulized mucolytics; early antibiotics if aspiration pneumonitis evolves to pneumonia.
- Document diet and feeding precautions clearly in the chart and escalate to ICU if airway protection is marginal.

When to escalate immunotherapy for airway/ swallowing muscle compromise
- Bulbar/airway muscle involvement with:
  - Severe dysphagia with aspiration on VFSS/FEES
  - Inability to manage secretions, recurrent aspiration events, or progressive respiratory compromise
  - Very high CK supporting active inflammatory myopathy
- In this case the team gave 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily, followed by an oral taper, with early IVIG (2 g/kg loading then monthly cycles), mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab—an aggressive approach targeted at rapid control of limb and bulbar myositis.
- “Pulse” steroids + IVIG should be considered earlier when bulbar/airway muscles are threatened; they tend to produce faster disease control than oral steroids alone.
- Coordinate infectious prophylaxis (PJP prophylaxis was attempted with TMP‑SMX in this patient but stopped due to hemolysis; nebulized pentamidine was used thereafter).

Airway monitoring and thresholds for ICU/airway intervention
- Admit to a higher level of care if:
  - Rapidly progressive neck swelling affecting respiration
  - Increasing secretions and ineffective cough, recurrent aspiration, or respiratory distress
  - Worsening gas exchange, rising pCO2, hypoxia, or inability to protect airway
- Consider early elective intubation if bulbar weakness is severe and airway protection is uncertain—late emergency airway is riskier.
- Tracheostomy is an option for prolonged ventilatory/airway support or secretion management if recovery is delayed.

Practical coordination — who should do what, early
- ED/ward team: suspect non‑infective causes if presentation atypical; send CK early; NBM and involve speech pathology; start aspiration precautions; notify ENT and rheumatology/immunology.
- ENT: nasendoscopy to exclude obstruction and document secretions/vocal fold motion.
- Speech pathology: bedside screen, arrange VFSS or FEES, recommend NG/PEG and rehab plan.
- Radiology: targeted neck CT, chest CT, MRI/PET if myositis suspected.
- Rheumatology/Immunology: initiate myositis serology, consider early escalation to pulse steroids/IVIG and steroid‑sparing agents in coordination with infection screening.
- ICU: when ventilatory/airway risk is present; coordinate airway protection and respiratory support.

A few pathophysiologic reminders that help decision‑making
- Neck/face “angioedema” in dermatomyositis is usually inflammatory edema from muscle/soft tissue inflammation, not mast cell or bradykinin driven. That explains lack of response to antihistamines and normal complement/C1 esterase in many cases.
- Bulbar muscle inflammation predisposes to aspiration and can produce radiographic peribronchial consolidation and lower‑zone airspace change from microaspiration—the CT chest in this patient showed exactly that.
- Extrathoracic restrictive PFT patterns can reflect weakness of extrathoracic (upper airway) musculature without intrinsic lung fibrosis; correlate with imaging and clinical picture.

Clinical pearls
- Think myositis when neck “angioedema” is atypical: look for skin/nailfold changes and check CK early.
- Nasendoscopy can be normal anatomically but highly informative: pooling secretions and cough during the exam point to bulbar weakness.
- VFSS/FEES are essential to define aspiration risk and guide decisions about NBM and enteral feeding.
- Severe bulbar/airway involvement is an indication for urgent aggressive immunotherapy (pulse steroids ± IVIG) and close airway monitoring.
- Coordinate ENT, speech pathology, rheumatology/immunology and ICU early—multidisciplinary care is what prevents bad outcomes.

Take‑home messages
- Neck swelling that doesn’t fit classic angioedema or infection: consider inflammatory myopathy/dermatomyositis.
- If bulbar signs or secretions are present, get nasendoscopy and prompt VFSS/FEES; err on the side of NBM and early NG feeding to prevent aspiration.
- Severe pharyngeal dysphagia or threatened airway from myositis warrants early escalation to pulse steroids and IVIG in consultation with rheumatology/immunology.
- Multidisciplinary coordination (ENT, speech pathology, radiology, rheum/immunology, ICU) is essential to secure the airway, mitigate aspiration, and deliver timely immunosuppression.

This case is a great reminder: not all neck swellings are ENT problems—sometimes the airway is at risk because the muscles that protect it are on fire. Catch it early, protect the airway and the lungs, and treat the inflammation aggressively.



# Decoding Anti‑SAE1 Dermatomyositis: Phenotype, Pitfalls, and Cancer Risk

This case is one of those “don’t miss it” presentations: a previously well man in his 40s arrives with progressive neck swelling that looks and feels like angioedema — but it isn’t. Careful exam (periungual changes, subtle rash), a prompt CK, and targeted myositis serology revealed anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with marked myositis, dysphagia and inflammatory cervical soft‑tissue swelling. The patient required early high‑dose steroids, IVIG and steroid‑sparing therapy and an appropriately aggressive malignancy screen. Why is this useful to EM/ENT/rheum/immunology clinicians? Because anti‑SAE1 DM can masquerade as ENT disease or idiopathic angioedema, and because the antibody carries meaningful clinical implications (cutaneous phenotype, frequent dysphagia and ILD, and a non‑negligible cancer risk) that should change your bedside workup and early screening strategy.

## What makes anti‑SAE1 cases special (and why this case surprised people)
- Neck “angioedema” that doesn’t respond like histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema should raise suspicion. In this patient the swelling was inflammatory — driven by myositis and subcutaneous edema — not mast cell or bradykinin mediated.
- Anti‑SAE1 disease commonly produces prominent cutaneous disease and, importantly, dysphagia and respiratory involvement. In a large series of SAE1+ patients, dysphagia occurred in ~46% and ILD in ~43%. Malignancy was reported in ~18% (literature review of 215 patients).
- Subcutaneous/face/neck edema is uncommon overall in dermatomyositis (about 6.6% in one cohort), so when you see it, think beyond primary ENT or allergic causes.

## Anti‑SAE1 phenotype — what to expect at the bedside
- Cutaneous: classically prominent skin findings (heliotrope, V/shawl signs, photodistributed rashes). In real life some patients may have subtle or atypical rashes — periungual erythema, Holster sign, violaceous facial erythema.
- Muscle: proximal weakness ± markedly elevated CK. Pharyngeal/upper‑airway muscle involvement is common and explains dysphagia and aspiration risk.
- Respiratory: ILD is frequent but heterogeneous; the pattern and severity vary.
- Edema: localized facial/neck subcutaneous edema can occur and may be mistaken for angioedema or infectious neck processes.
- Cancer association: intermediate risk (~18% in pooled data), but risk is modified by clinical context (see below).

## How anti‑SAE1 compares to MDA5 and TIF1‑γ at a glance
- TIF1‑γ: strong association with occult malignancy in adults (one of the highest cancer‑risk myositis antibodies). Prominent cutaneous disease, less frequently severe ILD (varies by cohorts).
- MDA5: classically associated with rapidly progressive ILD, severe skin ulceration, and high morbidity/mortality from pulmonary disease; cancer association is weaker than TIF1‑γ.
- SAE1: prominent cutaneous phenotype, high rates of dysphagia and a significant ILD risk; cancer risk sits between lower‑risk antibodies and TIF1‑γ but is influenced by age, sex and clinical features (e.g., dysphagia, classic dermatomyositis phenotype).

## The bedside decision tree — when to think SAE1/dermatomyositis and what to order early
If you see neck/face swelling labelled “angioedema” but features don’t fit (no medication trigger, no histaminergic features, normal complement/C1 tests for hereditary angioedema), ask:
- Is there proximal weakness (even subtle)? Test hip flexors and shoulder abductors.
- Any cutaneous clues? Periungual changes, rash in photodistributed areas, violaceous discoloration, Holster sign.
- Dysphagia, voice change, cough with secretions or signs of aspiration.
- Elevated inflammatory markers are nonspecific, but a very high CK is a red flag.

Immediate early tests to send:
- Serum CK (a single rapid test can change the trajectory).
- Basic inflammatory and infection workup as indicated (CBC, CRP, blood cultures/CT if thinking neck infection).
- If CK elevated or clinical suspicion high: ANA and myositis autoantibody panel (recognize turnaround times) and consider early MRI thigh/FDG‑PET or targeted imaging; early ENT flexible nasendoscopy and swallow study if dysphagia/airway risk.
- Nailfold capillaroscopy if available — cheap, quick, and informative.

Clinical actions while awaiting serology:
- Treat airway compromise and aspiration risk aggressively: nasogastric feeding if severe dysphagia; consider ICU involvement for bulbar/airway weakness.
- If clinical myositis with dysphagia/airway muscle involvement → consider early high‑dose steroids and/or IVIG (as in this case). Discuss steroid‑sparing agents with rheumatology/immunology.

## How to use myositis panels judiciously
Myositis autoantibody panels are valuable but not diagnostic in isolation. Key points:
- Use panels when pretest probability is moderate–high (e.g., elevated CK, objective weakness, typical rash, dysphagia, unexplained neck/face edema).
- A positive SAE1 supports the diagnosis and helps prognosticate (dysphagia/ILD/cancer risk). A negative panel does not rule out inflammatory myopathy—interpret in clinical context.
- Know your assay: different platforms (line immunoassays like Euroimmun, immunoprecipitation) have different sensitivities and cutoffs; check lab reporting and potential false positives/negatives.
- Avoid reflexively ordering broad autoantibody panels in low‑pretest‑probability scenarios to reduce false alarms and downstream testing.

## Translating an anti‑SAE1 result into cancer risk — applying IMACS principles at the bedside
IMACS and related expert recommendations emphasize that cancer risk in idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) is not driven solely by antibody status; it’s additive with clinical features. In practice:
- High‑risk features include: classic dermatomyositis phenotype, age >40–45, dysphagia, certain antibodies (TIF1‑γ strongest), and male sex (risk modifiers).
- SAE1 is considered an intermediate‑risk antibody. In the reported case the patient had: dermatomyositis phenotype + dysphagia (both high‑risk features) plus SAE1 positivity and male sex (intermediate), which together pushed overall risk into a high‑risk category — hence the aggressive baseline malignancy screen.
- Practical rule: combine antibody data with phenotype and clinical red flags (dysphagia, weight loss, new smoking history, refractory disease) to decide the intensity of cancer screening.

## Practical, stepwise malignancy workup and surveillance plan (balanced for yield and radiation)
Use shared decision‑making, tailored to risk. Below is an approach consistent with the case and international guidance principles (adapt to local resources and patient factors):

Initial baseline (for high‑risk patients — e.g., DM phenotype + dysphagia ± antibody risk modifiers):
1. Thorough history and focused physical exam (breast, pelvic, testicular, lymph nodes, skin).
2. Basic labs: CBC, LFTs, urinalysis; tumour markers as adjuncts (CEA, CA‑125, CA19‑9, PSA) — interpret cautiously, not as screening alone.
3. Cross‑sectional imaging: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis is a common baseline choice. In the presented case CT chest and PET‑CT were used.
4. FDG‑PET/CT: high sensitivity for occult malignancy and helpful when CT is unrevealing — consider once at baseline in high‑risk patients, especially when CT is non‑diagnostic. Weigh cost and radiation.
5. Age‑ and sex‑appropriate investigations: mammography, pelvic ultrasound/CA‑125/gynaecology consult, colonoscopy/gastroscopy (the team planned these for GI cancer exclusion).
6. Targeted investigations for suspicious symptoms or exam findings (e.g., ENT/endoscopy for oropharyngeal disease).

Surveillance strategy:
- Most cancers in IIM present within the first 3 years of diagnosis. Practical surveillance: clinical review every 3–6 months in year 1, then every 6–12 months for years 2–3, with a low threshold for repeat imaging if new symptoms or physical findings arise.
- Repeat imaging: no universal schedule. For high‑risk patients, consider repeat PET‑CT or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis at 6–12 months if the initial screen was negative and clinical suspicion persists, otherwise move to symptom‑driven imaging. Many centers perform more intensive surveillance during the first 3 years and then reduce frequency.
- Use lower‑radiation options where appropriate (ultrasound, endoscopy, MRI) and limit repeat full‑body CT/PET unless clinically indicated.
- Maintain routine, population‑based cancer screening as appropriate (colon, cervical, breast/prostate) in addition to IIM‑directed surveillance.

Document and communicate the plan clearly to the patient — the balance is identifying occult malignancy early without exposing every patient to repeated high‑dose imaging unnecessarily.

## Treatment implications relevant to SAE1 cases
- Early aggressive therapy is warranted for bulbar/airway involvement and severe weakness. High‑dose IV methylprednisolone and IVIG were appropriate in this case; steroid‑sparing agents (mycophenolate, rituximab) were used based on clinical course and ILD considerations.
- Methotrexate may be avoided when ILD is present or suspected; choose agents in multidisciplinary discussion.
- Dysphagia: early swallow evaluation, aspiration precautions and nutrition support (NG feeding) are critical.

## Clinical pearls
- “Angioedema” of the neck that is non‑responsive to antihistamines/epinephrine and has no trigger should prompt a neuromuscular/dermatomyositis workup — check CK.
- Nailfold capillary changes are an underused bedside clue — look for periungual erythema/microhemorrhages.
- Dysphagia and aspiration signs in suspected dermatomyositis mandate early ENT/swallow assessment and consideration of IVIG/steroids for bulbar weakness.
- A positive anti‑SAE1 helps explain a DM phenotype with dysphagia and ILD risk, and increases the probability of occult malignancy enough to justify a thorough baseline screen.

Key take‑home points:
- Consider dermatomyositis when neck or facial “angioedema” is atypical, especially with weakness, dysphagia or cutaneous signs — send a CK early.
- Anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis commonly presents with skin disease, frequent dysphagia and variable ILD; it carries an intermediate cancer risk that must be interpreted in context.
- Use myositis panels selectively and interpret results alongside clinical findings, imaging (MRI, PET), CK and biopsy.
- For SAE1+ patients with high‑risk features (DM phenotype, dysphagia, male sex), perform a comprehensive baseline malignancy screen (clinical exam, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and/or PET‑CT, age‑appropriate endoscopies) and plan close surveillance for the first 2–3 years, tailoring repeat imaging to clinical risk and minimizing unnecessary radiation.



# Imaging and Biopsy Strategy in Suspected Inflammatory Myopathy

What a case to wake up a team. A previously well man in his 40s arrives with subacute neck “angioedema,” dysphagia and subtle proximal weakness. CT shows marked soft‑tissue swelling in the neck but no abscess. Flexible nasendoscopy is unrevealing. It’s only after noting periungual changes and ordering a CK that the picture snaps into focus: CK >16,000 U/L, FDG‑PET with diffuse muscle uptake (head and neck conspicuous), thigh MRI with active myositis, and a muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis with C5b‑9 capillary deposition. Final diagnosis: anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis presenting as pseudo‑angioedema with significant bulbar/airway muscle involvement.

This case is a neat microcosm of modern myositis work‑up: use MRI and FDG‑PET not as redundant tests but as complementary tools — MRI to map muscle edema and phenotype, PET to survey for occult malignancy and to highlight metabolically active disease in anatomically difficult regions (eg, head/neck). Use those maps to target biopsy and avoid a useless sample.

Below I’ll walk through practical imaging protocols, how to interpret MRI edema patterns (and why they matter), FDG‑PET strengths and pitfalls, the pathology signatures that distinguish dermatomyositis, IMNM and polymyositis, and tactical tips to maximize diagnostic yield from a muscle biopsy.

## Start with the clinical context — imaging is to answer specific questions
Before ordering MRI or PET ask:
- Do I need to confirm active inflammatory myopathy vs chronic/degenerative change?
- Do I need to map the best biopsy site?
- Am I screening for malignancy as a trigger of myositis?
- Are there airway/bulbar muscles involved that MRI of the thighs will miss?

In our patient the neck swelling and dysphagia made mapping head/neck disease crucial — MRI thighs alone would have missed the most clinically threatening sites. FDG‑PET illuminated extensive head/neck muscle activity and showed no occult malignancy.

## MRI: how to tailor sequences and how to read the patterns
Protocol essentials
- Axial and coronal T1, axial and coronal fluid‑sensitive sequences (T2 FS or STIR), and post‑contrast T1 FS if available.
- Slice thickness ~4–5 mm; include pelvis and proximal thighs for routine proximal myopathy. For bulbar/neck symptoms, extend to neck and masticator spaces.
- Compare contralateral sides and include fat‑sensitive T1 to assess chronic fatty replacement.

What to look for (and what it suggests)
- Perifascicular/ perifascicular edema (signal hyperintensity at the muscle periphery on STIR/T2 FS): classic for dermatomyositis. Often patchy, near fascial planes.
- Subcutaneous and fascial edema: more common in dermatomyositis and antisynthetase syndromes; also explains “pseudo‑angioedema.”
- Symmetric proximal muscle edema with diffuse involvement: can be seen in IMNM and polymyositis; IMNM often shows more widespread, confluent edema reflecting necrosis.
- Endomysial involvement with fatty change and atrophy (T1 hyperintensity): suggests chronic disease or inclusion body myositis if distal/patchy and asymmetric.
- Enhancement after gadolinium correlates with active inflammation and may guide biopsy.
Practical MRI cues to pick a biopsy site
- Target areas with maximal STIR/T2 FS hyperintensity but avoid regions of advanced fatty replacement (high T1 signal) — fatty tissue yields little diagnostic inflammatory signal.
- Choose a muscle large enough to yield multiple cores or an open specimen (eg, vastus lateralis, rectus femoris, deltoid depending on imaging).
- If edema is patchy, pick the most hyperintense region and document exact location (level and relation to anatomical landmarks) for the surgeon.

In the case: thigh MRI showed active perifascicular changes in obturator internus/externus and distal iliopsoas — imaging helped document the extent and select a representative thigh site for biopsy even though the most life‑threatening activity lay in the neck.

## FDG‑PET: use it as a combined disease map and malignancy screen — but know its limits
What PET is good for
- Whole‑body survey for occult malignancy in myositis patients (high yield in dermatomyositis).
- Identifying metabolically active muscles in regions outside routine MRI coverage — e.g., bulbar and neck muscles where biopsy is impractical but recognition of involvement alters management.
- Highlighting multifocal active disease in patchy myositis.

Key PET pitfalls
- Muscle FDG uptake is nonspecific: recent exercise, corticosteroids, insulin, inflammation, infection and even physiologic uptake can produce diffuse or focal signals.
- PET picks up inflammation — not specific for autoimmune myositis versus infection/injury.
- Small mucosal or early cancers (eg, some GI neoplasms) can be PET‑occult; PET complements but does not replace directed endoscopy and CT as indicated.
- Timing matters: recent strenuous activity before PET can dramatically increase muscle uptake. Ensure patients avoid exercise 24–48 hours pre‑scan.
Practical PET tips
- Ask about recent exertion, steroid timing, or recent IVIG — these can alter uptake.
- Use PET to decide if additional imaging (MRI targeted to PET‑positive region) or urgent interventions (eg, protect airway when bulbar muscles light up) are needed.
- If PET shows focal uptake suspicious for malignancy, correlate with CT or targeted imaging and proceed to directed biopsy as indicated.

In our patient PET made two big contributions: it documented head/neck metabolic activity (explaining dysphagia and soft‑tissue swelling) and it showed no hypermetabolic malignancy elsewhere, which guided the initial cancer screen strategy.

## Pathology: what to expect and why it matters clinically
Dermatomyositis (DM)
- Histology: perifascicular atrophy/necrosis, perivascular/perimysial inflammation (often B cells and plasmacytoid dendritic cells), capillary loss.
- Immunopathology: deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) in endomysial/perifascicular capillaries is a supportive marker.
- Clinical correlation: skin findings, subcutaneous/fascial edema, higher association with ILD and malignancy in some subtypes (eg, anti‑TIF1‑γ).

Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)
- Histology: prominent myofibre necrosis with minimal lymphocytic inflammation; macrophage‑predominant cleanup; necrosis out of proportion to inflammatory infiltrate.
- Often associated antibodies: anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP.
- Clinical correlation: very high CKs, severe weakness, treatment may require aggressive immunosuppression.

Polymyositis (PM) — increasingly uncommon as a pure entity
- Histology: endomysial CD8+ T‑cell infiltrates with invasion of non‑necrotic fibers expressing MHC‑I.
- Consider alternative diagnoses (eg, inclusion body myositis) if there is distal, asymmetric weakness or older age.

How pathology feeds back into treatment
- Finding perifascicular necrosis with C5b‑9 deposition strongly supports dermatomyositis and prompts cancer screening, consideration of ILD evaluation, and early combined immunosuppression.
- IMNM pathology pushes toward rapid escalation (IVIG + steroid‑sparing agent, sometimes rituximab, earlier) because of necrotic dominance and potential refractoriness.

The muscle biopsy in our patient demonstrated classic dermatomyositis features including perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition — clinching the diagnosis when combined with CK, antibody profile and imaging.

## Avoiding a nondiagnostic biopsy — a checklist
1. Use imaging to map active disease and avoid fat‑replaced muscle. MRI STIR/T2 FS hyperintensity + PET hypermetabolism = best target.
2. Time biopsy before or as early as possible relative to immunosuppression. If high‑dose steroids or IVIG cannot be delayed (airway compromise, severe dysphagia), proceed with treatment but recognize sensitivity of biopsy may decrease.
3. Choose the right muscle and technique:
   - For patchy disease, open biopsy gives larger tissue and more diagnostic yield.
   - Core needle biopsy under ultrasound or MRI guidance is acceptable if you can accurately target the active area and obtain multiple cores.
4. Take sufficient tissue: aim for multiple cores or an open specimen including fascia; include skin if cutaneous signs are present and may help with diagnosis.
5. Communicate with pathology: indicate clinical suspicion, antibody results, recent treatments (steroids/IVIG), and imaging findings so special stains (eg, C5b‑9, MHC‑I, immune markers) are applied.
6. Avoid sampling chronically fatty muscles or atrophic ends of a muscle — these often show non‑specific findings.
7. Document exact biopsy location and orientation to correlate with imaging.

## Workflow example — a coordinated pathway
1. Clinical suspicion (CK elevated, weakness, skin signs): order targeted MRI (include symptomatic regions) and myositis antibody panel.
2. If malignancy is a concern or disease extends beyond MRI field (eg, head and neck), add whole‑body FDG‑PET.
3. Correlate MRI STIR/T2 hyperintense areas with PET hypermetabolism; choose the best accessible site for biopsy (active on MRI, hypermetabolic on PET).
4. Schedule biopsy prior to initiation of high‑dose immunosuppression if clinically safe. If not, proceed with therapy and still obtain biopsy when feasible — document timing.
5. Send tissue with a clear clinical note; include request for routine H&E, immunohistochemistry for complement (C5b‑9), MHC‑I, inflammatory cell markers and electron microscopy if IBM is in differential.
6. Parallel malignancy screening based on risk (age, male sex, dysphagia, myositis subtype/antibodies) — CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET, and directed endoscopy as indicated.

## Pitfalls to watch for
- Relying on a single imaging modality: MRI and PET answer different questions; use them synergistically.
- Misinterpreting PET muscle uptake without checking for recent exercise or steroid effects.
- Biopsying the “closest” weak muscle rather than the “most active” muscle on imaging — convenience leads to nondiagnostic samples.
- Forgetting to screen for malignancy in high‑risk myositis subtypes (eg, dermatomyositis with dysphagia).
- Ignoring skin biopsy when cutaneous signs are present — skin can aid diagnosis in dermatomyositis.

## Closing — keep the team talking
Imaging is not a ritual — it’s a map. MRI defines local anatomy and disease phenotype; FDG‑PET surveys the body and highlights metabolically active areas you might otherwise miss (head/neck, paraspinal, or multifocal disease). Use them together to guide biopsy selection, minimize nondiagnostic specimens, and prioritize malignancy screening. Communicate imaging, serology and clinical urgency with the surgeon and pathologist so the specimen is taken from the right place, at the right time, and processed with the right stains.

In our case, that coordinated approach transformed a confusing presentation masquerading as angioedema into a targeted diagnosis of SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis — and allowed rapid, appropriate immunosuppression that ultimately led to clinical recovery.

Key take‑home points
- Use MRI STIR/T2 FS to map active muscle edema and avoid fatty‑replaced or atrophic muscle when selecting biopsy sites.
- FDG‑PET is valuable both for malignancy screening and for detecting active disease in regions not covered by standard MRI (eg, head/neck, bulbar muscles), but interpret uptake in the context of recent exercise, steroids or IVIG.
- Dermatomyositis on biopsy: perifascicular atrophy/necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition; IMNM: prominent necrosis with scant inflammation; polymyositis: endomysial CD8+ T‑cell invasion.
- Coordinate timing: biopsy ideally before high‑dose immunosuppression; if treatment is urgent, document timing and proceed — pathology may still be informative.
- Multidisciplinary communication (clinician → radiologist → surgeon → pathologist) is the single most important step to avoid nondiagnostic biopsies and to get a rapid, accurate diagnosis.



# Therapeutic blitz for severe dermatomyositis: choosing and sequencing agents

This case — a previously well man in his 40s who presented with dramatic, non‑histaminergic neck swelling masquerading as angioedema and was ultimately diagnosed with SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with severe oropharyngeal involvement — is a great springboard to talk about early, aggressive, combination immunotherapy. Bulky cervical soft‑tissue oedema, marked dysphagia and very high CK (16,184 U/L) are red flags for threatened airway/aspiration and for rapidly progressive, organ‑threatening myositis. Waiting to “see if steroids alone will do” risks aspiration, prolonged ICU stays, and permanent disability. Here’s a practical, evidence‑aware roadmap for the kind of therapeutic blitz used in this case: pulse methylprednisolone, IVIG induction/maintenance, rapid institution of a steroid‑sparing agent (why mycophenolate over methotrexate when the lungs are involved), and where rituximab fits.

## Why go early and hard?
Severe bulbar/oropharyngeal weakness and inflammatory neck swelling signal high morbidity (aspiration, need for enteral feeding, airway compromise). The goals are immediate control of inflammation (reduce airway/aspiration risk), rapid recovery of strength and swallowing, and steroid‑sparing to avoid prolonged high‑dose steroids. Combining modalities speeds control via complementary mechanisms: high‑dose steroids for quick suppression, IVIG for immunomodulation (and dysphagia improvement in many series), and a steroid‑sparing DMARD to maintain control and allow steroid taper.

## The induction backbone — practical sequencing and doses
- Pulse IV methylprednisolone
  - When: immediate for threatened airway, severe dysphagia, rapidly rising CK or multi‑organ involvement.
  - Typical dose: 500–1000 mg IV daily for 3 days (this case: 1 g/day × 3). Rationale: rapid anti‑inflammatory effect and reduction of edema in critical locations (neck, pharynx).
  - Follow with oral prednisone: commonly 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day (often starting at 60 mg daily for an adult), then taper guided by clinical response and labs.

- IVIG (induction then maintenance)
  - Role: good evidence for steroid‑refractory or severe dermatomyositis and particular utility for bulbar weakness/dysphagia. Randomized and controlled data (and multiple observational series) support improved muscle strength and function with IVIG in dermatomyositis.
  - Typical dosing: 2 g/kg total per cycle (ideal body weight adjusted), given over 2–5 days for induction. Many centers give 2 g/kg over 2–5 days monthly for 3–6 cycles, then space/frequency is individualized (some continue monthly or every 4–8 weeks as maintenance).
  - Practical note from the case: a 2 g/kg “loading” split over 5 days followed by monthly cycles was used, with clinical improvement in swelling and muscle strength.

- Start a steroid‑sparing agent promptly (within days)
  - Why start early? DMARDs (mycophenolate, methotrexate, azathioprine) take weeks to months to work. Early initiation reduces steroid exposure and augments control as steroids are tapered.
  - Choice depends on co‑morbidities — see next section.

## Choosing the steroid‑sparing agent: why mycophenolate over methotrexate here
Methotrexate is a commonly used steroid‑sparing agent in dermatomyositis, but methotrexate carries a risk of pulmonary toxicity and can complicate underlying interstitial lung disease (ILD). In patients with abnormal lung function, ILD on imaging, or concern for aspiration pneumonitis — as in this case (abnormal PFTs, CT signs of peribronchial consolidation and aspiration) — many clinicians prefer mycophenolate mofetil (MMF).

Practical rationale for MMF:
- Better tolerated in patients with lung involvement and widely used for myositis‑associated ILD.
- Effective as a steroid‑sparing agent in observational series.
- Dosing: commonly 1 g twice daily (this case used MMF 1 g twice daily).
- Monitoring: CBC, LFTs, and renal function; watch for leukopenia and GI side effects.

When methotrexate may still be appropriate:
- No lung disease and patient can tolerate monitoring; common dosing 15–25 mg weekly with folic acid.

Choose azathioprine or tacrolimus in other scenarios (e.g., intolerance to MMF), but tailor to individual risks and monitoring capacity.

## Where rituximab fits
Rituximab (anti‑CD20) is a rational choice for:
- Refractory disease after initial therapy,
- Severe disease with myositis‑specific antibody phenotypes that have an autoantibody‑driven pathophysiology,
- Frequent relapses despite other agents,
- Or when B‑cell depletion is preferred (e.g., certain antibody profiles).

Evidence snapshot: The RIM trial did not meet its primary endpoint but many patients improved, and rituximab is widely used off‑label in refractory inflammatory myopathies. Dosing: two infusions of 1000 mg IV separated by 14 days (the regimen used in this case) or 375 mg/m2 weekly ×4. Monitor for infusion reactions and screen for HBV reactivation (HBsAg, anti‑HBc) before dosing.

## PJP prophylaxis — plan and alternatives
High‑dose steroids + lymphocyte‑depleting agents (rituximab) or prolonged MMF increase Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) risk. TMP‑SMX is first‑line; alternatives are needed when TMP‑SMX is not tolerated.

- First‑line: Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole
  - Dosing options: one double‑strength tablet (160/800 mg) three times weekly OR single‑strength daily — both commonly used.
  - Watch for cytopenias, rash, hyperkalemia, and rare hemolysis (especially in G6PD deficiency).
- Alternatives if TMP‑SMX intolerance:
  - Dapsone 100 mg PO daily (check G6PD before starting; risk of hemolysis and methemoglobinemia).
  - Atovaquone 1500 mg PO daily (given as 750 mg twice daily with a fatty meal, or 1500 mg once daily depending on formulation) — well tolerated but expensive.
  - Nebulized pentamidine 300 mg once monthly — used in this case after TMP‑SMX caused haemolytic anemia. Practical caveats: less systemic protection and evidence is mixed for prevention of extrapulmonary disease; watch for bronchospasm and local irritation. Ensure proper nebulization equipment and monitoring.
- Practical choice: TMP‑SMX when possible; if adverse effects occur, pick the alternative considering G6PD, tolerability, and logistics.

## Monitoring and tapering — a pragmatic plan
- Baseline before immunosuppression:
  - CBC, CMP (renal/hepatic), CK, AST/ALT, LDH
  - Viral screens: HBV, HCV, HIV (HBV particularly important before rituximab)
  - PFTs and HRCT chest if pulmonary symptoms or antibody known to be associated with ILD
  - Cancer screening per IMACS risk stratification (dermatomyositis + dysphagia = higher risk)
  - Vaccination status — give indicated inactivated vaccines before rituximab if possible; avoid live vaccines on immunosuppression.

- Early inpatient monitoring:
  - Daily clinical exam for airway/aspiration, bulbar strength.
  - CK and AST/ALT every 1–3 days while active and until trending down.
  - Monitor urine/renal function for myoglobinuria if CK very high.
  - Monitor for infections; prophylaxis as above.

- Tapering steroids (example used in the case):
  - After pulse IV methylprednisolone, convert to oral prednisone 60 mg daily.
  - Taper by roughly 5 mg every 2–3 weeks as tolerated, slower below 20 mg (decrements of 2.5–5 mg every 2–4 weeks) guided by strength, CK, and dysphagia.
  - Individualize: more rapid taper if IVIG and DMARDs are controlling disease; slow taper or escalation if relapse.
  - Use clinical end points (strength, swallowing, CK normalization) rather than arbitrary calendar alone.

- IVIG maintenance:
  - Continue monthly 2 g/kg cycles while steroids taper and until clinical remission is achieved.
  - Consider spacing to every 6–8 weeks then stop when stable off steroids and on DMARD for several months.

- DMARD monitoring:
  - MMF: CBC and CMP every 2 weeks initially, then monthly, then every 3 months once stable.
  - Rituximab: monitor CD19/20 and immunoglobulins if repeated doses; watch for hypogammaglobulinemia.

## Practical dosing pearls
- Methylprednisolone pulse: 500–1000 mg IV daily ×3 days for severe disease (airway/bulbar involvement).
- Prednisone oral starting range: 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day (typical adult cap ~60 mg); taper individualized.
- IVIG: 2 g/kg per cycle (use ideal body weight for dosing in obesity), given over 2–5 days; repeat monthly for induction; maintain frequency based on response.
- Mycophenolate mofetil: 1 g PO twice daily (adjust for intolerance).
- Rituximab: 1000 mg IV day 0 and day 14 (or 375 mg/m2 weekly ×4); pre‑screen for HBV and vaccinate when possible.
- PJP prophylaxis: TMP‑SMX one double‑strength tablet three times weekly; if intolerance: dapsone 100 mg daily (after G6PD check), atovaquone 750 mg twice daily with food, or nebulized pentamidine 300 mg monthly.

## Short evidence snapshot
- IVIG has randomized and observational evidence supporting benefit in dermatomyositis, particularly for refractory disease and bulbar involvement.
- Rituximab is commonly used in refractory disease; randomized data were mixed but many patients derive benefit in real‑world practice.
- Mycophenolate is supported by observational data for steroid‑sparing efficacy and for management of ILD‑associated myositis.
- There are no universal, high‑quality RCTs defining a single “best” sequence; clinical judgment, organ involvement, and comorbidities must guide therapy.

## Takeaway clinical pearls
- Severe bulbar/oropharyngeal involvement or threatening airway in dermatomyositis warrants immediate, combined therapy — IV pulse steroids + IVIG + early DMARD — rather than delayed stepwise escalation.
- Prefer mycophenolate over methotrexate when lung disease/aspiration risk exists; MMF is well tolerated and frequently effective for myositis with pulmonary involvement.
- Rituximab is a reasonable next step for refractory or relapsing, antibody‑positive disease; always screen for HBV before dosing.
- Start PJP prophylaxis with TMP‑SMX if feasible; when TMP‑SMX is not tolerated, alternatives are dapsone (after G6PD check), atovaquone, or inhaled pentamidine 300 mg monthly (used in this case).
- Use CK, strength (especially bulbar function), and clinical course to guide steroid tapering; IVIG cycles can be spaced and stopped when stable remission is achieved.

This patient’s course — rapid diagnostic pivot from presumed angioedema to SAE1 dermatomyositis, urgent pulse steroids, IVIG induction and maintenance, early MMF, and rituximab — shows how an assertive, multimodal approach can reverse life‑threatening features (neck swelling, dysphagia, very high CK) and return a patient to function. In severe myositis, think early combination therapy rather than prolonged single‑agent rescue.



# When ‘Hepatitis’ Is Muscle: Interpreting AST/ALT in Myositis

What makes this case interesting: a man in his 40s presents to the ED with progressive neck “angioedema” and dysphagia, is worked up for infection and angioedema, and then—because someone asked for a creatine kinase (CK)—turns out to have CK 16,184 U/L with AST 396 U/L, ALT 143 U/L and LDH 692 U/L. The diagnosis was anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with significant muscle inflammation around the head/neck causing soft‑tissue oedema and dysphagia. Liver enzymes that look “hepatic” were actually muscle-derived.

If you see unexplained transaminase elevations in a weak or dyspnoeic patient, don’t reflex into an exhaustive hepatic workup first. Muscle disease can produce very high CK and an AST>ALT pattern with raised LDH, and this pattern should redirect you to look for myositis—or other muscle injury—early.

## The trap
AST and ALT are often framed as “liver enzymes,” but AST is abundant in skeletal muscle (and heart, kidney, RBCs) while ALT is more liver‑predominant. In substantial muscle injury (trauma, rhabdomyolysis, inflammatory myopathy) CK skyrockets, AST typically rises higher than ALT, and LDH goes up. That pattern can mimic hepatocellular injury and prompt unnecessary imaging, hepatology referrals, or delays in immunosuppression when the real problem is inflammatory myositis.

In our case:
- CK = 16,184 U/L (massive)
- AST = 396 U/L, ALT = 143 U/L (AST > ALT)
- LDH = 692 U/L
- Urine myoglobin screen was negative, and renal function remained normal
- The patient’s CK normalized (37 U/L) within weeks of targeted immunosuppression

## Quick physiology primer
- CK is the most specific and sensitive marker of skeletal‑muscle necrosis in the acute/subacute setting. Very high CKs (often >1,000 U/L, sometimes tens of thousands) point to muscle injury.
- AST is present in both liver and muscle; ALT is more liver‑specific. So muscle disease more commonly gives an AST‑predominant picture.
- LDH rises in both liver and muscle injury and tends to parallel the CK/AST pattern.
- Urine myoglobin may be absent even with huge CK elevations; absence of myoglobinuria does not exclude severe muscle injury.
- Markers that support true hepatic injury: high bilirubin, elevated alkaline phosphatase/GGT consistent with cholestasis, synthetic dysfunction (INR ↑), hepatomegaly on exam/US, or ALT >> AST.

## Practical, bedside approach when you see elevated transaminases in a weak/dyspnoeic patient
1. Look at the clinical context first
   - Any proximal muscle weakness, myalgias, rashes (heliotrope, Gottron’s, shawl/V‑sign), dysphagia, or facial/neck swelling? These point to myositis.
2. Order CK (and repeat if clinical suspicion is high)
   - A very high CK should move muscle higher on your differential than isolated hepatic injury.
3. Check LDH, AST, ALT together and compare patterns
   - AST > ALT with markedly elevated CK suggests muscle source.
4. Check renal function and urine for myoglobin/urinalysis
   - Rhabdomyolysis → risk of AKI; manage with fluids and monitor closely even if urine myoglobin is negative.
5. Basic hepatic safety labs before immunosuppression
   - Viral hepatitis screen (HBsAg, anti‑HBc, HCV), HIV, and other infection screens as local guidance/oncologic risk dictate. (In the case above these screens were negative and informed safe initiation of immunosuppression.)
6. Image and subspecialty input as indicated
   - MRI of affected muscles, chest imaging (for ILD risk in myositis), myositis antibody panel and rheumatology/immunology input if dermatomyositis suspected. Muscle biopsy if diagnosis remains uncertain.
7. Use serial trends to monitor disease and treatment response
   - CK is typically the most responsive lab to treatment. In the reported case CK fell from 16k to normal within weeks after steroids, IVIG, MMF and rituximab—supporting muscle origin of the transaminase elevations.
8. Don’t forget other causes of AST>ALT
   - Alcoholic hepatitis, ischemic hepatitis, hemolysis, cardiac injury—consider the whole clinical picture.

## Case in point — what saved time here
- Careful bedside exam found periungual changes, a rash and proximal weakness. That prompted a CK, which flipped the workup from ENT/angioedema/infection to inflammatory myopathy.
- Appropriate infection screens were performed prior to aggressive immunosuppression.
- Treatment targeted the underlying immune myositis and the biochemical abnormalities (CK, AST/ALT, LDH) normalized as the myositis improved.

## Clinical pearls
- Always ask: does this patient have muscle signs? If yes, check CK.
- AST>ALT with markedly elevated CK almost always suggests muscle injury rather than primary liver disease.
- A normal urine myoglobin does not rule out severe muscle injury—don’t be reassured solely by a negative myoglobin test.
- Before starting high‑dose steroids or other immunosuppression, screen for chronic viral infections (HBV, HCV, HIV) and consider latent TB per local protocols—this was done in the reported case.
- Use serial CK (and AST/ALT/LDH) to monitor response to therapy; CK usually normalizes faster than clinical recovery of function.

## Take‑home messages
- In a weak or dyspnoeic patient, unexplained transaminase elevations warrant a CK. Very high CK with AST>ALT and raised LDH = think muscle, not liver.
- Don’t chase liver imaging or invasive hepatic workup first when the clinical picture fits myositis; pivot to muscle testing and rheumatology/immunology input.
- Screen for viral hepatitis and other infections before starting immunosuppression.
- Monitor serial CK/AST/ALT/LDH to follow disease activity and treatment response—the case above illustrates dramatic biochemical recovery after appropriate myositis therapy.

If you want a one‑line algorithm to tape to your chart: unexplained transaminase rise + weakness/myalgias → CHECK CK.
