Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with isolated cervical swelling, how can you distinguish myositis-related pseudoangioedema from histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema using key historical features, examination findings, and basic laboratory testing?

Direct answer
Myositis-related “pseudo‑angioedema” can be distinguished from histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema by the time course and response to therapy, associated systemic features on exam, and a very small set of targeted laboratory tests. In practice ask three questions: (1) Is the swelling acute, pruritic and steroid/antihistamine‑responsive (histaminergic)? (2) Is there a history of ACE‑inhibitor/DPP‑4 use, family history, or low C4/C1‑INH function and lack of response to antihistamines (bradykinin‑mediated)? (3) Is the swelling subacute/progressive with proximal muscle weakness, cutaneous myositis stigmata and marked muscle enzymes (suggesting myositis)?

Why this works (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- Histaminergic angioedema: mast‑cell degranulation → rapid onset (minutes–hours), often with urticaria/pruritus, erythema, and prompt improvement with antihistamines, corticosteroids or epinephrine. Systemic signs of muscle injury are absent.
- Bradykinin‑mediated angioedema (hereditary/acquired/C1‑INH deficiency or drug‑induced): slower onset than histaminergic, often prolonged and recurrent, nonpruritic, no urticaria, minimal erythema, and poor response to antihistamines/steroids. Complement testing often shows low C4 and abnormal C1‑INH function or levels in C1‑INH deficiency.
- Myositis‑related pseudoangioedema (eg, dermatomyositis): immune‑mediated inflammation of muscle and perifascicular capillaries causes capillary injury, interstitial and subcutaneous edema overlying affected muscle groups. Presentation is typically subacute to weeks, progressive, may be non‑tender, and is accompanied by proximal weakness, dysphagia, characteristic cutaneous findings (heliotrope, Gottron’s papules, shawl/V‑neck sign, periungual capillary changes), and markedly elevated muscle enzymes (CK, AST, LDH). Not mediated by histamine or bradykinin and therefore not rapidly responsive to antihistamines/epinephrine; treatment is immunosuppression.

How to apply this at the bedside (practical stepwise approach)
1. History
   - Timing: minutes–hours (histaminergic) vs hours–days with recurrence and lack of treatment response (bradykinin) vs days–weeks progressive (myositis).
   - Associated features: pruritus/urticaria/facial flushing → histaminergic. Recent ACE‑inhibitor/DPP‑4 use, family history of angioedema → bradykinin. Proximal muscle weakness, myalgias, dysphagia, photosensitive rash, periungual changes → myositis.
   - Medication and family history: specifically ask about ACE inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitors, estrogens.

2. Examination
   - Look for urticaria/pruritus (histaminergic). Angioedema from any cause is non‑pitting; erythema is more typical with histaminergic.
   - Test muscle strength (proximal power), swallow function, and inspect skin and nailfolds for myositis signs (heliotrope, Gottron’s, shawl sign, periungual telangiectasias/microhemorrhages).
   - Airway assessment and ENT exam/imaging: CT that shows edema centered in strap muscles or other musculature rather than isolated subcutaneous swelling points to muscle inflammation (as in this case where CT showed diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles).

3. Key (basic) laboratory tests to order immediately
   - Serum creatine kinase (CK) and muscle‑related labs (AST, ALT, LDH). Marked CK elevation strongly supports myositis (case: CK 16,184 U/L; AST 396 U/L; ALT 143 U/L; LDH 692 U/L).
   - Complement testing: C4 and C1‑esterase inhibitor (function and level). Low C4 or abnormal C1‑INH → hereditary/acquired bradykinin‑mediated angioedema. In the case C3/C4 and C1 esterase were normal, arguing against C1‑INH deficiency.
   - White cell count/CRP: may be mildly elevated with inflammation; pronounced leukocytosis or fevers suggest infection.
   - Therapeutic trial/response: a rapid clinical response to antihistamines/steroids supports histaminergic; lack of response should prompt evaluation for bradykinin causes and myositis.

Putting it together in this case
- The patient had a subacute 3‑week progressive, non‑tender cervical swelling with concurrent proximal weakness, dysphagia and subtle periungual capillary abnormalities — clinical clues pointing away from histaminergic or drug/bradykinin angioedema and toward an inflammatory myopathy. Complement studies (C3/C4 and C1‑INH) were normal, arguing against bradykinin‑mediated disease. A very high CK (16,184 U/L) and muscle inflammation on imaging/biopsy confirmed dermatomyositis causing muscle‑centered edema (pseudoangioedema). His swelling did not behave like histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema and required immunosuppression rather than antihistamines or C1‑INH‑directed therapies.

Takeaway pearl
When neck “angioedema” is subacute, progressive, refractory to usual measures, and accompanied by even subtle muscle weakness or cutaneous findings, check a CK (and C4/C1‑INH). A markedly elevated CK plus myositis signs points to inflammatory myositis with pseudoangioedema and mandates prompt immunologic evaluation and treatment rather than standard angioedema therapies.
