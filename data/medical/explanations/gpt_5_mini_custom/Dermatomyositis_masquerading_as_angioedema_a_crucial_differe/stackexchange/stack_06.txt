Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] What are the complementary roles of MRI of skeletal muscle and FDG-PET in inflammatory myopathy—both for confirming active myositis and for evaluating occult malignancy—in a patient with dermatomyositis presenting with neck swelling and dysphagia?

Answer (Clinical pearl)

In inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis the diagnostic and staging problem is twofold: first, prove there is active muscle inflammation and choose an optimal site for biopsy; second, because adult dermatomyositis carries an elevated cancer risk, search for an occult malignancy. MRI of skeletal muscle and whole‑body FDG‑PET are complementary tests that address these two problems in different but overlapping ways.

How MRI and FDG‑PET differ and complement each other

- MRI (muscle‑protocol, STIR/T2 fat‑suppressed and T1 sequences)
  - Best test for local muscle assessment. High soft‑tissue contrast identifies active edema/inflammation (T2/STIR hyperintensity), fasciitis, distribution (perifascicular vs diffuse), and chronic changes (T1 fatty replacement, atrophy).
  - Guides muscle‑biopsy site selection by showing muscles with active edema but not long‑standing fatty change—maximizing diagnostic yield. In this case the thigh MRI showed active myositis in bilateral obturators, gracilis and iliopsoas—information that supported the decision to biopsy and correlated with the histology of perifascicular necrosis and capillary C5b‑9 deposition.
  - Useful for quantifying local disease extent and monitoring structural changes over time. It is not a whole‑body test and gives no reliable information about occult malignancy.
  - Not affected by glycaemia or recent exercise in the way PET is; timing relative to immunosuppression is less likely to abolish the MRI signal than it is to blunt PET uptake.

- FDG‑PET (whole‑body)
  - Whole‑body metabolic survey: detects hypermetabolic foci consistent with malignancy and can also demonstrate diffuse or focal muscle inflammation (increased FDG uptake in involved muscles).
  - In dermatomyositis it serves two roles simultaneously: (1) screen for occult cancer (recommended when clinical risk is high by IMACS criteria), and (2) map systemic inflammatory activity (for example, prominent head/neck and bulbar muscle uptake can explain dysphagia or neck swelling).
  - In this patient PET showed diffuse abnormal FDG accumulation involving head/neck and multiple muscle groups but no metabolically active lesion to suggest malignancy—supporting that the neck swelling reflected active myositis rather than an occult tumor.
  - Caveats: FDG uptake is not specific — inflammation, recent exercise, insulin, infection, or recent steroid exposure can alter results (steroids may reduce FDG signal). PET can miss small or low‑FDG tumors and cannot replace targeted endoscopic/age‑appropriate cancer screening; negative PET lowers but does not eliminate cancer risk.

Practical approach illustrated by the case
- Use MRI first (or in parallel) to confirm active myositis, document distribution, and pick a biopsy target. In this case MRI confirmed active thigh myositis and supported the pathologic diagnosis.
- Use FDG‑PET when cancer risk is elevated (here: dermatomyositis, dysphagia, SAE1 positivity, male sex) to perform a whole‑body screen and to assess the systemic inflammatory burden. The PET in this patient both reinforced that the neck swelling was inflammatory (prominent FDG uptake in head/neck muscles) and showed no metabolically active malignancy.
- Remember limitations: neither study alone rules out occult cancer permanently—continue IMACS‑recommended surveillance (age‑appropriate imaging, endoscopy when indicated, interval clinical follow‑up). Also coordinate timing (avoid strenuous exercise before PET, control glucose; interpret PET with knowledge of recent high‑dose steroids/IVIG).

Bottom line
MRI is the anatomical/structural test of choice to prove and localize active myositis and to guide biopsy; FDG‑PET is the whole‑body metabolic test of choice when malignancy screening and assessment of systemic inflammatory burden are required. Used together, as in this patient, they corroborated active dermatomyositis as the cause of neck swelling and dysphagia and provided reassuring, contemporaneous exclusion of metabolically avid occult malignancy—while recognizing that ongoing, guideline‑directed surveillance remains necessary.
