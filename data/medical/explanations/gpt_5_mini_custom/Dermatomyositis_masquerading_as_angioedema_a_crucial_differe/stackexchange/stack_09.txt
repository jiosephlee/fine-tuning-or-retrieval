Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Management] For a dermatomyositis patient receiving high-dose glucocorticoids plus additional immunosuppression, what are evidence-based options for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis when trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole is not tolerated, and what important precautions or limitations accompany each alternative?

Short answer
Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP–SMX) is first‑line prophylaxis for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP). When TMP–SMX cannot be used, the evidence‑based alternatives are (1) oral dapsone, (2) oral atovaquone, and (3) aerosolized (nebulized) pentamidine. Each is imperfect and has important limitations that must be considered when choosing therapy — in this case the treating team stopped TMP–SMX for hemolytic anaemia and used monthly nebulized pentamidine 300 mg with good tolerance.

Clinical reasoning, options and key precautions

1) Dapsone (usual prophylactic dose 50–100 mg once daily)
- Rationale: good oral alternative with reasonable efficacy in non‑HIV immunocompromised hosts.
- Important precautions/limitations:
  - Must test for G6PD deficiency before starting because dapsone causes dose‑dependent oxidant haemolysis and can cause methemoglobinaemia.
  - Even with normal G6PD, dapsone can cause haemolysis and methemoglobinaemia, and it can cause hypersensitivity reactions (including a DRESS‑type syndrome) and neutropenia — baseline CBC and ongoing monitoring are required.
  - Prior TMP–SMX–related hemolytic anaemia (immune‑mediated) raises concern for further haemolysis with other oxidant agents; use dapsone only after careful risk assessment and hematology input in that setting.
  - Potential drug interactions (eg, with other oxidants) and dose adjustments in renal impairment.

2) Atovaquone suspension or tablets (usual prophylactic dose 1,500 mg once daily with a fatty meal)
- Rationale: oral, non‑sulfa option with good tolerability and no risk of oxidant hemolysis.
- Important precautions/limitations:
  - Absorption is markedly food‑dependent (requires a high‑fat meal); poor absorption in malabsorption or when taken fasting — failure to take with food reduces effectiveness.
  - Generally less well‑studied than TMP–SMX and probably somewhat less effective, but commonly used when sulfa drugs are contraindicated.
  - Can be costly and may not be formulary‑available in all settings.
  - Monitor liver function tests and for gastrointestinal side effects; interactions with rifampin and some other drugs can lower levels.

3) Aerosolized (nebulized) pentamidine 300 mg once monthly
- Rationale: inhaled therapy delivers drug to the lungs and is an established alternative when oral options are not tolerated — this was the regimen used successfully in the case report.
- Important precautions/limitations:
  - Provides mainly pulmonary protection; it does not reliably prevent extrapulmonary PCP.
  - Less effective than TMP–SMX in some series and associated with higher breakthrough rates; avoid as the preferred option if there is concern for systemic disease.
  - Can cause bronchospasm and cough — pre‑treatment bronchodilator may be needed; use cautiously in patients with active airways disease. Inpatients require appropriate nebulizer equipment and trained personnel.
  - Systemic adverse effects are less common than with IV pentamidine, but systemic toxicity (hypoglycaemia/hyperglycaemia, hypotension, renal dysfunction, electrolyte disturbances, pancreatitis, arrhythmia) can occur, particularly with IV formulations.
  - Not ideal when the patient has recurrent aspiration or significant lung heterogeneity that may limit drug deposition — weigh risks/benefits (note: in the presented case the patient had aspiration pneumonitis but tolerated nebulized pentamidine).

Other considerations
- IV pentamidine is used for treatment and occasionally prophylaxis but has more systemic toxicity and is not preferred for routine prophylaxis.
- Regimens such as primaquine + clindamycin are used for treatment of PCP; primaquine has haemolysis/contraindication issues (G6PD) and is not a routine prophylactic option.
- Duration: give prophylaxis while the patient is receiving high‑risk immunosuppression (commonly defined as prednisone ≥20 mg/day for ≥4 weeks in combination with another significant immunosuppressant) and continue until the dose of corticosteroid and other immunosuppressants have been tapered to lower risk levels. For patients receiving agents with prolonged immunosuppressive effects (eg, rituximab), individualize duration and consider longer prophylaxis; discuss with infectious diseases/immunology.
- Monitoring: baseline CBC and liver tests (dapsone/atovaquone), G6PD testing before oxidant drugs, assessment for bronchospasm with nebulized pentamidine, and clinical vigilance for breakthrough PCP.

Practical application to this case
- The treating team appropriately stopped TMP–SMX when haemolytic anaemia occurred and chose monthly nebulized pentamidine 300 mg — an acceptable alternative in this scenario that was tolerated. Given the prior haemolysis, dapsone would be relatively unattractive without hematology input and careful risk assessment. Atovaquone would have been an oral option if enteral absorption and cost/availability permitted and if the patient could reliably take it with a fatty meal.

Takeaway
When TMP–SMX is not tolerated, the three main evidence‑based alternatives are dapsone (test G6PD; risk of hemolysis/methemoglobinaemia), atovaquone (oral, requires high‑fat meal, more costly), and aerosolized pentamidine 300 mg monthly (pulmonary‑only protection, bronchospasm risk, specialized delivery). Choose the agent by balancing efficacy, toxicity (especially haemolysis risk), route and the patient’s pulmonary and gastrointestinal status; coordinate with infectious disease or immunology for complex cases.
