Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Pathophysiology] Which muscle biopsy and immunohistochemical findings are most characteristic of dermatomyositis (as opposed to other inflammatory myopathies), and how does the underlying complement-mediated microangiopathy mechanistically explain prominent subcutaneous/neck edema and dysphagia?

Short answer
- The most characteristic muscle‑biopsy/immunohistochemical pattern of dermatomyositis is a perifascicular process with microvascular complement deposition: perifascicular atrophy/necrosis and perivascular/perimysial inflammation (predominantly CD4+ lymphocytes and B‑cells), combined with membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition in endomysial/perifascicular capillaries and positive type I interferon markers (eg, MxA).  
- Mechanistically, dermatomyositis is a complement‑mediated microangiopathy: C5b‑9 deposition → endothelial injury and capillary drop‑out with increased microvascular permeability and local ischemia. When the head/neck and pharyngeal microvasculature are involved this produces marked soft‑tissue edema (pseudoangioedema) and edema plus inflammatory injury of pharyngeal/upper‑esophageal musculature, producing dysphagia.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (expanded)
1. Histologic/immunohistochemical hallmarks that distinguish dermatomyositis
   - Perifascicular pattern: preferential perifascicular fiber atrophy and necrosis is the classic morphologic clue. The biopsy in this case showed perifascicular necrotic, vacuolated and regenerating fibres — a prototypical finding.  
   - Microvascular complement deposition: immunostaining demonstrating C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) on endomysial/perifascicular capillaries is highly characteristic and supports a complement‑driven endothelial injury. The case reports C5b‑9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries.  
   - Inflammatory cell pattern and IFN signature: perivascular/perimysial inflammation with CD4+ T cells, B cells and plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and upregulation of type I interferon–induced proteins such as MxA on immunostain (helpful diagnostic marker).  
   - What it is not: polymyositis usually shows endomysial CD8+ T cells invading non‑necrotic fibres; immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) shows prominent necrosis with macrophages and minimal lymphocytes and typically lacks C5b‑9 capillary deposition; inclusion body myositis shows rimmed vacuoles and protein inclusions. These differences guide the pathology diagnosis.

2. How complement‑mediated microangiopathy produces edema and dysphagia
   - Trigger (autoantibody/immune complexes/type I IFN milieu) → activation of complement on muscle capillary endothelium → C5b‑9 inserts into endothelial cells.  
   - Endothelial injury results in: (a) increased capillary permeability with plasma leakage into interstitial and subcutaneous tissues (clinically seen as soft‑tissue/neck swelling that mimics angioedema), and (b) capillary loss (drop‑out) producing ischemia of perifascicular fibers → fibre necrosis and atrophy.  
   - When this process affects the head/neck and pharyngeal vascular beds and the pharyngeal and suprahyoid musculature, two related clinical consequences occur: pronounced subcutaneous/neck swelling (pseudoangioedema, non‑histaminergic and non‑bradykinin) and direct inflammatory/ischemic injury to swallowing muscles producing dysphagia (as in this patient’s severe pharyngeal dysphagia and aspiration).  
   - Serum complement levels may be normal because the pathogenic complement activation is focal at the microvascular level; hence normal C3/C4 (as in this case) does not exclude complement‑mediated muscle microangiopathy.

Clinical implication drawn from the case
- The patient’s thigh biopsy demonstrating perifascicular myofibre necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition neatly explains the phenotype: very high CK from muscle necrosis, prominent cervical soft‑tissue oedema from localized microvascular leak, and dysphagia from involvement of pharyngeal musculature.  
- Therapeutically, this pathogenesis explains why immunosuppression and modalities that modulate autoantibodies/complement (steroids, IVIG, B‑cell depletion) can rapidly reduce swelling and improve strength — as occurred in this patient — while antihistamines or C1‑inhibitor therapies directed at histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema would be ineffective.

Take‑home pearl
- Perifascicular atrophy/necrosis + C5b‑9 deposition in endomysial capillaries (± MxA positivity) = dermatomyositis; complement‑mediated endothelial injury causes capillary leak and ischemia that account for the unusual but important presentations of neck/facial oedema and dysphagia.
