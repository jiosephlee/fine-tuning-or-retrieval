Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Management] According to the IMACS guidelines for idiopathic inflammatory myopathy–associated cancer screening, which patient-specific factors categorize this dermatomyositis case as high risk for malignancy, and what baseline investigations are appropriate to pursue?

Direct answer
- Per the IMACS idiopathic inflammatory myopathy–associated cancer screening framework, this patient is classified as high risk because he has two high‑risk features — (1) dermatomyositis (the subtype of inflammatory myopathy most strongly linked to cancer) and (2) clinically significant dysphagia — and also has two intermediate‑risk features (male sex and a positive SAE1 myositis autoantibody). These combined findings place him in a high‑risk category that warrants an expanded baseline malignancy screen and close interval surveillance.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (brief)
- Dermatomyositis is a paraneoplastic-prone myositis subtype; tumor antigens can trigger cross‑reactive immune responses that manifest as skin and muscle disease. Certain clinical features (new‑onset dermatomyositis, older age, dysphagia) and some myositis autoantibodies (eg, anti–TIF1‑γ, NXP2) increase the pretest probability of occult malignancy. SAE1 antibodies are not among the highest‑risk antibodies but in this case were treated as an intermediate risk factor, adding to overall concern.

Appropriate baseline investigations (what to do for this patient)
- Comprehensive history and focused physical exam, emphasizing weight loss, smoking, lymphadenopathy, breast/genitourinary/pelvic and ENT findings.
- Age‑ and sex‑appropriate cancer screening per local guidelines (eg, colonoscopy, mammography, cervical screening), with lower threshold to perform missing routine screens urgently.
- Cross‑sectional imaging:
  - CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (contrast enhanced) or whole‑body FDG‑PET/CT as a baseline — PET/CT is reasonable/preferred in high‑risk patients because of higher sensitivity for occult malignancy. (In this case he had CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, a PET scan, and serial CT chest imaging.)
- Endoscopic evaluation targeted to symptoms and risk:
  - Colonoscopy and upper endoscopy (EGD) — planned in this patient to exclude occult GI malignancy.
- Tumor markers as adjuncts (not diagnostic alone): CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA in men when indicated — these were done and were normal in this patient.
- Site‑specific testing as indicated by sex and symptoms:
  - In women: pelvic exam, transvaginal ultrasound and CA‑125 if concern for ovarian malignancy; mammography for breast cancer screening.
  - ENT/nasopharyngeal evaluation (nasendoscopy) in appropriate ethnic/geographic risk groups or if head/neck symptoms present.
- Baseline labs (general): CBC, LFTs, renal function; consider ESR/CRP as inflammatory markers. (These were part of his initial workup.)
- Referral and coordination: involve oncology or a multidisciplinary myositis/cancer clinic when screening findings are equivocal or suspicion remains high.

Surveillance
- High‑risk patients merit interval surveillance because paraneoplastic myositis can precede detectable cancer. IMACS‑based practice is to repeat directed screening during the first 2–3 years (most cancers are found within the first 12–24 months); typical practice is clinical review and symptom‑directed testing every 3–6 months with repeat imaging or endoscopy guided by initial results and evolving symptoms. In this case, the team elected ongoing clinical and imaging surveillance and planned GI endoscopy as part of the baseline screen.

How the case followed these principles
- The team recognized two high‑risk and two intermediate‑risk factors per IMACS, performed CT imaging, whole‑body PET, tumor markers, and planned colonoscopy/EGD — an appropriate, guideline‑concordant baseline malignancy screen with plans for interval surveillance.
