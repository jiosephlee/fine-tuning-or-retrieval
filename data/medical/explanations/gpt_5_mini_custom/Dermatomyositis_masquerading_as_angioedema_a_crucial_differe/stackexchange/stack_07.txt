Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Management] Outline an initial treatment strategy for dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia and cervical soft tissue swelling threatening airway function, including glucocorticoids and steroid-sparing options. What factors would lead you to favor mycophenolate or rituximab and to avoid methotrexate in this setting?

Clinical question — priorities and initial strategy
When dermatomyositis presents with severe dysphagia and neck soft‑tissue swelling that threatens airway function the immediate priorities are (1) secure and monitor the airway and reduce risk of aspiration, (2) rapidly suppress the inflammatory myositis driving the swelling and pharyngeal weakness, and (3) introduce steroid‑sparing agents while screening/mitigating infectious and treatment risks. Early aggressive immunotherapy often prevents progression to intubation or tracheostomy.

What I would do initially (and what was done in the case)
- Airway and aspiration management: urgent ENT assessment with flexible nasendoscopy, bedside swallowing assessment and early video‑fluoroscopic swallow if feasible; early involvement of speech and language therapy; nil by mouth and enteral feeding (nasogastric) if unsafe to swallow — the patient in the report required NG feeding. Escalate to intubation/tracheostomy if progressive obstruction, loss of protective reflexes or respiratory compromise.
- Rapid immunosuppression (pulse then high‑dose oral steroid): intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 3 days for life‑threatening head/neck muscle involvement, then transition to oral prednisone ≈1 mg/kg (the team used 60 mg) with a planned gradual taper as clinical and biomarker (CK) response allow. This mirrors the reported care.
- Early use of fast‑acting adjunctive immunotherapy for severe dysphagia/airway threat: IVIG (total 2 g/kg given as a 5‑day loading course, then repeat monthly as needed) was used in the case and is reasonable because IVIG can produce more rapid clinical benefit for bulbar dysfunction and severe weakness than many steroid‑sparing agents.
- Add a steroid‑sparing disease‑modifying agent: mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 1 g twice daily was started in the case and is an appropriate first‑line steroid‑sparing choice for skin and muscle disease.
- Consider additional biologic therapy early when disease is severe, antibody‑mediated, or when rapid steroid reduction is desirable: rituximab 1000 mg IV ×2 doses two weeks apart was used for this patient and is an accepted option in severe/refractory dermatomyositis or when autoantibody‑mediated disease (here SAE1‑positive) is suspected.

Why favour mycophenolate in this scenario
- Efficacy/tolerability: MMF has good evidence and widespread clinical use for both cutaneous and myopathic manifestations of dermatomyositis. It is well tolerated as a daily oral agent and pairs well with IVIG when rapid control is needed.
- Safety in lung disease and liver enzyme abnormalities: unlike methotrexate, MMF lacks a strong association with drug‑induced interstitial lung disease and is less hepatotoxic, important in the present case where the patient had abnormal pulmonary function/CT changes and markedly elevated transaminases.
- Practical factors: patient preference for an oral daily medication and tolerability (both cited in the report) are reasonable considerations when multiple agents are available.

Why consider rituximab early here
- Autoantibody‑driven disease: SAE1 positivity indicates an autoantibody‑associated dermatomyositis phenotype. B‑cell depletion with rituximab can be particularly helpful when autoantibodies are implicated and when disease is severe or refractory.
- Severe extramuscular involvement: rituximab is often selected for severe dysphagia, refractory weakness, or other organ‑threatening manifestations when additional steroid‑sparing control is required. In the reported patient rituximab was given in combination with IVIG and MMF because of the severity and airway risk.
- Rituximab is a reasonable early adjunct when clinicians want to avoid other agents that carry organ‑specific toxicity or when faster steroid tapering is needed despite the biologic’s onset of effect being measured in weeks.

Why avoid methotrexate in this patient
- Pulmonary toxicity risk: methotrexate is associated with idiosyncratic methotrexate‑induced pneumonitis and can worsen or unmask interstitial lung disease. This patient had abnormal PFTs, CT chest nodules and aspiration‑related consolidation—features that increase the risk and make methotrexate an unfavorable choice.
- Hepatic injury concerns: the patient’s marked transaminase elevations (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L) increase the risk for methotrexate hepatotoxicity and contraindicate its use until LFTs normalize and alternative causes are addressed.
- Clinical precedent: the treating team explicitly avoided methotrexate for these reasons and selected MMF and rituximab with IVIG, which produced clinical improvement.

Other practical management points
- Baseline infection screening and prophylaxis before rituximab/MMF/IVIG: hepatitis B/C, HIV, TB screening (QuantiFERON indeterminate here but sputum AFB negative), and vaccination review. Start Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis with TMP‑SMX unless contraindicated (the patient developed haemolytic anemia and was switched to inhaled pentamidine).
- Monitor: CK, muscle strength, swallow function, LFTs, CBC, immunoglobulins (if repeated IVIG/rituximab), and serial chest imaging / PFTs for lung disease. Continue cancer screening per IMACS guidelines for dermatomyositis.
- Multidisciplinary care: rheumatology/immunology, ENT, speech/swallow therapy, physiotherapy, nutrition, and if needed, pulmonology and thoracic imaging.

Takeaway
In dermatomyositis with airway‑threatening dysphagia and cervical swelling, act promptly: secure the airway, give high‑dose IV methylprednisolone followed by high‑dose oral prednisone, and add IVIG for rapid control of bulbar disease. Introduce a steroid‑sparing agent such as mycophenolate for maintenance and consider rituximab early when disease is severe or antibody‑associated. Avoid methotrexate when there is concurrent lung disease or marked transaminase elevation, as in the presented case — these factors drove the team’s successful choice of MMF + IVIG + rituximab.
