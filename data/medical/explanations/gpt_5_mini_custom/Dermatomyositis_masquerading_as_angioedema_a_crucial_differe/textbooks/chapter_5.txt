Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 5: ## Outcomes, Pearls, and Pitfalls

Clinical course and outcome in this case were rapid and instructive. With early recognition and escalation to combination immunotherapy (high‑dose IV methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone taper, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab), the patient’s cervical soft‑tissue swelling and muscle strength improved quickly. CK fell from >16,000 U/L to normal (37 U/L) within about three weeks after hospital discharge, hip‑flexor strength recovered fully by that time, and the nasogastric tube used for severe dysphagia was removed by two months. Cervical swelling had completely resolved by three months, prednisone was successfully discontinued by seven months, and by 11–12 months chest imaging and pulmonary function tests showed near‑complete resolution of aspiration changes with stable respiratory function and good exercise tolerance. These time points illustrate that marked biochemical and clinical improvement in inflammatory myopathy can occur over weeks to months with appropriate, sustained immunosuppression, but that supportive measures (eg, enteral feeding, physiotherapy, infection surveillance) are often required during recovery.

Diagnostic pearls from this presentation are practical and easily applied to acute neck‑swelling evaluations. When cervical or facial “angioedema” is unexplained, persistent, or atypical in evolution, always obtain a serum creatine kinase: significant CK elevation points away from histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema and toward a myopathic inflammatory process. A careful skin and nailfold examination is high yield — periungual erythema/microhaemorrhages, violaceous erythema in photodistribution, or other subtle rashes (Holster sign) may be the only cutaneous clues early on. If angioedema workup (C4, C1‑INH level/function) is non‑diagnostic and CK or the clinical exam are suspicious, send a myositis antibody panel and obtain targeted muscle imaging (MRI) and/or FDG‑PET to map inflammatory burden.

Key distinguishing features that help separate dermatomyositis‑related subcutaneous oedema from true angioedema are embedded in the history and exam. Dermatomyositis‑associated swelling often has a subacute onset over days to weeks, is persistent and non‑tender, and lacks urticaria, pruritus, or a clear trigger (eg, ACE inhibitor). It commonly coexists with systemic myopathic signs such as proximal weakness or dysphagia and with biochemical muscle injury (marked CK, AST/ALT, LDH elevations). Complement profiles and C1‑esterase inhibitor studies are typically normal in these cases, as they were here, which argues against hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency.

Biopsy and advanced imaging play complementary roles. Muscle MRI and FDG‑PET are valuable to define the extent and distribution of active myositis, guide biopsy site selection, and to identify areas (eg, head and neck muscles) responsible for airway or swallowing compromise. Histopathology remains confirmatory: perifascicular necrosis/regeneration and capillary C5b‑9 deposition are characteristic of dermatomyositis and were decisive in this patient. Imaging and PET also aided the malignancy screen by showing no other metabolically active disease, helping to focus but not replace formal cancer surveillance.

Several management pitfalls are worth emphasizing. First, avoid over‑pursuing repeated invasive infection workups or delaying immunosuppression when the clinical picture and early investigations (CK, exam, imaging) point to inflammatory myopathy — unnecessary delay can permit progression of dysphagia and respiratory muscle involvement. Second, proactively assess swallowing and aspiration risk: early barium swallow and consideration of enteral feeding prevented further pulmonary complications in this patient. Third, be cautious with steroid tapering; objective markers (strength, CK, imaging) should guide taper speed rather than premature clinical optimism. Finally, choice of steroid‑sparing agent should account for comorbid lung disease (eg, methotrexate was avoided here given concern for ILD), and plan for Pneumocystis prophylaxis when using prolonged high‑intensity immunosuppression.

Prognostic considerations specific to SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis are illustrated by this case. SAE1‑positive patients frequently have cutaneous disease, and dysphagia and ILD are relatively common; nonetheless, many respond well to multimodal immunomodulation, as this patient did. Despite an initially reassuring malignancy workup, dermatomyositis — and SAE1 positivity as an intermediate risk factor — warrants continued vigilance for occult malignancy per established screening guidance; surveillance should be individualized based on risk factors and clinical evolution.

A practical, conceptual approach emerging from this case is as follows: when confronted with persistent cervical/facial oedema without a clear infective or allergic cause, immediately perform focused skin and nail examination and order a CK. If CK is elevated or the exam suggests dermatomyositis, obtain a myositis antibody panel and muscle imaging, assess airway and swallow function urgently, and consider early empiric immunosuppression while arranging definitive testing (biopsy, malignancy screen). This sequence minimizes delays in therapy for potentially life‑threatening muscle involvement while ensuring appropriate diagnostic confirmation and oncologic surveillance.
