Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 3: ## Pathophysiology Focus: Dermatomyositis, Anti-SAE1, and Pseudoangioedema

Dermatomyositis is fundamentally a microvascular disease of skeletal muscle and skin. Immune-mediated injury to endomysial capillaries leads to endothelial damage, capillary dropout and ischemia that is most pronounced at the periphery of muscle fascicles. This “perifascicular” ischemia produces the characteristic perifascicular atrophy and, when severe, necrosis of myofibres. On muscle biopsy this process is reflected both by the pattern of myofibre degeneration in perifascicular regions and by deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) in endomysial capillaries. In the presented patient the biopsy showed perifascicular necrotic and regenerative fibres with C5b‑9 deposition, a finding that links the clinical weakness and tissue oedema to complement‑mediated capillary injury.

How complement becomes activated in dermatomyositis is not completely resolved, but antibody‑dependent mechanisms are important. Autoantibodies or immune complexes can initiate classical complement activation, and resultant formation of the membrane attack complex injures capillary endothelium. Endothelial loss both reduces perfusion (accounting for perifascicular myofibre ischemia) and increases vascular permeability. The latter contributes directly to local interstitial fluid accumulation where inflammation is active.

Anti‑SAE1 (small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1) autoantibodies identify a subset of dermatomyositis with a characteristic clinical pattern. SAE1 targets the SUMO‑activating enzyme complex, a key early component of the sumoylation pathway. Sumoylation is a reversible post‑translational modification that regulates nuclear protein trafficking, transcriptional control, chromatin organization and cellular stress responses. Antibodies to SAE1 presumably reflect immune recognition of proteins involved in these nuclear regulatory pathways; in clinical practice SAE1 positivity is associated with prominent cutaneous manifestations (heliotrope, shawl or V‑sign, Gottron’s pattern), frequent dysphagia and a variable frequency of interstitial lung involvement. In this case the patient’s periungual changes and facial rash, together with severe dysphagia and positive SAE1 antibodies, fit that phenotype.

Subcutaneous and soft tissue swelling — sometimes called “pseudoangioedema” in the dermatomyositis literature — arises through a combination of focal muscle inflammation of the affected region (in this case the cervical and strap muscles), capillary leak from complement‑mediated endothelial injury, and impaired lymphatic drainage from tissue inflammation and fibrosis. When the muscles of the neck and superficial tissues are extensively inflamed, fluid accumulates in subcutaneous and interfascial planes producing a visibly swollen, non‑pitting, non‑pruritic neck. This swelling is driven by inflammatory capillary permeability and interstitial fluid/lymphatic stasis rather than by histamine or bradykinin. The patient’s CT and PET findings of diffuse FDG uptake in head‑and‑neck musculature and MRI evidence of active myositis in proximal muscle groups support this mechanism.

Because the swelling in dermatomyositis is not mediated by histamine or bradykinin, standard laboratory markers and functional tests used to evaluate primary angioedema are typically normal. Complement levels (C4) and C1‑esterase inhibitor quantity/function are preserved, there is no abrupt response to antihistamines or epinephrine, and the time course is usually subacute rather than minutes–hours. Clinically, dermatomyositis‑related swelling often lacks urticaria or intense pruritus and evolves over days to weeks, as seen in this patient, which argues against classic histaminergic or hereditary/bradykinin angioedema and explains the negative angioedema workup.

Dysphagia in dermatomyositis results from involvement of bulbar, pharyngeal and upper esophageal striated muscles. Weakness of these muscles impairs initiation and coordination of swallowing, causing pooling of secretions and food in the pyriform fossae and vallecula, with resultant penetration and aspiration of thin and sometimes thicker fluids. This mechanism explains the patient’s severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy, the aspiration‑type peribronchial consolidation on chest imaging, and the clinical need for enteral feeding. Bulbar and axial muscle weakness can also reduce effective inspiratory and expiratory mechanics, producing restrictive‑type patterns on pulmonary function testing and contributing to poor airway protection.

Serum biochemical findings in active myositis reflect muscle fibre injury rather than primary hepatic disease. Very high creatine kinase (CK) and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) arise from myocyte necrosis; transaminases (AST and ALT) are often raised as well because AST is abundant in muscle and typically exceeds ALT when the source is skeletal muscle. In this patient the markedly elevated CK with normal renal function and a negative urine myoglobin screen, plus AST predominance over ALT, support muscle origin for the enzyme elevations.

Putting the pathophysiology together explains the clinical picture seen here: autoantibody‑associated, complement‑mediated microangiopathy produced intense inflammation of neck musculature and adjacent soft tissues, increasing vascular permeability and lymphatic dysfunction that presented as subacute cervical swelling. Concurrent bulbar muscle involvement produced severe dysphagia with aspiration, and systemic muscle injury produced very high CK and transaminase elevations. Recognising these mechanistic links is important because the management targets immune‑mediated inflammation and complement‑driven tissue injury rather than therapies aimed at histamine or bradykinin pathways.
