Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Atypical Neck Swelling: Initial Presentation and Clinical Framing

The patient presented with a subacute, 3‑week history of progressive cervical soft tissue swelling accompanied by proximal hip flexor weakness and mild myalgia. Importantly he was afebrile, haemodynamically stable and saturating normally on room air, without the overt systemic toxicity that usually accompanies an acute deep neck infection. These features framed the diagnostic problem: an apparent head‑and‑neck process that did not fit the canonical patterns of either fulminant infection or classical angioedema and therefore required a broader, targeted evaluation.

From the emergency department perspective the clinical picture reasonably prompted urgent otolaryngology assessment. Ludwig’s angina and other deep neck space infections commonly present with neck swelling, involvement of the submandibular/strap muscle planes, and retropharyngeal inflammation; CT in this case did indeed show diffuse oedema of the strap muscles extending to the retropharyngeal space, findings that can mimic infective cellulitis. However, the endoscopic assessment did not identify an obstructing lesion, abscess cavity, dental source or mucosal necrosis; instead it revealed increased secretions and coughing during the examination, a clue pointing away from purely local structural disease and toward bulbar dysfunction or dysphagia with aspiration risk.

Early differentials for cervical swelling included the expected surgical and infectious entities (deep neck space infection, cellulitis/erysipelas, sialadenitis), venous congestion syndromes such as superior vena cava obstruction, and non‑infective soft tissue causes such as histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema. Distinguishing features in this case argued against several of these. The time course was subacute and progressive over weeks rather than the abrupt hours‑to‑days onset typical of histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema. The swelling was non‑tender and without urticaria or pruritus; there was no history of exposure to common triggers (no ACE inhibitor, NSAID or dipeptidyl peptidase‑4 inhibitor use), and no personal or family history of hereditary angioedema. Systemic inflammatory signs of severe infection (fever, marked leukocytosis, hemodynamic instability) were absent, and the white cell count was near normal despite a modestly elevated CRP—findings that reduce the likelihood of an untreated deep neck space abscess.

Careful, focused examination provided the diagnostic pivot. Subtle cutaneous and capillary findings were present: periungual erythema with nailfold microhaemorrhages on nailfold capillaroscopy, plus an erythematous rash over the lateral thigh consistent with a “Holster” distribution. Neuromuscular examination showed reduced hip flexor strength (4/5), an otherwise proximal pattern of weakness that is typical for inflammatory myopathies. Generalized facial erythema including involvement of the nasolabial folds further suggested a cutaneous inflammatory process rather than isolated angioedema. Together these findings raised suspicion for dermatomyositis presenting with subcutaneous oedema—so‑called pseudoangioedema—rather than a primary otolaryngologic or histaminergic/bradykinin‑mediated process.

Recognizing this constellation is clinically important because the pathophysiology and management differ: in dermatomyositis, inflammatory myositis and capillary injury can produce localized subcutaneous oedema and fascial swelling, and bulbar muscle involvement can explain increased secretions and dysphagia, whereas histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema or bacterial infection require entirely different immediate therapies. In practice, when neck swelling remains unexplained after initial ENT imaging and bedside assessment, a simple targeted set of steps—repeating a careful skin and neuromuscular examination, checking serum creatine kinase, and considering early rheumatology/immunology input—can expedite diagnosis. In this case those steps led to a prompt CK measurement and further myositis workup that established anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis as the unifying diagnosis, enabling timely immunosuppressive treatment and avoidance of unnecessary surgical intervention.
