Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## From Mimic to Myositis: Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning

This patient’s pathway from an initial working diagnosis of deep neck infection or angioedema to confirmed dermatomyositis illustrates how a focused, stepwise evaluation—integrating bedside findings with targeted laboratory tests, imaging, functional studies, autoantibody profiling and histology—unmasked an inflammatory myopathy whose presentation mimicked an otolaryngologic problem.

The first key diagnostic pivot was the serum enzyme pattern. A creatine kinase (CK) of 16,184 U/L is profoundly elevated and, in the setting of proximal weakness, strongly points to active skeletal muscle injury rather than an isolated soft-tissue process. Concomitant elevation of AST, ALT and LDH supported a muscle source for these enzymes (transaminases may be muscle-derived) rather than isolated hepatopathy. Normal renal function and a negative urine myoglobin screen argued against clinically significant myoglobinuria or frank rhabdomyolysis with renal compromise, but did not diminish the implication of active myositis. In this case the CK result was the turning point: it shifted the diagnostic focus away from primary infectious or histaminergic/bradykinin-mediated angioedema toward an inflammatory myopathy.

Concurrent laboratory testing helped exclude angioedema mechanisms that had originally seemed plausible. Normal C4 and normal C1 esterase inhibitor level and function argue strongly against hereditary or acquired C1-INH deficiency and against complement-consuming angioedema mediated by bradykinin. The clinical context—absence of ACE inhibitor exposure, lack of pruritus or urticaria, and the presence of cutaneous/nailfold changes and weakness—also made histaminergic angioedema unlikely. Thus, both biochemical and clinical features refuted the two most common etiologies of spontaneous neck swelling seen by emergency and ENT services.

Once a myopathic process was suspected, autoimmune serology and subtype testing became crucial. A high-titre ANA with a speckled pattern provided non-specific evidence of autoimmunity, but the extended myositis panel was more diagnostic: a strongly positive anti‑SAE1 antibody with absence of other myositis-specific antibodies (anti‑Mi‑2, TIF1‑γ, Jo‑1, OJ, etc.) pointed to an SAE1-positive dermatomyositis phenotype. Anti‑SAE1 is associated with prominent cutaneous features, dysphagia and a significant risk of systemic involvement (including interstitial lung disease and an association with malignancy). The antibody profile therefore both supported the diagnosis and informed subsequent cancer screening and prognostication.

Imaging localized and graded the inflammatory process. MRI of the thighs demonstrated muscle edema and active myositis in hip‑girdle muscles—information that both corroborated the CK/clinical picture and provided a safe, high-yield target for biopsy. FDG‑PET added complementary data by showing diffuse abnormal muscle uptake, most conspicuous in the head and neck, shoulders, trunk and pelvis; the head/neck uptake correlated with the clinical neck swelling and supported inflammatory myositis as the substrate of the apparent “angioedema.” Importantly, the PET study did not reveal occult hypermetabolic neoplasia, which informed—but did not end—the malignancy search.

Airway and swallowing assessments were essential because the neck swelling manifested with functional compromise. Video fluoroscopic swallow testing demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue and penetration to the vocal cords, confirming oropharyngeal and bulbar muscle involvement and explaining the aspiration‑type consolidation seen on chest imaging. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern that can be seen with impaired upper airway and bulbar muscle function. Together these findings established that the patient’s neck soft‑tissue swelling reflected inflamed and edematous muscles with consequential bulbar dysfunction and aspiration risk, mandating early aggressive immunosuppression to protect the airway and prevent further pulmonary complications.

Chest CT changed the differential by characterizing the pulmonary findings: multiple non‑cavitating nodules and peribronchial consolidation were interpreted as likely aspiration pneumonitis related to dysphagia rather than primary fibrotic interstitial lung disease. Absence of radiological fibrosis or a CT pattern typical for myositis‑associated ILD reduced the immediate likelihood of significant fibrosing lung disease, which influenced the choice and monitoring of immunosuppressive agents.

Histopathology provided the diagnostic confirmation. Muscle biopsy showed perifascicular degeneration/necrosis and regenerative change, scattered perimysial inflammation and deposition of C5b‑9 in endomysial capillaries—the microvascular complement deposition pattern that is characteristic of dermatomyositis. This combination distinguished dermatomyositis from other myopathies: immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy typically shows prominent necrosis with minimal inflammation and different serologies (for example anti‑HMGCR or SRP), inclusion body myositis has a distinct clinical pattern (older age, distal and finger flexor weakness) and rimmed vacuoles on biopsy, and neuromuscular junction disorders such as myasthenia gravis produce weakness without marked CK elevation and have different electrophysiologic and serologic findings. Thus, the concordance of clinical features, antibody profile, imaging and biopsy secured the diagnosis of SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis.

Given the well‑recognized association between dermatomyositis and cancer, the IMACS risk framework was applied: the patient had two high‑risk features (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate features (male sex and SAE1 positivity), placing him in a high‑risk category for an occult malignancy. This justified the comprehensive screening performed—CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET‑CT, tumour markers, and planned upper and lower endoscopy—with continued interval surveillance despite an initially negative oncologic evaluation.

In summary, the diagnostic reasoning moved from mimic to myositis by following discordant clinical clues (persistent, non‑tender neck swelling without infection or allergic trigger; cutaneous nailfold findings; proximal weakness) with a targeted laboratory test (CK) that reoriented the workup. Sequential application of autoantibody testing, muscle‑directed imaging, functional swallow and pulmonary studies, and confirmatory muscle histology not only established dermatomyositis as the unifying diagnosis but also defined disease extent, immediate risks (airway and aspiration), malignancy risk, and appropriate urgency of immunosuppressive therapy. The case underscores the value of early CK measurement and of integrating serology, imaging and biopsy to refine a differential that initially resembled ENT pathology.
