Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] A man in his 40s presents with 3 weeks of progressive, non-tender neck swelling initially presumed to be angioedema; exam reveals subtle periungual erythema and mild proximal hip flexor weakness without urticaria. What single laboratory test should be ordered urgently, and how would a marked abnormality redirect the diagnostic workup?

Order a serum creatine kinase (CK) immediately.

Why: the combination of progressive, non‑tender neck “angioedema,” subtle periungual/skin changes and proximal hip‑flexor weakness should raise suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis) rather than histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema. CK is the single fastest, most informative test to detect active muscle injury. In the reported case a CK of 16,184 U/L (many‑fold above normal) was the pivotal result that changed the working diagnosis.

How a marked CK elevation redirects the workup (practical steps and rationale)
- Diagnostic pivot: a very high CK points to myositis/rhabdomyolysis → stop treating this as isolated angioedema or primary ENT infection and begin a myositis workup.
- Immediate additional labs to obtain/monitor: renal function, electrolytes, AST/ALT, LDH, and urine myoglobin (to assess and manage rhabdomyolysis risk).
- Focused autoimmunity testing: order myositis‑specific/‑associated autoantibodies (eg, SAE1, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, anti‑Jo‑1) and ANA — these may identify dermatomyositis subtypes and cancer risk (as in this case, SAE1 positivity).
- Imaging and tissue diagnosis: obtain MRI of affected muscles and consider FDG‑PET for extent/malignancy screening; plan muscle biopsy if needed to confirm diagnosis (the case had typical biopsy with C5b‑9 deposition).
- Evaluate complications and organ involvement: formal swallowing evaluation (videofluoroscopic swallow) for dysphagia/aspiration; pulmonary assessment with PFTs and chest CT to screen for interstitial lung disease; malignancy screening per IMACS guidance (age‑ and risk‑appropriate imaging/endoscopy).
- Urgent management implications: marked CK with bulbar/airway involvement warrants early aggressive immunosuppression (high‑dose IV methylprednisolone ± IVIG and steroid‑sparing agents) and supportive measures (airway protection, NG feeding) — as was done in the reported patient.
- Continued monitoring: serial CKs to follow treatment response and ongoing surveillance for ILD or occult malignancy given dermatomyositis associations.

Bottom line: In this scenario, a serum CK is the single urgent test. A markedly elevated CK redirects evaluation from angioedema/infectious causes toward inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis), triggers autoimmune serologies, muscle imaging/biopsy, aspiration and pulmonary assessment, malignancy screening, and often urgent immunosuppressive therapy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with isolated cervical swelling, how can you distinguish myositis-related pseudoangioedema from histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema using key historical features, examination findings, and basic laboratory testing?

Direct answer
Myositis-related “pseudo‑angioedema” can be distinguished from histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema by the time course and response to therapy, associated systemic features on exam, and a very small set of targeted laboratory tests. In practice ask three questions: (1) Is the swelling acute, pruritic and steroid/antihistamine‑responsive (histaminergic)? (2) Is there a history of ACE‑inhibitor/DPP‑4 use, family history, or low C4/C1‑INH function and lack of response to antihistamines (bradykinin‑mediated)? (3) Is the swelling subacute/progressive with proximal muscle weakness, cutaneous myositis stigmata and marked muscle enzymes (suggesting myositis)?

Why this works (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- Histaminergic angioedema: mast‑cell degranulation → rapid onset (minutes–hours), often with urticaria/pruritus, erythema, and prompt improvement with antihistamines, corticosteroids or epinephrine. Systemic signs of muscle injury are absent.
- Bradykinin‑mediated angioedema (hereditary/acquired/C1‑INH deficiency or drug‑induced): slower onset than histaminergic, often prolonged and recurrent, nonpruritic, no urticaria, minimal erythema, and poor response to antihistamines/steroids. Complement testing often shows low C4 and abnormal C1‑INH function or levels in C1‑INH deficiency.
- Myositis‑related pseudoangioedema (eg, dermatomyositis): immune‑mediated inflammation of muscle and perifascicular capillaries causes capillary injury, interstitial and subcutaneous edema overlying affected muscle groups. Presentation is typically subacute to weeks, progressive, may be non‑tender, and is accompanied by proximal weakness, dysphagia, characteristic cutaneous findings (heliotrope, Gottron’s papules, shawl/V‑neck sign, periungual capillary changes), and markedly elevated muscle enzymes (CK, AST, LDH). Not mediated by histamine or bradykinin and therefore not rapidly responsive to antihistamines/epinephrine; treatment is immunosuppression.

How to apply this at the bedside (practical stepwise approach)
1. History
   - Timing: minutes–hours (histaminergic) vs hours–days with recurrence and lack of treatment response (bradykinin) vs days–weeks progressive (myositis).
   - Associated features: pruritus/urticaria/facial flushing → histaminergic. Recent ACE‑inhibitor/DPP‑4 use, family history of angioedema → bradykinin. Proximal muscle weakness, myalgias, dysphagia, photosensitive rash, periungual changes → myositis.
   - Medication and family history: specifically ask about ACE inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitors, estrogens.

2. Examination
   - Look for urticaria/pruritus (histaminergic). Angioedema from any cause is non‑pitting; erythema is more typical with histaminergic.
   - Test muscle strength (proximal power), swallow function, and inspect skin and nailfolds for myositis signs (heliotrope, Gottron’s, shawl sign, periungual telangiectasias/microhemorrhages).
   - Airway assessment and ENT exam/imaging: CT that shows edema centered in strap muscles or other musculature rather than isolated subcutaneous swelling points to muscle inflammation (as in this case where CT showed diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles).

3. Key (basic) laboratory tests to order immediately
   - Serum creatine kinase (CK) and muscle‑related labs (AST, ALT, LDH). Marked CK elevation strongly supports myositis (case: CK 16,184 U/L; AST 396 U/L; ALT 143 U/L; LDH 692 U/L).
   - Complement testing: C4 and C1‑esterase inhibitor (function and level). Low C4 or abnormal C1‑INH → hereditary/acquired bradykinin‑mediated angioedema. In the case C3/C4 and C1 esterase were normal, arguing against C1‑INH deficiency.
   - White cell count/CRP: may be mildly elevated with inflammation; pronounced leukocytosis or fevers suggest infection.
   - Therapeutic trial/response: a rapid clinical response to antihistamines/steroids supports histaminergic; lack of response should prompt evaluation for bradykinin causes and myositis.

Putting it together in this case
- The patient had a subacute 3‑week progressive, non‑tender cervical swelling with concurrent proximal weakness, dysphagia and subtle periungual capillary abnormalities — clinical clues pointing away from histaminergic or drug/bradykinin angioedema and toward an inflammatory myopathy. Complement studies (C3/C4 and C1‑INH) were normal, arguing against bradykinin‑mediated disease. A very high CK (16,184 U/L) and muscle inflammation on imaging/biopsy confirmed dermatomyositis causing muscle‑centered edema (pseudoangioedema). His swelling did not behave like histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema and required immunosuppression rather than antihistamines or C1‑INH‑directed therapies.

Takeaway pearl
When neck “angioedema” is subacute, progressive, refractory to usual measures, and accompanied by even subtle muscle weakness or cutaneous findings, check a CK (and C4/C1‑INH). A markedly elevated CK plus myositis signs points to inflammatory myositis with pseudoangioedema and mandates prompt immunologic evaluation and treatment rather than standard angioedema therapies.

### [Pathophysiology] Which muscle biopsy and immunohistochemical findings are most characteristic of dermatomyositis (as opposed to other inflammatory myopathies), and how does the underlying complement-mediated microangiopathy mechanistically explain prominent subcutaneous/neck edema and dysphagia?

Short answer
- The most characteristic muscle‑biopsy/immunohistochemical pattern of dermatomyositis is a perifascicular process with microvascular complement deposition: perifascicular atrophy/necrosis and perivascular/perimysial inflammation (predominantly CD4+ lymphocytes and B‑cells), combined with membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition in endomysial/perifascicular capillaries and positive type I interferon markers (eg, MxA).  
- Mechanistically, dermatomyositis is a complement‑mediated microangiopathy: C5b‑9 deposition → endothelial injury and capillary drop‑out with increased microvascular permeability and local ischemia. When the head/neck and pharyngeal microvasculature are involved this produces marked soft‑tissue edema (pseudoangioedema) and edema plus inflammatory injury of pharyngeal/upper‑esophageal musculature, producing dysphagia.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (expanded)
1. Histologic/immunohistochemical hallmarks that distinguish dermatomyositis
   - Perifascicular pattern: preferential perifascicular fiber atrophy and necrosis is the classic morphologic clue. The biopsy in this case showed perifascicular necrotic, vacuolated and regenerating fibres — a prototypical finding.  
   - Microvascular complement deposition: immunostaining demonstrating C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) on endomysial/perifascicular capillaries is highly characteristic and supports a complement‑driven endothelial injury. The case reports C5b‑9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries.  
   - Inflammatory cell pattern and IFN signature: perivascular/perimysial inflammation with CD4+ T cells, B cells and plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and upregulation of type I interferon–induced proteins such as MxA on immunostain (helpful diagnostic marker).  
   - What it is not: polymyositis usually shows endomysial CD8+ T cells invading non‑necrotic fibres; immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) shows prominent necrosis with macrophages and minimal lymphocytes and typically lacks C5b‑9 capillary deposition; inclusion body myositis shows rimmed vacuoles and protein inclusions. These differences guide the pathology diagnosis.

2. How complement‑mediated microangiopathy produces edema and dysphagia
   - Trigger (autoantibody/immune complexes/type I IFN milieu) → activation of complement on muscle capillary endothelium → C5b‑9 inserts into endothelial cells.  
   - Endothelial injury results in: (a) increased capillary permeability with plasma leakage into interstitial and subcutaneous tissues (clinically seen as soft‑tissue/neck swelling that mimics angioedema), and (b) capillary loss (drop‑out) producing ischemia of perifascicular fibers → fibre necrosis and atrophy.  
   - When this process affects the head/neck and pharyngeal vascular beds and the pharyngeal and suprahyoid musculature, two related clinical consequences occur: pronounced subcutaneous/neck swelling (pseudoangioedema, non‑histaminergic and non‑bradykinin) and direct inflammatory/ischemic injury to swallowing muscles producing dysphagia (as in this patient’s severe pharyngeal dysphagia and aspiration).  
   - Serum complement levels may be normal because the pathogenic complement activation is focal at the microvascular level; hence normal C3/C4 (as in this case) does not exclude complement‑mediated muscle microangiopathy.

Clinical implication drawn from the case
- The patient’s thigh biopsy demonstrating perifascicular myofibre necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition neatly explains the phenotype: very high CK from muscle necrosis, prominent cervical soft‑tissue oedema from localized microvascular leak, and dysphagia from involvement of pharyngeal musculature.  
- Therapeutically, this pathogenesis explains why immunosuppression and modalities that modulate autoantibodies/complement (steroids, IVIG, B‑cell depletion) can rapidly reduce swelling and improve strength — as occurred in this patient — while antihistamines or C1‑inhibitor therapies directed at histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema would be ineffective.

Take‑home pearl
- Perifascicular atrophy/necrosis + C5b‑9 deposition in endomysial capillaries (± MxA positivity) = dermatomyositis; complement‑mediated endothelial injury causes capillary leak and ischemia that account for the unusual but important presentations of neck/facial oedema and dysphagia.

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical phenotype is classically associated with anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, including typical cutaneous signs and its associations with dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and malignancy risk?

Anti‑SAE1 (small ubiquitin‑like modifier‑activating enzyme 1) dermatomyositis classically presents as a cutaneous‑predominant phenotype with frequent, but variably timed, muscle involvement and a propensity for dysphagia, interstitial lung disease (ILD) and an elevated—though not highest—malignancy risk.

Key clinical features and reasoning (grounded in the case):
- Cutaneous phenotype: patients typically have the canonical dermatomyositis rashes—heliotrope/periorbital violaceous erythema, Gottron’s papules, shawl/V‑neck (and Holster) signs and periungual changes/telangiectasias. The reported patient exemplified this with periungual erythema and a Holster sign even though classic heliotrope/Gottron’s were not prominent early.
- Muscle disease: SAE1 disease is often described as skin‑predominant with myositis that can be mild or develop later, but significant myonecrosis and very high CK (as in this patient: CK >16,000 U/L) and clinically important weakness (including bulbar/airway muscles) do occur and may require aggressive immunosuppression.
- Dysphagia: common in SAE1 dermatomyositis—about 46% in the large series cited in the case—and clinically important because of aspiration risk. The case had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue and penetration on swallow study and aspiration‑related consolidation on chest imaging.
- Interstitial lung disease: frequent (≈43% in the review cited) and may range from subclinical PFT abnormalities to radiographic ILD. In this patient PFTs showed a restrictive pattern and CT chest showed peribronchial consolidation (attributed mainly to aspiration), highlighting the need to evaluate respiratory involvement.
- Malignancy risk: intermediate to elevated (the literature review in the report found malignancy in ≈18% of SAE1 cases). Because dermatomyositis itself and features such as dysphagia increase cancer risk, SAE1 positivity contributes to the concern—hence the patient’s comprehensive malignancy screening per IMACS guidance (clinical review, imaging, tumour markers, planned endoscopies).

Practical points for learners:
- Consider SAE1 testing when dermatomyositis has prominent cutaneous findings with or without pronounced myositis, especially if dysphagia or respiratory symptoms are present.
- Recognize that subcutaneous/face or neck oedema can occur and may mimic angioedema or infection; lack of histaminergic/bradykinin features and concurrent skin/nail changes or muscle weakness should prompt CK and myositis antibody testing (as in this case).
- Perform baseline ILD assessment (history, PFTs, chest imaging) and age‑ and risk‑appropriate malignancy screening in SAE1 dermatomyositis—escalate screening when additional risk factors (dermatomyositis itself, dysphagia, male sex in this case) are present.
- Management often requires early, aggressive immunosuppression (glucocorticoids plus steroid‑sparing agents/IVIG/rituximab as indicated), and attention to aspiration risk from dysphagia.

Bottom line: anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis is classically a skin‑predominant dermatomyositis with frequent dysphagia (≈46%) and ILD (≈43%) and a modestly increased malignancy risk (≈18%); it can, however, present with severe myositis and unusual features such as neck/face oedema that mimic angioedema, so maintain a high index of suspicion and screen appropriately.

### [Clinical Pearls] In inflammatory myopathy with severe oropharyngeal dysphagia, how do an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on pulmonary function testing and a videofluoroscopic swallow study guide diagnosis of respiratory complications and immediate management to prevent aspiration-related lung injury?

Clinical pearl — how these two tests change diagnosis and immediate management

When an inflammatory myopathy presents with severe oropharyngeal dysphagia, combine pulmonary function testing and a videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) to (1) determine the mechanism of respiratory compromise, (2) establish aspiration as the cause of lung injury, and (3) trigger immediate protective interventions.

Why each test matters
- Pulmonary function testing: a restrictive pattern in this setting points to respiratory muscle involvement (diaphragm and accessory muscles) or extrathoracic/upper‑airway compromise from inflamed neck musculature and soft tissues. Documented falls in FVC (especially supine), low maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) or evidence of extrathoracic flow limitation imply reduced cough and impaired ability to protect the airway — physiologic risk factors for aspiration and for rapid respiratory decompensation. In the case, an extrathoracic restrictive pattern raised concern for neck/airway muscle dysfunction rather than primary obstructive lung disease.

- Videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS): the VFSS is the diagnostic standard for oropharyngeal dysphagia and aspiration risk. Findings of severe pharyngeal dysphagia, pyriform sinus residue and post‑swallow penetration to the vocal cords (as in this patient) objectively confirm impaired bolus clearance and high risk of micro‑aspiration and aspiration pneumonitis.

How the tests guide diagnosis of respiratory complications
- Correlation of VFSS (aspiration risk) with PFTs (reduced respiratory reserve and ineffective cough) makes aspiration the likely mechanism for the patient’s pulmonary findings. In the report, CT chest showed peribronchial consolidation in mid–lower zones consistent with aspiration pneumonitis; combined with VFSS and restrictive PFTs, this established micro‑aspiration from pharyngeal dysfunction as the cause rather than primary interstitial lung disease.

Immediate management priorities prompted by these findings
1. Protect the airway and prevent further aspiration
   - Make patient NPO immediately when VFSS shows high‑risk aspiration.
   - Institute enteral feeding by nasogastric tube (or consider early PEG if prolonged) — this was used in the case.
   - Positioning (upright during/after feeds), oral hygiene, and suctioning of secretions.
   - Early speech and language pathology (swallow therapy) and ENT input.

2. Treat respiratory compromise and monitor closely
   - Supplemental oxygen, chest physiotherapy, aggressive secretion management.
   - Monitor for respiratory muscle fatigue (serial FVC, MIP/MEP, overnight oximetry) — low reserve may prompt noninvasive ventilation or early intubation if protective reflexes fail or gas exchange deteriorates.
   - Repeat chest imaging to document evolving aspiration pneumonitis versus secondary infection.

3. Treat the underlying inflammatory myopathy urgently
   - Early high‑dose corticosteroids and additional immunotherapy (IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents or biologics) to reverse bulbar and respiratory muscle inflammation; this case received IV methylprednisolone, IVIG and steroid‑sparing therapy with clinical and radiologic improvement.
   - As swallowing recovers, repeat VFSS before reintroducing oral intake.

4. Avoid unnecessary antibiotics unless secondary bacterial pneumonia is suspected
   - Aspiration pneumonitis is often chemical and managed supportively; treat with antibiotics only when clinical, radiologic, or biomarker evidence suggests bacterial infection.

Practical monitoring and disposition
- Keep the patient in a setting where airway/respiratory status can be rapidly escalated.
- Reassess swallow function serially; remove enteral feeding only after a repeat VFSS shows safe swallow.
- Expect improvement in lung imaging and PFTs as muscle inflammation is treated — as happened in this patient, whose CT changes and PFTs improved over months.

Bottom line: In inflammatory myopathy with dysphagia, a restrictive/extrathoracic pattern on PFTs signals reduced respiratory reserve and airway protection, while VFSS documents the anatomic/functional risk of aspiration. Together they diagnose aspiration‑related lung injury and mandate immediate airway protection (NPO, enteral feeding), targeted respiratory monitoring/support, early swallow rehabilitation and prompt immunotherapy to reverse the process.

### [Morning Report] What are the complementary roles of MRI of skeletal muscle and FDG-PET in inflammatory myopathy—both for confirming active myositis and for evaluating occult malignancy—in a patient with dermatomyositis presenting with neck swelling and dysphagia?

Answer (Clinical pearl)

In inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis the diagnostic and staging problem is twofold: first, prove there is active muscle inflammation and choose an optimal site for biopsy; second, because adult dermatomyositis carries an elevated cancer risk, search for an occult malignancy. MRI of skeletal muscle and whole‑body FDG‑PET are complementary tests that address these two problems in different but overlapping ways.

How MRI and FDG‑PET differ and complement each other

- MRI (muscle‑protocol, STIR/T2 fat‑suppressed and T1 sequences)
  - Best test for local muscle assessment. High soft‑tissue contrast identifies active edema/inflammation (T2/STIR hyperintensity), fasciitis, distribution (perifascicular vs diffuse), and chronic changes (T1 fatty replacement, atrophy).
  - Guides muscle‑biopsy site selection by showing muscles with active edema but not long‑standing fatty change—maximizing diagnostic yield. In this case the thigh MRI showed active myositis in bilateral obturators, gracilis and iliopsoas—information that supported the decision to biopsy and correlated with the histology of perifascicular necrosis and capillary C5b‑9 deposition.
  - Useful for quantifying local disease extent and monitoring structural changes over time. It is not a whole‑body test and gives no reliable information about occult malignancy.
  - Not affected by glycaemia or recent exercise in the way PET is; timing relative to immunosuppression is less likely to abolish the MRI signal than it is to blunt PET uptake.

- FDG‑PET (whole‑body)
  - Whole‑body metabolic survey: detects hypermetabolic foci consistent with malignancy and can also demonstrate diffuse or focal muscle inflammation (increased FDG uptake in involved muscles).
  - In dermatomyositis it serves two roles simultaneously: (1) screen for occult cancer (recommended when clinical risk is high by IMACS criteria), and (2) map systemic inflammatory activity (for example, prominent head/neck and bulbar muscle uptake can explain dysphagia or neck swelling).
  - In this patient PET showed diffuse abnormal FDG accumulation involving head/neck and multiple muscle groups but no metabolically active lesion to suggest malignancy—supporting that the neck swelling reflected active myositis rather than an occult tumor.
  - Caveats: FDG uptake is not specific — inflammation, recent exercise, insulin, infection, or recent steroid exposure can alter results (steroids may reduce FDG signal). PET can miss small or low‑FDG tumors and cannot replace targeted endoscopic/age‑appropriate cancer screening; negative PET lowers but does not eliminate cancer risk.

Practical approach illustrated by the case
- Use MRI first (or in parallel) to confirm active myositis, document distribution, and pick a biopsy target. In this case MRI confirmed active thigh myositis and supported the pathologic diagnosis.
- Use FDG‑PET when cancer risk is elevated (here: dermatomyositis, dysphagia, SAE1 positivity, male sex) to perform a whole‑body screen and to assess the systemic inflammatory burden. The PET in this patient both reinforced that the neck swelling was inflammatory (prominent FDG uptake in head/neck muscles) and showed no metabolically active malignancy.
- Remember limitations: neither study alone rules out occult cancer permanently—continue IMACS‑recommended surveillance (age‑appropriate imaging, endoscopy when indicated, interval clinical follow‑up). Also coordinate timing (avoid strenuous exercise before PET, control glucose; interpret PET with knowledge of recent high‑dose steroids/IVIG).

Bottom line
MRI is the anatomical/structural test of choice to prove and localize active myositis and to guide biopsy; FDG‑PET is the whole‑body metabolic test of choice when malignancy screening and assessment of systemic inflammatory burden are required. Used together, as in this patient, they corroborated active dermatomyositis as the cause of neck swelling and dysphagia and provided reassuring, contemporaneous exclusion of metabolically avid occult malignancy—while recognizing that ongoing, guideline‑directed surveillance remains necessary.

### [Management] Outline an initial treatment strategy for dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia and cervical soft tissue swelling threatening airway function, including glucocorticoids and steroid-sparing options. What factors would lead you to favor mycophenolate or rituximab and to avoid methotrexate in this setting?

Clinical question — priorities and initial strategy
When dermatomyositis presents with severe dysphagia and neck soft‑tissue swelling that threatens airway function the immediate priorities are (1) secure and monitor the airway and reduce risk of aspiration, (2) rapidly suppress the inflammatory myositis driving the swelling and pharyngeal weakness, and (3) introduce steroid‑sparing agents while screening/mitigating infectious and treatment risks. Early aggressive immunotherapy often prevents progression to intubation or tracheostomy.

What I would do initially (and what was done in the case)
- Airway and aspiration management: urgent ENT assessment with flexible nasendoscopy, bedside swallowing assessment and early video‑fluoroscopic swallow if feasible; early involvement of speech and language therapy; nil by mouth and enteral feeding (nasogastric) if unsafe to swallow — the patient in the report required NG feeding. Escalate to intubation/tracheostomy if progressive obstruction, loss of protective reflexes or respiratory compromise.
- Rapid immunosuppression (pulse then high‑dose oral steroid): intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 3 days for life‑threatening head/neck muscle involvement, then transition to oral prednisone ≈1 mg/kg (the team used 60 mg) with a planned gradual taper as clinical and biomarker (CK) response allow. This mirrors the reported care.
- Early use of fast‑acting adjunctive immunotherapy for severe dysphagia/airway threat: IVIG (total 2 g/kg given as a 5‑day loading course, then repeat monthly as needed) was used in the case and is reasonable because IVIG can produce more rapid clinical benefit for bulbar dysfunction and severe weakness than many steroid‑sparing agents.
- Add a steroid‑sparing disease‑modifying agent: mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 1 g twice daily was started in the case and is an appropriate first‑line steroid‑sparing choice for skin and muscle disease.
- Consider additional biologic therapy early when disease is severe, antibody‑mediated, or when rapid steroid reduction is desirable: rituximab 1000 mg IV ×2 doses two weeks apart was used for this patient and is an accepted option in severe/refractory dermatomyositis or when autoantibody‑mediated disease (here SAE1‑positive) is suspected.

Why favour mycophenolate in this scenario
- Efficacy/tolerability: MMF has good evidence and widespread clinical use for both cutaneous and myopathic manifestations of dermatomyositis. It is well tolerated as a daily oral agent and pairs well with IVIG when rapid control is needed.
- Safety in lung disease and liver enzyme abnormalities: unlike methotrexate, MMF lacks a strong association with drug‑induced interstitial lung disease and is less hepatotoxic, important in the present case where the patient had abnormal pulmonary function/CT changes and markedly elevated transaminases.
- Practical factors: patient preference for an oral daily medication and tolerability (both cited in the report) are reasonable considerations when multiple agents are available.

Why consider rituximab early here
- Autoantibody‑driven disease: SAE1 positivity indicates an autoantibody‑associated dermatomyositis phenotype. B‑cell depletion with rituximab can be particularly helpful when autoantibodies are implicated and when disease is severe or refractory.
- Severe extramuscular involvement: rituximab is often selected for severe dysphagia, refractory weakness, or other organ‑threatening manifestations when additional steroid‑sparing control is required. In the reported patient rituximab was given in combination with IVIG and MMF because of the severity and airway risk.
- Rituximab is a reasonable early adjunct when clinicians want to avoid other agents that carry organ‑specific toxicity or when faster steroid tapering is needed despite the biologic’s onset of effect being measured in weeks.

Why avoid methotrexate in this patient
- Pulmonary toxicity risk: methotrexate is associated with idiosyncratic methotrexate‑induced pneumonitis and can worsen or unmask interstitial lung disease. This patient had abnormal PFTs, CT chest nodules and aspiration‑related consolidation—features that increase the risk and make methotrexate an unfavorable choice.
- Hepatic injury concerns: the patient’s marked transaminase elevations (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L) increase the risk for methotrexate hepatotoxicity and contraindicate its use until LFTs normalize and alternative causes are addressed.
- Clinical precedent: the treating team explicitly avoided methotrexate for these reasons and selected MMF and rituximab with IVIG, which produced clinical improvement.

Other practical management points
- Baseline infection screening and prophylaxis before rituximab/MMF/IVIG: hepatitis B/C, HIV, TB screening (QuantiFERON indeterminate here but sputum AFB negative), and vaccination review. Start Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis with TMP‑SMX unless contraindicated (the patient developed haemolytic anemia and was switched to inhaled pentamidine).
- Monitor: CK, muscle strength, swallow function, LFTs, CBC, immunoglobulins (if repeated IVIG/rituximab), and serial chest imaging / PFTs for lung disease. Continue cancer screening per IMACS guidelines for dermatomyositis.
- Multidisciplinary care: rheumatology/immunology, ENT, speech/swallow therapy, physiotherapy, nutrition, and if needed, pulmonology and thoracic imaging.

Takeaway
In dermatomyositis with airway‑threatening dysphagia and cervical swelling, act promptly: secure the airway, give high‑dose IV methylprednisolone followed by high‑dose oral prednisone, and add IVIG for rapid control of bulbar disease. Introduce a steroid‑sparing agent such as mycophenolate for maintenance and consider rituximab early when disease is severe or antibody‑associated. Avoid methotrexate when there is concurrent lung disease or marked transaminase elevation, as in the presented case — these factors drove the team’s successful choice of MMF + IVIG + rituximab.

### [Management] According to the IMACS guidelines for idiopathic inflammatory myopathy–associated cancer screening, which patient-specific factors categorize this dermatomyositis case as high risk for malignancy, and what baseline investigations are appropriate to pursue?

Direct answer
- Per the IMACS idiopathic inflammatory myopathy–associated cancer screening framework, this patient is classified as high risk because he has two high‑risk features — (1) dermatomyositis (the subtype of inflammatory myopathy most strongly linked to cancer) and (2) clinically significant dysphagia — and also has two intermediate‑risk features (male sex and a positive SAE1 myositis autoantibody). These combined findings place him in a high‑risk category that warrants an expanded baseline malignancy screen and close interval surveillance.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (brief)
- Dermatomyositis is a paraneoplastic-prone myositis subtype; tumor antigens can trigger cross‑reactive immune responses that manifest as skin and muscle disease. Certain clinical features (new‑onset dermatomyositis, older age, dysphagia) and some myositis autoantibodies (eg, anti–TIF1‑γ, NXP2) increase the pretest probability of occult malignancy. SAE1 antibodies are not among the highest‑risk antibodies but in this case were treated as an intermediate risk factor, adding to overall concern.

Appropriate baseline investigations (what to do for this patient)
- Comprehensive history and focused physical exam, emphasizing weight loss, smoking, lymphadenopathy, breast/genitourinary/pelvic and ENT findings.
- Age‑ and sex‑appropriate cancer screening per local guidelines (eg, colonoscopy, mammography, cervical screening), with lower threshold to perform missing routine screens urgently.
- Cross‑sectional imaging:
  - CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (contrast enhanced) or whole‑body FDG‑PET/CT as a baseline — PET/CT is reasonable/preferred in high‑risk patients because of higher sensitivity for occult malignancy. (In this case he had CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, a PET scan, and serial CT chest imaging.)
- Endoscopic evaluation targeted to symptoms and risk:
  - Colonoscopy and upper endoscopy (EGD) — planned in this patient to exclude occult GI malignancy.
- Tumor markers as adjuncts (not diagnostic alone): CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA in men when indicated — these were done and were normal in this patient.
- Site‑specific testing as indicated by sex and symptoms:
  - In women: pelvic exam, transvaginal ultrasound and CA‑125 if concern for ovarian malignancy; mammography for breast cancer screening.
  - ENT/nasopharyngeal evaluation (nasendoscopy) in appropriate ethnic/geographic risk groups or if head/neck symptoms present.
- Baseline labs (general): CBC, LFTs, renal function; consider ESR/CRP as inflammatory markers. (These were part of his initial workup.)
- Referral and coordination: involve oncology or a multidisciplinary myositis/cancer clinic when screening findings are equivocal or suspicion remains high.

Surveillance
- High‑risk patients merit interval surveillance because paraneoplastic myositis can precede detectable cancer. IMACS‑based practice is to repeat directed screening during the first 2–3 years (most cancers are found within the first 12–24 months); typical practice is clinical review and symptom‑directed testing every 3–6 months with repeat imaging or endoscopy guided by initial results and evolving symptoms. In this case, the team elected ongoing clinical and imaging surveillance and planned GI endoscopy as part of the baseline screen.

How the case followed these principles
- The team recognized two high‑risk and two intermediate‑risk factors per IMACS, performed CT imaging, whole‑body PET, tumor markers, and planned colonoscopy/EGD — an appropriate, guideline‑concordant baseline malignancy screen with plans for interval surveillance.

### [Management] For a dermatomyositis patient receiving high-dose glucocorticoids plus additional immunosuppression, what are evidence-based options for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis when trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole is not tolerated, and what important precautions or limitations accompany each alternative?

Short answer
Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP–SMX) is first‑line prophylaxis for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP). When TMP–SMX cannot be used, the evidence‑based alternatives are (1) oral dapsone, (2) oral atovaquone, and (3) aerosolized (nebulized) pentamidine. Each is imperfect and has important limitations that must be considered when choosing therapy — in this case the treating team stopped TMP–SMX for hemolytic anaemia and used monthly nebulized pentamidine 300 mg with good tolerance.

Clinical reasoning, options and key precautions

1) Dapsone (usual prophylactic dose 50–100 mg once daily)
- Rationale: good oral alternative with reasonable efficacy in non‑HIV immunocompromised hosts.
- Important precautions/limitations:
  - Must test for G6PD deficiency before starting because dapsone causes dose‑dependent oxidant haemolysis and can cause methemoglobinaemia.
  - Even with normal G6PD, dapsone can cause haemolysis and methemoglobinaemia, and it can cause hypersensitivity reactions (including a DRESS‑type syndrome) and neutropenia — baseline CBC and ongoing monitoring are required.
  - Prior TMP–SMX–related hemolytic anaemia (immune‑mediated) raises concern for further haemolysis with other oxidant agents; use dapsone only after careful risk assessment and hematology input in that setting.
  - Potential drug interactions (eg, with other oxidants) and dose adjustments in renal impairment.

2) Atovaquone suspension or tablets (usual prophylactic dose 1,500 mg once daily with a fatty meal)
- Rationale: oral, non‑sulfa option with good tolerability and no risk of oxidant hemolysis.
- Important precautions/limitations:
  - Absorption is markedly food‑dependent (requires a high‑fat meal); poor absorption in malabsorption or when taken fasting — failure to take with food reduces effectiveness.
  - Generally less well‑studied than TMP–SMX and probably somewhat less effective, but commonly used when sulfa drugs are contraindicated.
  - Can be costly and may not be formulary‑available in all settings.
  - Monitor liver function tests and for gastrointestinal side effects; interactions with rifampin and some other drugs can lower levels.

3) Aerosolized (nebulized) pentamidine 300 mg once monthly
- Rationale: inhaled therapy delivers drug to the lungs and is an established alternative when oral options are not tolerated — this was the regimen used successfully in the case report.
- Important precautions/limitations:
  - Provides mainly pulmonary protection; it does not reliably prevent extrapulmonary PCP.
  - Less effective than TMP–SMX in some series and associated with higher breakthrough rates; avoid as the preferred option if there is concern for systemic disease.
  - Can cause bronchospasm and cough — pre‑treatment bronchodilator may be needed; use cautiously in patients with active airways disease. Inpatients require appropriate nebulizer equipment and trained personnel.
  - Systemic adverse effects are less common than with IV pentamidine, but systemic toxicity (hypoglycaemia/hyperglycaemia, hypotension, renal dysfunction, electrolyte disturbances, pancreatitis, arrhythmia) can occur, particularly with IV formulations.
  - Not ideal when the patient has recurrent aspiration or significant lung heterogeneity that may limit drug deposition — weigh risks/benefits (note: in the presented case the patient had aspiration pneumonitis but tolerated nebulized pentamidine).

Other considerations
- IV pentamidine is used for treatment and occasionally prophylaxis but has more systemic toxicity and is not preferred for routine prophylaxis.
- Regimens such as primaquine + clindamycin are used for treatment of PCP; primaquine has haemolysis/contraindication issues (G6PD) and is not a routine prophylactic option.
- Duration: give prophylaxis while the patient is receiving high‑risk immunosuppression (commonly defined as prednisone ≥20 mg/day for ≥4 weeks in combination with another significant immunosuppressant) and continue until the dose of corticosteroid and other immunosuppressants have been tapered to lower risk levels. For patients receiving agents with prolonged immunosuppressive effects (eg, rituximab), individualize duration and consider longer prophylaxis; discuss with infectious diseases/immunology.
- Monitoring: baseline CBC and liver tests (dapsone/atovaquone), G6PD testing before oxidant drugs, assessment for bronchospasm with nebulized pentamidine, and clinical vigilance for breakthrough PCP.

Practical application to this case
- The treating team appropriately stopped TMP–SMX when haemolytic anaemia occurred and chose monthly nebulized pentamidine 300 mg — an acceptable alternative in this scenario that was tolerated. Given the prior haemolysis, dapsone would be relatively unattractive without hematology input and careful risk assessment. Atovaquone would have been an oral option if enteral absorption and cost/availability permitted and if the patient could reliably take it with a fatty meal.

Takeaway
When TMP–SMX is not tolerated, the three main evidence‑based alternatives are dapsone (test G6PD; risk of hemolysis/methemoglobinaemia), atovaquone (oral, requires high‑fat meal, more costly), and aerosolized pentamidine 300 mg monthly (pulmonary‑only protection, bronchospasm risk, specialized delivery). Choose the agent by balancing efficacy, toxicity (especially haemolysis risk), route and the patient’s pulmonary and gastrointestinal status; coordinate with infectious disease or immunology for complex cases.

### [Clinical Pearls] What nailfold capillaroscopy abnormalities (and related periungual findings) support a diagnosis of dermatomyositis, and how can recognizing these help avoid mislabeling atypical localized neck swelling as idiopathic angioedema?

Key point
Careful inspection of the periungual region — and, when available, nailfold capillaroscopy — can reveal a myositis microangiopathy that strongly favors dermatomyositis over primary angioedema. Recognizing those findings in a patient with atypical, subacute facial/neck swelling should prompt testing for myositis (CK, myositis antibodies, swallow assessment/imaging) and avoid mislabeling the process as idiopathic histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.

What to look for on examination and capillaroscopy
- Periungual erythema and dilated/telangiectatic capillary loops at the proximal nailfold.  
- Giant/dilated capillary loops and loss of normal hairpin architecture.  
- Capillary drop-out (avascular areas) reflecting capillary loss.  
- Nailfold microhaemorrhages.  
- Associated periungual findings: ragged/overgrown cuticles, cuticular hypertrophy, and periungual telangiectasias.

Why these findings point to dermatomyositis (pathophysiology and diagnostic reasoning)
- Dermatomyositis is a microangiopathy of the skin and muscle. Complement activation (membrane attack complex C5b‑9) injures endomysial and periungual capillaries → capillary dilatation, haemorrhage and eventual capillary loss. These changes are visible on nailfold capillaroscopy and correlate with disease activity.  
- The same microvascular injury increases permeability in affected regions and can produce subcutaneous/soft‑tissue edema (pseudo‑angioedema), especially in the face and neck. This mechanism is distinct from histamine‑mediated urticarial angioedema or bradykinin‑mediated swelling, which have different clinical features and laboratory correlates (eg, urticaria/pruritus, rapid fluctuation for histaminergic; ACE inhibitor exposure or C1‑esterase dysfunction for bradykinin types).  
- Thus periungual capillary abnormalities are a window into systemic microangiopathy and should raise suspicion for dermatomyositis even when classic skin stigmata (Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash) are absent or subtle.

How this applied in the case
- The patient had proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy with periungual erythema despite absence of classic Gottron’s or heliotrope signs. That finding led clinicians to check CK (16,184 U/L), do myositis serology (anti‑SAE1 positive) and obtain muscle biopsy showing C5b‑9 deposition — confirming dermatomyositis as the cause of the neck swelling rather than idiopathic angioedema. Early recognition led to appropriate immunosuppression and resolution of the swelling and muscle disease.

Practical clinical pearls
- In any patient with unexplained, subacute or persistent facial/neck swelling, examine the periungual area and perform nailfold capillaroscopy if available.  
- If you see dilated capillaries, giant loops, microhaemorrhages or avascular zones, immediately check CK, consider myositis antibody testing, assess swallowing/airway function, and involve rheumatology/immunology.  
- Distinguish dermatomyositis‑related edema from angioedema by time course (subacute, persistent), absence of urticaria/pruritus, lack of ACE‑inhibitor or family history, and normal complement/C1 tests in many cases.  
- Early recognition prevents delays in immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) and avoids inappropriate angioedema treatments while protecting airway and swallowing function.

Bottom line: periungual telangiectasias, dilated/giant capillaries, microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on nailfold capillaroscopy are red flags for dermatomyositis. Spotting them in a patient with an otherwise unexplained neck or facial swelling should shift your differential away from idiopathic angioedema and toward prompt myositis workup and treatment — as in this case.
