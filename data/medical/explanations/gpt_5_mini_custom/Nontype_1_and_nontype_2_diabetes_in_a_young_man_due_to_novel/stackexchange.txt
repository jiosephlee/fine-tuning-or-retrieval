Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with non-ketotic hyperglycaemia, negative GAD/IA-2/ZnT8 antibodies, glycosuria, a family history of early-onset diabetes across generations, and preserved stimulated C-peptide, what specific clinical and laboratory features should prompt genetic testing for MODY and help distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes?

Short answer
Suspect MODY — and specifically HNF1A‑MODY — when a young adult with hyperglycaemia has an autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes, non‑ketotic disease, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia (often with disproportionate glycosuria), and little or no evidence of insulin resistance. These features should prompt molecular genetic testing (targeted MODY/monogenic diabetes panel with HNF1A included). Genetic confirmation changes management (oral agents — classically low‑dose sulfonylureas; GLP‑1 RA or metformin alternatives) and triggers cascade testing in relatives.

Clinical reasoning and key discriminating features (with case correlations)
- Age and inheritance pattern: diabetes diagnosed in adolescence/young adulthood with clear multigenerational transmission (affected maternal grandmother, mother and brother in this case) strongly favors MODY over typical type 1 or type 2 diabetes.
- Autoimmunity: repeatedly negative anti‑GAD, IA‑2 and ZnT8 antibodies argues against classic autoimmune type 1 diabetes. The case had negative GADA and IA‑2.
- Endogenous insulin secretion: preserved stimulated C‑peptide supports non‑type 1 disease. In this case the glucagon stimulation test rose from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L; the authors cite a stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L as indicating preserved secretion.
- Lack of ketotic presentation: absence of diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation (non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia) points away from fulminant type 1.
- Glycosuria disproportionate to glycaemia: glycosuria despite varying glucose levels is a characteristic of HNF1A‑MODY because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the proximal tubule; the case demonstrates glycosuria on health exam and the report discusses reduced renal threshold for glucose.
- Pattern of hyperglycaemia: marked postprandial hyperglycaemia with elevated 2‑hour OGTT (case: 2‑hr glucose 19.8 mmol/L) is typical of early HNF1A disease when fasting hyperglycaemia may be less prominent.
- Low insulin‑resistance phenotype: absence of acanthosis nigricans, normal/near‑normal BMI (case BMI 24.6 kg/m2) and no strong features of metabolic syndrome support MODY over typical T2D (though rising obesity can blur this distinction).
- Clinical course and treatment response: sulfonylurea sensitivity or marked improvement on oral agents rather than high early insulin requirement is suggestive. In the case, insulin was stopped after genetic confirmation and the patient attained excellent control on GLP‑1 RA and metformin.

Laboratory features that should trigger genetic testing
- Negative diabetes autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8).
- Demonstrable endogenous insulin secretion: stimulated C‑peptide above commonly used thresholds (the report uses >0.60 nmol/L as supportive).
- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (e.g., 2‑hr OGTT ≥11.1 mmol/L) with glycosuria.
- Family history consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance (multiple successive affected generations, early onset).
- Early onset (diagnosis in adolescence or young adulthood) or atypical clinical course for type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

How to distinguish HNF1A‑MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes (practical comparisons)
- vs type 1:
  - HNF1A‑MODY: autoantibody negative, preserved stimulated C‑peptide at presentation, non‑ketotic course, progressive β‑cell failure over years.
  - Type 1: typically autoantibody positive and rapid loss of C‑peptide; DKA more likely at presentation.
- vs type 2:
  - HNF1A‑MODY: strong autosomal dominant family history, younger age at onset, minimal insulin resistance signs, prominent postprandial glycaemic excursions and glycosuria from low renal threshold.
  - Type 2: associated with obesity, acanthosis, metabolic syndrome and polygenic risk; family history is common but not classically multigenerational early‑onset pattern.

Diagnostic standard and practical steps
- When the clinical picture above is present, obtain: diabetes autoantibodies; fasting and stimulated C‑peptide (eg, glucagon stimulation); OGTT if needed; careful family pedigree.
- Use a validated MODY probability tool as an adjunct (recognize limitations in non‑European populations).
- Definitive diagnosis requires molecular testing (targeted MODY gene panel or NGS); HNF1A is the commonest MODY gene and should be included. Interpret variants per ACMG criteria and perform cascade testing in relatives if pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant is found (as in this case: novel HNF1A p.N270S variant classified pathogenic/likely pathogenic).
- Genetic diagnosis impacts therapy (consider sulfonylurea first‑line for HNF1A; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an effective alternative and may be safer than sulfonylureas in some patients; avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors without caution because glycosuria is intrinsic and DKA risk may be higher).

Bottom line
In any young adult with non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia with disproportionate glycosuria, and a multigenerational history of early‑onset diabetes, pursue genetic testing for MODY (including HNF1A). Confirmation guides therapy (often oral agents rather than insulin), enables family cascade testing, and prevents misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes — exactly as illustrated by the patient with the novel HNF1A variant in this report.

### [Pathophysiology] Why is glycosuria common in HNF1A-MODY at comparatively lower plasma glucose levels, and what is the role of HNF1A in regulating renal glucose handling via SGLT2 and the renal threshold for glucose?

Short answer
Glycosuria in HNF1A‑MODY occurs at lower plasma glucose levels because loss-of-function HNF1A mutations reduce expression of the proximal‑tubule sodium‑glucose cotransporter SGLT2 (SLC5A2). That lowers the kidney’s maximal reabsorptive capacity (Tm) and therefore lowers the renal threshold for glucose so that filtered glucose exceeds reabsorption at lower plasma glucose concentrations.

Why this happens — physiology and molecular basis
- Normal: filtered glucose = GFR × plasma glucose. The proximal tubule (mostly via SGLT2 in the early segment) reabsorbs the large majority of filtered glucose up to a Tm. The plasma concentration at which filtered load exceeds Tm is the renal threshold for glucosuria (classically ≈10 mmol/L or ~180 mg/dL).  
- In HNF1A‑MODY: HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in renal proximal tubular cells that directly regulates SLC5A2 (the gene for SGLT2). Heterozygous HNF1A mutations → reduced transcription of SGLT2 → lower SGLT2 protein and reduced Tm. As a result, the renal threshold falls and glycosuria appears at lower plasma glucose levels (and sometimes even with only modest hyperglycaemia).

Clinical relevance and implications (linked to the case)
- Diagnostic clue: early glycosuria out of proportion to insulin requirement or BMI and a strong family history should raise suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY — exactly what occurred in this case, and the authors note reduced SGLT2 expression as the mechanism.  
- Management caution: because these patients already have impaired SGLT2–mediated reabsorption, adding pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition can produce marked glycosuria, volume depletion, genital infections and, rarely, euglycaemic ketoacidosis. The case discussion explicitly warns about these risks and argues caution using SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY.  
- Symptoms and course: increased urinary glucose contributes to polyuria, polydipsia and weight loss and may exaggerate dehydration during intercurrent illness.

Takeaway pearl
When you see glycosuria at relatively low plasma glucose in a young patient with autosomal dominant early‑onset diabetes, think HNF1A‑MODY — the mechanism is reduced HNF1A‑driven SGLT2 expression → lower Tm and lower renal glucose threshold, a useful diagnostic clue and an important treatment consideration (avoid reflex use of SGLT2 inhibitors).

### [Morning Report] How do you interpret a glucagon-stimulated C-peptide test in suspected monogenic diabetes (e.g., fasting C-peptide with a rise to >0.60 nmol/L after glucagon), and how should these results guide initial insulin use and subsequent decisions to de-escalate insulin in a clinically stable patient?

Clinical answer (morning‑report pearl)

What the glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide means
- The glucagon stimulation test assesses residual β‑cell secretory capacity. In this case the patient’s fasting C‑peptide was 0.39 nmol/L and rose to 0.83 nmol/L after IV glucagon. The authors use a stimulated threshold >0.60 nmol/L to indicate preserved endogenous insulin secretion. Practically, a low fasting C‑peptide with a robust stimulated rise means baseline secretion is suppressed by hyperglycaemia but a measurable β‑cell reserve remains — a pattern typical of early HNF1A‑MODY rather than autoimmune type 1 diabetes.
- Remember the test is supportive, not definitive. Interpret C‑peptide in context: timing/duration of diabetes, current glucose level, renal function, recent insulin or secretagogue use, and islet autoantibodies (both were negative in this patient).

How this guides initial insulin use
- When patients present with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, glucose 12.4 mmol/L here) it is reasonable to start short‑term insulin to rapidly reduce glucotoxicity, relieve symptoms, and protect β‑cells — even if MODY is suspected. That is what was done in the case (basal degludec 8 U/day plus metformin/saxagliptin).
- The presence of a preserved stimulated C‑peptide supports the expectation that ongoing basal insulin may not be required long term once glucotoxicity is reversed and a more specific therapy is chosen.

How to decide and proceed to de‑escalate insulin
Use a stepwise, safety‑minded approach:
1. Confirm ancillary data
   - Negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide (>0.60 nmol/L as in this case), family history suggest MODY and make type 1 diabetes unlikely.
2. Treat the immediate problem with insulin if needed
   - Use short course/low‑dose basal insulin to control hyperglycaemia and relieve symptoms.
3. Reassess after glycaemic improvement
   - Recheck glucose control (SMBG readings) and HbA1c after a few weeks. In the case the HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 9.1% at 1 month.
4. Trial structured insulin withdrawal when conditions are met
   - Preconditions: preserved stimulated C‑peptide, negative autoantibodies, clinical stability (no DKA history), improving glucose control and patient able to monitor closely.
   - Practical cues in everyday practice: low insulin requirement (many clinicians consider a basal dose very low or total daily insulin requirement reduced substantially), absence of frequent hyperglycaemia during a supervised trial, and/or genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY when available.
   - In the reported patient, insulin was stopped after genetic confirmation and evidence of β‑cell reserve; therapy was switched to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist plus metformin with excellent result.
5. Monitor intensively after withdrawal
   - Frequent capillary glucose checks initially, prompt adjustments of non‑insulin agents, and close follow‑up (early visits, repeat HbA1c at 3 months). Reassess C‑peptide over time as needed.
6. Choose maintenance therapy appropriate to the genotype
   - For HNF1A‑MODY, sulfonylureas are classic first‑line, with GLP‑1 RAs and metformin reasonable alternatives or adjuncts depending on hypoglycaemia risk and comorbidities (as illustrated by this case). Avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors without careful consideration because glycosuria is often exaggerated in HNF1A‑MODY.

Bottom line
- A stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L (even if fasting C‑peptide is low) indicates preserved β‑cell reserve and supports the feasibility of withdrawing insulin after initial stabilization and with close monitoring. Start insulin acutely to control severe hyperglycaemia and reverse glucotoxicity; then, once clinical and biochemical improvement and supportive immunologic/genetic data are available, de‑escalate insulin in a supervised, stepwise fashion and switch to genotype‑tailored oral/GLP‑1 therapy — exactly the strategy that succeeded in the reported HNF1A‑MODY patient.

### [Management] What are the preferred first-line and alternative glucose-lowering therapies in HNF1A-MODY, when might GLP-1 receptor agonists be favored over sulfonylureas, and why should SGLT2 inhibitors be used cautiously (or avoided) in this genotype?

First-line therapy
- Low‑dose sulfonylureas are the classic first‑line oral therapy for HNF1A‑MODY. These patients have a primary defect of insulin secretion but intact KATP‑channel responsiveness, so small doses of sulfonylureas often produce large glucose‑lowering effects and can maintain glycaemic control for years.

When to use alternatives (and which)
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) are a well‑supported alternative and are increasingly favored when there is:
  - progressive decline in β‑cell reserve (higher hypoglycaemia risk with sulfonylureas),
  - obesity or need for weight loss, or
  - a clinical preference to avoid frequent hypoglycaemia.
  A randomized crossover trial cited in the report found similar glycaemic efficacy between liraglutide and glimepiride in HNF1A‑MODY but substantially less hypoglycaemia with liraglutide.
- Metformin is appropriate as adjunctive therapy when features of insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome are present (as in this case) but is not genotype‑specific therapy.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors are modest alternatives when GLP‑1 RAs are not tolerable or contraindicated, though their potency is less.
- Insulin is indicated acutely for severe hyperglycaemia, DKA, or when endogenous insulin secretion is exhausted; after genetic diagnosis many patients can often be transitioned off insulin (as in this case where insulin was stopped and dulaglutide + metformin achieved A1c 6.0%).

Why GLP‑1 RAs may be favored over sulfonylureas
- Mechanism: GLP‑1 RAs stimulate glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon and promote weight loss — reducing hypoglycaemia risk compared with sulfonylureas that stimulate insulin regardless of ambient glucose.
- Clinical tradeoffs: sulfonylureas are inexpensive and highly effective early, but cause more hypoglycaemia and may accelerate β‑cell exhaustion; GLP‑1 RAs preserve better safety (less hypoglycaemia), cardiovascular/weight benefits in many patients, and were effective in the presented patient (dulaglutide + metformin reduced A1c from 9.1% → 6.0%).

Why SGLT2 inhibitors should be used cautiously or avoided
- Pathophysiology: HNF1A is a transcriptional regulator of SGLT2 in the proximal tubule. Loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression and lower the renal glucose threshold, causing baseline glycosuria.
- Clinical consequences of adding an SGLT2 inhibitor:
  - Exaggerated glycosuria and volume depletion (risk of marked dehydration),
  - Increased risk of genital infections,
  - Reported cases of euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and severe dehydration in HNF1A‑MODY when SGLT2 activity is already low.
- Efficacy may also be diminished because the target protein (SGLT2) is downregulated.
- Therefore, the potential harms (volume depletion, DKA) often outweigh modest incremental glycaemic benefits in this genotype; if considered, use extreme caution, ensure adequate insulin reserve, counsel about ketone monitoring, and avoid during intercurrent illness or if insulin is being withdrawn.

Practical clinical pearls
- Suspect HNF1A‑MODY with young onset, strong autosomal dominant family history, glycosuria out of proportion to hyperglycaemia, negative autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide.
- Start low‑dose sulfonylurea as first-line in many patients, but choose GLP‑1 RA when hypoglycaemia risk, weight, or cardiovascular considerations favor it — and particularly when β‑cell function is waning.
- Use metformin when insulin resistance coexists; reserve insulin for severe presentation and reassess after genetic confirmation.
- Avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY because of impaired SGLT2 expression and documented risks of dehydration and DKA; if used, do so only with careful counselling and close monitoring.
- The case illustrates these principles: initial insulin for severe hyperglycaemia, genetic diagnosis prompted transition to dulaglutide + metformin with excellent glycaemic response, and the authors appropriately caution against SGLT2 inhibitors for this genotype.

### [Clinical Pearls] A novel missense HNF1A variant is absent from ClinVar/gnomAD, lies in the DNA-binding domain adjacent to known pathogenic residues, segregates with early-onset diabetes in the family, and has a high deleterious computational score. Which ACMG criteria apply, and how would you justify classifying this variant as likely pathogenic?

Short answer: Apply PM1 (location in a critical functional domain), PM2 (absent from population databases), PP1 (co‑segregation with disease), PP3 (multiple computational predictions support deleterious effect) and PP4 (patient phenotype highly specific for the gene). The combination of two moderate-level criteria (PM1 + PM2) plus multiple supporting criteria (PP1, PP3, PP4) meets ACMG rules for a likely pathogenic classification.

Why each criterion applies in this case
- PM1 (moderate): The variant is a missense change in the HNF1A DNA‑binding domain, a well‑established mutational hot‑spot for HNF1A‑MODY. The authors note most pathogenic HNF1A mutations cluster in the DNA‑binding domain and that the novel change lies adjacent to known pathogenic residues — exactly the situation PM1 is intended to capture (critical domain without benign variation).  
- PM2 (moderate): The variant is absent from large public databases (ClinVar, gnomAD) and was not previously reported in the literature or VarSome, consistent with rarity in controls.  
- PP1 (supporting → can be upgraded toward moderate with multiple meioses): The variant segregates with early‑onset diabetes in the family (affected maternal grandmother, mother, younger brother and proband). That co‑segregation across multiple affected relatives supports pathogenicity; with documented segregation across several meioses this criterion may be considered stronger than “supporting.”  
- PP3 (supporting): In silico tools strongly predict a deleterious effect (AlphaMissense score 0.995 and similar computational evidence), supporting an impact on protein function.  
- PP4 (supporting): The proband’s phenotype (early‑onset, non‑autoimmune diabetes with glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but insufficient C‑peptide and a dominant family history) is highly specific for HNF1A‑MODY.

Why these criteria justify “likely pathogenic”
- Under ACMG combinatorial rules, two moderate criteria (PM1 + PM2) plus two or more supporting criteria (PP1, PP3, PP4) are sufficient to classify a variant as likely pathogenic. Practically: PM1 (domain hotspot) + PM2 (absent in controls) + PP3 (computational) + PP4 (phenotype) already meets the threshold; the observed familial segregation (PP1) strengthens the assignment and could upgrade evidence if additional meioses are documented.  
- There is no currently available strong or very strong evidence (no well‑established functional assay demonstrating a damaging effect, no de novo occurrence), so “pathogenic” is not yet established — but the aggregate evidence is robust enough for “likely pathogenic,” which is appropriate for clinical decision‑making (as in this report, where the genetic result guided transition from insulin to targeted therapy).

Practical note
- Additional experimental functional data (transcriptional activity, DNA binding) or formalized segregation analysis with additional affected/unaffected relatives could elevate the classification to pathogenic. For now, the ACMG‑consistent “likely pathogenic” call is well justified and supports the precision‑medicine management described in the case.

### [Differential Diagnosis] At the bedside, how would you differentiate among common MODY subtypes—HNF1A-, HNF4A-, HNF1B-, and GCK-MODY—based on characteristic clinical clues (for example, neonatal history, renal/structural abnormalities, severity and pattern of hyperglycaemia), to prioritize gene testing?

Direct answer
At the bedside you can often prioritize which MODY gene to test by looking for a small set of clinical “red flags”: the pattern and severity of hyperglycaemia (stable mild vs progressive marked), presence of neonatal problems (macrosomia or hypoglycaemia), renal/urogenital or pancreatic structural disease, glycosuria, and the family history. Use these features to choose a single-gene test when the phenotype is classic, or a multi‑gene NGS panel when features overlap.

How the four common MODY subtypes differ (practical bedside clues)

- GCK‑MODY (MODY2)
  - Typical bedside picture: lifelong, mild fasting hyperglycaemia that is stable over years (fasting glucose mildly ↑; HbA1c usually only mildly raised, e.g. ~5.5–7.5%).
  - OGTT: only a small increment above baseline; patients often asymptomatic.
  - Neonatal history: no severe neonatal hypoglycaemia; glucose set-point is higher from birth.
  - Complications/treatment: minimal microvascular risk; usually no pharmacologic treatment outside pregnancy.
  - When to prioritise testing: incidentally discovered, mild, non‑progressive hyperglycaemia with family history of similar mild elevations.

- HNF1A‑MODY
  - Typical bedside picture: adolescent/young‑adult onset with progressively worsening β‑cell dysfunction; prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia and often marked rises on OGTT.
  - Distinctive clues: glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose because of lowered renal glucose reabsorption (reduced SGLT2 expression); strong autosomal‑dominant family history of early diabetes; good initial response to low‑dose sulfonylureas (and GLP‑1 agents are effective).
  - Autoantibodies: usually negative; C‑peptide often preserved early.
  - When to prioritise testing: symptomatic young adult with marked postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, negative autoantibodies and dominant family history — exactly the phenotype in this case. The team’s clinical suspicion of HNF1A was appropriate and was confirmed by NGS (novel HNF1A p.N270S).

- HNF4A‑MODY
  - Typical bedside picture: resembles HNF1A in adolescence/young adulthood (progressive insulin secretory defect).
  - Distinctive clues: a history of neonatal macrosomia and transient neonatal hypoglycaemia (hyperinsulinaemia) is characteristic and should prompt HNF4A testing.
  - When to prioritise testing: presence of neonatal hypoglycaemia or large birthweight in the proband or parent plus later-onset familial diabetes.

- HNF1B‑MODY (also called renal cysts and diabetes syndrome)
  - Typical bedside picture: diabetes can be variable; often accompanied by renal structural disease (cysts, hypoplasia), early CKD, abnormal urinalysis, electrolyte abnormalities, genital tract malformations, and sometimes pancreatic hypoplasia.
  - Distinctive clues: renal/urogenital anomalies on imaging, unexplained early kidney disease, or pancreatic exocrine insufficiency – these point to HNF1B rather than an HNF1A/HNF4A phenotype. Many HNF1B cases are de novo, so a negative family history does not exclude it.
  - When to prioritise testing: diabetes plus any renal structural abnormality or early CKD.

Putting it into practice (a simple bedside algorithm)
1. Mild, lifelong, stable fasting hyperglycaemia → test GCK first.
2. Neonatal macrosomia + transient neonatal hypoglycaemia → test HNF4A.
3. Progressive postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, strong autosomal‑dominant family history, negative autoantibodies → test HNF1A first (as in this case).
4. Diabetes with renal/urogenital malformations, early CKD, electrolyte abnormalities or pancreatic hypoplasia → test HNF1B.
5. If features overlap or are not classic, perform a multi‑gene NGS MODY panel.

Clinical relevance illustrated by the case
This patient had marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (OGTT 2‑hr 19.8 mmol/L), glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but insufficient stimulated C‑peptide, and a clear dominant family history — the classic bedside pattern that prioritizes HNF1A testing. Genetic confirmation changed management (discontinuation of insulin, effective use of GLP‑1 receptor agonist and oral agents, and counseling about sulfonylurea sensitivity and SGLT2 inhibitor cautions).

Bottom line
Use the pattern of hyperglycaemia (stable mild vs progressive severe), neonatal history, and presence of renal/structural anomalies to guide targeted testing: GCK for mild stable fasting hyperglycaemia, HNF4A when neonatal macrosomia/hypoglycaemia is present, HNF1A for progressive postprandial disease with glycosuria and strong family history, and HNF1B when renal/urogenital abnormalities accompany diabetes. If uncertainty exists, order a MODY gene panel.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the strengths and limitations of using the Exeter MODY probability calculator in non-European populations, and how should its output be integrated with clinical judgment when selecting patients for genetic testing?

Direct answer
- The Exeter MODY probability calculator is a useful, evidence‑based screening tool that standardizes pretest probability for monogenic diabetes, but it was developed and calibrated in predominantly White European cohorts and therefore can misestimate risk in non‑European populations. Use its output as one component of decision making — a quantitative prompt, not a gatekeeper — and always integrate it with clinical features, biochemical tests (autoantibodies, C‑peptide), family segregation and local epidemiology before ordering genetic testing.

Why it helps (strengths)
- Combines key clinical variables into a single, reproducible pretest probability so clinicians avoid ad hoc referrals and can prioritize testing resources.  
- Improves diagnostic yield in populations similar to the derivation cohort and helps identify patients who might benefit from targeted genetic panels (for example, it flagged a 62.4% probability in the presented patient and helped justify sequencing).  
- Transparent and easy to apply at the bedside or clinic visit; complements rather than replaces confirmatory genetic sequencing.

Why you must be cautious in non‑European populations (limitations)
- Derivation/calibration bias: the model parameters reflect disease prevalence and phenotype in White European cohorts. In other ethnic groups the baseline prevalence, phenotype (age at onset, BMI, insulin resistance), and family patterns differ — so the calculator may overestimate (or less commonly underestimate) true MODY probability. The case report notes specifically that it “may overestimate the probability of MODY in non‑White groups.”  
- Variable predictive value of input variables: rising obesity and high background prevalence of type 2 diabetes in some populations reduce the discriminatory power of BMI and family history. Autoantibody and C‑peptide distributions may also differ by ancestry and age.  
- It does not incorporate some helpful contextual clues (glycosuria with low renal threshold, marked sensitivity to low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 response, specific extrapancreatic features), nor does it account for population‑specific variant spectra or founder mutations.  
- A high calculator score does not guarantee a pathogenic finding, and a low score does not exclude MODY — especially when family history and biochemistry are strongly suggestive.

How to integrate the calculator with clinical judgment (practical approach)
1. Treat the score as pretest probability, not a diagnosis. Use it to prioritize who to test when resources are limited.  
2. Always couple the calculator result with:
   - Islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8) — negative antibodies raise suspicion for MODY.  
   - Endogenous insulin secretion (fasting or stimulated C‑peptide) — preserved C‑peptide supports non‑type 1 diabetes.  
   - Multigenerational autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes (look for affected members in successive generations).  
   - Clinical phenotype: lack of insulin resistance signs, disproportionate glycosuria, age at onset, low insulin requirements or atypical drug response.  
3. In non‑European patients: lower your threshold to test when several concordant clinical features exist despite uncertainty in the calculator, because MODY is often underdiagnosed in these groups. Conversely, if the calculator is high but the phenotype is inconsistent and local T2D prevalence is very high, interpret the result cautiously and consider additional supportive tests before sequencing.  
4. Use broad NGS panels and apply ACMG criteria with segregation analysis for novel variants. As in the case, a novel HNF1A variant absent from databases still reached pathogenic classification based on segregation, functional prediction and location in the DNA‑binding domain — illustrating why genetic testing and careful interpretation are essential.  
5. Localize thresholds to practice setting: many centers adopt probability cutoffs to trigger testing, but thresholds should be adapted to available resources and local validation data. If no local validation exists, give more weight to concordant clinical/biochemical evidence than to the numerical score alone.  
6. Communicate uncertainty and counsel patients: explain that the calculator helps estimate risk but that testing decisions also depend on family implications, cost, and impact on management.

Clinical pearl (one‑line takeaway)
The Exeter calculator is a helpful, standardized pretest tool — particularly in European populations — but in non‑European patients its score should be integrated with antibody testing, C‑peptide, family history, phenotype and local prevalence; when multiple clinical features align (as in this case: negative antibodies, preserved C‑peptide, multigenerational early‑onset diabetes), proceed to genetic testing even if the calculator’s performance in that ethnicity is imperfect.

### [Management] Given the autosomal dominant inheritance of HNF1A-MODY, what are the key points for family counseling, including recurrence risk to offspring, who should undergo cascade genetic testing, and how results would alter screening and treatment in relatives?

Bottom line
- HNF1A‑MODY is inherited in an autosomal‑dominant manner: each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of inheriting the pathogenic HNF1A variant. Penetrance is age‑dependent and variable, so genotype does not always equal immediate symptomatic disease.
- Offer genetic cascade testing to first‑degree relatives (parents, siblings, children) of the proband and to other relatives with early‑onset diabetes; testing should begin with a targeted assay for the known family variant.
- A positive test changes management: confirm diagnosis, prompt MODY‑specific treatment (usually oral agents rather than automatic lifelong insulin), directed surveillance for diabetic complications from the time of diagnosis, and informed reproductive planning. A negative test obviates the need for MODY‑directed surveillance and returns the person to population‑based diabetes screening.

Clinical reasoning and counseling points (how to explain this to the family)

1) Recurrence risk
- Autosomal dominant transmission → 50% chance that any biological child of the proband will inherit the same HNF1A variant.
- Because expressivity and age of onset vary, an affected genotype may present in adolescence, young adulthood, or later; discuss that an unaffected child (negative genotype) still has background population risk for type 2 diabetes.

2) Who to test first (practical cascade strategy)
- Start with first‑degree relatives (mother/father, siblings, children) — in this case the maternal line already has multiple early‑onset cases (grandmother, mother, brother), so test the mother and brother if not already genotyped and then asymptomatic children.
- Prioritize relatives with early‑onset hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, or those misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes.
- Use a targeted test for the familial variant (cheaper and faster than full panels). If a relative has a high clinical suspicion but is negative for the familial variant, consider broader sequencing panels.
- For children: testing is appropriate because the diagnosis often appears in adolescence and because management differs early on; do this after pre‑test counseling about implications and consent/assent per local practice.

3) How test results change screening and treatment
- Positive for the familial HNF1A pathogenic variant:
  - Confirms MODY diagnosis. In the reported case the genetic result supported stopping insulin and moving to GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin with marked HbA1c improvement (10.2% → 6.0%), illustrating how genotype directed therapy.
  - First‑line therapy for many adults with HNF1A‑MODY is low‑dose sulfonylurea; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an effective alternative and may be preferred in patients at risk for hypoglycaemia or with declining β‑cell function (as in this case). Insulin can often be de‑escalated or avoided when endogenous insulin is adequate.
  - Avoid presumptive use of therapies that may pose specific risks without benefit: HNF1A mutations lower SGLT2 expression and renal glucose threshold, so SGLT2 inhibitors can increase glycosuria and—per case discussion—may increase risk of dehydration or ketoacidosis; weigh risks carefully.
  - Institute diabetes surveillance from time of diagnosis (glycaemic monitoring/HbA1c at appropriate intervals, annual retinal screening and urine albumin per diabetes guidelines) rather than waiting for symptom development.
  - Offer genetic counseling about family planning (50% transmission risk; options such as prenatal testing or preimplantation genetic diagnosis may be discussed if desired).

- Negative for the familial variant:
  - The relative does not carry that familial MODY mutation and does not require MODY‑specific therapy or intensified MODY surveillance.
  - Continue standard population or risk‑based diabetes screening (lifestyle counseling, periodic glucose/HbA1c), because a negative result does not eliminate lifetime risk of common type 2 diabetes.

4) Dealing with uncertain results and segregation
- If a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) is found, interpret in the context of family segregation and phenotype. Segregation with multiple affected relatives, as in this report, increases confidence in pathogenicity; involve a genetics professional for interpretation.
- In this case the novel p.N270S variant segregated with early‑onset diabetes in the maternal lineage and met ACMG criteria for pathogenic/likely pathogenic classification, which strengthened the decision to change therapy.

5) Practical points to communicate
- Arrange formal genetic counseling before and after testing to discuss implications (medical, psychological, insurance/ employment where relevant).
- Test symptomatic relatives first to confirm segregation; then offer testing to asymptomatic at‑risk relatives.
- Document the familial variant in the medical record and provide relatives a copy of results to facilitate testing.

Tie back to the case
- This patient’s family history (grandmother, mother, brother all early‑onset) and the confirmed heterozygous HNF1A variant illustrate autosomal‑dominant transmission and the value of targeted cascade testing. The genetic diagnosis permitted de‑escalation from insulin and selection of GLP‑1 agonist + metformin with excellent glycaemic response—exactly the type of precision change to communicate to relatives considering testing.

### [Morning Report] Early in HNF1A-MODY, postprandial hyperglycaemia may predominate. How does a 2-hour OGTT value ≥11.1 mmol/L with lesser fasting elevation inform the diagnosis and raise suspicion for HNF1A-MODY despite absent ketosis and negative autoantibodies?

Bottom line: A marked 2‑hour OGTT glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L with only modest fasting elevation in a young adult—especially with a strong family history, negative islet autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide—should increase suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY because it reflects an early, predominantly postprandial β‑cell secretory defect rather than autoimmune insulin deficiency.

Why this pattern points to HNF1A‑MODY (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- HNF1A mutations preferentially impair glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion (loss of first‑phase and meal‑stimulated release) and reduce expression of glucose‑sensing genes (eg, GLUT2, insulin), so early disease produces striking postprandial excursions while fasting glucose may be only mildly or intermittently elevated.  
- With time β‑cell failure progresses and fasting hyperglycaemia develops; but in the early stage an isolated or disproportionately high 2‑hour OGTT result is a characteristic signal of a primary β‑cell defect rather than insulin resistance or autoimmune destruction.
- HNF1A also lowers renal SGLT2 expression → glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels, a supportive clinical clue that can accompany postprandial glycaemia.

How the case illustrates this
- The patient’s 2‑hour OGTT was 19.8 mmol/L (well above the diabetes threshold of ≥11.1 mmol/L) while fasting glucose was less markedly elevated (12.4 mmol/L) — a disproportionate postprandial rise consistent with impaired stimulated insulin secretion.  
- Autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2) were negative and stimulated C‑peptide was moderately preserved (0.83 nmol/L), arguing against classic type 1 autoimmune diabetes and supporting a non‑autoimmune β‑cell defect. The strong family history of early‑onset diabetes further supports a monogenic MODY picture; genetic testing confirmed an HNF1A pathogenic variant.

Diagnostic implication and action
- A 2‑hour OGTT ≥11.1 mmol/L in a young patient with the above features meets the biochemical criterion for diabetes but, when disproportionate to fasting glucose and accompanied by negative autoantibodies/preserved C‑peptide and autosomal dominant family history, should prompt consideration of MODY and referral for genetic testing (NGS panel or targeted HNF1A sequencing).  
- Recognising this pattern matters therapeutically: HNF1A‑MODY patients frequently respond to oral secretagogues (low‑dose sulfonylureas) or GLP‑1 receptor agonists and can often avoid long‑term insulin if correctly diagnosed.

Practical teaching point
- Don’t rely solely on fasting glucose or assume young-onset diabetes = type 1. In young patients with a high 2‑hour OGTT relative to fasting glucose, negative autoantibodies and familial diabetes, think HNF1A‑MODY and pursue genetic confirmation—this changes prognosis, family counselling and treatment.
