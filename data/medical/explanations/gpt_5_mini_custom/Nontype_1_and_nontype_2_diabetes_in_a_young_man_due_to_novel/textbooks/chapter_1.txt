Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 1: ## From Screening Abnormality to Suspicion of MODY: Case Presentation and Key Clues

This patient was first identified because of routine screening that revealed hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. Rather than treating that finding in isolation, the clinical approach should integrate age, family history, biochemical pattern and physical examination to judge whether the picture fits type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes (T2D) or a monogenic cause such as MODY. In this case several features pointed away from straightforward T1D or T2D and raised suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY.

The demographics and family pedigree are the strongest early clue. The patient is a young adult with multiple first‑degree and second‑degree relatives who developed diabetes in their 20s–30s (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in late 20s, younger brother at 20). That pattern—diabetes in successive generations with early onset—is characteristic of an autosomal dominant inheritance and should prompt consideration of MODY. By contrast, T1D typically presents with acute autoimmune destruction without this clear multigenerational pattern, and T2D family histories are often broader and associated with obesity and older age of onset.

The biochemical phenotype at presentation supports a β‑cell secretory defect with prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia. Laboratory values included an HbA1c of 10.2% and fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L with a 2‑hour OGTT glucose of 19.8 mmol/L, documenting marked hyperglycaemia and impaired postprandial glucose handling. HNF1A‑MODY classically produces progressive β‑cell dysfunction with disproportionate postprandial rises on OGTT, so the OGTT result fits this diagnosis. Importantly there were no features of diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation, which makes fulminant autoimmune T1D less likely.

Physical exam and metabolic profile give mixed signals that can confuse classification. The patient’s BMI was 24.6 kg/m2 and there was no acanthosis nigricans—findings that argue against insulin‑resistant T2D. However, the lipid profile (triglycerides 4.31 mmol/L, HDL 1.01 mmol/L) suggested dyslipidaemia that could be interpreted as metabolic syndrome and bias clinicians toward a T2D diagnosis. As obesity becomes more common, reliance on BMI or isolated lipid abnormalities will increasingly mislead; the absence of clinical stigmata of insulin resistance (acanthosis, marked central obesity) should prompt careful consideration of alternative diagnoses when other clues are present.

Glycosuria is a particularly helpful sign in HNF1A‑MODY. HNF1A regulates expression of SGLT2 in the proximal tubule; pathogenic HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression and lower the renal threshold for glucose, producing glycosuria that can be disproportionate to blood glucose levels. In practice, finding glycosuria in a young patient—especially when ketones or DKA are absent—should raise the possibility of HNF1A‑MODY. In this case glycosuria was detected on screening despite the absence of DKA, consistent with reduced renal glucose reabsorption mediated by HNF1A dysfunction.

Early symptoms (polydipsia, polyphagia, weight loss) are common to T1D, T2D and MODY and therefore do not reliably distinguish them. The patient’s report of increased thirst, appetite and weight loss illustrates this overlap and explains why initial classification is often difficult. Because symptom overlap is common, the diagnostic process must rely on objective investigations (autoantibodies, stimulated C‑peptide, OGTT pattern) and family history rather than symptoms alone.

Putting the pieces together: red flags for MODY in this case were young age at onset, multigenerational early‑onset diabetes suggesting dominant inheritance, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but inadequate stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L), pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia on OGTT, and renal glycosuria without DKA. Common pitfalls that delay the MODY diagnosis include anchoring on initial hyperglycaemia and treating empirically for T1D or T2D, over‑weight or dyslipidaemia biasing toward T2D, and low clinical awareness of monogenic diabetes. When several of the red flags above are present, the appropriate next steps are targeted testing (autoantibodies and assessment of endogenous insulin secretion) and timely genetic testing for MODY, because confirming a monogenic aetiology can materially change management and prognosis.
