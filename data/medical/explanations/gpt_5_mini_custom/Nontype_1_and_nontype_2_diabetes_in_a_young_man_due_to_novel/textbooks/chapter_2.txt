Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 2: ## Building the Differential and Executing the Workup

When a young adult presents with marked hyperglycaemia, glycosuria and a family history of early-onset diabetes, the clinician must move beyond the default labels of “type 1” or “type 2” diabetes and assemble a focused differential that considers monogenic forms (MODY), latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) and secondary causes. In this case the stepwise approach—clinical features, targeted biochemical tests and physiological stimulation testing—served both to lower the pretest probability of some diagnoses and to raise the suspicion for HNF1A-MODY, prompting molecular testing.

Differential diagnosis and how clinical features weigh in
- Type 1 diabetes: classically presents with acute insulin deficiency, ketonemia/ketonuria and positive islet autoantibodies. Rapidly falling C‑peptide and requirement for insulin soon after diagnosis are typical. The absence of diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation, negative islet autoantibodies and a measurable stimulated C‑peptide argued against fulminant autoimmune type 1 disease in this patient, although early type 1 can temporarily have preserved C‑peptide and negative antibodies.
- Type 2 diabetes (T2D): typically associated with obesity, insulin resistance, acanthosis nigricans and features of metabolic syndrome. This patient had a near‑normal BMI (24.6 kg/m2) and no acanthosis, although his triglycerides and HDL suggested some metabolic risk. Because increasing obesity blurs the clinical distinction, metabolic features alone are insufficient to exclude monogenic diabetes.
- LADA (latent autoimmune diabetes of adults): an autoimmune, slower‑progressing form of β‑cell failure that occurs in adults and usually shows one or more positive autoantibodies. Negative antibodies make LADA unlikely in this case.
- MODY and its subtypes: MODY should be considered in young patients with dominant family history of early diabetes, non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia and preserved—but often progressively declining—β‑cell function. HNF1A‑MODY classically shows marked postprandial excursions and glycosuria at relatively low plasma glucose because of a lower renal glucose threshold.
- Secondary diabetes: pancreatic disease, medications, endocrinopathies and genetic syndromes can produce hyperglycaemia; these were not suggested by the history or examination in this patient.

Islet autoantibodies: interpretation and caveats
Measurement of islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A and increasingly ZnT8) is a frontline discriminant between autoimmune and non‑autoimmune diabetes. In this patient GADA and IA‑2A were negative, lowering the likelihood of classic type 1 diabetes and LADA. Important caveats: (1) autoantibodies can be absent early in type 1 disease or in a minority of LADA cases, and (2) low‑titer autoantibody positivity has been reported occasionally in patients with genetically confirmed MODY. Thus antibody results are supportive but not absolute: negative antibodies here decreased the probability of autoimmune diabetes and increased the relative likelihood of a non‑autoimmune cause such as MODY.

C‑peptide assessment: fasting versus stimulated and clinical interpretation
C‑peptide quantifies endogenous insulin secretion and is a practical discriminator of insulin deficiency. A fasting value gives a baseline estimate, but stimulated testing (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal tolerance test) is more sensitive for residual β‑cell function. In this case a glucagon stimulation test produced a rise from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L. Using the commonly applied threshold cited in the report (>0.60 nmol/L as preserved secretion), the stimulated value indicates preserved but inadequate insulin secretion—more consistent with progressive β‑cell dysfunction (as in MODY) than with absolute insulin deficiency characteristic of established type 1 disease. Nuances to remember: early type 1 may still have measurable stimulated C‑peptide, and C‑peptide assays and threshold cutoffs vary between labs, so results should always be interpreted in the clinical context. Here the pattern (measurable stimulated C‑peptide but need for early glycaemic control) fit a non‑autoimmune, progressive insulin secretory defect.

OGTT and postprandial dysglycaemia as supportive evidence
HNF1A‑MODY often produces exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia because of defective glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion. The patient’s 2‑hour glucose of 19.8 mmol/L on a 75 g OGTT (very high postload value) reinforces the presence of marked postprandial intolerance rather than isolated fasting hyperglycaemia. In the context of a young patient with family history and preserved stimulated C‑peptide, a striking OGTT abnormality increases suspicion for HNF1A‑type MODY.

Sudomotor testing: what reduced electrochemical skin conductance indicates and limitations
Electrochemical skin conductance testing (e.g., Sudoscan) probes small‑fiber autonomic function by measuring sweat gland activity. Severely reduced conductance in the hands, as reported here, suggests autonomic small‑fiber dysfunction, which can be an early complication of diabetes. Interpretation caveats: results are influenced by glycaemic status, recent hyperglycaemia, hydration, skin temperature and other small‑fiber neuropathy causes. In a newly diagnosed young patient, reduced sudomotor function may reflect early hyperglycaemia‑related neuropathic change but is not specific for a particular diabetes aetiology; it is a supportive finding of end‑organ effect, not a diagnostic discriminator between MODY and other forms.

Clinical prediction tools: the Exeter MODY calculator—use and limits
Clinical calculators (for example, the Exeter MODY probability calculator) combine age of onset, BMI, family history, antibody status and treatment to estimate the probability of MODY and help select candidates for genetic testing. When the patient’s data were entered, the calculator estimated a ~62% probability of MODY—meaning genetic testing was strongly indicated. Limitations include that derivation cohorts were largely White European; performance is poorer and may overestimate probability in non‑White populations. Therefore scores should not be used in isolation but integrated with the full clinical and biochemical picture.

How each test shifted diagnostic likelihood in this patient
- Strong autosomal dominant family history (multiple first‑degree/close relatives with early‑onset diabetes) increased pretest probability for a monogenic aetiology.
- Absence of acidosis and ketosis at presentation, together with negative islet autoantibodies, decreased the likelihood of classic autoimmune type 1 diabetes.
- A preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) argued against absolute insulin deficiency and was compatible with MODY or early T2D rather than long‑standing type 1.
- A marked 2‑hour OGTT elevation and glycosuria were concordant with HNF1A‑MODY physiology (postprandial insulin secretory defect and reduced renal glucose threshold).
- Metabolic features (elevated triglycerides, lower HDL) introduced some ambiguity by suggesting possible insulin resistance/T2D, but the combination of family history, age at onset and the biochemical pattern still favored MODY over typical T2D.

Indications to proceed to genetic testing and choosing a molecular approach
Given the cumulative evidence—young age at onset, strong multigenerational early‑onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, measurable stimulated C‑peptide and a clinical picture consistent with HNF1A physiology—the team appropriately proceeded to genetic testing. Contemporary practice favours targeted next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panels that include the common MODY genes (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B and others) because NGS permits parallel, cost‑effective analysis of many relevant loci and can detect small sequence variants and many indels. For specific genes (e.g., HNF1B) copy‑number testing may also be required. Importantly, genetic results must be integrated with clinical and biochemical data and interpreted using standard criteria (ACMG guidelines)—for example, co‑segregation in affected family members, absence from population databases, in silico predictions and location in functionally important domains strengthen pathogenic classification. In this case a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant was identified and, together with the clinical picture and family segregation, supported the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY.

Practical summary for clinical decision making
In a young patient with hyperglycaemia, proceed through a structured diagnostic funnel: detailed family history → islet autoantibodies → assessment of endogenous insulin secretion (preferably stimulated C‑peptide) → evaluation of postprandial glucose handling (OGTT) → application of clinical prediction tools as adjunctive support. When these data align toward MODY—especially with autosomal dominant family history and negative autoantibodies—genetic testing with an NGS MODY panel is indicated. In this case each step progressively reduced the likelihood of type 1 or classical T2D and increased the probability of HNF1A‑MODY, culminating in genetic confirmation that then guided precision treatment.
