Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# SGLT2 meets HNF1A: Why glycosuria is a clue — and why SGLT2 inhibitors may backfire

What grabbed my attention in this case was the mismatch: a young man with marked glycosuria and a family history of early‑onset diabetes, but without the autoimmune markers of type 1 diabetes. Genetic testing revealed a novel HNF1A mutation — and the glycosuria wasn’t just a symptom, it was a mechanism. HNF1A regulates renal SGLT2 expression, so loss of function can lower the renal threshold for glucose and cause glycosuria that outpaces the measured plasma glucose. That’s a diagnostic pearl — and it also creates a therapeutic red flag when you consider prescribing pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition.

Case in a paragraph
A young adult with a strong family history of early‑onset diabetes was referred after routine testing found hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) and marked glycosuria. He had negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A), a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (modestly preserved β‑cell function), and an OGTT 2‑hour glucose of 19.8 mmol/L. Genetic testing found a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S). Initial insulin was later stopped after genetic confirmation; treatment was changed to dulaglutide + metformin (and later maintained on metformin + saxagliptin), and HbA1c improved to 6.0% at 6 months.

Why glycosuria can be a clue to HNF1A‑MODY
HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in liver, pancreas and kidney. In the proximal tubule it drives expression of SGLT2 (the major apical Na+/glucose cotransporter). Loss‑of‑function HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression → lower renal threshold for glucose → glycosuria at plasma glucose levels that would not normally cause it. Clinically that looks like disproportionate glucosuria for the measured hyperglycaemia. If you see that, especially in a young patient with an autosomal dominant family history and negative autoantibodies, think MODY — HNF1A top of the list.

Why adding an SGLT2 inhibitor can be hazardous in HNF1A‑MODY
The mechanisms that make HNF1A patients glucosuric are the same ones that theoretically (and anecdotally) increase harm if you pharmacologically block SGLT2 on top of that baseline defect:

- Pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition supercharges glycosuria on an already reduced renal threshold → greater osmotic diuresis and intravascular volume depletion. Cases and reports suggest risk of severe dehydration.
- Reduced insulin reserve (progressive β‑cell failure in HNF1A) plus increased glycosuria can create a milieu for ketogenesis; SGLT2 inhibitors are well known to increase euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (euDKA) risk. In HNF1A patients who already have compromised insulin secretion, that risk may be amplified.
- Hypovolemia from osmotic diuresis can cause hypotension, syncope or renal hypoperfusion — especially in younger patients who are not volume‑replete or during intercurrent illness.
- Genital mycotic infections and other SGLT2 adverse effects remain possible and can be more bothersome if glycosuria is already pronounced.

The case report explicitly warns about dehydration and DKA risk from “high inhibition of residual SGLT‑2 activity” in HNF1A‑MODY. That’s not theoretical — it’s a plausible clinical consequence of combining an intrinsic reduction in SGLT2 with pharmacologic inhibition.

How I think about SGLT2 inhibitors in a patient with suspected/confirmed HNF1A‑MODY
- If MODY is suspected (young onset, autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, disproportionate glycosuria) prioritize genetic testing. Therapy can change after confirmation (as in this case where insulin was stopped and GLP‑1 RA instituted).
- Avoid reflex starting of SGLT2 inhibitors in newly diagnosed young patients with suspected HNF1A‑MODY — there’s potential for harm before you even know the genotype.
- If HNF1A‑MODY is confirmed, reserve SGLT2 inhibitors for uncommon situations where their proven cardiorenal benefits outweigh risks (for example, older patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or advanced CKD where SGLT2 benefit is compelling). Even then, use extreme caution, ensure shared decision‑making, and implement mitigation strategies (ketone surveillance, volume counselling, sick‑day plans).
- Prefer alternative agents with a stronger evidence base and better safety profile in this population: low‑dose sulfonylureas historically, and increasingly GLP‑1 receptor agonists (the case patient did well on dulaglutide), plus metformin when indicated. These strategies address insulin secretion or insulin sensitivity without amplifying osmotic diuresis.

Practical counseling and mitigation if an SGLT2 inhibitor is used
If, after informed consent and careful weighing of risks/benefits, an SGLT2 inhibitor is started in a patient with confirmed HNF1A‑MODY, make these explicit to the patient:

- Expect more urinary glucose and possibly larger urine volumes — maintain hydration.
- Check ketones (blood beta‑hydroxybutyrate preferred) if unwell, nauseated, vomiting, reduced oral intake, or if blood glucose is unexpectedly low. Stop the drug and seek urgent review for any positive ketone test or symptoms of DKA.
- Suspend the SGLT2 inhibitor during acute illness, before surgery, or when you can’t maintain fluids/food.
- Be cautious with alcohol or prolonged fasting.
- Monitor blood pressure and renal function after initiation and during episodes of intercurrent illness.
- Watch for genital infections and treat early.

Alternatives and the precision‑medicine opportunity
This case neatly illustrates precision medicine in diabetes. Genetic diagnosis allowed stopping insulin and shifting to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin, with excellent glycaemic response (HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% at 6 months). Historically sulfonylureas are the canonical first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY (they enhance insulin secretion), but GLP‑1 RAs are attractive as they improve glycaemia with lower hypoglycaemia risk and may better preserve β‑cell function. Metformin remains reasonable for insulin resistance/metabolic syndrome features.

Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- Disproportionate glycosuria in a young patient — especially with early‑onset family history and negative autoantibodies — should prompt consideration of HNF1A‑MODY.
- HNF1A loss‑of‑function reduces renal SGLT2 expression → lowers the renal glucose threshold → glycosuria that may outpace plasma glucose.
- Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor on top of HNF1A‑related SGLT2 reduction can markedly increase osmotic diuresis, dehydration risk, and the possibility of euglycaemic DKA; avoid routine use in newly diagnosed or confirmed HNF1A‑MODY patients.
- If SGLT2 inhibitors are considered for cardiorenal indications, use careful shared decision‑making, ketone monitoring, sick‑day rules, and close follow‑up.
- Genetic testing matters — it changes therapy: low‑dose sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 receptor agonists and metformin are reasonable alternatives that avoid amplifying osmotic diuresis.

Bottom line: glycosuria is not just a lab curiosity in young patients — it can be the key to a monogenic diagnosis. And once you recognize HNF1A‑MODY, rethink SGLT2 inhibitors: the very transporter they target is already downregulated, and blocking the remainder pharmacologically may do more harm than good.
