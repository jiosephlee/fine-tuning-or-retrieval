Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# MODY Red Flags: When Early-Onset Hyperglycaemia Isn’t Type 1 or Type 2

What makes this case worth your attention? A young adult with very high HbA1c (10.2%) and marked post‑load hyperglycaemia was almost boxed into a type 2 pathway (insulin + metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor) — until family history, physiology and genetics flipped management. Genetic testing identified a novel HNF1A variant (p.N270S), and after switching to GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin the patient’s HbA1c fell to 6.0% within months and insulin was stopped. This is a neat illustration of how looking beyond the obvious can produce rapid, durable benefit for patients and families.

## Why HNF1A‑MODY should be on your radar in young adults

MODY isn’t common, but it’s underdiagnosed. HNF1A mutations are among the most frequent MODY causes and have direct therapeutic implications. HNF1A‑MODY patients classically have:

- Autosomal dominant, early‑onset diabetes across generations.
- Prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia.
- Glycosuria (lower renal glucose threshold due to reduced SGLT2 expression).
- Negative islet autoantibodies and preserved—but progressively failing—β‑cell function.

This case ticks those boxes: strong family history (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in late 20s, younger brother at 20), negative GADA and IA‑2 antibodies, a 2‑hour OGTT of 19.8 mmol/L, glycosuria on screening, and a stimulated C‑peptide that supports residual endogenous insulin secretion (0.83 nmol/L at 6 min; >0.60 nmol/L often cited as preserved). The novel HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) mapped to the DNA‑binding domain and met ACMG evidence to be reported as pathogenic/likely‑pathogenic—clinically confirming MODY.

## Common traps — why MODY gets misclassified

- Rising obesity and metabolic syndrome blur the phenotype. This patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2 and raised triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L), which might steer clinicians toward T2D. BMI or features of metabolic syndrome do not exclude MODY.
- Early insulin dependence or very high HbA1c does not automatically mean type 1. Absence of DKA and negative autoantibodies should prompt reconsideration.
- Family history can be missed or misattributed (many families have type 2 diabetes too). In families with early‑onset diabetes across generations, the pretest suspicion should be high.
- Overreliance on a single lab: a single normal C‑peptide early on doesn’t rule out evolving type 1 or confirm MODY — stimulated testing (glucagon or mixed‑meal) is more informative.

## Practical bedside cues that should trigger MODY suspicion (guided by this case)

- Diabetes onset at a young age or detection on routine screening in a young adult with a family pattern of early diabetes across successive generations.
- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (OGTT 2‑hour glucose markedly elevated; here 19.8 mmol/L).
- Glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose, suggesting a lower renal threshold.
- Negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8 when available).
- Evidence of residual β‑cell function (stimulated C‑peptide > ~0.6 nmol/L), despite hyperglycaemia.
- No DKA at presentation.
- Atypical features for type 2 disease: younger age at onset, less convincing insulin resistance signs (no acanthosis), or unusual family aggregation.

## Why getting the diagnosis right changes treatment

- Sulfonylureas have historically been first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY because they potentiate insulin secretion. But GLP‑1 receptor agonists are emerging as effective and may carry less hypoglycaemia risk and offer weight benefits. In this case, switching from basal insulin to weekly dulaglutide plus metformin led to a rapid HbA1c fall (10.2% → 6.0% at 6 months), insulin cessation, and stable maintenance on metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor thereafter.
- SGLT2 inhibitors warrant caution. HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression—patients often have low renal thresholds and significant glycosuria already. Reports of dehydration and euglycaemic ketoacidosis caution against routine SGLT2 use in this group without careful consideration.
- Genetic diagnosis enables cascade testing, anticipatory monitoring, tailored therapy, and reproductive/family counselling.

## Tips for the emergency/clinic physician evaluating young hyperglycaemia

- Ask about family history of diabetes and age at diagnosis in relatives — it matters.
- Check islet autoantibodies if presentation is atypical for classic type 1 (no DKA, some preserved insulin secretion) — negative results increase MODY pretest probability.
- Consider a stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal) to assess endogenous insulin reserve.
- If MODY is suspected, use resources (Exeter MODY calculator as a guide, mindful of population differences) and refer for genetic testing (NGS panels are increasingly available). In this case the Exeter calculator estimated a 62.4% probability.
- Don’t delay genetics if treatment implications are likely — a confirmed diagnosis can allow removal of insulin and institution of oral/GLP‑1 therapies that may be safer/more appropriate.

## A few caveats and nuances

- Some MODY patients can have positive autoantibodies; autoantibody positivity does not absolutely exclude a monogenic cause. Interpret antibody results in the full clinical context.
- Not all sequence variants are straightforward. This case’s variant was novel but had multiple supporting lines of evidence (location in DNA‑binding domain, segregation with disease in family, high in‑silico score) to meet ACMG criteria. Use genetics input when available.
- Autonomic testing in this patient showed reduced sudomotor function — reminding us that complications or atypical features can appear early; continue appropriate surveillance after diagnosis.

## Key take‑home points

- Think MODY when you see young-onset diabetes with autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia, and glycosuria without DKA.
- Rising BMI and some features of metabolic syndrome do not rule out MODY.
- Genetic confirmation changes management: sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists can replace insulin in many HNF1A‑MODY patients; be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors.
- Use clinical tools (MODY probability calculators) and a low threshold for genetic referral in suspicious cases — early diagnosis improves care for the patient and family.

If you see a young patient with unexplained hyperglycaemia and a family history of early diabetes, pause before locking in “type 1” or “type 2.” A short diagnostic detour (antibodies, stimulated C‑peptide, consider OGTT, and genetic referral) can be a decisive step toward precision therapy.
