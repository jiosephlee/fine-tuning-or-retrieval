Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with non-ketotic hyperglycaemia, negative GAD/IA-2/ZnT8 antibodies, glycosuria, a family history of early-onset diabetes across generations, and preserved stimulated C-peptide, what specific clinical and laboratory features should prompt genetic testing for MODY and help distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes?

Short answer
Suspect MODY — and specifically HNF1A‑MODY — when a young adult with hyperglycaemia has an autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes, non‑ketotic disease, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia (often with disproportionate glycosuria), and little or no evidence of insulin resistance. These features should prompt molecular genetic testing (targeted MODY/monogenic diabetes panel with HNF1A included). Genetic confirmation changes management (oral agents — classically low‑dose sulfonylureas; GLP‑1 RA or metformin alternatives) and triggers cascade testing in relatives.

Clinical reasoning and key discriminating features (with case correlations)
- Age and inheritance pattern: diabetes diagnosed in adolescence/young adulthood with clear multigenerational transmission (affected maternal grandmother, mother and brother in this case) strongly favors MODY over typical type 1 or type 2 diabetes.
- Autoimmunity: repeatedly negative anti‑GAD, IA‑2 and ZnT8 antibodies argues against classic autoimmune type 1 diabetes. The case had negative GADA and IA‑2.
- Endogenous insulin secretion: preserved stimulated C‑peptide supports non‑type 1 disease. In this case the glucagon stimulation test rose from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L; the authors cite a stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L as indicating preserved secretion.
- Lack of ketotic presentation: absence of diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation (non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia) points away from fulminant type 1.
- Glycosuria disproportionate to glycaemia: glycosuria despite varying glucose levels is a characteristic of HNF1A‑MODY because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the proximal tubule; the case demonstrates glycosuria on health exam and the report discusses reduced renal threshold for glucose.
- Pattern of hyperglycaemia: marked postprandial hyperglycaemia with elevated 2‑hour OGTT (case: 2‑hr glucose 19.8 mmol/L) is typical of early HNF1A disease when fasting hyperglycaemia may be less prominent.
- Low insulin‑resistance phenotype: absence of acanthosis nigricans, normal/near‑normal BMI (case BMI 24.6 kg/m2) and no strong features of metabolic syndrome support MODY over typical T2D (though rising obesity can blur this distinction).
- Clinical course and treatment response: sulfonylurea sensitivity or marked improvement on oral agents rather than high early insulin requirement is suggestive. In the case, insulin was stopped after genetic confirmation and the patient attained excellent control on GLP‑1 RA and metformin.

Laboratory features that should trigger genetic testing
- Negative diabetes autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8).
- Demonstrable endogenous insulin secretion: stimulated C‑peptide above commonly used thresholds (the report uses >0.60 nmol/L as supportive).
- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (e.g., 2‑hr OGTT ≥11.1 mmol/L) with glycosuria.
- Family history consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance (multiple successive affected generations, early onset).
- Early onset (diagnosis in adolescence or young adulthood) or atypical clinical course for type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

How to distinguish HNF1A‑MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes (practical comparisons)
- vs type 1:
  - HNF1A‑MODY: autoantibody negative, preserved stimulated C‑peptide at presentation, non‑ketotic course, progressive β‑cell failure over years.
  - Type 1: typically autoantibody positive and rapid loss of C‑peptide; DKA more likely at presentation.
- vs type 2:
  - HNF1A‑MODY: strong autosomal dominant family history, younger age at onset, minimal insulin resistance signs, prominent postprandial glycaemic excursions and glycosuria from low renal threshold.
  - Type 2: associated with obesity, acanthosis, metabolic syndrome and polygenic risk; family history is common but not classically multigenerational early‑onset pattern.

Diagnostic standard and practical steps
- When the clinical picture above is present, obtain: diabetes autoantibodies; fasting and stimulated C‑peptide (eg, glucagon stimulation); OGTT if needed; careful family pedigree.
- Use a validated MODY probability tool as an adjunct (recognize limitations in non‑European populations).
- Definitive diagnosis requires molecular testing (targeted MODY gene panel or NGS); HNF1A is the commonest MODY gene and should be included. Interpret variants per ACMG criteria and perform cascade testing in relatives if pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant is found (as in this case: novel HNF1A p.N270S variant classified pathogenic/likely pathogenic).
- Genetic diagnosis impacts therapy (consider sulfonylurea first‑line for HNF1A; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an effective alternative and may be safer than sulfonylureas in some patients; avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors without caution because glycosuria is intrinsic and DKA risk may be higher).

Bottom line
In any young adult with non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia with disproportionate glycosuria, and a multigenerational history of early‑onset diabetes, pursue genetic testing for MODY (including HNF1A). Confirmation guides therapy (often oral agents rather than insulin), enables family cascade testing, and prevents misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes — exactly as illustrated by the patient with the novel HNF1A variant in this report.
