Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Management] Given the autosomal dominant inheritance of HNF1A-MODY, what are the key points for family counseling, including recurrence risk to offspring, who should undergo cascade genetic testing, and how results would alter screening and treatment in relatives?

Bottom line
- HNF1A‑MODY is inherited in an autosomal‑dominant manner: each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of inheriting the pathogenic HNF1A variant. Penetrance is age‑dependent and variable, so genotype does not always equal immediate symptomatic disease.
- Offer genetic cascade testing to first‑degree relatives (parents, siblings, children) of the proband and to other relatives with early‑onset diabetes; testing should begin with a targeted assay for the known family variant.
- A positive test changes management: confirm diagnosis, prompt MODY‑specific treatment (usually oral agents rather than automatic lifelong insulin), directed surveillance for diabetic complications from the time of diagnosis, and informed reproductive planning. A negative test obviates the need for MODY‑directed surveillance and returns the person to population‑based diabetes screening.

Clinical reasoning and counseling points (how to explain this to the family)

1) Recurrence risk
- Autosomal dominant transmission → 50% chance that any biological child of the proband will inherit the same HNF1A variant.
- Because expressivity and age of onset vary, an affected genotype may present in adolescence, young adulthood, or later; discuss that an unaffected child (negative genotype) still has background population risk for type 2 diabetes.

2) Who to test first (practical cascade strategy)
- Start with first‑degree relatives (mother/father, siblings, children) — in this case the maternal line already has multiple early‑onset cases (grandmother, mother, brother), so test the mother and brother if not already genotyped and then asymptomatic children.
- Prioritize relatives with early‑onset hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, or those misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes.
- Use a targeted test for the familial variant (cheaper and faster than full panels). If a relative has a high clinical suspicion but is negative for the familial variant, consider broader sequencing panels.
- For children: testing is appropriate because the diagnosis often appears in adolescence and because management differs early on; do this after pre‑test counseling about implications and consent/assent per local practice.

3) How test results change screening and treatment
- Positive for the familial HNF1A pathogenic variant:
  - Confirms MODY diagnosis. In the reported case the genetic result supported stopping insulin and moving to GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin with marked HbA1c improvement (10.2% → 6.0%), illustrating how genotype directed therapy.
  - First‑line therapy for many adults with HNF1A‑MODY is low‑dose sulfonylurea; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an effective alternative and may be preferred in patients at risk for hypoglycaemia or with declining β‑cell function (as in this case). Insulin can often be de‑escalated or avoided when endogenous insulin is adequate.
  - Avoid presumptive use of therapies that may pose specific risks without benefit: HNF1A mutations lower SGLT2 expression and renal glucose threshold, so SGLT2 inhibitors can increase glycosuria and—per case discussion—may increase risk of dehydration or ketoacidosis; weigh risks carefully.
  - Institute diabetes surveillance from time of diagnosis (glycaemic monitoring/HbA1c at appropriate intervals, annual retinal screening and urine albumin per diabetes guidelines) rather than waiting for symptom development.
  - Offer genetic counseling about family planning (50% transmission risk; options such as prenatal testing or preimplantation genetic diagnosis may be discussed if desired).

- Negative for the familial variant:
  - The relative does not carry that familial MODY mutation and does not require MODY‑specific therapy or intensified MODY surveillance.
  - Continue standard population or risk‑based diabetes screening (lifestyle counseling, periodic glucose/HbA1c), because a negative result does not eliminate lifetime risk of common type 2 diabetes.

4) Dealing with uncertain results and segregation
- If a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) is found, interpret in the context of family segregation and phenotype. Segregation with multiple affected relatives, as in this report, increases confidence in pathogenicity; involve a genetics professional for interpretation.
- In this case the novel p.N270S variant segregated with early‑onset diabetes in the maternal lineage and met ACMG criteria for pathogenic/likely pathogenic classification, which strengthened the decision to change therapy.

5) Practical points to communicate
- Arrange formal genetic counseling before and after testing to discuss implications (medical, psychological, insurance/ employment where relevant).
- Test symptomatic relatives first to confirm segregation; then offer testing to asymptomatic at‑risk relatives.
- Document the familial variant in the medical record and provide relatives a copy of results to facilitate testing.

Tie back to the case
- This patient’s family history (grandmother, mother, brother all early‑onset) and the confirmed heterozygous HNF1A variant illustrate autosomal‑dominant transmission and the value of targeted cascade testing. The genetic diagnosis permitted de‑escalation from insulin and selection of GLP‑1 agonist + metformin with excellent glycaemic response—exactly the type of precision change to communicate to relatives considering testing.
