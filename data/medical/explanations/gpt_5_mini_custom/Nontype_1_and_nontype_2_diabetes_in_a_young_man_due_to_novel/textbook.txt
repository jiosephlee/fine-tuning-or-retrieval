Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## From Screening Abnormality to Suspicion of MODY: Case Presentation and Key Clues

This patient was first identified because of routine screening that revealed hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. Rather than treating that finding in isolation, the clinical approach should integrate age, family history, biochemical pattern and physical examination to judge whether the picture fits type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes (T2D) or a monogenic cause such as MODY. In this case several features pointed away from straightforward T1D or T2D and raised suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY.

The demographics and family pedigree are the strongest early clue. The patient is a young adult with multiple first‑degree and second‑degree relatives who developed diabetes in their 20s–30s (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in late 20s, younger brother at 20). That pattern—diabetes in successive generations with early onset—is characteristic of an autosomal dominant inheritance and should prompt consideration of MODY. By contrast, T1D typically presents with acute autoimmune destruction without this clear multigenerational pattern, and T2D family histories are often broader and associated with obesity and older age of onset.

The biochemical phenotype at presentation supports a β‑cell secretory defect with prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia. Laboratory values included an HbA1c of 10.2% and fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L with a 2‑hour OGTT glucose of 19.8 mmol/L, documenting marked hyperglycaemia and impaired postprandial glucose handling. HNF1A‑MODY classically produces progressive β‑cell dysfunction with disproportionate postprandial rises on OGTT, so the OGTT result fits this diagnosis. Importantly there were no features of diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation, which makes fulminant autoimmune T1D less likely.

Physical exam and metabolic profile give mixed signals that can confuse classification. The patient’s BMI was 24.6 kg/m2 and there was no acanthosis nigricans—findings that argue against insulin‑resistant T2D. However, the lipid profile (triglycerides 4.31 mmol/L, HDL 1.01 mmol/L) suggested dyslipidaemia that could be interpreted as metabolic syndrome and bias clinicians toward a T2D diagnosis. As obesity becomes more common, reliance on BMI or isolated lipid abnormalities will increasingly mislead; the absence of clinical stigmata of insulin resistance (acanthosis, marked central obesity) should prompt careful consideration of alternative diagnoses when other clues are present.

Glycosuria is a particularly helpful sign in HNF1A‑MODY. HNF1A regulates expression of SGLT2 in the proximal tubule; pathogenic HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression and lower the renal threshold for glucose, producing glycosuria that can be disproportionate to blood glucose levels. In practice, finding glycosuria in a young patient—especially when ketones or DKA are absent—should raise the possibility of HNF1A‑MODY. In this case glycosuria was detected on screening despite the absence of DKA, consistent with reduced renal glucose reabsorption mediated by HNF1A dysfunction.

Early symptoms (polydipsia, polyphagia, weight loss) are common to T1D, T2D and MODY and therefore do not reliably distinguish them. The patient’s report of increased thirst, appetite and weight loss illustrates this overlap and explains why initial classification is often difficult. Because symptom overlap is common, the diagnostic process must rely on objective investigations (autoantibodies, stimulated C‑peptide, OGTT pattern) and family history rather than symptoms alone.

Putting the pieces together: red flags for MODY in this case were young age at onset, multigenerational early‑onset diabetes suggesting dominant inheritance, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but inadequate stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L), pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia on OGTT, and renal glycosuria without DKA. Common pitfalls that delay the MODY diagnosis include anchoring on initial hyperglycaemia and treating empirically for T1D or T2D, over‑weight or dyslipidaemia biasing toward T2D, and low clinical awareness of monogenic diabetes. When several of the red flags above are present, the appropriate next steps are targeted testing (autoantibodies and assessment of endogenous insulin secretion) and timely genetic testing for MODY, because confirming a monogenic aetiology can materially change management and prognosis.

## Building the Differential and Executing the Workup

When a young adult presents with marked hyperglycaemia, glycosuria and a family history of early-onset diabetes, the clinician must move beyond the default labels of “type 1” or “type 2” diabetes and assemble a focused differential that considers monogenic forms (MODY), latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) and secondary causes. In this case the stepwise approach—clinical features, targeted biochemical tests and physiological stimulation testing—served both to lower the pretest probability of some diagnoses and to raise the suspicion for HNF1A-MODY, prompting molecular testing.

Differential diagnosis and how clinical features weigh in
- Type 1 diabetes: classically presents with acute insulin deficiency, ketonemia/ketonuria and positive islet autoantibodies. Rapidly falling C‑peptide and requirement for insulin soon after diagnosis are typical. The absence of diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation, negative islet autoantibodies and a measurable stimulated C‑peptide argued against fulminant autoimmune type 1 disease in this patient, although early type 1 can temporarily have preserved C‑peptide and negative antibodies.
- Type 2 diabetes (T2D): typically associated with obesity, insulin resistance, acanthosis nigricans and features of metabolic syndrome. This patient had a near‑normal BMI (24.6 kg/m2) and no acanthosis, although his triglycerides and HDL suggested some metabolic risk. Because increasing obesity blurs the clinical distinction, metabolic features alone are insufficient to exclude monogenic diabetes.
- LADA (latent autoimmune diabetes of adults): an autoimmune, slower‑progressing form of β‑cell failure that occurs in adults and usually shows one or more positive autoantibodies. Negative antibodies make LADA unlikely in this case.
- MODY and its subtypes: MODY should be considered in young patients with dominant family history of early diabetes, non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia and preserved—but often progressively declining—β‑cell function. HNF1A‑MODY classically shows marked postprandial excursions and glycosuria at relatively low plasma glucose because of a lower renal glucose threshold.
- Secondary diabetes: pancreatic disease, medications, endocrinopathies and genetic syndromes can produce hyperglycaemia; these were not suggested by the history or examination in this patient.

Islet autoantibodies: interpretation and caveats
Measurement of islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A and increasingly ZnT8) is a frontline discriminant between autoimmune and non‑autoimmune diabetes. In this patient GADA and IA‑2A were negative, lowering the likelihood of classic type 1 diabetes and LADA. Important caveats: (1) autoantibodies can be absent early in type 1 disease or in a minority of LADA cases, and (2) low‑titer autoantibody positivity has been reported occasionally in patients with genetically confirmed MODY. Thus antibody results are supportive but not absolute: negative antibodies here decreased the probability of autoimmune diabetes and increased the relative likelihood of a non‑autoimmune cause such as MODY.

C‑peptide assessment: fasting versus stimulated and clinical interpretation
C‑peptide quantifies endogenous insulin secretion and is a practical discriminator of insulin deficiency. A fasting value gives a baseline estimate, but stimulated testing (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal tolerance test) is more sensitive for residual β‑cell function. In this case a glucagon stimulation test produced a rise from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L. Using the commonly applied threshold cited in the report (>0.60 nmol/L as preserved secretion), the stimulated value indicates preserved but inadequate insulin secretion—more consistent with progressive β‑cell dysfunction (as in MODY) than with absolute insulin deficiency characteristic of established type 1 disease. Nuances to remember: early type 1 may still have measurable stimulated C‑peptide, and C‑peptide assays and threshold cutoffs vary between labs, so results should always be interpreted in the clinical context. Here the pattern (measurable stimulated C‑peptide but need for early glycaemic control) fit a non‑autoimmune, progressive insulin secretory defect.

OGTT and postprandial dysglycaemia as supportive evidence
HNF1A‑MODY often produces exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia because of defective glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion. The patient’s 2‑hour glucose of 19.8 mmol/L on a 75 g OGTT (very high postload value) reinforces the presence of marked postprandial intolerance rather than isolated fasting hyperglycaemia. In the context of a young patient with family history and preserved stimulated C‑peptide, a striking OGTT abnormality increases suspicion for HNF1A‑type MODY.

Sudomotor testing: what reduced electrochemical skin conductance indicates and limitations
Electrochemical skin conductance testing (e.g., Sudoscan) probes small‑fiber autonomic function by measuring sweat gland activity. Severely reduced conductance in the hands, as reported here, suggests autonomic small‑fiber dysfunction, which can be an early complication of diabetes. Interpretation caveats: results are influenced by glycaemic status, recent hyperglycaemia, hydration, skin temperature and other small‑fiber neuropathy causes. In a newly diagnosed young patient, reduced sudomotor function may reflect early hyperglycaemia‑related neuropathic change but is not specific for a particular diabetes aetiology; it is a supportive finding of end‑organ effect, not a diagnostic discriminator between MODY and other forms.

Clinical prediction tools: the Exeter MODY calculator—use and limits
Clinical calculators (for example, the Exeter MODY probability calculator) combine age of onset, BMI, family history, antibody status and treatment to estimate the probability of MODY and help select candidates for genetic testing. When the patient’s data were entered, the calculator estimated a ~62% probability of MODY—meaning genetic testing was strongly indicated. Limitations include that derivation cohorts were largely White European; performance is poorer and may overestimate probability in non‑White populations. Therefore scores should not be used in isolation but integrated with the full clinical and biochemical picture.

How each test shifted diagnostic likelihood in this patient
- Strong autosomal dominant family history (multiple first‑degree/close relatives with early‑onset diabetes) increased pretest probability for a monogenic aetiology.
- Absence of acidosis and ketosis at presentation, together with negative islet autoantibodies, decreased the likelihood of classic autoimmune type 1 diabetes.
- A preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) argued against absolute insulin deficiency and was compatible with MODY or early T2D rather than long‑standing type 1.
- A marked 2‑hour OGTT elevation and glycosuria were concordant with HNF1A‑MODY physiology (postprandial insulin secretory defect and reduced renal glucose threshold).
- Metabolic features (elevated triglycerides, lower HDL) introduced some ambiguity by suggesting possible insulin resistance/T2D, but the combination of family history, age at onset and the biochemical pattern still favored MODY over typical T2D.

Indications to proceed to genetic testing and choosing a molecular approach
Given the cumulative evidence—young age at onset, strong multigenerational early‑onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, measurable stimulated C‑peptide and a clinical picture consistent with HNF1A physiology—the team appropriately proceeded to genetic testing. Contemporary practice favours targeted next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panels that include the common MODY genes (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B and others) because NGS permits parallel, cost‑effective analysis of many relevant loci and can detect small sequence variants and many indels. For specific genes (e.g., HNF1B) copy‑number testing may also be required. Importantly, genetic results must be integrated with clinical and biochemical data and interpreted using standard criteria (ACMG guidelines)—for example, co‑segregation in affected family members, absence from population databases, in silico predictions and location in functionally important domains strengthen pathogenic classification. In this case a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant was identified and, together with the clinical picture and family segregation, supported the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY.

Practical summary for clinical decision making
In a young patient with hyperglycaemia, proceed through a structured diagnostic funnel: detailed family history → islet autoantibodies → assessment of endogenous insulin secretion (preferably stimulated C‑peptide) → evaluation of postprandial glucose handling (OGTT) → application of clinical prediction tools as adjunctive support. When these data align toward MODY—especially with autosomal dominant family history and negative autoantibodies—genetic testing with an NGS MODY panel is indicated. In this case each step progressively reduced the likelihood of type 1 or classical T2D and increased the probability of HNF1A‑MODY, culminating in genetic confirmation that then guided precision treatment.

## HNF1A‑MODY: Molecular Basis, Pathophysiology, and Variant Interpretation

HNF1A encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor‑1α, a homeodomain transcription factor on chromosome 12q24 that is highly expressed in pancreatic β‑cells, hepatocytes and renal tubular cells. The HNF1A protein has three functional regions: an N‑terminal dimerization domain that mediates protein–protein interactions, a central DNA‑binding/homeodomain that recognizes target promoters, and a C‑terminal transactivation domain that recruits the transcriptional machinery. Mutations anywhere in the protein can affect transcriptional output, but missense variants in the DNA‑binding domain commonly disrupt target gene regulation and are over‑represented among disease‑causing alleles.

How HNF1A dysfunction produces the clinical phenotype can be traced to loss of regulated transcription in β‑cells and kidney. In pancreatic β‑cells HNF1A positively regulates genes required for glucose sensing and insulin secretion, including GLUT2 (the major facilitative glucose transporter in rodents and an important component of glucose sensing in humans) and enzymes such as pyruvate kinase that support glycolytic flux. Reduced expression of these targets leads to impaired glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion and a progressive decline in β‑cell function. Clinically this appears as nonautoimmune, early‑onset hyperglycaemia with preserved but inadequate endogenous insulin secretion early in disease (illustrated in this case by a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L). In the kidney, HNF1A controls SGLT2 expression; loss of HNF1A activity lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption and produces pronounced glycosuria at blood glucose levels where people without the mutation would not spill glucose. These molecular links explain the combination of early hyperglycaemia, glycosuria and a pattern of progressive β‑cell failure seen in HNF1A‑MODY.

The clinical signature of HNF1A‑MODY is therefore: diabetes diagnosed at a young age (often <25 but sometimes later), absent islet autoantibodies, family history compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance, marked postprandial hyperglycaemia, and glycosuria disproportionate to blood glucose. Sensitivity to insulin secretagogues (classically low‑dose sulfonylureas) is a hallmark because residual β‑cells can be stimulated to secrete insulin; as β‑cell function declines, incretin‑based therapies (GLP‑1 receptor agonists/DPP‑4 inhibitors) or insulin may be required. Importantly, overlap with type 1 or type 2 diabetes is common: early in HNF1A‑MODY the absence of autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide differentiate it from type 1, but rising obesity and metabolic features can blur distinctions from type 2.

Genetic testing workflow and variant interpretation are critical for establishing an HNF1A‑MODY diagnosis and guiding therapy. In practice clinicians select a monogenic diabetes NGS panel that includes HNF1A (and other common MODY genes) when the clinical picture raises suspicion (young age at onset, autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide). Variant curation follows established frameworks (ACMG/AMP): evidence types considered include population frequency (absence or rarity in large control databases supports pathogenicity), segregation with disease in the family (co‑segregation is strong supportive evidence), in silico predictions (computational tools that predict deleteriousness), location within a critical functional domain (mutational hotspots), and functional data when available. No single line of evidence is usually definitive; the ACMG scoring system integrates multiple criteria (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, benign) to reach a classification.

Applying these principles to the case variant p.N270S (c.809A>G) places it within the context of the DNA‑binding domain, a region enriched for pathogenic missense changes. In this patient the variant was absent from public databases (supporting PM2 — rarity in controls), has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), indicating a strong computational prediction of deleterious effect (supporting PP3), and segregates with early‑onset diabetes in multiple affected relatives (supporting PP1). Its location in the DNA‑binding domain provides domain‑specific evidence (PM1). Functional assays were not reported, so PS3 (well‑established functional studies) is not available. Combining these lines of evidence commonly yields a classification in the likely pathogenic/pathogenic range under ACMG rules; the authors applied ACMG criteria and considered the variant disease‑causing in the clinical context. It is worth underscoring that in silico predictions and absence from databases strengthen but do not replace segregation and functional data; reporting novel variants to shared databases (ClinVar/VarSome) and, when possible, obtaining functional confirmation improve confidence over time.

The genetic diagnosis has immediate implications for relatives and counseling. HNF1A‑MODY is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, so first‑degree relatives have an approximate 50% risk of carrying the same variant. Penetrance can be high but age at onset and severity are variable even within families, so asymptomatic carriers may develop diabetes later; conversely, incomplete penetrance and phenocopies (common type 2 diabetes) can complicate pedigree interpretation. Cascade testing of at‑risk relatives is recommended: identified carriers should undergo baseline metabolic assessment and periodic glucose surveillance, and a confirmed negative result can spare unaffected relatives unnecessary long‑term screening and alter pregnancy planning. From a management standpoint, knowing the diagnosis informed therapy in this case — insulin was discontinued after genetic confirmation and the patient was transitioned to GLP‑1 receptor agonist plus metformin with good glycaemic response — and it also raises specific medication considerations (for example, awareness that SGLT2 inhibitors may have exaggerated glycosuric effects and dehydration risk in HNF1A‑deficient patients).

In summary, the molecular biology of HNF1A explains the characteristic combination of early nonautoimmune β‑cell failure and renal glycosuria. Variant interpretation requires integration of domain location, population data, computational predictions, and family segregation; in this case the novel p.N270S change in the DNA‑binding domain, absent from databases and segregating with disease, meets multiple ACMG criteria that support a likely pathogenic interpretation and has direct implications for treatment choice and cascade testing in relatives.

## Precision Therapy in HNF1A-MODY: From Initial Control to Long-term Management

Patients with HNF1A-MODY require a treatment approach guided by the degree of hyperglycaemia at presentation, residual β‑cell function, and the natural history of progressive insulin secretory failure. In this case the patient presented with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L) but had negative islet autoantibodies and a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L—evidence of significant glucose toxicity with partially preserved endogenous insulin secretion. These features illustrate two therapeutic principles: (1) rapidly correcting severe hyperglycaemia to relieve glucotoxicity and preserve β‑cell function, and (2) once the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY is established, shifting to therapies that exploit the pathophysiology of insulin secretory failure while minimising hypoglycaemia and unnecessary insulin exposure.

Initial management of marked hyperglycaemia
When hyperglycaemia is severe (markedly elevated glucose or HbA1c, symptomatic polyuria/polydipsia, weight loss) short‑term insulin is commonly used to achieve prompt glycaemic control and reverse glucotoxicity. In monogenic diabetes this is a temporary measure unless absolute insulin deficiency is present. Practical indications for starting basal insulin include inability to control fasting glucose with oral agents, significant symptoms, or very high A1c that warrants rapid lowering. De‑escalation is appropriate when glycaemia has improved, there is no ketosis, and objective evidence of β‑cell reserve exists—typically a stimulated C‑peptide above thresholds used clinically (in this report a stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L is considered preserved). In the presented patient, basal insulin degludec was started for rapid control and then discontinued after one month once HbA1c improved (to 9.1%), clinical suspicion of HNF1A‑MODY was supported by genetic testing, and endogenous insulin secretion was judged sufficient to attempt non‑insulin therapy. De‑escalation should be gradual, with frequent capillary glucose monitoring (or CGM) during dose reduction to detect fasting and postprandial hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia.

Sulfonylureas as traditional first‑line in HNF1A‑MODY
HNF1A mutations cause progressive β‑cell secretory defects but leave the machinery for stimulus‑secretion coupling intact; consequently, insulin secretagogues—especially sulfonylureas—have long been the classical pharmacotherapy for HNF1A‑MODY. Patients with HNF1A‑MODY are often very sensitive to sulfonylureas, and low starting doses are recommended (for example glimepiride 0.5–1 mg or equivalent gliclazide MR lower than typical T2D starting doses), with cautious titration. Sulfonylureas can produce rapid improvements in fasting and postprandial glucose because they close KATP channels on β‑cells to augment insulin release. The principal downside is hypoglycaemia risk, which can be substantial in this genetically predisposed population; long‑acting agents and high doses should be avoided when possible. Sulfonylureas remain an important option, particularly in younger patients with clear HNF1A pathogenic variants and retained β‑cell function, but choice must balance efficacy against hypoglycaemia and weight gain.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists versus sulfonylureas: evidence and rationale in this case
Recent controlled data comparing incretin‑based therapy and sulfonylureas in HNF1A‑MODY provide useful context. A small randomized, double‑blind, crossover study (as noted in the case discussion) found that liraglutide and glimepiride produced comparable reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose in HNF1A‑MODY, but GLP‑1 receptor agonists had a more favourable hypoglycaemia profile. GLP‑1 RAs additionally offer weight‑lowering or neutral effects, potential cardiovascular benefit, and a lower risk of hypoglycaemia because their insulinotropic action is glucose‑dependent.

In the reported patient, these considerations supported switching from insulin to a weekly GLP‑1 RA (dulaglutide 1.5 mg) after genetic confirmation. Advantages specific to this decision included once‑weekly dosing (improving adherence), effective HbA1c lowering in patients with residual β‑cell function, and a lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas—an important safety consideration given the patient’s young age and lifestyle. The clinical outcome—HbA1c falling from 9.1% at one month to 6.0% at six months after initiating GLP‑1 RA plus metformin—illustrates that GLP‑1 RAs can be a powerful alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A‑MODY, particularly when preservation of β‑cell function and hypoglycaemia avoidance are priorities.

Roles of metformin and DPP‑4 inhibitors as adjuncts
Metformin is not disease‑specific for MODY but remains a useful adjunct when there is evidence of insulin resistance (higher BMI, adverse lipid profile) or to provide modest, weight‑neutral glycaemic lowering and hepatic glucose reduction. In this case metformin was continued throughout as part of combination therapy addressing possible insulin resistance (BMI 24.6 kg/m2 and elevated triglycerides).

DPP‑4 inhibitors (used here initially as saxagliptin) are less potent than GLP‑1 RAs but can be used when GLP‑1 RAs are contraindicated, not tolerated, or when only modest HbA1c lowering is required. Their glucose‑dependent mechanism means low hypoglycaemia risk, but their efficacy depends on residual incretin responsiveness and β‑cell capacity; they are therefore generally adjunctive rather than first‑line agents in HNF1A‑MODY. In this patient a strategy of GLP‑1 RA induction followed by simplification to metformin plus a DPP‑4 inhibitor maintained glycaemic targets, demonstrating step‑down therapy can work when β‑cell function and previous response are favourable.

SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY: mechanistic concerns and caution
HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the proximal tubule, and HNF1A‑MODY is associated with a lower renal threshold for glucose and baseline glycosuria. Theoretical and reported concerns exist about adding SGLT2 inhibitors in this context: augmented glycosuria may provoke dehydration, hypotension or precipitate euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis, and genital infections risk is increased. Given these mechanistic considerations and reported cases of severe dehydration/DKA with SGLT2 inhibition in HNF1A‑MODY, clinicians should be cautious and generally avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with marked baseline glycosuria, low insulin reserve, or risk factors for DKA. If considered (for example, when cardiovascular or renal indications strongly favour them), careful patient selection, education, and close monitoring are essential.

Targets, monitoring, and lifestyle
Glycaemic targets in HNF1A‑MODY are individualized: for most young, otherwise healthy patients seeking durable complication prevention, an HbA1c target <7.0% (and often <6.5% if achievable without hypoglycaemia) is reasonable. Because hypoglycaemia risk differs across agents, monitoring should include regular SMBG or CGM, particularly during therapy transitions (insulin withdrawal, initiation of sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA) to detect hypoglycaemia, fasting/postprandial excursions, and to guide titration. Routine screening for microvascular complications (annual urine albumin, periodic retinal exams) is indicated as in other diabetes types. Lifestyle measures—individualised nutrition counselling, regular physical activity, and weight management—remain foundational; however, when intensifying insulin secretagogues, dietary plans should be paired with education on hypoglycaemia recognition and management.

When to reinstitute insulin and long‑term considerations
Insulin may be reinstated temporally for intercurrent illness, surgery, pregnancy, or permanently if progressive β‑cell failure produces inadequate glycaemic control despite optimal non‑insulin therapy. Regular reassessment of C‑peptide and clinical response helps anticipate this transition. Finally, the case demonstrates the value of genetic diagnosis in guiding therapy: identifying HNF1A‑MODY allowed safe discontinuation of insulin, selection of a GLP‑1 RA to preserve function and reduce hypoglycaemia risk, and later simplification to oral therapy with sustained HbA1c control—an example of precision medicine enabling better‑targeted, individualized long‑term management.

## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Key Clinical Lessons

The clinical course in this patient illustrates how an accurate molecular diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY can rapidly change management and prognosis. At presentation his HbA1c was 10.2% and, after initial insulin plus oral agents, fell to 9.1% within one month. Genetic confirmation of a pathogenic HNF1A variant prompted discontinuation of insulin and initiation of a GLP‑1 receptor agonist while continuing metformin; over six months his HbA1c fell further to 6.0% and has been maintained below 6.5% on a simplified regimen (metformin plus a DPP‑4 inhibitor). These measurable improvements show two important points: (1) HNF1A-MODY patients often retain enough β‑cell function to respond to non‑insulin secretagogues, and (2) a genetic diagnosis can enable safe deprescribing of insulin with good glycaemic outcomes.

Surveillance for diabetes complications was appropriately instituted and remains important. At the time of reporting there was no microalbuminuria and no retinopathy, findings that reduce but do not eliminate short‑term risk. Standard follow‑up should include annual screening for albuminuria (urine albumin‑to‑creatinine ratio) and an annual or baseline retinal examination with subsequent frequency determined by findings. The patient’s severely reduced hand sudomotor function on electrochemical skin conductance suggests early small‑fiber autonomic involvement; this should prompt symptom assessment (orthostatic intolerance, resting tachycardia, gastrointestinal or genitourinary symptoms) and periodic reassessment. Repeat autonomic testing, consideration of cardiovascular autonomic reflex testing if symptoms or signs develop, and symptomatic management when indicated are reasonable steps. Documentation of baseline complication status is valuable because it contextualises future findings as progression versus pre‑existing disease.

Genetic confirmation had direct, practical benefits beyond glycaemic control. It justified stopping insulin, supported the choice of GLP‑1 receptor agonist therapy (and later oral combination therapy) tailored to the underlying pathophysiology, and facilitated targeted patient education about inheritance, prognosis and the need for family screening. Cascade testing of first‑degree relatives is an important downstream action once a pathogenic variant is identified, because it enables earlier diagnosis and avoids inappropriate long‑term insulin therapy in affected relatives. Counseling should include explanation of autosomal dominant transmission, the variable age at onset, and implications for pregnancy and contraception where relevant.

From a systems and cost‑effectiveness perspective, genetic testing yields the greatest clinical and economic value when the result will change management or prompt cascade testing. In practice, testing is most defensible when there is young onset (<25–35 years in many algorithms), a multigenerational pattern of diabetes, negative pancreatic autoantibodies, preserved endogenous insulin secretion (measurable C‑peptide), atypical features for type 2 diabetes (eg, lack of marked insulin resistance), or marked glycosuria out of proportion to hyperglycaemia. Using these selection criteria—or validated probability calculators—improves pretest yield and helps prioritise resources. Clinicians should be mindful that many published tools were derived in European cohorts and may under‑ or overestimate likelihood in other populations; cost‑benefit assessments should therefore account for local prevalence, test costs, and downstream savings from altered therapy and family screening.

For frontline clinicians, a practical approach to suspecting MODY can be framed as a stepwise evaluation. When a young adult presents with non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia, obtain a family history looking for diabetes in multiple consecutive generations and check pancreatic autoantibodies and at least a stimulated or random C‑peptide. If antibodies are absent and C‑peptide is preserved, particularly with a family history or unusual features (pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia, prominent glycosuria, minimal insulin resistance), proceed to genetic testing with a MODY NGS panel. Interpreting results requires integrating genetic classification with clinical context: pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants support gene‑directed therapy and family screening; variants of uncertain significance require segregation studies, functional data or expert consultation before altering therapy; a negative panel does not exclude a monogenic cause if clinical suspicion remains high. Be aware of common pitfalls: reliance on BMI alone (obesity does not rule out MODY), overdependence on calculators developed in different ancestries, assuming antibody positivity completely excludes MODY (rare co‑occurrence is reported), and making irreversible treatment changes (eg, stopping insulin) before confirming the diagnosis in unstable patients.

Looking ahead, improving care for patients like this one depends on broader genomic resources and better predictive tools. Expanding variant catalogs and reclassifying variants across diverse populations will reduce the number of uncertain results. Predictive algorithms and probability calculators need validation and recalibration outside European cohorts to avoid under‑ or over‑testing. Operationally, integrating genomic triggers into electronic health records—such as prompts when a young patient has negative autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide—or streamlining access to targeted NGS panels will shorten the diagnostic pathway. Finally, ongoing collection of outcome data after genotype‑guided treatment changes will strengthen evidence on long‑term safety, cost‑effectiveness and best practices for surveillance.

In sum, this case demonstrates how genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY changed therapy, enabled insulin withdrawal with excellent glycaemic control, and redirected the focus to appropriate complication surveillance and family screening. Clinicians who apply a targeted, evidence‑informed testing strategy can identify patients who will benefit most from precision therapy, while health systems should prioritise tools and policies that expand equitable access to genetic diagnosis.
