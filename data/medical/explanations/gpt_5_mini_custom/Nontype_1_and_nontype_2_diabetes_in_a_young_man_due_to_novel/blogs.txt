Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# MODY Red Flags: When Early-Onset Hyperglycaemia Isn’t Type 1 or Type 2

What makes this case worth your attention? A young adult with very high HbA1c (10.2%) and marked post‑load hyperglycaemia was almost boxed into a type 2 pathway (insulin + metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor) — until family history, physiology and genetics flipped management. Genetic testing identified a novel HNF1A variant (p.N270S), and after switching to GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin the patient’s HbA1c fell to 6.0% within months and insulin was stopped. This is a neat illustration of how looking beyond the obvious can produce rapid, durable benefit for patients and families.

## Why HNF1A‑MODY should be on your radar in young adults

MODY isn’t common, but it’s underdiagnosed. HNF1A mutations are among the most frequent MODY causes and have direct therapeutic implications. HNF1A‑MODY patients classically have:

- Autosomal dominant, early‑onset diabetes across generations.
- Prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia.
- Glycosuria (lower renal glucose threshold due to reduced SGLT2 expression).
- Negative islet autoantibodies and preserved—but progressively failing—β‑cell function.

This case ticks those boxes: strong family history (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in late 20s, younger brother at 20), negative GADA and IA‑2 antibodies, a 2‑hour OGTT of 19.8 mmol/L, glycosuria on screening, and a stimulated C‑peptide that supports residual endogenous insulin secretion (0.83 nmol/L at 6 min; >0.60 nmol/L often cited as preserved). The novel HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) mapped to the DNA‑binding domain and met ACMG evidence to be reported as pathogenic/likely‑pathogenic—clinically confirming MODY.

## Common traps — why MODY gets misclassified

- Rising obesity and metabolic syndrome blur the phenotype. This patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2 and raised triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L), which might steer clinicians toward T2D. BMI or features of metabolic syndrome do not exclude MODY.
- Early insulin dependence or very high HbA1c does not automatically mean type 1. Absence of DKA and negative autoantibodies should prompt reconsideration.
- Family history can be missed or misattributed (many families have type 2 diabetes too). In families with early‑onset diabetes across generations, the pretest suspicion should be high.
- Overreliance on a single lab: a single normal C‑peptide early on doesn’t rule out evolving type 1 or confirm MODY — stimulated testing (glucagon or mixed‑meal) is more informative.

## Practical bedside cues that should trigger MODY suspicion (guided by this case)

- Diabetes onset at a young age or detection on routine screening in a young adult with a family pattern of early diabetes across successive generations.
- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (OGTT 2‑hour glucose markedly elevated; here 19.8 mmol/L).
- Glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose, suggesting a lower renal threshold.
- Negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8 when available).
- Evidence of residual β‑cell function (stimulated C‑peptide > ~0.6 nmol/L), despite hyperglycaemia.
- No DKA at presentation.
- Atypical features for type 2 disease: younger age at onset, less convincing insulin resistance signs (no acanthosis), or unusual family aggregation.

## Why getting the diagnosis right changes treatment

- Sulfonylureas have historically been first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY because they potentiate insulin secretion. But GLP‑1 receptor agonists are emerging as effective and may carry less hypoglycaemia risk and offer weight benefits. In this case, switching from basal insulin to weekly dulaglutide plus metformin led to a rapid HbA1c fall (10.2% → 6.0% at 6 months), insulin cessation, and stable maintenance on metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor thereafter.
- SGLT2 inhibitors warrant caution. HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression—patients often have low renal thresholds and significant glycosuria already. Reports of dehydration and euglycaemic ketoacidosis caution against routine SGLT2 use in this group without careful consideration.
- Genetic diagnosis enables cascade testing, anticipatory monitoring, tailored therapy, and reproductive/family counselling.

## Tips for the emergency/clinic physician evaluating young hyperglycaemia

- Ask about family history of diabetes and age at diagnosis in relatives — it matters.
- Check islet autoantibodies if presentation is atypical for classic type 1 (no DKA, some preserved insulin secretion) — negative results increase MODY pretest probability.
- Consider a stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal) to assess endogenous insulin reserve.
- If MODY is suspected, use resources (Exeter MODY calculator as a guide, mindful of population differences) and refer for genetic testing (NGS panels are increasingly available). In this case the Exeter calculator estimated a 62.4% probability.
- Don’t delay genetics if treatment implications are likely — a confirmed diagnosis can allow removal of insulin and institution of oral/GLP‑1 therapies that may be safer/more appropriate.

## A few caveats and nuances

- Some MODY patients can have positive autoantibodies; autoantibody positivity does not absolutely exclude a monogenic cause. Interpret antibody results in the full clinical context.
- Not all sequence variants are straightforward. This case’s variant was novel but had multiple supporting lines of evidence (location in DNA‑binding domain, segregation with disease in family, high in‑silico score) to meet ACMG criteria. Use genetics input when available.
- Autonomic testing in this patient showed reduced sudomotor function — reminding us that complications or atypical features can appear early; continue appropriate surveillance after diagnosis.

## Key take‑home points

- Think MODY when you see young-onset diabetes with autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia, and glycosuria without DKA.
- Rising BMI and some features of metabolic syndrome do not rule out MODY.
- Genetic confirmation changes management: sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists can replace insulin in many HNF1A‑MODY patients; be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors.
- Use clinical tools (MODY probability calculators) and a low threshold for genetic referral in suspicious cases — early diagnosis improves care for the patient and family.

If you see a young patient with unexplained hyperglycaemia and a family history of early diabetes, pause before locking in “type 1” or “type 2.” A short diagnostic detour (antibodies, stimulated C‑peptide, consider OGTT, and genetic referral) can be a decisive step toward precision therapy.



# Stimulated C‑peptide in the MODY workup: supportive, not definitive

What grabbed my attention in this case was how a single stimulated C‑peptide value (0.83 nmol/L) almost became the clincher for "non‑type 1" — yet the team didn’t stop there. A young adult with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria, a strong multigenerational early‑onset diabetes history, negative islet autoantibodies and a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide that was modestly preserved — all of which culminated in genetic confirmation of a novel HNF1A mutation and a rapid de‑escalation off insulin onto GLP‑1RA + metformin. That outcome illustrates the right way to use C‑peptide in practice: as a helpful piece of the puzzle, never the whole picture.

## Why we check C‑peptide at all
C‑peptide is the byproduct of endogenous insulin production. It’s useful because:
- It distinguishes endogenous insulin secretion from exogenous insulin (no cross‑reactivity with most insulin assays),
- It helps separate absolute insulinopenia (typical of long‑standing type 1 diabetes) from preserved β‑cell function (more consistent with T2D or some forms of MODY),
- It can guide treatment decisions (can we safely stop insulin? will sulfonylureas/GLP‑1RA work?).

But caveat: C‑peptide is an imperfect test. Many factors alter the result — assay, timing, glycaemic state, renal function and the stage of disease.

## Fasting vs stimulated C‑peptide — what each tells you
- Fasting (or random) C‑peptide: easy, outpatient, but less sensitive. A very low fasting C‑peptide supports severe insulin deficiency. A normal fasting value doesn’t exclude evolving type 1 diabetes.
- Stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon or mixed‑meal tolerance test [MMTT]): more sensitive. Glucagon stimulation is practical in clinic (measure baseline, give 1 mg IV glucagon, measure at 6 minutes as in this case). MMTT is more physiologic but takes longer (peaks ~90–120 min).
- Practical thresholds vary by lab/assay and population. In this report the authors use stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L as “preserved.” That’s consistent with many centres where stimulated values >0.6 nmol/L (≈1.8–2 ng/mL) argue against absolute insulin deficiency. The patient’s 0.83 nmol/L (≈2.5 ng/mL) suggested residual β‑cell function — supportive of non‑type 1 aetiology.

Important: always interpret against your lab’s reference ranges and units (nmol/L vs ng/mL). Conversion ~1 nmol/L ≈ 3.02 ng/mL.

## Assay and pre‑analytic caveats you must remember
- Different immunoassays are not interchangeable. Check the method and local cutoffs.
- Hyperglycaemia can transiently increase C‑peptide; conversely, prolonged hyperglycaemia and glucotoxicity can blunt β‑cell responsiveness.
- Renal dysfunction raises C‑peptide (reduced clearance) — can give false reassurance.
- Exogenous insulin doesn’t affect C‑peptide directly, but recent high‑dose insulin can suppress endogenous secretion.
- Cross‑reactivity: some assays detect proinsulin fragments differently — know your lab.
- Urine C‑peptide/creatinine ratio (UCPCR) is a convenient outpatient alternative; published thresholds exist but are lab‑dependent.

Bottom line: the number alone is only as good as the assay and the context.

## Timing matters — when a “normal” C‑peptide can be misleading
Early in autoimmune (type 1) diabetes there is often a “honeymoon” window with preserved C‑peptide. Testing too soon after symptom onset can give a false sense of security. Conversely, late testing in T1D will show very low C‑peptide.

So:
- If clinical suspicion for autoimmune diabetes is high (classic presentation, DKA, progressive insulin requirement, positive autoantibodies), repeat testing or short‑interval clinical reassessment is warranted.
- If the C‑peptide is borderline and the antibody panel is negative, combine with family history, BMI, age of onset, glycosuria and consider genetics rather than assuming non‑T1 disease.

The reported patient had negative GADA and IA‑2A, a family history of early‑onset diabetes and a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L — a constellation that made a MODY workup reasonable rather than diagnostic.

## False reassurance: don’t let one lab value rule your decision‑making
Some patients with MODY have positive autoantibodies; some with type 1 diabetes are antibody negative early on. A preserved C‑peptide in the first months after presentation does not exclude type 1 disease. Practice tip: if a patient is antibody‑positive or has a clinical course typical for T1D, manage as T1D until proven otherwise. If antibody‑negative with preserved C‑peptide and strong family history, pursue genetic testing.

## Who should you consider for MODY genetic testing?
Think MODY (and consider testing) when:
- Diabetes onset <25 years in a non‑obese individual OR strong autosomal dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes across generations,
- Negative islet autoantibodies,
- Preserved C‑peptide inconsistent with insulin‑dependence,
- Absence of features of insulin resistance despite hyperglycaemia (but be aware obesity blurs the line),
- Atypical course for T1D or T2D (e.g., early sulfonylurea sensitivity, glycosuria disproportionate to glucose levels).

Genetic testing is definitive — and directly impacts therapy (sulfonylureas for many HNF1A cases, avoidance of unnecessary lifelong insulin, risk counseling). In this case the combination of family pattern + negative antibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide prompted NGS and found a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant.

## The Exeter MODY calculator — useful but not universal
The Exeter MODY calculator is a validated clinical tool to estimate probability of MODY and can help triage testing. But know its limits:
- It was derived largely from White European cohorts and may miscalibrate in other ethnic groups (it can over‑ or under‑estimate probability).
- It’s best used as one input — alongside C‑peptide, antibody status, family history and clinical judgment.
- A “low” Exeter score shouldn’t close the door if compelling family history or other red flags are present — and a “high” score shouldn’t replace antibody and C‑peptide assessment.

In this case the Exeter tool returned ~62% probability — helpful but not the final arbiter.

## Putting it together — the practical approach
- Use fasting and/or stimulated C‑peptide as supportive data, not definitive proof.
- Always interpret against the assay and the clinical timeline. If in early disease, repeat testing or use serial clinical follow‑up.
- Combine antibody testing, C‑peptide, phenotype and family history to decide on genetic testing.
- When genetic testing is positive for HNF1A (or other MODY genes), change management accordingly — as in this case where insulin was stopped and GLP‑1RA/metformin proved effective.

## Clinical pearls / take‑home points
- Stimulated C‑peptide is more sensitive than fasting C‑peptide but remains supportive, not diagnostic, for MODY vs type 1 diabetes.
- Always check assay units and lab‑specific cutoffs; 1 nmol/L ≈ 3.02 ng/mL.
- Timing matters: preserved C‑peptide early after presentation does not exclude evolving autoimmune diabetes.
- Use C‑peptide + antibody testing + family history + phenotype to triage genetic testing — don’t rely on a single result or the Exeter calculator alone, particularly in non‑European populations.
- When clinical suspicion is high, pursue genetic testing — it changes therapy, prognosis and family counselling (as the HNF1A case nicely demonstrates).

If you’re going to remember just one thing from this case: a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L is strong supportive evidence of residual β‑cell function, but it alone should not be the last word. Use it to build a case — then confirm with genetics when the clinical picture fits.



# Treating HNF1A‑MODY in 2026: Sulfonylurea Tradition vs GLP‑1 Pragmatism

What makes this case worth reading? A young man with severe hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%) and glycosuria, negative diabetes autoantibodies, a strong family history of early‑onset diabetes — and a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant that changed management. He started on insulin for rapid control but, after genetic confirmation and evidence of some residual β‑cell function, was successfully de‑intensified to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) plus metformin and ultimately maintained on metformin + a DPP‑4 inhibitor with HbA1c down to 6.0%. This case captures the modern tension in HNF1A‑MODY care: stick with the historical “use low‑dose sulfonylurea” teaching — or pragmatically prefer GLP‑1 RAs for durability, weight and hypoglycaemia profile?

Below I walk through the physiology that matters, the evidence and the practical algorithm I use in clinic when genetics turns the light on.

## Why HNF1A changes everything (brief refresher)
HNF1A mutations produce progressive β‑cell dysfunction rather than autoimmune destruction. Clinically you’ll often see:
- Young onset, autosomal dominant family history
- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria (reduced SGLT2 expression in the kidney)
- Negative islet autoantibodies and measurable C‑peptide for longer than classic type 1

That biology explains treatment responsiveness: sulfonylureas often produce dramatic early responses because the β‑cell can be stimulated, but β‑cell function is progressive and patients may later fail oral secretagogues.

## The therapeutic crossroads: SU tradition vs GLP‑1 pragmatism
Classic teaching: HNF1A‑MODY → low‑dose sulfonylurea. Why? Potent insulin secretagogue effect, oral, inexpensive, and historically driven by case series showing marked glucose reductions.

But in 2026 we have additional considerations:
- Hypoglycaemia risk and weight gain with sulfonylureas — real downsides in young patients who are otherwise healthy.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists (injectable or oral) stimulate glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, reduce appetite/weight and carry a lower hypoglycaemia risk. Small randomized crossover data in HNF1A patients showed liraglutide and glimepiride achieved similar glycaemic control, but GLP‑1 therapy had less hypoglycaemia.
- GLP‑1 RAs have class cardiovascular and renal benefits in selected populations (e.g., dulaglutide and liraglutide have proven CV benefit in at‑risk cohorts). For a young, low‑CV‑risk person this is less decisive, but it matters for older patients or those with established disease.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors are weight‑neutral, oral and lower risk for hypoglycaemia — but generally less potent than GLP‑1 RAs and limited trial data in HNF1A‑MODY.
- Metformin still has a role for insulin resistance components and as backbone therapy unless contraindicated.

So choice isn’t binary: it’s individualized.

## How this played out in the case
- Presentation: HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, OGTT 2‑hr 19.8 mmol/L, symptomatic but no DKA. Autoantibodies negative. Stimulated C‑peptide after glucagon = 0.83 nmol/L (supporting residual insulin secretion).
- Practical emergency step: start insulin (degludec) + metformin + saxagliptin to quickly bring hyperglycaemia under control.
- After genetic confirmation (novel pathogenic HNF1A variant) and demonstration of residual β‑cell function, clinicians discontinued insulin and started dulaglutide + metformin. HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months and regimen was simplified to metformin + saxagliptin for maintenance.

This is a model of safe de‑intensification: use insulin to manage acute severe hyperglycaemia, then step down guided by genotype and physiology.

## Practical decision framework (how I choose therapy for HNF1A‑MODY)

1. Initial assessment
   - Confirm family history, age at onset, negative autoantibodies.
   - Measure stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon or mixed‑meal) to assess β‑cell reserve.
   - Consider urgent insulin if severe hyperglycaemia, catabolism or symptoms (as in this case).

2. Genetics
   - Arrange targeted MODY genetic testing early when suspicion is high. A positive HNF1A result changes long‑term strategy.

3. Pick therapy after genetic confirmation and stabilization
   - If stimulated C‑peptide is low (clear insulin deficiency): continue insulin.
   - If C‑peptide shows meaningful reserve (case used >0.6 nmol/L as supportive): consider switching off long‑term insulin.

4. Drug selection considerations
   - Sulfonylurea (low dose) — good if:
     - Mild–moderate hyperglycaemia (A1c modest)
     - Cost/simplicity paramount
     - Patient accepts hypoglycaemia risk and potential weight gain
     - No strong contraindications (e.g., high-risk occupation)
   - GLP‑1 receptor agonist — prefer when:
     - Hypoglycaemia avoidance important
     - Weight loss or weight neutrality desired
     - Patient prefers lower hypoglycaemia risk and is willing to use injectables (or oral semaglutide)
     - A higher upfront cost is acceptable or covered
   - DPP‑4 inhibitor — consider if:
     - Patient wants oral therapy and lower side‑effect profile
     - Glycaemic targets are modest and GLP‑1 RA not tolerated/acceptable
     - Note: less robust evidence for potency in HNF1A‑MODY
   - Metformin — keep as adjunct when insulin resistance features exist or per general diabetes care
   - SGLT2 inhibitors — use caution:
     - HNF1A mutation reduces renal SGLT2 expression and increases baseline glycosuria; adding an SGLT2 inhibitor may increase dehydration/DKA risk. Avoid unless compelling reason, with close monitoring.

5. Dosing and monitoring
   - Sulfonylureas: start low and titrate cautiously; counsel on hypoglycaemia recognition.
   - GLP‑1 RAs: choose agent based on access, tolerability and patient preference (daily vs weekly; oral vs injectable). Monitor GI side effects and HbA1c response.
   - Reassess β‑cell function and glycaemic control at 3 months; adjust therapy if progressive failure.

## Specific clinical pearls from this case
- Starting insulin for rapid control of severe hyperglycaemia is reasonable even when MODY is suspected; do not delay acute stabilization while awaiting genetic tests.
- Negative autoantibodies plus measurable stimulated C‑peptide should prompt MODY evaluation in young patients with family history.
- Genetic confirmation (even of a novel HNF1A variant) enables safe de‑intensification and more tailored long‑term therapy.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a pragmatic alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A‑MODY when hypoglycaemia risk, weight or preference are considerations — supported by small randomized crossover data showing similar glycaemic efficacy but less hypoglycaemia with GLP‑1 therapy.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY because of baseline reduced SGLT2 expression and potential for excessive glycosuria/dehydration and DKA.

## Take‑home messages
- Genetic testing matters: confirming HNF1A‑MODY can and will change therapy.
- In severe hyperglycaemia, stabilize with insulin if needed; then reassess and de‑intensify when genotype and β‑cell reserve allow.
- Sulfonylureas remain an effective, low‑cost option for many HNF1A patients, but carry hypoglycaemia and weight penalties.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a compelling, pragmatic alternative — lower hypoglycaemia risk, weight benefit and good efficacy; consider as first‑line after insulin de‑intensification when cost/route are acceptable.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors can be used as adjuncts for oral maintenance but are generally less potent than GLP‑1 RAs.
- Avoid reflexive use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A without careful thought.

This case exemplifies precision medicine in everyday endocrinology: genotype plus physiology (C‑peptide) yields individualized, safer, and often simpler care.



# SGLT2 meets HNF1A: Why glycosuria is a clue — and why SGLT2 inhibitors may backfire

What grabbed my attention in this case was the mismatch: a young man with marked glycosuria and a family history of early‑onset diabetes, but without the autoimmune markers of type 1 diabetes. Genetic testing revealed a novel HNF1A mutation — and the glycosuria wasn’t just a symptom, it was a mechanism. HNF1A regulates renal SGLT2 expression, so loss of function can lower the renal threshold for glucose and cause glycosuria that outpaces the measured plasma glucose. That’s a diagnostic pearl — and it also creates a therapeutic red flag when you consider prescribing pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition.

Case in a paragraph
A young adult with a strong family history of early‑onset diabetes was referred after routine testing found hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) and marked glycosuria. He had negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A), a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (modestly preserved β‑cell function), and an OGTT 2‑hour glucose of 19.8 mmol/L. Genetic testing found a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S). Initial insulin was later stopped after genetic confirmation; treatment was changed to dulaglutide + metformin (and later maintained on metformin + saxagliptin), and HbA1c improved to 6.0% at 6 months.

Why glycosuria can be a clue to HNF1A‑MODY
HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in liver, pancreas and kidney. In the proximal tubule it drives expression of SGLT2 (the major apical Na+/glucose cotransporter). Loss‑of‑function HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression → lower renal threshold for glucose → glycosuria at plasma glucose levels that would not normally cause it. Clinically that looks like disproportionate glucosuria for the measured hyperglycaemia. If you see that, especially in a young patient with an autosomal dominant family history and negative autoantibodies, think MODY — HNF1A top of the list.

Why adding an SGLT2 inhibitor can be hazardous in HNF1A‑MODY
The mechanisms that make HNF1A patients glucosuric are the same ones that theoretically (and anecdotally) increase harm if you pharmacologically block SGLT2 on top of that baseline defect:

- Pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition supercharges glycosuria on an already reduced renal threshold → greater osmotic diuresis and intravascular volume depletion. Cases and reports suggest risk of severe dehydration.
- Reduced insulin reserve (progressive β‑cell failure in HNF1A) plus increased glycosuria can create a milieu for ketogenesis; SGLT2 inhibitors are well known to increase euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (euDKA) risk. In HNF1A patients who already have compromised insulin secretion, that risk may be amplified.
- Hypovolemia from osmotic diuresis can cause hypotension, syncope or renal hypoperfusion — especially in younger patients who are not volume‑replete or during intercurrent illness.
- Genital mycotic infections and other SGLT2 adverse effects remain possible and can be more bothersome if glycosuria is already pronounced.

The case report explicitly warns about dehydration and DKA risk from “high inhibition of residual SGLT‑2 activity” in HNF1A‑MODY. That’s not theoretical — it’s a plausible clinical consequence of combining an intrinsic reduction in SGLT2 with pharmacologic inhibition.

How I think about SGLT2 inhibitors in a patient with suspected/confirmed HNF1A‑MODY
- If MODY is suspected (young onset, autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, disproportionate glycosuria) prioritize genetic testing. Therapy can change after confirmation (as in this case where insulin was stopped and GLP‑1 RA instituted).
- Avoid reflex starting of SGLT2 inhibitors in newly diagnosed young patients with suspected HNF1A‑MODY — there’s potential for harm before you even know the genotype.
- If HNF1A‑MODY is confirmed, reserve SGLT2 inhibitors for uncommon situations where their proven cardiorenal benefits outweigh risks (for example, older patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or advanced CKD where SGLT2 benefit is compelling). Even then, use extreme caution, ensure shared decision‑making, and implement mitigation strategies (ketone surveillance, volume counselling, sick‑day plans).
- Prefer alternative agents with a stronger evidence base and better safety profile in this population: low‑dose sulfonylureas historically, and increasingly GLP‑1 receptor agonists (the case patient did well on dulaglutide), plus metformin when indicated. These strategies address insulin secretion or insulin sensitivity without amplifying osmotic diuresis.

Practical counseling and mitigation if an SGLT2 inhibitor is used
If, after informed consent and careful weighing of risks/benefits, an SGLT2 inhibitor is started in a patient with confirmed HNF1A‑MODY, make these explicit to the patient:

- Expect more urinary glucose and possibly larger urine volumes — maintain hydration.
- Check ketones (blood beta‑hydroxybutyrate preferred) if unwell, nauseated, vomiting, reduced oral intake, or if blood glucose is unexpectedly low. Stop the drug and seek urgent review for any positive ketone test or symptoms of DKA.
- Suspend the SGLT2 inhibitor during acute illness, before surgery, or when you can’t maintain fluids/food.
- Be cautious with alcohol or prolonged fasting.
- Monitor blood pressure and renal function after initiation and during episodes of intercurrent illness.
- Watch for genital infections and treat early.

Alternatives and the precision‑medicine opportunity
This case neatly illustrates precision medicine in diabetes. Genetic diagnosis allowed stopping insulin and shifting to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin, with excellent glycaemic response (HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% at 6 months). Historically sulfonylureas are the canonical first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY (they enhance insulin secretion), but GLP‑1 RAs are attractive as they improve glycaemia with lower hypoglycaemia risk and may better preserve β‑cell function. Metformin remains reasonable for insulin resistance/metabolic syndrome features.

Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- Disproportionate glycosuria in a young patient — especially with early‑onset family history and negative autoantibodies — should prompt consideration of HNF1A‑MODY.
- HNF1A loss‑of‑function reduces renal SGLT2 expression → lowers the renal glucose threshold → glycosuria that may outpace plasma glucose.
- Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor on top of HNF1A‑related SGLT2 reduction can markedly increase osmotic diuresis, dehydration risk, and the possibility of euglycaemic DKA; avoid routine use in newly diagnosed or confirmed HNF1A‑MODY patients.
- If SGLT2 inhibitors are considered for cardiorenal indications, use careful shared decision‑making, ketone monitoring, sick‑day rules, and close follow‑up.
- Genetic testing matters — it changes therapy: low‑dose sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 receptor agonists and metformin are reasonable alternatives that avoid amplifying osmotic diuresis.

Bottom line: glycosuria is not just a lab curiosity in young patients — it can be the key to a monogenic diagnosis. And once you recognize HNF1A‑MODY, rethink SGLT2 inhibitors: the very transporter they target is already downregulated, and blocking the remainder pharmacologically may do more harm than good.



# Calling a Novel Variant Pathogenic: From ACMG Criteria to Clinical Action

Why this case grabbed my attention: a young man with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes—classic red flags for MODY—turned out to carry a previously unreported HNF1A missense change (c.809A>G, p.N270S). The team used molecular evidence, domain context, in‑silico prediction and family segregation to classify the variant as likely pathogenic and changed management: insulin was stopped, dulaglutide + metformin controlled glucose (HbA1c down to 6.0% at 6 months). This is a tidy example of how variant interpretation translates to precision medicine—but it also highlights the caution required when moving from sequence to bedside.

## The clinician’s problem: novel variant, real patient decisions
When you get a novel missense change, questions you must answer quickly include: is this change really disease causing, or a rare benign polymorphism? Will I change therapy (stop insulin? switch agents?) or order cascade testing for the family? The ACMG/AMP framework gives a structured way to weigh the evidence, but you need to apply it in the clinical context.

Here’s how the team approached p.N270S and how you might think about similar cases.

## Applying ACMG criteria to HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S) — the evidence stack
The case report describes multiple independent lines of evidence used to support a likely pathogenic call. The key elements:

- Domain context (PM1 — moderate): p.N270S lies in HNF1A’s DNA-binding domain, a region where pathogenic missense variants cluster. Variants in this domain are more likely to disrupt transcriptional activity and cause HNF1A-MODY.

- Absence from population databases (PM2 — moderate): the variant was not reported in ClinVar, gnomAD or other major population resources, arguing against a common benign allele.

- Segregation with disease (PP1 — supporting): the variant co-segregates with early-onset diabetes within the patient’s family (maternal grandmother, mother and brother had early-onset diabetes). While the report doesn’t give exhaustive genotype/phenotype counts, reported familial segregation supports pathogenicity.

- Computational evidence (PP3 — supporting): high deleteriousness scores (AlphaMissense 0.995 and conservation of the residue) provide additional supportive in‑silico evidence that the missense change affects protein function.

- Contextual/adjacent residue evidence (could contribute PM5_supporting): the variant affects a residue adjacent to other known pathogenic residues in the DNA-binding domain—this strengthens the plausibility even if it isn’t standalone functional proof.

Combining two moderate (PM1 + PM2) and two supporting (PP1 + PP3) criteria fits well within the ACMG pathway to a Likely Pathogenic classification (two moderate + ≥2 supporting). The team therefore designated p.N270S as likely pathogenic and acted on it.

A couple of practical notes about the mapping:
- Don’t invent strong/very strong claims (e.g., PS1/PS3) unless you have direct functional assay data or a previously established pathogenic amino acid change at the exact residue.
- Co‑segregation strength depends on family size and whether genotypes were obtained; a few affected relatives can be “supporting,” larger pedigrees can upgrade to moderate evidence.
- Computational predictions are supportive, not determinative; they help tip the balance but don’t substitute for experimental or robust segregation data.

## From classification to bedside: why it mattered here
This wasn’t just an academic exercise. The molecular call changed care in several concrete ways in this patient:

- Therapeutic de‑intensification: insulin was stopped after genetic confirmation and preserved (though reduced) β‑cell function—switching to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) + metformin led to durable glycaemic control (HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months). Sulfonylureas are historically first-line for HNF1A-MODY, but GLP‑1RA was chosen here as a safer alternative with good efficacy and less hypoglycaemia risk as β-cell function declines.

- Avoidance/precaution around SGLT2 inhibitors: HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression and HNF1A-MODY patients can have a lower renal threshold for glucose (glycosuria). There are case reports of dehydration and DKA in HNF1A-MODY with exaggerated glycosuria; that risk informed therapy choices.

- Cascade testing and counseling: a likely pathogenic call supports offering targeted testing to at-risk relatives for early diagnosis and tailored therapy. It also provides a rationale for genetic counseling about inheritance, testing options, and reproductive implications.

## Communicating uncertainty — the critical conversation
Lab calls are not gospel forever. You must be explicit with patients about what “likely pathogenic” means, and plan for re-evaluation:

- Explain the classification in plain language: this variant is very likely to be the cause of the family’s early-onset diabetes based on multiple lines of evidence, but it is not yet proven by direct functional experiments.

- Document the rationale in the chart and give the patient a copy of the genetic report. Note that reclassification is possible as new data arrive; commit to notifying them if that happens.

- Offer referral to genetics/genetic counseling before cascade testing. Discuss implications for insurability and psychosocial impact as appropriate for your healthcare setting.

- Submit / encourage variant submission to public databases (ClinVar) so the global knowledge base can grow. Many labs do this automatically, but clinicians can help by providing phenotype details.

## Pitfalls: don’t over-call, don’t under-call
- Over-calling (labeling VUS as pathogenic) risks inappropriate therapy changes and unnecessary anxiety or invasive screening in relatives. Never base irreversible decisions solely on a VUS.

- Under-calling (leaving a true pathogenic variant as a VUS) denies patients the benefits of precision therapy and cascade testing. If your clinical picture is strongly suggestive (negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, strong family history, compatible phenotype), push for a formal variant review or second opinion, and consider family segregation testing if available.

A pragmatic approach is to combine variant evidence with robust clinical data (autoantibody status, stimulated C‑peptide, OGTT, family history). In this case the phenotype and genetics aligned and the classification supported a reasonable therapeutic de‑escalation that improved outcomes.

## Practical tips for clinicians ordering/interpreting MODY testing
- Trigger testing when you see: diabetes onset <25–30 years, strong autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, atypical phenotypes for T1D/T2D, or unexplained glycosuria.

- Use an NGS MODY panel that includes HNF1A and other common genes; confirm findings with appropriate lab standards and ensure the lab follows ACMG criteria for reporting.

- If a novel missense change is reported, ask the lab for the ACMG criterion mapping they used and for any segregation/functional data they have. Ask if they will submit the variant to ClinVar.

- Prioritize genetic counseling before family cascade testing. A likely pathogenic or pathogenic HNF1A result usually justifies predictive testing in adult family members, and in adolescents if results would change management.

- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY patients due to glycosuria and potential dehydration/DKA risk.

## Closing thought
Variant interpretation is part genetics, part detective work, and part clinical judgment. The ACMG framework gives you the structure, but the clinical phenotype and family context are often what tip the scales from “maybe” to “likely.” In this case, disciplined application of ACMG criteria to a novel HNF1A missense change—combined with a congruent clinical picture—allowed a move from insulin dependence to GLP‑1RA + metformin and enabled meaningful cascade testing. That’s precision medicine done well: sequence-to-bedside with appropriate humility and safeguards.

## Clinical pearls / take-home points
- Suspect MODY when diabetes presents young with negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide and autosomal dominant family history.
- Use ACMG criteria systematically: domain/context (PM1) + absence from population (PM2) + segregation (PP1) + computational support (PP3) can combine to a Likely Pathogenic call for novel missense variants.
- “Likely pathogenic” is actionable but not absolute—communicate uncertainty, document rationale, offer genetic counseling and cascade testing.
- A genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY can justify de‑intensifying insulin and guiding therapy (sulfonylurea historically, GLP‑1RA a safe/effective option), and raises caution about SGLT2 inhibitors due to glycosuria-related risks.
- Submit and share variant data (ClinVar) and re-evaluate variants as new evidence accumulates.



## Early autonomic neuropathy in MODY? Making sense of abnormal sudomotor tests

What grabbed me about this case was not the genetic novelty — although a new HNF1A variant is interesting — but the finding of “severely reduced sudomotor function in the hands” in a young adult with a short interval of symptomatic hyperglycaemia. That’s not what we classically expect: severe small‑fiber/autonomic dysfunction is usually a late complication of longstanding diabetes. So how should clinicians interpret an abnormal electrochemical skin conductance (ESC, e.g., Sudoscan) in someone with newly diagnosed HNF1A‑MODY and an HbA1c that improved dramatically after treatment? Below I walk through what ESC measures, its strengths and limitations, how hyperglycaemia can produce early reversible autonomic changes, and a pragmatic approach to follow‑up and management.

## What electrochemical skin conductance actually measures
- ESC devices apply a low‑voltage current to palms and soles and measure chloride ion conductance produced by sweat gland function. The output is an index of sudomotor activity, which is driven by small unmyelinated sympathetic C‑fibres innervating eccrine glands.
- Important: ESC is an indirect, functional test of small‑fiber autonomic function. It is not a direct structural test (like an intraepidermal nerve fibre density biopsy) and is influenced by skin factors (temperature, hydration), local skin integrity (callus, eczema), and patient factors (medications that alter sweating, sympathetic tone).
- Clinically, ESC is a screening/monitoring tool — quick, non‑invasive, and reproducible in many settings — but it is not diagnostic in isolation.

## How good is ESC? (sensitivity / specificity)
- ESC performance is variable between studies and populations. It generally has moderate sensitivity and specificity for detecting diabetic small‑fiber neuropathy when compared to reference tests (QSART, skin biopsy, clinical scoring), and performs better as a screening tool than as a stand‑alone diagnostic test.
- Bottom line: an abnormal ESC increases suspicion for small‑fiber/autonomic dysfunction and should prompt clinical correlation and, where needed, confirmatory testing — not reflexive labeling of “autonomic neuropathy” without context.

## How hyperglycaemia and early disease can affect sudomotor measures
- Acute and subacute hyperglycaemia can impair small‑fiber and autonomic function. Mechanisms include reversible metabolic dysfunction of nerve fibres, oxidative stress and impaired microvascular perfusion. This can produce abnormal functional tests before irreversible structural changes develop.
- In this case the patient had very high HbA1c (10.2%) and symptomatic hyperglycaemia. Rapid improvement in glycaemia (to HbA1c 6.0% over months) could plausibly reverse or substantially improve ESC abnormalities if those abnormalities were metabolic rather than structural.
- Other diabetes‑specific factors to consider: glycosuria (seen in HNF1A‑MODY due to lower renal glucose threshold) can cause volume losses and sympathetic activation; heat/intense sweating, dehydration or recent febrile illness can alter ESC. Medications (anticholinergics, beta‑blockers) and skin issues (calluses on palms/soles) are confounders.

## Specific considerations in HNF1A‑MODY
- HNF1A is expressed in multiple organs including kidney and pancreas; the case report mentions reduced SGLT2 expression as part of HNF1A biology and glycosuria as a typical feature. That renal phenotype may predispose to volume issues but there is no established mechanism that HNF1A variants directly cause early small‑fiber degeneration.
- There are case reports of autonomic dysfunction in early diabetes and small‑fiber neuropathy in young people, but we don’t yet have disease‑specific data linking HNF1A mutations to rapid small‑fiber loss. In short: the MODY diagnosis changes how we think about treatment and prognosis, but an abnormal ESC remains a functional finding that needs interpretation in context.

## A practical, evidence‑minded approach when ESC is abnormal in short‑duration diabetes

Step 1 — don’t panic; put the result in context
- Correlate ESC with symptoms: orthostatic lightheadedness, syncope, abnormal sweating, erectile dysfunction, gastrointestinal dysmotility, or pain/burning. An isolated abnormal ESC without symptoms is common and less ominous.
- Review history and exam for other neuropathy signs (foot sensation, pinprick, vibration, ankle reflexes) and autonomic clues (resting tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension).

Step 2 — exclude reversible confounders
- Check skin integrity at test sites (calluses, dermatitis).
- Review medications that affect sweating (anticholinergics, tricyclics, some antipsychotics).
- Ensure hydration, normothermia at time of testing.
- Screen for other causes of small‑fiber/autonomic neuropathy: B12, TSH, fasting glucose profile, alcohol use, HIV, recent infections and paraneoplastic clues as clinically indicated.

Step 3 — optimise glucose and reassess
- Given the likely contribution of hyperglycaemia, aim for prompt but safe glycaemic control. This HNF1A‑MODY patient was transitioned to GLP‑1RA and metformin with excellent improvement in HbA1c — a plausible reason to expect partial or full recovery of functional sudomotor measures.
- Plan a repeat ESC (or clinical reassessment) after a period of glycaemic optimisation — commonly 3 months — before labeling progressive autonomic neuropathy or starting invasive investigations.

Step 4 — escalate testing only when clinically indicated
- If the patient has symptoms consistent with autonomic neuropathy or if ESC abnormalities persist or worsen despite euglycaemia, consider:
  - Quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART) — a reference physiological test for sudomotor function.
  - Heart rate variability / deep‑breath and Valsalva testing, tilt‑table for orthostatic symptoms.
  - Skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve fibre density (IENFD) when small‑fiber neuropathy diagnosis would change management or for prognostication.
- These tests are specialist and not needed for every abnormal screening ESC.

Step 5 — treat what needs treating; avoid over‑treating tests
- If symptoms present:
  - Symptomatic therapies for orthostatic intolerance (fluid/salt, compression, midodrine) or for autonomic GI symptoms/gastroparesis as indicated.
  - Neuropathic pain agents (duloxetine, pregabalin) only when neuropathic pain exists.
- If only an abnormal ESC is present without symptoms: no need for specific autonomic pharmacotherapy. Focus on glycaemic control, counselling, foot care and surveillance.

## Clinical pearls
- ESC (Sudoscan) is a useful, rapid screening test for sudomotor dysfunction but is influenced by many non‑neurologic factors. Interpret results together with symptoms, exam and glycaemic history.
- Markedly abnormal ESC early after diagnosis in a patient with very poor glycaemic control is more likely to reflect reversible metabolic/autonomic dysfunction than irreversible structural small‑fiber loss. Reassess after optimisation of glucose.
- Don’t alter diabetes‑specific disease‑directed therapy (e.g., stop GLP‑1RA or avoid metformin) solely on the basis of an abnormal ESC. For HNF1A‑MODY specifically, precision diagnosis guided the team to stop insulin and use GLP‑1RA/metformin — an example of genetic results changing management.
- Escalate to QSART, heart rate variability testing, skin biopsy or specialist referral when clinical symptoms are present, when abnormal testing persists despite good glycaemic control, or when the diagnosis will change management.
- Always address reversible contributors (meds, hydration, skin lesions) and screen for other causes of small‑fiber/autonomic neuropathy (B12, thyroid, alcohol).

## Take‑home bullets
- An abnormal sudomotor test in early diabetes raises suspicion but is not diagnostic of irreversible autonomic neuropathy.
- Hyperglycaemia can cause early, reversible dysfunction of small‑fiber/autonomic pathways — optimise glucose first and recheck.
- Use ESC as a screening/monitoring tool; confirm with QSART/HRV/skin biopsy only when symptoms or persistent abnormalities justify further workup.
- In MODY, genetic diagnosis should guide diabetes therapy; management of possible autonomic dysfunction follows the same pragmatic, symptom‑driven pathway as in other forms of diabetes.

If you care for a young person with newly diagnosed diabetes and an unexpected abnormal autonomic screen — look, listen, fix the glucose and re‑test. Only then decide if you need to escalate.
