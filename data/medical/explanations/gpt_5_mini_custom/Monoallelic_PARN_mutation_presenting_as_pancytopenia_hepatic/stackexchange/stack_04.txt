Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Morning Report] What is the gold-standard diagnostic approach to confirm a telomere biology disorder in adults, and how should age-adjusted telomere length by flow-FISH be integrated with next-generation sequencing results when a PARN variant is classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance?

Short answer
The diagnostic gold standard for confirming a telomere biology disorder (TBD) in an adult is a combined approach: (1) objective measurement of age‑adjusted telomere length by flow cytometry–fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow‑FISH) in defined leukocyte subsets (or non‑hematopoietic tissue when blood is confounded), together with (2) targeted next‑generation sequencing (NGS) for telomere‑related genes. When NGS yields a monoallelic PARN variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the flow‑FISH result and clinical phenotype determine how to integrate and act on that VUS.

Why this combination is the standard
- Flow‑FISH provides cell‑type specific, age‑adjusted telomere lengths and is the most clinically validated assay for TBD. Southern blot and qPCR are less reliable or reproducible for clinical decision making.  
- Pathogenic variants in telomere maintenance genes (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, etc.) are not uniformly penetrant and adults may present without classic mucocutaneous features. Demonstration of abnormally short telomeres plus a compatible germline variant gives the strongest evidence for a causal TBD.

How to interpret flow‑FISH in practice and integrate with a PARN VUS
1. Obtain the right test in the right circumstances
   - Request age‑adjusted flow‑FISH on lymphocyte and granulocyte subsets (or CD3+ T cells) from a reference laboratory experienced in clinical telomere testing.  
   - Beware confounders: recent donor blood transfusions (as in this case) can dilute patient leukocytes and falsely normalize telomere lengths. If the patient has received transfusions, either delay testing (commonly ~3 months after transfusion) or measure telomere length in non‑hematopoietic tissue (skin fibroblasts or buccal cells) to obtain an unconfounded result.

2. Thresholds and their implications
   - Telomere length <1st percentile for age (by flow‑FISH) is strongly supportive of a TBD in adults and children when correlated with compatible clinical features. Values between the 1st and 10th percentile are supportive but less definitive; values above the 10th percentile make a TBD due to classic telomerase‑pathway loss much less likely, although late‑onset or hypomorphic variants can rarely present with only modest shortening. Always interpret in the clinical context.

3. How telomere result modifies interpretation of a PARN VUS
   - If flow‑FISH shows very short telomeres (especially <1st percentile) in a patient whose presentation fits a TBD (pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying as in this case), that functional/phenotypic evidence materially increases the likelihood that the monoallelic PARN VUS is pathogenic. This supports re‑classification discussions with the molecular laboratory (segregation testing, application of ACMG criteria—PP4, and possibly upgrading with strong functional/phenotypic evidence). Arrange family testing for segregation where possible.  
   - If telomere lengths are borderline low (1–10th percentile), interpret cautiously: the VUS may still be contributory in the correct phenotype, but additional evidence is needed (segregation, targeted functional assays of PARN, telomerase activity studies, or telomere measurement in another tissue).  
   - If telomere lengths are within expected age‑ranges, the VUS is less likely to be disease‑causing for a classic telomere maintenance disorder. Continued clinical surveillance is reasonable, and further workup (broader genetics, functional assays, or re‑evaluation over time) can be pursued if phenotype evolves.

Practical next steps for the patient in this case
- Defer peripheral blood flow‑FISH until the potential confounding effect of recent whole blood transfusion is mitigated (or obtain telomere length from cultured skin fibroblasts).  
- Send age‑adjusted flow‑FISH from a reference lab; if telomeres are profoundly short (<1st percentile), treat the PARN variant as likely disease‑causing in multidisciplinary review and pursue family segregation testing and expanded functional studies.  
- If telomeres are not short, continue hematologic and organ surveillance, re‑assess variant classification as new data emerge, and consider additional functional or segregation testing rather than assuming causation.

Bottom line
Diagnosing TBD in adults rests on concordant objective telomere shortening by age‑adjusted flow‑FISH and a compatible pathogenic germline variant. When NGS yields a PARN VUS, an unequivocally short age‑adjusted telomere length (measured appropriately, avoiding transfusion artifacts) substantially strengthens the argument that the variant is pathogenic; borderline or normal telomere lengths require further family and functional studies and careful clinical follow‑up.
