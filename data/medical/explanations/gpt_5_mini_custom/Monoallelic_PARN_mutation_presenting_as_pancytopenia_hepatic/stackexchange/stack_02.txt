Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient with pancytopenia has a positive direct antiglobulin test (IgG 2+) but a low reticulocyte count, hypersegmented neutrophils, dimorphic red cells, elevated serum iron and ferritin, and a bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia without dysplasia. How do these data distinguish ineffective erythropoiesis from autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and how would you interpret the positive DAT in this context?

Bottom line: the constellation of a low reticulocyte count, marrow erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective maturation, hypersegmented neutrophils and iron overload (high serum iron and ferritin) points to intramedullary (ineffective) erythropoiesis from severe vitamin B12–related dyserythropoiesis rather than peripheral immune destruction. The positive DAT in this patient is most likely an incidental or non–clinically significant finding and does not, by itself, establish autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA).

Why these data favor ineffective erythropoiesis over AIHA
- Reticulocyte response: AIHA (peripheral RBC destruction) characteristically provokes a brisk marrow response with reticulocytosis. This case shows a low reticulocyte count (0.39%), arguing that erythroid precursors are being lost before release (intramedullary destruction) or the marrow cannot mount an appropriate response.  
- Bone marrow and peripheral smear: erythroid hyperplasia without dysplasia indicates increased erythroid production with failure of maturation/egress. Hypersegmented neutrophils and dimorphic red cells are classic for severe B12/folate deficiency and megaloblastic ineffective erythropoiesis. In AIHA you would expect polychromasia and spherocytes on the peripheral smear rather than hypersegmentation or megaloblastic features.  
- Iron studies: ineffective erythropoiesis leads to poor iron utilization and release of iron from dying precursors → elevated serum iron and ferritin. In brisk peripheral hemolysis the marrow avidly uses iron for increased erythropoiesis (often lower ferritin or normal iron indices) unless chronic transfusions or concomitant iron loading are present.  
- Markers of hemolysis: LDH and indirect bilirubin may be elevated in both intramedullary and peripheral destruction; they do not distinguish the site of destruction. The key discriminator here is the inadequate reticulocyte response and marrow picture.  
- Clinical context: severe B12 deficiency documented in this patient explains the marrow findings and ineffective erythropoiesis; correction of the deficiency produced clinical improvement.

How to interpret the positive DAT here
- A positive DAT (IgG 2+) indicates IgG bound to RBCs but is not proof of clinically significant AIHA. DAT can be positive in a variety of settings without causing a clinically important hemolytic anemia: low‑affinity or low‑titer autoantibodies, prior transfusion, certain drugs, autoimmune gastritis or other inflammatory states, and even in some healthy people.  
- Supporting evidence against warm AIHA in this case: negative indirect antiglobulin test, low reticulocyte count, marrow evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis, and absence of typical peripheral smear features of AIHA. Those points argue that the DAT is an epiphenomenon rather than the driver of the anaemia.  
- When to ascribe significance to the DAT: if there is progressive hemoglobin fall with reticulocytosis, new spherocytosis, rising bilirubin/hemoglobinuria, or clinical deterioration despite B12 repletion, then the DAT should be re-evaluated and investigated (elution studies to identify antibody specificity, thermal amplitude/strength testing, complement testing) and treatment for AIHA considered.

Practical management steps in this patient
1. Treat the identified cause of ineffective erythropoiesis—aggressive correction of B12/folate (as done) and monitor for reticulocyte response over days–weeks. A rising retic count after repletion confirms marrow recovery.  
2. Do not reflexively immunosuppress for a positive DAT in the absence of convincing peripheral hemolysis and an appropriate marrow response.  
3. If hemolysis persists or clinical features of AIHA develop, perform targeted tests (repeat DAT, elution, complement, peripheral smear for spherocytes, hemoglobinuria) and consider empiric therapy only if there is clear evidence of immune-mediated peripheral destruction.

In short: the laboratory pattern and marrow findings in this case are classic for ineffective erythropoiesis from severe B12 deficiency (exacerbated by the underlying telomere biology disorder), and the positive DAT is most likely an incidental finding that should be interpreted in the full clinical and laboratory context rather than taken as proof of AIHA.
