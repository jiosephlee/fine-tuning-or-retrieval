Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a young adult with pancytopenia, premature greying, osteoporosis, and unexplained hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis but without the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, what red-flag clinical features should prompt evaluation for a telomere biology disorder?

Answer (clinical pearl)

Think “young person with unexplained premature ageing + marrow failure or organ fibrosis” — those are the red flags that should trigger evaluation for a telomere biology disorder (TBD), even when the classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad is absent.

Red-flag clinical features
- Age: presentation in young adults (often <40–50 years) with findings usually seen in older patients.  
- Bone marrow abnormalities: unexplained cytopenias or bone- marrow failure (pancytopenia, persistent macrocytosis, low reticulocyte response, marrow failure out of proportion to a reversible cause). In the case, persistent pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count despite correction of B12 raised suspicion.  
- Early-onset pulmonary fibrosis: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or interstitial lung disease occurring at an unusually young age. The patient had diffuse fibrotic changes and ground‑glass opacities in her 20s.  
- Unexplained hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis in a young adult without usual causes (negative viral and autoimmune testing). This patient had cirrhosis on transient elastography despite exclusion of common etiologies.  
- Premature ageing stigmata: premature greying of hair, early osteoporosis/fragility fractures, nail dystrophy or mucosal changes (even if the full triad is absent). The case had premature greying and osteoporosis with fractures.  
- Multisystem involvement or disproportionate severity: two or more end‑organ manifestations that reflect high-turnover tissues (marrow, lung, liver, skin, hair, gut, gonads, eyes, nervous system). The combination of marrow failure + pulmonary + hepatic disease in this patient is characteristic.  
- Family history or clustering of related phenotypes (early pulmonary fibrosis, aplastic anaemia, liver disease, premature greying) or excessive toxicity to cytotoxic therapy or HSCT in relatives.

Why these features matter (pathophysiologic reasoning)
TBDs cause accelerated telomere shortening in highly proliferative tissues (haematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, alveolar epithelium, skin/hair), so they present as premature organ failure rather than isolated single-organ disease. Classic mucocutaneous signs of dyskeratosis congenita may be absent in adults; therefore, the combination of early‑onset fibrosis and marrow dysfunction is the practical clinical signal. In this case the young age plus pancytopenia, early greying, osteoporosis and both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis form a syndromic pattern typical of a TBD and supported the identification of a PARN variant.

Diagnostic approach and standards
- Measure telomere length (flow-FISH on lymphocytes is the reference standard in clinical practice; very short telomeres for age strongly support TBD).  
- Germline genetic testing with a targeted telomere gene panel or broad clinical exome/genome sequencing (include TERT, TERC, DKC1, PARN, RTEL1, TINF2 and others). The case found a monoallelic PARN variant on clinical exome — concordant with the suspected diagnosis.  
- Interpret genetics in context: incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity and monoallelic vs biallelic effects are common; a variant of uncertain significance (as here) may still be clinically meaningful when phenotype fits.  
- Evaluate family members and offer genetic counselling.

Immediate clinical implications
- Early recognition changes management: surveillance for progressive marrow failure, pulmonary and hepatic complications; avoidance/modification of cytotoxic exposure (conditioning regimens for HSCT should be tailored), and early referral to centres experienced with TBDs for HSCT planning if progressive bone marrow failure develops.  
- Consider telomere-directed therapies experimentally (androgens) only in specialised settings and discuss risks/benefits.  
- Smoking cessation, vaccination, and vigilance for malignancy and treatment toxicity.

Bottom line
In any young adult with unexplained cytopenias or bone‑marrow dysfunction plus early‑onset pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, premature greying/osteoporosis, or multisystem premature‑ageing features — even without the mucocutaneous triad — test telomere length and perform germline telomere‑gene sequencing. The presented case (late‑20s woman with pancytopenia, premature greying, osteoporosis, liver cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis who harboured a monoallelic PARN variant) exemplifies this pattern and the need for early genetic evaluation and specialized follow‑up.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient with pancytopenia has a positive direct antiglobulin test (IgG 2+) but a low reticulocyte count, hypersegmented neutrophils, dimorphic red cells, elevated serum iron and ferritin, and a bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia without dysplasia. How do these data distinguish ineffective erythropoiesis from autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and how would you interpret the positive DAT in this context?

Bottom line: the constellation of a low reticulocyte count, marrow erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective maturation, hypersegmented neutrophils and iron overload (high serum iron and ferritin) points to intramedullary (ineffective) erythropoiesis from severe vitamin B12–related dyserythropoiesis rather than peripheral immune destruction. The positive DAT in this patient is most likely an incidental or non–clinically significant finding and does not, by itself, establish autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA).

Why these data favor ineffective erythropoiesis over AIHA
- Reticulocyte response: AIHA (peripheral RBC destruction) characteristically provokes a brisk marrow response with reticulocytosis. This case shows a low reticulocyte count (0.39%), arguing that erythroid precursors are being lost before release (intramedullary destruction) or the marrow cannot mount an appropriate response.  
- Bone marrow and peripheral smear: erythroid hyperplasia without dysplasia indicates increased erythroid production with failure of maturation/egress. Hypersegmented neutrophils and dimorphic red cells are classic for severe B12/folate deficiency and megaloblastic ineffective erythropoiesis. In AIHA you would expect polychromasia and spherocytes on the peripheral smear rather than hypersegmentation or megaloblastic features.  
- Iron studies: ineffective erythropoiesis leads to poor iron utilization and release of iron from dying precursors → elevated serum iron and ferritin. In brisk peripheral hemolysis the marrow avidly uses iron for increased erythropoiesis (often lower ferritin or normal iron indices) unless chronic transfusions or concomitant iron loading are present.  
- Markers of hemolysis: LDH and indirect bilirubin may be elevated in both intramedullary and peripheral destruction; they do not distinguish the site of destruction. The key discriminator here is the inadequate reticulocyte response and marrow picture.  
- Clinical context: severe B12 deficiency documented in this patient explains the marrow findings and ineffective erythropoiesis; correction of the deficiency produced clinical improvement.

How to interpret the positive DAT here
- A positive DAT (IgG 2+) indicates IgG bound to RBCs but is not proof of clinically significant AIHA. DAT can be positive in a variety of settings without causing a clinically important hemolytic anemia: low‑affinity or low‑titer autoantibodies, prior transfusion, certain drugs, autoimmune gastritis or other inflammatory states, and even in some healthy people.  
- Supporting evidence against warm AIHA in this case: negative indirect antiglobulin test, low reticulocyte count, marrow evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis, and absence of typical peripheral smear features of AIHA. Those points argue that the DAT is an epiphenomenon rather than the driver of the anaemia.  
- When to ascribe significance to the DAT: if there is progressive hemoglobin fall with reticulocytosis, new spherocytosis, rising bilirubin/hemoglobinuria, or clinical deterioration despite B12 repletion, then the DAT should be re-evaluated and investigated (elution studies to identify antibody specificity, thermal amplitude/strength testing, complement testing) and treatment for AIHA considered.

Practical management steps in this patient
1. Treat the identified cause of ineffective erythropoiesis—aggressive correction of B12/folate (as done) and monitor for reticulocyte response over days–weeks. A rising retic count after repletion confirms marrow recovery.  
2. Do not reflexively immunosuppress for a positive DAT in the absence of convincing peripheral hemolysis and an appropriate marrow response.  
3. If hemolysis persists or clinical features of AIHA develop, perform targeted tests (repeat DAT, elution, complement, peripheral smear for spherocytes, hemoglobinuria) and consider empiric therapy only if there is clear evidence of immune-mediated peripheral destruction.

In short: the laboratory pattern and marrow findings in this case are classic for ineffective erythropoiesis from severe B12 deficiency (exacerbated by the underlying telomere biology disorder), and the positive DAT is most likely an incidental finding that should be interpreted in the full clinical and laboratory context rather than taken as proof of AIHA.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the role of PARN in telomere maintenance and TERC RNA processing, and how do monoallelic versus biallelic PARN variants correlate with differing clinical phenotypes (telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis/bone marrow failure versus dyskeratosis congenita/Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome)?

Clinical teaching answer:

What PARN does and how that affects telomeres
- PARN is a nuclear 3′–5′ deadenylase that removes short, post‑transcriptionally added oligo(A) tails from the 3′ end of several noncoding RNAs—including the telomerase RNA component (TERC). This 3′ end trimming is an essential maturation step: without PARN activity, nascent TERC molecules retain oligo(A) tails that mark them for nuclear surveillance and degradation, so steady‑state TERC levels fall. Lower TERC → reduced telomerase ribonucleoprotein activity → accelerated telomere shortening in highly proliferative tissues (haematopoietic stem cells, mucosa/skin, lung epithelium, hepatocytes). PARN also deadenylates other mRNAs and noncoding RNAs, so its loss can have broader effects on RNA stability and cellular homeostasis.

Why monoallelic versus biallelic PARN variants give different clinical pictures
- Dosage effect (haploinsufficiency versus near‑complete loss): monoallelic (heterozygous) pathogenic PARN variants typically produce partial loss of PARN function. The consequent moderate reduction in TERC causes telomerase insufficiency that is often tissue‑selective and of later onset—classically adult‑onset idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), predisposition to bone‑marrow failure and cirrhosis, with variable and incomplete penetrance. By contrast, biallelic (recessive) loss‑of‑function variants produce a much more severe reduction in PARN activity and profound TERC depletion, driving markedly short telomeres from early life and the severe DC spectrum (dyskeratosis congenita) including Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson syndrome with early bone marrow failure, neurodevelopmental defects and classical mucocutaneous findings.
- Empiric support: the literature and the case report note monoallelic PARN variants identified in adult IPF and telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis/bone‑marrow failure cohorts, whereas biallelic PARN mutations have been reported in children with Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson syndrome and severe DC. PARN is among the genes least tolerant of disruptive variation, consistent with a critical, dose‑sensitive role.

How this explains the present case
- The patient carries a monoallelic missense PARN variant (p.Cys205Arg) in the R3H domain predicted damaging. Her clinical picture—early premature greying, pancytopenia (worsened by severe B12 deficiency), hepatic fibrosis and early pulmonary fibrosis in her 20s—fits a telomere biology disorder driven by partial loss of PARN function. The phenotype’s heterogeneity (absence of classical DC triad, later onset) is typical of monoallelic PARN‑related disease where partial TERC deficiency predisposes tissues to failure under stress (nutritional deficiency, environmental exposures) and shows incomplete penetrance.

Clinical implications (diagnosis and management)
- Suspect a TBD when young patients have unexplained marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis or hepatic fibrosis—even without the classical DC mucocutaneous triad.
- Diagnostic steps: measure telomere length (flow‑FISH or qPCR) and confirm germline variants by targeted sequencing; interpret PARN variants in the context of zygosity, predicted effect, telomere length and phenotype.
- Counseling/management: monoallelic carriers require surveillance for progressive marrow, lung and liver disease, avoidance of genotoxic exposures, family genetic counseling, and early consideration of referral for hematopoietic stem cell transplant if progressive marrow failure occurs. Biallelic disease warrants earlier, multisystem surveillance and more urgent consideration of definitive therapies.

Takeaway
- PARN maintains telomeres by promoting 3′‑end maturation and stability of TERC; the clinical phenotype tracks PARN dosage—monoallelic (haploinsufficiency) → later‑onset, organ‑predominant telomere disease (IPF, marrow or liver fibrosis), biallelic loss → severe, early DC/HH syndrome from profound telomerase deficiency—explaining the patient’s multisystem fibrosis and marrow vulnerability despite a single damaging PARN allele.

### [Morning Report] What is the gold-standard diagnostic approach to confirm a telomere biology disorder in adults, and how should age-adjusted telomere length by flow-FISH be integrated with next-generation sequencing results when a PARN variant is classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance?

Short answer
The diagnostic gold standard for confirming a telomere biology disorder (TBD) in an adult is a combined approach: (1) objective measurement of age‑adjusted telomere length by flow cytometry–fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow‑FISH) in defined leukocyte subsets (or non‑hematopoietic tissue when blood is confounded), together with (2) targeted next‑generation sequencing (NGS) for telomere‑related genes. When NGS yields a monoallelic PARN variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the flow‑FISH result and clinical phenotype determine how to integrate and act on that VUS.

Why this combination is the standard
- Flow‑FISH provides cell‑type specific, age‑adjusted telomere lengths and is the most clinically validated assay for TBD. Southern blot and qPCR are less reliable or reproducible for clinical decision making.  
- Pathogenic variants in telomere maintenance genes (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, etc.) are not uniformly penetrant and adults may present without classic mucocutaneous features. Demonstration of abnormally short telomeres plus a compatible germline variant gives the strongest evidence for a causal TBD.

How to interpret flow‑FISH in practice and integrate with a PARN VUS
1. Obtain the right test in the right circumstances
   - Request age‑adjusted flow‑FISH on lymphocyte and granulocyte subsets (or CD3+ T cells) from a reference laboratory experienced in clinical telomere testing.  
   - Beware confounders: recent donor blood transfusions (as in this case) can dilute patient leukocytes and falsely normalize telomere lengths. If the patient has received transfusions, either delay testing (commonly ~3 months after transfusion) or measure telomere length in non‑hematopoietic tissue (skin fibroblasts or buccal cells) to obtain an unconfounded result.

2. Thresholds and their implications
   - Telomere length <1st percentile for age (by flow‑FISH) is strongly supportive of a TBD in adults and children when correlated with compatible clinical features. Values between the 1st and 10th percentile are supportive but less definitive; values above the 10th percentile make a TBD due to classic telomerase‑pathway loss much less likely, although late‑onset or hypomorphic variants can rarely present with only modest shortening. Always interpret in the clinical context.

3. How telomere result modifies interpretation of a PARN VUS
   - If flow‑FISH shows very short telomeres (especially <1st percentile) in a patient whose presentation fits a TBD (pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying as in this case), that functional/phenotypic evidence materially increases the likelihood that the monoallelic PARN VUS is pathogenic. This supports re‑classification discussions with the molecular laboratory (segregation testing, application of ACMG criteria—PP4, and possibly upgrading with strong functional/phenotypic evidence). Arrange family testing for segregation where possible.  
   - If telomere lengths are borderline low (1–10th percentile), interpret cautiously: the VUS may still be contributory in the correct phenotype, but additional evidence is needed (segregation, targeted functional assays of PARN, telomerase activity studies, or telomere measurement in another tissue).  
   - If telomere lengths are within expected age‑ranges, the VUS is less likely to be disease‑causing for a classic telomere maintenance disorder. Continued clinical surveillance is reasonable, and further workup (broader genetics, functional assays, or re‑evaluation over time) can be pursued if phenotype evolves.

Practical next steps for the patient in this case
- Defer peripheral blood flow‑FISH until the potential confounding effect of recent whole blood transfusion is mitigated (or obtain telomere length from cultured skin fibroblasts).  
- Send age‑adjusted flow‑FISH from a reference lab; if telomeres are profoundly short (<1st percentile), treat the PARN variant as likely disease‑causing in multidisciplinary review and pursue family segregation testing and expanded functional studies.  
- If telomeres are not short, continue hematologic and organ surveillance, re‑assess variant classification as new data emerge, and consider additional functional or segregation testing rather than assuming causation.

Bottom line
Diagnosing TBD in adults rests on concordant objective telomere shortening by age‑adjusted flow‑FISH and a compatible pathogenic germline variant. When NGS yields a PARN VUS, an unequivocally short age‑adjusted telomere length (measured appropriately, avoiding transfusion artifacts) substantially strengthens the argument that the variant is pathogenic; borderline or normal telomere lengths require further family and functional studies and careful clinical follow‑up.

### [Management] How would you manage cytopenias in a patient with severe vitamin B12 deficiency and a suspected telomere biology disorder, and what strategies differ from standard aplastic anemia care (e.g., role and monitoring of androgens like danazol/nandrolone, timing and indications for HSCT, and cautions with immunosuppression)?

Direct answer
- First correct reversible causes (here, severe vitamin B12 deficiency) and reassess blood counts and reticulocyte response. If cytopenias persist after nutritional repletion, treat as possible evolving telomere biology disorder (TBD)–associated marrow failure rather than routine acquired aplastic anemia.
- For TBDs the management priorities differ from standard aplastic anemia care: androgens can be therapeutic but carry important risks (especially hepatotoxicity) and require close monitoring; standard immunosuppressive therapy (ATG + cyclosporine) is less reliably effective and may be avoided as first‑line once a germline TBD is suspected; definitive therapy for progressive marrow failure is hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) but timing, donor choice and conditioning must be individualized because telomere defects increase transplant toxicity.

Clinical reasoning anchored to the case
- This patient’s marrow failure was largely explained initially by severe B12 deficiency, and her counts improved after replacement — underscoring the first principle: always correct reversible and nutritional causes before labeling inherited marrow failure. The concomitant features (premature greying, pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, PARN variant) raise genuine concern for an underlying TBD that will influence subsequent therapy choices.
- In TBDs hematopoietic stem cells have limited replicative reserve and patients commonly have pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis; these comorbidities both alter tolerance to cytotoxic conditioning and increase transplant-related mortality. Thus management pathways used for idiopathic aplastic anemia cannot be applied without modification.

Practical management approach

1) Immediate/short term
- Correct vitamin B12 (as was done) and folate; give transfusions for symptomatic anemia and platelets per standard thresholds. Reassess counts and reticulocyte index after nutritional correction.
- Supportive care: infection surveillance, vaccinations as appropriate, iron overload monitoring if transfusion-dependent.

2) If cytopenias persist after correction or are progressive
- Evaluate and confirm TBD: telomere length testing (flow-FISH where available) and targeted germline testing for telomere genes; counsel family and test potential related donors.
- Avoid reflex use of standard IST (horse or rabbit ATG + cyclosporine) as first‑line therapy when a TBD is suspected. Response rates are lower and relapse/complications higher in TBD-associated marrow failure compared with immune aplastic anemia.

3) Role of androgens (danazol, nandrolone)
- Evidence and indication: small studies (including clinical trial data) show androgens can produce hematologic responses and may lengthen telomeres in some patients; therefore androgens are used as a non‑curative therapy or a bridge to HSCT in patients with progressive cytopenias who are not immediate HSCT candidates.
- Monitoring and risks: baseline and serial LFTs, bilirubin; baseline lipid profile; pregnancy test and contraception for women of childbearing potential; monitor hemoglobin, platelets, and for virilizing effects. Check for viral hepatitis before therapy.
- Dose and agents: danazol is the best‑studied androgen in TBDs; nandrolone is used anecdotally. Regimens and doses vary by center. Importantly, androgens are relatively contraindicated in patients with significant hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis because of hepatotoxicity and risk of worsening portal hypertension—this is directly relevant to this patient, who already has cirrhosis on elastography. In such patients androgens should generally be avoided or used only after careful multidisciplinary discussion with hepatology, with very close LFT surveillance.

4) Hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT)
- Indications: progressive marrow failure with transfusion dependence, evolution to MDS/AML, or failure of medical/androgen therapy. Early referral to a transplant center experienced with TBDs is advisable once a germline TBD is documented or strongly suspected.
- Timing: balance the risk of waiting (ongoing transfusions, iron overload, clonal evolution) against increased transplant risk in patients with advanced pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis. Many centers favor earlier transplant before irreversible organ fibrosis progresses—but only after thorough organ evaluation and optimization.
- Donor selection: avoid related donors who carry the same germline mutation or have short telomeres; screen family donors genetically and with telomere length testing.
- Conditioning regimens: use reduced‑intensity or nonmyeloablative regimens tailored to minimize DNA‑damaging agents (avoid or reduce busulfan/alkylator exposure when possible). Fludarabine‑based, low‑dose TBI or other reduced intensity protocols are commonly used. Coordinate care with pulmonology and hepatology because pulmonary and hepatic reserve predict transplant outcome.
- Post‑transplant: anticipate prolonged cytopenias, infection risk, and organ complications; long‑term surveillance for malignancy.

5) Immunosuppression cautions
- Standard IST (ATG + cyclosporine) is less effective in TBD-associated marrow failure and relapse is common. IST may be considered only if a clear immune aplastic anemia picture exists and telomere disease is not confirmed, or as a temporizing measure in selected cases, but should be used cautiously.
- Immunosuppression increases infection risk; in a patient with pulmonary fibrosis or hepatic disease, infectious complications are particularly dangerous. Thorough infectious screening and prophylaxis per institutional protocols are required when IST is used.

Summary specific to this patient
- You correctly treated the immediate reversible cause (severe B12 deficiency) and the patient’s counts stabilized. Because she also has cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis and a monoallelic PARN variant consistent with a TBD, plan longitudinal surveillance rather than routine aplastic‑anemia algorithms. Androgens (danazol) might help hematologic recovery in TBDs but are relatively contraindicated here because of established hepatic fibrosis and thus are not a safe choice without hepatology input and very close monitoring. If marrow failure becomes progressive or transfusion dependence develops despite supportive measures, early referral to an HSCT center with experience in TBDs is essential for donor evaluation (exclude mutation carriers), full pulmonary/hepatic assessment, and use of reduced‑intensity, telomere‑sparing conditioning. Avoid uncritical use of standard IST once a TBD is suspected.

Key monitoring checklist if considering androgen therapy or HSCT referral
- Baseline: CBC with reticulocyte, LFTs and bilirubin, hepatitis serologies, pregnancy test, lipid profile, PFTs with DLCO, HRCT chest, liver elastography/biochemistry, telomere length testing if available, and germline testing of potential related donors.
- Ongoing: monthly LFTs and CBC early after androgen initiation, serial pulmonary and liver surveillance, and MDT coordination for transplant timing.

This approach preserves the initial lessons from the case (always correct reversible causes), recognizes the special biology of TBDs, and adapts therapy—use androgens cautiously (contraindicated with cirrhosis), avoid routine IST, and plan HSCT with telomere‑aware donor selection and reduced‑intensity conditioning.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which organ systems require routine surveillance in adults with telomere biology disorders, and what specific tests and intervals would you use to monitor for progression of pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, marrow failure, and malignancy risk?

Bottom line: Adults with telomere biology disorders require structured, lifelong surveillance of four principal domains—lungs, liver, bone marrow (haematopoiesis), and cancer risk—because telomere shortening preferentially damages highly proliferative tissues. In the presented case the patient already illustrates why: radiographic interstitial lung disease (diffuse ground‑glass with subpleural fibrosis), a FibroScan consistent with cirrhosis (18.1 kPa), and persistent cytopenias (pancytopenia with low reticulocyte count). Surveillance should be risk‑stratified to the patient’s current organ involvement and intensify with any clinical or laboratory change.

1) Pulmonary disease (monitoring for progression of pulmonary fibrosis)
- Rationale: monoallelic PARN and other TBD genes commonly present with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; progression determines timing of transplant referral.
- Baseline: high‑resolution chest CT (HRCT) and full pulmonary function testing (PFTs) including spirometry, lung volumes and DLCO; 6‑minute walk test (6MWT) with oximetry and resting pulse‑ox.
- Interval:
  - If lung involvement present but clinically stable: PFTs and DLCO every 6 months for the first year, then every 6–12 months thereafter; 6MWT and oximetry at similar intervals.
  - HRCT: repeat at 12 months or sooner if PFTs or symptoms worsen; thereafter every 12–24 months if stable (more frequent if clinical change).
  - If accelerated decline (FVC fall >10% or DLCO fall >15% in 12 months, or increasing oxygen requirement): refer promptly to an interstitial lung disease/transplant center.
- Triggers to escalate: new/worsening dyspnoea, ≥10% decline in FVC, ≥15% decline in DLCO, new resting hypoxemia or exercise desaturation.

2) Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- Rationale: TBDs commonly involve the liver and can progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The case had objective cirrhosis on transient elastography.
- Baseline: LFTs (AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, total bilirubin), albumin, INR, platelet count, serum AFP, transient elastography (FibroScan) and abdominal ultrasound.
- Interval:
  - For cirrhosis or high fibrosis (FibroScan ≥12–15 kPa or imaging evidence): LFTs, INR, albumin and platelets every 3–6 months; abdominal ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months for HCC surveillance.
  - For moderate fibrosis without cirrhosis: LFTs every 3–6 months and FibroScan annually; ultrasound annually (or sooner if FibroScan increases or labs worsen).
- Other actions: screen for portal hypertension (baseline endoscopy if cirrhosis per local variceal screening protocols), vaccinate against hepatitis A/B if nonimmune, avoid hepatotoxins (alcohol, hepatotoxic drugs), and engage hepatology early for management and transplant evaluation if decompensation or progressive fibrosis.

3) Bone marrow failure and haematologic surveillance
- Rationale: bone marrow is highly telomere‑sensitive; marrow failure, cytopenias and evolution to MDS/AML are risks. In this patient pancytopenia and ineffective erythropoiesis were prominent.
- Baseline: complete blood count (CBC) with differential and reticulocyte count, peripheral smear, iron studies, B12/folate (correct reversible causes), and baseline bone marrow biopsy if unexplained cytopenias persist.
- Interval:
  - For stable counts: CBC with differential and reticulocyte count every 1–3 months (3 months is reasonable for clinically stable mild cytopenias); more frequently (every 2–4 weeks) if symptomatic or transfusion dependent.
  - Repeat bone marrow biopsy and cytogenetics/NGS if there is new or progressive cytopenia, rising transfusion requirements, new blasts, dysplastic changes, or otherwise unexplained laboratory changes.
- Additional monitoring: ferritin/iron overload assessment if transfusion dependent (ferritin every 2–3 months; consider hepatic and cardiac iron MRI when indicated). Early referral to a haematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) centre is mandatory when progressive marrow failure or high‑risk clonal evolution is detected—note that HSCT regimens are modified for TBD patients because of increased toxicity.

4) Malignancy surveillance (haematologic and solid tumors)
- Rationale: TBDs carry increased risk of haematologic malignancies and selected solid tumors (oral cavity, skin, potentially HCC in cirrhosis).
- Practical approach:
  - Ongoing haematologic surveillance is principally by routine CBCs and bone marrow evaluation as above; low threshold for marrow workup if counts change.
  - For HCC risk in those with cirrhosis: abdominal ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months (as above).
  - Mucocutaneous and oral surveillance: periodic (at least annual) dermatologic and oral/oral‑medicine exams to inspect for leukoplakia, dysplasia or suspicious lesions; prompt biopsy of any lesions.
  - Lung cancer: counsel strongly against smoking; apply local LDCT screening guidelines if smoking history meets criteria, but management is individualized because of concurrent ILD.
  - Formal population‑wide cancer screening otherwise follows age‑ and risk‑appropriate guidelines but consider earlier or more frequent surveillance when family history or prior lesions exist.
- Counseling and family testing: offer genetic counselling and cascade testing for relatives; patients should be informed of elevated cancer risk and avoid carcinogenic exposures.

General practical pearls relevant to the case
- Tailor surveillance intensity to current organ involvement: the patient already needs semiannual HCC surveillance, at least 6‑monthly PFTs with annual HRCT, and CBCs every 1–3 months until stable.
- Minimize exposures that accelerate telomere attrition or organ injury: no smoking, avoid unnecessary cytotoxic chemotherapy or ionizing radiation when possible, and avoid hepatotoxins/alcohol.
- Coordinate care in a multidisciplinary clinic (haematology, hepatology, ILD/transplant, genetics, dermatology, ENT/dental) and consider early referral to transplant centers (lung and liver and HSCT) because timing and conditioning regimens require specialist planning in TBD.
- Document and correct reversible contributors to cytopenias (as in this case: treat B12/folate deficiency) but continue genetic‑guided surveillance because marrow insufficiency may progress despite correction of nutritional deficits.

In short: baseline comprehensive testing (HRCT + PFTs; FibroScan/ultrasound + liver labs; CBC with smear ± marrow; exam for mucocutaneous lesions) and then scheduled surveillance driven by severity—typically PFTs every 6–12 months and HRCT every 12–24 months if stable; liver labs every 3–6 months with FibroScan annually and ultrasound/AFP every 6 months if cirrhosis; CBC every 1–3 months with marrow reassessment for decline; and focused cancer surveillance (HCC in cirrhosis every 6 months; annual skin/oral exams), together with early multidisciplinary referral. These steps reflect the pathophysiology and the multisystem involvement illustrated in the case.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, neuropathy, and weight loss, list three high-priority alternative diagnoses to telomere biology disorders and the single best test for each that helps distinguish or exclude them (e.g., myelodysplastic syndrome, autoimmune hepatitis, chronic viral hepatitis, Wilson disease, infiltrative or malignant processes, copper deficiency).

Direct answer: three high‑priority alternative diagnoses and the single best test to distinguish or exclude each

1) Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)
- Why consider: persistent pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis (dimorphic cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings) and splenomegaly can reflect an underlying clonal marrow disorder in a young adult. MDS can present without overt dysplasia early and mimic marrow-failure syndromes.
- Single best test: diagnostic bone marrow aspirate/biopsy with formal morphologic assessment plus conventional cytogenetics and a targeted NGS myeloid panel for somatic mutations (eg, TP53, ASXL1, SF3B1, TET2, DNMT3A).  
- How this applies to the case: the patient already had a marrow exam showing trilineage haematopoiesis without dysplasia and normal cytogenetics, arguing against overt MDS; targeted NGS for myeloid mutations is the next step if clinical suspicion remains because small clones can be missed by morphology/cytogenetics alone.

2) Wilson disease (hereditary copper overload)
- Why consider: young age, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis and neurologic findings (neuropathy, movement/neurologic signs can occur) raise high suspicion for Wilson disease as a reversible cause of hepatic failure and neurodegeneration.
- Single best test: hepatic copper quantification on a liver biopsy (measurement of liver copper content; >250 μg/g dry weight is diagnostic).  
- How this applies to the case: autoimmune and common viral causes were negative; given the combination of early cirrhosis and neurologic disease in a young adult, hepatic copper measurement is the definitive test even when serum ceruloplasmin or urinary copper are equivocal.

3) Copper deficiency (nutritional or malabsorptive)
- Why consider: copper deficiency produces cytopenias (typically anemia and neutropenia), a subacute axonal myeloneuropathy that can mimic B12 deficiency, and can coexist with malabsorption—features present here (pancytopenia, neuropathy, GI findings, B12 malabsorption). It is an important treatable mimic of marrow failure and neurologic disease.
- Single best test: serum copper concentration (with ceruloplasmin measurement to interpret results).  
- How this applies to the case: the patient had severe B12 deficiency and neuropathy; if neurologic or hematologic abnormalities persist despite B12 repletion one must check serum copper because low copper would point toward a treatable nutritional/malabsorptive cause rather than a genetic TBD.

Brief clinical reasoning wrap‑up
- These three diagnoses are high‑priority because each either explains multisystem fibrosis/neuropathy with cytopenias (Wilson, copper deficiency) or can masquerade as marrow failure (MDS). In this patient, the available data (normal marrow morphology/cytogenetics, negative autoimmune and routine viral serologies) already argue against classic MDS and common autoimmune/viral hepatitis; nonetheless targeted molecular testing for clonal hematopoiesis, hepatic copper quantification for Wilson disease, and a serum copper/ceruloplasmin level to exclude copper deficiency are the single most informative next tests to confidently exclude these alternative, potentially treatable diagnoses.

### [Management] For patients with telomere biology disorders who progress to bone marrow failure, what hematopoietic stem cell transplantation considerations are unique, including donor selection (screening related donors for pathogenic variants/short telomeres) and conditioning regimen modifications (reduced-intensity, radiation-sparing approaches), and why?

Direct answer
- Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only potentially curative therapy for marrow failure in telomere biology disorders (TBDs), but transplantation in these patients requires disease‑specific modifications because short/dysfunctional telomeres cause outsized sensitivity to DNA‑damaging conditioning and because related donors may carry the same germline defect. Two key principles guide practice: (1) avoid using a donor with the same pathogenic variant or demonstrably short telomeres, and (2) minimize DNA‑ and radiation‑mediated toxicity by using reduced‑intensity, radiation‑sparing conditioning regimens tailored to the patient’s pulmonary and hepatic reserve.

Why these differences matter (pathophysiology → clinical risk)
- TBDs (eg, PARN, TERT, TERC mutations) cause premature telomere attrition in highly proliferative tissues. Cells with short telomeres have impaired ability to tolerate DNA cross‑linking, alkylation and ionizing radiation; the result is excess marrow graft‑failure, mucosal injury, veno‑occlusive liver disease, and life‑threatening pulmonary and hepatic toxicity if standard myeloablative/TBI regimens are used. The case illustrates this risk: a young woman with a monoallelic PARN variant who already has pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis—organs that are especially vulnerable to transplant‑related damage—underscoring the need for a tailored approach.

Donor selection: mandatory screening and preferred donors
- Always screen related potential donors for the familial pathogenic variant and for telomere length before using them.
  - Genetic testing: test first‑degree relatives for the known PARN (or other TBD) variant; any carrier should be excluded as a donor.
  - Telomere length testing: flow cytometry–based FISH (flow‑FISH) is the clinical standard for telomere length assessment; quantitative PCR is less reliable clinically. Relatives with short telomeres even in the absence of a known variant should likewise be avoided.
- Preferred donors:
  - HLA‑matched unrelated donor without the familial variant and with normal telomere length (ideal).
  - Cord blood and haploidentical donors have been used, but each choice must be weighed against donor telomere status and center experience. Do not assume a related matched sibling is safe without genetic/telomere testing—many families carry occult mutations or short telomeres with incomplete penetrance.
- Rationale: using an affected or telomere‑short donor transfers defective hematopoietic stem cells and increases risk of graft failure and poor long‑term hematopoiesis.

Conditioning regimen modifications
- General strategy: use reduced‑intensity or nonmyeloablative, radiation‑sparing conditioning that minimizes alkylator and TBI exposure while achieving engraftment.
  - Avoid or minimize high‑dose alkylators (busulfan, melphalan) and TBI when possible because of excess organ toxicity in TBDs.
  - Common contemporary approaches:
    - Fludarabine‑based reduced‑intensity regimens (eg, fludarabine + low‑dose busulfan with careful pharmacokinetic monitoring) used to limit cumulative alkylator exposure.
    - Treosulfan‑based regimens are increasingly used because treosulfan has substantial myeloablative activity with a different toxicity profile and may be less hepatotoxic than busulfan in some series.
    - Nonirradiation regimens (no TBI) whenever feasible to reduce pulmonary and long‑term risks.
    - Incorporation of serotherapy (ATG) to reduce graft‑versus‑host disease (GVHD) risk and promote engraftment with lower intensity.
- Center‑ and patient‑specific tailoring:
  - Degree of conditioning will depend on donor type, urgency, and the patient’s lung and liver function. For a patient like the case subject who already has pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis, choose the least toxic regimen that reliably secures engraftment and avoid TBI and full‑dose myeloablation.
  - Pre‑HCT optimization: treat reversible contributors (eg, correct nutritional deficiencies, consider androgens as a bridge to improve counts if used), and stabilize pulmonary and hepatic status.
- Rationale: these modifications reduce transplant‑related mortality while still allowing donor engraftment. Historical data show standard myeloablative regimens in TBD patients are associated with high early mortality from organ failure.

Other practical considerations and counseling points
- HSCT may correct the hematopoietic defect but will not reliably reverse established pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis; fibrosis may progress despite successful engraftment. In the case report the patient already has IPF and cirrhosis—this must be discussed in counseling.
- Pre‑transplant evaluation must be exhaustive: pulmonary function testing (including DLCO), high‑resolution CT chest, liver imaging and stiffness assessment, and portal hypertension evaluation. These findings influence candidacy and conditioning choices.
- Post‑transplant vigilance: higher risk of pulmonary complications, hepatic VOD, delayed mucosal healing and infections—monitor closely and involve experienced transplant teams.
- If no appropriate donor without the familial defect is available, weigh alternatives (unrelated donor search, umbilical cord blood units with normal telomere length, or clinical trials) rather than using an untested related donor.
- Early referral to a transplant center experienced with TBDs is essential.

Bottom line (how this applies to the presented case)
- For this young woman with a monoallelic PARN variant and evolving marrow involvement plus established lung and liver fibrosis, the transplant team should only consider HSCT after:
  1) rigorous donor screening (genetic testing for the PARN variant and flow‑FISH telomere measurement in all potential related donors), and
  2) planning a reduced‑intensity, radiation‑sparing conditioning regimen tailored to her pulmonary and hepatic reserve (avoiding full‑dose alkylators/TBI when possible).  
- These steps reduce the otherwise excessive transplant‑related toxicity seen in TBDs and avoid transferring the underlying telomere defect from an affected relative donor.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how critically short telomeres in alveolar epithelial cells and hepatocytes lead to pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, emphasizing stem/progenitor cell exhaustion, impaired epithelial regeneration, and downstream profibrotic signaling (e.g., TGF-β–mediated pathways) that culminate in maladaptive repair and scarring.

Bottom line: critically short telomeres in high‑turnover parenchymal cells — notably alveolar type II (AT2) cells in the lung and hepatocytes/hepatic progenitors in the liver — abrogate normal epithelial regeneration and drive a senescence/apoptosis program that both removes competent stem/progenitor cells and actively promotes profibrotic signaling (principally TGF‑β), so that injury is repaired not by epithelium but by persistent myofibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition → fibrosis.

Mechanistic sequence (how short telomeres beget scarring)
1) Telomere dysfunction triggers a DNA‑damage response. Critically shortened telomeres are sensed as double‑strand breaks, activating ATM/ATR–p53–p21 (and p16INK4a) pathways. Cells either undergo apoptosis or enter irreversible growth arrest (cellular senescence).

2) Stem/progenitor cell exhaustion and impaired epithelial regeneration. AT2 cells are the lung’s primary alveolar stem/progenitor population; hepatocytes and liver progenitor (oval) cells renew the liver. When telomeres are critically short these populations lose proliferative capacity (senescence/apoptosis), so after routine or repeated microinjury the epithelium cannot repopulate the niche. The net result is failed re‑epithelialization and reliance on non‑epithelial repair pathways.

3) Senescent cells actively promote fibrosis via SASP. Senescent epithelial cells secrete a senescence‑associated secretory phenotype (SASP) — cytokines, chemokines and growth factors including TGF‑β, IL‑6, PDGF and matrix‑remodeling enzymes. TGF‑β is central: it induces fibroblast activation, myofibroblast differentiation (α‑SMA expression), epithelial–mesenchymal transition (in some contexts) and upregulates collagen and other ECM synthesis while downregulating matrix degradation.

4) Aberrant epithelial–mesenchymal crosstalk and persistent myofibroblast activation. Loss of healthy epithelial signals and gain of profibrotic SASP shifts the wound response toward expansion and persistence of activated fibroblasts/myofibroblasts. These cells lay down collagen and other ECM, contract tissue, and create stiffness that further perpetuates TGF‑β activation (mechanotransduction) — a self‑sustaining fibrotic circuit.

5) Organ‑specific manifestations linked to the same biology. In the lung this process converts repeated alveolar injury into progressive interstitial fibrosis (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis phenotype), with loss of gas‑exchanging surface and progressive dyspnea. In the liver, hepatocyte senescence and failed regeneration activate hepatic stellate cells and progenitor‑driven ductular reactions; chronic stellate cell activation produces bridging fibrosis and finally cirrhosis.

How this explains the case
- PARN is required for proper maturation of TERC and therefore for telomerase function. A monoallelic PARN variant can cause accelerated telomere shortening (haploinsufficiency), disproportionately affecting tissues with high replicative demand — bone marrow, skin, lung and liver — as seen in this young patient.
- The patient’s pulmonary ground‑glass and subpleural fibrotic changes and cirrhosis are the expected downstream phenotypes when AT2 cells and hepatocytes cannot regenerate and instead produce profibrotic signals (TGF‑β–rich SASP), leading to myofibroblast–ECM–driven scarring.
- Clinically, this biology explains early onset fibrosis, multisystem involvement, and why correcting reversible insults (eg, B12 deficiency) may improve symptoms but does not reverse organ fibrosis once maladaptive repair is established.

Clinical pearls
- Telomere‑driven fibrosis is a problem of regenerative failure plus active profibrotic signaling, not merely passive wear‑and‑tear.
- Recognizing telomere biology disorders (eg, PARN, TERT, TERC mutations) in young patients with unexplained pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis is important because it affects prognosis, transplant candidacy, donor selection and family counselling.
- Therapeutic strategies that solely suppress inflammation are often insufficient; approaches that preserve or restore epithelial progenitor function (experimental telomerase activation, careful use of androgens in selected trials) or interrupt profibrotic signaling (anti‑TGF‑β pathway agents under investigation) are biologically rational but remain investigational.
