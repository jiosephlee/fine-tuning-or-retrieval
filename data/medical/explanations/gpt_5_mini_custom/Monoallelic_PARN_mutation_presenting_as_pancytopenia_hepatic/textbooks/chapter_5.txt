Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 5: ## Management, surveillance, and prognosis in PARN-related telomere biology disorders

Immediate and supportive care
Management begins by addressing reversible contributors to the presenting problems while recognizing the underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD) that may predispose to progressive, multisystem disease. In this patient the initial, effective measures were correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency with parenteral cyanocobalamin (1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, then monthly maintenance), oral folic acid, transfusion support for symptomatic anaemia, and correction of electrolytes and vitamin D/calcium repletion for osteoporosis. These measures are appropriate first steps because nutritional deficiency accounted for much of the ineffective erythropoiesis and some symptoms; they should be continued and monitored. Supportive interventions such as physiotherapy and bone health optimization (calcium, vitamin D, antiresorptive therapy when indicated) improve function and reduce fracture risk and are important adjuncts.

Hematologic follow‑up and indications for escalation
Patients with a TBD require longitudinal haematologic surveillance because bone marrow failure can evolve over months to years. After initial correction of reversible causes, repeat CBCs with reticulocyte counts should be performed frequently (for example every 4–12 weeks initially, then every 3 months once stable). Worsening cytopenias, a falling reticulocyte count despite correction of nutritional deficits, new or progressive transfusion dependence, emergence of dysplastic features on peripheral smear, increasing marrow hypocellularity on prior exam, or new cytogenetic abnormalities are all indications to repeat bone marrow examination and consider referral to a tertiary haematology centre.

Early referral to a transplant centre is recommended when there is progressive marrow failure (severe aplastic anaemia, transfusion dependence, or high-grade cytopenias refractory to supportive care). Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) may be curative for the marrow component but requires special considerations in TBDs: standard myeloablative conditioning causes excessive toxicity in patients with short telomeres. Reduced‑intensity conditioning (RIC) regimens tailored to minimize pulmonary and hepatic toxicity are therefore preferred, and transplant teams experienced in inherited bone marrow failure syndromes should be involved. Donor selection must exclude related donors who may carry the same pathogenic variant; genetic testing of potential related donors is mandatory. Patients with significant pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis have higher transplant-related morbidity and mortality, so timing of referral—before end‑organ deterioration—can be critical.

Pulmonary management and transplant considerations
Pulmonary involvement in PARN-related disease commonly manifests as pulmonary fibrosis or an idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) phenotype. Baseline assessment should include high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) with spirometry and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO). Serial monitoring is guided by symptoms and baseline severity: in patients with evidence of ILD or symptoms, PFTs every 3–6 months and HRCT every 6–12 months (or sooner if decline is suspected) are reasonable; in stable, asymptomatic patients intervals may be extended to 6–12 months.

Antifibrotic therapy used for IPF (nintedanib or pirfenidone) can be considered for progressive fibrotic disease, recognizing that evidence specific to TBDs is limited and the benefit is extrapolated from IPF trials. Pulmonary transplant is an option for end‑stage disease, but outcomes in short‑telomere syndromes are more complicated: patients are at risk of bone marrow dysfunction, delayed wound healing, and other organ complications after transplant. Pre‑transplant identification of a telomere disorder alters candidacy assessment and perioperative planning. Multidisciplinary discussion between pulmonology, transplant surgery, haematology and hepatology is required.

Hepatic management
Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are increasingly recognized in TBDs. Baseline liver assessment should include noninvasive fibrosis staging (transient elastography), liver biochemistry, and imaging. Serial elastography and clinical review every 6–12 months are reasonable to detect progression; more frequent follow-up is required for decompensation. Screen for portal hypertension with platelet counts, abdominal ultrasound, and consider endoscopic variceal screening when clinical indicators arise (thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly, signs of portal hypertension). Avoidance of hepatotoxins (anabolic-androgenic steroids unless risks are justified and closely monitored, unnecessary medications with known hepatic injury) and counselling about alcohol abstinence are essential. In patients who develop decompensated cirrhosis, evaluation for liver transplantation should be performed, with recognition that coexisting pulmonary or haematologic disease may complicate candidacy and outcomes.

Potential disease‑modifying therapies
Androgens (danazol, oxymetholone, nandrolone) have shown haematologic responses and, in some reports, telomere length stabilization in patients with TBDs and progressive cytopenias. Indications typically include symptomatic or progressive cytopenias when HSCT is not immediately undertaken. Use requires careful patient selection and monitoring: baseline and serial liver function tests, lipid profile, coagulation studies, and surveillance for virilisation and thromboembolic events. Importantly, androgens can be hepatotoxic and should be used cautiously or avoided in patients with advanced hepatic fibrosis or active liver disease; multidisciplinary input is necessary.

Other investigational approaches to ameliorate telomere dysfunction are under study (telomerase activators, gene‑based therapies), but these remain experimental and not standard of care. Enrollment in clinical trials at specialised centres should be considered when available.

Risk mitigation and infection prevention
Because individuals with TBDs are vulnerable to organ toxicity and marrow suppression, avoid myelotoxic medications and unnecessary ionizing radiation when possible. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy plans should be modified in consultation with specialists. Smoking cessation and avoidance of environmental lung toxins are imperative to slow ILD progression. Vaccination against influenza, pneumococcus and other relevant pathogens is recommended; consider prophylactic antimicrobials for severe, chronic neutropenia on a case‑by‑case basis. Agents such as granulocyte‑colony stimulating factor (G‑CSF) may be used transiently for severe neutropenia, but their role is supportive rather than disease‑modifying.

Genetic counselling, family testing and reproductive options
Identification of a monoallelic PARN variant warrants genetic counselling for the patient and at‑risk relatives. Monoallelic variants behave with variable penetrance and may present as pulmonary fibrosis, marrow failure or a mixed phenotype; counselling should address uncertainty in penetrance and prognosis. Cascade testing of family members — and exclusion of related donors for HSCT — is important. Reproductive options include prenatal testing and preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is established; in the case of a variant of uncertain significance, counselling should emphasize the limits of prediction. Psychosocial support and linkage to patient support groups or specialty clinics (telomere disorders/bone marrow failure registries) can help with coping and long‑term care planning.

Prognosis and application to this patient
Prognosis in PARN‑related TBDs is highly variable and driven by the degree and progression of marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic involvement. Some patients have indolent disease for years; others progress to life‑threatening organ failure. The patient described in this report showed symptomatic and haematologic improvement after correction of vitamin B12 and other supportive measures, which confirms that reversible factors contributed substantially to her presentation. Nevertheless, the presence of pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis and a monoallelic PARN variant confer an ongoing risk of progressive organ failure, marrow failure, and malignancy.

For practical follow‑up in this patient, a reasonable surveillance plan would include regular CBCs with reticulocyte counts every 3 months (more frequently if unstable), PFTs with DLCO every 3–6 months and chest imaging (HRCT) at least annually or sooner with decline, transient elastography and liver biochemical monitoring every 6–12 months, bone density follow‑up per osteoporosis guidelines, and age‑appropriate cancer surveillance. Early discussion with an HSCT centre is prudent so that escalating marrow failure can be managed expeditiously with appropriately tailored conditioning and donor selection. Measurement of telomere length (flow‑FISH) may be useful as a baseline prognostic marker and for family assessment.

In summary, management of PARN-related TBD combines treatment of reversible causes and vigilant, organ‑directed surveillance with early multidisciplinary involvement to time disease‑modifying interventions appropriately. The patient’s early improvement is encouraging, but lifelong, proactive monitoring and counselling remain central to her care.
