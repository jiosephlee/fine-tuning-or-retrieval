Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a post–cardiopulmonary bypass patient whose platelet count falls from 210×10^9/L preoperatively to 50×10^9/L by postoperative day 5, what specific preoperative exposure most increases the pretest probability of HIT when thrombocytopenia begins before POD5, and why does its timing matter?

Short answer: recent preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin — for example the intra‑procedural UFH given during the diagnostic cardiac catheterization one week before surgery — most increases the pretest probability of HIT when thrombocytopenia begins before POD5.

Why: HIT is an immune reaction against PF4/heparin complexes that usually takes 5–10 days to appear after a first exposure. If a patient has been exposed to heparin in the days to weeks before surgery, anti‑PF4/heparin IgG may already be circulating (or the immune system is primed). Re‑exposure to heparin (as during CPB) therefore produces a rapid, “early‑onset” fall in platelets—earlier than the usual POD5–10 window. Unfractionated heparin is particularly immunogenic (higher risk than LMWH), and even small intra‑procedural doses (the 2000 U given at catheterization in this case) can prime or provoke antibody formation. 

Clinical pearl from the case: the patient’s thrombocytopenia beginning before POD5 was explained by the 1‑week preoperative UFH during catheterization; that historical detail meaningfully raised the pretest probability for HIT and justified prompt HIT testing and switching to non‑heparin anticoagulation once antibodies were confirmed. Always ask explicitly about any heparin exposure in the days–weeks before surgery when early postoperative thrombocytopenia appears.
