Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 1: ## Perioperative Presentation and Clinical Course

The patient’s clinical course began with a potentially important sensitizing heparin exposure. One week before surgery he underwent transradial diagnostic coronary angiography during which he received 2,000 U of unfractionated heparin while still taking apixaban. He then proceeded to a planned open-heart operation seven days later that required cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and a standard intraoperative bolus of 45,000 U of unfractionated heparin. The operation (aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic graft and single bypass) was uncomplicated and the patient came off bypass with good hemodynamics and no major bleeding or transfusion requirements.

Cardiac surgery with CPB virtually always produces an early, predictable fall in platelet count related to hemodilution, platelet activation/consumption on artificial surfaces and sequestration; typically the nadir is seen immediately postoperatively and counts recover by about postoperative day (POD) 5. In this patient the immediate preoperative platelet count was 210 × 10^9/L and the POD1 count was 155 × 10^9/L, an expected early decline after CPB. What became concerning, however, was the subsequent trend: instead of recovering, the platelet count continued to fall progressively to 50 × 10^9/L by POD5 and then to the mid‑20s on POD6–7, with a nadir of 13 × 10^9/L noted on POD7. This magnitude (≈75% fall from baseline) and persistence of thrombocytopenia beyond the usual post‑CPB recovery window deviated from the expected postoperative course and should prompt consideration of pathologic causes.

Routine postoperative thromboprophylaxis was relevant to the unfolding picture. Dalteparin (subcutaneous low‑molecular‑weight heparin) was given on POD1–4, and was withheld on POD5 because of the falling platelet count; prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started late on POD5. Exposure to further heparin (LMWH) in the early postoperative days is clinically important because, in a patient already primed by a recent heparin exposure (here the angiography 7 days earlier), re‑exposure during surgery and in the postoperative period can lead to an accelerated or “early‑onset” immune response. Thus the timing—preoperative cath heparin, large intraoperative UFH dose, and postoperative LMWH—creates the immunologic backdrop for heparin‑induced antibody formation and an early HIT presentation.

Neurologic change was the first major clinical sign that this thrombocytopenia was not simply expected post‑CPB physiology. The patient developed worsening postoperative delirium, which by itself is common after cardiac surgery; however, the delirium progressed despite otherwise stable hemodynamics and coincided with a sharp fall in platelets (to 24–26 × 10^9/L). This prompted neuroimaging on POD6, which revealed an acute left parietal infarct. The occurrence of an arterial thrombosis (stroke) in the setting of new, progressive thrombocytopenia strongly pointed toward a prothrombotic cause such as HIT rather than isolated platelet loss from dilution or consumption without thrombosis.

Initial laboratory data helped narrow the differential. On POD5 the hemoglobin was 97 g/L, consistent with expected perioperative loss but without ongoing hemorrhage, and the white count was normal. Markers of hemolysis were absent (reticulocyte count unremarkable; haptoglobin elevated rather than decreased), and coagulation studies showed a normal PT/INR and PTT; fibrinogen was elevated (6.6 g/L), an acute‑phase finding typical after major surgery. Examination of a peripheral smear on POD7 confirmed true thrombocytopenia without platelet clumping and—at that early stage—without schistocytes, arguing against immediate microangiopathic hemolysis as the explanation. Taken together, these findings made common postoperative causes of thrombocytopenia (hemodilution, transient CPB‑related drop, infection, transfusion reactions, or early TTP) unlikely as sole explanations for the progressive and profound platelet loss.

It was the combination of an atypical temporal pattern for post‑CPB thrombocytopenia (progressive decline rather than recovery), the magnitude of the platelet fall (>50% from baseline to very low absolute values), recent heparin exposures (angiography and intraoperative UFH plus postoperative LMWH), and the development of an acute ischemic stroke that first raised strong clinical suspicion for heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia. Because this presentation was earlier and more severe than usual for classic HIT after cardiac surgery, autoimmune HIT (aHIT) was also in the differential from the outset: aHIT can present with heparin‑independent platelet activation and very profound thrombocytopenia, and recent heparin exposure explains why onset occurred before the usual POD5–10 window. These clinical clues led to sending HIT testing (PF4/polyanion immunoassay and functional platelet activation testing) and to early modification of anticoagulation and monitoring decisions described later in the report.
