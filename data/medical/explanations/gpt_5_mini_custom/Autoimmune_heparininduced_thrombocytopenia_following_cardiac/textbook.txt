Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Perioperative Presentation and Clinical Course

The patient’s clinical course began with a potentially important sensitizing heparin exposure. One week before surgery he underwent transradial diagnostic coronary angiography during which he received 2,000 U of unfractionated heparin while still taking apixaban. He then proceeded to a planned open-heart operation seven days later that required cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and a standard intraoperative bolus of 45,000 U of unfractionated heparin. The operation (aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic graft and single bypass) was uncomplicated and the patient came off bypass with good hemodynamics and no major bleeding or transfusion requirements.

Cardiac surgery with CPB virtually always produces an early, predictable fall in platelet count related to hemodilution, platelet activation/consumption on artificial surfaces and sequestration; typically the nadir is seen immediately postoperatively and counts recover by about postoperative day (POD) 5. In this patient the immediate preoperative platelet count was 210 × 10^9/L and the POD1 count was 155 × 10^9/L, an expected early decline after CPB. What became concerning, however, was the subsequent trend: instead of recovering, the platelet count continued to fall progressively to 50 × 10^9/L by POD5 and then to the mid‑20s on POD6–7, with a nadir of 13 × 10^9/L noted on POD7. This magnitude (≈75% fall from baseline) and persistence of thrombocytopenia beyond the usual post‑CPB recovery window deviated from the expected postoperative course and should prompt consideration of pathologic causes.

Routine postoperative thromboprophylaxis was relevant to the unfolding picture. Dalteparin (subcutaneous low‑molecular‑weight heparin) was given on POD1–4, and was withheld on POD5 because of the falling platelet count; prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started late on POD5. Exposure to further heparin (LMWH) in the early postoperative days is clinically important because, in a patient already primed by a recent heparin exposure (here the angiography 7 days earlier), re‑exposure during surgery and in the postoperative period can lead to an accelerated or “early‑onset” immune response. Thus the timing—preoperative cath heparin, large intraoperative UFH dose, and postoperative LMWH—creates the immunologic backdrop for heparin‑induced antibody formation and an early HIT presentation.

Neurologic change was the first major clinical sign that this thrombocytopenia was not simply expected post‑CPB physiology. The patient developed worsening postoperative delirium, which by itself is common after cardiac surgery; however, the delirium progressed despite otherwise stable hemodynamics and coincided with a sharp fall in platelets (to 24–26 × 10^9/L). This prompted neuroimaging on POD6, which revealed an acute left parietal infarct. The occurrence of an arterial thrombosis (stroke) in the setting of new, progressive thrombocytopenia strongly pointed toward a prothrombotic cause such as HIT rather than isolated platelet loss from dilution or consumption without thrombosis.

Initial laboratory data helped narrow the differential. On POD5 the hemoglobin was 97 g/L, consistent with expected perioperative loss but without ongoing hemorrhage, and the white count was normal. Markers of hemolysis were absent (reticulocyte count unremarkable; haptoglobin elevated rather than decreased), and coagulation studies showed a normal PT/INR and PTT; fibrinogen was elevated (6.6 g/L), an acute‑phase finding typical after major surgery. Examination of a peripheral smear on POD7 confirmed true thrombocytopenia without platelet clumping and—at that early stage—without schistocytes, arguing against immediate microangiopathic hemolysis as the explanation. Taken together, these findings made common postoperative causes of thrombocytopenia (hemodilution, transient CPB‑related drop, infection, transfusion reactions, or early TTP) unlikely as sole explanations for the progressive and profound platelet loss.

It was the combination of an atypical temporal pattern for post‑CPB thrombocytopenia (progressive decline rather than recovery), the magnitude of the platelet fall (>50% from baseline to very low absolute values), recent heparin exposures (angiography and intraoperative UFH plus postoperative LMWH), and the development of an acute ischemic stroke that first raised strong clinical suspicion for heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia. Because this presentation was earlier and more severe than usual for classic HIT after cardiac surgery, autoimmune HIT (aHIT) was also in the differential from the outset: aHIT can present with heparin‑independent platelet activation and very profound thrombocytopenia, and recent heparin exposure explains why onset occurred before the usual POD5–10 window. These clinical clues led to sending HIT testing (PF4/polyanion immunoassay and functional platelet activation testing) and to early modification of anticoagulation and monitoring decisions described later in the report.

## Mechanisms and Phenotypes: Classic HIT versus Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an immune‑mediated complication in which antibodies target complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to polyanions such as heparin. In the canonical or “classic” model, PF4 is released from platelet alpha granules when platelets are activated. PF4 is highly cationic and, when it binds to an anionic glycosaminoglycan such as heparin, forms multimolecular complexes that expose neoepitopes. IgG antibodies directed against these PF4/heparin complexes form immune complexes that crosslink FcγRIIa receptors on platelets. FcγRIIa clustering triggers potent platelet activation, aggregation and procoagulant microparticle release, with downstream thrombin generation. Activated platelets and thrombin convert hemostasis into a marked prothrombotic state — explaining why HIT is paradoxically associated more with thrombosis than bleeding despite thrombocytopenia. In addition to platelets, PF4/antibody complexes can activate monocytes (upregulating tissue factor) and injure endothelium, further amplifying thrombosis.

Cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is a particularly high‑risk setting for HIT for several mechanistic reasons that are directly relevant to this case. CPB and the surgical procedure cause substantial platelet activation and degranulation (thus increased PF4 release), a systemic inflammatory response, and exposure of blood to artificial surfaces that promote PF4 binding and complex formation. In addition, cardiac surgery patients routinely receive large intraoperative doses of unfractionated heparin (UFH) and postoperative heparin or low‑molecular‑weight heparin, providing the polyanion partner for PF4 to form the antigenic complexes. These features increase both the likelihood of generating pathogenic anti‑PF4 antibodies and the clinical consequences when such antibodies arise.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) refers to a subset of HIT in which the patient’s antibodies can activate platelets independently of exogenous heparin. In aHIT, antibodies recognize PF4 alone or PF4 complexes that are stable without added heparin, so patient serum can cause platelet activation even when no heparin is present. Clinically, aHIT tends to be more severe: platelet counts are often lower, thrombosis risk and consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes) are greater, and antibody effects are more persistent. The heparin‑independent activity also explains why stopping heparin alone may be insufficient to immediately reverse the process.

Early‑onset HIT (platelet fall before post‑operative day 5 in cardiac surgery patients) is best explained by an anamnestic immune response after recent prior heparin exposure. Memory B cells primed by a recent heparin exposure (for example, UFH given during a preoperative catheterization) can rapidly produce IgG on re‑exposure, so immune complex–mediated platelet activation and thrombocytopenia occur earlier than the usual 5–10 day interval needed for a primary response. The present case illustrates this mechanism: the patient’s intraprocedural UFH during the heart catheterization a week before surgery provided the priming exposure that allowed the postoperative antibody–mediated platelet fall to begin before POD5.

Understanding the laboratory assays helps link serology to these pathophysiologic phenotypes. The PF4/polyanion ELISA is a screening immunoassay that detects antibodies binding PF4/polyanion complexes regardless of whether they are functionally platelet‑activating. A high optical density (OD) in the ELISA correlates with a larger antibody burden and increases the pretest probability that antibodies are clinically significant. The functional serotonin‑release assay (SRA) tests whether patient antibodies actually activate platelets: donor platelets labeled with radiolabeled serotonin are exposed to patient serum with varying heparin concentrations, and serotonin release indicates platelet activation. In classic HIT, the SRA classically shows little activation at 0 U/mL heparin, strong activation at low (therapeutic) heparin concentrations (eg, 0.1–0.3 U/mL), and inhibition of activation at very high heparin concentrations (eg, 100 U/mL) because excess heparin disrupts the multimeric PF4 complexes needed for antibody binding. In aHIT, by contrast, strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL (heparin‑independent activation) is a defining feature.

This case’s serology illustrates an extreme aHIT phenotype. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2), consistent with a high burden of anti‑PF4 antibodies. The SRA showed robust platelet activation at 0, 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin and the expected inhibition at 100 U/mL; the activation at 0 U/mL identifies heparin‑independent (autoimmune) activity. A further unusual laboratory finding was only partial inhibition of SRA activity by the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3. In typical HIT, blockade of FcγRIIa abolishes antibody‑mediated platelet activation in the SRA, confirming that Fc receptor crosslinking is the dominant mechanism. Partial resistance to IV.3, as seen here, implies an unusually strong or multivalent interaction between antibodies and platelets: possible explanations include very high‑avidity IgG, large immune complex formation that overwhelms the blocking antibody, or engagement of additional platelet‑activating pathways. The clinical correlate was an unusually severe, persistent thrombocytopenia, partial biochemical signs of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation, and a blunted platelet recovery after high‑dose IVIG — all features compatible with refractory aHIT.

Finally, the evolution of antibody phenotype in this patient illustrates the dynamic nature of the response. Over weeks to months, the heparin‑independent activating activity waned: later SRA profiles showed the classic pattern of heparin dependence (loss of activation at 0 U/mL with activation at 0.3 U/mL), reflecting decline of the aHIT‑type antibodies and emergence or persistence of more conventional heparin‑dependent antibodies. This serologic evolution paralleled clinical recovery of platelet counts and resolution of neurologic injury, underscoring how shifting immunologic phenotypes map to the clinical course in HIT and aHIT.

In summary, classic HIT depends on PF4/heparin complexes and FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation, producing a prothrombotic state; aHIT represents antibodies that can activate platelets without heparin and therefore tend to cause earlier, more severe, and more persistent disease. The patient’s preoperative heparin exposure explains the early onset, and the combination of high ELISA OD, heparin‑independent SRA activation, and partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade documents an unusually intense aHIT serologic phenotype that accounts for the severe clinical course and modest initial response to therapies such as IVIG.

## Diagnostic Reasoning in Early Post-Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia

Evaluating thrombocytopenia after cardiopulmonary bypass requires a structured approach that separates the expected, transient platelet changes of surgery from immune-mediated processes such as heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Cardiopulmonary bypass typically causes an immediate postoperative platelet decline because of hemodilution, platelet activation and sequestration on extracorporeal surfaces; platelet counts commonly fall to a nadir on POD1–2 and then recover over the first week. By contrast, HIT classically produces a second, delayed decline in platelets beginning about POD5–10 (the “biphasic” pattern): an initial post‑CPB drop followed by recovery and then a new fall driven by anti‑PF4/heparin antibodies. Recognizing this timing pattern is central to distinguishing routine post‑operative thrombocytopenia from HIT, but the pattern is not absolute—recent heparin exposure can cause earlier HIT onset, and many non‑immune causes of platelet fall complicate interpretation after heart surgery.

Common diagnostic algorithms such as the 4Ts (Thrombocytopenia degree, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher causes) remain useful but have important limitations after cardiac surgery. The specificity of the 4Ts score is reduced because most cardiac surgery patients have an early platelet fall (lowering the “Timing” and “Other causes” discrimination), and postoperative conditions that mimic thrombosis (for example, delirium or stroke masked by sedation) may be difficult to interpret. Thus, the 4Ts should be applied cautiously, with a low threshold to pursue laboratory testing when clinical features raise concern (notably a >50% platelet fall, new thrombosis, or very low nadir counts).

The differential diagnosis in the postoperative cardiac patient is broad. Sepsis or severe infection causes thrombocytopenia through consumption and bone marrow suppression; disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) produces falling fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer and schistocytes; cardiogenic shock and severe illness cause platelet consumption and hemodilution; drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP) may occur from perioperative antibiotics or other agents and typically has an abrupt fall soon after drug exposure; post‑transfusion purpura (PTP) and thrombotic microangiopathies such as TTP are other rare causes—PTP requires recent transfusion and TTP is suggested by microangiopathic hemolysis, schistocytes, and severe organ dysfunction. Ongoing bleeding or surgical consumption typically produces falling hemoglobin and hemodynamic instability in addition to thrombocytopenia. Each competing diagnosis has specific clinical or laboratory signatures that guide exclusion: hemodynamic stability and negative cultures argue against shock or sepsis; normal haptoglobin and absence of schistocytes argue against hemolysis or TTP early on; stable hemoglobin and no transfusions argue against bleeding or PTP; and an elevated fibrinogen (an acute‑phase response) argues against fulminant consumption as seen in overt DIC at that time point.

Applying these principles to the presented patient clarifies why HIT rose to the top of the differential despite some atypical features. Clinically he was hemodynamically stable with normal perfusion, blood and urine cultures were negative, and no perioperative transfusions were given—findings that make sepsis, cardiogenic shock and PTP unlikely. The early laboratory profile showed a normal reticulocyte count, elevated (not low) haptoglobin and, initially, a peripheral smear without schistocytes; hemoglobin remained at expected postoperative levels, arguing against ongoing bleeding, hemolysis or TTP. The elevated fibrinogen was consistent with a postoperative acute‑phase response rather than consumptive coagulopathy at that time. Together, these data reduced the likelihood of most alternative explanations and focused attention on an immune cause.

Timing of heparin exposure was the key to resolving the apparent temporal mismatch. Although the intraoperative UFH exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass is usually too near the surgery to produce HIT within the first few postoperative days, the patient had been exposed to UFH (2,000 U) during a transradial heart catheterization one week before surgery. Recent heparin exposure is a well‑recognized precipitant of earlier or “rapid‑onset” HIT: patients who are sensitized by prior heparin within the preceding 30 days can develop antibody‑mediated platelet activation at a time that, superficially, appears earlier than the usual POD5–10 window. Recognition of that preoperative exposure changed the timing interpretation and increased pretest probability for HIT.

Laboratory testing then provided two complementary pieces of evidence. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2), and high OD values strongly suggest the presence of pathogenic anti‑PF4 antibodies rather than low‑titer, clinically irrelevant antibodies frequently seen after surgery. However, immunoassays are sensitive but not entirely specific, particularly after CPB when many patients form anti‑PF4 antibodies without clinical HIT; therefore a functional platelet activation assay is required to confirm that antibodies are platelet‑activating.

The serotonin‑release assay (SRA) reported an “indeterminate” result, but careful interpretation showed that the pattern was actually diagnostic of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In classic heparin‑dependent HIT, the SRA shows platelet activation only in the presence of low therapeutic heparin concentrations (eg, 0.1–0.3 U/mL) and is inhibited at very high heparin concentrations because excess heparin prevents pathogenic PF4/heparin complex formation. Importantly, activation is absent when no heparin is added. In this patient the SRA demonstrated very strong serotonin release at 0.1–0.3 U/mL (consistent with HIT) but also strong release at 0 U/mL (heparin‑independent activation), a hallmark of aHIT. In addition, addition of the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 incompletely suppressed activation; most HIT antibodies are fully blocked by IV.3 in vitro, so incomplete inhibition suggested that the patient’s antibodies were unusually potent or used partially Fc‑independent mechanisms of platelet activation. Rather than excluding HIT, this “atypical” SRA pattern, together with the high ELISA OD and compatible clinical picture, strongly supported a diagnosis of aHIT.

Because functional serology can evolve, repeat and serial testing are important. In this case subsequent samples over weeks showed changing SRA profiles—initially dominated by heparin‑independent activation and later reverting toward a heparin‑dependent pattern as aHIT antibodies waned—correlating with the clinical course and platelet recovery. This temporal evolution both confirmed the diagnosis and documented the expected serologic decline.

Finally, the occurrence of an ischemic stroke on POD6 is a critical piece of the diagnostic puzzle. HIT is prothrombotic and paradoxically more often causes thrombosis than bleeding; new arterial or venous thrombosis substantially increases the post‑test probability that anti‑PF4 antibodies are clinically significant. The brain infarct therefore strengthened the clinical diagnosis and justified urgent management decisions even in the setting of an unconventional SRA result.

In sum, diagnostic reasoning in this patient required integrating the expected postoperative platelet dynamics, careful exclusion of competing causes by clinical and laboratory data, recognition of an antecedent heparin exposure that explained early timing, and nuanced interpretation of immunologic and functional assays. The constellation of a very high‑titer PF4 ELISA, heparin‑independent platelet activation with partial resistance to Fc blockade on SRA, the temporal evolution of serology, and a new thrombotic event together established autoimmune HIT as the leading and ultimately confirmed diagnosis.

## Management of Severe aHIT with Arterial Thrombosis

Immediate management priorities in this case were to stop all heparin exposures, assess bleeding risk, and institute effective non‑heparin anticoagulation while limiting interventions that could worsen antibody‑driven platelet activation. As soon as HIT was suspected because of the postoperative platelet fall, strong PF4/polyanion ELISA and the new cerebral infarct, all heparin products and LMWH were discontinued. Platelet transfusions were avoided because transfused platelets can provide additional substrate for HIT immune complexes and worsen thrombosis; they are reserved only for life‑threatening hemorrhage or urgent procedures that cannot be deferred.

Choice of non‑heparin anticoagulant was guided by two competing needs: rapid control of hypercoagulability and the ability to rapidly reduce anticoagulant effect if hemorrhagic transformation of the stroke or major bleeding occurred. The treating team selected argatroban, a parenteral direct thrombin inhibitor with a short half‑life and easy titration by aPTT, allowing prompt adjustment. In this patient argatroban was started at 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a target aPTT range of 55–85 s, reflecting therapeutic anticoagulation chosen to treat arterial thrombosis while permitting prompt discontinuation if bleeding developed. When using argatroban in HIT, clinicians must monitor aPTT and liver function (hepatic clearance), and be aware there is no specific reversal agent; its short half‑life is the main safety feature.

Adjunctive high‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was used because autoimmune HIT (aHIT) antibodies can activate platelets independently of heparin and often respond to immune modulation. Mechanistically, high‑dose IVIG competitively saturates FcγRIIa on platelets and other effector cells and can interrupt Fc‑mediated platelet activation by pathogenic IgG. In this case the team administered large doses (100 g on two consecutive days, followed by a third 100 g dose) because of severe thrombocytopenia and ongoing thrombosis. Clinically, a brisk platelet rise is commonly seen after IVIG in aHIT, but the response can be attenuated in very severe antibody phenotypes; this patient had only a modest platelet increase (from 13×10^9/L to an interim 54×10^9/L), indicating unusually refractory disease.

Because aHIT commonly produces a consumptive, hypercoagulable state, serial laboratory monitoring was used to assess ongoing coagulation activation and treatment adequacy rather than relying solely on platelet counts. In this patient the treating team followed fibrinogen (which fell from an elevated postoperative level toward nadir 2.49 g/L by POD17), d‑dimer (peaked ~POD12), and peripheral blood films that began showing schistocytes from POD9–16. A falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer and new schistocytosis despite anticoagulation suggested ongoing thrombin generation and microangiopathic red‑cell injury from uncontrolled HIT‑associated coagulation, and therefore argued for reassessment of anticoagulant strategy.

Because the above markers indicated persistent hypercoagulability on argatroban, the team pivoted to fondaparinux on POD14 (therapeutic dose 10 mg once daily). The reasons were twofold: concern that argatroban was not adequately suppressing thrombin generation in this case, and practical advantages (once‑daily subcutaneous dosing that facilitates outpatient management). Fondaparinux is an indirect, AT‑dependent factor Xa inhibitor that has been used off‑label in HIT with good efficacy in many reports. Its disadvantages include renal clearance and a long half‑life without an approved reversal agent, so renal function must be considered when choosing dosing. In this patient fondaparinux was preferred despite those drawbacks because it offered a different mechanism of anticoagulation and easier long‑term administration while the patient’s clinical picture stabilized.

Because fondaparinux requires antithrombin (AT) for activity, the team measured AT and found it mildly reduced (0.70 U/mL; RR 0.80–1.40). To optimize fondaparinux efficacy, they administered 1000 U of AT concentrate prior to the first therapeutic fondaparinux dose. This step illustrates an important principle when using AT‑dependent agents in patients with consumptive coagulopathy or low AT levels: correcting AT can be necessary to achieve the expected anticoagulant effect.

Stroke management in the setting of HIT requires careful balance between preventing further arterial thrombosis and avoiding hemorrhagic transformation. The clinicians favored an anticoagulant strategy that could be rapidly stopped or adjusted if bleeding developed (hence argatroban initially). When the clinical picture later suggested inadequate control of coagulation, they accepted the trade‑off of switching to fondaparinux for better long‑term suppression of thrombin generation, monitoring closely for bleeding. Decisions about timing and intensity of anticoagulation in stroke with HIT must be individualized, taking infarct size, evolving neurological status, and imaging evidence of hemorrhagic conversion into account; in this patient a 3.2 cm parietal infarct was managed with therapeutic anticoagulation without hemorrhagic complication.

Duration and transitions of therapy were determined by platelet recovery and clinical stabilization. In this case fondaparinux was continued until the platelet count had recovered (platelets normalized by POD62) and the patient had stabilized; fondaparinux was stopped on POD65 and long‑term oral anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation (apixaban 5 mg twice daily) was restarted. Serial functional assays (SRA) remained positive for several months despite clinical recovery, underscoring that serological tests may lag behind clinical improvement and that management decisions are guided primarily by clinical status and platelet recovery.

Key practical takeaways from this case: stop all heparin immediately; avoid platelet transfusion unless absolutely necessary; choose a non‑heparin agent that matches the clinical needs (short half‑life and titratability for high bleeding‑risk situations such as recent stroke, or longer‑acting agents for convenience and sustained anticoagulation); use IVIG for severe aHIT to interrupt FcγRIIa‑mediated activation; monitor markers of consumptive coagulopathy (fibrinogen, d‑dimer, peripheral smear) as adjuncts to platelet counts; and be prepared to change anticoagulant strategy if laboratory or clinical evidence suggests ongoing hypercoagulability. In this patient, that staged approach—argatroban plus IVIG, followed by fondaparinux with AT supplementation and continued monitoring—was associated with eventual platelet recovery and full neurological recovery.

## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons

Outcome and recovery in this case were favorable but prolonged. The patient’s platelet count, which had nadired at 13×10^9/L, recovered slowly over weeks to 155×10^9/L by postoperative day (POD) 62 and to 202×10^9/L by POD87. His neurological deficit from the left parietal infarct also resolved with time. Clinically, recovery required sustained non‑heparin anticoagulation and supportive care while pathogenic antibodies declined.

Serial functional testing reflected the biological course of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Early sera produced strong serotonin release even without added heparin (0 U/mL), and this hyper‑reactivity persisted on functional assays for several months. Over time the serology evolved: later samples showed a diminishing heparin‑independent signal and ultimately a pattern more typical of heparin‑dependent HIT (minimal release at 0 U/mL but strong release with therapeutic heparin concentrations). This trajectory—initial dominant heparin‑independent platelet activation with gradual waning toward heparin dependence—illustrates that aHIT antibodies can persist for weeks to months and that repeat functional testing can help document antibody evolution and guide timing of therapy changes.

The long‑term anticoagulation plan in this case balanced stroke risk, ongoing thrombosis risk from HIT, and bleeding concerns. Fondaparinux was continued until platelet recovery (stopped at POD65), after which the patient was transitioned back to his baseline oral anticoagulant, apixaban. Important practical steps were taken and should be taken for all patients with HIT/aHIT: avoidance of any future heparin exposure, clear documentation of heparin allergy/HIT in the medical record, and patient education (written alert or card) so perioperative teams and emergency clinicians will avoid heparin in the future.

Clinical pearls from this case
- Early‑onset postoperative thrombocytopenia (before POD5) after cardiac surgery should raise suspicion for HIT/aHIT if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure (for example, catheterization or dialysis). The usual 5–10 day window can be shifted earlier by recent sensitizing heparin exposure.
- aHIT often presents with more severe thrombocytopenia than classic HIT and can be associated with laboratory evidence of consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, appearance of schistocytes) despite anticoagulation—findings that should prompt escalation of therapy and closer monitoring.
- Do not dismiss a strong positive immunoassay (ELISA) plus atypical functional testing as “indeterminate” without integrating the clinical picture; in a patient with thrombosis and compatible timing, an indeterminate SRA that shows heparin‑independent activation is highly suggestive of aHIT.

Laboratory nuance and interpretation
- A very strong PF4/polyanion ELISA (high optical density) supports the presence of pathologic anti‑PF4 antibodies but does not by itself distinguish heparin dependence versus autoantibody behavior. Functional platelet activation assays (eg, SRA) are required for confirmation and to characterize heparin dependence.
- In aHIT the SRA often shows substantial serotonin release even at 0 U/mL heparin; classic HIT shows release only at therapeutic heparin concentrations and inhibition at very high heparin. This case demonstrated that aHIT antibodies can be so potent that platelet activation is only partially inhibited by an FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3), producing an “indeterminate” pattern. Recognize that incomplete FcγRIIa blockade in vitro does not exclude immune HIT: it may reflect unusually strong or multiple mechanisms of platelet activation and should prompt further reference‑lab evaluation rather than dismissal.
- When HIT/aHIT is suspected, request a complete functional panel (0 U/mL, therapeutic heparin concentrations, high heparin, and FcγRIIa blockade) from an experienced reference laboratory, and consider serial testing to follow antibody evolution.

Therapeutic insights and monitoring
- Immediate cessation of all heparin products and initiation of a non‑heparin parenteral anticoagulant (eg, argatroban, bivalirudin, or fondaparinux off‑label) is essential when HIT/aHIT is suspected and thrombosis is present or the probability is high. Choice of agent should account for bleeding risk, renal/hepatic function, and practical considerations (argatroban’s short half‑life and PTT titration vs fondaparinux’s once‑daily dosing).
- High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is often effective in aHIT because it competes for Fc receptors and can rapidly blunt platelet activation; however, response can be variable. In this patient IVIG produced only a modest platelet rise, underscoring that some aHIT cases are relatively refractory and may require continued alternative anticoagulation and close monitoring.
- Monitor not only platelet counts but also markers of consumptive coagulopathy (fibrinogen, D‑dimer) and peripheral smear for schistocytes; falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, or schistocytes despite anticoagulation indicate ongoing uncontrolled thrombin generation and may prompt change of anticoagulant or additional immunomodulation.
- Be prepared to switch anticoagulants if laboratory or clinical markers suggest inadequate control of hypercoagulability (as in this case, where argatroban was changed to fondaparinux). When using AT‑dependent agents such as fondaparinux in the setting of low antithrombin, consider AT supplementation.

When to involve specialists and reference laboratories
- Early hematology consultation is warranted whenever HIT is suspected—particularly with atypical features, severe thrombocytopenia, thrombosis, or poor response to initial therapy. Hematology guides choice and dosing of non‑heparin anticoagulants, IVIG use, and transition to long‑term anticoagulation.
- Send samples to an experienced platelet immunology reference laboratory for comprehensive functional testing (SRA with multiple heparin concentrations and FcγRIIa blockade) and for serial testing to document the waning of pathogenic antibodies. Results from reference labs can be critical to resolve “indeterminate” local assays and to tailor therapy.
- Coordinate care with surgery, neurology (for stroke), transfusion medicine, and pharmacy when managing bleeding risk, transfusion decisions, and complex anticoagulant changes.

In summary, this case illustrates that aHIT after cardiac surgery can present early, be severely thrombocytopenic, provoke consumptive coagulopathy, and show prolonged heparin‑independent serologic activity. Recognition requires integration of timing, clinical thrombosis, strong immunoassay results, and a nuanced interpretation of functional assays. Management combines prompt heparin avoidance, appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulation, consideration of IVIG, frequent laboratory monitoring, involvement of hematology, and explicit long‑term documentation to prevent future heparin exposure.
