Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Anticoagulating the New Stroke in HIT: Walking the Tightrope

This case is a textbook example of the worst‑case scenario that hematology and neuro teams dread: an autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) after cardiac surgery, an acute ischemic stroke on postoperative day (POD) 6, and a platelet count in the 20s (nadir 13×10^9/L). The patient’s serology showed strong PF4/polyanion ELISA positivity and heparin‑independent platelet activation on the serotonin‑release assay — the kind of antibody that drives relentless thrombosis. The clinical challenge is immediate and stark: do you anticoagulate to stop the clotting storm, risking hemorrhagic transformation of the cerebral infarct — or do you withhold anticoagulation and let the thrombotic momentum of HIT ripen further?

The team in this report chose a rational middle path: immediate therapeutic non‑heparin anticoagulation with a short‑half‑life direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban), aggressive measures to block immune platelet activation (high‑dose IVIG), close neurologic surveillance and imaging, and step‑down to fondaparinux once initial control was achieved. The patient ultimately recovered neurologically and the platelet count normalized — but only after a prolonged course and careful balancing of risks.

## Why this is different from “routine” ischemic stroke
- HIT (and particularly aHIT) is a pro‑thrombotic immunologic state. New thrombosis is the dominant clinical risk — withholding anticoagulation can permit extension of cerebral infarction or new thrombotic events elsewhere.
- aHIT antibodies can activate platelets without heparin (heparin‑independent activation), producing severe thrombocytopenia (platelets as low as 13×10^9/L here) and persistent hypercoagulability despite standard measures.
- Timing may be atypical: this patient’s platelet fall began before POD5 because of preoperative UFH exposure at cardiac cath — don’t dismiss early postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery.

So the problem is not whether HIT causes bleeding — it usually causes thrombosis. The neurologic question becomes: can you safely anticoagulate an ischemic stroke when platelets are very low?

## The practical decision framework (how this case approached it)

1. Rapid evaluation to exclude hemorrhage
   - Non‑contrast CT brain was performed when the platelet count dropped into the 20s and neuro status worsened (delirium → imaging showed a 3.2×1.8 cm left parietal infarct). No hemorrhagic transformation was seen.
   - Imaging to exclude hemorrhage is mandatory before therapeutic anticoagulation in this context.

2. Assume ongoing thrombotic risk unless proven otherwise
   - With a strongly positive PF4 ELISA and SRA showing heparin‑independent activation, clinicians treated this as active thrombotic HIT (aHIT). The presence of clinical thrombosis (acute ischemic stroke) makes therapeutic anticoagulation urgent despite thrombocytopenia.

3. Choose an anticoagulant you can stop quickly if hemorrhage occurs
   - The team chose argatroban (a short half‑life direct thrombin inhibitor) rather than an agent with a longer pharmacologic footprint. Argatroban was started at 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a PTT goal of 55–85 s in this patient.
   - Rationale: rapid offset if bleeding/hemorrhagic conversion occurs, easy titration using PTT, hepatic clearance (useful when renal function is fine; bivalirudin is an alternative where preferred).
   - Alternatives and sequencing: start with a short‑acting IV DTI (argatroban/bivalirudin). Once the acute period is controlled and bleeding risk is felt acceptable, consider transition to an off‑label but commonly used parenteral anti‑Xa agent (fondaparinux) or to a DOAC for outpatient maintenance once platelets recover.

4. Treat the immune driver aggressively
   - High‑dose IVIG was used here (100 g on two consecutive days, followed by a third 100 g dose) to block FcγR‑mediated platelet activation. In aHIT, IVIG can rapidly blunt platelet activation and is often needed because antibody activity can be refractory.
   - Expect variability: this patient’s platelet response to IVIG was modest (from 13→54×10^9/L over a week), reflecting unusually severe antibody activity. Be prepared for an attenuated response.

5. Monitor both thrombotic activity and bleeding risk closely
   - Neurologic checks and repeat imaging as clinically indicated to detect hemorrhagic transformation early.
   - Frequent platelet counts and coagulation labs. In this case, fibrinogen and D‑dimer were serially monitored as surrogate markers of consumptive coagulopathy and anticoagulant effect (fibrinogen fell and d‑dimer peaked early as the team judged hypercoagulability evolving).
   - Have a low threshold to stop the DTI if new bleeding or hemorrhagic conversion occurs — the short half‑life makes this feasible.

6. Avoid further iatrogenic heparin exposure
   - Stop all UFH/LMWH (including heparin flushes, heparin‑coated lines, heparinized catheters). In this case, prophylactic dalteparin given POD1–4 was stopped; earlier smaller UFH dose at the diagnostic cath was likely the sensitizing exposure.
   - Communicate “no heparin” clearly across teams and pharmacy.

7. Transition strategy
   - Once the acute phase is controlled and platelet counts begin to rise (and no hemorrhage is present or suspected), transition to an agent more practical for outpatient use was performed: this patient moved to therapeutic fondaparinux 10 mg daily on POD14 (after supplemental AT concentrate was given because AT activity was mildly low).
   - Fondaparinux is effective but has a long half‑life and no reversal; that is why it was deferred initially. Long‑term oral anticoagulation (apixaban) was resumed only after platelet recovery (apixaban restarted POD65).

8. Platelet transfusion and procedures
   - Platelet transfusion in HIT is generally avoided unless life‑threatening bleeding or immediate neurosurgical intervention is required; transfusion can exacerbate thrombosis.
   - If an invasive neuraxial or neurosurgical procedure is required, coordinate with hematology and neurosurgery and consider temporary platelet transfusion while controlling HIT with IVIG and DTI.

## Practical checklist you can use in similar cases
- Immediate: urgent non‑contrast head CT to exclude hemorrhage.
- If CT shows no hemorrhage and HIT suspected/confirmed with thrombosis → start therapeutic non‑heparin anticoagulation promptly (prefer short half‑life IV DTI).
- Simultaneously start immune‑modulating therapy for aHIT (high‑dose IVIG) when serology/clinical picture indicates autoimmune phenotype or severe thrombocytopenia.
- Stop all heparin exposures and alert all teams.
- Monitor neuro status and repeat imaging as needed; monitor platelets, PTT (for argatroban), fibrinogen, d‑dimer.
- If bleeding occurs, stop the DTI immediately; short half‑life helps limit bleeding time.
- Once stabilized and platelets recover, transition to practical outpatient anticoagulant (fondaparinux → DOAC) with hematology guidance.

## Pearls and pitfalls from this case
- Early postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery can still be HIT if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure (even a small cath heparin dose).
- aHIT can produce heparin‑independent platelet activation — the SRA may show strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin. Expect prolonged, severe thrombocytopenia and possible partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.
- Don’t let low platelet count alone dictate withholding anticoagulation in HIT with new thrombosis. The thrombotic risk of HIT often outweighs the bleeding risk — provided no intracranial hemorrhage is present on imaging.
- Use a short‑acting IV agent first when anticoagulating a recent stroke to preserve the option of rapid cessation if hemorrhage occurs.
- IVIG is a key adjunct in aHIT, but responses can be blunted in very aggressive serologic phenotypes — prepare for a prolonged course.

Key take‑home messages
- In HIT (especially aHIT) with new thrombosis, therapeutic non‑heparin anticoagulation should generally be started promptly, even with severe thrombocytopenia, after excluding intracranial hemorrhage.
- When stroke is present, favor a short‑half‑life IV direct thrombin inhibitor (eg, argatroban) initially to allow rapid cessation if hemorrhagic transformation occurs.
- Start immune‑modulating therapy (high‑dose IVIG) when autoimmune HIT is suspected; expect variable platelet responses.
- Stop all heparin exposures (flushes, LMWH prophylaxis, coated circuits) and communicate this clearly across teams.
- Monitor neurologic status, repeat imaging as indicated, and follow platelets, PTT, fibrinogen and d‑dimer trends to gauge bleeding risk and control of hypercoagulability.
- Transition to longer‑acting outpatient agents (fondaparinux, DOAC) only after stabilization and hematology input.

This case highlights a principle more than a protocol: HIT is primarily a thrombotic disease. In the face of a new thrombotic event (even ischemic stroke) and strongly pro‑thrombotic serology, the clinical priority is to arrest thrombus propagation with a controllable, rapidly reversible anticoagulant and to neutralize the immune driver — while watching the brain very closely.
