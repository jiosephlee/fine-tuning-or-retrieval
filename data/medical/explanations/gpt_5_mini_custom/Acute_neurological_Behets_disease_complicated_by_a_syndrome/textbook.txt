Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Clinical background and pathobiology: Behçet’s disease, neuro-Behçet’s, and SIADH

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a relapsing multisystem inflammatory disorder best recognised by its mucocutaneous hallmarks—recurrent painful oral aphthae, genital ulcers and characteristic skin lesions (erythema nodosum–like or folliculitis‑like eruptions). Beyond the mucosa and skin, BD commonly involves the eyes (uveitis), joints (arthralgia/arthritis), vasculature (both arterial and venous thrombosis/aneurysm) and the gastrointestinal tract. The clinical course is relapsing–remitting; major organ involvement (eye, central nervous system, vessels) often appears years after initial mucocutaneous disease. In practical terms, the presence of typical genital and oral ulcers plus compatible skin findings strongly points to BD in the appropriate clinical context, even when other organ manifestations are concurrent or evolving.

Immunopathogenesis relevant to this case centers on innate and adaptive inflammatory pathways. BD lesions show prominent neutrophilic infiltration and hyperactivity; neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation contribute directly to mucocutaneous and vascular damage. Adaptive immunity in BD is skewed toward Th1 and Th17 responses with increased production of cytokines such as interferon-γ, IL‑17 and IL‑1β. Proinflammatory cytokines, notably IL‑1β and IL‑6, are elevated in serum and, in cases with CNS involvement, in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). These cytokines drive systemic acute‑phase responses (CRP elevation, fever) and can have remote effects—for example, IL‑6 can influence hypothalamic regulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). Colchicine exerts benefit in mucocutaneous BD in part by suppressing neutrophil function and reducing production of some inflammasome‑linked cytokines.

Neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) encompasses several distinct patterns of CNS involvement with differing pathobiology and clinical implications. The major categories are:
- Parenchymal neuro‑BD: inflammatory lesions of the brain parenchyma, most commonly involving the brainstem, diencephalon (including hypothalamic regions) and basal ganglia. Presentations include meningoencephalitis, focal deficits, motor and gait disturbances, and cognitive or behavioural changes.
- Non‑parenchymal (vascular) neuro‑BD: predominantly cerebral venous thrombosis and other vascular events related to BD‑associated vasculitis and thrombophilia.
- Meningeal/aseptic presentations: CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis or meningoencephalitis may occur with minimal or no visible parenchymal lesions on imaging, particularly early in the disease.

A key practical point illustrated by this case is that acute neuro‑BD can present primarily as aseptic meningitis or meningoencephalitis with lymphocytic CSF pleocytosis and elevated protein, yet early brain MRI may be normal. Radiological abnormalities—when they occur—often involve the brainstem or diencephalon, but absence of such findings does not exclude CNS inflammation attributable to BD. Because neuro‑BD typically appears several years after mucocutaneous onset, simultaneous presentation of systemic BD and neuro‑BD is uncommon but well documented; when it does occur, clinicians must integrate mucocutaneous signs, CSF findings and exclusion of infection or malignancy to make the diagnosis.

Normal ADH physiology and mechanisms leading to SIADH help explain the endocrine complication in this case. ADH (arginine vasopressin) is synthesised in hypothalamic supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei and released from the posterior pituitary. The principal regulator is plasma osmolality: small rises above the osmoreceptor set point increase ADH secretion to conserve water; large changes in blood volume or pressure (sensed by low‑pressure atrial and high‑pressure arterial baroreceptors) can also modulate ADH. Non‑osmotic stimuli that increase ADH release include nausea, pain, hypoxia, certain drugs and inflammatory signals originating in or affecting the central nervous system.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is defined clinically and biochemically as hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and euvolaemia, in the absence of adrenal, thyroid or renal failure and without recent use of diuretics. Typical diagnostic elements are: low serum sodium and low plasma osmolality, urine osmolality that is not maximally dilute (usually >100 mOsm/kg and often markedly elevated), elevated urine sodium (>30–40 mmol/L) despite normal salt intake, clinical euvolaemia, and normal adrenal and thyroid function. In cases of CNS disease, SIADH reflects inappropriate, often non‑osmotic, ADH secretion leading to water retention and hyponatraemia. Common CNS triggers include meningitis and encephalitis (infectious or inflammatory), subarachnoid haemorrhage, intracranial tumours and traumatic brain injury.

The link between neuroinflammation and dysregulated ADH secretion is biologically plausible and increasingly supported by mechanistic data. Cytokines produced during CNS inflammation—especially IL‑6—can stimulate ADH release directly or sensitize hypothalamic pathways that control ADH secretion. Elevated IL‑6 levels have been documented in CSF from patients with neuroinflammatory disorders and, in BD specifically, intrathecal IL‑6 elevation has been reported in acute neuro‑BD. Thus, CNS inflammatory activity may provoke non‑osmotic ADH release via cytokine signalling, producing the SIADH pattern seen in this patient (low plasma osmolality, very high urine osmolality and urine sodium, normal volume status and normal thyroid/adrenal tests). Recovery of hyponatraemia in parallel with control of neuroinflammation—whether spontaneous or treatment‑mediated—supports a causal association, although it does not prove one.

Finally, HLA‑B51 deserves brief comment: it is the strongest genetic association known for BD and is more prevalent among affected individuals than in the general population. HLA‑B51 confers increased susceptibility, particularly in certain geographic populations, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient for the diagnosis. Because its sensitivity and specificity are imperfect and because BD remains a clinical diagnosis based on characteristic manifestations, HLA‑B51 is not part of the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease and has limited diagnostic utility at the bedside. In this case a positive HLA‑B51 supports the clinical picture but would not by itself establish the diagnosis.

In sum, this patient’s presentation—typical mucocutaneous BD lesions, aseptic lymphocytic meningitis with normal early MRI, biochemical features of SIADH and an inflammatory cytokine milieu—is coherent with current understanding of BD immunopathology and the capacity of CNS inflammation (potentially IL‑6 mediated) to provoke inappropriate ADH release. Recognising these mechanistic links helps explain the clinical constellation and guides focused diagnostic evaluation and conservative management of hyponatraemia while addressing the underlying inflammatory disease.

## Presentation and early bedside approach to fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatraemia

When a patient presents with fever, headache and mucocutaneous lesions plus hyponatraemia, the immediate bedside approach balances two simultaneous priorities: (1) rapid safety triage for life‑threatening neurologic and metabolic complications, and (2) pattern recognition to guide focused investigations that will separate infectious, inflammatory and endocrine causes. The index case illustrates how these priorities play out: an older man with fever, headache, vertigo, persistent hiccups, arthralgia and overt mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae and later a painful genital ulcer and folliculitis‑like rash) accompanied by marked hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L) and an elevated CK.

Synthesis of the presenting symptoms
Taken together, the symptom cluster suggests a systemic inflammatory or infectious process with central nervous system (CNS) involvement and concurrent mucocutaneous disease. Fever, headache and worsening vertigo raise concern for meningoencephalitis or meningitis; persistent hiccups, while non‑specific, can be a red flag for brainstem irritation or severe metabolic disturbance. Oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rash point toward a mucocutaneous inflammatory syndrome (in this case Behçet’s phenotype), and the later emergence of a genital ulcer is a highly specific clinical clue that ties the dermatologic and neurologic features together. From the bedside perspective, this pattern should prompt parallel workstreams: (a) immediate neurologic and metabolic stabilization, and (b) focused tests to distinguish infection from aseptic neuroinflammation.

Volume status assessment and recognizing euvolaemic hyponatraemia at the bedside
Determining the patient’s volume status is essential for narrowing the causes of hyponatraemia. A focused bedside exam assesses blood pressure (including orthostatic changes), heart rate, peripheral perfusion, mucous membranes, skin turgor, jugular venous pressure and presence of peripheral or pulmonary oedema. In this case the patient was clinically euvolemic (no orthostatic hypotension, no peripheral oedema, no signs of volume depletion), which shifts the differential toward euvolemic causes of hyponatraemia such as SIADH, hypocortisolism or hypothyroidism rather than hypovolaemic or hypervolaemic etiologies. Clinicians should document the euvolemic exam because it immediately changes the laboratory priorities and initial management.

Hyponatraemia triage: symptom severity, seizure risk, and initial monitoring
Hyponatraemia must be triaged by clinical severity, not just absolute sodium. Severe or acute symptomatic hyponatraemia (confusion, seizure, obtundation, respiratory compromise) mandates urgent treatment with hypertonic saline. In the absence of severe neurologic symptoms—as in this patient, who was alert but symptomatic with headache, vertigo and hiccups—initial management can be conservative but requires close monitoring. Early steps include strict fluid restriction, frequent serum sodium measurements (initially every 4–6 hours while changes are being made), avoidance of drugs that worsen hyponatraemia, and careful attention to the rate of correction (commonly accepted limits are <8–10 mmol/L per 24 hours to reduce the risk of osmotic demyelination). Record serial neurologic checks and consider telemetry/ICU monitoring if the sodium is very low or the patient’s mentation deteriorates.

Laboratory priorities in the early assessment
Immediate laboratory tests should be aimed at defining the type of hyponatraemia and identifying reversible causes. Essential early tests include serum sodium, serum osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium. In SIADH the typical pattern is hypotonic hyponatraemia (low serum osmolality), inappropriately concentrated urine (high urine osmolality) and elevated urine sodium in the presence of euvolaemia—exactly the pattern seen here (serum osmolality low, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L). Concurrent assessment of thyroid function and adrenal function is required because hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency can present with hyponatraemia; a morning serum cortisol and TSH/free T4 should be obtained promptly. Baseline inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) and muscle enzymes (CK) are useful if systemic inflammation or rhabdomyolysis is suspected. In this patient a markedly elevated CRP and CK supported an inflammatory process with metabolic sequelae. If CNS infection is suspected, blood cultures and early CSF studies (after appropriate neuroimaging if indicated) are also priorities.

Clues to severe hyponatraemia: persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis
Be alert for less obvious indicators of clinically important hyponatraemia. Persistent hiccups may reflect brainstem irritation or severe electrolyte disturbance and in this case served as an early clinical clue prompting further metabolic evaluation. Elevated CK (the patient’s CK >1000 U/L) may indicate rhabdomyolysis, which can follow severe hyponatraemia and contribute to further clinical deterioration. Recognising these associations helps avoid anchoring on a single organ system and prompts the clinician to broaden monitoring (including urine output, serial CK, renal function) and to treat the electrolyte disorder as a potential driver of multi‑system complications.

Early empiric coverage rationale in fever and headache pending exclusion of CNS infection
When fever and headache raise the possibility of meningitis or encephalitis, empiric antimicrobial therapy should be started promptly after obtaining blood cultures and before delays for neuroimaging or lumbar puncture when indicated. The choice of empiric agents depends on local epidemiology and the suspected pathogens; in this case broad‑spectrum antibacterial coverage (cefepime) was initiated and acyclovir added empirically when CNS viral encephalitis remained a concern. The rationale is risk mitigation: bacterial meningitis and HSV encephalitis have high morbidity without early treatment. Empiric therapy is discontinued if CSF and microbiologic studies exclude infection, as happened here. Clinicians must also assess for contraindications to immediate lumbar puncture (signs of raised intracranial pressure or focal deficits) and obtain a head CT or MRI first when indicated.

Recognizing a Behçet’s disease phenotype during acute evaluation
Behçet’s disease is a clinical diagnosis. At initial presentation mucocutaneous findings may be subtle or incomplete, so vigilance is needed for evolving lesions. Oral aphthae are common and often the earliest sign; a painful genital ulcer that appears later is a highly specific clue that, when combined with skin lesions (folliculitis‑like papules) and systemic inflammatory signs, should raise suspicion for BD. During the acute assessment, document the number, appearance and timing of mucosal lesions and examine the eyes for uveitis. The presence of mucocutaneous disease in a patient with aseptic meningitis or meningoencephalitis should prompt consideration of neuro‑Behçet and influence the balance between continued empiric antimicrobials and early immunomodulatory therapy once infection is excluded.

Putting it together at the bedside
In practice, the first hour is spent securing the airway/breathing/circulation, assessing neurologic status, measuring serum sodium and osmolality, and establishing whether the patient has severe symptoms that require hypertonic saline. Simultaneously, obtain blood cultures, start empiric antimicrobials if bacterial or viral CNS infection is a real possibility, and perform a focused volume assessment to steer the hyponatraemia workup. In this case, the euvolemic exam, laboratory pattern (low serum osmolality, concentrated urine, elevated urine sodium) and elevated ADH supported SIADH; negative CSF infectious testing and the appearance of genital ulcers and folliculitis‑like skin lesions supported acute neuro‑Behçet. Management then focused on treating the underlying inflammatory disease (colchicine in this case) and conservative correction of hyponatraemia with fluid restriction and close monitoring rather than aggressive hypertonic therapy.

## Diagnostic workup and reasoning: from broad differential to definitive BD, acute neuro-BD, and SIADH

The diagnostic approach in this case proceeded from a broad, infection‑first differential for fever, headache and hyponatraemia toward a focused evaluation that integrated mucocutaneous findings and objective laboratory and CSF data. Early on the team prioritized common and high‑risk causes of meningoencephalitis (viral and bacterial pathogens, tuberculosis), as well as non‑infectious mimics that can present with CNS inflammation (autoimmune encephalitis, neurosarcoidosis, CNS lymphoma and other systemic inflammatory diseases). Each potential diagnosis was tested for and, where possible, excluded systematically.

CSF findings were critical to narrowing the differential. The CSF showed a mildly elevated opening pressure, lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/μL) and an elevated protein (0.97 g/L ≈ 97 mg/dL). This pattern—lymphocyte‑predominant pleocytosis with raised protein but no neutrophilic predominance—is most consistent with aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis rather than acute bacterial meningitis, which typically has a high neutrophil count, very low glucose and rapid clinical deterioration. Targeted infectious testing in CSF (cultures, bacterial/acid‑fast/tuberculous PCR, HSV and VZV assays, cryptococcal antigen) returned negative, arguing against common viral encephalitides and tuberculosis in this patient. CSF cytology did not show malignant cells, making leptomeningeal spread of malignancy or primary CNS lymphoma less likely—although these cannot be absolutely excluded by a single negative CSF study, especially early in disease.

Neuroimaging added nuance but did not settle the diagnosis. Brain MRI was reported as normal, including no hypothalamic or brainstem lesions. In neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) MRI may show parenchymal lesions classically involving the brainstem (mesodiencephalic junction), thalamus, basal ganglia and deep white matter, but early presentations can show no abnormalities, particularly when the clinical picture is dominated by meningeal inflammation rather than large parenchymal lesions. Thus a normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑BD or an aseptic meningitis picture, and clinical and CSF data must be integrated rather than relying solely on imaging.

Concurrently, the mucocutaneous examination provided the decisive evidence for Behçet’s disease (BD). Applying the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient had recurrent oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis that appeared during admission (2 points), and a folliculitis‑type skin eruption (1 point), totaling five points (≥4 indicates definite BD). This standardized scoring converted a constellation of otherwise non‑specific inflammatory signs into a defensible diagnosis of systemic BD. HLA‑B51 was positive but, as emphasized in practice, HLA typing is supportive at best and not required for classification; it neither establishes nor rules out the diagnosis by itself.

With definite systemic BD established, the team then asked whether the CNS syndrome represented neuro‑BD. Establishing acute neuro‑BD requires compatible neurological manifestations in the setting of BD and the reasonable exclusion of alternate explanations. In this case the timeline (new fever, headache, worsening vertigo and aseptic CSF inflammation), negative infectious studies, absence of CSF cytology evidence of malignancy, and lack of clinical/imaging support for other autoimmune or granulomatous conditions (neurosarcoidosis workup with ACE and lysozyme was unrevealing, and no systemic sarcoid features were present) all supported neuro‑BD as the most parsimonious diagnosis. The possibility of spontaneous aseptic meningitis unrelated to BD was considered, but the concurrent evolution of BD‑defining mucocutaneous lesions strengthened the link between systemic BD and the CNS inflammation.

Ancillary tests that were obtained illustrate the limits of unspecific biomarkers. Serum ACE and lysozyme have modest sensitivity for sarcoidosis; their normal values do not definitively exclude neurosarcoidosis, but in the absence of radiographic or clinical features they contributed little to the diagnostic probability. A mildly elevated soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) is non‑specific and may rise in inflammatory states as well as lymphoma; without corroborating imaging or cytology this result did not change management. Thus these tests should be used selectively and interpreted in clinical context, not as standalone proof.

The hyponatraemia prompted a targeted endocrine and fluid‑status evaluation to identify the mechanism. The patient was hypotonic (serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg) with inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urine sodium (88 mmol/L) despite a clinically euvolaemic state. Thyroid and adrenal causes were excluded by normal TSH, free T4 and an adequate serum cortisol level. These findings meet standard diagnostic criteria for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH): hypotonic hyponatraemia, euvolaemia, concentrated urine with high urine sodium and normal thyroid/adrenal function. An elevated measured plasma ADH (22.4 pg/mL) supported the diagnosis in this case but is not required for making it in routine practice because ADH assays are labile and not widely available; the biochemical pattern is the key. The team appropriately managed SIADH with fluid restriction and careful monitoring while addressing the underlying inflammatory process.

Finally, linking the entities—BD, neuro‑BD and SIADH—rests on temporal association plus biologic plausibility rather than absolute proof. Central nervous system inflammation is a recognized trigger for SIADH, and cytokines such as interleukin‑6 (IL‑6), which are often elevated in neuroinflammatory states including neuro‑BD, can stimulate ADH release. In this patient, the onset of aseptic meningitis‑type symptoms and SIADH clustered in time with the evolution of mucocutaneous BD lesions, and all improved after anti‑inflammatory therapy with colchicine. While this temporal correlation and the known pathophysiology make neuroinflammation the likeliest proximate cause of SIADH here, the report correctly notes that causality cannot be definitively proven on the basis of a single case.

In summary, the stepwise reasoning combined: (1) an initial broad infectious and inflammatory workup to exclude treatable mimics, (2) interpretation of CSF as aseptic meningitis, (3) recognition that a normal MRI does not exclude early neuro‑BD, (4) application of validated BD criteria to secure the systemic diagnosis, and (5) standard biochemical criteria to establish SIADH after excluding endocrine causes. The convergence of these findings—CSF inflammation without identifiable pathogen, definite systemic BD, and appropriate SIADH physiology—supported the final diagnoses of definite BD with acute neuro‑BD complicated by SIADH.

## Management strategy: treating acute neuro-BD and controlling SIADH safely

When confronted with a patient who has suspected CNS inflammation and systemic findings pointing to Behçet’s disease (BD), management must balance three simultaneous goals: (1) promptly treat potentially life‑threatening infections while they are being excluded, (2) control neuroinflammation in accordance with standard neuro‑BD practice, and (3) recognise and manage electrolyte and supportive‑care needs such as SIADH. In this case the team started broad empiric therapy early, then narrowed therapy as infectious causes were excluded and clinical data supported neuro‑BD; they simultaneously applied conservative measures to correct hyponatraemia and provided local and rehabilitation care.

Empiric antimicrobials and antivirals
When bacterial meningitis or viral encephalitis (notably HSV) is in the differential, empiric antibiotics and antivirals are indicated immediately after appropriate cultures and LP are obtained. Typical empiric choices are a third‑ or fourth‑generation cephalosporin ± vancomycin for bacterial meningitis and IV acyclovir for suspected HSV encephalitis. These agents should be reassessed and de‑escalated promptly once diagnostic tests return: discontinue acyclovir when HSV PCR is negative and clinical picture is incompatible with HSV; stop broad‑spectrum antibiotics once blood and CSF cultures remain negative at 48–72 hours and an alternative non‑infectious cause (for example neuro‑BD) is convincing. In the reported case empiric cefepime and acyclovir were given early and stopped after negative microbiology and improving clinical status; this exemplifies the principle of short, targeted empiric therapy with early de‑escalation to avoid unnecessary prolonged antimicrobials.

Standard therapy for acute neuro‑BD
The accepted first‑line therapy for acute parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s is high‑dose corticosteroids to control CNS inflammation rapidly. A common regimen is IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days followed by an oral prednisone taper. For patients with recurrent, severe or steroid‑dependent disease, addition of steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants is recommended: azathioprine or methotrexate are commonly used for maintenance; cyclophosphamide may be considered for life‑ or organ‑threatening presentations; and anti‑TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab) are options for refractory disease or severe parenchymal involvement. These agents are chosen based on disease severity, focal deficits, parenchymal brainstem lesions on imaging, and risk of relapse. Frequent neurological reassessment and MRI surveillance guide escalation. In the present case the neuro‑inflammation manifested as aseptic meningitis without focal deficits or MRI lesions; therefore the treating physicians considered the balance of risks and benefits before initiating systemic steroids.

Colchicine in BD: mechanism and role
Colchicine inhibits microtubule polymerisation in neutrophils, reducing neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation, and modulates inflammasome activity and downstream cytokine release (including IL‑1 and IL‑6). Clinically, colchicine is established for mucocutaneous and joint manifestations of BD and is often used long‑term to reduce recurrence of oral and genital ulcers. Evidence for colchicine as monotherapy in acute neuro‑BD is limited; most neuro‑BD patients require corticosteroids and additional immunosuppression. In this case colchicine was started after BD was diagnosed clinically. The rationale was that the neurological syndrome was relatively mild (aseptic meningitis without focal deficits or MRI parenchymal lesions), the patient had significant hyperglycaemia that increased the risk of steroid complications, and colchicine’s anti‑inflammatory effects could address systemic mucocutaneous disease and possibly attenuate CNS inflammation. Rapid clinical improvement after colchicine supports that this was an appropriate, case‑specific choice, but it should not be generalized to all neuro‑BD presentations.

Contextualising a steroid‑sparing approach
Avoiding or delaying systemic corticosteroids is reasonable when the neuro presentation is mild, there is no radiological parenchymal involvement or focal deficit, and there are compelling contraindications to steroids—poorly controlled diabetes being a prominent example. In severe neuro‑BD (marked focal deficits, progressive course, brainstem lesions, altered consciousness) high‑dose steroids should not be withheld because the risk of neurological morbidity is high. In the present patient, hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 8.2%) and the mild, non‑focal clinical course supported a trial of colchicine with close monitoring rather than immediate steroids.

SIADH management principles and safe sodium correction
Management of SIADH begins with confirming euvolemic, hypoosmolar hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urinary sodium, then instituting measures to raise serum sodium safely. First‑line therapy for mild–moderate SIADH is usually fluid restriction; targets are individualized but commonly 800–1000 mL/day or less when feasible, adjusted for insensible losses and comorbidities. Monitor serum sodium frequently—at least every 6–12 hours when sodium is changing and every 2–4 hours if hypertonic saline is used. Safe correction limits are critical: avoid increasing serum sodium by more than about 8 mmol/L in the first 24 hours (many guidelines recommend ≤8 mmol/L/day for high‑risk patients) and by no more than 18 mmol/L over 48 hours. Overly rapid correction risks osmotic demyelination syndrome; for patients at high risk for ODS (malnutrition, alcoholism, advanced liver disease, profound hyponatraemia, or hypokalemia), be even more conservative. If severe neurologic symptoms develop (seizures, coma, severe obtundation) or sodium is very low with signs of cerebral oedema, bolus hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) is indicated to promptly raise sodium by 4–6 mmol/L to stabilise the patient; thereafter correction should proceed cautiously. Vasopressin receptor antagonists (tolvaptan, conivaptan) can be used in euvolemic hyponatraemia refractory to fluid restriction, but they rapidly increase free water excretion and must be used with strict monitoring because of ODS risk; they are typically avoided in acute severe neurological illness or when rapid shifts could be hazardous. If sodium overcorrection occurs, desmopressin and free water (e.g., D5W) can be used to re‑lower sodium. In this case conservative fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) and close monitoring were effective; hypertonic saline or vaptans were not required.

Topical therapies and supportive care for mucocutaneous lesions
Local measures remain important adjuncts: topical corticosteroids for oral ulcers reduce pain and promote healing, and topical antimicrobial creams (silver sulfadiazine as used here) or antiseptics may be helpful for genital ulcers and secondary infection prevention. Pain control, nutritional support (important if oral ulcers impair intake), and wound care are part of routine management. Ophthalmology review is essential even when uveitis is not clinically apparent because eye involvement can be vision‑threatening.

Comorbidity management and rehabilitation
Concurrent medical issues must be optimised in parallel. Poorly controlled diabetes increases the risks of infection, steroid complications, and osmotic disturbances; definitive glucose‑lowering therapy can be deferred until the acute inflammatory state is controlled if steroids are avoided, but a plan for glycaemic optimisation should be made early. In this report glucose‑lowering therapy was restarted after BD stabilised. Rhabdomyolysis and deconditioning identified on admission require supportive care, fluid management, and physiotherapy; early mobilisation and rehabilitation mitigate functional decline. Involving endocrinology, neurology, rheumatology, infectious disease and rehabilitation teams supports coordinated care.

Monitoring response and triggers for escalation
Close clinical and laboratory surveillance guides success and need for escalation. Early indicators of response include improvement in fever, headache, hiccups and mucocutaneous lesions, falling inflammatory markers (CRP), normalising CK, and rising serum sodium toward normal with decreasing ADH levels. In this case sodium normalised by day 16 and ADH fell markedly, paralleling clinical recovery. Triggers for re‑assessment and escalation to steroids or additional immunosuppression include new or worsening focal neurologic deficits, progressive cognitive decline, radiological progression (new parenchymal lesions on MRI), failure to improve or clinical deterioration despite initial therapy, recurrent severe hyponatraemia or development of severe neurologic symptoms from hyponatraemia. If escalation is needed, high‑dose corticosteroids should be started promptly and consideration given to adding azathioprine, cyclophosphamide or anti‑TNF agents per disease severity and specialist input.

Multidisciplinary coordination and individualized care
Finally, management decisions in neuro‑BD complicated by SIADH must be individualized. The multidisciplinary team should include neurology (for CNS disease monitoring and decision on immunotherapy escalation), rheumatology (BD‑specific therapy), infectious disease (antimicrobial stewardship and diagnostic interpretation), endocrinology or nephrology (SIADH and sodium management), ophthalmology, and rehabilitation services. The therapeutic pathway chosen in this case—a short empiric antimicrobial/antiviral window, conservative SIADH management, and a trial of colchicine because of mild neuro symptoms and concerning hyperglycaemia—illustrates how dynamic reassessment and interdisciplinary collaboration allow safe, effective care while minimising unnecessary exposure to corticosteroids and their metabolic complications.

## Outcomes, follow-up, and clinical lessons

Clinical course and immediate outcome
In this patient the clinical course was notable for a rapid clinical and biochemical response after initiation of colchicine. Colchicine was started on hospital day 8 and the patient’s fever and appetite improved within 24 hours; over the next two weeks his mucocutaneous lesions, vertigo, hiccups and general malaise gradually resolved. Serum sodium rose from 124 mmol/L to normal (136 mmol/L) by day 16, and measured ADH fell from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL as hyponatraemia corrected. No systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were required, hypertonic saline and vasopressin antagonists were not used, and the patient was discharged after approximately one month once clinical recovery and rehabilitation goals were met.

Long-term outcome
The report documents sustained remission on colchicine monotherapy for roughly four years without relapse of mucocutaneous or neurological symptoms. After the acute illness stabilised, diabetes treatment was safely re‑initiated (biguanide therapy) with good glycaemic control. These outcomes indicate that, in this individual case, colchicine alone coincided with durable disease control and resolution of SIADH, though spontaneous remission cannot be definitively excluded.

When to suspect acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease
This case highlights the importance of suspecting acute neuro‑BD when patients with (known or newly presenting) BD have non‑specific CNS complaints—headache, vertigo, behavioural change, persistent hiccups—or unexplained hyponatraemia. Crucially, neuro‑BD can present early in the disease course and may lack focal neurological deficits. The combination of characteristic mucocutaneous findings (multiple oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis‑type rash) together with aseptic CSF inflammation and euvolemic hyponatraemia should raise suspicion for neuro‑BD even when brain MRI is normal.

Diagnostic teaching points and test interpretation
The diagnosis of BD remains clinical and is guided by established criteria (eg, the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease); supportive tests such as HLA‑B51 can add weight but are neither necessary nor diagnostic. Early neuro‑inflammatory lesions in BD may be missed on MRI; a normal early MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD and CSF analysis can be diagnostically valuable when it shows aseptic lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. Ancillary blood tests used in exclusion-based workups (serum ACE, sIL‑2R) have limited sensitivity and specificity: ACE is an insensitive screen for neurosarcoidosis and mild sIL‑2R elevations are non‑specific and may reflect systemic inflammation. Avoid indiscriminate testing—interpret these markers in the clinical context and pursue further investigations only when symptoms or imaging suggest those alternative diagnoses.

Recognising SIADH in CNS inflammation
SIADH should be suspected when hypotonic hyponatraemia occurs with euvolemia, low plasma osmolality, inappropriately high urine osmolality and elevated urine sodium, and after exclusion of adrenal and thyroid dysfunction. In CNS inflammatory states, SIADH can present with non‑specific symptoms (nausea, headache, confusion) and with more unusual clues such as persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, both of which were seen in this patient and can reflect severe hyponatraemia. Initial management is conservative (fluid restriction, careful monitoring) unless there are severe or rapidly progressive neurologic symptoms, in which case controlled hypertonic therapy and specialist input are indicated. Avoid overly rapid sodium correction to prevent osmotic demyelination.

Evidence, pathophysiology and knowledge gaps
A plausible mechanistic link exists between neuroinflammation and SIADH: inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin‑6, can stimulate ADH release and have been reported elevated in neuro‑BD. In this case the temporal association and the fall in ADH with clinical improvement make an inflammatory driver plausible, but IL‑6 was not measured and causality cannot be proven. It remains uncertain whether SIADH in BD is disease‑specific or a non‑specific consequence of CNS inflammation; systematic data are sparse and further studies measuring cytokines and larger case series would be needed to clarify mechanisms and risk factors.

Follow‑up and practical surveillance
After discharge, early follow‑up should focus on electrolyte stability, neurologic symptom surveillance and BD activity. Practical measures include frequent sodium checks until stable (eg, within 1–2 weeks of discharge, then at intervals guided by stability and symptoms), outpatient neurology or rheumatology review within 2–4 weeks, and then periodic reviews (commonly every 3–6 months) tailored to disease course. Ophthalmologic assessment is important because uveitis can develop later; consider baseline and periodic eye exams. Re‑imaging of the brain or repeat CSF analysis is warranted if new or progressive focal deficits, seizures, altered consciousness, persistent or recurrent hyponatraemia, or failure to respond to therapy occur. Monitor for colchicine adverse effects (gastrointestinal intolerance, cytopenias, hepatic dysfunction) with periodic clinical and laboratory checks, and coordinate chronic disease care (eg, diabetes and cardiovascular risk reduction). Multidisciplinary coordination between neurology and rheumatology (or internal medicine) facilitates early recognition of relapse and timely escalation to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants when indicated.

Key clinical lessons
• In patients with BD, non‑specific CNS symptoms plus euvolemic hyponatraemia should prompt consideration of acute neuro‑BD complicated by SIADH, even if MRI is normal.  
• Diagnosis of BD and neuro‑BD relies on clinical criteria and supportive CSF findings; ancillary biomarkers must be interpreted cautiously.  
• SIADH in CNS inflammation may present with hiccups or rhabdomyolysis and usually responds to conservative measures, but close monitoring and avoidance of rapid sodium shifts are essential.  
• Although colchicine monotherapy coincided with complete recovery in this case, corticosteroids remain the mainstay for many patients with neuro‑BD; treatment should be individualized and relapse surveillance maintained.
