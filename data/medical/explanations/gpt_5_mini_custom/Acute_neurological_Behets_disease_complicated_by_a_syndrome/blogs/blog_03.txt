Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Hiccup, the CK, and the Fluid: Navigating SIADH’s Hidden Complications

This case surprises in three little findings that don’t often show up together: persistent hiccups, marked hyponatraemia from SIADH, and an elevated CK consistent with rhabdomyolysis — all in a patient with newly diagnosed systemic Behçet’s disease and aseptic meningitis (acute neuro‑BD). That constellation forced a practical bedside tension: fluid restriction for SIADH versus aggressive IV fluids to protect the kidney from myoglobin injury. The good news: recognising the pattern and tailoring management avoided both neurologic catastrophe and renal failure.

## Why this matters (and why you should care)
- Hyponatraemia is common and often straightforward. But when it’s driven by CNS inflammation (neuro‑BD in this case), SIADH can present with odd clues — hiccups, refractory nausea, or subtle neurologic worsening — and can be complicated by rhabdomyolysis.
- Rhabdomyolysis from profound hyponatraemia is underappreciated. If missed, the usual treatment for rhabdo (large-volume isotonic fluids) can worsen the SIADH‑related hyponatraemia; conversely, strict fluid restriction to treat SIADH can allow CK to climb and precipitate myoglobinuric AKI.
- The case highlights the importance of early CK measurement in hyponatraemic patients with neuroinflammatory features or compatible symptoms, and of a pragmatic, monitored plan when the goals for sodium and renal protection conflict.

Brief recap of the case (stick-to-facts):
An Asian man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, persistent hiccups and mucocutaneous lesions. Labs showed serum Na 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality low (267 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L, ADH elevated — diagnostic of SIADH. CSF had aseptic meningitis; MRI was non‑diagnostic. CK was 1,349 U/L (rhabdomyolysis), renal function was preserved. Painful genital ulcer and folliculitis‑like rash led to a diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease with acute neuro‑BD. Colchicine and fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) were used; hyponatraemia and CK normalized without hypertonic saline or immunosuppression.

## Pathophysiologic snapshot (why hiccups, SIADH and rhabdo can cluster)
- CNS inflammation can trigger inappropriate ADH release. In neuroinflammatory conditions IL‑6 and other cytokines promote ADH secretion; ADH elevation leads to water retention and dilutional hyponatraemia (SIADH phenotype: low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, high urine Na, euvolemic exam).
- Severe hyponatraemia can injure skeletal muscle: cell swelling, calcium influx and protease activation may precipitate muscle necrosis and CK release. Seizures, prolonged immobility or metabolic derangements amplify that risk.
- Hiccups are a useful, often-overlooked clue. Persistent hiccups have been reported with meningitis and with significant hyponatraemia and may reflect brain‑stem or meningeal irritation — flagging potential neuro‑SIADH.

## The bedside tension: fluid restriction vs fluid resuscitation
Two incompatible goals can collide:
- SIADH → primary therapy is free water restriction (and in some cases hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms), because giving water or unmonitored isotonic fluids may worsen hyponatraemia.
- Rhabdomyolysis → mainstay is early, adequate IV isotonic fluids to maintain urine output and prevent myoglobinuria/AKI.

So how do you reconcile them? The approach is pragmatic and driven by what is most time‑sensitive: neurologic risk (severe hyponatraemia with seizures/altered mental status) and evidence of evolving renal threat from rhabdo (rapidly rising CK, dark urine, oliguria, rising creatinine).

## Practical, stepwise approach (what I’d do at the bedside)

1. Rapid assessment and categorisation
   - Neurologic status first: seizures, severe obtundation, or focal deficits → treat as severe symptomatic hyponatraemia.
   - Rhabdomyolysis risk: CK >1,000 U/L, muscle pain/weakness, dark urine, or rising creatinine demand early renal protection.

2. Immediate actions based on dominant problem
   - If severe neurologic symptoms from hyponatraemia:
     - Give boluses of hypertonic saline (e.g., 100 mL of 3% saline IV over 10 minutes, repeat as needed) to acutely raise serum Na enough to relieve life‑threatening cerebral edema.
     - Simultaneously monitor urine output, Na and CK closely. Get nephrology involved early if CK is elevated.
   - If no severe neuro features but significant rhabdomyolysis risk:
     - Start isotonic saline to protect kidneys — but do it cautiously and under strict monitoring of serum sodium (q2–4 h).
     - Aim for urine output to prevent myoglobin accumulation; consider loop diuretic if volume overload or if the urine is not clearing myoglobin despite fluids.
   - If both conditions are moderate (e.g., Na 120–130, CK 1,000–5,000, patient clinically stable):
     - Tailor a middle path: modest IV isotonic saline (to protect kidney) while intensively monitoring Na and urine osmolality, and be prepared to intervene (hypertonic saline or desmopressin) if rapid Na shifts or neurologic deterioration occur.
     - Consider using a loop diuretic with isotonic saline to increase free water excretion if the urine remains too concentrated (this can help prevent further dilutional hyponatraemia while maintaining renal perfusion).

3. Monitoring and safety nets
   - Serum sodium checks every 2–4 hours while actively correcting or giving fluids.
   - Target safe correction: generally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours (many aim for ≤6–8 mmol/L in high‑risk patients); avoid rapid overcorrection.
   - If a spontaneous water diuresis causes too-rapid rise in Na, stop interventions and give desmopressin (and D5W if needed) to relower/slow correction.
   - Check CK, creatinine, K+, phosphate and calcium frequently; monitor urine color/volume and urine myoglobin if available.
   - Early nephrology consult when CK is high, urine output falls, or if you need aggressive fluids that risk worsening hyponatraemia.

4. Therapies to consider (and pitfalls)
   - Hypertonic saline: life‑saving in severe neurologic hyponatraemia; must be used with strict monitoring to avoid osmotic demyelination.
   - Isotonic saline: mainstay of rhabdo care, but in SIADH it can be ineffective or worsen hyponatraemia if not paired with strategies to enhance free water excretion.
   - Loop diuretics: can be used to induce free water diuresis when combined with isotonic saline (helps clear urine and increase Na), but may also shift electrolytes — monitor K+ and Mg2+.
   - Vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans): effective aquaretics for chronic SIADH or persistent euvolemic hyponatraemia, but are not first‑line for acute severe hyponatraemia and can cause rapid Na rises — use only with specialist input.
   - Desmopressin: paradoxically useful to prevent or treat overly rapid correction (i.e., “clamp” the kidney when water diuresis causes too fast a Na rise).

5. Treat the underlying cause
   - In this case, colchicine for Behçet’s-related inflammation correlated with clinical and sodium recovery. For neuroinflammatory SIADH, treating the inflammation can be curative for the hyponatraemia.

## Monitoring specifics (practical order set)
- Baseline: Na, K, Cl, HCO3, creatinine, CK, phosphate, Ca, Mg, serum/urine osmolality, urine Na, urine output.
- While intervening: serum Na q2–4 h, urine output hourly, CK daily (or more often if rising), creatinine daily or more if trending up.
- Document neurologic exam and urine color hourly in unstable patients.

## Red flags that demand escalation
- Seizure or decreased consciousness → urgent hypertonic saline.
- Rising creatinine or oliguria with rising CK → escalate fluids and nephrology input.
- Rapid spontaneous rise in Na (water diuresis) → give desmopressin to slow correction.
- Na correction >8 mmol/L in 24 hours (or if approaching that threshold) → consider measures to re‑lower Na.

## Clinical pearls
- Persistent hiccups in a febrile patient with headaches should raise the index of suspicion for CNS inflammation and hyponatraemia; don’t shrug them off as benign.
- Check CK in patients with moderate–severe hyponatraemia who have neuro symptoms, muscle pain, a history of immobility, seizures, or dark urine. Rhabdomyolysis may be present even without classic pain.
- In SIADH, the urine is concentrated. Isotonic saline can be counterproductive unless you simultaneously promote free water excretion (loop diuretic) or you need to protect the kidney from myoglobin injury — choose strategy based on which organ is at immediate risk.
- When in doubt, prioritise what’s immediately life‑threatening: severe cerebral edema and seizures > preventing potential AKI. But anticipate the other problem and preemptively involve nephrology to manage fluid and electrolyte tradeoffs.
- Treating the underlying neuroinflammation may reverse SIADH — point illustrated by rapid improvement after colchicine in this Behçet’s case.

## Take‑home messages
- Persistent hiccups and hyponatraemia in a patient with meningitic features should trigger evaluation for SIADH due to CNS inflammation.
- Always check CK in hyponatraemic patients with neuro signs, myalgias, immobility or dark urine — rhabdomyolysis can coexist and change management.
- The bedside tension between fluid restriction (SIADH) and aggressive IV fluids (rhabdomyolysis) requires individualized prioritisation, frequent sodium monitoring (q2–4 h during active management), early nephrology consult, and the use of adjuncts (loop diuretics, desmopressin, or hypertonic saline) as appropriate.
- Treat the underlying inflammatory cause when possible — resolving the driver of SIADH can remove the dilemma.
