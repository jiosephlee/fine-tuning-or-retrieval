Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In an adult with years of paroxysmal choking on both solids and liquids, normal laryngoscopy and endoscopy, and normal pulmonary testing, what neurologic etiologies should be prioritized and what initial neuroimaging would you order?

Bottom line: when ENT and pulmonary work‑ups are unrevealing in a patient with chronic, paroxysmal choking to solids and liquids and unexplained nocturnal desaturation, prioritize structural brainstem and craniocervical‑junction disorders that can impair lower cranial nerve function and automatic respiratory control—most classically Chiari I malformation—and obtain an MRI of the brain and cervical spine targeted to the posterior fossa/foramen magnum.

Why (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- The combination of neurogenic dysphagia (equal difficulty with solids and liquids, normal upper‑airway endoscopy) and episodes of severe desaturation without dyspnea points to disruption of lower cranial nerve nuclei and/or medullary respiratory centers (automatic breathing pathways) rather than primary ENT or lung disease. The case demonstrates this: a missed crowded foramen magnum on CT and an MRI showing 11 mm tonsillar descent explained both bulbar dysfunction and central apneas from direct brainstem compression.
- Structural lesions at the foramen magnum can produce intermittent bulbar symptoms and central hypoventilation by kinking/compressing the medulla, impeding afferent interoceptive signalling (loss of dyspnea) and automatic respiratory drive—hence the “forgetting to breathe” phenotype.

Prioritized neurologic etiologies to consider (ranked)
1. Chiari I malformation / craniocervical junction crowding — classic cause of lower cranial nerve dysfunction, syrinx formation, and central sleep‑disordered breathing; shown in this case.
2. Posterior fossa mass lesions (tumor, epidermoid, arachnoid cyst) compressing brainstem or craniovertebral junction.
3. Syringobulbia / syringomyelia extending into the medulla — can produce progressive bulbar signs and central respiratory events.
4. Direct brainstem intrinsic lesions: medullary infarct (ischemic stroke), demyelinating plaques (MS), inflammatory or infectious brainstem encephalitis — consider when the history is subacute/acute or there are focal neuro deficits.
5. Lower cranial neuropathies / bulbar motor neuron disease (bulbar ALS) or autoimmune neuropathies (rarely GBS variants) — consider if progressive weakness, fasciculations, or EMG abnormalities.
6. Central hypoventilation from neurodegenerative or genetic causes (rare in adults) — low likelihood but relevant if other features suggest these syndromes.

Initial neuroimaging and protocol (what to order and why)
- MRI brain with focused posterior fossa / craniovertebral junction protocol (sagittal and axial T1, T2, FLAIR, diffusion): to detect tonsillar descent, assess medullary compression, and look for intrinsic brainstem lesions.
- MRI cervical spine (sagittal and axial T2) in the same study to evaluate for syringomyelia/syringobulbia and the full extent of tonsillar descent.
- Cine phase‑contrast (CSF flow) MRI at the foramen magnum if Chiari is suspected and for surgical planning: demonstrates flow obstruction that correlates with symptomatic significance.
- Gadolinium contrast MRI if concern for tumor, infection, or inflammatory disease.
- If MRI is contraindicated, obtain high‑resolution CT with sagittal reformats centered on the foramen magnum—but recognize CT can miss soft‑tissue tonsillar descent and CSF flow abnormalities (as occurred in this case where the crowded foramen magnum was initially overlooked on CT).

Practical next steps
- If imaging confirms a structural lesion (eg, Chiari I with symptomatic brainstem compression or syrinx), early neurosurgical referral and polysomnography with capnography are indicated because posterior fossa decompression can reverse respiratory and bulbar symptoms.
- If imaging is normal, pursue brainstem‑targeted evaluations (diffusion MRI for acute lesions, CSF studies if inflammatory/infectious process suspected, EMG for motor neuron disease) and formal type I polysomnography with CO2 monitoring to characterize central hypoventilation.

Takeaway: normal laryngoscopy and pulmonary testing in a patient with lifelong/years of choking and unexplained desaturations should prompt MRI brain ± cervical spine with a posterior fossa/foramen magnum protocol early—Chiari I and other compressive brainstem lesions must be excluded because timely recognition can change management and prognosis, as illustrated by this case.
