Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] What are the key elements of discharge planning after multiphasic anaphylaxis to a new antibiotic, including selection and dosing of epinephrine auto-injectors for an adult over 50 kg, patient education and action plan, medication avoidance and documentation, and timing of allergy/immunology follow-up?

Bottom line
- Discharge after multiphasic anaphylaxis must treat the event as high-risk: give the patient the correct epinephrine auto‑injector(s), a written action plan, clear avoidance instructions and documentation, and timely allergy/immunology follow‑up to define the trigger and investigate mast‑cell disease. In this case (woman in her  60s, 104 kg, multiphasic reaction to clarithromycin with elevated tryptase), that means prescribing and teaching use of 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine devices (and providing two devices), explicit macrolide avoidance until specialist review, and rapid allergist referral.

Key elements — practical checklist with clinical reasoning

1) Epinephrine auto‑injector selection and dosing
- Target dose: epinephrine IM 0.01 mg/kg, maximum single IM dose 0.5 mg. For adults >50 kg this is effectively 0.5 mg. (Case note: the team initially used 0.3 mg auto‑injectors; authors emphasise this can underdose heavier adults and prompt additional doses or escalation.)
- Prescribe a 0.5 mg auto‑injector for this patient (if available in your country). Because she had severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis and elevated tryptase suggesting mast‑cell disease, prescribe two auto‑injectors (carry two at all times). Rationale: severe prior reaction and suspected mastocytosis increase risk of recurrence and need for repeated dosing before EMS arrival.
- If a 0.5 mg auto‑injector is not available locally, give a 0.3 mg device but reinforce that repeat dosing is appropriate: if symptoms persist/worsen after 5 minutes, give a second injection (repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed) and call emergency services. Preferably replace 0.3 mg devices with a 0.5 mg device as soon as available.

2) Patient education and written action plan
- Teach and practice injection technique (anterolateral thigh, through clothing if necessary). Emphasize to hold device in place per manufacturer instructions (follow device label for seconds to hold). Demonstrate removal and disposal.
- Explicit action steps to give to the patient in writing and review verbally:
  1. At first signs of anaphylaxis (respiratory difficulty, throat tightness, hives with hypotension, collapse, severe lip/tongue swelling, persistent lightheadedness) administer epinephrine immediately.
  2. Call emergency services immediately after injection — go to the ED even if symptoms improve.
  3. If no improvement or symptoms recur, administer a second epinephrine dose after 5 minutes (repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed) and continue to seek emergency care.
  4. Lie supine with legs elevated if tolerable; if vomiting or respiratory distress, sit up to protect airway.
  5. Inform caregivers and wear medical‑alert identification.
- Reinforce that antihistamines and corticosteroids are NOT substitutes for epinephrine; they may be used for symptom control but do not prevent respiratory/circulatory collapse.
- Demonstrate storage (room temperature, check expiry), replacement after use, and advising family members/caregivers in device use.
- Provide printed emergency card summarizing the allergic trigger, reaction severity, epinephrine dosing, and emergency contacts.

3) Short‑term discharge medications and counseling
- Provide two epinephrine auto‑injectors with clear dosing labeled (0.5 mg IM each) and prescriptions for replacements.
- Non‑sedating H1 antihistamine (eg, cetirizine/levocetirizine) may be prescribed for urticaria/pruritus; explain it is adjunctive only.
- Routine systemic corticosteroids are not recommended solely to prevent biphasic reactions; evidence does not support routine use and harms exist. Use steroids only if there is a separate clinical indication or by specialist advice. (In the case report steroids were used empirically, reflecting variability in practice.)
- Consider advising on avoidance of beta‑blockers if possible (they blunt epinephrine response) — discuss with primary clinician if patient needs beta‑blocker therapy.

4) Medication avoidance, documentation and communication
- Explicitly document “Anaphylaxis to clarithromycin (macrolide?) — avoid clarithromycin and consider avoiding all macrolides until evaluated by an allergist” in the hospital chart, discharge summary, and primary care record.
- Provide the patient with a written allergy card/letter that includes: agent (clarithromycin), date, nature and severity (anaphylaxis with hypotension and hypoxaemia), treatments given (epinephrine x number of doses, ICU admission), and recommended avoidance.
- Update electronic medical record allergy list and communicate directly (phone or secure message) with the primary care physician and any treating specialists about the reaction and planned follow‑up.

5) Allergy/immunology follow‑up: timing and testing principles
- Arrange allergy/immunology follow‑up promptly (preferably within 1–4 weeks) to:
  - Review the event and determine plan for further testing.
  - Arrange formal baseline tryptase measurement (≥24 hours after full resolution) and repeat testing to establish baseline if elevated — important because persistent elevation suggests mast‑cell disorder and alters risk counseling.
  - Plan definitive allergy testing only when safe: skin testing and drug provocation (if indicated) are usually deferred for at least 4–6 weeks after anaphylaxis, and require stopping antihistamines for an appropriate washout before skin testing.
- In patients with elevated baseline tryptase or clinical suspicion of systemic mastocytosis (as in this case), expedite specialist assessment (allergy/immunology ± hematology) because mast‑cell disease changes risk stratification and peri‑procedural/premedication recommendations.
- Do not attempt provocative testing with the implicated antibiotic in the acute post‑event period; consider in vitro testing only if available and useful; in many instances avoidance without provocation is reasonable if safe alternative antibiotics exist.

6) Special considerations for this case (illustrated by the report)
- The patient weighed 104 kg and had recurrent episodes requiring multiple epinephrine doses; this history supports prescribing 0.5 mg auto‑injector(s) and carrying two devices.
- Her elevated tryptase and suspected systemic mastocytosis raise her baseline risk for severe and recurrent episodes — strengthen the case for two devices, close follow‑up, and expedited specialist assessment.
- Document the multiphasic course and ICU admission in discharge paperwork so that outpatient providers understand the severity and rationale for aggressive follow‑up and device prescriptions.

Summary statement for discharge note
- “Severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin with ICU admission and elevated tryptase. Discharged with two epinephrine auto‑injectors (0.5 mg IM each), instructed in immediate use and to call EMS after use; carrying medical‑alert ID; avoid clarithromycin (and consider avoiding macrolides until allergist review); started on oral nonsedating antihistamine for symptom control. Allergy/immunology follow‑up arranged within 1–4 weeks for tryptase baseline and to plan safe testing/definitive management; consider hematology input for suspected mastocytosis.”
