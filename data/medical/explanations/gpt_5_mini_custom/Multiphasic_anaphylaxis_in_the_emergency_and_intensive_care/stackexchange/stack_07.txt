Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] Outline the recommended timing and interpretation of serum tryptase measurements in suspected anaphylaxis, including when to obtain acute and baseline samples and how to apply the “20% + 2 ng/mL” rule; how would persistent baseline elevation influence evaluation for a mast cell disorder?

Key point: serum tryptase is a marker of mast‑cell degranulation and can support (but not replace) the clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis. Timing of sampling and comparison with a patient’s baseline are essential for correct interpretation.

Recommended timing
- Obtain an acute sample as soon as possible after recognition of anaphylaxis — do not delay life‑saving treatment to draw blood.  
- Obtain a second “peak” sample at about 1–2 hours after symptom onset (acceptable up to 4 hours) because tryptase generally peaks about 1–2 hours after mast‑cell activation.  
- Obtain a baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution (commonly 24–48 hours) to establish the patient’s steady‑state tryptase. Ideally you will have: (1) immediate, (2) 1–2 h (peak), and (3) ≥24 h (baseline) samples; many centres obtain at least an acute and a baseline sample.

Interpretation: the “20% + 2 ng/mL” rule
- Absolute cutoffs (eg, >11.4 ng/mL) are insensitive. The accepted diagnostic approach is to compare the acute (peak) level to the patient’s baseline using the “20% + 2 ng/mL” rule.  
- Formula: a rise is significant if acute tryptase ≥ (baseline × 1.2) + 2 ng/mL.  
- Examples:
  - baseline 5 ng/mL → threshold = 5×1.2 + 2 = 8 ng/mL (acute ≥8 is significant).  
  - baseline 11 ng/mL → threshold = 11×1.2 + 2 = 15.2 ng/mL (acute ≥15.2 is significant).  
This rule increases sensitivity for true mast‑cell activation across a range of baselines.

Clinical caveats
- A normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis (especially food‑triggered anaphylaxis or if sampling is late). Clinical diagnosis and prompt treatment remain paramount.  
- Missing the peak sample (eg, only a late acute sample or only a single baseline later) limits interpretability — as in the presented case, where recommended intervals were not fully adhered to and peak measurements were therefore suboptimal.

Persistent baseline elevation — next steps and implications
- A persistently elevated baseline tryptase (measured after full recovery, and confirmed on repeat testing) raises concern for an underlying mast‑cell disorder.  
- WHO minor criterion for systemic mastocytosis: baseline serum tryptase persistently >20 ng/mL (in the absence of an associated clonal myeloid neoplasm). Even lower persistent elevations merit further evaluation when clinical features (recurrent severe anaphylaxis, hepatosplenomegaly, skin lesions, bone disease) are present.  
- Recommended evaluation if baseline is persistently elevated or clinical suspicion is high:
  - Repeat baseline tryptase to confirm persistence.  
  - Refer to allergy/immunology and hematology for: bone marrow examination with morphology and immunophenotyping (CD25/CD2 on mast cells), testing for KIT D816V mutation (PCR), and consideration of other investigations (abdominal imaging for hepatosplenomegaly, bone density).  
  - Test for hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (TPSAB1 copy number) because increased TPSAB1 copies can raise baseline tryptase and increase anaphylaxis risk; this has different implications from clonal mast‑cell disease.  
  - Exclude other causes of elevated tryptase (eg, certain myeloid neoplasms, severe renal failure) as clinically indicated.

Apply this to the case
- The case illustrates the pitfalls of incomplete sampling: the team obtained a tryptase 1 hour after the third episode and a “baseline” 27 hours after the last recurrence, but missed earlier recommended timepoints, limiting some interpretability. Nevertheless, the report documents persistently elevated baseline tryptase on follow‑up and other suggestive findings (hepatomegaly, recurrent severe/multiphasic anaphylaxis), prompting further evaluation for systemic mastocytosis. Bone‑marrow sampling was inconclusive, so formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met — a situation where KIT testing, flow cytometry for aberrant mast‑cell markers and TPSAB1 genotyping are particularly useful next steps.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- In any suspected anaphylaxis: draw tryptase early and again at ~1–2 hours (or within 4 hours), and obtain a baseline ≥24 h after complete recovery. Use the 20% + 2 ng/mL rule to identify significant mast‑cell activation. If baseline tryptase is persistently elevated, refer for evaluation for systemic mast‑cell disease (including KIT mutation testing, bone‑marrow assessment) and consider hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia testing — because identifying a mast‑cell disorder alters risk stratification and long‑term management.
