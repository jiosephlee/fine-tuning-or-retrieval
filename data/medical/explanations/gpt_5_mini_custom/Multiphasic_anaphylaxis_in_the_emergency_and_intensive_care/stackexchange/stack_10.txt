Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] List established and proposed risk factors for biphasic anaphylaxis (e.g., delayed epinephrine, severe initial presentation, need for multiple epinephrine doses, prior biphasic reactions), and identify which of these were present in this case.

Clinical answer (Clinical Pearl)

What predicts biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis?
- Established and repeatedly proposed risk factors include:
  - Severe initial presentation (circulatory collapse, hypoxaemia, airway compromise). Severe initial mediator release and end‑organ involvement are consistently associated with higher risk of recurrence.
  - Delayed administration of epinephrine. Data show that delayed first epinephrine is linked to more complications and higher biphasic risk—presumably because ongoing mediator release is less well controlled.
  - Need for multiple doses of epinephrine or escalation of care during the index event. Requirement for repeated epinephrine is a marker of refractory or more intense mediator activation and correlates with recurrence.
  - High or rising serum tryptase during the event and elevated baseline tryptase. These reflect a greater mast cell burden/activation and are increasingly recognized as markers of severe and potentially recurrent reactions.
  - Prior biphasic or severe anaphylactic reactions (history of recurrent severe reactions) — a history of complicated prior events suggests predisposition.
- Other proposed/less well‑validated risk factors (reported variably):
  - Large or ongoing antigen exposure (oral ingestion with ongoing absorption, depot formulations).
  - Older age, obesity, or significant cardiopulmonary comorbidity.
  - Concomitant medications that interfere with response to epinephrine (eg, beta‑blockers) or enhance severity (ACE inhibitors) — data are mixed.
  - Absence of early cutaneous signs or predominant cardiovascular/respiratory features (harder to recognize early).
- Important caveat: predictors are imperfect; overall incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis is low and the literature shows wide variability. Routine corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions.

Applying these to the presented case
- Present and likely contributed:
  - Severe initial presentation: yes — profound hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO2 88%), widespread urticaria and facial swelling with rapid progression. This meets the classic “severe” phenotype linked to recurrence.
  - Requirement for multiple epinephrine doses: yes — the patient had four anaphylactic episodes and required repeated intramuscular epinephrine to treat each relapse, which is a marker of more refractory mediator activity and higher relapse risk.
  - Elevated/baseline tryptase and probable systemic mastocytosis: yes — tryptase was elevated during the course and remained high on follow‑up, and further workup suggested a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. A high mast cell burden is a biologically plausible and increasingly recognized risk factor for severe and recurrent (including biphasic or multiphasic) anaphylaxis.
  - Ongoing antigen exposure: unlikely — only a single 500 mg oral clarithromycin dose was taken and the drug was discontinued; ongoing absorption was not suspected.
  - Prior biphasic reactions: no — this was her first anaphylactic episode historically.
- Potentially important caveat in this case:
  - Timing of first epinephrine was prompt after ED arrival (not classically “delayed”), but the initial dose used was 0.3 mg IM (auto‑injector). For a 60s woman weighing 104 kg this is below the commonly cited adult target (0.01 mg/kg, often rounded to 0.5 mg for adults), and underdosing has been associated in some series with need for additional doses/escalation of care — effectively behaving like inadequate initial therapy. Thus, although treatment was timely, the initial underdosing and the patient’s obesity likely reduced initial potency of intervention and may have contributed to relapses.
- Other factors in the case that do not explain biphasic risk:
  - No beta‑blocker use or other medications clearly known to blunt epinephrine effect; no clear ongoing allergen source; age and comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, obesity) may have modest influence but are not primary drivers in the literature.

Bottom line (teaching point)
- Biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but more likely after a severe initial event, when multiple epinephrine doses are required, and when mast cell burden is high (eg, elevated tryptase/systemic mastocytosis). In this patient the key risk factors present were severe initial shock, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and raised tryptase with probable systemic mastocytosis; initial epinephrine dosing was timely but suboptimal for body weight, which may have functionally contributed to relapse risk. These findings support prolonged observation and specialist follow‑up (allergology/immunology, consideration of mast cell disorder workup) after the index event.
