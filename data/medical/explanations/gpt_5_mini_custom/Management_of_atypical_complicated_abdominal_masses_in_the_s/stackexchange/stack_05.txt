Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] What are the accepted indications to operate on a pelvic mass or leiomyoma during pregnancy (for example, torsion, rupture, infection, intractable pain, obstructive symptoms, or strong oncologic suspicion), and what timing and operative principles minimize maternal–fetal risk?

Short answer
Operate in pregnancy only for true indications: acute complications (torsion, rupture, hemorrhage), uncontrolled infection of the mass, severe intractable pain or cardiorespiratory/urinary obstruction from mass effect, or strong oncologic concern that cannot be resolved non‑operatively. When surgery is required, the second trimester (mid‑trimester) is the usual window for elective procedures; emergencies are managed regardless of gestation. Minimize maternal–fetal risk by multidisciplinary planning, limiting the procedure to what is necessary, choosing the safest surgical approach for the size/location, meticulous haemostasis, intra‑ and postoperative fetal monitoring, and perioperative measures to preserve uterine quiescence and hemodynamic stability.

Clinical reasoning and principles (with points illustrated by the case)
1) Accepted indications to operate
- Acute complications: torsion of a pedunculated mass, rupture with haemorrhage, or infected/necrotic leiomyoma that risks sepsis — these are accepted urgent indications. In the case, the cranial mass was a highly vascular, twisted (torsed) pedunculated leiomyoma with leakage and features consistent with ischemic necrosis and infection; that justified resection.
- Intractable symptoms or organ compromise: severe, progressive pain, restrictive dyspnoea or urinary/intestinal obstruction that cannot be controlled expectantly. The patient’s worsening pain and dyspnoea from mass effect were important drivers for intervention.
- Strong oncologic suspicion or diagnostic uncertainty that cannot be resolved with safe imaging and would change management—surgery may be required for diagnosis and cure. In this case a rising CA‑125 and mixed solid/cystic imaging features contributed to diagnostic uncertainty and factored into the decision.
- Elective myomectomy for asymptomatic leiomyomas is generally deferred to the postpartum period because of risks (bleeding, preterm labour, pregnancy loss). The multidisciplinary team in this report therefore removed only the dangerous pedunculated lesion and left the adherent pelvic myoma in situ.

2) Timing
- Elective procedures that can be delayed are usually scheduled in the second trimester (classically 14–28 weeks) because organogenesis is complete and the risk of spontaneous abortion is lower than in the first trimester, and the uterus is not yet at its maximal size as in the third trimester. The team operated at 21+6 weeks, a typical mid‑trimester timing for an elective/urgent procedure.
- Emergency surgery (torsion, rupture, severe infection, uncontrolled haemorrhage) must be performed regardless of gestational age.

3) Preoperative evaluation and imaging
- Use pelvic/abdominal ultrasound with Doppler as first line; add non‑contrast MRI if further characterization is needed. Avoid gadolinium unless the benefit clearly outweighs fetal risk. In the case, ultrasound followed by non‑contrast MRI clarified the anatomy without contrast exposure.
- Laboratory evaluation (CBC, blood type and crossmatch) and selective tumour markers can inform the probability of malignancy, but markers such as CA‑125 are physiologically altered in pregnancy and interpreted cautiously; the slight CA‑125 rise in this patient contributed to concern but was not diagnostic.

4) Operative approach and intraoperative principles to minimize risk
- Multidisciplinary team: obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anesthesia, and neonatology must be involved. Obtain informed consent that includes risk of pregnancy loss, preterm labour and need for transfusion or obstetric interventions.
- Choose approach based on size/location and surgeon experience: laparoscopy can be safe early in pregnancy for small, accessible lesions (Hasson/open entry preferential), but large, multiple, posterior or adherent masses frequently necessitate laparotomy — as in this report where the large, vascular masses prompted a longitudinal laparotomy.
- Limit the operation to what is necessary: remove the offending lesion(s) only. In this case the team excised the torsed pedunculated myoma and left the large adherent pelvic myoma in situ to avoid undue uterine trauma.
- Minimize uterine manipulation and incision length; keep operative time short to reduce fetal stress. Use careful technique for pedicle control (double clamp or ligation), and close the uterine or stalk defect with absorbable suture to restore integrity.
- Meticulous haemostasis and blood preparedness: crossmatch blood, have cell salvage or transfusion capability. Be prepared for significant bleeding during myomectomy.
- Anesthesia goals: maintain uteroplacental perfusion (avoid hypotension), position the patient with left lateral tilt, and avoid teratogenic drugs. Document fetal heart activity preoperatively, intra‑operatively as feasible, and postoperatively — the team followed this principle per cited recommendations.
- Tocolysis/prophylaxis: routine prophylactic tocolytics are not universally recommended, but many teams give perioperative progesterone or tocolytics selectively. The team used intramuscular progesterone intra‑ and post‑operatively until delivery.
- Antibiotics: perioperative prophylaxis and targeted therapy for documented infection (the patient had Klebsiella UTI and received cefazolin and clarithromycin guided by cultures).

5) Postoperative care and delivery planning
- Monitor for preterm labour, fetal growth, and maternal anaemia/infection. Correct anaemia promptly as the team did with IV iron and transfusion.
- Decide delivery mode based on residual myomas’ size/location and obstetric considerations. A large residual myoma that obstructs the birth canal may mandate elective cesarean; here the residual pelvic myoma predicted obstructed labour and the team planned an elective CS at 37 weeks via the same scar, with good outcome.

Bottom line
Surgery in pregnancy is justified for acute complications (torsion, rupture, infection), severe mass effect not amenable to conservative care, or compelling oncologic concern. When required, aim to operate in the safest window (mid‑second trimester if elective), limit the operation to the minimum necessary, use appropriate imaging (US and non‑contrast MRI), plan with a multidisciplinary team, secure haemostasis and fetal monitoring, and individualize perioperative measures (antibiotics, progesterone/tocolysis) to preserve pregnancy and reduce maternal and fetal risk. The presented case exemplifies these principles: targeted resection of a torsed, leaking pedunculated leiomyoma in the mid‑second trimester preserved the pregnancy and led to a favorable maternal and neonatal outcome.
