Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] How do the size, number, and location of residual uterine leiomyomas guide delivery planning—including the need for cesarean delivery, choice of uterine incision, and timing near term—particularly when a large posterior lower-segment mass could obstruct descent?

Bottom line
- Size, number and—most important—the location of leiomyomas determine whether vaginal birth is reasonable or whether a planned cesarean is the safer option. A fixed, large mass in the lower uterine segment or pelvis that occupies the pelvic outlet (as in this case) is a mechanical obstruction to descent and is an indication to plan a cesarean delivery rather than attempt labour.

Why these factors matter (clinical reasoning)
- Mechanical obstruction: a large posterior or isthmic myoma that projects into the pelvic cavity can physically block engagement and the expulsive phase of labour. In the case presented the residual “caudal” myoma was adherent to the posterior uterine torus and measured on follow‑up ~7.5 cm; the team judged it would impede fetal head progression and therefore elected an early-term CS.
- Uterine contractility and malpresentation: multiple or large intramural myomas distort uterine geometry and may impair effective contractions and lead to malpresentation or labour dystocia; this increases the likelihood of intrapartum cesarean.
- Size dynamics in pregnancy: myomas often enlarge in early pregnancy (growth greatest in 1st–2nd trimester) but may stabilize or decrease in the late third trimester/postpartum. That potential for partial regression must be weighed against the risk of spontaneous labour and acute complications (torsion, degeneration, haemorrhage).

How to evaluate pre‑delivery
- Map size, number and exact relation to the uterine cavity and lower uterine segment with transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI when needed—this was done in the case to define continuity with the posterior wall. Bimanual exam also helps assess pelvic obstruction.
- Reassess near term: imaging close to delivery is useful because sizes can change during pregnancy and the operative plan (incision choice, need for myomectomy) depends on current anatomy.

Implications for mode and timing of delivery
- Vaginal delivery can be attempted when myomas are small, not in the lower segment, not distorting the pelvis or presenting as fixed obstructing masses, and when there are no other obstetric contraindications.
- Plan an elective cesarean when a myoma is:
  - Fixed in the lower uterine segment or pelvis and likely to obstruct descent (as in this report), or
  - Multiple/large and causing malpresentation, significant haemorrhage risk, or placenta-related problems.
- Timing: arrange delivery once fetal maturity is achieved (often 37 weeks) rather than waiting for spontaneous labour if obstruction is likely—this minimizes emergency surgery and the risk of scar rupture or intrapartum complications. The team in the case scheduled CS at 37 weeks for these reasons.

Choice of uterine incision and intraoperative considerations
- If the lower uterine segment is unobstructed, a low transverse incision is preferred for least morbidity and lower risk of uterine rupture in future pregnancies.
- If a lower-segment myoma occupies the planned incision site, options are:
  - Perform a transverse or classical vertical (midline) uterine incision above the myoma (classical incision carries higher subsequent rupture risk), or
  - Remove the myoma (cesarean myomectomy) to allow a low transverse incision—this is feasible but requires experienced surgeons, good haemostatic technique, readiness for transfusion, and patient counselling about risks. The literature cited in the report suggests CS myomectomy can be done safely in select patients but increases operative time and hospital stay.
- Prepare for blood loss: crossmatched blood available, uterotonics, cell‑salvage if available, and experienced surgical team. The report notes the team considered bleeding risk and chose a staged approach (left the posterior myoma in situ) to minimise obstetric risk.

Counselling and multidisciplinary planning
- Discuss with the patient the likelihood of cesarean vs trial of labour, the possible need for intraoperative myomectomy, the potential for increased bleeding, and implications of different uterine incisions for future pregnancies.
- Multidisciplinary planning (obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anesthesia, blood bank) is essential. In the case, that approach guided the decision to leave the caudal myoma in place and to schedule an elective CS at term, balancing maternal/fetal safety with avoidance of overtreatment.

Application to this case
- The team removed the pedunculated upper myoma at 21+6 weeks for symptom control and diagnostic certainty but left the fixed caudal posterior myoma in situ because it was adherent and removal would increase obstetric risk. They monitored the residual mass (which regressed slightly to ~7.5 cm) and elected an elective cesarean at 37 weeks because the mass was anticipated to obstruct head descent—an individualized, evidence‑based plan that led to an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

Practical checklist before delivery in similar patients
- Repeat imaging (US ± MRI) near term to map current anatomy.
- Decide mode of delivery based on location/size: lower‑segment/pelvic obstruction → plan cesarean.
- Decide planned uterine incision and whether removal of the myoma at CS is intended.
- Ensure blood products and multidisciplinary team availability.
- Counsel patient about risks, alternatives, and implications for future pregnancies.
