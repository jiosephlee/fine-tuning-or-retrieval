Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a second-trimester pregnant patient with large symptomatic abdominopelvic masses, which imaging modalities are preferred for characterization, what safety considerations apply (including recommended magnet strength and the use of gadolinium), and what added value can non-contrast MRI provide beyond ultrasound?

Direct answer
- First-line imaging for large symptomatic abdominopelvic masses in the second trimester is transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler; non‑contrast MRI is the preferred second‑line study for further characterisation. CT and contrast studies are avoided when possible because they use ionizing radiation or intravenous gadolinium.
- Safety considerations: MRI without gadolinium is considered acceptably safe in pregnancy and was appropriately used in this case. Most centres prefer a 1.5‑T magnet in pregnant patients (the team in the report specifically avoided >1.5 T). Intravenous gadolinium crosses the placenta and should be avoided in pregnancy unless the diagnostic information cannot be obtained any other way and the benefit outweighs the theoretical fetal risk; routine gadolinium is not recommended.
- Added value of non‑contrast MRI beyond ultrasound: superior tissue characterisation, precise anatomic mapping (number, size, intramural vs subserosal vs pedunculated), evaluation of degeneration/haemorrhage/necrosis, detection of cleavage planes and continuity with the uterus, assessment of relationship to the uterine cavity and placenta, and improved surgical planning — all without ionizing radiation or gadolinium exposure.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (with reference to the case)
- Start with ultrasound: it is bedside, fast, safe and excellent for initial triage (solid vs cystic, vascularity with Doppler, approximate size and pelvic origin). In the case report ultrasound identified two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularisation and suggested the caudal lesion was adherent to a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma.
- When ultrasound is limited by body habitus, gravid uterus, large lesion size, posterior location or indeterminate tissue features, non‑contrast MRI adds important information. MRI sequences (T1, T2, fat‑suppressed, diffusion‑weighted imaging) allow characterisation of internal contents (blood products, proteinaceous/decidualised material, cystic degeneration, solid components) and depict thickened walls, focal solid nodules, and vascular pedicles. In this patient MRI showed thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content and focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components and suggested continuity of the caudal mass with the posterior leiomyoma — findings that narrowed the differential between decidualised endometrioma and cystic degenerating leiomyoma and guided management.
- MRI also clarifies surgical anatomy: it can show whether a mass is pedunculated (risk of torsion), whether it communicates with the uterine wall (myoma vs adnexal), and absence/presence of adenopathy or invasive features. These data informed the team’s decision to remove the twisted pedunculated uterine mass and leave the adherent pelvic myoma in situ to minimise obstetric risk.

Safety details to remember
- Timing: MRI can be performed in any trimester when clinically indicated; many teams perform non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester when practicable, but urgent indications may warrant earlier imaging.
- Magnet strength: 1.5 T is the customary and conservative choice in pregnancy; routine avoidance of higher field strengths (3 T) is common practice in many centres because of more limited safety data and higher specific‑absorption rates.
- Gadolinium: crosses the placenta and is retained longer in the fetal compartment; observational data suggest a small association with adverse neonatal outcomes in some cohorts. Use only when essential for management and when no alternative sequence or imaging will provide the diagnosis; if used, document informed consent and use the lowest effective dose and preferred agents per local policy.
- No ionizing radiation: MRI avoids radiation exposure and is preferable to CT when soft‑tissue characterisation is required.

Practical clinical pearl
- Use ultrasound first for rapid assessment and Doppler evaluation; proceed to non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T, tailored T1/T2/fat‑sat/DWI sequences) when ultrasound is inconclusive or when precise anatomic mapping will change management. Avoid gadolinium in pregnancy unless there is a compelling, documented indication and the benefit clearly outweighs risk. The case illustrates how non‑contrast MRI added decisive information (inhomogeneous contents, solid nodularity, uterine continuity) that helped distinguish cystic degenerating leiomyoma from adnexal pathology and guided a safe, conservative surgical approach.
