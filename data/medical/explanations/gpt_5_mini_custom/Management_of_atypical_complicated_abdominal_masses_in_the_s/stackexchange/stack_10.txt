Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] How should perioperative iron-deficiency anemia in mid-pregnancy be managed when urgent non-obstetric surgery is needed—what are the roles of intravenous iron versus red blood cell transfusion, and what thresholds and targets balance maternal symptoms, fetal oxygen delivery, and procedural risk?

Direct answer
In a pregnant patient needing urgent non‑obstetric surgery, treat iron‑deficiency anemia by balancing the time available before operation, the immediacy of oxygen‑carrying capacity required, and fetal oxygenation. Use intravenous (IV) iron when there is time to correct stores or to accelerate postoperative recovery; use red‑cell transfusion when immediate improvement in oxygen delivery is required (hemodynamic instability, severe or symptomatic anemia, or anticipated major blood loss). Aim pragmatically for a post‑treatment hemoglobin (Hb) that relieves maternal symptoms and avoids perioperative hypoxia—usually ≥8 g/dL, higher (≈9–10 g/dL) when there is ongoing bleeding, cardiopulmonary disease, or concern for fetal compromise.

Why this approach (clinical reasoning and physiology)
- Pregnancy increases plasma volume more than red‑cell mass, so physiologic dilutional anemia occurs, but true iron‑deficiency reduces maternal oxygen content and impairs fetal oxygen delivery if severe. Fetal oxygenation depends on maternal oxygen content and uterine perfusion; severe maternal anemia risks fetal hypoxia.
- IV iron replenishes iron stores and allows erythropoiesis, but it is not an immediate correction of oxygen‑carrying capacity. Reticulocytosis begins within days; clinically meaningful Hb rises usually take 1–2 weeks (though some rise may be seen earlier).
- Transfusion provides immediate, predictable increase in oxygen‑carrying capacity (one adult unit ≈ +1 g/dL Hb), and is therefore indicated when rapid correction is necessary for maternal stability or to mitigate fetal risk during/after surgery. Transfusion carries infectious, immunologic and volume risks and should be used selectively.

Practical thresholds and targets
- Stable pregnant patient, no active bleeding, short delay possible: if Hb ≥10 g/dL, proceed with careful monitoring; if Hb 8–10 g/dL and iron deficiency documented, give IV iron pre‑op if >3–7 days to surgery (or intra/post‑op if earlier) and consider proceeding; transfuse only if symptomatic or hemodynamically unstable.
- Hb <7 g/dL (or <8 g/dL with symptoms, cardiac disease, or ongoing bleeding): transfuse red cells to achieve an Hb ≥8 g/dL, with an individualized higher target (≈9–10 g/dL) if there is major blood loss risk or concern for fetal oxygenation.
- For planned transfusion, estimate number of units using desired Hb increment (each unit ≈ +1 g/dL) and body size; reassess after each unit.
- Use IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose, iron sucrose) in the second and third trimesters as standard therapy for iron‑deficiency during pregnancy to restore stores and reduce subsequent transfusions; typical single dosing (eg, ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) is effective and was used safely in the reported case.

How this applied in the case
- The patient presented pre‑operatively with iron‑deficiency anemia (Hb 9.9 g/dL, ferritin 25 mcg/L) and required urgent laparotomy at 21+ weeks. Because immediate correction of oxygen carrying capacity was not required pre‑op, surgery proceeded with intraoperative fetal monitoring and hemodynamic surveillance.
- Postoperative Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL with ongoing iron deficiency; clinicians appropriately combined RBC transfusion (two units) to quickly restore oxygen delivery and IV ferric carboxymaltose 1 g to replenish iron and support erythropoietic recovery. The combined strategy raised Hb to 10 g/dL and the mother and fetus did well—illustrating the complementary roles of transfusion (immediate correction) and IV iron (rapid restoration of iron and sustained Hb recovery).

Monitoring, safety and team processes
- Document maternal hemodynamics and fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery. Discuss transfusion risks and alternatives with the patient and obtain consent.
- Screen for iron deficiency preoperatively with Hb, ferritin and, if available, transferrin saturation; if surgery can be delayed, give IV iron to avoid transfusion.
- Be prepared for transfusion if intraoperative or postoperative bleeding occurs. Use multidisciplinary care (obstetrics, anesthesia, surgery, hematology/blood bank) to individualize thresholds.

Clinical pearl
When time is limited, IV iron is an important tool to reduce later transfusion needs and speed recovery, but it does not replace transfusion when immediate oxygen‑carrying capacity is required; integrate both strategies based on Hb level, symptoms, hemodynamics, bleeding risk and fetal considerations, as in the presented case.
