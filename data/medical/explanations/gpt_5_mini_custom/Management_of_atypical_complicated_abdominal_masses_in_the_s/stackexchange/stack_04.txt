Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] On ultrasound or MRI, what specific signs help localize a large pelvic mass to uterine versus ovarian origin in pregnancy (for example, bridging vessels from the uterine artery on Doppler/MRI, continuity with myometrium or a pedicle, identification of a normal ipsilateral ovary, the ovarian crescent sign), and how would each influence management?

Bottom line
- On pregnancy ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI, the single most useful clues that a large pelvic mass is uterine (leiomyoma) rather than ovarian are: direct continuity with the myometrium (claw sign/“bulging” myometrium), a pedicle or stalk, and vascular connections to the uterine arterial system (bridging‑vessel or vascular pedicle sign on color Doppler/MRI). Findings that favour ovarian origin are visualization of the normal ipsilateral ovary separate from the mass and the ovarian crescent sign (rim of ovarian tissue abutting a cyst). Each sign changes preoperative planning and management (expectation of hemorrhage, likelihood of conservative management, timing and indication for surgery).  

Key imaging signs and what they mean for management
- Continuity with myometrium (claw sign, myometrial “cleavage”): when the mass appears to be part of or continuous with the uterine wall on ultrasound or MRI, a uterine origin is very likely. Clinical consequence: most intramural/subserosal leiomyomas are managed conservatively in pregnancy unless they cause severe pain, torsion, infection, obstruct delivery, or necrosis; if surgery is required, anticipate a myometrial repair rather than adnexal resection and prepare for potential bleeding and fetal monitoring.

- Pedicle/stalk (visible on US or MRI): a pedunculated subserosal fibroid often demonstrates a discrete stalk attaching to the uterus. Clinical consequence: pedunculated fibroids can torsion, become ischemic/infected and often mandate surgical removal in pregnancy if symptomatic (as in this case, where the cranial mass proved to be a twisted, pedunculated leiomyoma). Knowing a stalk exists helps plan a focused myomectomy with control of the root.

- Bridging vessels / vascular pedicle sign on color Doppler or MR angiography sequences: vessels running between uterus and mass (feeding from uterine arteries) strongly favour uterine origin. Clinical consequence: confirms uterine supply → anticipate heavy vascularity at surgery, consider cross‑matched blood, hemostatic strategies and uterine‑sparing repair; helps differentiate from ovarian masses which are usually supplied by ovarian vessels.

- Myoma MRI signal pattern: classic leiomyomas are T2‑hypointense relative to myometrium; degenerating/cystic fibroids have mixed signal but retain continuity with uterus. Clinical consequence: signal patterns plus continuity support conservative management unless complications occur.

- Visualization of a normal ipsilateral ovary separate from the mass: seeing the ovary distinct from the lesion makes ovarian origin likely. Clinical consequence: ovarian cysts in pregnancy that are simple and small can be observed; large, complex, symptomatic, torsed or suspicious lesions require surgical management (adnexectomy or cystectomy) with oncologic precautions when indicated.

- Ovarian crescent sign: a rim or crescent of ovarian tissue around a cystic lesion supports ovarian origin. Clinical consequence: if benign‑appearing, can often be observed; if complex or growing, surgical intervention is more likely.

Practical caveats illustrated by the case
- Imaging can be limited in pregnancy by uterine enlargement and mass bulk. In this patient the large cranial lesion was mischaracterized preoperatively as likely ovarian because a pedicle and feeding vessel were not identified on imaging; intraoperatively it was a pedunculated, twisted uterine leiomyoma. That teaches that absence of a visible stalk/bridging vessel does not exclude uterine origin when anatomy is distorted.  
- The caudal mass showed apparent continuity with a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma on imaging and was left in situ to avoid obstetric risk—an example of how demonstrating uterine continuity can push toward expectant management during pregnancy.

Imaging strategy in pregnancy
- Start with targeted transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound with color Doppler: seek a pedicle, bridging vessels, a claw of myometrium, and visualize ovaries.  
- Use non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T, no gadolinium) when ultrasound is inconclusive to demonstrate myometrial continuity, stalks, and tissue signal characteristics.  
- Do not rely solely on CA‑125 or tumor markers in pregnancy; they are often non‑specific.

How findings change management
- Uterine origin with continuity/pedicle/bridging vessels → usually conservative; intervene surgically only for complications (torsion, severe degeneration/infection, refractory pain, obstruction of labour). If surgery is done, prepare for uterine bleeding and fetal monitoring.  
- Ovarian origin with separate normal ovary/ovarian crescent → small, simple cysts may be observed; complex, large, torsed or suspicious lesions require timely surgical management with considerations for oncologic staging if features suggest malignancy.  
- Indeterminate imaging + symptoms or signs of complication (fever, persistent severe pain, respiratory compromise from mass effect) → multidisciplinary discussion and early surgical exploration may be required, accepting that intraoperative findings may differ from preop imaging (as in this report).

Clinical pearl
- Look first for continuity with the uterus (claw sign/pedicle) and for bridging vessels on Doppler; these two findings most reliably point to a leiomyoma and change the risk profile (vascular uterine supply, myometrial repair, obstetric implications). If neither is demonstrable and the ovary is seen apart from the lesion or an ovarian crescent is present, consider ovarian origin and manage according to ovarian‑mass principles. Always integrate imaging with symptoms and multidisciplinary risk assessment when deciding on surgery in pregnancy.
