Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Myomectomy During Pregnancy: Take One, Leave One

What makes this case worth pausing for? A woman in her 40s at 21+ weeks gestation presented with two very large abdominal cystic masses and respiratory compromise from sheer space-occupying effect. Imaging and tumour markers were inconclusive. At laparotomy the “upper” mass — thought possibly ovarian or decidualised endometrioma — turned out to be a pedunculated, twisted, highly vascular uterine leiomyoma that was leaking and likely the source of fever and pain. The team resected that lesion and deliberately left a second large intramural-subserosal myoma in situ to preserve the pregnancy. Mother and baby did well — a tidy example of targeted, risk-balanced surgery in pregnancy.

This case illustrates the core decision-making we face with symptomatic pelvic masses in pregnancy: when to operate, what to remove, and how to plan for the worst while aiming for the best obstetric outcome.

## Why not remove both? The rationale for "take one, leave one"
- One lesion was pedunculated, twisted, leaking, highly vascular and producing acute symptoms (pain, low‑grade fever) plus restrictive dyspnoea from mass effect. These factors raised the immediate risk of torsion, necrosis, infection and further deterioration — reasonable indications for surgical intervention in pregnancy.
- The caudal mass was an adherent intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma fixed to the uterine torus and likely to interfere with labour if left large — but it was not acutely complicated. Removing it would have prolonged the operation, increased uterine manipulation and bleeding risk, and increased chance of pregnancy loss. Leaving it reduced operative time and obstetric risk.
- The operative plan prioritized maternal stability and fetal viability: fix the imminent problem (twisted pedunculated myoma), minimise uterine trauma, and accept staged management postpartum if needed.

That risk‑stratified approach — remove the lesion that threatens mother/fetus now, defer the rest — is the practical essence of “take one, leave one.”

## Key practical points from the case (operative planning and execution)
- Multidisciplinary prep is essential. This team included obstetrics, gynecology, surgery and anesthesiology — coordinate blood availability, neonatal team notification, and agree fetal monitoring plan.
- Imaging: use targeted ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI in pregnancy to characterise masses. Avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary. In this case MRI without contrast helped establish cystic nature and relationships.
- Indications for surgery in pregnancy remain acute symptoms (intractable pain), complications (torsion, rupture, infection, bleeding), diagnostic uncertainty suggesting malignancy, or when mass effect threatens maternal respiratory or circulatory status. This patient had pain, dyspnoea, fever and imaging uncertainty — reasonable grounds for laparotomy.
- Choose approach to minimise operative time and uterine handling: in large, multiple, posterior or intramural lesions and at mid‑pregnancy, open laparotomy may be safer than laparoscopy. Laparoscopy can be considered for smaller, pedunculated or accessible lesions in early gestation.
- Hemorrhage planning:
  - Have cross‑matched blood available and transfusion plan ready. In this case the patient required post‑op transfusion (and IV iron).
  - Prepare uterotonics and surgical hemostatic adjuncts. Use efficient uterine repair (suture the pedicle root promptly and securely after excision).
  - Use techniques to reduce bleeding: double clamps on the pedicle, prompt suture closure of the implantation site, judicious electrosurgery or topical hemostatics as indicated.
- Uterine repair: use absorbable sutures to close the root of the excised stalk; keep sutures neat and minimal to reduce nidus for scar dehiscence while achieving hemostasis. Minimise deep myometrial disruption where possible.
- Intraoperative fetal assessment: document fetal heart rate before, during (intermittently) and after the procedure. Use intraoperative ultrasound if available to confirm fetal cardiac activity and position; continuous fetal monitoring isn’t always possible in mid‑pregnancy so document intermittently.
- Perioperative medications: the team used progesterone prophylaxis intraoperatively then until discharge, and continued with hydroxyprogesterone caproate until elective delivery. Antibiotic prophylaxis tailored to culture results was used. (Follow local protocols — evidence on routine progesterone in this context is limited but was used here as a precautionary measure.)
- Postoperative care: monitor for anaemia, infection and uterine activity. This patient needed IV iron and two transfusions for postoperative Hb nadir. Schedule close obstetric follow‑up with serial ultrasounds for fetal growth and for assessment of the residual myoma.
- Delivery planning: plan the mode of delivery based on residual myoma size, location and effect on the birth canal. In this case the residual posterior isthmic myoma was large and could obstruct labour, so an elective CS at term via the same midline incision was chosen.

## Pearls from this case
- Mass effect (dyspnoea, restricted diaphragmatic excursion) is a valid reason to consider operative intervention in pregnancy when conservative measures fail.
- CA-125 can be mildly elevated in pregnancy and with benign conditions (endometriosis, degenerating myoma, inflammation). Trends, not single values, help. A modest rise alone should not drive radical decisions.
- MRI without gadolinium at 1.5T is a useful adjunct when ultrasound is inconclusive. Avoid contrast agents in pregnancy whenever possible.
- If one mass is acutely compromised (torsed, leaking, infected), resect it alone where feasible. Limiting the procedure lowers operative time and bleeding risk and preserves pregnancy.
- Prepare for blood loss — plan for transfusion, have cell‑saver if available and acceptable, and use hemostatic strategies (clamps, timely suturing, topical agents).
- Document fetal status before, during and after surgery. Use intraop ultrasound when continuous electronic monitoring isn’t practical at mid‑gestation.
- Expectant management of non‑acute myomas is often appropriate; many regress postpartum. Avoid overtreatment.

## Take‑home messages
- Operate in pregnancy only for clear indications (persistent severe symptoms, torsion/rupture/infection, diagnostic uncertainty suggesting malignancy, or mass effect threatening maternal/fetal health).
- Targeted surgery — treat the lesion that is acutely dangerous and leave stable lesions in situ — is often the safest strategy to preserve pregnancy.
- Multidisciplinary planning, blood and hemorrhage preparedness, efficient uterine repair, and intraoperative fetal assessment are the pillars of safe myomectomy in pregnancy.
- Use ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI for characterization; interpret tumour markers cautiously in pregnancy.
- Post‑op follow‑up and delivery planning should be individualized based on residual myoma size/location and obstetric considerations.

This case is a neat reminder: in pregnancy the best surgery is the least surgery that adequately treats the immediate threat. Cut what must be cut, leave what can safely wait, and plan for both the obstetric and surgical consequences.
