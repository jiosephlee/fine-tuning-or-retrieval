Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Morning Report] In a child with recurrent unilateral pleural effusions, a mildly elevated pleural-fluid ADA, and TB exposure, which clinical and laboratory features should prompt reconsideration of tuberculosis and trigger evaluation for a pancreatic source?

Short answer
In a child with recurrent unilateral pleural effusions, a mildly elevated pleural ADA and TB exposure, you should reconsider tuberculosis and evaluate the pancreas when the clinical picture is inconsistent with pulmonary TB and when pleural fluid or imaging features point away from a lymphocytic tuberculous effusion and toward a pancreatic leak.

Why — clinical reasoning anchored in the case
This child had a family TB contact and a mildly raised pleural ADA, which led to empiric antitubercular therapy. Important features in the case that argued against TB and ultimately pointed to a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) were:
- Recurrent, large unilateral pleural effusions that re‑accumulated rapidly despite thoracentesis (three large taps/ICD required).
- Pleural fluid that was dark red/blood‑stained rather than the classic straw‑coloured, lymphocyte‑predominant exudate of tuberculous pleuritis.
- Lack of systemic or pulmonary TB features (no chronic cough, fever, weight loss, or lymphadenopathy).
- Negative microbiologic testing for MTB (GeneXpert negative).
- A relevant pancreatic history: blunt abdominal trauma (bicycle handlebar) preceding the pleural effusions.
- Very high pleural‑fluid amylase and lipase (pleural amylase 6,118 U/L, lipase 7,211 U/L) and elevated serum pancreatic enzymes.
- Cross‑sectional imaging demonstrating a collection tracking from the retroperitoneum/pancreas into the pleural space (CT and confirmatory MRCP).

Practical clinical and laboratory “red flags” that should prompt reassessment of TB and evaluation for a pancreatic source
- Pleural fluid characteristics
  - Large, recurrent, unilateral effusions that reaccumulate quickly after drainage.
  - Bloody/dark‑red pleural fluid rather than the typical lymphocyte‑predominant effusion.
  - Pleural fluid that is exudative but not dominantly lymphocytic.
- Pleural fluid tests
  - Pleural‑fluid ADA only mildly elevated (borderline) — ADA alone is not diagnostic; values >40 U/L support TB but are not absolute, and mild elevations should not trump the clinical picture.
  - Negative MTB tests (smear, GeneXpert, culture) — if these are repeatedly negative, reconsider TB.
  - Very high pleural fluid amylase and/or lipase (pleural amylase ≥1,000 U/L is strongly suggestive — often called pathognomonic — and levels like those in this case are diagnostic of pancreatic origin).
  - Consider amylase isoenzyme typing (pancreatic vs salivary) if available to distinguish pancreatic leak from oesophageal rupture or malignancy.
- Systemic/clinical context
  - Absence of constitutional TB symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats) or local pulmonary findings.
  - History of pancreatitis, abdominal pain, or recent blunt abdominal trauma (important in children).
  - Risk factors for pancreatitis (trauma, hereditary pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis) or preceding abdominal symptoms even if mild.
- Imaging clues
  - CT chest/abdomen showing pleural/mediastinal collections tracking toward the pancreas, hydropneumothorax, or retroperitoneal fluid tracking through the oesophageal/aortic hiatus.
  - MRCP demonstrating communication between pancreatic duct/pseudocyst and pleural space (sensitivity ~70–80%).
  - If MRCP/CT are equivocal and suspicion persists, consider ERCP for diagnostic/therapeutic evaluation.

What to do
- In any child with recurrent unilateral pleural effusion — especially when the fluid is bloody, reaccumulates rapidly, ADA is only mildly elevated, MTB tests are negative, or there is a history of abdominal trauma — immediately add pleural fluid and serum amylase/lipase to the workup.
- Obtain cross‑sectional imaging (CT chest/abdomen and MRCP) to look for a pancreatopleural fistula or pancreatic collection.
- If PPF is confirmed, pivot management (nil per os, TPN, somatostatin analogues, thoracic drainage ± ERCP/stenting or surgery) and stop empiric antitubercular therapy unless TB is otherwise proven.

Bottom line
Mildly raised pleural ADA and TB exposure do not rule in TB when the clinical syndrome is dominated by recurrent, large, often bloody unilateral effusions, rapid re‑accumulation after drainage, negative MTB testing, and either elevated pleural/serum pancreatic enzymes or a history of abdominal trauma. In that setting, measuring pleural and serum amylase/lipase and obtaining CT/MRCP to look for a pancreatopleural fistula is the critical next step — as illustrated by this case in which pleural amylase >6,000 U/L and MRCP confirmed the diagnosis.
