Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Morning Report] When evaluating suspected pancreatopleural fistula in a child, what is the preferred sequence and role of chest radiography, CT, MRCP, and ERCP, and how do their sensitivities for fistula visualization compare?

Short answer
Start with chest radiography (± bedside ultrasound) and diagnostic thoracentesis (including pleural amylase). If clinical suspicion for pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) persists, obtain contrast CT of the chest and abdomen to look for collections, pseudocysts and tracking of fluid. Use MRCP next as the preferred non‑invasive study to define pancreatic duct anatomy and often to visualize the fistulous tract. Reserve ERCP for therapeutic planning and intervention (transpapillary stent or ductal therapy) or when MRCP is inconclusive. Sensitivities cited in the pediatric and mixed-age literature: chest radiograph – useful for effusion but not for fistula; CT visualizes the fistula in roughly 33–47% of cases; MRCP visualizes fistula in ~70–80%; ERCP can visualize the fistula in children in 38–78% (and is the invasive gold standard for ductal imaging plus therapy).

Clinical reasoning and practical sequence (why and when)
1) Chest radiograph ± bedside ultrasound and thoracentesis first
- Rationale: rapid, bedside, shows effusion and guides safe drainage. Thoracentesis provides diagnostic fluid; a pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L (pancreatic type) is highly suggestive of PPF and should immediately redirect the workup to the pancreas. In the case, the chest x‑ray and bedside ultrasound documented a large right pleural effusion and thoracentesis produced dark‑red, amylase‑rich fluid, which raised pancreatic concern.

2) Contrast-enhanced CT chest and abdomen next
- Rationale: CT is widely available, fast, and excellent for detecting pancreatic inflammation, pseudocysts, pancreatic calcifications, and extrapancreatic collections or tracts tracking to the mediastinum/pleura. CT often shows the extrapancreatic collection even when the ductal fistula is not directly visualized. Expect only modest sensitivity for direct fistula visualization (~33–47%), but a CT that demonstrates a fluid collection tracking from the pancreas to the pleura (as in this patient) is highly informative and can guide further imaging or intervention.

3) MRCP as the preferred noninvasive ductal study
- Rationale: MRCP delineates the pancreatic duct and fluid collections without ionizing radiation or endoscopy. It has higher sensitivity for detecting duct‑to‑pleura communications (reported ~70–80%) and can often directly demonstrate the fistulous communication and any ductal narrowing or disruption. In the presented child MRCP confirmed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas and the ductal communication seen on MRCP clarified the diagnosis and supported conservative management.

4) ERCP reserved for diagnostic/therapeutic use
- Rationale: ERCP is invasive and carries procedure-related risks (including pancreatitis), but it allows direct ductal opacification and immediate therapy (transpapillary stent placement, dilation). ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning. Visualization rates in children are variable (reported 38–78%), reflecting operator dependence and anatomy; however, ERCP is selected when (a) MRCP is inconclusive but high suspicion remains, (b) ductal anatomy suggests a stenosis or leak likely to benefit from transpapillary stent, or (c) conservative therapy has failed (typically after 2–3 weeks) and endoscopic therapy is indicated. In this case the child improved with conservative care and did not require ERCP.

Putting it together for bedside decision‑making
- Immediate: CXR/ultrasound + thoracentesis with amylase/lipase measurement if pleural effusion is present.
- If pleural amylase elevated or history suggests pancreatic injury: do contrast CT chest/abdomen to look for collections/tracts.
- If CT suggests pancreatic source or is equivocal: perform MRCP to define the duct and search for the fistula.
- Use ERCP when endoscopic management is being contemplated or MRCP is nondiagnostic and you need ductal definition plus potential intervention.

This staged approach minimizes unnecessary invasive procedures, uses noninvasive high‑yield imaging to define anatomy (MRCP), and reserves ERCP for therapy — exactly the strategy used in the presented child, where CXR/US and thoracenteses, CT suggesting a tracking collection, and MRCP confirmation allowed successful conservative management without ERCP.
