Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning: From Thoracic Symptoms to a Pancreatic Source

Recurrent, large unilateral pleural effusions in a child should prompt systematic reconsideration of the source when initial thoracic workup is unrevealing. In this case the clinical pattern — repeated massive right-sided effusions that were dark red, prompt symptomatic improvement after drainage, and little evidence of systemic infection — provided the first clues that the pleural problem might arise outside the chest. Key clinical triggers that should raise suspicion for a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) are: (1) recurrent, often massive pleural effusions (frequently unilateral), (2) hemorrhagic or highly enzyme‑rich fluid rather than purulent fluid, (3) only minimal systemic or pulmonary inflammatory signs, and (4) a history of pancreatitis or abdominal trauma (the latter was elicited here only after repeated admissions).

Pleural fluid analysis is central to shifting diagnostic thinking toward a pancreatic origin. A very high pleural fluid amylase — classically cited as ≥1000 U/L — is strongly suggestive of a pancreatic source; pleural fluid lipase is typically elevated as well and supports the diagnosis. In this child the pleural fluid amylase was 6,118 U/L and lipase 7,211 U/L, values that are far above infectious or other non‑pancreatic causes and effectively confirm pancreatic leakage into the pleural space when interpreted with clinical and imaging data. Cytology was negative for malignancy, and the absence of a predominantly neutrophilic exudate argued against empyema. It is also important to recognize limitations of individual tests: ADA can be mildly elevated in hemorrhagic or exudative effusions and is therefore not specific for tuberculosis in this context; the case illustrates this pitfall because the child initially received empirical antitubercular therapy despite a later negative GeneXpert and an overall clinical picture inconsistent with TB.

Imaging should progress from bedside to targeted cross‑sectional studies guided by evolving suspicion. A chest radiograph and point‑of‑care ultrasound are appropriate first steps to confirm and quantify effusion and guide safe thoracentesis or chest tube placement. When pleural fluid characteristics or the clinical course suggest an extrapulmonary or complex process, contrast CT is the next practical test: CT reliably delineates pleural collections, hydropneumothorax, consolidation and — importantly — can identify collections tracking into the mediastinum or retroperitoneum. CT demonstrates the pancreatic fistulous tract itself less often (fistula visualization rates quoted around 33–47%), but it is useful for showing pseudocysts or multiloculated collections and their anatomic course. In this patient CT demonstrated a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal/pleural space toward the pancreatic tail, which shifted the diagnostic focus to the pancreas.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the noninvasive imaging test best suited to define ductal anatomy and to visualize fistulous tracts; reported sensitivity is about 70–80% for fistula visualization. In this case MRCP identified a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail that tracked superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity with a narrow communication to the left pleural space and communication with the distal pancreatic duct — imaging findings that corroborated the biochemical evidence of pancreatic leakage and provided the anatomic explanation for recurrent right‑sided hemorrhagic effusions. When MRCP is inconclusive or when therapeutic endoscopic intervention is contemplated, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) serves as both a diagnostic and therapeutic gold standard; ERCP allows direct opacification of the ductal system, possible visualization of the fistulous tract, and placement of transpapillary stents to reduce ductal pressure and promote fistula closure.

A structured differential diagnosis remains necessary because other conditions can produce enzyme‑rich or bloody pleural effusions. In this child tuberculosis was considered because of family contact and mildly elevated ADA, but GeneXpert was negative and the overall presentation lacked systemic TB features. Empyema was unlikely owing to absence of fever and neutrophil‑predominant fluid. Malignancy was excluded by normal blood counts and negative cytology. Boerhaave’s syndrome was unlikely given the absence of forceful vomiting, severe chest pain, or alimentary symptoms and normal gastrointestinal transit. Diaphragmatic hernia, hepatic hydrothorax and renal causes were excluded by imaging and organ‑specific tests. The combination of extremely high pleural amylase/lipase, imaging that demonstrated a retroperitoneal/pancreatic collection tracking into the chest, and the later‑obtained history of blunt abdominal trauma provided a coherent explanation and allowed discontinuation of inappropriate antitubercular therapy.

Two diagnostic lessons from this case are practical and generalizable. First, measure pleural fluid amylase (and lipase) early when pleural effusion is recurrent, hemorrhagic, or discordant with respiratory infection; a very high value shifts the workup toward the pancreas. Second, maintain a low threshold for cross‑sectional abdominal and mediastinal imaging (contrast CT and MRCP) when pleural fluid biochemistry or clinical history suggest an extrapulmonary source. Eliciting a remote history of abdominal trauma — the critical inflection point in this case — and integrating biochemical and imaging findings allowed prompt diagnosis of PPF and appropriate conservative management.
