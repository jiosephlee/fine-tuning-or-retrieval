Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] Define prosthesis–patient mismatch after TAVI: What threshold values for indexed effective orifice area and Doppler velocity index indicate severe mismatch, and how do these thresholds influence planning for redo-TAVI to avoid recurrent PPM?

Clinical pearl — definition and thresholds
- Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) occurs when the prosthetic effective orifice area (EOA) is too small for the patient’s body size, producing abnormally high transvalvular gradients despite a well‑seated valve. The diagnostic standard is the EOA indexed to body‑surface area (iEOA).
- Conventional thresholds: moderate PPM = iEOA 0.65–0.85 cm²/m²; severe PPM = iEOA ≤0.65 cm²/m². (Some authors apply slightly lower cut‑offs in obese patients or in valve‑in‑valve contexts, but ≤0.65 cm²/m² is the widely accepted benchmark.)
- Doppler velocity (dimensionless) index (DVI = VTI_LVOT/VTI_AV or peak LVOT velocity/peak transvalvular velocity) is a useful corroborative measure when EOA is uncertain. A low DVI indicates obstruction: values <0.35 suggest clinically important obstruction and values ≲0.25–0.30 correlate with severe prosthetic stenosis/PPM. (In the presented case a DVI of 0.24 and an iEOA of 0.4 cm²/m² were interpreted as severe PPM.)

Why these numbers matter (pathophysiology)
- PPM is simply a mismatch between orifice area and flow demand. A small iEOA produces higher velocities and gradients, promotes adverse left‑ventricular remodeling and symptoms, and adversely affects long‑term outcome after valve replacement. In redo‑TAVI (TAV‑in‑TAV or more), stacked frames and constrained internal diameters make achieving an adequate iEOA especially challenging.

How thresholds influence planning for redo‑TAVI — practical principles
1. Predict iEOA before you commit
   - Use MSCT to measure the true internal diameter of the existing prosthesis and apply valve‑in‑valve (ViV) prediction tools (manufacturer EOA tables, ViV app, published iEOA estimates) to forecast the post‑implant iEOA and DVI.
   - If predicted iEOA ≤0.65 cm²/m² (or predicted DVI in the low range), you must assume severe PPM risk and alter strategy.

2. Select valve and implantation technique to maximize EOA
   - Prefer devices with supra‑annular leaflet position (tend to give larger EOA for a given internal diameter).
   - Choose the largest valve that safely fits the internal frame and coronary anatomy.
   - Plan commissural alignment and a high implant to avoid pushing old leaflets inward and creating an additional neo‑skirt that reduces EOA (as in this case: high implantation and alignment to preserve orifice).

3. Use adjunctive maneuvers when needed
   - Consider bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) where the old frame is fracture‑amenable and coronary obstruction risk is acceptable — BVF can increase internal diameter and projected iEOA.
   - Aggressive balloon post‑dilatation can expand prior frames (as happened here) and improve gradients, but balance against annular injury and coronary risk.
   - If predicted residual iEOA remains unacceptably low despite these options, discuss surgical explantation rather than proceeding with a redo‑TAVI that will produce severe, symptomatic PPM.

4. Balance PPM risk against coronary and infection risks
   - In complex anatomies (multiple frames, prior PVE), preserving coronary access and avoiding obstruction may limit your ability to up‑size or fracture frames. Multidisciplinary decision‑making is essential.

Application to the case
- The team anticipated the PPM risk and selected a supra‑annular Acurate neo2, performed high implantation, commissural alignment and post‑dilatation to maximize EOA. Immediate post‑implant hemodynamics showed severe PPM (iEOA 0.4 cm²/m², DVI 0.24), but further mechanical expansion and early remodeling produced marked improvement (mean gradient ~26–27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²) by 1 month and sustained at 1 year. This illustrates both the importance of predicting PPM and the practical steps (valve choice, implant height, post‑dilatation, and CT‑driven planning) used to avoid or mitigate recurrent severe PPM in redo‑TAVI.
