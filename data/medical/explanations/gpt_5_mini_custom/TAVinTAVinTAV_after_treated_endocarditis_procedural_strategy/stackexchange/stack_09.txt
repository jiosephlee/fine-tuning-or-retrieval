Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] In a patient with prior gastrointestinal bleeding and no other indication for anticoagulation undergoing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, what is the preferred postprocedural antithrombotic regimen, and what clinical scenarios would prompt deviation from that plan?

Bottom line answer
- In this patient — prior recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, no other indication for anticoagulation, and a successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV with no active infection — the preferred antithrombotic strategy is single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT), typically low‑dose aspirin (75–100 mg daily) started immediately and continued long‑term, together with gastrointestinal protection (proton‑pump inhibitor) and targeted evaluation/treatment of the bleeding source.
- Deviations from SAPT are prompted by clear clinical indications for oral anticoagulation (eg, new atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, documented valve thrombosis or left‑ventricular thrombus), by recent coronary stenting that mandates dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), by proven transcatheter valve thrombosis or by uncontrolled thrombotic risk despite SAPT. Conversely, in a patient with ongoing high‑risk bleeding unresponsive to therapy, stopping antiplatelet therapy or using an extremely abbreviated course may be appropriate after multidisciplinary discussion.

Why SAPT here
- Randomized and guideline‑directed practice after TAVR has moved away from routine DAPT because DAPT increases clinically important bleeding without clear ischemic benefit in most patients. When there is no independent indication for anticoagulation, SAPT is the default, and in patients with a strong bleeding history the balance favors minimizing antithrombotic intensity.
- In this case the patient has recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding (since 2022) and no ongoing infective endocarditis or other indication for anticoagulation; the team therefore should minimise bleeding risk while protecting against prosthetic‑related thrombosis — SAPT plus PPI achieves that balance.

When to change the plan (clinical scenarios and preferred alternative)
- New, durable indication for anticoagulation (eg, atrial fibrillation, recent VTE, LV thrombus): start oral anticoagulation and generally stop SAPT unless a specific short‑term combination is required. Choice of agent (VKA versus DOAC) should follow the usual indication‑specific guidance; for suspected/confirmed bioprosthetic valve thrombosis many teams prefer VKA (INR 2.0–3.0) though DOACs are used depending on indication and local practice.
- Documented or strongly suspected transcatheter valve thrombosis or hypoattenuated leaflet thickening with clinical sequelae: treat with therapeutic anticoagulation (often VKA) rather than antiplatelet therapy.
- Recent coronary stent (especially within the past months) requiring DAPT: use the shortest effective DAPT duration (co‑managed with interventional cardiology), then step down to SAPT; if bleeding risk is high, strategies such as clopidogrel‑only or abbreviated DAPT may be considered case‑by‑case.
- Extremely high bleeding risk with recurrent uncontrolled GI bleeding: consider withholding antiplatelet therapy after multidisciplinary review (cardiology, GI, hematology), focus on definitive GI interventions (endoscopy, PPI, H. pylori testing, interventional therapies) and close surveillance.
- Persistent or relapsing infective endocarditis or when long‑term suppressive antibiotics are planned: antithrombotic decisions should be individualized in consultation with infectious diseases; active infection may alter timing of initiation but does not automatically mandate anticoagulation.

Practical points for this patient
- Continue aspirin monotherapy long‑term rather than routine DAPT.
- Start/continue a proton‑pump inhibitor and arrange GI input to address the recurrent bleeding source (optimize H. pylori testing, endoscopic therapy if indicated, evaluate antiplatelet necessity).
- Document the absence of atrial fibrillation/other OAC indications and re‑assess rhythm surveillance during follow‑up.
- Educate the patient about signs of bleeding and plan early echocardiographic surveillance; if clinical concern for valve thrombosis arises, obtain diagnostic imaging (TTE ± 4D CT or transesophageal echo) and escalate to anticoagulation if confirmed.
- Decisions that deviate from SAPT should be made in a multidisciplinary heart‑team context, balancing thrombotic versus bleeding risks and patient preferences.

In short: minimize antithrombotic intensity in this bleeding‑prone patient — lifelong aspirin plus GI protection — and reserve anticoagulation or DAPT for clear, guideline‑based indications or proven valve thrombosis after multidisciplinary discussion.
