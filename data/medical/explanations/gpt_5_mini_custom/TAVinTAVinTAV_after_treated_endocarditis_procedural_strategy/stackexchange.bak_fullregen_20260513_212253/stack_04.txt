Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Management] Contemporary ESC guidance summarized in this case states that surgery remains the standard for PVE with heart failure or uncontrolled infection, yet this patient underwent TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. Which patient- and anatomy-specific factors would justify favoring a transcatheter strategy over surgery in such a scenario?

The contemporary ESC guidance rightly prioritises surgery for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) when there is heart failure, uncontrolled infection, root/abscess involvement, or other surgical indications. But guidelines also emphasise individualisation. In practice a transcatheter approach can be justified when a clear combination of patient‑specific and anatomy‑specific factors makes open surgery excessively risky or unlikely to improve outcome and when imaging confirms that a redo‑TAVI can be performed with an acceptable procedural risk and expected haemodynamic benefit. The key elements that would tip the balance toward TAVI in a patient like this are:

First, patient factors that make surgery prohibitive. Advanced age, severe comorbidity burden (eg, frailty, COPD, diabetes, prior multi‑vessel PCI, prior pacemaker, recurrent GI bleeding), a very high calculated operative risk (eg, EuroSCORE II in the high 20s), and prior intravascular or chest interventions that render re‑sternotomy hazardous argue strongly for a less invasive option. If explantation would likely require complex root and ascending aortic replacement rather than straightforward valve replacement, the incremental surgical morbidity and mortality must be weighed heavily.

Second, infection control. TAVI should only be considered after documented eradication of infection: completed appropriate antibiotic therapy, persistently negative blood cultures, no clinical or laboratory signs of ongoing sepsis, and high‑quality imaging (TEE and MSCT) demonstrating absence of vegetations, paravalvular abscesses, fistulae or prosthetic dehiscence. Active or uncontrolled infection remains a surgical indication.

Third, favourable anatomy on high‑resolution CT and TEE that predicts low procedural risk and acceptable long‑term haemodynamics. This includes adequate valve‑to‑coronary distance and sinus of Valsalva dimensions that make coronary obstruction unlikely (or feasible to protect), the ability to achieve commissural alignment to preserve coronary access, an identified landing zone without paravalvular disruption, and sufficient residual lumen or frame geometry to accept a new valve with anticipated effective orifice area that will not result in prohibitive prosthesis–patient mismatch. In sequential TAV‑in‑TAV cases one must also confirm that frame overlap and leaflet configuration permit deployment (and, if needed, safe leaflet fixation or laceration strategies), and that post‑dilation or partial expansion of the prior frame can reasonably improve flow without undue risk.

Fourth, expected clinical benefit and patient preference. The intervention should be expected to relieve symptoms and improve cardiac function (eg, prior improvement in EF after the second TAV), and the patient (after informed shared decision‑making) should accept the non‑surgical strategy given the risks of both options.

Putting it together: a transcatheter strategy is reasonable when infection is clearly eradicated, the patient is at prohibitive surgical risk or surgery would require extensive root replacement, preprocedural CT/TEE show low coronary obstruction and an acceptable landing zone with predicted haemodynamics, commissural alignment/future coronary access are achievable, and the projected symptomatic and functional benefit justifies the procedure. In that setting a carefully planned, imaging‑guided TAV‑in‑TAV (even TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) performed by a multidisciplinary heart team can be an appropriate alternative to surgery.
