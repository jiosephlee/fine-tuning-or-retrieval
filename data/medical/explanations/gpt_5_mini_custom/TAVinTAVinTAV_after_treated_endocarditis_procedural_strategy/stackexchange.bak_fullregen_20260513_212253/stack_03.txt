Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Pathophysiology] In this case, E. faecalis PVE was managed with a 6-week ampicillin-based regimen. Briefly explain the pathophysiologic mechanisms by which a successfully treated episode of PVE can still lead to late prosthetic valve dysfunction (stenosis and/or regurgitation) months later.

Teaching pearl:

Successful eradication of bacteremia does not erase the mechanical and biological injury that infective endocarditis (IE) inflicts on a prosthetic valve. In prosthetic‑valve endocarditis the organisms and the host inflammatory response damage leaflet tissue, the sewing ring–annulus interface and the surrounding aortic wall; antibiotic cure removes the pathogen but the structural sequelae evolve over weeks to months and can produce late stenosis, regurgitation, or both.

Specifically, valve dysfunction after treated Enterococcus faecalis PVE can occur by several pathophysiologic pathways that often coexist. Infection may produce focal leaflet tissue destruction or micro‑perforations that are not apparent after therapy; healing of those defects with fibrosis, retraction and scar shortens and stiffens cusps, causing both central regurgitation (from poor coaptation) and progressive stenosis (from restricted opening). Deep or subannular infection and abscess formation can weaken the annulus or cause partial dehiscence; even after sterilization the fibrotic remodeling and loss of normal valve‑annulus apposition produce paravalvular leaks and altered valve geometry that worsen over time. Persistent microbial biofilm seeded on prosthetic material can be clinically silent yet promote ongoing low‑grade damage and accelerated structural degeneration of leaflets and glutaraldehyde‑fixed tissue. Finally, the intense inflammatory milieu accelerates pannus formation, calcification and thrombus deposition on leaflets or frame, reducing effective orifice area and increasing gradients.

Translate this to the case: the patient’s E. faecalis PVE was treated, but the infection likely caused leaflet injury and/or annular damage followed by fibrotic remodeling and possibly accelerated structural degeneration of the transcatheter leaflets and frames. That process explains the interval development of both increased transvalvular gradient (stenosis from leaflet stiffness/pannus/calcification) and intraprosthetic regurgitation (from retraction or small perforations or altered seating), months after microbiologic cure.

Clinical implications are practical: eradication of infection is necessary but not sufficient—patients with PVE require prolonged imaging surveillance (TEE, CT), attention to changing gradients or new regurgitation, and early multidisciplinary reassessment because structural sequelae may mandate reintervention despite microbiologic cure.
