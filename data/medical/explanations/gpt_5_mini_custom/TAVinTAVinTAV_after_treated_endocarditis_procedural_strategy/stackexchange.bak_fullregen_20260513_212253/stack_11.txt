Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Clinical Pearls] What procedural tactics help preserve present and future coronary access in patients who already have two transcatheter valves in place, and what trade-offs do these tactics entail?

Key teaching point: when a patient already has two transcatheter aortic valves in place, every technical choice for a repeat TAVI must balance two goals that can conflict — preserve immediate coronary perfusion and maintain practical coronary re‑access for the future — against the competing need to optimise valve haemodynamics. Thoughtful pre‑procedural CT and a targeted procedural strategy mitigate the most important risks, but each maneuver has predictable trade‑offs.

Tactics that help preserve present and future coronary access and the trade‑offs they entail

1) Preprocedural CT‑guided planning and 3‑D modelling
Why it matters: precise measurement of valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions, frame interactions and predicted neo‑skirt location lets you choose valve type, size and implantation depth to minimise coronary obstruction and preserve a working lateral window for future PCI.
Trade‑off: time, need for expert imaging and sometimes imperfect prediction of final frame conformation when multiple stents are present.

2) Commissural alignment
Why it matters: deliberate rotational orientation of the new valve to align its commissures with the existing prosthesis preserves ostial windows through the stent cell geometry and eases future coronary cannulation.
Trade‑off: requires valves and deployment techniques that allow controlled rotation, adds procedural complexity and fluoroscopy time, and alignment may still be imperfect — leaving residual difficulty for future coronary access.

3) High implantation (implanting the new valve at or above the level of the previous leaflets)
Why it matters: keeps the neo‑skirt and displaced leaflets away from the coronary ostia, reducing immediate obstruction risk and leaving larger cells for re‑access.
Trade‑off: higher implantation can increase risk of valve migration/embolisation, paravalvular leak if sealing zone is suboptimal, and may sacrifice some effective orifice area if positioning is suboptimal; it may also reduce radial support for anchoring in some anatomies.

4) Valve selection with an eye to frame height, cell geometry and leaflet position
Why it matters: choosing a valve with lower frame height, large open cells and supra‑annular leaflets (as in this case with Acurate neo2) tends to preserve coronary windows and gives better haemodynamics in small inner diameters.
Trade‑off: some devices with favorable access profiles may provide less radial force (less ability to expand prior frames), may be off‑label in certain scenarios, or have other device‑specific risks (e.g., higher pacemaker rates or anchoring issues).

5) Coronary protection strategies (wiring ostia, pre‑positioned stents or standby PCI)
Why it matters: advancing a guidewire into the coronary (or having a stent ready) provides an immediate bailout if the ostium becomes compromised during deployment.
Trade‑off: routine wiring increases procedure time, stroke and coronary dissection risk, and if a chimney stent is deployed it creates a permanently protruding stent with higher long‑term restenosis and reintervention complexity.

6) Preventive leaflet laceration (BASILICA) in selected anatomies
Why it matters: cutting the leaflet that would otherwise form a neo‑skirt prevents obstruction while maintaining access.
Trade‑off: BASILICA adds technical complexity, requires experience, lengthens the procedure, and carries risks including stroke, coronary embolisation and perforation; its feasibility in multiple prior frames is less certain.

7) Post‑dilatation or intentional bioprosthetic frame fracture
Why it matters: post‑dilatation can help expand prior frames and improve haemodynamics, and fracture (where possible) increases inner diameter and preserves flow.
Trade‑off: high‑pressure dilatation risks annular injury, annular rupture, stroke, and may worsen coronary compromise transiently; fracture is device‑dependent (not always possible) and unpredictable in layered frames.

8) Accepting a hemodynamic compromise to prioritise coronary preservation
Why it matters: deliberate choices (smaller valve, higher implant, limited oversizing) can avoid coronary obstruction.
Trade‑off: higher residual gradients and prosthesis–patient mismatch, which may limit symptomatic improvement and longevity of the valve.

Clinical principle to apply: plan the lifetime strategy at the first TAVI in patients likely to need reintervention — prioritize commissural alignment, choose valves and implantation depths that preserve future access, and document fluoroscopic landmarks and rotational orientation to guide future procedures. In redo and redo‑redo settings, combine meticulous CT modelling with selective protection (wiring, BASILICA) and valve selection that balances anchoring and access, recognizing that every maneuver to reduce obstruction risk carries procedural risk or may worsen haemodynamics.

In practice, the team must individualize the approach: use CT to guide whether high implantation + a low‑frame, supra‑annular valve (accepting possible higher gradients) is preferable, or whether coronary protection/BASILICA is required despite added complexity. Shared decision‑making with the patient and multidisciplinary review are essential because the chosen trade‑offs determine both immediate safety and the feasibility of future coronary or valve interventions.
