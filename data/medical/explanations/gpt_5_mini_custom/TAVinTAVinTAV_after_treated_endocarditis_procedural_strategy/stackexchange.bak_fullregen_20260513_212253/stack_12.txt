Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Morning Report] If this triple-stented aortic valve fails in the future, outline the remaining therapeutic options and their key risks, including surgical explantation and root replacement, bioprosthetic valve fracture, leaflet laceration (BASILICA), and the feasibility concerns unique to a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV anatomy.

If this patient’s newly implanted valve fails in the future, the clinical reality is that true “options” become few and high-risk. Management must be individualized by a multidisciplinary heart team with updated CT, TEE and careful assessment of surgical candidacy. The main strategies and their key risks are:

Surgical explantation and aortic root replacement
Because the prior transcatheter frames are endothelialised and often incorporated into the aortic root, surgical removal will be technically demanding. The operation commonly requires full explant of layered stent frames, aortic root replacement (Bentall or homograft), possible coronary reimplantation and prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass. Risks include very high perioperative mortality and morbidity in this frail, comorbid patient (bleeding, stroke, acute kidney injury, prolonged ventilation), annular or ventricular injury during explant, coronary injury requiring bypass, and the possibility that complete removal is not possible without extensive aortic reconstruction. Prior endocarditis raises infectious and tissue-fraction concerns (adhesions, friable tissue), increasing complexity. Surgical therapy remains the only definitive option when obstruction, abscess, fistula, uncontrolled infection or root destruction are present, but for many elderly, high-risk patients it may be effectively prohibitive.

Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) or intentional frame expansion
BVF uses high-pressure balloon inflation to crack the rigid sewing ring of surgical bioprostheses to increase effective orifice area; in transcatheter frames the concept is attempting to expand an inner frame mechanically to improve orifice. In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV anatomy, BVF faces technical and safety limits. Many transcatheter frames (particularly nitinol self‑expanding frames like Evolut) do not have a defined ring designed to fracture, so fracture may be impossible. Even if the outer frame gives way mechanically, risks include uncontrolled frame disruption, leaflet tearing, valve embolization, acute severe aortic regurgitation, annular rupture, coronary obstruction from displaced leaflets or frame portions, and stroke from debris. Because the final orifice is small and the layered frames transmit high radial forces to the annulus, the risk of annular injury is real. BVF is therefore rarely reliable in multiple overlapping TAVs and should be considered only after CT demonstrates an anatomy amenable to safe, controlled expansion and with surgical backup.

Leaflet laceration (BASILICA) or other commissural interventions
BASILICA was developed to lacerate a leaflet to prevent iatrogenic coronary obstruction during TAV-in‑SAV or TAV-in‑TAV. In the triple‑stented setting its feasibility is limited. Multiple overlapping leaflets and frames make traversal, positioning, and effective laceration technically difficult: the target leaflet may be crushed between frames, electrically insulated, or inaccessible to the electrified wire. Laceration may be incomplete, fail to create an adequate coronary flow channel, or damage adjacent frames. Risks include failed prevention of coronary obstruction (with catastrophic myocardial ischemia), aortic root injury, embolization of leaflet tissue, stroke, and procedure-related bleeding. In some cases, BASILICA through the outermost leaflet might be possible but may not protect against obstruction caused by deeper, rigid frame/leaflet assemblies. Finally, access for the laceration equipment may be limited by small transcatheter lumens and previous frame geometry.

Coronary protection strategies / chimney or snorkel stenting
When coronary obstruction risk is high and BASILICA is not feasible or reliable, upfront coronary protection with guidewires, undeployed stents ready to be deployed (chimney), or even prophylactic surgical bypass can be considered. In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV the mesh density and commissural misalignment may make guidewire crossing difficult; chimney stents carry risks of stent crush, thrombosis, impaired long-term patency, and marginal coronary flow if the ostium is partially covered. These are bailout, not ideal, definitive solutions.

Repeat transcatheter valve-in-valve (another TAV-in-TAV)
Implanting another valve into an already small, triple-stented orifice will usually worsen patient–prosthesis mismatch and may not be feasible if the residual lumen is too small. Hemodynamic benefit is limited and the risk of coronary obstruction and further loss of coronary access increases. This is thus generally a temporizing or last-resort option when no other route exists.

Hybrid approaches and palliative care
For patients who remain poor surgical candidates and in whom catheter techniques are either infeasible or too risky, a pragmatic strategy may be optimized medical therapy and symptom-directed care, with consideration of palliative strategies. In selected cases, a hybrid approach (limited surgical debridement or conduit creation plus transcatheter implantation) could be considered in experienced centers.

Practical principles going forward
Decisions hinge on up-to-date high-resolution CT with 3‑D modelling to define valve-to-coronary distances, frame interactions, and annular integrity; coronary angiography and TEE; and reassessment of surgical risk. Prior attempts at commissural alignment and preservation of coronary access are meaningful now — they determine whether coronary protection or BASILICA are possible. If surgical risk improves or the patient becomes a better operative candidate, early referral to an experienced aortic root surgical team is reasonable because delayed surgery after multiple failed catheter procedures is even more hazardous.

Bottom line: definitive surgical root replacement is the only curative option when anatomy or infection mandates removal, but it carries very high risk after multiple TAVs. Catheter approaches such as BVF or BASILICA may be useful in select anatomies, but in a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV their technical feasibility and safety are limited; they carry substantial risks of annular injury, valve embolization, coronary obstruction and stroke. Management requires individualized heart‑team deliberation, detailed CT planning, and honest discussion with the patient about procedural risk, likely durability and realistic outcomes.
