Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Lessons, Decision Pathways, and Lifetime Management

This case illustrates pragmatic, patient-centred decision making when facing a failed transcatheter aortic valve in the setting of prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) and multiple prior TAVI frames. The core teaching point is that a third transcatheter intervention can be feasible and durable when strict prerequisites are met and when CT-guided procedural strategy addresses the unique anatomical and haemodynamic challenges of layered valve frames. Below are the practical lessons and decision pathways distilled from the case.

Candidacy for a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure must be conditional rather than automatic. Before considering reintervention, infection must be controlled and reasonably eradicated: the patient should have completed targeted antimicrobial therapy, persistently negative blood cultures, and no morphological evidence of active endocarditis on transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or CT. In addition, CT-derived measures must demonstrate coronary safety (adequate valve-to-coronary distances and sinus anatomy) and a realistic plan for implantation depth and commissural alignment that preserves coronary flow and allows acceptable effective orifice area (EOA). The patient’s surgical risk and comorbidities must make repeat sternotomy or root replacement prohibitive; in this case a EuroSCORE II of ~27% and prior deep stent integration into the aortic wall supported a catheter-based strategy. Finally, multidisciplinary heart-team consensus and a documented informed-consent discussion with the patient and family about limited future options are essential prerequisites.

Preprocedural imaging drives feasibility and technique. Multislice CT should be used to measure internal valve diameters (true internal lumen of the existing frame), coronary ostial heights, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary (VTC) distances and valve-to-sinotubular junction relationships. The team must model how a new valve will sit relative to prior leaflets and frames and anticipate the neo-skirt that could cover coronary ostia or further reduce the EOA. In this report MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame; that finding directly informed valve selection (a supra‑annular, low‑height device) and the high‑implant strategy. CT planning should also define fluoroscopic cusp‑overlap views and landmarks for commissural alignment and document likely coronary reaccess routes if future PCI is needed.

Device selection and implantation technique should be chosen to minimise additional frame burden and to maximise haemodynamic gain. When anatomy allows, favour prostheses with supra‑annular leaflet position and a frame geometry that limits extra vertical height and mesh density. Aim for the highest safe implantation that will pin the incompetent leaflets of the prior valve outwards rather than create an obstructive neo‑skirt beneath the coronaries; this was the rationale for placing an Acurate neo2 supra‑annular valve in the presented patient. Commissural alignment is important to preserve coronary access; plan alignment using CT landmarks and fluoroscopy. Coronary protection or BASILICA should be reserved for borderline VTC or unfavourable sinus anatomy; in this case CT modelling demonstrated adequate clearance so the team proceeded without routine coronary protection. Consider the option of bioprosthetic valve fracture to improve expansion where a rigid frame limits EOA, but weigh this against risks in a multi‑frame setting: fracture may be less predictable and could damage surrounding structures when several stents are layered, so it should not be routine. Balloon post‑dilatation can produce partial mechanical expansion of previous frames and can meaningfully improve gradients, as likely occurred here after post‑dilatation reduced the patient’s transvalvular gradient from an immediate post‑implant mean of 67 mm Hg to mid‑term values in the mid‑20s.

Management after treated PVE requires close infectious-disease collaboration. Before reintervention there must be documented completion of an appropriate antibiotic regimen (the patient received 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin for Enterococcus faecalis), persistently negative cultures, and no echocardiographic or CT signs of active infection or paravalvular complications. Routine long‑term suppressive antibiotics are not mandated if infection is eradicated; decisions about suppressive therapy should be individualized and supervised by infectious disease specialists. After implantation, maintain standard endocarditis prevention counselling and surveillance; any new febrile illness or unexplained symptoms should prompt urgent evaluation.

Plan the patient’s valve‑lifetime trajectory and document details that will matter for future teams. Each new frame increases mesh burden and may compromise coronary reaccess and future interventions. Record commissural orientation, fluoroscopic implantation depth, device type and CT images in the chart and imaging archive so that subsequent operators can make informed decisions. Inform patients and families up front that options become progressively limited with each reintervention and that, although a transcatheter approach may be feasible now, surgical explant later will be technically challenging and high risk.

Finally, recognise the evidence gaps and need for systematic data collection. Outcomes after TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV—especially in patients with prior PVE—are reported only in small series and case reports. Important unanswered questions include the durability of third‑valve implants, the role of specific CT‑driven modelling protocols and the safety of adjunctive maneuvers such as valve fracture or leaflet laceration in triple‑stented anatomy. Where possible, cases like the one presented should be entered into registries and multidisciplinary databases to refine patient selection, procedural algorithms and long‑term surveillance strategies.

In summary, a third transcatheter valve can be a reasonable, effective option in carefully selected patients with treated PVE when infection is resolved, CT anatomy allows coronary safety and acceptable residual EOA, surgical risk is prohibitive, and a clear, image‑guided implantation strategy is agreed by a heart team. Meticulous imaging, device choice prioritising supra‑annular performance and commissural alignment, and transparent lifetime planning are the pillars that make such complex reinterventions feasible and safe.
