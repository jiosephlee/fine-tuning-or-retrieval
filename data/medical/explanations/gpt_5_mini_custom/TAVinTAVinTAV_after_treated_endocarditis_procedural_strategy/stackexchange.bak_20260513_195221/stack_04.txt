Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Clinical Pearls] What CT-based measurements are most critical to estimate the risk of coronary obstruction in a TAV-in-TAV or TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, and what threshold values suggest high risk?

The single most important CT-based concept is not a lone number but the predicted spatial relationship between the coronary ostia and the “neo‑skirt” that will be created by the implanted valve(s). Practically, that is best assessed by a small set of CT measurements plus virtual valve implantation/simulation. Measure and report these key parameters and compare them to the summed heights of existing valve leaflets/frames and the planned prosthesis skirt:

1) Coronary ostial height (from annular plane to ostium). Low ostial heights leave less room for displaced leaflets and are high risk. In re‑interventions, a height ≤10–12 mm is concerning; the lower end (≤10 mm) carries the greatest risk.

2) Valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC) or “virtual transcatheter valve to coronary” distance measured on CT after virtual valve implantation (distance from anticipated prosthetic leaflet/skirt plane to the coronary ostium). VTC ≤4 mm is generally considered high risk for obstruction; 4–6 mm is intermediate risk and prompts additional planning (coronary protection, BASILICA), >6 mm is low risk.

3) Sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameter and depth. Small, shallow sinuses provide no room for displaced leaflets. SOV diameter <30–32 mm (and shallow sinus depth) increases obstruction risk.

4) Sinotubular junction (STJ) diameter and height. A low/ narrow STJ can trap leaflets at the coronary level and is a risk factor when STJ diameter is small relative to SOV/annulus.

5) Thickness/height of the existing valve leaflets, stent frame height and skirt position. In valve‑in‑valve and especially TAV‑in‑TAV procedures, the cumulative leaflet/skirt height (and whether prior valve leaflets will be pinned outward toward the ostia) determine whether the ostium will be covered even if ostial height alone looks acceptable.

6) Annulus size/landing zone morphology and coronary takeoff angulation. Severe leaflet calcification, horizontal takeoff, and commissural misalignment that will position a neo‑skirt directly in front of an ostium increase risk.

Clinical thresholds to trigger high‑risk planning: coronary height ≤10–12 mm, VTC ≤4 mm, SOV diameter <30–32 mm (or very shallow sinus depth), or any predicted coverage of the ostium on virtual valve implantation. If any of these are met, treat the case as high risk: plan coronary protection (wires, balloons), consider BASILICA where feasible, or rethink valve choice/implant strategy. Always perform CT-based virtual implantation/3‑D simulation in TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios because ostial compromise often results from frame and leaflet interactions that static single measurements cannot fully capture.
