Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Pathophysiology] How can high implantation of a third transcatheter valve 'pin' the leaflets of the previously implanted valve and improve the effective orifice area in a triple-stented anatomy?

Think of the failing double-stented valve as three concentric components: a rigid or semi-rigid outer frame, the leaflets of the older prosthesis that can be stiffened, folded or prolapsed inward, and the second-frame scaffolding that constrains geometry of the orifice. In this case the single biggest physiological problem was inward leaflet restriction of the previously implanted valve(s), which effectively narrowed the flow lumen despite the presence of two frames around it.

High implantation of the new valve changes the geometry in two mechanically important ways. First, by positioning the new stent and its sewing skirt at or slightly above the level of the existing prosthetic leaflets, the new frame physically “pins” those leaflets outward against the inner surface of the older frame. That tethering converts previously flail, folded or immobile leaflets from obstructing tissue into contributions to the new valve’s orifice perimeter, restoring a more central and fixed flow channel. Second, when the new valve is supra‑annular (leaflets sit above the annulus) and is expanded at the leaflet level of the prior device, its radial force and post‑dilation can partially re-expand the constrained inner frame (the Evolut in this case), increasing the minimal lumen diameter and relieving a component of the stenosis.

CT planning and fluoroscopic alignment matter because you need to predict where the old leaflets sit relative to the coronaries and to choose an implantation depth that will pin leaflets without creating a neo‑skirt that covers coronary ostia. Commissural alignment helps orient the new frame so its leaflets open in the largest available flow window and preserve coronary access. Balloon pre‑ and post‑dilation augment this process by: separating fused leaflet tissue, optimizing expansion of the new valve, and transmitting radial force to the prior frame to increase lumen area.

Mechanistically then, the immediate improvement in effective orifice area after a high, supra‑leaflet implant reflects a combination of leaflet immobilization (pinning), restoration of a coaxial central orifice by the new valve’s leaflets, and mechanical partial expansion of the constrained prior frame. Those effects reduce prosthesis‑patient mismatch and improve gradients — provided coronary flow is preserved and the landing zone was correctly planned on CT.
