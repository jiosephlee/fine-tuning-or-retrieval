Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# TMA on the Clock: PLASMIC, ADAMTS13, and the Bridge to the Right Therapy

What makes this case interesting? A previously well woman in her late 40s is bitten by her dog, develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with DIC, then—after initial improvement—develops new, isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis-dependent AKI. Peripheral smear shows 15–20% schistocytes, haptoglobin is gone, LDH rises, DAT turns out to be a false positive, and ADAMTS13 comes back at 43.5% (ruling out TTP). Kidney biopsy confirms thrombotic microangiopathy (fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli, crescents). The diagnosis: infection-triggered complement-mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). She received plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridge and then eculizumab after blood cultures were negative, with partial renal recovery.

This is a classic diagnostic and therapeutic crossroads: when you are faced with MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI, you must move fast—decisions made before formal test results can save lives. Here’s a practical, stepwise approach grounded in the case.

## Stepwise approach when TMA is on the differential

1) Rapid clinical triage: think TTP first because it is the most time-critical.
- Calculate a PLASMIC score while you work up the patient. A high score supports empiric PEX; a low score lowers immediate suspicion but doesn’t rule it out.
- In this case the PLASMIC score was 4/7 (low pretest probability), but clinical concern remained because of ongoing MAHA and severe AKI.

2) Send the right tests immediately (before you change the blood):
- ADAMTS13 activity (urgent): severe deficiency (<10%) confirms TTP and mandates urgent daily PEX ± steroids, and usually rituximab.
- CBC with smear (look for schistocytes), haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, reticulocyte count.
- Coagulation panel (to assess DIC vs TMA—both can coexist).
- Blood cultures (sepsis can mimic or trigger TMA; in this case they grew C. canimorsus).
- Stool PCR for Shiga toxin (rule out STEC‑HUS).
- Basic complement tests (C3/C4) and, if possible, send samples for extended complement testing and genetic testing—ideally before PEX or complement blockade.

3) Start empiric therapy while you wait for ADAMTS13:
- If TTP is possible (high PLASMIC or strong clinical suspicion), start PEX immediately—do not wait for ADAMTS13. PEX can be lifesaving in TTP.
- If infection is suspected or confirmed, treat aggressively with appropriate antibiotics and supportive care (this patient received piperacillin/tazobactam then ampicillin and later oral phenoxymethylpenicillin once cultures allowed).
- Remember that PEX is a bridge, not a definitive therapy for CM‑HUS—its benefit in CM‑HUS is transient.

4) Reassess when ADAMTS13 and microbiology results return:
- ADAMTS13 <10% → TTP: continue PEX and treat for TTP.
- ADAMTS13 >10% (as here, 43.5%) → TTP unlikely, pivot to alternative causes (STEC vs CM‑HUS vs other secondary TMA). If stool PCR is negative for Shiga toxin and there is renal biopsy evidence of TMA with ongoing haemolysis, CM‑HUS becomes more likely.
- In this case, ADAMTS13 effectively ruled out TTP and stool PCR ruled out STEC.

5) When to use eculizumab (complement blockade):
- Eculizumab is the treatment that changes outcomes in CM‑HUS; earlier initiation is associated with better renal recovery.
- But eculizumab blocks terminal complement and increases risk for overwhelming infection with encapsulated organisms (Neisseria spp., and potentially others like Capnocytophaga). So timing matters.
- Practical strategy:
  - Ideally obtain diagnostic blood for ADAMTS13 and complement studies before PEX or eculizumab.
  - If active, uncontrolled bacteraemia is present, treat the infection first and wait for cultures to clear if clinically possible. In this case eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative.
  - If delaying eculizumab risks irreversible renal damage and infection is controlled with antibiotics, proceed with vaccination (meningococcal ACWY + B) and give prophylactic antibiotics if vaccines cannot reach effective immunity before starting therapy. In this case the patient received Menveo and Bexsero and was given phenoxymethylpenicillin prophylaxis, then eculizumab induction (900 mg IV weekly ×4) and later maintenance (1200 mg IV q2 weeks).
  - If you have already started PEX, remember PEX removes eculizumab—coordinate timing (either give eculizumab after PEX is paused or be aware that repeated PEX will reduce its effect). In this case PEX was used as a bridge; eculizumab was given after PEX and after cultures were negative.

6) Use kidney biopsy when it will change management:
- If diagnosis remains uncertain and platelet counts recover enough to allow safe biopsy, renal histology can confirm TMA (fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, crescents) and help exclude immune-complex or other primary glomerulopathies. In this patient biopsy after platelet recovery showed TMA and helped justify ongoing complement blockade.
- Timing: biopsy is most useful once bleeding risk is acceptable (platelets typically >50 ×10^9/L depending on local practice).

## Practical pitfalls illustrated by the case

- Don’t be reassured by normal C3/C4—complement levels can be normal in CM‑HUS. Normal C3/C4 does not exclude complement-mediated disease.
- PEX and eculizumab alter complement assays and ADAMTS13—draw diagnostic samples before these therapies, whenever possible. In this case extended complement testing was done after PEX and eculizumab, and the low AH50 likely reflected treatment effects.
- A positive DAT (direct Coombs) can be misleading. Drug-induced or transfusion-related false positives occur (penicillin can cause false-positive DAT). The repeat DAT was negative and the peripheral smear was classic for MAHA (schistocytes).
- Sepsis and DIC can mask or even trigger TMA. New haemolysis after the sepsis starts improving is a red flag for superimposed TMA/CM‑HUS.

## How I think about the immediate algorithm at the bedside
1. Patient with MAHA + thrombocytopenia ± AKI → send ADAMTS13 stat, blood cultures, stool Shiga PCR, CBC + smear, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, coagulation, complement labs; consider genetic complement testing if CM‑HUS suspected.
2. If PLASMIC suggests TTP or clinical picture is concerning for TTP → start PEX immediately and give steroids.
3. If ADAMTS13 returns >10% and STEC negative → consider CM‑HUS: ensure infection is controlled, vaccinate if planning eculizumab, consider PEX only as a temporary measure, start eculizumab when safe and indicated.
4. If uncertain and platelets recover → consider renal biopsy to confirm TMA and guide prognosis/therapy intensity.

## Case-specific learning points
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an unusual but documented trigger of CM‑HUS in immunocompetent adults; the infectious trigger can precede the haemolytic picture by days, sometimes surfacing only after the sepsis starts to settle.
- ADAMTS13=43.5% in this case excluded TTP. Schistocytes 15–20%, undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH clinched MAHA. Negative Shiga PCR and biopsy-proven TMA plus clinical response to eculizumab supported CM‑HUS.
- PEX helped transiently; sustained haematologic and renal improvement came only after complement blockade. Immunization and antibiotic prophylaxis were used in tandem with eculizumab to mitigate infectious risk.

## Key take-home messages
- Early triage with PLASMIC and urgent ADAMTS13 testing saves lives—start PEX when TTP is possible; stop or de-escalate if ADAMTS13 >10%.
- Send stool PCR for Shiga toxin early to exclude STEC‑HUS.
- Obtain ADAMTS13 and complement samples before PEX or eculizumab if you can; interpret post‑treatment results with caution.
- Eculizumab is the therapy that changes outcomes in CM‑HUS but weigh timing against active infection risk—vaccinate and provide prophylactic antibiotics if immediate blockade is needed.
- Renal biopsy, when feasible after platelet recovery, is a powerful tool to confirm TMA and aid management decisions.

This case is a vivid reminder: when TMA is suspected, act quickly, send the right tests early, bridge safely with PEX when needed, and use targeted therapy (eculizumab) once the diagnosis and infectious risks are addressed.
