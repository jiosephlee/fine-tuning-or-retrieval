Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Eculizumab in the Hot Zone: Starting Complement Blockade Around Active Infection

What makes this case interesting? A previously healthy woman develops fulminant sepsis after a dog bite from Capnocytophaga canimorsus, evolves into dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, and then—after an initial clinical improvement—develops a thrombotic microangiopathy consistent with complement‑mediated HUS. Eculizumab ultimately produces haematologic remission and partial renal recovery. The clinical tension here is obvious: eculizumab can be kidney‑saving in CM‑HUS but disables a crucial arm of host defence against encapsulated organisms. When your patient is recovering from—or still being treated for—an invasive bacterial infection, when do you pull the trigger on complement blockade? This post walks through practical, bedside decisions: timing, vaccine logistics, antibiotic prophylaxis options, how PEX affects everything, and how to mitigate infectious risk without unnecessarily delaying organ‑saving therapy.

Why the dilemma matters
- Terminal complement blockade (eculizumab) markedly reduces morbidity and improves renal outcomes in CM‑HUS, particularly when started early.
- But it removes critical defence against encapsulated bacteria—most classically Neisseria meningitidis—and also raises concern for other encapsulated pathogens (the case here highlights Capnocytophaga canimorsus).
- Starting eculizumab during active bacteraemia risks uncontrolled infection; delaying it risks irreversible kidney injury. There is no one‑size‑fits‑all answer, but there are pragmatic, evidence‑informed steps to reduce harm.

Principles to guide timing
- Ideal: Obtain source control, ensure blood cultures are negative (or robustly improving on appropriate antibiotics), vaccinate, then start eculizumab. In practice this ideal is sometimes unattainable because CM‑HUS progression can be rapid.
- If the bloodstream infection is ongoing and culture‑positive for an encapsulated organism, aim to clear bacteremia (documented negative cultures and clinical improvement) before complement blockade whenever feasible.
- If CM‑HUS is rapidly progressive and waiting materially risks irreversible organ loss, you may need to start eculizumab sooner—accept the infectious risk but mitigate it aggressively (see below) and involve Infectious Diseases early.

Vaccine logistics — what, when, and why it matters
- Minimum recommended vaccines before eculizumab: MenACWY (conjugate quadrivalent for serogroups A, C, W, Y) and MenB (serogroup B). These target the primary meningococcal serogroups of concern.
- Timing: aim for vaccination ≥14 days before first eculizumab dose to allow protective antibody generation. This is the ideal.
- If you cannot wait: give both MenACWY and MenB immediately and do not delay eculizumab if clinical urgency dictates. Understand that immunity will not be mature for ~2 weeks.
- Practical caveat: plasma exchange (PEX) removes circulating antibodies. If PEX is ongoing or planned, vaccine antibody titers may be blunted or removed—document timing carefully. If you must vaccinate while PEX is ongoing, plan for revaccination/boosting and prolonged antibiotic prophylaxis; consult ID.

Antibiotic prophylaxis strategies when you can’t wait
- Rationale: bridge the vulnerable window between vaccine administration and development of protective immunity, and mitigate immediate risk when eculizumab is started during or shortly after infection.
- Short‑term bridging (until 2 weeks after vaccine series or until cultures are convincingly sterile): many centres use oral beta‑lactam prophylaxis (phenoxymethylpenicillin/penicillin V or amoxicillin) while on eculizumab if the organism is susceptible and the patient can take oral drugs. In the reported case the team used phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin).
- Typical practical options (adjust to local formularies, susceptibilities and allergies; check local guidance and ID input):
  - Oral penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin) 250–500 mg twice daily, or
  - Amoxicillin 500 mg two–three times daily.
  - If severe penicillin allergy: discuss alternatives with ID—options may include azithromycin (limited meningococcal coverage), or targeted agents guided by local susceptibility; some centres consider ciprofloxacin as a single‑dose post‑exposure option but not ideal for continuous prophylaxis.
- Single‑dose or short‑course options (useful when immediate rapid protection is needed or for non‑compliant patients):
  - Single dose ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO (used for meningococcal post‑exposure prophylaxis in adults), or
  - Single IM ceftriaxone (250–500 mg) — useful if adherence or oral therapy is a concern.
  - Rifampin 600 mg PO twice daily for 2 days is another contact prophylaxis regimen, but rifampin has many drug interactions and is not ideal for ongoing prophylaxis.
- Duration: at minimum until 2 weeks after vaccination if vaccines were given immediately prior to eculizumab. In practice, many centres continue antibiotic prophylaxis for the duration of complement blockade or until ID advises otherwise—there is no universally accepted standard. Shared decision‑making with nephrology/haematology/ID is key.

Special considerations with plasma exchange (PEX) and timing
- PEX removes both eculizumab and vaccine‑induced antibodies. If you are doing PEX:
  - Avoid scheduling vaccination immediately before a PEX session if possible.
  - If PEX cannot be avoided, expect reduced vaccine efficacy; plan for revaccination/boosting after PEX and prolonged antibiotic bridging.
  - Similarly, if you give eculizumab and then perform PEX, eculizumab levels fall and re‑dosing may be required—coordinate dosing with the team performing PEX.
- Practically: if eculizumab must be administered while PEX is ongoing, give the dose after the PEX session and expect to monitor drug levels/clinical response.

Microbiology nuance: Capnocytophaga canimorsus
- C. canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow‑growing gram‑negative rod from dog/cat mouths. It can produce fulminant sepsis and DIC and, as in this case, has been reported rarely to trigger CM‑HUS.
- Because it is encapsulated and complement is important for host defence against encapsulated organisms, complement blockade raises theoretical and practical concerns about worsening infection. In the reported case, the team waited for negative blood cultures and gave vaccines before initiating eculizumab—an approach that balances infection control with need for complement blockade.

Practical bedside tactics and safety nets
- Get blood cultures (and other appropriate cultures) before starting or changing antibiotics. Aim for documented clearance of bacteremia when possible.
- Involve Infectious Diseases early. If you are unsure which prophylactic regimen to use, get ID input.
- If vaccination cannot precede eculizumab by 2 weeks, give MenACWY + MenB immediately and start antibiotic prophylaxis; document why the ideal interval could not be achieved.
- Provide the patient with education and an action plan: any fever or meningitis‑type symptom requires urgent evaluation. Consider giving an emergency supply of antibiotics (local practice variable).
- Document vaccination dates, lot numbers and discussions about infectious risk in the chart; provide the patient with written vaccine and safety information.
- Recognize that even vaccinated patients on eculizumab remain at higher risk for meningococcal disease; vigilance and low threshold for evaluation are warranted.
- Coordinate with nephrology/haematology for PEX timing and with pharmacy for eculizumab logistics and meningococcal vaccine availability.

When to stop prophylactic antibiotics and how long to continue eculizumab
- Duration of eculizumab depends on the underlying diagnosis (genetic complement variants, severity, renal recovery). In CM‑HUS, some patients require prolonged or indefinite therapy; others can discontinue after disease quiescence with close monitoring.
- If eculizumab is continued long term, consider ongoing vaccination (boosters per national schedules) and discuss the pros/cons of continued antibiotic prophylaxis with ID. In the reported case, the treating team continued eculizumab for months with prophylactic penicillin while monitoring renal function.
- If you stop eculizumab, maintain a plan for relapse monitoring and re‑vaccination status.

Bottom line — balancing risks and benefits
- The decision to start eculizumab in the setting of recent or ongoing infection is nuanced. Ideal strategy: clear bacteremia, vaccinate ≥2 weeks, then treat. When urgency precludes that, vaccinate immediately, start antibiotic prophylaxis, coordinate with ID and PEX teams, and proceed with eculizumab to avoid irreversible organ damage.
- Clear documentation, patient counselling, and close follow‑up are essential.

Key take‑home messages
- Eculizumab can be organ‑saving in CM‑HUS, but initiation around active infection requires balancing infection risk against the risk of irreversible kidney injury.
- Aim to start eculizumab after bacteremia is cleared and after MenACWY + MenB vaccination ≥14 days prior when clinically feasible.
- If you cannot wait: give MenACWY + MenB immediately, start antibiotic prophylaxis (commonly oral penicillin V or amoxicillin; alternatives and single‑dose options available), and involve Infectious Diseases early.
- PEX complicates timing: it removes vaccine antibodies and eculizumab—coordinate dosing and consider revaccination or prolonged prophylaxis.
- Provide patients with clear instructions, vaccination documentation, and urgent evaluation plans for fever or meningitic symptoms; consider an emergency antibiotic supply in selected cases.
