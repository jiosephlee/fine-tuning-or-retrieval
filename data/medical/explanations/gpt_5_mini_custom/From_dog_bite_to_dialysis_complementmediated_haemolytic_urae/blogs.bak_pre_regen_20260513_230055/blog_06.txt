Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# Blue Toes, Warm Pulses: Recognizing Microthrombotic Ischemia in DIC and TMA

What stops you cold in a busy ED is not always the dramatic — sometimes it’s the small, odd findings that unlock the diagnosis. A woman in her late 40s presented after a dog bite with fulminant sepsis, mottled lower extremities and later frank cyanosis of toes. Her pulses were intact and sensation preserved. That combination — distal ischemia with warm, well‑perfused limbs — should put microthrombosis high on your differential. In her case the story evolved from DIC in Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis to a superimposed complement‑mediated TMA (CM‑HUS) that required plasma exchange and eculizumab to get her off dialysis.

This post pulls practical lessons from that case: how to recognise microthrombotic ischemia versus embolic or vasculitic causes, what bedside and lab cues to chase, and how that recognition changes your immediate diagnostic and management priorities.

## Why “blue toes with pulses” matters
Peripheral ischemia with palpable pulses is almost always microvascular. Embolic or large‑vessel occlusion gives cold, pulseless, painful limbs with clear vascular territory deficits. Vasculitis can mimic microvascular disease but usually has systemic inflammatory features, livedo reticularis, palpable purpura, neuropathy and often abnormal autoimmune serology. In the septic patient, the rapid development of distal cyanosis and ecchymoses with intact pulses is a classic hint toward disseminated intravascular coagulation and small‑vessel platelet/fibrin microthrombi. But don’t stop there — if haemolysis, schistocytes and worsening AKI appear (or persist) as sepsis improves, think TMA layered on top of sepsis/DIC.

Why this distinction matters: the underlying pathophysiology and treatments differ. DIC is driven by systemic coagulation activation (treat the trigger, support with blood products judiciously). TMA is endothelial and platelet‑microthrombus mediated — think ADAMTS13/TTP, STEC‑HUS, complement‑mediated HUS — and may require plasma exchange or targeted complement blockade (eculizumab).

## Bedside and early diagnostic approach
When you see blue toes with warm pulses, do a quick targeted assessment to separate major possibilities:

- Check pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature and sensory/motor status. Intact pulses and preserved sensation point to microvascular process.
- Review timing: emboli are often sudden and unilateral; vasculitis/subacute processes are slower; DIC/TMA in sepsis can be rapid and bilateral.
- Look for systemic clues:
  - Fever, hypotension, lactic acidosis → sepsis/DIC.
  - Purpura fulminans pattern or extensive bleeding → DIC.
  - New anemia with schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, thrombocytopenia and AKI → think TMA/MAHA.
  - Painful livedo, neuropathy, or positive ANCA/ANA → vasculitis.
- Immediate bedside tests to order:
  - CBC with smear (seek schistocytes), reticulocytes
  - Coagulation panel (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer)
  - Hemolysis panel (LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin)
  - Renal function and urine dip (proteinuria/hematuria)
  - Blood cultures if infection suspected
  - Consider point‑of‑care glucose, lactate, ABG

Remember: DIC and TMA can coexist or sequentially overlap. Initial labs in sepsis can be misleading — normal haptoglobin or absent schistocytes early on doesn’t exclude evolving MAHA.

## Differentiating DIC vs TMA vs embolic vs vasculitis — the practical distinctions
- Embolic/large vessel
  - Pulses: diminished/absent
  - Temperature: cold
  - Onset: abrupt, often unilateral
  - Vascular imaging will localise occlusion
- Vasculitis
  - Systemic symptoms, rashes (purpura), neuropathy
  - Autoimmune serologies may be positive
  - Biopsy often diagnostic
- DIC (consumptive coagulopathy)
  - Labs: prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer, thrombocytopenia
  - Bleeding and thrombotic manifestations can co‑exist
  - Trigger: sepsis, trauma, malignancy
- TMA (TTP/HUS/CM‑HUS)
  - Labs: MAHA (schistocytes, ↑LDH, ↓haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia, often AKI (HUS) or neurological features (TTP)
  - ADAMTS13 activity low in TTP (<10%)
  - Complement studies can be normal in CM‑HUS; diagnosis is clinicopathological and by exclusion
  - Kidney biopsy (if safe) can show microthrombi if diagnosis unclear

Key clinical hint from the case: the patient’s coagulopathy initially looked like DIC in severe sepsis (mottling, bleeding, low fibrinogen), but after systemic inflammation improved she developed an isolated haemolytic drop in haemoglobin, new schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopenia with worsening AKI — classic evolution suggesting TMA superimposed on sepsis.

## What this sign should trigger in your management plan
- Early:
  - Treat the obvious trigger. In sepsis with possible Capnocytophaga after dog bite, start broad‑spectrum antibiotics covering dog bite pathogens promptly.
  - Resuscitate as usual (IV fluids, oxygen, vasopressors as needed).
  - Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics when feasible.
- Immediate investigations:
  - CBC with peripheral smear, hemolysis labs, coagulation profile, creatinine, urinalysis.
  - Send ADAMTS13 and Shiga toxin PCR/stool cultures if TMA suspected.
  - Consult hematology and nephrology early if thrombocytopenia + MAHA + AKI.
- While awaiting specialized tests:
  - If TTP is a concern (high pretest probability or unstable patient), start plasma exchange emergently — do not wait for ADAMTS13.
  - If DIC dominates (active bleeding, very low fibrinogen), manage with targeted blood product support (platelets, cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen concentrate) and treat trigger.
- If TMA is confirmed or strongly suspected and infection/bleeding risk is controlled:
  - Consider plasma exchange as a bridging therapy.
  - For CM‑HUS, plan for complement blockade (eculizumab) after weighing infection risks. Vaccinate (meningococcal) and give antibiotic prophylaxis if initiating eculizumab — ideally after blood cultures are negative and infection treated.
- Avoid knee‑jerk anticoagulation for microvascular ischemia unless there’s a clear large‑vessel thrombosis — anticoagulation won’t reverse microthrombi and may worsen bleeding.

## Clinical pearls
- Distal cyanosis with preserved pulses = think microthrombi (DIC/TMA) not embolus.
- DIC and TMA can coexist or evolve sequentially in severe infections; persistence or late onset of MAHA and thrombocytopenia despite resolution of sepsis should prompt reconsideration for TMA.
- Normal C3/C4 or normal complement functional tests do not exclude CM‑HUS.
- ADAMTS13 activity <10% is diagnostic of TTP — anything higher makes TTP unlikely and redirects therapy.
- Start plasma exchange quickly if TTP cannot be excluded; use PEX as a bridge for other TMAs while confirmatory tests and specialist input are obtained.
- Eculizumab is highly effective for CM‑HUS but requires vaccination and consideration of ongoing infection; timing matters.

## Take‑home messages
- Blue toes with warm pulses = small‑vessel microthrombosis until proven otherwise.
- In septic patients with distal ischemia, track trend labs: new or worsening schistocytosis, low haptoglobin, rising LDH and falling platelets point to TMA.
- Early specialist involvement (hematology, nephrology) and targeted diagnostics (ADAMTS13, stool Shiga testing, complement workup, blood cultures) change outcomes.
- Management is nuanced: treat infection, support coagulation as needed, use PEX for TTP/bridging, and consider eculizumab for CM‑HUS once infection control and vaccination/prophylaxis are addressed.

Spot the blue toes, check the smear, call hematology. Small peripheral clues save kidneys — and lives.
