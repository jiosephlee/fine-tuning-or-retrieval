Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 5: ## Therapeutic strategy in infection-triggered CM-HUS: sequencing PEX, eculizumab, and supportive care

Managing a thrombotic microangiopathy that emerges during or after sepsis requires coordinated decisions about temporizing therapies, definitive complement blockade and organ support, all while minimising infectious risk. The treatment course in this case illustrates common practical principles.

Plasma exchange as a temporising bridge
Plasma exchange (PEX) is rapidly life‑saving in suspected thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) because it removes autoantibodies against ADAMTS13 and replenishes ADAMTS13 activity. In contrast, PEX only transiently benefits complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS): it can remove circulating complement activation products, inflammatory mediators or anti‑factor H antibodies and provide short‑term haemostatic improvement, but it does not correct the underlying dysregulation. Therefore, when a patient presents with MAHA and severe thrombocytopenia before ADAMTS13 results are available, prompt initiation of PEX is appropriate as a bridge. In the present case PEX was started while awaiting ADAMTS13 and complement results; this approach correctly prioritised immediate risk, and PEX partly stabilised the patient (diuresis began to return) although sustained remission required complement blockade. Remember that PEX can confound complement and serologic testing (and remove recently administered monoclonal antibodies), so baseline blood for ADAMTS13, complement functional assays and autoantibodies should be drawn before PEX whenever feasible.

Eculizumab — mechanism, timing and dosing used here
Eculizumab is a monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal complement component C5, preventing formation of the membrane attack complex and interrupting the endothelial injury cascade central to CM‑HUS. Early initiation is associated with better renal recovery. However, because the terminal complement pathway is crucial for defence against encapsulated organisms, timing relative to active infection is critical. In this case the treating team waited until blood cultures were negative and the acute bacteremic phase had abated before initiating eculizumab, reducing the risk of worsening uncontrolled infection.

The induction and maintenance regimen used in this patient was standard for CM‑HUS: induction with eculizumab 900 mg intravenously once weekly for four weeks, followed by maintenance dosing of 1200 mg every two weeks. Individual dosing schedules may vary by indication and local protocols, and treatment duration should be re‑assessed periodically based on clinical course, renal recovery and genetic testing results.

Vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis
Because eculizumab markedly increases susceptibility to meningococcal disease (and may increase risk from other encapsulated bacteria), vaccination against meningococci is recommended ideally at least two weeks before starting complement blockade. Both quadrivalent conjugate vaccines covering A/C/W/Y (e.g., MenACWY) and serogroup B vaccines (e.g., MenB) are recommended. If immediate treatment is necessary and pre‑vaccination is not possible, give the vaccines as soon as feasible and provide antibiotic prophylaxis until two weeks after vaccination. In the reported case the first doses of MenACWY (Menveo) and MenB (Bexsero) were administered immediately prior to starting eculizumab and phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was used as prophylaxis while the vaccine response matured. Counseling should emphasise the need for rapid medical attention for any febrile illness during complement blockade and long‑term vaccination/antibiotic strategies if therapy is continued.

Renal support: indications and monitoring for recovery
Acute kidney injury from CM‑HUS is often severe. The indications for initiating continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) in the critically ill include refractory volume overload, severe acidosis or electrolyte disturbance, and anuria with rising urea/creatinine in the context of haemodynamic instability. In this patient CRRT was started for anuria, fluid overload and septic cardiomyopathy. Close monitoring of urine output and creatinine trends is essential: recovery of diuresis and downward creatinine trajectory are the earliest signs of renal improvement. In CM‑HUS, earlier initiation of eculizumab improves chances for dialysis independence; in this case diuresis returned after PEX and then consolidated after eculizumab, yielding partial renal recovery but ongoing reduced function. Decisions about continuing outpatient dialysis versus expectant recovery depend on serial renal function measurements over weeks to months.

Transfusion strategy in TMA overlapping with DIC
Transfusion decisions must balance bleeding risk from thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy against the prothrombotic milieu of TMA. Red blood cell transfusion is indicated for symptomatic anaemia or haemodynamic instability; in this case transfusions were given for a rapid haemoglobin fall. Platelet transfusions are generally avoided in TMA because they can fuel microthrombus formation, unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or the patient requires an invasive procedure. In DIC with active bleeding or profound coagulopathy, platelets and fibrinogen may be given; this patient received supportive platelet and fibrinogen transfusions early while the diagnosis was uncertain. These interventions should be individualised and frequently re‑evaluated as the diagnostic picture clarifies.

Monitoring response and adjusting therapy
Clinical and laboratory markers guide response assessment. Haematological remission is reflected by rising platelet count, falling lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), rising haptoglobin, stabilisation of haemoglobin, and decreasing schistocyte burden on blood film. Renal response is judged by increasing urine output and falling creatinine/urea. In this case, platelets and LDH were monitored closely and improved after PEX and eculizumab. Steroids that were started empirically for a presumed autoimmune haemolytic anaemia should be tapered once AIHA is excluded (negative repeat direct antiglobulin test and predominance of schistocytes), to avoid unnecessary immunosuppression during infection or while on complement blockade.

Risk–benefit considerations and mitigation when blocking complement during or after infection
Initiating complement blockade in the setting of recent or ongoing bacteraemia — particularly with encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria or Capnocytophaga — presents a clear risk. The benefit is stopping ongoing endothelial injury and preventing irreversible renal damage; the hazard is rendering the patient unable to clear certain pathogens. Mitigation strategies include: ensuring bloodstream infection is controlled (negative cultures) before eculizumab when possible; administering meningococcal (MenACWY and MenB) vaccines at least two weeks prior when feasible; providing prophylactic antibiotics if vaccination cannot be completed two weeks before treatment; close clinical monitoring and low threshold for empirical antibiotics if fever develops; and individualising timing of therapy when the patient’s TMA is life‑threatening. In this case the team balanced these considerations by awaiting negative cultures, vaccinating, administering prophylactic penicillin, and starting eculizumab promptly once infection control permitted.

In summary, management of infection‑triggered CM‑HUS often requires an initial pragmatic use of PEX while excluding TTP, rapid initiation of eculizumab once the infectious risk is mitigated, aggressive organ support (including CRRT when indicated), cautious transfusion practices, and vigilant infection prevention and monitoring during complement blockade. Timing of interventions, collection of baseline diagnostic samples prior to PEX, and clear communication about infection risks are key operational points illustrated by this case.
