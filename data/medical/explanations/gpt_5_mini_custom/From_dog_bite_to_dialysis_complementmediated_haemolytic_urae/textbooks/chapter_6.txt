Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 6: ## Outcomes, prognosis, and clinical lessons

Clinical outcome and trajectory in this patient were encouraging but incomplete. After a turbulent ICU course with DIC and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, the patient experienced return of spontaneous diuresis following plasma exchange and, more durably, haematologic remission after initiation of eculizumab. At follow‑up she was dialysis independent with partial renal recovery (serum creatinine ~175 µmol/L), reflecting meaningful but not full restoration of kidney function. This trajectory—haematologic control followed by partial but persistent renal impairment—is typical of severe complement‑mediated HUS when therapy is started after a period of established microvascular injury.

Eculizumab produced durable haematologic remission in this case, and maintenance dosing was continued with a plan to reassess for discontinuation once renal function stabilises. The team’s approach—continuing complement blockade short term because of the severity of presentation, then reconsidering duration in light of genetic results and clinical course—is a practical strategy commonly used in CM‑HUS. Long‑term therapy is most strongly favoured when a pathogenic complement variant or anti‑factor H antibodies are identified, whereas absence of such findings reduces (but does not eliminate) the perceived need for indefinite treatment. In this patient, extended genetic testing did not reveal pathogenic variants; that lowers the presumptive lifetime relapse risk but does not guarantee against recurrence, so surveillance and individualized weighing of relapse risk versus infection risk from continued complement inhibition guided the planned reassessment.

Timing of complement blockade matters. Early and targeted complement inhibition tends to be associated with improved renal outcomes because it halts ongoing endothelial injury and microthrombus formation before irreversible parenchymal loss occurs. In contrast, delays in recognizing CM‑HUS or in initiating eculizumab increase the probability of incomplete renal recovery and chronic kidney disease. This case illustrates that diagnostic uncertainty in the setting of sepsis and DIC can postpone recognition of a superimposed TMA; clinicians should therefore remain alert for a “second hit” when haemolysis or schistocytosis appears as the infectious picture improves.

Interpreting negative genetic testing requires nuance when planning prognosis and follow‑up. A negative extended complement panel lowers the likelihood of an inherited complement dysregulation as the major predisposing factor, but does not preclude a complement‑mediated process triggered by infection or transient autoantibodies. Decisions about the duration of eculizumab should therefore integrate: the severity of the index episode (degree of renal injury and need for dialysis), speed and completeness of recovery after complement blockade, absence or presence of pathogenic variants/autoantibodies, and patient preferences. Even without a genetic cause, a severe episode like the one described justifies a period of maintenance therapy and close outpatient surveillance for relapse after discontinuation is attempted.

There are several practical clinical pearls illustrated by this case. First, consider CM‑HUS when new or isolated haemolysis and thrombocytopenia emerge as sepsis appears to resolve—this temporal pattern (haemolysis arising while inflammatory markers fall) is an important clue that a TMA may be superimposed. Second, routine complement measures (C3, C4) can be normal in CM‑HUS and therefore do not exclude the diagnosis; normal levels should not delay further evaluation when the clinical picture fits. Third, a peripheral blood smear is a high‑yield, inexpensive test: identification of schistocytes is pivotal to recognize MAHA and prompt a TMA workup. Fourth, kidney biopsy can be diagnostic when it is safe to perform: the finding of ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and thrombotic microangiopathic changes supported the diagnosis here and helped exclude immune‑complex glomerulopathy, but biopsy should be balanced against bleeding risk and performed selectively.

Broader implications from this case include awareness that uncommon pathogens such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus may act as triggers for complement‑mediated TMA. Early clinical suspicion of such organisms—prompted by exposure history (dog bite) and by using molecular or targeted culture techniques for fastidious bacteria—can shorten the interval to appropriate antimicrobial therapy and help time decisions about complement inhibition. Finally, initiating complement blockade raises specific infection‑prevention issues: vaccination against encapsulated organisms (meningococcal serogroups and others as indicated), appropriate timing of immunization relative to therapy, and consideration of short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis when vaccines cannot be given ≥2 weeks before eculizumab. These measures were applied in this patient once blood cultures were negative, reflecting the necessary balance between treating a life‑threatening TMA and minimising infectious risk from terminal complement inhibition.

In sum, the case underscores three actionable lessons: maintain a high index of suspicion for CM‑HUS when haemolysis arises in the recovery phase of sepsis; use peripheral smear, ADAMTS13, stool PCR (to exclude STEC) and, when safe, renal biopsy to define the diagnosis rapidly; and, once TTP and STEC‑HUS are excluded or unlikely, consider early complement blockade while implementing vaccine and prophylactic strategies to mitigate infectious risks.
