Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Strategy for CM‑HUS in the Context of Recent Bacteremia

Managing complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) that arises or becomes evident shortly after bacteraemia forces clinicians to balance two competing imperatives: arresting a rapidly destructive complement‑driven thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and avoiding suppression of a complement system that is still needed to control infection. The clinical course of the patient described—severe sepsis from Capnocytophaga canimorsus followed by delayed microangiopathic haemolysis, renal TMA on biopsy and response to complement blockade—illustrates practical principles that guide therapy in this setting.

When to use plasma exchange (PEX)
- Role and rationale. In adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI, the immediate priority is to exclude TTP because severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) requires urgent PEX. PEX is therefore appropriate as an immediate, potentially lifesaving intervention when TTP is suspected, while ADAMTS13 testing is pending. In CM‑HUS, PEX does not correct the underlying dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, but it can remove circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators and (when present) anti‑factor H autoantibodies; thus it may produce transient hematologic and clinical improvement and is a reasonable temporizing measure.
- Practical use as a bridge. Use PEX as a bridge: start immediately when TTP cannot be excluded, in fulminant TMA, or when anti‑factor H antibodies are suspected. Recognize the benefit in CM‑HUS is usually temporary; definitive control generally requires complement blockade. In the case presented, PEX was used while TTP was ruled out and prior to eculizumab—diuresis returned transiently after PEX but sustained remission followed complement inhibition.

When and how to initiate complement blockade (eculizumab and alternatives)
- Eculizumab is the standard of care for CM‑HUS. Mechanism: monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal complement component C5, preventing formation of C5a and the membrane attack complex and thereby halting endothelial injury and microthrombus formation.
- Timing matters. Earlier initiation correlates with more rapid hematologic control and greater renal recovery. However, in the setting of active, culture‑proven bacteraemia with an encapsulated organism, immediate complement blockade can substantially increase risk of overwhelming infection. The decision is individualized:
  - If bacteraemia is controlled (negative cultures, clinical and biochemical improvement) but TMA persists or progresses, start eculizumab without delay.
  - If infection remains uncontrolled, treat aggressively with targeted antimicrobials and consider PEX as a temporizing measure; discuss risk/benefit with infectious diseases and nephrology. In life‑threatening TMA refractory to other measures, some teams will start eculizumab despite recent infection, with intensive antibiotic coverage and close monitoring.
- Dosing and alternatives. Typical induction (adult) regimens follow product labeling (in many centres: 900 mg IV weekly ×4, then 1200 mg IV every 2 weeks maintenance), as in this case. Ravulizumab is a longer‑acting C5 inhibitor with less frequent dosing and may be considered for maintenance once complement blockade is established and infection risk mitigated.

Vaccination, antibiotic prophylaxis and infection precautions
- Vaccination. Because terminal complement blockade substantially increases susceptibility to Neisseria meningitidis and may increase risk from other encapsulated organisms, administer meningococcal vaccines before starting eculizumab whenever possible:
  - MenACWY (conjugate A, C, W, Y) and MenB (serogroup B) are recommended, ideally ≥2 weeks prior to the first dose to allow protective immunity to develop.
  - If other encapsulated organisms are a concern or if immunization history is incomplete, consider pneumococcal and Hib vaccination as guided by infectious diseases or local recommendations.
- Urgent therapy and bridging antibiotics. If urgent eculizumab is indicated and vaccines cannot be given ≥2 weeks beforehand, begin appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis and continue until 2 weeks after vaccination (or longer per local guidance). Oral penicillin (phenoxymethylpenicillin/penicillin V or amoxicillin) is commonly used when organisms are penicillin‑susceptible; use an alternative based on allergies and local susceptibility patterns and involve infectious diseases. In the presented case, eculizumab was started after blood cultures had become negative and the patient received antibiotic prophylaxis.
- Patient education and safety netting. Counsel patients and caregivers about the increased risk of fulminant meningococcal disease: fever or acute illness should prompt immediate medical review. Provide written action plans and consider supplying emergency antibiotics where locally recommended.

Renal replacement therapy (RRT) and kidney‑focused care
- Indications and modality. Treat according to standard AKI/RRT indications: refractory hyperkalaemia, severe acidaemia, volume overload with cardiorespiratory compromise, severe uraemia, and certain toxin removals. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is often used in haemodynamically unstable patients; intermittent haemodialysis can be used as stability permits.
- Weaning and monitoring for recovery. Monitor urine output (a key early sign of renal recovery), serum creatinine trends, solute control and acid–base status. Typical practical criteria to consider dialysis weaning include sustained urine output (>0.5–1.0 mL/kg/h with improving creatinine and electrolyte control) and clinical stability; decisions are individualized and coordinated with nephrology. In this patient, spontaneous diuresis returned after PEX sessions and further sustained recovery followed eculizumab.
- Preserve renal reserve. Avoid nephrotoxins (aminoglycosides, NSAIDs, contrast when possible), optimize haemodynamics, control blood pressure and treat sepsis aggressively to limit further renal injury.

Treatment duration and follow‑up
- Individualize duration of complement blockade. The optimal duration of eculizumab is individualized. Key determinants are the presence of a pathogenic complement gene variant or anti‑factor H antibodies, clinical severity, extent of renal recovery and patient preference. General considerations:
  - If pathogenic complement variants or anti‑factor H antibodies are present, many experts favour prolonged or lifelong therapy with close monitoring because relapse risk is high.
  - If no genetic predisposition is identified and the episode appears to be clearly triggered and singular (infection‑triggered CM‑HUS), a finite course (several months) with careful trial discontinuation after sustained remission may be reasonable.
- Structured monitoring. After stabilization, perform frequent laboratory monitoring to detect relapse early: CBC with platelets, LDH, haptoglobin (if available), creatinine, urinalysis and blood pressure—initially weekly or more frequently during induction or PEX changes, then spacing to monthly once stable. Reinstitute complement blockade promptly if hematologic or renal relapse occurs.
- Genetic and complement testing logistics. Obtain ADAMTS13, C3/C4 and genetic and functional complement testing early, ideally before PEX or eculizumab because both interventions confound results. If testing is delayed or performed after PEX/therapy, interpret values cautiously; coordinate with reference laboratories to time samples appropriately.

Adjunctive supportive care and complications management
- Haematologic support. Transfuse red cells for symptomatic anaemia. Avoid prophylactic platelet transfusion unless there is active bleeding or an urgent invasive procedure—platelet transfusions can worsen microthrombosis in TMA but are sometimes necessary; manage on a case‑by‑case basis with hematology input.
- Thrombosis and bleeding. TMA confers both thrombotic and bleeding risks, compounded by DIC in sepsis. Anticoagulation decisions require individualized risk–benefit assessment; prophylactic anticoagulation may be appropriate once platelets recover to a safe threshold (commonly >50 × 10^9/L for therapeutic anticoagulation, but institutional practice varies). In the presented case, bleeding manifestations and DIC were managed supportively as the coagulopathy resolved.
- Blood pressure and proteinuria. Control hypertension aggressively—ACE inhibitors or ARBs are useful for proteinuria and kidney protection once renal function and haemodynamics allow.
- Nutrition, rehabilitation and psychosocial support. Address malnutrition, deconditioning and psychosocial needs during recovery; prolonged hospitalisation and dialysis dependence carry significant functional and emotional impact.

Operational issues and laboratory interactions
- Interference of therapies with testing. Both PEX and eculizumab interfere with complement functional assays and with measurements of complement components; PEX can lower autoantibody titers and remove eculizumab; eculizumab can render assays of terminal pathway activity uninterpretable. Draw baseline genetic and functional complement tests before initiating PEX or eculizumab whenever feasible.
- Timing of eculizumab relative to PEX. Because PEX can remove eculizumab, when both are used concurrently give eculizumab after PEX and anticipate possible need for additional or adjusted dosing; discuss logistics with pharmacy and transfusion services.

Summary applied to the case
This patient’s care illustrates prudent application of these principles. PEX was employed early as a bridge while excluding TTP; ADAMTS13 activity effectively excluded TTP and anti‑factor H antibodies were not identified. Eculizumab was commenced after blood cultures were sterilized and after meningococcal vaccination were begun, with antibiotic prophylaxis administered because the vaccine interval was shortened. Following complement blockade the patient achieved hematologic remission and partial renal recovery. Duration of maintenance therapy is being individualized according to genetic testing results and the degree of renal recovery, with a plan for close laboratory and clinical surveillance and a low threshold to reinitiate therapy if relapse is suspected.

In sum, managing CM‑HUS after recent bacteremia requires early recognition of TMA, rapid exclusion of TTP, judicious use of PEX as a bridge, timely initiation of complement blockade when infection risk is controlled or acceptable, strict infection prophylaxis and vaccination strategies, careful renal support, and individualized decisions about duration of therapy informed by genetic testing and clinical course. Collaboration between nephrology, hematology, infectious diseases and transfusion services is essential for safe and effective care.
