Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 1: ## Thrombotic Microangiopathy in Infection: Concepts, Classifications, and Complement Biology

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a clinicopathologic syndrome defined by the triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and end‑organ injury—most commonly the kidneys and brain. The hemolysis is mechanical, reflected by schistocytes on the peripheral smear, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), low haptoglobin and often reticulocytosis. The thrombocytopenia reflects platelet consumption in microvascular thrombi. Organ dysfunction ranges from oligo‑anuric acute kidney injury (AKI) to neurologic deficits, and the pattern of organ involvement helps to narrow the underlying etiology.

TMAs are not a single disease but a group of overlapping entities that require different, often mutually exclusive, therapies. The major categories to consider in adults are:
- Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP): characteristically caused by severe deficiency of the von Willebrand factor‑cleaving protease ADAMTS13 (activity <10%). Clinically, TTP often has prominent neurologic findings and responds urgently to plasma exchange.
- Shiga toxin–associated HUS (STEC‑HUS): usually follows an enteric infection with Shiga toxin–producing E. coli; diagnosis is supported by stool testing for Shiga toxin and a compatible prodrome.
- Complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS; historically “atypical HUS”): results from dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, often in patients with genetic variants or acquired autoantibodies to complement regulators. Terminal complement blockade (eculizumab) is the disease‑specific therapy.
- Secondary TMAs: a heterogeneous group in which systemic processes (sepsis/DIC, malignant hypertension, transplantation, pregnancy/postpartum, certain drugs, autoimmune disease) produce endothelial injury and TMA features. Management focuses on treating the underlying trigger; in some cases complement blockade is considered.

Understanding complement biology is central to CM‑HUS. The alternative pathway is constitutively active at a low level by a “tick‑over” mechanism in which spontaneous C3 hydrolysis generates C3b that can bind surfaces and form the alternative pathway C3 convertase (C3bBb). Complement regulators—soluble (factors H and I) and membrane‑bound (e.g., membrane cofactor protein/CD46)—normally restrict activation on host endothelium. Genetic variants or autoantibodies that impair these regulators permit uncontrolled amplification of C3/C5 convertases, generation of the membrane attack complex and release of anaphylatoxins. On microvascular endothelium this produces endothelial activation and injury, exposure of subendothelial matrix, platelet aggregation and formation of fibrin‑rich occlusive microthrombi—clinically manifest as MAHA, thrombocytopenia and tissue ischemia, especially in the kidney. Importantly, penetrance of complement gene variants is incomplete; most patients require a secondary trigger (infection, pregnancy, surgery, certain drugs) to precipitate clinical disease.

Infections can play a dual role: they are common triggers for CM‑HUS yet may also mimic or produce other hematologic phenomena such as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Capnocytophaga canimorsus, the organism in the present case, is a slow‑growing, encapsulated gram‑negative rod from the oral flora of dogs and cats. It can cause fulminant sepsis, DIC and multiorgan failure even in previously healthy hosts. From a complement standpoint C. canimorsus shows variable serum susceptibility; some capsulated strains resist complement‑mediated killing. Thus, infection with this organism may both drive complement activation (providing the “second hit”) and, through capsular properties, challenge host defense—creating the clinical paradox of infection‑triggered complement overactivation that can culminate in CM‑HUS.

Distinguishing DIC from TMA is a key practical challenge, particularly in sepsis when both processes can coexist. DIC is a systemic consumptive coagulopathy characterized by prolonged prothrombin time (PT/INR), activated partial thromboplastin time, low fibrinogen, marked elevation of D‑dimer and bleeding diathesis; microthrombi are usually fibrinaceous and widespread. TMA, by contrast, classically presents with prominent MAHA and thrombocytopenia with relatively preserved coagulation tests (unless superimposed DIC exists). In practice a patient with sepsis may present initially with DIC and bleeding, and then develop or unmask a TMA once the consumptive coagulopathy begins to resolve—an important pattern illustrated by this case, where coagulation indices normalized while hemolysis and renal injury persisted or worsened. Key distinguishing features favoring a superimposed TMA are persistent or new schistocytosis, rising LDH with undetectable haptoglobin, sustained thrombocytopenia despite correction of coagulation, worsening renal function out of proportion to hemodynamics, and kidney biopsy showing glomerular/arteriolar thrombi with ischemic glomeruli and fibrin deposition.

A pragmatic diagnostic framework in a patient with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI is:
- Rapidly obtain ADAMTS13 activity (and use clinical scores such as PLASMIC to prioritize empiric plasma exchange if suspicion is high); ADAMTS13 <10% points to TTP and mandates immediate PEX.
- Test for Shiga toxin (stool PCR or culture) when enteric prodrome is present.
- Look for secondary causes (sepsis, drugs, autoimmune disease, pregnancy, malignant hypertension).
- Consider CM‑HUS when ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced, Shiga toxin is negative and a TMA persists or progressive AKI occurs; remember that routine complement tests (C3/C4) can be normal. Extended complement functional and genetic testing can support the diagnosis but are often delayed and can be confounded by prior plasma exchange or complement blockade.
- Use kidney biopsy selectively, especially when diagnosis is unclear or to assess the degree of chronicity; classic findings in CM‑HUS include thrombi in glomerular capillaries and arterioles, endothelial swelling, mesangiolysis and ischemic changes.

Therapeutic implications follow directly from diagnosis. TTP needs urgent plasma exchange and often immunosuppression. STEC‑HUS is managed supportively. CM‑HUS benefits from early complement blockade with eculizumab, which reduces hemolysis and improves renal outcomes; however, initiating eculizumab during active bacteremia raises concern because the terminal complement pathway is critical for defense against encapsulated organisms (classically Neisseria meningitidis, but also other capsulated bacteria). When complement inhibition is contemplated, clinicians should strive to document microbiologic control, administer appropriate vaccines (meningococcal and, when indicated, Haemophilus and pneumococcal), and provide antimicrobial prophylaxis until effective vaccination or clearance of infection. Plasma exchange may provide temporary benefit in CM‑HUS—by removing circulating complement fragments or autoantibodies—but does not address the underlying complement dysregulation and may complicate timing of diagnostic testing and vaccination.

In summary, TMAs encompass a spectrum of disorders with overlapping clinical features but distinct pathobiology and divergent treatments. Infection is a frequent precipitant of TMA and—depending on the organism and host factors—may produce DIC, secondary TMA or trigger complement‑mediated HUS. A high index of suspicion is required when hemolysis and kidney injury persist or worsen despite resolution of sepsis or coagulopathy. Rapid, directed testing (ADAMTS13, Shiga toxin assays, peripheral smear for schistocytes) combined with a careful assessment for secondary causes allows timely institution of disease‑specific therapy and improves the chances of renal and hematologic recovery.
