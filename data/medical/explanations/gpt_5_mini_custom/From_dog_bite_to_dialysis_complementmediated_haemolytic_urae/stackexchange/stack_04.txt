Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient with hemolysis has a direct antiglobulin test (IgG) initially positive while receiving penicillin and after recent transfusions. What are two common causes of a false-positive DAT in this context, and what bedside clues help distinguish autoimmune hemolysis from microangiopathic hemolysis?

Direct answer
- Two common causes of a false‑positive direct antiglobulin (DAT) in this setting are: (1) drug‑induced coating of red cells (penicillin‑type hapten adsorption or formation of drug‑dependent antibodies) and (2) passive transfer of antibodies from recent transfusions or plasma products (including plasma or pooled‑plasma preparations). In this case the patient was receiving penicillin and had recent blood/product exposures, both well‑recognized causes of a spurious DAT.

Clinical reasoning and bedside discrimination between autoimmune hemolysis and microangiopathic hemolysis (MAHA/TMA)
- Platelet count: thrombocytopenia is a cardinal feature of TMA. AIHA usually has preserved platelets unless there is a concurrent process. In the case the platelets remained low (30–46 ×10^9/L), pointing away from isolated AIHA and toward a TMA.
- Peripheral blood smear: schistocytes (fragmented RBCs) indicate mechanical shearing of red cells and support MAHA/TMA; spherocytes favour warm AIHA. In this patient the smear showed 15–20% schistocytes—strong evidence for MAHA.
- DAT and treatment response: a true warm AIHA usually has a reproducible positive IgG DAT and often responds to corticosteroids. A DAT that is transient or converts to negative on repeat testing (especially when predating extensive immune workup) suggests a false positive. Here the initial DAT was positive while on penicillin/after transfusion, corticosteroids produced no durable improvement, and the repeat DAT was negative — consistent with a false‑positive DAT and a non‑autoimmune cause.
- Reticulocyte response and timing: AIHA typically produces a brisk reticulocytosis (unless marrow suppressed); MAHA can occur with variable retic response. In this case the reticulocyte response was blunted earlier (likely sepsis/marrow effects), but the sudden fall in hemoglobin with schistocytes and ongoing thrombocytopenia argued for TMA.
- Organ dysfunction and context: prominent acute kidney injury, new anuria, and other end‑organ ischemic signs (digital ischemia/necrosis) favour TMA. The patient’s severe AKI and biopsy showing fibrin thrombi confirmed TMA.
- Additional labs are supportive but nonspecific: both entities can have low haptoglobin and high LDH and indirect bilirubin; coagulation tests (prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen) suggest DIC rather than isolated AIHA.

Practical teaching points
- Always interpret a positive DAT in clinical context: recent penicillin exposure and transfusions are common causes of a false‑positive test.
- Immediate bedside clues that push you toward TMA rather than AIHA are persistent thrombocytopenia, abundant schistocytes on smear, end‑organ ischemia/AKI, lack of response to steroids, and a DAT that is not reproducibly positive — exactly the pattern seen in this patient that led to the diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS.
