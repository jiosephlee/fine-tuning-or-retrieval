Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] What clinical factors predict renal recovery and guide duration of complement blockade in complement-mediated HUS, and how do time-to-eculizumab initiation and the presence or absence of pathogenic complement gene variants inform these decisions?

Direct answer
- The strongest clinical determinants of renal recovery in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) are the severity and duration of the acute kidney injury (degree of oliguria/anuria and need for dialysis), the timing of effective complement blockade (earlier is better), the histologic severity/chronicity on kidney biopsy, and the initial haematologic response to therapy.  
- The presence of a pathogenic complement gene variant predicts a higher risk of relapse and incomplete renal recovery and therefore favors prolonged or indefinite complement inhibition. By contrast, a clearly identified single infectious trigger and absence of pathogenic variants support a time‑limited course of eculizumab with close monitoring and a planned trial of discontinuation after renal and haematologic stabilization.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (concise)
- CM‑HUS is driven by uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway causing endothelial injury, microthrombi, and downstream renal ischaemia. The longer terminal complement-mediated endothelial injury persists, the more likely irreversible parenchymal loss (tubular necrosis, cortical scarring, glomerular sclerosis) will occur. Thus, time from onset of TMA to effective blockade of C5 is a biologically plausible determinant of renal salvage.
- Genetic defects in complement regulators (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP/CD46, or anti‑FH antibodies) lower the threshold for activation and increase relapse risk; patients with such variants frequently require prolonged therapy because the predisposing abnormality remains.

How this applies to the reported case
- Severity at presentation: the patient developed complete anuria and dialysis dependence — a high‑risk presentation for incomplete renal recovery. Kidney biopsy showed acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescents — features that reflect severe acute injury but limited evidence of chronicity, which is compatible with potential partial recovery.
- Timing of eculizumab: eculizumab was started after clinical control of the bacteraemia (negative cultures) and after initial PEX; haematologic remission and sustained renal improvement (partial recovery to creatinine 175 µmol/L and dialysis independence) followed. This course illustrates the principle that once infection is controlled, timely complement blockade can stop further endothelial injury and allow recovery.
- Complement genetics: extended genetic testing in this patient did not identify pathogenic variants. This finding lowers the predicted risk of relapse and supports the treating team’s plan for a finite maintenance period (they continued eculizumab every 2 weeks and planned reassessment for discontinuation once renal function stabilised). It also explains the treating team’s willingness to stop after a period of stability rather than commit to life‑long therapy.

Practical management framework to guide duration of complement blockade
1. Acute management and timing
   - Start eculizumab promptly once CM‑HUS is diagnosed or strongly suspected and active infection has been controlled or when benefits outweigh infection risks. Earlier initiation (days rather than weeks after TMA recognition) is associated with higher likelihood of renal recovery.
   - If active encapsulated infection is present, control with appropriate antibiotics and obtain negative cultures if feasible before starting eculizumab; vaccinate and provide antimicrobial prophylaxis when initiating therapy.

2. Risk stratification to plan duration
   - High‑risk for relapse/poor recovery (consider prolonged therapy): pathogenic/likely pathogenic complement gene variant or anti‑factor H autoantibody; recurrent prior episodes; ongoing complement activation without clear transient trigger; incomplete renal recovery with ongoing proteinuria or hematuria.
   - Lower‑risk for relapse (consider time‑limited therapy and reassessment): clear single trigger (eg, infection) that is controlled, no pathogenic variants, full haematologic remission, dialysis independence with stabilised creatinine, absence of significant proteinuria or chronic histologic changes.

3. Suggested practical timelines (institutional practices vary)
   - For infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without pathogenic variants: continue induction and short maintenance (examples range from 4 weeks to 3–6 months) until haematologic remission and renal function have plateaued; then consider supervised discontinuation with close surveillance.
   - For patients with pathogenic variants or recurrent disease: continue maintenance eculizumab long term, with individualization and specialist nephrology/genetics input.

4. Monitoring after discontinuation
   - Frequent checks initially (eg, weekly to biweekly for first 1–2 months, then spacing out) of urine output, creatinine, urinalysis (protein/hematuria), platelets, hemoglobin, LDH. Re‑initiate eculizumab promptly at first sign of relapse (new MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI).

Important caveats and practical points
- Complement assays and AH50 can be misleading if obtained after plasma exchange or after eculizumab administration; genetic testing should ideally be sent prior to these interventions but treatment should not be delayed for testing results. In the case presented, AH50 was low after PEX and eculizumab, consistent with treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement deficiency.
- Vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis are essential when starting terminal complement inhibitors because of the increased risk for infection with encapsulated organisms; in this case eculizumab was started after first meningococcal vaccine doses and while on prophylactic antibiotics.
- Decisions about duration should be multidisciplinary (nephrology, hematology, infectious diseases, genetics) and individualized; when in doubt, a fixed period of maintenance followed by careful discontinuation and surveillance is a reasonable approach for infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without identified pathogenic variants — exactly the strategy used in this patient.

Bottom line
- Time to effective complement blockade and the degree/duration of acute renal injury are the principal determinants of renal recovery in CM‑HUS. Pathogenic complement gene variants markedly increase relapse risk and usually mandate prolonged therapy. In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without pathogenic variants (as in this case), a limited course of eculizumab with planned reassessment after haematologic remission and renal stabilisation is an evidence‑informed and pragmatic strategy.
