Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A septic patient initially meeting criteria for disseminated intravascular coagulation later shows an isolated drop in hemoglobin with undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, persistent thrombocytopenia, and normalizing PT/aPTT. Which clinical and laboratory features most strongly suggest superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy rather than ongoing DIC?

The constellation of an isolated, new-onset intravascular haemolysis with ongoing thrombocytopaenia despite normalization of clotting tests — occurring after the septic/DIC phase has settled — is the single strongest clinical signal that a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) has been superimposed rather than that DIC is ongoing.

Why: DIC is a consumptive coagulopathy driven by systemic activation of coagulation; it produces prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen and very high D‑dimer, and bleeding/skin necrosis from fibrin deposition. In contrast, TMA (eg, HUS/TTP) is driven by endothelial injury with platelet‑rich microthrombi in arterioles and capillaries; it causes microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) with schistocytes, severe intravascular haemolysis, organ ischemia (classically kidneys and brain), and typically normal PT/aPTT and fibrinogen because the plasmatic coagulation cascade is not the dominant process.

Features in this case that most strongly point to TMA over persistent DIC
- New, abrupt isolated fall in haemoglobin (111 → 68 g/L over 6 days) without external bleeding or haemodynamic instability — suggests intravascular haemolysis rather than ongoing bleeding from DIC.
- Laboratory evidence of MAHA: undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and a peripheral smear with 15–20% schistocytes — classic for microangiopathic red‑cell fragmentation.
- Persistent severe thrombocytopenia (30–40 × 10^9/L) despite normalization of coagulation studies (PT/aPTT and fibrinogen) — shows platelet consumption from microthrombi rather than global consumption coagulopathy.
- Timing: haemolysis and thrombocytopenia emerged after clinical improvement of sepsis and resolution of coagulopathy — argues for a new process (TMA) triggered by infection rather than ongoing DIC.
- Exclusion of alternate causes: negative repeat DAT (the initial IgG likely false positive from penicillin/transfusion) excludes warm autoimmune haemolysis; ADAMTS13 activity 43.5% rules out TTP (would be <10%); stool Shiga toxin PCR negative.
- Organ‑specific evidence of microvascular thrombosis: kidney biopsy showing ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and TMA morphology — pathologic confirmation that microvascular platelet thrombi were driving the clinical picture.
- Therapeutic response: transient improvement with plasma exchange and sustained haematologic/renal recovery after complement blockade (eculizumab) supports complement‑mediated TMA rather than DIC.

Practical diagnostic takeaways
- In a septic patient whose coagulopathy is improving, the new appearance of severe intravascular haemolysis (low haptoglobin, high LDH), schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopenia should prompt urgent evaluation for TMA (peripheral smear review, DAT, ADAMTS13, complement studies) and consideration of renal biopsy when safe.
- Use the pattern of coagulation tests to discriminate: abnormal PT/aPTT and low fibrinogen favour ongoing DIC; normalizing PT/aPTT with continued MAHA/thrombocytopenia favors TMA.

In short: isolated MAHA with schistocytosis, undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH, persistent thrombocytopenia despite normalization of PT/aPTT and fibrinogen, severe AKI with biopsy‑proven microvascular thrombi, and exclusion of TTP point to a superimposed TMA (in this case complement‑mediated HUS) rather than ongoing DIC.
