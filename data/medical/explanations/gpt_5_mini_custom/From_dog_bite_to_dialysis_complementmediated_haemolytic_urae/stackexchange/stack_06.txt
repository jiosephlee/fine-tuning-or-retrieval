Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] In suspected complement-mediated HUS complicating recent bacteremia, outline immediate management while awaiting ADAMTS13 and Shiga-toxin results: when to initiate plasma exchange as a bridge, when to start eculizumab, and how to mitigate infectious risk with meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis.

Direct answer
- While awaiting ADAMTS13 and Shiga‑toxin results in a patient with new MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI, treat emergently for the most immediately life‑threatening and remediable diagnoses: assume possible TTP and start plasma exchange (PEX) if the clinical picture or a high PLASMIC score supports it; send ADAMTS13 immediately and continue PEX until severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) is excluded. If TTP is unlikely (low PLASMIC, ADAMTS13 likely >10%) and the clinical picture is instead consistent with complement‑mediated HUS (severe AKI, schistocytes, negative STEC testing), arrange prompt eculizumab — ideally after the infective trigger is controlled or blood cultures are negative and after meningococcal vaccination or while on antibiotic prophylaxis. In all cases obtain baseline complement and genetic testing before PEX or complement blockade when possible, and mitigate meningococcal risk by vaccinating (MenACWY + MenB) and providing immediate antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine protection is achieved.

Clinical reasoning and practical steps (what to do now and why)
1. Rapid diagnostic priorities
   - Immediately send ADAMTS13 activity (reference lab) and stool PCR for Shiga toxin. These are the decisive tests to distinguish TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC‑HUS.
   - Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics if that does not meaningfully delay therapy; start empiric antibiotics promptly for sepsis.

2. When to start plasma exchange (PEX)
   - Indication: start urgent PEX if TTP cannot be excluded and the patient has clinically significant MAHA ± severe thrombocytopenia ± neurologic features — i.e., high or intermediate pretest probability (PLASMIC score high/intermediate) or when ADAMTS13 result is not immediately available.
   - Rationale: PEX is lifesaving in TTP; delaying PEX while waiting for ADAMTS13 risks irreversible organ injury.
   - Use plasma replacement (FFP/Octaplas); reassess daily and stop PEX once ADAMTS13 activity is >10% and an alternative diagnosis is established.
   - In suspected CM‑HUS PEX can be used as a temporizing measure (it may transiently remove complement fragments or autoantibodies) but it is not disease modifying; expect only temporary improvement. This is what occurred in the case: PEX was used as a bridge and produced transient diuresis before definitive therapy.

3. When to start eculizumab (complement blockade)
   - Indication: strong clinical and laboratory evidence of complement‑mediated TMA (MAHA, schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia, severe AKI or biopsy‑proven TMA) once TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC‑HUS are excluded or unlikely.
   - Timing with infection: because terminal complement is important for defence against encapsulated organisms, delay eculizumab until active, uncontrolled bacteraemia is controlled (blood cultures negative or on effective therapy) if clinically feasible. However, in fulminant CM‑HUS with life‑ or organ‑threatening disease, start eculizumab urgently after multidisciplinary discussion and with maximal antimicrobial coverage — balancing risk of infection against risk of irreversible organ damage.
   - Practical coordination: do not start PEX immediately after giving eculizumab (PEX removes drug). If PEX is required after starting eculizumab, expect to redose eculizumab or adjust dosing strategy.

4. Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis to mitigate meningococcal (and other encapsulated) risk
   - Vaccinate with both a conjugate quadrivalent meningococcal vaccine (MenACWY) and a serogroup B vaccine (MenB). Best practice is to give both vaccines at least 2 weeks before initiating eculizumab to allow protective immunity.
   - If eculizumab cannot be delayed for 2 weeks after vaccination (common in urgent cases), give vaccines immediately and start antibiotic prophylaxis until 2 weeks after completion of the vaccine series (or until protective period has passed). In practice many centers:
     - Give MenACWY + MenB at diagnosis or immediately before eculizumab.
     - Start oral antibiotic prophylaxis (eg, penicillin V or amoxicillin) until 2 weeks after vaccination; for true penicillin allergy use an alternative per local guidance (eg, ciprofloxacin or doxycycline) and specialist advice.
   - During ongoing eculizumab therapy many centers continue long‑term prophylactic antibiotics and counsel patients to seek urgent care for fever; some also provide a course of standby antibiotics and recommend prompt medical evaluation for any febrile illness.
   - In the reported case the team gave MenACWY (Menveo) and MenB (Bexsero) immediately before starting eculizumab and provided prophylactic amoxicillin because the vaccines had not been given ≥2 weeks beforehand; blood cultures were negative when eculizumab was started — a cautious, guideline‑concordant approach.

5. Testing logistics and interpretation
   - Draw complement tests and genetic testing samples before PEX and before eculizumab whenever feasible; both PEX and eculizumab alter complement assays and can confound interpretation. The case illustrates this: standard C3/C4 were normal and extended testing was done later — some abnormalities were attributed to treatment effects.
   - ADAMTS13 should be drawn before first PEX if possible but do not delay PEX for the result if clinical suspicion of TTP exists.

6. Communication and follow‑up
   - Provide clear counseling and written instructions about infection risk (meningococcal and other encapsulated organisms), the need for urgent evaluation for fever, and the vaccination schedule (boosters as recommended).
   - Coordinate infectious disease, hematology and nephrology input for individual risk–benefit decisions (especially when active bacteremia is present).

Bottom line practical algorithm for the bedside (while awaiting ADAMTS13 and Shiga‑toxin)
   1. Send ADAMTS13, Shiga PCR/stool, blood cultures, baseline complement/genetics.
   2. Empiric antibiotics for sepsis immediately.
   3. If TTP cannot be excluded (high/intermediate PLASMIC or severe clinical disease) → start daily PEX now; continue until ADAMTS13 excludes TTP.
   4. If clinical picture favors CM‑HUS (severe AKI, dialysis dependence, schistocytes, negative STEC) and TTP is unlikely → arrange eculizumab promptly. If active bacteraemia, prefer to wait for culture clearance if feasible; if not feasible because of life‑threatening TMA, start eculizumab with maximal antibiotics and meningococcal prophylaxis.
   5. Give MenACWY + MenB vaccines ideally ≥2 weeks before eculizumab; if eculizumab must start sooner, vaccinate immediately and give antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine protection is established (and consider continued prophylaxis during therapy).
   6. Reassess daily and tailor therapy (stop PEX if ADAMTS13 >10%; continue or stop eculizumab per response and genetic testing).

The case in point
- This patient exemplifies the approach: ADAMTS13 and complement studies were sent; PEX was started as bridging therapy while TTP was excluded; blood cultures became negative and meningococcal vaccines were given; eculizumab induction followed and produced sustained hematologic and renal improvement. This sequencing minimized infectious risk while treating the complement‑driven TMA in a timely fashion.
