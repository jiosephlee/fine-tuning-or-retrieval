Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic workup and clinico-pathologic confirmation of complement-mediated HUS

The diagnostic approach in this case focused on three parallel goals: (1) rapidly exclude entities that require different immediate therapy (especially immune thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, TTP), (2) rule out typical Shiga-toxin mediated HUS (STEC-HUS), and (3) obtain histologic proof of renal thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) while interpreting complement studies in the context of ongoing treatments. Together these steps permitted a clinicopathologic diagnosis of infection‑triggered complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS).

TTP was the first critical consideration because severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) mandates urgent plasma exchange (PEX). Clinical triage using the PLASMIC score gives a pretest probability while awaiting assays; this patient’s PLASMIC score was low (4/7). More importantly, ADAMTS13 activity measured early was 43.5%, well above the threshold for TTP and thus effectively excluded immune TTP. Because results can take time, PEX was nevertheless begun empirically as a bridging therapy until the ADAMTS13 result was available—a common and pragmatic step when TTP remains a realistic early differential.

STEC‑HUS was excluded by clinical and microbiological data. The patient lacked a diarrhoeal prodrome typical of Shiga‑toxin associated disease and stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative. In adults, the absence of an enteric illness plus a negative toxin assay makes STEC‑HUS unlikely and shifts focus to alternative causes of TMA.

Routine complement tests (serum C3 and C4) and broad pathway screens are commonly ordered but have limited sensitivity for CM‑HUS. Normal C3 and C4 concentrations do not exclude dysregulation of the alternative pathway; complement consumption may be focal, transient, or masked by acute phase responses, and levels can be within reference ranges even during active disease. Thus, normal routine complement results in this patient did not argue against CM‑HUS and did not obviate further evaluation.

Renal biopsy provided the decisive tissue evidence of TMA. At the time of biopsy the platelet count had recovered to 77 × 10^9/L, reducing bleeding risk and permitting safe sampling. Light microscopy showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic-appearing glomeruli with intraluminal fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation—features characteristic of a renal TMA rather than an immune-complex glomerulonephritis. Immunofluorescence revealed no immune complex deposition and electron microscopy showed no features of a primary glomerulopathy, findings that help distinguish CM‑HUS from immune‑complex or other glomerular diseases. These histologic changes together with the peripheral blood picture (schistocytes and ongoing microangiopathic haemolysis) establish the presence of microvascular thrombotic injury in the kidneys.

Extended complement testing sent after initial therapy requires cautious interpretation. In this patient the AH50 (functional assay of the alternative pathway) was markedly reduced (6%). However, those samples were obtained after several sessions of PEX (which removes circulating complement proteins and regulators) and after the first dose of eculizumab (a terminal complement C5 inhibitor). Both interventions alter functional assays and component levels, so the abnormal AH50 is most consistent with treatment effects rather than proof of an intrinsic complement deficiency or ongoing unmitigated complement activation. Likewise, definitive testing for anti‑factor H autoantibodies and genetic sequencing for pathogenic variants were negative; absence of an identifiable variant does not exclude CM‑HUS because not all pathogenic mechanisms are detectable and penetrance of known variants is incomplete.

Putting the pieces together, the diagnosis rests on exclusion and concordance of clinical, laboratory and histologic data: TTP and STEC‑HUS were ruled out, kidney biopsy demonstrated TMA without immune complex disease, haemolysis and thrombocytopenia were consistent with MAHA, and the temporal sequence—an episode of C. canimorsus sepsis followed by a delayed, isolated haemolytic TMA—fits infection‑triggered CM‑HUS. The negative extended genetic workup argues against a known inherited complement mutation in this case, but CM‑HUS remains a clinicopathologic diagnosis of exclusion in which an infectious “second hit” commonly precipitates disease in a susceptible host.
