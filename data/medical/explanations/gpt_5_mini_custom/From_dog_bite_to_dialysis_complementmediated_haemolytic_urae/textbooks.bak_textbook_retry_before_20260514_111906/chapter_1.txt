Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 1: ## Initial presentation: fulminant sepsis after dog bite and early management

This patient’s illness began four days after a dog bite to the upper lip and progressed rapidly to fulminant sepsis with multisystem involvement. Her initial presentation is important because it illustrates both the typical clinical course of severe Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection and the key early management decisions when a patient presents with suspected bite-associated sepsis.

Clinical picture and early physiologic derangement
On arrival she had high-grade fever (>40 °C), marked malaise, mottled and cool lower extremities, refractory hypoglycaemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), and biochemical evidence of tissue hypoperfusion (metabolic acidosis, pH 7.27, lactate 5.7 mmol/L). These findings reflect severe systemic inflammatory response and impaired perfusion typical of septic shock physiology. Hypoglycaemia in sepsis can result from impaired hepatic gluconeogenesis, increased peripheral glucose utilization, and, less commonly, adrenal insufficiency; it may be particularly marked with fulminant bacterial infections.

Laboratory pattern and interpretation as DIC
Initial laboratory testing showed severe inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated transaminases and a coagulopathy. This combination—especially thrombocytopenia, prolonged coagulation tests and bleeding/mottling of the extremities—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia. In DIC activated coagulation leads to widespread fibrin deposition with microvascular thrombosis (contributing to distal ischaemia) and consumption of platelets and clotting factors (causing bleeding). In the context of sepsis, DIC commonly contributes to organ dysfunction through both microthrombi and haemorrhagic complications.

Microthrombotic phenomena and skin findings
Clinically she developed diffuse skin bleeding, ecchymoses and distal cyanosis/ischemia of toes and fingers despite preserved pulses and sensation. These findings are characteristic of microvascular occlusion from DIC/TMA-type processes: preserved pulses suggest a small–vessel problem rather than large-vessel occlusion. Such peripheral ischaemia in sepsis should prompt consideration of DIC or other microangiopathic processes and usually warrants supportive correction of coagulopathy when bleeding or impending tissue loss is present.

Early empiric antimicrobial therapy and microbiologic considerations
Because the history of a dog bite raised strong suspicion for zoonotic pathogens, empiric broad‑spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was started. This choice provides immediate coverage for common dog/cat bite pathogens (including Pasteurella species), anaerobes and many gram‑negative rods, and it also covers Capnocytophaga while cultures are pending. Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow‑growing, fastidious, encapsulated gram‑negative rod found in canine and feline oral flora; invasive infection is rare but can be fulminant. Its fastidious growth can delay culture confirmation (as in this case, where blood cultures grew the organism after 7 days), so early empiric coverage based on exposure history is critical. Once blood cultures were positive and susceptibilities known, therapy was narrowed to ampicillin and later oral phenoxymethylpenicillin as appropriate.

Rationale for ICU-level supportive care
Rapid progression to complete anuria and fluid overload required ICU-level management. The patient developed septic cardiomyopathy, failed to respond to diuretics, and became fluid overloaded; continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was therefore initiated for volume control, solute clearance and support during anuric AKI. Indications for CRRT in septic patients include refractory fluid overload, severe acid‑base or electrolyte disturbances, or hemodynamic instability where intermittent dialysis is poorly tolerated. In this case, CRRT addressed both solute control and life‑threatening fluid accumulation when the heart and kidneys could not maintain balance.

Management of coagulopathy and transfusion decisions
Given active bleeding and laboratory evidence of consumption coagulopathy, supportive haemostatic therapy (platelet and fibrinogen-containing products) was administered. In DIC, transfusion decisions are guided by bleeding, planned procedures and laboratory thresholds: platelets are generally transfused for active bleeding or before invasive procedures when counts are low (commonly <50 × 10^9/L if bleeding), and fibrinogen is replaced when markedly reduced and bleeding is present. Albumin was given as part of fluid resuscitation and to manage intravascular volume and oncotic pressure in the setting of capillary leak and hypoalbuminaemia.

Diagnostic evaluation for alternate causes of pancytopenia
The marked pancytopenia raised concern for alternative diagnoses such as acute leukaemia or haemophagocytic syndromes. Haematology input and flow cytometry were obtained, and a bone marrow biopsy was planned but ultimately cancelled when flow cytometry showed no abnormal blasts or phenotypes. Severe leukopaenia can occur in overwhelming sepsis (including C. canimorsus infection) because of marrow suppression, peripheral consumption, or cytokine‑mediated effects; thus, in the appropriate infectious context a marrow diagnosis may not be necessary once malignancy is reasonably excluded.

Other early investigations and monitoring
Autoimmune screens (ANA, ANCA) were negative, which helped focus management on an infectious cause. Because Capnocytophaga is fastidious, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion with relevant exposure history and consider molecular diagnostic methods when cultures are slow or negative. In this case cultures eventually confirmed C. canimorsus at day 7, allowing de‑escalation to narrower beta‑lactam therapy.

Putting it together: why these early decisions matter
Early recognition of bite‑associated sepsis and prompt empiric broad‑spectrum antimicrobial therapy are crucial because delays in appropriate antibiotics in fulminant infections are associated with worse outcomes. Supportive ICU care (including CRRT for anuric AKI and targeted blood product transfusions for DIC with bleeding) stabilised the patient through the initial septic phase. The history of a dog bite, rapid progression to DIC and pancytopenia should immediately raise Capnocytophaga as a potential pathogen and justify empiric coverage while awaiting culture confirmation.
