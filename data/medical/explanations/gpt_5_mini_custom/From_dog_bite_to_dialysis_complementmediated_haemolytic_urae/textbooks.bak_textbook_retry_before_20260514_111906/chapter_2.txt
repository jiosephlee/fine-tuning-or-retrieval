Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 2: ## From DIC to TMA: recognizing a new problem

This patient’s course demonstrates a classic inflection point: the initial life‑threatening sepsis and consumptive coagulopathy were improving, yet a new, isolated haemolytic process abruptly appeared. On transfer to the nephrology ward her inflammatory markers and conventional coagulation tests had normalised and platelets were recovering from the nadir associated with DIC, but kidney failure persisted. Over the next 6 days she suffered a rapid fall in haemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L without any clinical bleeding, while platelets remained in the 30–40 × 10^9/L range. At the same time LDH rose and haptoglobin became undetectable — a laboratory profile consistent with active intravascular haemolysis rather than ongoing simple blood loss.

Differentiating late or persistent DIC from a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is critical because the diagnostic approach and therapy diverge. DIC reflects systemic coagulation activation with consumption of clotting factors and platelets, prolonged PT/aPTT and low fibrinogen; bleeding and end‑organ ischaemia are typical when DIC is florid. By contrast, TMA is characterised by microvascular platelet‑rich thrombi causing microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and organ dysfunction (frequently kidneys), while routine coagulation tests are often normal. In this case the normalization of coagulation parameters while haemolysis and thrombocytopenia persisted argued against ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and raised concern for a discrete TMA process.

The initial positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG prompted a reasonable early consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and led to empiric high‑dose corticosteroids. Lack of clinical or laboratory response to steroids, together with a repeat negative DAT, made warm autoimmune haemolysis unlikely. Importantly, penicillins and recent transfusions can give false‑positive DATs or induce drug‑related antibodies, so an isolated early positive DAT should be interpreted cautiously in the setting of recent antibiotics and transfusions.

The peripheral blood film provided the decisive evidence. The presence of abundant schistocytes (15–20%) demonstrated fragmentation haemolysis and established MAHA. The combination of MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia and severe, dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury shifted the diagnostic focus firmly to TMA and prompted the appropriate next steps: measurement of ADAMTS13 activity to exclude TTP, stool testing for Shiga toxin, complement studies and tissue confirmation by renal biopsy. In this patient, ADAMTS13 activity was not consistent with TTP and the biopsy showed thrombotic lesions consistent with TMA, supporting a diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS.

In short, the clinical cue that haemolysis emerged as sepsis and DIC were resolving — together with laboratory features of intravascular haemolysis, a negative reproducible DAT and schistocytosis on smear — changed the working diagnosis from a consumptive coagulopathy to a new, superimposed TMA. Recognising this transition is essential because it redirects management from primarily supportive care for sepsis/DIC to targeted TMA therapies (for example, plasma exchange as a bridge and consideration of complement blockade), while also guiding appropriate further investigations.
