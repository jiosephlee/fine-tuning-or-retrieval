Chapter 4: ## Diagnostic Workup and Interpretation in Suspected Telomere Biology Disorders

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are protean and often masquerade as common acquired diseases. The diagnostic evaluation therefore has two parallel aims: (1) exclude reversible and common causes of the patient’s abnormalities, and (2) assemble phenotype-directed, telomere‑specific data that permit objective interpretation of genetic findings. A stepwise, multidisciplinary approach reduces misclassification, guides management, and informs family counselling.

Initial clinical and laboratory evaluation
Begin with a focused haematologic and metabolic screen. A complete blood count with red‑cell indices, reticulocyte count and careful peripheral smear review are essential. In TBDs the marrow often demonstrates ineffective haematopoiesis with low reticulocyte production despite cytopenias; the presence of macrocytosis or dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, hypersegmented neutrophils or marrow erythroid hyperplasia should prompt investigation for nutritional and inherited marrow failure syndromes. Concurrent measurement of serum vitamin B12, folate, iron studies (iron, TIBC, ferritin), and copper is important because deficiencies can mimic or unmask an underlying telomeropathy, as in this case where severe B12 deficiency explained some but not all features. A haemolysis panel (LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin fractions) and direct antiglobulin test help distinguish peripheral destruction from ineffective erythropoiesis. Bone marrow examination—including morphology, iron stores, cytogenetics and targeted flow cytometry—should be performed when there is persistent unexplained cytopenia to exclude myelodysplasia, marrow infiltration, aplastic anaemia and clonal disorders.

Because TBDs are multisystem, baseline organ assessments are mandatory. Liver tests (transaminases, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, albumin, coagulation profile) and imaging—ultrasound and, where available, transient elastography—screen for hepatic steatosis, fibrosis and portal hypertension. For the lungs, spirometry, DLCO and high‑resolution CT (HRCT) are required to detect early interstitial lung disease (ILD) or usual interstitial pneumonia patterns that often herald telomeropathy in adults. Bone density testing is indicated in patients with fractures, early osteoporosis or bone pain. Neurologic evaluation and electromyography are appropriate if neuropathy or neuromuscular features are present. Infectious and autoimmune serologies should be obtained where clinically indicated to exclude secondary causes of organ fibrosis or cytopenia. Importantly, reversible contributors (nutritional deficiencies, alcohol, viral hepatitis, medications, occupational exposures) must be corrected or excluded before attributing findings to a genetic TBD.

Telomere length testing: methods and interpretation
Measurement of telomere length is a key step in confirming a TBD and contextualizing genetic variants. Flow‑FISH (flow cytometry combined with fluorescence in situ hybridization) performed on leukocyte subsets (commonly lymphocytes and granulocytes) is the preferred clinical assay because it provides cell‑type–specific, age‑adjusted percentiles with good reproducibility. Results are interpreted against population norms: telomere lengths below the first percentile for age are highly supportive of a TBD in the appropriate clinical context; lengths between the 1st and 10th percentiles are suggestive when the phenotype is concordant. qPCR based assays are widely used in research and population studies but have greater interassay variability and are less reliable for diagnostic decision‑making in an individual patient; Southern blot Terminal Restriction Fragment analysis is historically considered a gold standard but is less practical clinically.

Timing and sample considerations matter. Flow‑FISH requires viable fresh blood and should ideally be performed before transfusions or allogeneic cell infusions because donor leukocytes can confound results. Likewise, recent hematopoietic growth factor therapy, severe infections or profound cytopenias can affect leukocyte subset distribution and interpretation; specialist laboratories can provide guidance on preanalytic requirements.

Genetic testing strategy
Genetic confirmation is the cornerstone of modern TBD diagnosis. Testing should start with a targeted gene panel that includes canonical telomere and telomerase pathway genes (for example, TERT, TERC, DKC1, RTEL1, PARN, TINF2, POT1, NAF1, WRAP53/TCAB1, CTC1 and others). Because TERC is an RNA gene and some pathogenic changes are copy‑number variants (deletions), ensure the chosen platform reliably interrogates noncoding loci and includes copy‑number analysis or a separate assay for large deletions/duplications. If a targeted panel is unrevealing and the clinical index of suspicion remains high, exome or genome sequencing with attention to coverage of telomere genes is reasonable.

Interpretation of sequence variants should follow ACMG/AMP guidelines but always be integrated with clinical and telomere length data. Loss‑of‑function variants (nonsense, frameshift, canonical splice) in genes where haploinsufficiency is a known mechanism are more readily declared pathogenic. Missense variants require careful assessment of conservation, in silico predictions, location in functional domains, absence from population databases and, when available, functional data. The PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) change described in this case, for example, hits a conserved residue within the R3H domain and meets several supportive ACMG criteria (PM1, PM2, PP3, PP2) but lacks definitive functional or segregation evidence and therefore remains a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS).

Approach to a VUS in a telomere gene
A VUS should not by itself trigger irreversible clinical decisions (for example, exclusion of a relative from bone marrow donation or definitive curative procedures) but can be meaningful when it is integrated with phenotype and telomere length. A VUS in a gene with strong biological plausibility (PARN), located in a critical domain, and predicted deleterious gains weight if the patient has very short telomeres (<1st percentile) and a compatible multisystem phenotype (bone marrow dysfunction, early greying, pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis). In such circumstances the variant is provisionally considered likely relevant and prompts intensified surveillance and family studies, but management decisions that could cause harm should rely on a combination of genetic, biochemical and clinical evidence.

Reclassification efforts: functional testing and segregation
To move a VUS toward pathogenic or benign status, pursue additional evidence. Segregation analysis in affected and unaffected family members is highly informative: co‑segregation of the variant with disease (and short telomeres) supports pathogenicity. Conversely, identification of the variant in healthy elderly relatives with normal telomere lengths argues against pathogenicity. Telomere length testing in relatives provides complementary data because telomere shortening often segregates with disease risk even before clinical manifestations. Where available, specialized functional assays—such as assessment of TERC maturation for PARN variants, telomerase activity assays, or cellular telomere biology studies—can provide direct evidence of mechanistic impact, though these tests are largely research tools and available only in specialized centers.

Family testing and implications for donor selection
Cascade genetic testing and telomere length measurement in relatives are critical for counselling and management. Family members who carry a pathogenic variant or who have short telomeres should undergo clinical surveillance for cytopenias, pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic disease. Importantly, family members with either a pathogenic variant or demonstrably short telomeres should generally be excluded as hematopoietic stem cell donors because donor telomere length influences post‑transplant outcomes and short‑telomere donors have been associated with graft complications. When a proband has only a VUS, combine targeted sequencing of the variant with telomere length testing in potential donors to reduce uncertainty prior to transplant decisions.

When to involve specialists and how results affect management
Early involvement of a medical geneticist or a specialized inherited marrow failure clinic is recommended to coordinate testing, interpret complex results, counsel the family and arrange longitudinal surveillance. Pulmonology (ILD specialists) and hepatology should be engaged when there is pulmonary or hepatic involvement; transplant teams (both lung and hematopoietic stem cell) should be involved early if progressive organ failure is a concern because conditioning regimens and donor choice must be individualized for telomere syndromes. Psychosocial support and reproductive counselling (including discussion of prenatal or preimplantation genetic diagnosis when a pathogenic variant is identified) are often appropriate.

Practical diagnostic algorithm for the presented patient
For the patient described here the practical sequence would be: (1) complete the initial workup and correct reversible contributors (vitamin B12, folate, electrolyte abnormalities); (2) obtain baseline organ assessments (HRCT chest and pulmonary function tests, transient elastography, bone density) and continue routine surveillance labs; (3) perform telomere length testing by flow‑FISH on fresh blood as soon as feasible (preferably before further transfusions); and (4) pursue targeted genetic testing with a telomere gene panel that includes copy‑number detection and noncoding loci, or confirmatory testing for the PARN variant found on exome sequencing if exome coverage was incomplete for relevant loci. If telomere length is very short and the phenotype remains concordant, the PARN VUS can be provisionally treated as contributory for clinical decision‑making (surveillance intensity, avoidance of toxic exposures, careful donor selection), while efforts continue to obtain segregation and functional data that might permit reclassification.

Summary
Diagnosis of TBDs rests on the integration of clinical phenotype, organ‑specific evaluation, telomere length measurement and robust genetic testing. Flow‑FISH telomere measurement and targeted gene panels that include copy‑number analysis are the core diagnostic tools. A VUS in a biologically plausible gene should prompt additional investigations—family telomere testing, segregation analysis, and, where possible, functional testing—before it is used to justify irreversible therapeutic decisions. Multidisciplinary care and longitudinal reassessment are essential because penetrance is variable, telomere length and clinical manifestations can evolve, and variant classification may change as new evidence emerges.
