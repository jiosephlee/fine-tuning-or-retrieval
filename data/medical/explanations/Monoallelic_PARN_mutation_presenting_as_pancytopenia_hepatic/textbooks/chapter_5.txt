Chapter 5: ## Management: Supportive Care, Organ‑Specific Therapy, and Transplant Considerations

Management of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is principally pragmatic and multidisciplinary. Because there are no uniformly effective disease‑modifying therapies for all manifestations, care focuses on treating organ‑specific complications, minimizing exposures that accelerate telomere attrition, and identifying patients who may benefit from targeted or curative interventions such as androgen therapy or haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). The clinical course can be highly variable—even among carriers of the same pathogenic variant—so management is individualized and ideally delivered from a centre experienced with inherited marrow failure and interstitial lung disease.

General supportive care and prevention
Every patient with a suspected or confirmed TBD should have a systematic baseline assessment and be given interventions to correct reversible contributors to morbidity. Nutritional deficiencies (for example vitamin B12, folate, vitamin D and calcium) should be identified and corrected promptly; in our patient the severe B12 deficiency explained much of the ineffective erythropoiesis and required urgent replacement. Electrolyte abnormalities should be corrected and bone health optimized with calcium, vitamin D repletion and weight‑bearing exercise; baseline and interval bone mineral density testing are appropriate given the high prevalence of osteoporosis. Vaccination against influenza, pneumococcus and SARS‑CoV‑2 is recommended; hepatitis A and B vaccination is particularly important in patients with liver disease. Live attenuated vaccines should be avoided in patients who are moderately to severely immunocompromised or who are anticipated to receive HSCT or potent immunosuppression.

Lifestyle and medication counselling are central. Patients should be counselled to avoid tobacco, marijuana, occupational inhalational toxins and recreational drugs that harm the lung, and to avoid excess alcohol and other hepatotoxins. Review medication lists and minimize or avoid agents with known myelo-, hepato- or pulmonary toxicity (for example high‑dose alkylators, bleomycin, and amiodarone when alternatives exist). Routine cancer screening should follow standard age‑appropriate guidelines, but clinicians should maintain a lower threshold to investigate new cytopenias, pulmonary symptoms or abnormal liver tests.

Hematologic management
Treatment of cytopenias in TBD begins with supportive measures. Red‑cell and platelet transfusions should be given for symptomatic anaemia or bleeding, and iron overload must be monitored when transfusion dependence develops—serum ferritin and hepatic iron quantification with MRI guide decisions about chelation. For neutropenia complicated by recurrent infections, short courses of granulocyte colony‑stimulating factor (G‑CSF) may be used judiciously to reduce infectious complications; however, growth‑factor therapy should be managed by specialists because of theoretical concerns about clonal evolution and because the long‑term safety data in TBD are limited.

Where bone marrow failure progresses, androgens (danazol or nandrolone) are an option that has shown benefit in small prospective and retrospective series. Androgen therapy has been associated with haematologic improvement and, in some reports, with measurable telomere lengthening. Typical regimens have used danazol 600–800 mg/day or nandrolone formulations, but dosing must be individualized and monitored closely. Important potential toxicities include hepatotoxicity (including cholestasis and elevation of liver enzymes), adverse lipid changes, virilization, fluid retention, and an increased thrombotic risk; these are especially concerning in patients with underlying hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis. Before and during therapy, obtain baseline and periodic liver function tests, lipid profiles, and clinical assessments for virilization and thrombosis. Because evidence derives from small cohorts, androgen therapy is best initiated and followed in specialized centres and after a careful discussion of risks and benefits.

Other pharmacologic approaches (erythropoiesis‑stimulating agents, thrombopoietin receptor agonists such as eltrombopag) have limited and mixed evidence in TBDs; their use should be considered on a case‑by‑case basis in consultation with experts. When progression to myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) or acute leukaemia occurs, management follows standard haematologic oncology principles, but outcomes may be poorer and therapies more toxic in the setting of short telomeres.

Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)
HSCT is the only potentially curative therapy for progressive marrow failure or transformation to MDS/AML in TBD. However, HSCT in patients with telomere syndromes carries higher peri‑transplant morbidity and mortality, particularly from pulmonary and hepatic complications, and requires careful risk‑benefit assessment. Indications for HSCT include severe aplastic anaemia refractory to supportive care, transfusion dependence with progressive cytopenias, or evolution to MDS/AML where transplant is otherwise indicated.

Conditioning regimens should be modified to reduce toxicity: reduced‑intensity, radiation‑sparing regimens (fludarabine‑based) are preferred over myeloablative conditioning that includes high‑dose alkylators and total body irradiation, both of which have been linked to excessive pulmonary and hepatic toxicity in TBD. Regimens that minimize or avoid busulfan and cyclophosphamide dose intensity are commonly used. Outcomes are improved with careful pre‑transplant optimization of pulmonary and hepatic function, and candid discussions about increased risks.

Family and donor evaluation is critical. Related donors should undergo genetic testing for the familial pathogenic variant and assessment of telomere length; asymptomatic carriers should not be used as stem cell donors. Unrelated matched donors may be preferable when a related donor is affected or of uncertain status. Pre‑transplant counseling should also address fertility preservation and long‑term complications.

Pulmonary disease: antifibrotic therapy, rehabilitation and transplant
Pulmonary fibrosis is a common and often progressive manifestation of TBD, and management aligns with established idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) principles while accounting for the haematologic background. Antifibrotic agents—nintedanib and pirfenidone—slow the rate of decline in forced vital capacity in IPF and are reasonable therapeutic options in TBD‑associated pulmonary fibrosis; data specific to TBD are limited but emerging and these drugs are not contraindicated by short telomeres. Close monitoring for drug interactions and hepatic adverse effects is necessary, particularly in patients with concurrent hepatic fibrosis.

Immunosuppression (for example chronic high‑dose corticosteroids or cytotoxic regimens) is generally avoided because it offers little benefit in fibrotic lung disease and increases infection risk in patients already prone to cytopenias. Pulmonary rehabilitation, supplemental oxygen for hypoxaemia, early vaccination and proactive treatment of infections are important supportive measures. Given the risk of progressive respiratory failure, early referral to a lung transplant centre is appropriate for patients with advanced disease; however, pre‑transplant evaluation must include haematologic assessment because cytopenias and marrow insufficiency complicate peri‑operative and post‑transplant care. Post‑lung transplant immunosuppression may precipitate marrow suppression; thus transplant teams should tailor regimens and plan for close haematologic follow‑up.

Hepatic disease: conservative management and transplant considerations
Liver involvement in TBD ranges from steatosis and fibrosis to cirrhosis and portal hypertension. Management begins with general measures to reduce metabolic liver injury: control of weight, glucose and lipids; abstinence from alcohol; avoidance of hepatotoxic medications; and treatment of coexisting viral hepatitis if present. Patients with cirrhosis require routine hepatology surveillance—screening for portal hypertension, oesophageal varices, hepatocellular carcinoma where indicated, and vaccinations for hepatitis A and B.

Androgen therapy may be limited or contraindicated in patients with significant hepatic fibrosis because of hepatotoxicity. For patients who progress to decompensated liver disease, liver transplantation is an accepted option, but outcomes are influenced by the underlying TBD: coexisting marrow failure increases peri‑operative risk, and post‑transplant immunosuppression can exacerbate cytopenias and infection risk. Multidisciplinary discussion among hepatology, transplant surgery, haematology and genetics is essential; combined organ transplantation or staged approaches are considered in selected centres but carry high complexity and risk.

Bone, endocrine and neuromuscular complications
Osteoporosis and fractures are common in TBD and should be managed actively. Ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D, encourage weight‑bearing activity when feasible, and consider antiresorptive therapy (bisphosphonates) or other bone‑active agents according to standard osteoporosis guidelines and individual bleeding risk or renal function. Evaluate and treat secondary causes of bone loss, including vitamin D deficiency and hypogonadism. Endocrine screening for thyroid dysfunction, glucose intolerance and hypogonadism should be performed at baseline and periodically.

Neuromuscular symptoms, including peripheral neuropathy, require standard neurologic evaluation to identify treatable causes (nutritional deficiencies, metabolic derangements, medication toxicities) and symptomatic management including physiotherapy—an important part of rehabilitative care for patients with fatigue, dyspnoea and deconditioning.

Practical approach in this case and monitoring strategy
For the patient described, the immediate priorities were correction of reversible contributors (vitamin B12, folate and vitamin D), symptomatic transfusions as needed, and comprehensive baseline organ assessment (CBC, bone marrow, liver elastography, high‑resolution CT chest, pulmonary‑function testing including DLCO, and bone density). Given her monoallelic PARN variant and hepatic fibrosis, androgen therapy would be relatively contraindicated or used with extreme caution because of hepatotoxicity; therefore androgens would be considered only if marrow failure progressed and after hepatology input. HSCT would be reserved for frank, progressive marrow failure or malignant transformation and, if contemplated, requires a fludarabine‑based reduced‑intensity regimen and careful donor screening to exclude relatives with the PARN variant or short telomeres.

Longitudinal care should include periodic CBCs (at least every 3–6 months or more often with instability), annual or semiannual liver testing and elastography as guided by hepatology, interval pulmonary PFTs and repeat high‑resolution CT imaging if symptoms change, and monitoring of bone density every 1–3 years. Consider telomere length testing if not already performed and referral to a TBD registry or clinical trial where available.

Multidisciplinary care, genetic counselling and family implications
Because TBDs affect multiple organs and have important reproductive and family implications, multidisciplinary care is essential. Genetic counselling should be offered to the patient and at‑risk relatives; cascade testing for the familial variant and assessment of telomere length (where available) should guide donor selection and reproductive planning (including the option of preimplantation genetic testing). Psychosocial support and coordination with specialty centres that treat inherited marrow failure syndromes and interstitial lung disease improve outcomes and facilitate access to clinical trials.

Conclusion
Management of TBD is complex and individualized: immediate attention to correctable problems and preventive care, organ‑directed therapies (antifibrotics for pulmonary fibrosis, standard cirrhosis care for liver disease, osteoporosis management), cautious use of androgens and growth factors, and timely consideration of HSCT for progressive marrow failure. All decisions—particularly those about disease‑modifying therapies and transplantation—require careful multidisciplinary assessment, donor genetic screening and clear discussion of risks, given the increased peri‑procedural vulnerability of patients with short‑telomere syndromes.
