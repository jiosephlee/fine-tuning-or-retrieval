Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology and Genetics of Telomere Biology Disorders with Emphasis on PARN

Telomeres are repetitive DNA–protein structures that cap chromosome ends and distinguish them from DNA breaks. Each round of DNA replication shortens telomeric DNA because of the end‑replication problem; when telomeres reach a critically short length cells enter senescence or undergo apoptosis. This replicative limit is physiologic and important for tumour suppression, but when telomere shortening is accelerated by inherited defects in telomere maintenance, tissues with high proliferative demand — bone marrow, skin and mucosa, hair follicles, the pulmonary alveolar epithelium and hepatocytes — cannot sustain normal regeneration. The clinical consequences of this premature replicative exhaustion are the hallmark features of telomere biology disorders (TBDs): bone marrow failure, mucocutaneous changes, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis and a predisposition to malignancy.

Telomerase is the principal enzyme that counteracts telomere shortening. It is a ribonucleoprotein complex whose core components are the catalytic telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which provides the template for telomeric repeat addition. A cohort of associated proteins and complexes — shelterin components (TINF2, POT1, ACD), helicases (RTEL1), assembly and processing factors (NAF1, dyskerin/DC1), and nucleases/deadenylases (for example, PARN) — are essential for proper telomerase biogenesis, localization and function. Germline pathogenic variants in any of these genes can impair telomerase activity or telomere protection, producing a spectrum of clinical phenotypes grouped under TBDs. Classical dyskeratosis congenita (DC) was the first disease linked to very short telomeres; since then many genes (DKC1, TERT, TERC, RTEL1, TINF2, POT1, NAF1, ACD, CTC1, USB1 and PARN among others) have been implicated.

PARN (poly(A)‑specific ribonuclease) occupies a distinct mechanistic niche among telomere genes. It encodes a highly conserved 3′→5′ exoribonuclease that shortens poly(A) tails of RNA — a central step in regulated RNA decay and in processing certain noncoding RNAs. Critical to telomere biology, PARN is required for the 3′‑end maturation and stability of TERC. During TERC biogenesis, aberrant oligo‑adenylation can mark nascent transcripts for degradation. PARN removes these post‑transcriptional oligo(A) tails, rescuing TERC from degradation and thereby permitting assembly of a functional telomerase RNP. Loss or reduction of PARN activity therefore reduces steady‑state TERC levels and telomerase activity, producing progressive telomere shortening.

The clinical consequences of PARN defects depend importantly on zygosity and on the degree of functional impairment. Biallelic loss‑of‑function PARN mutations produce severe, early‑onset phenotypes in the DC/ Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson (HH) spectrum — profound bone marrow failure, profound developmental features and multisystem involvement. In contrast, monoallelic (heterozygous) PARN variants are increasingly recognised as contributors to adult‑onset disease manifesting predominantly as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and, variably, bone marrow failure. The patient in this case illustrates that monoallelic PARN variants can present with multisystem manifestations — pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and IPF — even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad. This highlights that genotype–phenotype correlations in TBDs are probabilistic rather than deterministic.

Several mechanisms explain the phenotypic variability seen with the same gene defect. First, haploinsufficiency — where a single functional allele does not produce sufficient protein — is an accepted mechanism for many monoallelic PARN variants. Partial loss of PARN reduces TERC enough to accelerate telomere shortening over decades, so clinical manifestations often appear in young adults rather than in infancy. Second, the specific nature and location of the variant matters: missense changes in critical domains (for example the R3H RNA‑binding domain) can disrupt specific PARN functions and produce variable residual activity. Third, genetic background and modifier genes alter penetrance; variants in other telomere genes (even common polymorphisms), DNA repair genes, or genes affecting RNA metabolism may magnify or mitigate the impact of a PARN lesion.

Non‑genetic factors also shape clinical expression. Environmental and lifestyle stressors that increase cellular turnover or injure vulnerable organs (smoking, recurrent infections, alcohol or hepatotoxic exposures, nutritional deficiencies) accelerate telomere attrition and can unmask or worsen disease. In the presented patient, a severe vitamin B12 deficiency likely imposed additional replicative stress on haematopoietic cells, converting a subclinical telomere maintenance defect into overt cytopenias. Conversely, somatic genetic rescue — the emergence of revertant clones in which a second somatic event restores function — can attenuate disease in some tissues and contribute to mosaic clinical pictures.

An important clinical genetic phenomenon in TBDs is anticipation. Telomere length itself is heritable: if a parent passes on already shortened telomeres, offspring may manifest disease earlier or with greater severity, even if the same pathogenic variant is present. Thus apparent anticipation across generations may reflect inherited telomere shortening rather than change in the primary DNA sequence. This has implications for counselling: a young child of an affected parent may inherit shorter telomeres and be at higher risk despite uncertainty regarding the exact pathogenicity of a detected variant.

From a diagnostic standpoint this biology dictates two complementary approaches. Measurement of telomere length (by flow‑FISH or quantitative PCR) provides functional evidence of telomere shortening; values below the first percentile for age are strongly supportive of a TBD. Genetic testing (targeted panels or exome sequencing) identifies causative variants; interpretation requires attention to zygosity, domain affected and prior evidence linking specific variants to disease. Functional assays — telomerase activity (TRAP assay), TERC RNA quantification, and, less commonly, cellular studies — can clarify the impact of novel variants, such as the monoallelic PARN missense change in this patient.

Therapeutic and counselling implications flow from these mechanisms. Organ systems with high turnover are at ongoing risk; surveillance for progressive bone marrow failure, liver disease and pulmonary fibrosis is essential. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation is a potential cure for marrow failure but requires telomere‑aware planning (reduced‑intensity conditioning regimens and careful donor selection) because of enhanced sensitivity to DNA‑damaging conditioning and extra‑haematopoietic organ vulnerability. Emerging medical strategies (androgens, telomerase‑modulating agents) aim to restore telomerase activity but remain investigational and require long‑term safety data. Genetic counselling must cover variable expressivity, incomplete penetrance, the possibility of de novo variants, and the risk of passing on shorter telomeres to descendants.

In summary, PARN connects RNA metabolism to telomere maintenance: defects reduce TERC stability and telomerase activity, accelerating telomere attrition and producing a spectrum of disease from childhood DC/HH to adult‑onset pulmonary fibrosis and marrow failure. The clinical picture in any individual reflects variant type and zygosity, modifying genetic and environmental factors, somatic events and inherited telomere length — which together explain why a young adult with a monoallelic PARN variant can present with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis even when classical mucocutaneous stigmata are absent. Recognising these mechanistic links is essential for diagnosis, family counselling and tailoring surveillance and treatment.
