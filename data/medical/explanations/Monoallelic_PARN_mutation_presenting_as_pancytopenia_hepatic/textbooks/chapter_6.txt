Chapter 6: ## Prognosis, Surveillance, Counseling, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is heterogeneous and depends primarily on the affected gene, whether mutations are monoallelic or biallelic, the degree of telomere shortening, and which organ systems are involved. Patients with monoallelic PARN variants most commonly present with pulmonary fibrosis or marrow vulnerability rather than the full childhood dyskeratosis congenita phenotype, but hepatic fibrosis, premature hair greying, bone disease and other features may also occur, as exemplified by the case presented here. When pulmonary fibrosis is the dominant manifestation, survival approximates that seen with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)—often measured in years—but outcomes are worse when there is concurrent progressive bone marrow failure or advanced hepatic fibrosis. Conversely, some carriers, even within the same family, remain oligosymptomatic for long periods because penetrance is variable and environmental or secondary stressors (nutritional deficiency, cytotoxic drugs, smoking, viral infections) can precipitate or accelerate organ dysfunction. For an individual patient, therefore, prognosis should be individualized and periodically reassessed as organ-specific disease evolves.

Because target organs (bone marrow, liver, lungs, skin/mucosa, bone) can deteriorate over time, structured surveillance is essential. Initial baseline evaluation should include a complete blood count with reticulocyte count, comprehensive metabolic panel with liver tests, and an assessment of bone marrow function if cytopenias are present. For stable patients without cytopenia, CBC and reticulocyte counts every 3–6 months are reasonable; if cytopenias are evolving or therapy (for example androgens or immunosuppression) is being used, monitoring should be more frequent. Liver evaluation should begin with routine LFTs and an imaging-based assessment; transient elastography provides a noninvasive estimate of fibrosis and may be repeated every 6–12 months when fibrosis is known or suspected, or sooner if clinical or laboratory indicators worsen. Pulmonary surveillance should include baseline pulmonary function tests (PFTs) with spirometry and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), and a high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest at baseline if symptoms or PFT abnormalities exist. Thereafter, PFTs with DLCO are typically repeated every 6–12 months (more frequently if declining), and HRCT should be symptom-triggered or obtained at intervals determined by clinical course and local practice (for example annually to every 2 years in progressive lung disease). Bone health assessment with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) is indicated at baseline and then every 1–2 years if abnormal or if additional risk factors are present. Because mucosal and cutaneous malignancies are relatively more frequent in TBDs, an annual focused oral and skin examination is prudent, with a low threshold for dermatology or ENT referral for suspicious lesions. Finally, any patient receiving immunosuppressives, androgens, or preparing for transplant should have infectious risk assessed, vaccinations updated (influenza, pneumococcus, COVID-19 as indicated), and appropriate monitoring for drug-specific toxicities (for example, LFTs during androgen therapy).

Surveillance and management must be coordinated with multidisciplinary input from hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics and transplant teams. Transplant readiness (hematopoietic or solid organ) requires careful pre‑transplant assessment because patients with short telomeres experience higher rates of post‑transplant complications: poor wound healing, increased mucositis and pulmonary toxicity, prolonged cytopenias and heightened susceptibility to infections. For hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), conditioning regimens should generally be reduced intensity to minimize organ toxicity; myeloablative regimens that include high-dose alkylators or full-dose total body irradiation carry greater risk of transplant-related mortality in TBD patients. Pulmonary and hepatic reserve must be quantified objectively before HSCT, and the presence of significant pulmonary fibrosis or cirrhosis may influence timing, conditioning choice, candidacy, or consideration of combined or sequential organ-specific strategies. For solid organ transplantation (lung or liver), the transplant team should anticipate potential post‑transplant bone marrow failure and infection risk; perioperative planning should include hematology input and long-term surveillance. Wherever transplantation is contemplated, a center experienced in TBDs and complex transplant decisions should be involved early.

Genetic counselling is fundamental: PARN-associated disease exhibits variable inheritance patterns and expressivity. Monoallelic PARN variants can act through haploinsufficiency and present as adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis or marrow vulnerability, while biallelic variants more often produce severe, early-onset syndromes. Counsel patients and families that penetrance is incomplete and that clinical manifestations may worsen or shift between generations (a phenomenon sometimes referred to as genetic anticipation because telomere length can shorten across generations). Offer targeted cascade testing of at‑risk relatives when a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is identified. Telomere length measurement is useful adjunct information: flow-FISH performed in a diagnostic laboratory is the gold-standard telomere assay for clinical decision-making; qPCR-based telomere assays are less reliable for individual patient management and should not be used alone to guide irreversible choices such as donor selection or HSCT. When a clearly pathogenic variant is found, discuss reproductive options including preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic diseases (PGT‑M) and prenatal diagnostic testing; these are appropriate only after thorough counselling regarding variable penetrance and uncertain phenotype prediction.

Donor selection for transplantation requires particular caution. Related donors who are carriers of the familial mutation or who have demonstrably short telomeres should be avoided even if they are clinically asymptomatic, because such donors may have subclinical marrow vulnerability that can compromise donor safety and recipient outcomes. When unrelated donors are used, consider telomere-length testing and careful evaluation of donor health; in HSCT, haploidentical related donation should be approached only after carrier status is excluded. In addition, testing for somatic genetic rescue or mosaicism in potential donors may be informative in special circumstances and should be discussed with the genetics team.

Therapeutic pitfalls and practical cautions: do not base irreversible therapies (for example, proceeding to HSCT or choosing a related donor) solely on a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). A VUS requires further functional or segregation evidence before being used to justify high‑risk interventions. Avoid azathioprine-based regimens for IPF—studies have shown harm with immunosuppressive regimens combining prednisone, azathioprine and N‑acetylcysteine in IPF, and azathioprine in short-telomere patients is associated with additional myelotoxicity. Exercise caution with any myelotoxic drugs, radiation and cytotoxic chemotherapy in patients with short telomeres; lower doses or alternative agents may be necessary. Androgen therapy (danazol, nandrolone) has been shown in small studies to improve cytopenias and sometimes increase telomere length, but androgens carry significant side effects—hepatic toxicity, virilization, lipid changes—and are contraindicated or risky in patients with advanced liver disease such as cirrhosis; their use should be individualized and monitored closely by hematology and hepatology. Finally, counsel patients strongly to avoid tobacco and other exposures known to accelerate telomere attrition (smoking, excessive alcohol), and to maintain up‑to‑date vaccinations.

Practical steps for the patient in this case: confirm the clinical significance of the PARN variant by (1) measuring leukocyte telomere length using flow‑FISH, (2) offering segregation testing to first‑degree relatives and (3) discussing possible functional testing with a specialized genetics laboratory if available. Because she already has documented hepatic fibrosis and radiographic pulmonary fibrosis, ongoing surveillance should include CBC and reticulocyte counts every 3 months initially, LFTs and elastography at least every 6–12 months, PFTs with DLCO every 6–12 months and symptom‑triggered HRCT, and annual bone density and mucocutaneous exams. Given her cirrhosis, androgens are relatively contraindicated; decisions about HSCT should be deferred until multidisciplinary assessment and objective quantification of pulmonary and hepatic reserve are performed at a specialized center. Offer family genetic counselling and discuss reproductive options if a pathogenic classification is established.

In summary, the cardinal messages for clinicians are: TBDs like PARN‑associated disease are multisystem and variably penetrant; prognosis is organ‑specific and dynamic; surveillance must be proactive and multidisciplinary; avoid high‑risk drugs and procedures without full genetic and telomere evaluation; and always confirm pathogenicity before committing to irreversible therapies. High clinical suspicion—young adults with cryptogenic pulmonary fibrosis, unexplained early cirrhosis, unexplained cytopenias, premature greying or mucocutaneous abnormalities—should prompt genetic evaluation and telomere measurement because early recognition changes counselling, monitoring, donor selection and treatment planning.
