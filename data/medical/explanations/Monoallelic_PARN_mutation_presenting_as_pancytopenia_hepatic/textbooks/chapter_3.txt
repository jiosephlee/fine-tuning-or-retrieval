Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis: Distinguishing TBD from Common Mimics

The diagnostic challenge in this case—an adult with pancytopenia, neuropathy, premature greying, hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis—is to separate common, potentially reversible disorders from an uncommon but unifying diagnosis: a telomere biology disorder (TBD). A systematic, hypothesis-driven approach is essential. Start by asking whether the features can be explained by one or more common acquired conditions (nutritional/metabolic deficiency, infection, immune disease, toxin exposure, primary marrow disorders or the usual causes of chronic liver disease) and then determine whether the clinical pattern, response to therapy and targeted investigations point instead toward an inherited syndrome of short telomeres. The remainder of this section contrasts the major alternative diagnoses with features that support or argue against a TBD, and highlights the most discriminating investigations.

Nutritional and metabolic causes are the commonest reversible explanation for ineffective erythropoiesis and neuropathy in young adults. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency produce macrocytic or dimorphic anaemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, low reticulocyte responses, basophilic stippling on the smear at times, glossitis and a sensory‑dominant neuropathy—much of which was seen in this patient. The key clinical test is therapeutic: haematologic indices and neurological symptoms should begin to improve promptly after appropriate repletion (parenteral B12 if malabsorption is suspected). If cytopenias and organ dysfunction persist despite corrected B12/folate and normalisation of metabolic indices (including methylmalonic acid and homocysteine where appropriate), consider an alternative or additional diagnosis. Copper deficiency and secondary copper deficiency from excess zinc can mimic B12 deficiency with cytopenias and a myeloneuropathy; bone marrow in copper deficiency classically shows vacuolated precursors and ring sideroblast‑like features. Checking serum copper and zinc is inexpensive and should be done when neurological signs or marrow abnormalities are disproportionate to nutritional deficits. In the reported patient, although severe B12 deficiency plausibly accounted for many haematologic and neurologic findings, the persistence of multisystem fibrosis and marrow abnormality out of proportion to the documented nutritional lesion argues for an additional, unifying process such as a TBD.

Primary marrow disorders form another large category of mimics. Aplastic anaemia and hypoplastic myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) present with cytopenias and hypocellular marrow or hypoplastic trilineage haematopoiesis. Classic MDS presents later in life with cytopenias, dysplasia and characteristic cytogenetic abnormalities or somatic mutations detected by next‑generation sequencing panels. Distinguishing TBD‑associated marrow failure from idiopathic aplastic anaemia or MDS requires integration of marrow morphology, cytogenetics and molecular data. In TBDs marrow may show cellularity that is variable (from hypoplastic to frankly hypercellular when ineffective erythropoiesis predominates), and dysplasia can be absent. The presence of age‑inappropriate telomere shortening (measured by flow‑FISH) or germline pathogenic variants in telomere‑related genes points to TBD. Conversely, a clonal cytogenetic abnormality or a pathogenic somatic mutation typical of MDS (for example, SF3B1, TET2, DNMT3A) supports a diagnosis of MDS or clonal haematopoiesis; these entities can coexist with or be precipitated by short telomeres, so careful interpretation is required. In the present case the marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia without dysplasia and normal cytogenetics—findings more compatible with ineffective erythropoiesis than classical MDS—which, together with extra‑haematologic fibrosis, increased the pretest probability of a TBD.

Immune‑mediated cytopenias can produce confusing laboratory pictures. Warm autoimmune hemolytic anaemia (AIHA) typically presents with a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), brisk haemolysis (elevated indirect bilirubin and LDH), reticulocytosis and spherocytes on the peripheral smear. Evans syndrome (coexisting immune thrombocytopenia and AIHA) can cause pancytopenia through immune mechanisms. Critical discriminators are the degree of hemolysis and the reticulocyte response: a positive DAT in isolation—particularly when the reticulocyte count is low and marrow erythropoiesis is not suppressed—should prompt consideration of ineffective erythropoiesis, nutrient deficiency or marrow failure rather than pure immune destruction. In our patient the DAT was positive but the reticulocyte response was inadequate and marrow was erythroid‑hyperplastic, supporting ineffective generation rather than peripheral destruction as the dominant process; this argues against isolated AIHA as the explanation for the full clinical picture.

Infectious and inflammatory disorders can involve bone marrow, liver and lung. Chronic viral hepatitis, HIV, active Epstein–Barr virus or cytomegalovirus infection may cause cytopenias and hepatic inflammation; disseminated infections can also seed the marrow. Sarcoidosis or histiocytic disorders can produce non‑caseating granulomas in lung, liver and marrow with constitutional symptoms and cytopenias. Distinguishing features include specific serologies, nucleic acid testing (PCR), characteristic imaging patterns and, when necessary, tissue biopsy demonstrating granulomas or viral cytopathic effects. In the case above, serologies were unrevealing (EBV IgG only, consistent with past exposure) and PET‑CT did not show focal findings suggestive of granulomatous disease, making an infectious/inflammatory explanation less likely for the multisystem fibrosis.

When liver fibrosis is prominent, the usual causes should be exhaustively excluded before invoking a genetic TBD. Alcoholic liver disease, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), autoimmune hepatitis, cholestatic disorders, Wilson disease and hereditary haemochromatosis are the common considerations. Clinical context—alcohol use, metabolic risk factors, autoantibodies, ceruloplasmin/urinary copper studies and iron studies/transferrin saturation—will usually identify one of these etiologies. Young adults with cryptogenic cirrhosis, particularly when accompanied by early pulmonary fibrosis or marrow failure, should prompt testing for TBDs once routine causes have been ruled out. Importantly, telomere disorders can cause hepatic fibrosis even in the absence of classic metabolic risk factors; transient elastography and evidence of portal hypertension in a young person should therefore expand the differential to include inherited telomere defects.

Inherited marrow failure syndromes and segmental progeroid disorders overlap phenotypically with TBD and merit careful consideration. Fanconi anaemia presents in childhood or young adulthood with congenital anomalies, short stature and marrow failure; it is diagnosed by chromosomal breakage testing. Coats plus (CTC1 mutations), Revesz syndrome and Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson syndrome are genetically distinct but phenotypically overlapping entities in which telomere biology is often implicated; they typically present earlier with retinal, neurologic or developmental features. Mitochondrial cytopathies can cause multi‑system disease including cytopenias, neurodegeneration and hepatic involvement. The presence of classic mucocutaneous findings—nail dystrophy, reticulate skin pigmentation or oral leukoplakia—would strongly suggest dyskeratosis congenita, but their absence does not exclude a TBD, particularly in adults where presentation can be organ‑specific. Targeted testing (chromosomal breakage, muscle biopsy, targeted gene panels including DKC1, TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, CTC1, etc.) is guided by the phenotype; in this patient the combination of early‑onset pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis with marrow involvement made telomere testing and sequencing of telomere‑related genes a high‑yield next step.

Drug and toxin exposures account for many cases of acquired cytopenia and marrow suppression. Alkylating agents, azathioprine, chloramphenicol and other myelotoxic drugs cause marrow aplasia; heavy metals such as lead produce basophilic stippling and neuropathy. A careful exposure history (occupational, dietary and medication) is therefore essential. The presence of basophilic stippling in this case raises the possibility of lead or other heavy metal exposure, but without a supportive history or biochemical confirmation (blood lead level), that finding is nonspecific and can also be seen in ineffective erythropoiesis. If exposure is identified, removal of the offending agent should produce haematologic recovery; failure to respond should prompt evaluation for an underlying predisposition such as TBD.

How to integrate these considerations into practice? First, correct reversible and common causes. Treat documented B12 and folate deficiency and recheck counts and clinical status. Simultaneously screen for copper and zinc abnormalities and perform infectious serologies and testing appropriate for the clinical context (HIV, hepatitis panel, CMV/EBV PCR if indicated). If cytopenias, organ dysfunction or imaging abnormalities persist despite correction of reversible insults—or if fibrosis, premature greying, early osteoporosis or unexplained pulmonary fibrosis are present—pursue targeted marrow evaluation (morphology, iron studies, cytogenetics) and consider telomere length measurement (flow‑FISH; leukocyte telomere length below the first percentile for age strongly supports a TBD). If telomere testing is abnormal or clinical suspicion remains high, germline genetic testing with a multigene telomere panel or exome sequencing should be performed, with attention to variants in PARN, TERT, TERC, RTEL1 and other related genes. In parallel, perform somatic mutation profiling to evaluate for MDS or clonal haematopoiesis, because therapeutic decisions (immunosuppression, androgen therapy, or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation) and transplant conditioning differ substantially for TBD versus other marrow disorders.

Finally, the distinction has critical therapeutic and prognostic implications. Immune cytopenias are treated with corticosteroids, IVIG and other immune therapies; classical aplastic anaemia may respond to immunosuppression or require HSCT with standard conditioning. In contrast, patients with TBD are at higher risk of transplant‑related toxicity, and HSCT regimens must be tailored (reduced‑intensity and/or donor selection with genetic counselling). Surveillance for progressive pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis and malignancy is essential in TBD, and family members may need testing because of variable penetrance and autosomal dominant or recessive inheritance patterns. Recognizing when common explanations are insufficient—when a young patient has early fibrosis, age‑inappropriate premature greying or multisystem shortening of replicative tissues—should prompt a directed evaluation for a telomere biology disorder rather than continued attribution to isolated nutritional or acquired disease.
