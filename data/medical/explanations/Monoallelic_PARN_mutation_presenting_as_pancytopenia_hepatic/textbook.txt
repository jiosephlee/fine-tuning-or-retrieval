Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

## Case presentation and early red flags for a telomere biology disorder

This patient’s illness unfolded over approximately five years as a slowly progressive, multisystem syndrome rather than an acute nutritional collapse. Her dominant symptoms were progressive fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance with gradual loss of functional capacity, progressing to dependence for some activities of daily living. Systemic features that developed over this interval included weight loss, anorexia and exertional dyspnoea. The chronicity and insidious decline are important: they point to a sustained defect in tissue maintenance or regenerative capacity rather than a single, fully reversible insult.

On examination she had several findings that extend beyond what would be expected from isolated vitamin deficiency. In addition to pallor and mild icterus, there were stigmata of premature ageing: early greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald, atrophic tongue. Physical signs of multisystem involvement included hepatosplenomegaly, reduced breath sounds bilaterally in the lung bases, thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy. Neurological testing (supported later by electrodiagnostics) revealed a sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. Together, these signs involve tissues with high cellular turnover (hair, mucosa, bone marrow, liver, lung) — a pattern that should raise concern for a disorder of cellular replicative capacity.

The initial laboratory picture combined pancytopenia with a very low reticulocyte response (reticulocyte count 0.39%), and a peripheral smear showing dimorphic erythrocytes with basophilic stippling, Cabot rings and hypersegmented neutrophils. These morphologic features reflect ineffective erythropoiesis and megaloblastic change consistent with severe vitamin B12/folate deficiency, which was biochemically confirmed. Iron studies and marrow evaluation were notable for increased iron stores and erythroid hyperplasia on a background of trilineage haematopoiesis, without dysplasia, infiltration or clonal cytogenetic abnormalities. Markers of haemolysis (elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin) and a positive direct antiglobulin test might suggest immune haemolysis, but the very low reticulocyte count and marrow response argue that ineffective erythropoiesis — not brisk peripheral destruction — is the dominant mechanism. Importantly, despite correction of the documented nutritional deficiencies, many clinical and laboratory abnormalities (multiorgan fibrosis, osteoporosis, neuropathy and persistent cytopenia) remained disproportionate to what would be expected from vitamin deficiency alone.

Putting these findings together yields a compact problem representation: a young adult with progressive, multisystem disease characterized by cytopenia caused by ineffective haematopoiesis, premature-ageing stigmata (early greying, mucosal atrophy, skin pigmentation), and fibrotic disease of high-turnover organs (hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis and interstitial lung fibrosis), plus bone fragility and neuropathy. This constellation is age-incongruent — severe organ fibrosis and marrow dysfunction in the third decade — and suggests a unifying defect affecting cellular longevity or telomere maintenance rather than isolated nutrient deficiency, autoimmune disease or a primary acquired liver/pulmonary condition.

Early red flags in this case that should trigger consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) are therefore: (1) organ fibrosis (pulmonary and hepatic) at a young age, which is atypical for common causes of cirrhosis or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; (2) persistent cytopenias with a blunted reticulocyte response and marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis that are not fully explained by the severity of vitamin B12/folate deficiency and that fail to normalize after correction; (3) premature-ageing physical signs (early greying, mucosal atrophy, skin hyperpigmentation) and multisystem involvement of tissues with high proliferative demand; and (4) coexisting extra-haematopoietic problems (osteoporosis, neuropathy) that suggest systemic stem‑cell exhaustion. These features — especially when several are present together — should prompt early consideration of telomere length assessment and genetic testing for TBD-associated genes, because the diagnosis has implications for surveillance, family counselling and potential future management decisions.

## Structured differential and workup of pancytopenia with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis

When pancytopenia coexists with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult, the diagnostic approach must be systematic and iterative: first exclude common, reversible causes; then use marrow morphology, targeted serologies and imaging to discriminate marrow failure/infiltration from ineffective peripheral processes; and finally consider inherited multisystem disorders such as telomere biology disorders (TBDs) when multisystem fibrosis, premature ageing features and negative routine workup point to a unifying genetic cause. Below is an explanation of how the available data from this case were interpreted to narrow the differential and justify genetic testing.

Differential for pancytopenia
The differential diagnosis of pancytopenia is broad but can be pragmatically divided into decreased marrow production (aplastic anaemia, myelodysplastic neoplasms, inherited bone marrow failure syndromes including TBDs), ineffective haematopoiesis (megaloblastic anaemia, sideroblastic anaemias), peripheral destruction/sequestration (immune cytopenias, hypersplenism), marrow infiltration (leukaemia, lymphoma, metastatic malignancy, granulomatous disease), and infections (parvovirus, HIV, tuberculosis). In this patient the peripheral smear (dimorphic cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils), low reticulocyte count and severely low B12/folate pointed strongly toward ineffective erythropoiesis from megaloblastic change as an important contributor. However, persistence of cytopenia after nutritional correction and concurrent organ fibrosis argued for a coexisting marrow vulnerability rather than isolated nutritional disease.

Interpretation of bone marrow and cytogenetics
Bone marrow examination showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, without dysplasia, infiltration or malignancy; cytogenetics were normal. These findings favor ineffective erythropoiesis (intramedullary destruction of erythroid precursors) over marrow aplasia, where one would expect hypocellularity, or overt myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS) with clear dysplastic features and often cytogenetic abnormalities. Erythroid hyperplasia with a low peripheral reticulocyte count is classic for ineffective erythropoiesis (eg, severe megaloblastic anemia, congenital dyserythropoiesis) rather than peripheral haemolysis. Normal karyotype lowers the probability of a cytogenetically abnormal MDS but does not exclude MDS or other clonal marrow disorders; newer molecular testing (NGS panels) may detect mutations missed on karyotype. In this case, the marrow picture corroborated the laboratory evidence of B12 deficiency as a major driver of anaemia, while not supporting aplastic anaemia or overt infiltration as the cause of pancytopenia.

Hemolysis panel and interpretation of a positive DAT
The haemolysis panel showed elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin, which can be seen both with peripheral haemolysis and with intramedullary (ineffective) haemolysis. A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG (2+) raises the possibility of warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, which typically features reticulocytosis and peripheral spherocytes. Here, the low reticulocyte count and marrow erythroid hyperplasia argue against a brisk compensatory peripheral haemolytic process. DAT positivity can occur in the absence of clinically significant immune haemolysis (eg, low-titre autoantibodies, recent transfusion, chronic inflammatory states), and intramedullary destruction from ineffective erythropoiesis can also raise LDH and indirect bilirubin without a corresponding reticulocyte response. Thus, in this patient the constellation favored intramedullary destruction from megaloblastic change rather than active warm autoimmune haemolysis, although the positive DAT should prompt careful clinical correlation and repeat testing if haemolytic features evolve.

Gastrointestinal evaluation and B12 malabsorption
Upper GI endoscopy was macroscopically normal but duodenal biopsy showed reduced parietal cells. Loss of parietal cells produces intrinsic factor deficiency and phenocopies pernicious anaemia physiology, causing B12 malabsorption and severe megaloblastic changes. Demonstration of parietal cell loss helps explain the profound B12 deficiency even in the absence of overt endoscopic mucosal lesions. Nevertheless, although B12 deficiency accounted for several haematologic and neurologic findings, the severity of multisystem fibrosis and premature ageing in a patient of this age suggested that B12 deficiency was not the sole explanation.

Hepatic assessment and causes of fibrosis in young adults
Imaging and transient elastography documented hepatosplenomegaly, steatosis and a high liver stiffness consistent with cirrhosis. In young adults, causes of hepatic fibrosis include viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, alcoholic or metabolic (NASH) liver disease, genetic/metabolic conditions (Wilson disease, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, haemochromatosis), vascular causes and less commonly systemic or genetic fibrotic syndromes (including telomere disorders). Negative viral and autoimmune serologies made the common acquired causes less likely in this case. The combination of cirrhosis with marrow abnormalities and pulmonary fibrosis should raise suspicion for multisystem inherited disorders; TBDs are now recognised to cause hepatic fibrosis and even cirrhosis, so the unexplained liver disease here supported the search for a unifying genetic diagnosis. Elevated ferritin and iron stores in marrow can reflect ineffective erythropoiesis and iron loading, not necessarily hereditary haemochromatosis, and must be interpreted in that context.

Pulmonary findings and differential of interstitial lung disease (ILD)
High-resolution imaging demonstrated diffuse ground-glass change and subpleural fibrotic/atelectatic changes bilaterally. In adults, a subpleural-predominant fibrosis pattern suggests the usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) spectrum, classically seen with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) but also with connective tissue diseases and telomeropathy-associated fibrosis. In younger patients, alternative considerations include connective tissue disease–associated ILD, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, drug/toxin exposures, infection and genetic causes such as telomere biology disorders. The combination of early fibrotic changes in the lungs plus hepatic cirrhosis and marrow dysfunction is characteristic of telomeropathies, where pulmonary fibrosis may present at younger ages than idiopathic IPF. In this case, radiology did not show findings suggestive of active infection or malignancy, making a fibrotic ILD in the telomere-disease spectrum a leading consideration.

Role and limitations of PET‑CT in systemic cytopenia
PET‑CT was used here principally to search for occult malignancy or inflammatory foci and demonstrated inhomogeneous bone‑marrow FDG uptake without focal malignant lesions. PET can detect marrow hyperactivity (reactive or neoplastic), occult malignancy and inflammatory processes, but it is not specific: increased marrow uptake can reflect reactive erythropoiesis, infection, inflammation or haematologic malignancy. Likewise, PET is insensitive for many marrow disorders (eg, MDS without focal tumours). Thus, an abnormal PET should be interpreted alongside marrow morphology, cytogenetics and molecular studies. In this patient, heterogeneous marrow uptake correlated with marrow hyperactivity from ineffective erythropoiesis and with systemic inflammation rather than focal malignancy.

Ancillary abnormalities: bone mineral disease, electrolytes and neuropathy
The patient had vitamin D deficiency, secondary hyperparathyroidism, hypocalcaemia, osteoporosis and axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy on EMG/NCS. Severe B12 deficiency readily explains glossitis, hypersegmented neutrophils, and subacute neuropathy with both sensory and motor involvement; improvement with B12 replacement is expected and supports a nutritional cause for the neuropathy. Osteoporosis in a young adult is atypical and may reflect multiple contributors here—vitamin D deficiency, chronic inflammation, malnutrition, and possibly accelerated senescence related to an underlying telomere disorder. While TBDs have been associated with premature osteoporosis, the electrolyte and vitamin abnormalities should also be corrected and monitored. Overall, some neurologic and skeletal findings are attributable to documented nutritional deficits, but their severity and occurrence alongside organ fibrosis reinforce the broader syndromic consideration.

Synthesis toward an inherited marrow‑fibrosis syndrome
The diagnostic reasoning proceeds from attribution of the haematologic abnormalities to severe B12 deficiency (supported by marrow erythroid hyperplasia and classic smear findings) while recognizing discordant red flags: young age for cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying and osteoporosis, persistent cytopenias disproportionate to corrected nutritional status, and lack of alternative causes on serology and imaging. Normal marrow cellularity without dysplasia and normal karyotype make aplastic anaemia and overt MDS less likely; negative infectious and autoimmune testing argue against those secondary causes. The constellation of multisystem fibrosis, premature ageing features and marrow vulnerability is characteristic of telomere biology disorders, justifying genetic evaluation. In this patient, clinical exome sequencing identifying a monoallelic PARN variant provided a plausible unifying explanation consistent with the working diagnosis of a TBD, even though functional confirmation (telomere length testing) and long-term follow-up are important to define penetrance and progression.

Clinical lessons
A structured approach—exclude common reversible causes, interpret marrow findings in the context of reticulocyte response and peripheral smear, use imaging to characterise organ involvement, and reassess when findings remain discordant—facilitates timely recognition of rare inherited syndromes. In young adults with unexplained hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis accompanying cytopenia (especially when associated with premature ageing features), early consideration of telomere disorders is warranted because it changes counselling, surveillance and management decisions (including transplant planning).

## Telomere biology disorders and PARN: focused mechanisms and phenotype correlations

Telomeres are repetitive DNA–protein structures that cap chromosome ends and protect them from degradation and end‑to‑end fusion. With each cell division telomeres shorten; when a critically short length is reached, cells undergo senescence or apoptosis. Telomerase is the principal counter‑measure to replication‑associated telomere loss in highly proliferative tissues. The telomerase ribonucleoprotein complex contains the catalytic reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which provides the template for telomere repeat synthesis; accessory proteins and the shelterin complex regulate its recruitment and activity. Disruption of any essential component of this machinery reduces telomerase activity, accelerates telomere attrition and predisposes tissues with high replicative demand to dysfunction.

PARN (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease) is an exoribonuclease whose canonical role is removal of poly(A) tails from mRNAs, but in the context of telomere biology it has a more specific and critical function. PARN is required for proper 3′‑end maturation of TERC: it removes aberrant oligo(A) tails on nascent TERC and thereby prevents its degradation by the RNA surveillance machinery. Loss or reduction of PARN activity leads to decreased levels of mature TERC, destabilization of the telomerase RNP and consequent reduction in telomerase enzymatic function. Functionally, reduced PARN activity therefore phenocopies defects in TERC or TERT by causing faster telomere shortening in stem and progenitor cells.

Reduced telomerase activity and accelerated telomere shortening produce two proximate cellular consequences relevant to disease: stem/progenitor cell exhaustion (impaired regenerative capacity) and entry of stressed cells into senescence, with attendant pro‑inflammatory and pro‑fibrotic signalling. In the bone marrow this results in failing hematopoiesis and a spectrum of cytopenias; in the lung and liver it predisposes to fibrosis through maladaptive repair and chronic injury responses. Thus a single mechanistic lesion (impaired TERC maturation from deficient PARN activity) explains the combination of marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis seen in telomere biology disorders (TBDs).

Genotype–phenotype correlations in PARN-related disease follow a general pattern but with notable variability. Biallelic (recessive) loss‑of‑function PARN variants are associated with severe, early‑onset TBDs such as Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson–type phenotypes and autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), often with pronounced mucocutaneous features, growth failure and profound bone marrow failure in childhood. By contrast, monoallelic (heterozygous) PARN variants more commonly present later in life and predispose to organ‑limited adult phenotypes—most classically idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and variable bone marrow failure (often grouped as telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure). However, these distinctions are not absolute: monoallelic variants can be associated with multi‑organ involvement, and penetrance is incomplete.

Organ tropism in TBDs reflects differences in the proliferative demands and regenerative pathways of tissues. Hematopoietic stem cells are highly susceptible, so cytopenias and progressive marrow failure are common and are an early clinical clue. The lung—particularly the alveolar epithelium—also requires robust repair capacity; shortened telomeres predispose to alveolar epithelial cell senescence and fibrosis, producing imaging features such as diffuse ground‑glass change and subpleural reticulation or honeycombing, as seen on this patient’s CT. The liver is similarly vulnerable to cumulative injury; impaired hepatocyte regeneration can manifest as steatosis progressing to fibrosis and cirrhosis, consistent with this patient’s elevated liver stiffness on transient elastography. Mucocutaneous findings (premature greying, nail changes, oral papillary atrophy) and skeletal effects (osteoporosis, fractures) reflect involvement of other replicating compartments and are commonly reported in TBDs; their presence in this case strengthens the link between the clinical phenotype and an underlying telomere defect.

Clinical expression is heavily modified by additional genetic, environmental and physiologic stressors. Heterozygous defects may remain subclinical until an added insult unmasks a vulnerability. In this patient, severe vitamin B12 deficiency caused marked ineffective erythropoiesis and may have precipitated clinically apparent cytopenias on a background of compromised marrow reserve from shortened telomeres. Other recognized modifiers include smoking, chronic inflammation, hepatotoxic exposures, and co‑occurring variants in other telomere genes; somatic genetic rescue and variable telomere attrition rates also contribute to intrafamilial and interindividual variability. These modifier effects explain why monoallelic PARN carriers can present with diverse organ involvement and why classical mucocutaneous stigmata may be absent.

Laboratory and imaging signatures expected in PARN‑related TBD align with this case. Definitive evidence of telomere dysfunction is provided by markedly short telomere length in leukocyte subsets (flow‑FISH is the gold standard; qPCR–based assays are less specific), and genetic sequencing of telomere‑related genes can identify causative variants—both approaches are complementary. Hematologic testing commonly shows cytopenias with low reticulocyte responses when marrow reserve is limited; bone marrow examination may reveal hypocellularity or, as here, erythroid hyperplasia in the setting of ineffective erythropoiesis without malignant dysplasia. Pulmonary high‑resolution CT classically demonstrates subpleural fibrosis and reticulation or ground‑glass change; liver assessment by elastography can detect increased stiffness consistent with fibrosis or cirrhosis. The combination of these findings in a young adult is characteristic of TBD and should prompt targeted telomere length testing and genetic evaluation.

In this patient, a monoallelic PARN missense change affecting a conserved residue in the R3H domain was identified and classified as a variant of uncertain significance, yet the clinical constellation—premature greying, multisystem fibrosis, pancytopenia with poor marrow reserve and osteoporosis—fits the expanding phenotype associated with PARN‑related telomere disease. Practically, this mechanistic understanding guides management: monitor blood counts, pulmonary and hepatic function closely; avoid marrow‑toxic agents when possible; consider early referral for hematopoietic stem cell transplant in progressive marrow failure (with telomere‑specific conditioning considerations); and offer genetic counselling and family evaluation given variable penetrance and potential implications for relatives.

## Diagnostic synthesis and genetic variant interpretation in suspected TBD

When faced with a young adult who has otherwise unexplained pancytopenia, features of premature ageing (eg, early greying, osteopenia/osteoporosis) and fibrotic disease of at least two organs (in this case lung and liver), it is important to move beyond treating individual abnormalities and ask whether a single disease process can explain the pattern. Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) classically target tissues with high replicative demand—haematopoietic cells, mucocutaneous surfaces, lung and liver—and can present heterogeneously in adulthood without the full mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. In this case the key unifying clues were persistent cytopenias with ineffective erythropoiesis that did not fully resolve after correcting nutritional deficits, early ageing phenotypes (premature greying, osteoporosis), and imaging evidence of both pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis in a young patient. Taken together these features raise the pre-test probability for an inherited telomere maintenance defect and justify targeted genomic and telomere-length evaluation.

Indications to pursue genetic testing and direct telomere measurement are therefore pragmatic and phenotype-driven. Genetic sequencing (targeted gene panels, exome or genome sequencing) is indicated when a patient has multilineage marrow dysfunction unexplained by acquired causes, fibrotic lung disease or hepatic fibrosis occurring at an age younger than expected, or a combination of these findings especially with other premature-ageing stigmata or a suggestive family history. Simultaneous or sequential measurement of telomere length by flow-FISH (flow cytometry fluorescence in situ hybridisation) is often helpful: markedly short telomeres for age provide strong biological corroboration of a TBD and can strengthen interpretation of uncertain variants. Flow-FISH on leukocyte subsets is considered the laboratory gold standard for telomere-length assessment in clinical TBD evaluation and should be requested when available.

The exome in this patient found a heterozygous missense change in PARN: c.613T>C, p.Cys205Arg, localized to the R3H domain (amino acids ~178–245). R3H is a conserved nucleic-acid-binding motif, and the substituted cysteine is evolutionarily conserved across species, arguing that the residue is functionally important. Multiple in silico tools predicted a deleterious effect on protein function. Those features—conservation, location in a critical domain and damaging computational predictions—represent supportive evidence that the variant could impair PARN function and thus plausibly contribute to telomere dysfunction.

Interpreting a novel missense variant requires structured application of ACMG/AMP criteria. In this case several moderate/supporting criteria are met: PM1 (location in a mutational hot-spot or critical domain without benign variation—here the R3H domain), PM2 (absent or extremely rare in population databases), PP3 (multiple computational predictions of deleterious effect) and PP2 (gene constrained for benign missense variation and missense a known mechanism of disease). However, these do not reach the level of “likely pathogenic” or “pathogenic” because stronger evidence is lacking: there is no well-documented segregation with disease in multiple affected family members, no validated functional assay demonstrating pathogenic impact for this specific change, no de novo confirmation, and no prior reports definitively linking this exact variant to disease. Absent one or more of the higher-weight criteria (for example PS3 functional evidence, PS4 strong case-control evidence, or PS2 confirmed de novo occurrence), the appropriate classification remains Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). That designation reflects both the possibility of pathogenicity and the level of uncertainty that prevents definitive attribution of disease causation.

A VUS does not end the diagnostic process; rather it directs the next steps for clarifying clinical relevance. Practical measures include (1) targeted telomere-length testing (flow-FISH) to see whether the patient has telomeres markedly below the expected range for age—this biological evidence can strongly support that a PARN variant is functionally relevant; (2) segregation studies in parents and available relatives—demonstration that the variant arose de novo (with parental samples negative) would add strong evidence for pathogenicity, while co-segregation with disease in multiple affected relatives would also support pathogenic classification; (3) functional assays—laboratory studies that assess PARN protein stability, deadenylation activity, effects on TERC maturation or telomerase activity can provide PS3-level evidence if well validated; and (4) literature and database surveillance with periodic reclassification as new cases or experimental data emerge. If functional or segregation data are unobtainable, measurement of telomere length and careful longitudinal phenotyping can still allow clinicians to act on the clinical syndrome even while the variant remains a VUS.

Clinically, it is appropriate—and often necessary—to manage the patient as having a TBD when the phenotype, telomere length and gene implicated are concordant, even if the specific variant is a VUS. Management and surveillance decisions (regular pulmonary and hepatic monitoring, haematology follow-up, avoidance of cytotoxic agents or radiation when possible, vaccination and infection prevention, bone health management, and early involvement of transplant teams if progressive marrow failure occurs) should be driven by the clinical risk and not solely by genetic certainty. Important practical implications of presumed TBD include caution with potential stem cell donors: related donors should be genetically and/or telomere-length tested before use because unrecognised carrier status can lead to donor-derived complications. Discussions about fertility preservation, reproductive options (including preimplantation genetic testing only if a variant is classified pathogenic/likely pathogenic), and the risks of cytotoxic therapies should be part of care planning.

Counselling around a VUS requires transparency about uncertainty. Explain that the variant may or may not be disease-causing and that further studies (family testing, telomere measurement, functional work-up) may clarify its significance. Discuss variable penetrance and expressivity typical of TBD genes—carriers may remain asymptomatic into later life or develop only organ-specific manifestations. Also mention the possibilities of de novo events and somatic genetic rescue or mosaicism: a pathogenic germline change may be absent in one parent because it arose de novo, or mosaicism in blood can complicate detection and telomere measurements. For cascade testing, avoid predictive genetic testing of asymptomatic relatives based solely on a VUS; instead, recommend targeted testing in the context of clinical features or after reclassification. Finally, document plans to re-evaluate the variant as new evidence accrues and to re-contact the family when classification changes.

In summary, bringing diverse findings together into a unifying TBD diagnosis depends on recognizing the pattern of premature ageing plus organ fibrosis and marrow dysfunction; confirming short telomeres with flow-FISH; and using genomic data in combination with functional, familial and phenotypic evidence. A monoallelic PARN p.Cys205Arg variant that meets PM1/PM2/PP2/PP3 remains a VUS until stronger segregation or functional data are available, but clinicians may reasonably manage the patient as having a TBD when phenotype and ancillary testing (especially short telomeres) point in the same direction, with careful counselling, surveillance and family evaluation undertaken in parallel.

## Management, surveillance, and prognosis in PARN-related telomere biology disorders

Immediate and supportive care
Management begins by addressing reversible contributors to the presenting problems while recognizing the underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD) that may predispose to progressive, multisystem disease. In this patient the initial, effective measures were correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency with parenteral cyanocobalamin (1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, then monthly maintenance), oral folic acid, transfusion support for symptomatic anaemia, and correction of electrolytes and vitamin D/calcium repletion for osteoporosis. These measures are appropriate first steps because nutritional deficiency accounted for much of the ineffective erythropoiesis and some symptoms; they should be continued and monitored. Supportive interventions such as physiotherapy and bone health optimization (calcium, vitamin D, antiresorptive therapy when indicated) improve function and reduce fracture risk and are important adjuncts.

Hematologic follow‑up and indications for escalation
Patients with a TBD require longitudinal haematologic surveillance because bone marrow failure can evolve over months to years. After initial correction of reversible causes, repeat CBCs with reticulocyte counts should be performed frequently (for example every 4–12 weeks initially, then every 3 months once stable). Worsening cytopenias, a falling reticulocyte count despite correction of nutritional deficits, new or progressive transfusion dependence, emergence of dysplastic features on peripheral smear, increasing marrow hypocellularity on prior exam, or new cytogenetic abnormalities are all indications to repeat bone marrow examination and consider referral to a tertiary haematology centre.

Early referral to a transplant centre is recommended when there is progressive marrow failure (severe aplastic anaemia, transfusion dependence, or high-grade cytopenias refractory to supportive care). Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) may be curative for the marrow component but requires special considerations in TBDs: standard myeloablative conditioning causes excessive toxicity in patients with short telomeres. Reduced‑intensity conditioning (RIC) regimens tailored to minimize pulmonary and hepatic toxicity are therefore preferred, and transplant teams experienced in inherited bone marrow failure syndromes should be involved. Donor selection must exclude related donors who may carry the same pathogenic variant; genetic testing of potential related donors is mandatory. Patients with significant pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis have higher transplant-related morbidity and mortality, so timing of referral—before end‑organ deterioration—can be critical.

Pulmonary management and transplant considerations
Pulmonary involvement in PARN-related disease commonly manifests as pulmonary fibrosis or an idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) phenotype. Baseline assessment should include high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) with spirometry and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO). Serial monitoring is guided by symptoms and baseline severity: in patients with evidence of ILD or symptoms, PFTs every 3–6 months and HRCT every 6–12 months (or sooner if decline is suspected) are reasonable; in stable, asymptomatic patients intervals may be extended to 6–12 months.

Antifibrotic therapy used for IPF (nintedanib or pirfenidone) can be considered for progressive fibrotic disease, recognizing that evidence specific to TBDs is limited and the benefit is extrapolated from IPF trials. Pulmonary transplant is an option for end‑stage disease, but outcomes in short‑telomere syndromes are more complicated: patients are at risk of bone marrow dysfunction, delayed wound healing, and other organ complications after transplant. Pre‑transplant identification of a telomere disorder alters candidacy assessment and perioperative planning. Multidisciplinary discussion between pulmonology, transplant surgery, haematology and hepatology is required.

Hepatic management
Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are increasingly recognized in TBDs. Baseline liver assessment should include noninvasive fibrosis staging (transient elastography), liver biochemistry, and imaging. Serial elastography and clinical review every 6–12 months are reasonable to detect progression; more frequent follow-up is required for decompensation. Screen for portal hypertension with platelet counts, abdominal ultrasound, and consider endoscopic variceal screening when clinical indicators arise (thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly, signs of portal hypertension). Avoidance of hepatotoxins (anabolic-androgenic steroids unless risks are justified and closely monitored, unnecessary medications with known hepatic injury) and counselling about alcohol abstinence are essential. In patients who develop decompensated cirrhosis, evaluation for liver transplantation should be performed, with recognition that coexisting pulmonary or haematologic disease may complicate candidacy and outcomes.

Potential disease‑modifying therapies
Androgens (danazol, oxymetholone, nandrolone) have shown haematologic responses and, in some reports, telomere length stabilization in patients with TBDs and progressive cytopenias. Indications typically include symptomatic or progressive cytopenias when HSCT is not immediately undertaken. Use requires careful patient selection and monitoring: baseline and serial liver function tests, lipid profile, coagulation studies, and surveillance for virilisation and thromboembolic events. Importantly, androgens can be hepatotoxic and should be used cautiously or avoided in patients with advanced hepatic fibrosis or active liver disease; multidisciplinary input is necessary.

Other investigational approaches to ameliorate telomere dysfunction are under study (telomerase activators, gene‑based therapies), but these remain experimental and not standard of care. Enrollment in clinical trials at specialised centres should be considered when available.

Risk mitigation and infection prevention
Because individuals with TBDs are vulnerable to organ toxicity and marrow suppression, avoid myelotoxic medications and unnecessary ionizing radiation when possible. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy plans should be modified in consultation with specialists. Smoking cessation and avoidance of environmental lung toxins are imperative to slow ILD progression. Vaccination against influenza, pneumococcus and other relevant pathogens is recommended; consider prophylactic antimicrobials for severe, chronic neutropenia on a case‑by‑case basis. Agents such as granulocyte‑colony stimulating factor (G‑CSF) may be used transiently for severe neutropenia, but their role is supportive rather than disease‑modifying.

Genetic counselling, family testing and reproductive options
Identification of a monoallelic PARN variant warrants genetic counselling for the patient and at‑risk relatives. Monoallelic variants behave with variable penetrance and may present as pulmonary fibrosis, marrow failure or a mixed phenotype; counselling should address uncertainty in penetrance and prognosis. Cascade testing of family members — and exclusion of related donors for HSCT — is important. Reproductive options include prenatal testing and preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is established; in the case of a variant of uncertain significance, counselling should emphasize the limits of prediction. Psychosocial support and linkage to patient support groups or specialty clinics (telomere disorders/bone marrow failure registries) can help with coping and long‑term care planning.

Prognosis and application to this patient
Prognosis in PARN‑related TBDs is highly variable and driven by the degree and progression of marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic involvement. Some patients have indolent disease for years; others progress to life‑threatening organ failure. The patient described in this report showed symptomatic and haematologic improvement after correction of vitamin B12 and other supportive measures, which confirms that reversible factors contributed substantially to her presentation. Nevertheless, the presence of pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis and a monoallelic PARN variant confer an ongoing risk of progressive organ failure, marrow failure, and malignancy.

For practical follow‑up in this patient, a reasonable surveillance plan would include regular CBCs with reticulocyte counts every 3 months (more frequently if unstable), PFTs with DLCO every 3–6 months and chest imaging (HRCT) at least annually or sooner with decline, transient elastography and liver biochemical monitoring every 6–12 months, bone density follow‑up per osteoporosis guidelines, and age‑appropriate cancer surveillance. Early discussion with an HSCT centre is prudent so that escalating marrow failure can be managed expeditiously with appropriately tailored conditioning and donor selection. Measurement of telomere length (flow‑FISH) may be useful as a baseline prognostic marker and for family assessment.

In summary, management of PARN-related TBD combines treatment of reversible causes and vigilant, organ‑directed surveillance with early multidisciplinary involvement to time disease‑modifying interventions appropriately. The patient’s early improvement is encouraging, but lifelong, proactive monitoring and counselling remain central to her care.
