# Case-Based Textbook Chapter: Monoallelic_PARN_mutation_presenting_as_pancytopenia_hepatic

## Clinical Approach to a Young Adult with Multisystem Premature‑Ageing Features and Cytopenias

This case illustrates a critical diagnostic challenge: a young adult with progressive fatigue, pancytopenia and multisystem findings that mimic acquired nutritional, immune or inflammatory disease but ultimately reflect an underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD). The clinical approach has two parallel and equally important goals. First, identify and promptly treat reversible and common causes of cytopenia and end‑organ dysfunction (nutritional deficiencies, infection, autoimmune disease). Second, maintain a high index of suspicion for an inherited telomere syndrome when the clinical picture is disproportionate, multisystemic, or unexplained after initial therapy — because early recognition changes surveillance, family counselling and definitive management decisions such as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).

Recognize red flags that should prompt consideration of TBDs
- Age and phenotype mismatch: significant marrow dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, liver fibrosis or premature osteoporosis in a young adult is unlike typical degenerative disease of older age. Premature greying, nail changes, oral leukoplakia or subtle skin hyperpigmentation are helpful when present but are frequently absent in adults.
- Multiorgan fibrosis: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or cryptogenic hepatic fibrosis, especially when disproportionate to common exposures, is a cardinal clue. Concurrent involvement of two or more high‑turnover tissues (bone marrow, lung, liver, skin, mucosa, hair) increases the pretest probability.
- Hematologic pattern: unexplained cytopenias with a low reticulocyte index, dimorphic anaemia or marrow erythroid hyperplasia suggest ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral immune destruction. A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) does not exclude a marrow problem — interpret haemolysis markers (LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin) and reticulocyte response in context.
- Family history: young relatives with early lung, liver disease or marrow failure may be present, but incomplete penetrance or de novo variants is common, so absence of family history does not rule out genetic disease.

Initial bedside and laboratory evaluation: parallel diagnostic and therapeutic planning
On presentation, pursue a concise but broad workup while simultaneously treating reversible contributors. For example, in this case the severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency required immediate correction because it accounted for much of the ineffective erythropoiesis and clinical pallor. At the same time, the clinician should continue evaluating for an inherited syndrome if features persist or are disproportionate to the deficiency.

Immediate/initial tests and actions:
- CBC with differential and reticulocyte count, peripheral smear (look for dimorphism, basophilic stippling, hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings).
- Haemolysis panel (LDH, indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin), DAT to assess immune haemolysis — but interpret with marrow data; a low reticulocyte count despite signs of haemolysis argues against a purely peripheral process.
- Nutritional studies (B12, folate, iron studies), thyroid function, vitamin D, calcium and PTH.
- Viral serologies and basic autoimmune testing to exclude treatable causes of fibrosis or cytopenia.
- Prompt replacement of deficiencies (for B12, parenteral therapy when severe) and supportive transfusion as indicated.
- Bone marrow examination early in the course if cytopenias persist or there is concern for marrow failure, MDS, or infiltrative disease. Expect trilineage hypoplasia in classic TBDs, but findings can be non‑specific; erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective erythropoiesis is common when nutritional deficiencies coexist.

Interpreting marrow and haemolysis findings in context
TBDs often present with marrow dysfunction that may be unmasked or worsened by external stresses (nutritional deficiency, infection, cytotoxic drugs). A low reticulocyte count despite anemia and marrow erythroid hyperplasia favors ineffective erythropoiesis — the marrow makes red cell precursors but they fail to mature or survive. This explains why a patient can have marrow erythroid activity and biochemical signs suggesting hemolysis (elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin) while still mounting a poor reticulocyte response. In such situations, immunosuppressive therapy for autoimmune haemolysis is rarely helpful and might be harmful; instead, correcting reversible causes and further assessing marrow function is appropriate.

When to suspect and test for a telomere biology disorder
Suspect TBD when:
- Cytopenias (single-lineage or pancytopenia) occur in young adults without an alternative explanation or are persistent after correction of reversible causes.
- There is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic liver fibrosis or early-onset osteoporosis, especially when combined with subtle stigmata of premature ageing.
- Family history is suggestive, or there is a constellation of multisystem disease affecting high‑turnover tissues.

Recommended confirmatory testing:
- Telomere length testing by flow‑FISH (preferred, age‑adjusted) or qPCR. Telomere lengths below age‑adjusted percentiles (eg, <1st–10th percentile depending on lab) strongly support TBD.
- Multigene panel or whole‑exome sequencing targeting telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1 and others). Interpret variants in the clinical context; monoallelic variants can be disease‑causing with variable penetrance. Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) are common — additional evidence (telomere length, segregation studies, functional assays) can help clarify their significance.

Concurrent organ evaluation: screen broadly at presentation
Because TBDs are multisystem, initial assessment should extend beyond hematology:
- Pulmonary: high‑resolution CT chest and pulmonary function tests including diffusing capacity (DLCO). Even subclinical fibrosis may be present.
- Hepatic: liver enzymes, synthetic function (albumin, INR), abdominal ultrasound and noninvasive fibrosis assessment (transient elastography). TB-driven hepatic fibrosis can be disproportionate to classic causes.
- Skeletal/endocrine: bone mineral density (DEXA), vitamin D and PTH levels, electrolyte evaluation; osteoporosis is common.
- Neurologic: if neuropathy is suspected, nerve conduction studies and electromyography can delineate axonal polyneuropathy.
- Cancer surveillance: TBDs increase the risk of certain malignancies; maintain vigilance and appropriate age‑appropriate screening.

Management principles while pursuing a genetic diagnosis
- Treat reversible contributors promptly: nutritional repletion, correction of electrolytes, vaccination, and supportive transfusions as needed.
- Avoid unnecessary or potentially harmful therapies until the diagnosis is clear. For example, myeloablative chemotherapy or standard conditioning regimens for HSCT carry increased toxicity in TBD patients; if HSCT is considered, early genetic diagnosis influences donor selection (test related donors) and conditioning choices (reduced‑intensity regimens are preferred).
- Avoid exposures that accelerate telomere attrition and organ injury: tobacco cessation, limit alcohol (especially with hepatic fibrosis), and avoid cytotoxic agents where possible.
- Consider specialist referrals early: coordinated care with hematology, pulmonology, hepatology and clinical genetics optimizes surveillance and timing of definitive therapies.

Genetic counselling, family testing and interpretation of variants
A genetic diagnosis has immediate implications: cascade testing of family members can reveal presymptomatic carriers; this affects donor suitability for HSCT and informs surveillance. When sequencing returns a VUS (as in the presented PARN missense), integrate additional data: telomere length assays, in silico predictions, literature on similar variants, and segregation analysis in family members. Functional assays (where available) and longitudinal clinical follow‑up may be necessary to resolve uncertain results. Counsel patients that monoallelic variants can have variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance; not all carriers will have identical phenotypes.

Long‑term surveillance and therapy options
- Hematologic: periodic CBC and bone marrow evaluation if counts decline; consider early HSCT for progressive marrow failure, but plan conditioning and donor selection carefully in TBDs.
- Pulmonary: regular PFTs and HRCT to detect progressive fibrosis. Antifibrotic therapies (eg, pirfenidone, nintedanib) may be considered in IPF, and transplant assessment is necessary for progressive disease.
- Hepatic: monitor for progression of fibrosis, portal hypertension and hepatic synthetic dysfunction; referral to hepatology for consideration of transplantation when indicated.
- Supportive care: manage osteoporosis, neuropathy, nutritional deficits, and psychosocial issues.
- Experimental and disease‑modifying therapies: androgens (danazol, nandrolone) have shown telomere‑lengthening effects in small studies but long‑term benefit/risks are uncertain; enrolment in clinical trials or specialist centers is advised where available.

Avoid premature diagnostic closure
A pragmatic but cautious approach in young adults with multisystem premature‑ageing signs is essential. Correct clear, reversible causes first, but if the overall clinical burden is disproportionate, persistently abnormal or involves multiple telomere‑sensitive organs, broaden the differential to include TBDs even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad. Early genetic and telomere testing allows proactive surveillance, targeted counselling and better planning for potentially curative but high‑risk therapies such as HSCT.

Practical checklist to apply at the bedside
- Treat emergent/reversible problems immediately (eg, parenteral B12 for severe deficiency).
- Order baseline studies: CBC + reticulocyte, smear, haemolysis panel, marrow exam if unexplained cytopenia; B12/folate/iron; viral and autoimmune tests.
- Screen organs: HRCT chest and PFTs, liver imaging and elastography, DEXA, endocrine panel as indicated.
- If clinical pattern suggests TBD, obtain telomere length testing and refer for genetic testing and counselling.
- Coordinate multidisciplinary follow‑up and discuss implications for family screening and future therapies.

In summary, this patient’s course illustrates the diagnostic interplay: prompt correction of B12 deficiency produced symptomatic improvement, yet the persistent, multisystem features and young age rightly prompted testing for a telomere disorder. Recognizing the red flags and maintaining parallel diagnostic and therapeutic tracks — treat what you can now, while investigating what explains the rest — is the cornerstone of caring for young adults with premature‑ageing features and cytopenias.

## Pathophysiology and Genetics of Telomere Biology Disorders with Emphasis on PARN

Telomeres are repetitive DNA–protein structures that cap chromosome ends and distinguish them from DNA breaks. Each round of DNA replication shortens telomeric DNA because of the end‑replication problem; when telomeres reach a critically short length cells enter senescence or undergo apoptosis. This replicative limit is physiologic and important for tumour suppression, but when telomere shortening is accelerated by inherited defects in telomere maintenance, tissues with high proliferative demand — bone marrow, skin and mucosa, hair follicles, the pulmonary alveolar epithelium and hepatocytes — cannot sustain normal regeneration. The clinical consequences of this premature replicative exhaustion are the hallmark features of telomere biology disorders (TBDs): bone marrow failure, mucocutaneous changes, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis and a predisposition to malignancy.

Telomerase is the principal enzyme that counteracts telomere shortening. It is a ribonucleoprotein complex whose core components are the catalytic telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which provides the template for telomeric repeat addition. A cohort of associated proteins and complexes — shelterin components (TINF2, POT1, ACD), helicases (RTEL1), assembly and processing factors (NAF1, dyskerin/DC1), and nucleases/deadenylases (for example, PARN) — are essential for proper telomerase biogenesis, localization and function. Germline pathogenic variants in any of these genes can impair telomerase activity or telomere protection, producing a spectrum of clinical phenotypes grouped under TBDs. Classical dyskeratosis congenita (DC) was the first disease linked to very short telomeres; since then many genes (DKC1, TERT, TERC, RTEL1, TINF2, POT1, NAF1, ACD, CTC1, USB1 and PARN among others) have been implicated.

PARN (poly(A)‑specific ribonuclease) occupies a distinct mechanistic niche among telomere genes. It encodes a highly conserved 3′→5′ exoribonuclease that shortens poly(A) tails of RNA — a central step in regulated RNA decay and in processing certain noncoding RNAs. Critical to telomere biology, PARN is required for the 3′‑end maturation and stability of TERC. During TERC biogenesis, aberrant oligo‑adenylation can mark nascent transcripts for degradation. PARN removes these post‑transcriptional oligo(A) tails, rescuing TERC from degradation and thereby permitting assembly of a functional telomerase RNP. Loss or reduction of PARN activity therefore reduces steady‑state TERC levels and telomerase activity, producing progressive telomere shortening.

The clinical consequences of PARN defects depend importantly on zygosity and on the degree of functional impairment. Biallelic loss‑of‑function PARN mutations produce severe, early‑onset phenotypes in the DC/ Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson (HH) spectrum — profound bone marrow failure, profound developmental features and multisystem involvement. In contrast, monoallelic (heterozygous) PARN variants are increasingly recognised as contributors to adult‑onset disease manifesting predominantly as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and, variably, bone marrow failure. The patient in this case illustrates that monoallelic PARN variants can present with multisystem manifestations — pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and IPF — even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad. This highlights that genotype–phenotype correlations in TBDs are probabilistic rather than deterministic.

Several mechanisms explain the phenotypic variability seen with the same gene defect. First, haploinsufficiency — where a single functional allele does not produce sufficient protein — is an accepted mechanism for many monoallelic PARN variants. Partial loss of PARN reduces TERC enough to accelerate telomere shortening over decades, so clinical manifestations often appear in young adults rather than in infancy. Second, the specific nature and location of the variant matters: missense changes in critical domains (for example the R3H RNA‑binding domain) can disrupt specific PARN functions and produce variable residual activity. Third, genetic background and modifier genes alter penetrance; variants in other telomere genes (even common polymorphisms), DNA repair genes, or genes affecting RNA metabolism may magnify or mitigate the impact of a PARN lesion.

Non‑genetic factors also shape clinical expression. Environmental and lifestyle stressors that increase cellular turnover or injure vulnerable organs (smoking, recurrent infections, alcohol or hepatotoxic exposures, nutritional deficiencies) accelerate telomere attrition and can unmask or worsen disease. In the presented patient, a severe vitamin B12 deficiency likely imposed additional replicative stress on haematopoietic cells, converting a subclinical telomere maintenance defect into overt cytopenias. Conversely, somatic genetic rescue — the emergence of revertant clones in which a second somatic event restores function — can attenuate disease in some tissues and contribute to mosaic clinical pictures.

An important clinical genetic phenomenon in TBDs is anticipation. Telomere length itself is heritable: if a parent passes on already shortened telomeres, offspring may manifest disease earlier or with greater severity, even if the same pathogenic variant is present. Thus apparent anticipation across generations may reflect inherited telomere shortening rather than change in the primary DNA sequence. This has implications for counselling: a young child of an affected parent may inherit shorter telomeres and be at higher risk despite uncertainty regarding the exact pathogenicity of a detected variant.

From a diagnostic standpoint this biology dictates two complementary approaches. Measurement of telomere length (by flow‑FISH or quantitative PCR) provides functional evidence of telomere shortening; values below the first percentile for age are strongly supportive of a TBD. Genetic testing (targeted panels or exome sequencing) identifies causative variants; interpretation requires attention to zygosity, domain affected and prior evidence linking specific variants to disease. Functional assays — telomerase activity (TRAP assay), TERC RNA quantification, and, less commonly, cellular studies — can clarify the impact of novel variants, such as the monoallelic PARN missense change in this patient.

Therapeutic and counselling implications flow from these mechanisms. Organ systems with high turnover are at ongoing risk; surveillance for progressive bone marrow failure, liver disease and pulmonary fibrosis is essential. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation is a potential cure for marrow failure but requires telomere‑aware planning (reduced‑intensity conditioning regimens and careful donor selection) because of enhanced sensitivity to DNA‑damaging conditioning and extra‑haematopoietic organ vulnerability. Emerging medical strategies (androgens, telomerase‑modulating agents) aim to restore telomerase activity but remain investigational and require long‑term safety data. Genetic counselling must cover variable expressivity, incomplete penetrance, the possibility of de novo variants, and the risk of passing on shorter telomeres to descendants.

In summary, PARN connects RNA metabolism to telomere maintenance: defects reduce TERC stability and telomerase activity, accelerating telomere attrition and producing a spectrum of disease from childhood DC/HH to adult‑onset pulmonary fibrosis and marrow failure. The clinical picture in any individual reflects variant type and zygosity, modifying genetic and environmental factors, somatic events and inherited telomere length — which together explain why a young adult with a monoallelic PARN variant can present with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis even when classical mucocutaneous stigmata are absent. Recognising these mechanistic links is essential for diagnosis, family counselling and tailoring surveillance and treatment.

## Differential Diagnosis: Distinguishing TBD from Common Mimics

The diagnostic challenge in this case—an adult with pancytopenia, neuropathy, premature greying, hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis—is to separate common, potentially reversible disorders from an uncommon but unifying diagnosis: a telomere biology disorder (TBD). A systematic, hypothesis-driven approach is essential. Start by asking whether the features can be explained by one or more common acquired conditions (nutritional/metabolic deficiency, infection, immune disease, toxin exposure, primary marrow disorders or the usual causes of chronic liver disease) and then determine whether the clinical pattern, response to therapy and targeted investigations point instead toward an inherited syndrome of short telomeres. The remainder of this section contrasts the major alternative diagnoses with features that support or argue against a TBD, and highlights the most discriminating investigations.

Nutritional and metabolic causes are the commonest reversible explanation for ineffective erythropoiesis and neuropathy in young adults. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency produce macrocytic or dimorphic anaemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, low reticulocyte responses, basophilic stippling on the smear at times, glossitis and a sensory‑dominant neuropathy—much of which was seen in this patient. The key clinical test is therapeutic: haematologic indices and neurological symptoms should begin to improve promptly after appropriate repletion (parenteral B12 if malabsorption is suspected). If cytopenias and organ dysfunction persist despite corrected B12/folate and normalisation of metabolic indices (including methylmalonic acid and homocysteine where appropriate), consider an alternative or additional diagnosis. Copper deficiency and secondary copper deficiency from excess zinc can mimic B12 deficiency with cytopenias and a myeloneuropathy; bone marrow in copper deficiency classically shows vacuolated precursors and ring sideroblast‑like features. Checking serum copper and zinc is inexpensive and should be done when neurological signs or marrow abnormalities are disproportionate to nutritional deficits. In the reported patient, although severe B12 deficiency plausibly accounted for many haematologic and neurologic findings, the persistence of multisystem fibrosis and marrow abnormality out of proportion to the documented nutritional lesion argues for an additional, unifying process such as a TBD.

Primary marrow disorders form another large category of mimics. Aplastic anaemia and hypoplastic myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) present with cytopenias and hypocellular marrow or hypoplastic trilineage haematopoiesis. Classic MDS presents later in life with cytopenias, dysplasia and characteristic cytogenetic abnormalities or somatic mutations detected by next‑generation sequencing panels. Distinguishing TBD‑associated marrow failure from idiopathic aplastic anaemia or MDS requires integration of marrow morphology, cytogenetics and molecular data. In TBDs marrow may show cellularity that is variable (from hypoplastic to frankly hypercellular when ineffective erythropoiesis predominates), and dysplasia can be absent. The presence of age‑inappropriate telomere shortening (measured by flow‑FISH) or germline pathogenic variants in telomere‑related genes points to TBD. Conversely, a clonal cytogenetic abnormality or a pathogenic somatic mutation typical of MDS (for example, SF3B1, TET2, DNMT3A) supports a diagnosis of MDS or clonal haematopoiesis; these entities can coexist with or be precipitated by short telomeres, so careful interpretation is required. In the present case the marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia without dysplasia and normal cytogenetics—findings more compatible with ineffective erythropoiesis than classical MDS—which, together with extra‑haematologic fibrosis, increased the pretest probability of a TBD.

Immune‑mediated cytopenias can produce confusing laboratory pictures. Warm autoimmune hemolytic anaemia (AIHA) typically presents with a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), brisk haemolysis (elevated indirect bilirubin and LDH), reticulocytosis and spherocytes on the peripheral smear. Evans syndrome (coexisting immune thrombocytopenia and AIHA) can cause pancytopenia through immune mechanisms. Critical discriminators are the degree of hemolysis and the reticulocyte response: a positive DAT in isolation—particularly when the reticulocyte count is low and marrow erythropoiesis is not suppressed—should prompt consideration of ineffective erythropoiesis, nutrient deficiency or marrow failure rather than pure immune destruction. In our patient the DAT was positive but the reticulocyte response was inadequate and marrow was erythroid‑hyperplastic, supporting ineffective generation rather than peripheral destruction as the dominant process; this argues against isolated AIHA as the explanation for the full clinical picture.

Infectious and inflammatory disorders can involve bone marrow, liver and lung. Chronic viral hepatitis, HIV, active Epstein–Barr virus or cytomegalovirus infection may cause cytopenias and hepatic inflammation; disseminated infections can also seed the marrow. Sarcoidosis or histiocytic disorders can produce non‑caseating granulomas in lung, liver and marrow with constitutional symptoms and cytopenias. Distinguishing features include specific serologies, nucleic acid testing (PCR), characteristic imaging patterns and, when necessary, tissue biopsy demonstrating granulomas or viral cytopathic effects. In the case above, serologies were unrevealing (EBV IgG only, consistent with past exposure) and PET‑CT did not show focal findings suggestive of granulomatous disease, making an infectious/inflammatory explanation less likely for the multisystem fibrosis.

When liver fibrosis is prominent, the usual causes should be exhaustively excluded before invoking a genetic TBD. Alcoholic liver disease, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), autoimmune hepatitis, cholestatic disorders, Wilson disease and hereditary haemochromatosis are the common considerations. Clinical context—alcohol use, metabolic risk factors, autoantibodies, ceruloplasmin/urinary copper studies and iron studies/transferrin saturation—will usually identify one of these etiologies. Young adults with cryptogenic cirrhosis, particularly when accompanied by early pulmonary fibrosis or marrow failure, should prompt testing for TBDs once routine causes have been ruled out. Importantly, telomere disorders can cause hepatic fibrosis even in the absence of classic metabolic risk factors; transient elastography and evidence of portal hypertension in a young person should therefore expand the differential to include inherited telomere defects.

Inherited marrow failure syndromes and segmental progeroid disorders overlap phenotypically with TBD and merit careful consideration. Fanconi anaemia presents in childhood or young adulthood with congenital anomalies, short stature and marrow failure; it is diagnosed by chromosomal breakage testing. Coats plus (CTC1 mutations), Revesz syndrome and Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson syndrome are genetically distinct but phenotypically overlapping entities in which telomere biology is often implicated; they typically present earlier with retinal, neurologic or developmental features. Mitochondrial cytopathies can cause multi‑system disease including cytopenias, neurodegeneration and hepatic involvement. The presence of classic mucocutaneous findings—nail dystrophy, reticulate skin pigmentation or oral leukoplakia—would strongly suggest dyskeratosis congenita, but their absence does not exclude a TBD, particularly in adults where presentation can be organ‑specific. Targeted testing (chromosomal breakage, muscle biopsy, targeted gene panels including DKC1, TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, CTC1, etc.) is guided by the phenotype; in this patient the combination of early‑onset pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis with marrow involvement made telomere testing and sequencing of telomere‑related genes a high‑yield next step.

Drug and toxin exposures account for many cases of acquired cytopenia and marrow suppression. Alkylating agents, azathioprine, chloramphenicol and other myelotoxic drugs cause marrow aplasia; heavy metals such as lead produce basophilic stippling and neuropathy. A careful exposure history (occupational, dietary and medication) is therefore essential. The presence of basophilic stippling in this case raises the possibility of lead or other heavy metal exposure, but without a supportive history or biochemical confirmation (blood lead level), that finding is nonspecific and can also be seen in ineffective erythropoiesis. If exposure is identified, removal of the offending agent should produce haematologic recovery; failure to respond should prompt evaluation for an underlying predisposition such as TBD.

How to integrate these considerations into practice? First, correct reversible and common causes. Treat documented B12 and folate deficiency and recheck counts and clinical status. Simultaneously screen for copper and zinc abnormalities and perform infectious serologies and testing appropriate for the clinical context (HIV, hepatitis panel, CMV/EBV PCR if indicated). If cytopenias, organ dysfunction or imaging abnormalities persist despite correction of reversible insults—or if fibrosis, premature greying, early osteoporosis or unexplained pulmonary fibrosis are present—pursue targeted marrow evaluation (morphology, iron studies, cytogenetics) and consider telomere length measurement (flow‑FISH; leukocyte telomere length below the first percentile for age strongly supports a TBD). If telomere testing is abnormal or clinical suspicion remains high, germline genetic testing with a multigene telomere panel or exome sequencing should be performed, with attention to variants in PARN, TERT, TERC, RTEL1 and other related genes. In parallel, perform somatic mutation profiling to evaluate for MDS or clonal haematopoiesis, because therapeutic decisions (immunosuppression, androgen therapy, or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation) and transplant conditioning differ substantially for TBD versus other marrow disorders.

Finally, the distinction has critical therapeutic and prognostic implications. Immune cytopenias are treated with corticosteroids, IVIG and other immune therapies; classical aplastic anaemia may respond to immunosuppression or require HSCT with standard conditioning. In contrast, patients with TBD are at higher risk of transplant‑related toxicity, and HSCT regimens must be tailored (reduced‑intensity and/or donor selection with genetic counselling). Surveillance for progressive pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis and malignancy is essential in TBD, and family members may need testing because of variable penetrance and autosomal dominant or recessive inheritance patterns. Recognizing when common explanations are insufficient—when a young patient has early fibrosis, age‑inappropriate premature greying or multisystem shortening of replicative tissues—should prompt a directed evaluation for a telomere biology disorder rather than continued attribution to isolated nutritional or acquired disease.

## Diagnostic Workup and Interpretation in Suspected Telomere Biology Disorders

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are protean and often masquerade as common acquired diseases. The diagnostic evaluation therefore has two parallel aims: (1) exclude reversible and common causes of the patient’s abnormalities, and (2) assemble phenotype-directed, telomere‑specific data that permit objective interpretation of genetic findings. A stepwise, multidisciplinary approach reduces misclassification, guides management, and informs family counselling.

Initial clinical and laboratory evaluation
Begin with a focused haematologic and metabolic screen. A complete blood count with red‑cell indices, reticulocyte count and careful peripheral smear review are essential. In TBDs the marrow often demonstrates ineffective haematopoiesis with low reticulocyte production despite cytopenias; the presence of macrocytosis or dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, hypersegmented neutrophils or marrow erythroid hyperplasia should prompt investigation for nutritional and inherited marrow failure syndromes. Concurrent measurement of serum vitamin B12, folate, iron studies (iron, TIBC, ferritin), and copper is important because deficiencies can mimic or unmask an underlying telomeropathy, as in this case where severe B12 deficiency explained some but not all features. A haemolysis panel (LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin fractions) and direct antiglobulin test help distinguish peripheral destruction from ineffective erythropoiesis. Bone marrow examination—including morphology, iron stores, cytogenetics and targeted flow cytometry—should be performed when there is persistent unexplained cytopenia to exclude myelodysplasia, marrow infiltration, aplastic anaemia and clonal disorders.

Because TBDs are multisystem, baseline organ assessments are mandatory. Liver tests (transaminases, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, albumin, coagulation profile) and imaging—ultrasound and, where available, transient elastography—screen for hepatic steatosis, fibrosis and portal hypertension. For the lungs, spirometry, DLCO and high‑resolution CT (HRCT) are required to detect early interstitial lung disease (ILD) or usual interstitial pneumonia patterns that often herald telomeropathy in adults. Bone density testing is indicated in patients with fractures, early osteoporosis or bone pain. Neurologic evaluation and electromyography are appropriate if neuropathy or neuromuscular features are present. Infectious and autoimmune serologies should be obtained where clinically indicated to exclude secondary causes of organ fibrosis or cytopenia. Importantly, reversible contributors (nutritional deficiencies, alcohol, viral hepatitis, medications, occupational exposures) must be corrected or excluded before attributing findings to a genetic TBD.

Telomere length testing: methods and interpretation
Measurement of telomere length is a key step in confirming a TBD and contextualizing genetic variants. Flow‑FISH (flow cytometry combined with fluorescence in situ hybridization) performed on leukocyte subsets (commonly lymphocytes and granulocytes) is the preferred clinical assay because it provides cell‑type–specific, age‑adjusted percentiles with good reproducibility. Results are interpreted against population norms: telomere lengths below the first percentile for age are highly supportive of a TBD in the appropriate clinical context; lengths between the 1st and 10th percentiles are suggestive when the phenotype is concordant. qPCR based assays are widely used in research and population studies but have greater interassay variability and are less reliable for diagnostic decision‑making in an individual patient; Southern blot Terminal Restriction Fragment analysis is historically considered a gold standard but is less practical clinically.

Timing and sample considerations matter. Flow‑FISH requires viable fresh blood and should ideally be performed before transfusions or allogeneic cell infusions because donor leukocytes can confound results. Likewise, recent hematopoietic growth factor therapy, severe infections or profound cytopenias can affect leukocyte subset distribution and interpretation; specialist laboratories can provide guidance on preanalytic requirements.

Genetic testing strategy
Genetic confirmation is the cornerstone of modern TBD diagnosis. Testing should start with a targeted gene panel that includes canonical telomere and telomerase pathway genes (for example, TERT, TERC, DKC1, RTEL1, PARN, TINF2, POT1, NAF1, WRAP53/TCAB1, CTC1 and others). Because TERC is an RNA gene and some pathogenic changes are copy‑number variants (deletions), ensure the chosen platform reliably interrogates noncoding loci and includes copy‑number analysis or a separate assay for large deletions/duplications. If a targeted panel is unrevealing and the clinical index of suspicion remains high, exome or genome sequencing with attention to coverage of telomere genes is reasonable.

Interpretation of sequence variants should follow ACMG/AMP guidelines but always be integrated with clinical and telomere length data. Loss‑of‑function variants (nonsense, frameshift, canonical splice) in genes where haploinsufficiency is a known mechanism are more readily declared pathogenic. Missense variants require careful assessment of conservation, in silico predictions, location in functional domains, absence from population databases and, when available, functional data. The PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) change described in this case, for example, hits a conserved residue within the R3H domain and meets several supportive ACMG criteria (PM1, PM2, PP3, PP2) but lacks definitive functional or segregation evidence and therefore remains a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS).

Approach to a VUS in a telomere gene
A VUS should not by itself trigger irreversible clinical decisions (for example, exclusion of a relative from bone marrow donation or definitive curative procedures) but can be meaningful when it is integrated with phenotype and telomere length. A VUS in a gene with strong biological plausibility (PARN), located in a critical domain, and predicted deleterious gains weight if the patient has very short telomeres (<1st percentile) and a compatible multisystem phenotype (bone marrow dysfunction, early greying, pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis). In such circumstances the variant is provisionally considered likely relevant and prompts intensified surveillance and family studies, but management decisions that could cause harm should rely on a combination of genetic, biochemical and clinical evidence.

Reclassification efforts: functional testing and segregation
To move a VUS toward pathogenic or benign status, pursue additional evidence. Segregation analysis in affected and unaffected family members is highly informative: co‑segregation of the variant with disease (and short telomeres) supports pathogenicity. Conversely, identification of the variant in healthy elderly relatives with normal telomere lengths argues against pathogenicity. Telomere length testing in relatives provides complementary data because telomere shortening often segregates with disease risk even before clinical manifestations. Where available, specialized functional assays—such as assessment of TERC maturation for PARN variants, telomerase activity assays, or cellular telomere biology studies—can provide direct evidence of mechanistic impact, though these tests are largely research tools and available only in specialized centers.

Family testing and implications for donor selection
Cascade genetic testing and telomere length measurement in relatives are critical for counselling and management. Family members who carry a pathogenic variant or who have short telomeres should undergo clinical surveillance for cytopenias, pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic disease. Importantly, family members with either a pathogenic variant or demonstrably short telomeres should generally be excluded as hematopoietic stem cell donors because donor telomere length influences post‑transplant outcomes and short‑telomere donors have been associated with graft complications. When a proband has only a VUS, combine targeted sequencing of the variant with telomere length testing in potential donors to reduce uncertainty prior to transplant decisions.

When to involve specialists and how results affect management
Early involvement of a medical geneticist or a specialized inherited marrow failure clinic is recommended to coordinate testing, interpret complex results, counsel the family and arrange longitudinal surveillance. Pulmonology (ILD specialists) and hepatology should be engaged when there is pulmonary or hepatic involvement; transplant teams (both lung and hematopoietic stem cell) should be involved early if progressive organ failure is a concern because conditioning regimens and donor choice must be individualized for telomere syndromes. Psychosocial support and reproductive counselling (including discussion of prenatal or preimplantation genetic diagnosis when a pathogenic variant is identified) are often appropriate.

Practical diagnostic algorithm for the presented patient
For the patient described here the practical sequence would be: (1) complete the initial workup and correct reversible contributors (vitamin B12, folate, electrolyte abnormalities); (2) obtain baseline organ assessments (HRCT chest and pulmonary function tests, transient elastography, bone density) and continue routine surveillance labs; (3) perform telomere length testing by flow‑FISH on fresh blood as soon as feasible (preferably before further transfusions); and (4) pursue targeted genetic testing with a telomere gene panel that includes copy‑number detection and noncoding loci, or confirmatory testing for the PARN variant found on exome sequencing if exome coverage was incomplete for relevant loci. If telomere length is very short and the phenotype remains concordant, the PARN VUS can be provisionally treated as contributory for clinical decision‑making (surveillance intensity, avoidance of toxic exposures, careful donor selection), while efforts continue to obtain segregation and functional data that might permit reclassification.

Summary
Diagnosis of TBDs rests on the integration of clinical phenotype, organ‑specific evaluation, telomere length measurement and robust genetic testing. Flow‑FISH telomere measurement and targeted gene panels that include copy‑number analysis are the core diagnostic tools. A VUS in a biologically plausible gene should prompt additional investigations—family telomere testing, segregation analysis, and, where possible, functional testing—before it is used to justify irreversible therapeutic decisions. Multidisciplinary care and longitudinal reassessment are essential because penetrance is variable, telomere length and clinical manifestations can evolve, and variant classification may change as new evidence emerges.

## Management: Supportive Care, Organ‑Specific Therapy, and Transplant Considerations

Management of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is principally pragmatic and multidisciplinary. Because there are no uniformly effective disease‑modifying therapies for all manifestations, care focuses on treating organ‑specific complications, minimizing exposures that accelerate telomere attrition, and identifying patients who may benefit from targeted or curative interventions such as androgen therapy or haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). The clinical course can be highly variable—even among carriers of the same pathogenic variant—so management is individualized and ideally delivered from a centre experienced with inherited marrow failure and interstitial lung disease.

General supportive care and prevention
Every patient with a suspected or confirmed TBD should have a systematic baseline assessment and be given interventions to correct reversible contributors to morbidity. Nutritional deficiencies (for example vitamin B12, folate, vitamin D and calcium) should be identified and corrected promptly; in our patient the severe B12 deficiency explained much of the ineffective erythropoiesis and required urgent replacement. Electrolyte abnormalities should be corrected and bone health optimized with calcium, vitamin D repletion and weight‑bearing exercise; baseline and interval bone mineral density testing are appropriate given the high prevalence of osteoporosis. Vaccination against influenza, pneumococcus and SARS‑CoV‑2 is recommended; hepatitis A and B vaccination is particularly important in patients with liver disease. Live attenuated vaccines should be avoided in patients who are moderately to severely immunocompromised or who are anticipated to receive HSCT or potent immunosuppression.

Lifestyle and medication counselling are central. Patients should be counselled to avoid tobacco, marijuana, occupational inhalational toxins and recreational drugs that harm the lung, and to avoid excess alcohol and other hepatotoxins. Review medication lists and minimize or avoid agents with known myelo-, hepato- or pulmonary toxicity (for example high‑dose alkylators, bleomycin, and amiodarone when alternatives exist). Routine cancer screening should follow standard age‑appropriate guidelines, but clinicians should maintain a lower threshold to investigate new cytopenias, pulmonary symptoms or abnormal liver tests.

Hematologic management
Treatment of cytopenias in TBD begins with supportive measures. Red‑cell and platelet transfusions should be given for symptomatic anaemia or bleeding, and iron overload must be monitored when transfusion dependence develops—serum ferritin and hepatic iron quantification with MRI guide decisions about chelation. For neutropenia complicated by recurrent infections, short courses of granulocyte colony‑stimulating factor (G‑CSF) may be used judiciously to reduce infectious complications; however, growth‑factor therapy should be managed by specialists because of theoretical concerns about clonal evolution and because the long‑term safety data in TBD are limited.

Where bone marrow failure progresses, androgens (danazol or nandrolone) are an option that has shown benefit in small prospective and retrospective series. Androgen therapy has been associated with haematologic improvement and, in some reports, with measurable telomere lengthening. Typical regimens have used danazol 600–800 mg/day or nandrolone formulations, but dosing must be individualized and monitored closely. Important potential toxicities include hepatotoxicity (including cholestasis and elevation of liver enzymes), adverse lipid changes, virilization, fluid retention, and an increased thrombotic risk; these are especially concerning in patients with underlying hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis. Before and during therapy, obtain baseline and periodic liver function tests, lipid profiles, and clinical assessments for virilization and thrombosis. Because evidence derives from small cohorts, androgen therapy is best initiated and followed in specialized centres and after a careful discussion of risks and benefits.

Other pharmacologic approaches (erythropoiesis‑stimulating agents, thrombopoietin receptor agonists such as eltrombopag) have limited and mixed evidence in TBDs; their use should be considered on a case‑by‑case basis in consultation with experts. When progression to myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) or acute leukaemia occurs, management follows standard haematologic oncology principles, but outcomes may be poorer and therapies more toxic in the setting of short telomeres.

Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)
HSCT is the only potentially curative therapy for progressive marrow failure or transformation to MDS/AML in TBD. However, HSCT in patients with telomere syndromes carries higher peri‑transplant morbidity and mortality, particularly from pulmonary and hepatic complications, and requires careful risk‑benefit assessment. Indications for HSCT include severe aplastic anaemia refractory to supportive care, transfusion dependence with progressive cytopenias, or evolution to MDS/AML where transplant is otherwise indicated.

Conditioning regimens should be modified to reduce toxicity: reduced‑intensity, radiation‑sparing regimens (fludarabine‑based) are preferred over myeloablative conditioning that includes high‑dose alkylators and total body irradiation, both of which have been linked to excessive pulmonary and hepatic toxicity in TBD. Regimens that minimize or avoid busulfan and cyclophosphamide dose intensity are commonly used. Outcomes are improved with careful pre‑transplant optimization of pulmonary and hepatic function, and candid discussions about increased risks.

Family and donor evaluation is critical. Related donors should undergo genetic testing for the familial pathogenic variant and assessment of telomere length; asymptomatic carriers should not be used as stem cell donors. Unrelated matched donors may be preferable when a related donor is affected or of uncertain status. Pre‑transplant counseling should also address fertility preservation and long‑term complications.

Pulmonary disease: antifibrotic therapy, rehabilitation and transplant
Pulmonary fibrosis is a common and often progressive manifestation of TBD, and management aligns with established idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) principles while accounting for the haematologic background. Antifibrotic agents—nintedanib and pirfenidone—slow the rate of decline in forced vital capacity in IPF and are reasonable therapeutic options in TBD‑associated pulmonary fibrosis; data specific to TBD are limited but emerging and these drugs are not contraindicated by short telomeres. Close monitoring for drug interactions and hepatic adverse effects is necessary, particularly in patients with concurrent hepatic fibrosis.

Immunosuppression (for example chronic high‑dose corticosteroids or cytotoxic regimens) is generally avoided because it offers little benefit in fibrotic lung disease and increases infection risk in patients already prone to cytopenias. Pulmonary rehabilitation, supplemental oxygen for hypoxaemia, early vaccination and proactive treatment of infections are important supportive measures. Given the risk of progressive respiratory failure, early referral to a lung transplant centre is appropriate for patients with advanced disease; however, pre‑transplant evaluation must include haematologic assessment because cytopenias and marrow insufficiency complicate peri‑operative and post‑transplant care. Post‑lung transplant immunosuppression may precipitate marrow suppression; thus transplant teams should tailor regimens and plan for close haematologic follow‑up.

Hepatic disease: conservative management and transplant considerations
Liver involvement in TBD ranges from steatosis and fibrosis to cirrhosis and portal hypertension. Management begins with general measures to reduce metabolic liver injury: control of weight, glucose and lipids; abstinence from alcohol; avoidance of hepatotoxic medications; and treatment of coexisting viral hepatitis if present. Patients with cirrhosis require routine hepatology surveillance—screening for portal hypertension, oesophageal varices, hepatocellular carcinoma where indicated, and vaccinations for hepatitis A and B.

Androgen therapy may be limited or contraindicated in patients with significant hepatic fibrosis because of hepatotoxicity. For patients who progress to decompensated liver disease, liver transplantation is an accepted option, but outcomes are influenced by the underlying TBD: coexisting marrow failure increases peri‑operative risk, and post‑transplant immunosuppression can exacerbate cytopenias and infection risk. Multidisciplinary discussion among hepatology, transplant surgery, haematology and genetics is essential; combined organ transplantation or staged approaches are considered in selected centres but carry high complexity and risk.

Bone, endocrine and neuromuscular complications
Osteoporosis and fractures are common in TBD and should be managed actively. Ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D, encourage weight‑bearing activity when feasible, and consider antiresorptive therapy (bisphosphonates) or other bone‑active agents according to standard osteoporosis guidelines and individual bleeding risk or renal function. Evaluate and treat secondary causes of bone loss, including vitamin D deficiency and hypogonadism. Endocrine screening for thyroid dysfunction, glucose intolerance and hypogonadism should be performed at baseline and periodically.

Neuromuscular symptoms, including peripheral neuropathy, require standard neurologic evaluation to identify treatable causes (nutritional deficiencies, metabolic derangements, medication toxicities) and symptomatic management including physiotherapy—an important part of rehabilitative care for patients with fatigue, dyspnoea and deconditioning.

Practical approach in this case and monitoring strategy
For the patient described, the immediate priorities were correction of reversible contributors (vitamin B12, folate and vitamin D), symptomatic transfusions as needed, and comprehensive baseline organ assessment (CBC, bone marrow, liver elastography, high‑resolution CT chest, pulmonary‑function testing including DLCO, and bone density). Given her monoallelic PARN variant and hepatic fibrosis, androgen therapy would be relatively contraindicated or used with extreme caution because of hepatotoxicity; therefore androgens would be considered only if marrow failure progressed and after hepatology input. HSCT would be reserved for frank, progressive marrow failure or malignant transformation and, if contemplated, requires a fludarabine‑based reduced‑intensity regimen and careful donor screening to exclude relatives with the PARN variant or short telomeres.

Longitudinal care should include periodic CBCs (at least every 3–6 months or more often with instability), annual or semiannual liver testing and elastography as guided by hepatology, interval pulmonary PFTs and repeat high‑resolution CT imaging if symptoms change, and monitoring of bone density every 1–3 years. Consider telomere length testing if not already performed and referral to a TBD registry or clinical trial where available.

Multidisciplinary care, genetic counselling and family implications
Because TBDs affect multiple organs and have important reproductive and family implications, multidisciplinary care is essential. Genetic counselling should be offered to the patient and at‑risk relatives; cascade testing for the familial variant and assessment of telomere length (where available) should guide donor selection and reproductive planning (including the option of preimplantation genetic testing). Psychosocial support and coordination with specialty centres that treat inherited marrow failure syndromes and interstitial lung disease improve outcomes and facilitate access to clinical trials.

Conclusion
Management of TBD is complex and individualized: immediate attention to correctable problems and preventive care, organ‑directed therapies (antifibrotics for pulmonary fibrosis, standard cirrhosis care for liver disease, osteoporosis management), cautious use of androgens and growth factors, and timely consideration of HSCT for progressive marrow failure. All decisions—particularly those about disease‑modifying therapies and transplantation—require careful multidisciplinary assessment, donor genetic screening and clear discussion of risks, given the increased peri‑procedural vulnerability of patients with short‑telomere syndromes.

## Prognosis, Surveillance, Counseling, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is heterogeneous and depends primarily on the affected gene, whether mutations are monoallelic or biallelic, the degree of telomere shortening, and which organ systems are involved. Patients with monoallelic PARN variants most commonly present with pulmonary fibrosis or marrow vulnerability rather than the full childhood dyskeratosis congenita phenotype, but hepatic fibrosis, premature hair greying, bone disease and other features may also occur, as exemplified by the case presented here. When pulmonary fibrosis is the dominant manifestation, survival approximates that seen with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)—often measured in years—but outcomes are worse when there is concurrent progressive bone marrow failure or advanced hepatic fibrosis. Conversely, some carriers, even within the same family, remain oligosymptomatic for long periods because penetrance is variable and environmental or secondary stressors (nutritional deficiency, cytotoxic drugs, smoking, viral infections) can precipitate or accelerate organ dysfunction. For an individual patient, therefore, prognosis should be individualized and periodically reassessed as organ-specific disease evolves.

Because target organs (bone marrow, liver, lungs, skin/mucosa, bone) can deteriorate over time, structured surveillance is essential. Initial baseline evaluation should include a complete blood count with reticulocyte count, comprehensive metabolic panel with liver tests, and an assessment of bone marrow function if cytopenias are present. For stable patients without cytopenia, CBC and reticulocyte counts every 3–6 months are reasonable; if cytopenias are evolving or therapy (for example androgens or immunosuppression) is being used, monitoring should be more frequent. Liver evaluation should begin with routine LFTs and an imaging-based assessment; transient elastography provides a noninvasive estimate of fibrosis and may be repeated every 6–12 months when fibrosis is known or suspected, or sooner if clinical or laboratory indicators worsen. Pulmonary surveillance should include baseline pulmonary function tests (PFTs) with spirometry and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), and a high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest at baseline if symptoms or PFT abnormalities exist. Thereafter, PFTs with DLCO are typically repeated every 6–12 months (more frequently if declining), and HRCT should be symptom-triggered or obtained at intervals determined by clinical course and local practice (for example annually to every 2 years in progressive lung disease). Bone health assessment with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) is indicated at baseline and then every 1–2 years if abnormal or if additional risk factors are present. Because mucosal and cutaneous malignancies are relatively more frequent in TBDs, an annual focused oral and skin examination is prudent, with a low threshold for dermatology or ENT referral for suspicious lesions. Finally, any patient receiving immunosuppressives, androgens, or preparing for transplant should have infectious risk assessed, vaccinations updated (influenza, pneumococcus, COVID-19 as indicated), and appropriate monitoring for drug-specific toxicities (for example, LFTs during androgen therapy).

Surveillance and management must be coordinated with multidisciplinary input from hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics and transplant teams. Transplant readiness (hematopoietic or solid organ) requires careful pre‑transplant assessment because patients with short telomeres experience higher rates of post‑transplant complications: poor wound healing, increased mucositis and pulmonary toxicity, prolonged cytopenias and heightened susceptibility to infections. For hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), conditioning regimens should generally be reduced intensity to minimize organ toxicity; myeloablative regimens that include high-dose alkylators or full-dose total body irradiation carry greater risk of transplant-related mortality in TBD patients. Pulmonary and hepatic reserve must be quantified objectively before HSCT, and the presence of significant pulmonary fibrosis or cirrhosis may influence timing, conditioning choice, candidacy, or consideration of combined or sequential organ-specific strategies. For solid organ transplantation (lung or liver), the transplant team should anticipate potential post‑transplant bone marrow failure and infection risk; perioperative planning should include hematology input and long-term surveillance. Wherever transplantation is contemplated, a center experienced in TBDs and complex transplant decisions should be involved early.

Genetic counselling is fundamental: PARN-associated disease exhibits variable inheritance patterns and expressivity. Monoallelic PARN variants can act through haploinsufficiency and present as adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis or marrow vulnerability, while biallelic variants more often produce severe, early-onset syndromes. Counsel patients and families that penetrance is incomplete and that clinical manifestations may worsen or shift between generations (a phenomenon sometimes referred to as genetic anticipation because telomere length can shorten across generations). Offer targeted cascade testing of at‑risk relatives when a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is identified. Telomere length measurement is useful adjunct information: flow-FISH performed in a diagnostic laboratory is the gold-standard telomere assay for clinical decision-making; qPCR-based telomere assays are less reliable for individual patient management and should not be used alone to guide irreversible choices such as donor selection or HSCT. When a clearly pathogenic variant is found, discuss reproductive options including preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic diseases (PGT‑M) and prenatal diagnostic testing; these are appropriate only after thorough counselling regarding variable penetrance and uncertain phenotype prediction.

Donor selection for transplantation requires particular caution. Related donors who are carriers of the familial mutation or who have demonstrably short telomeres should be avoided even if they are clinically asymptomatic, because such donors may have subclinical marrow vulnerability that can compromise donor safety and recipient outcomes. When unrelated donors are used, consider telomere-length testing and careful evaluation of donor health; in HSCT, haploidentical related donation should be approached only after carrier status is excluded. In addition, testing for somatic genetic rescue or mosaicism in potential donors may be informative in special circumstances and should be discussed with the genetics team.

Therapeutic pitfalls and practical cautions: do not base irreversible therapies (for example, proceeding to HSCT or choosing a related donor) solely on a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). A VUS requires further functional or segregation evidence before being used to justify high‑risk interventions. Avoid azathioprine-based regimens for IPF—studies have shown harm with immunosuppressive regimens combining prednisone, azathioprine and N‑acetylcysteine in IPF, and azathioprine in short-telomere patients is associated with additional myelotoxicity. Exercise caution with any myelotoxic drugs, radiation and cytotoxic chemotherapy in patients with short telomeres; lower doses or alternative agents may be necessary. Androgen therapy (danazol, nandrolone) has been shown in small studies to improve cytopenias and sometimes increase telomere length, but androgens carry significant side effects—hepatic toxicity, virilization, lipid changes—and are contraindicated or risky in patients with advanced liver disease such as cirrhosis; their use should be individualized and monitored closely by hematology and hepatology. Finally, counsel patients strongly to avoid tobacco and other exposures known to accelerate telomere attrition (smoking, excessive alcohol), and to maintain up‑to‑date vaccinations.

Practical steps for the patient in this case: confirm the clinical significance of the PARN variant by (1) measuring leukocyte telomere length using flow‑FISH, (2) offering segregation testing to first‑degree relatives and (3) discussing possible functional testing with a specialized genetics laboratory if available. Because she already has documented hepatic fibrosis and radiographic pulmonary fibrosis, ongoing surveillance should include CBC and reticulocyte counts every 3 months initially, LFTs and elastography at least every 6–12 months, PFTs with DLCO every 6–12 months and symptom‑triggered HRCT, and annual bone density and mucocutaneous exams. Given her cirrhosis, androgens are relatively contraindicated; decisions about HSCT should be deferred until multidisciplinary assessment and objective quantification of pulmonary and hepatic reserve are performed at a specialized center. Offer family genetic counselling and discuss reproductive options if a pathogenic classification is established.

In summary, the cardinal messages for clinicians are: TBDs like PARN‑associated disease are multisystem and variably penetrant; prognosis is organ‑specific and dynamic; surveillance must be proactive and multidisciplinary; avoid high‑risk drugs and procedures without full genetic and telomere evaluation; and always confirm pathogenicity before committing to irreversible therapies. High clinical suspicion—young adults with cryptogenic pulmonary fibrosis, unexplained early cirrhosis, unexplained cytopenias, premature greying or mucocutaneous abnormalities—should prompt genetic evaluation and telomere measurement because early recognition changes counselling, monitoring, donor selection and treatment planning.
