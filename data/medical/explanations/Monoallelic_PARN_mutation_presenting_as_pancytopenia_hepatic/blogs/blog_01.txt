Title: Blog about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

# When B12 Isn’t the Whole Story: Pancytopenia, Low Retics, and the Telomere Trap

What grabs you about this case is the mismatch. A young woman with 5 years of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia and frank B12 deficiency — that explains a lot. But the picture didn’t fit cleanly: a strikingly low reticulocyte count, marrow showing ineffective erythropoiesis (not hyperplastic regenerative marrow), premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, hepatosplenomegaly with cirrhosis on elastography, and CT chest with fibrotic changes. Correcting B12 improved symptoms a bit, but the constellation of multisystem “premature ageing” features pushed the team to look beyond nutritional deficiency. Clinical exome sequencing found a monoallelic PARN missense variant — not a slam-dunk pathogenic call, but compelling when you put the phenotype and gene together. This is the kind of case that teaches you to avoid diagnostic anchoring.

Why this matters for EM/IM/hematology clinicians
- Nutritional deficiencies (B12, folate) are common and can produce dramatic cytopenias — but not every cytopenia in a patient with B12 deficiency is due only to B12. Look for discordant clues.
- Low retic count despite anemia suggests marrow failure or ineffective erythropoiesis, not brisk peripheral hemolysis or purely nutrient-responsive anemia.
- Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can present in adulthood without the “classic” dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad. Instead they may present as bone marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis, early greying, osteoporosis, and increased malignancy risk.
- PARN variants are increasingly recognized in TBD spectra — monoallelic variants often link to adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis or marrow dysfunction; biallelic variants cause more severe pediatric phenotypes. Variant interpretation may be tricky (VUS is common), but the phenotype can tip you toward a TBD diagnosis.

Clinical reasoning walkthrough — where anchoring goes wrong
- Anchoring step: severe B12 deficiency found → assume all findings are explained. That’s tempting and sometimes correct.
- Red flags against simple B12-only etiology:
  - Pancytopenia with very low retics (0.39%) despite anemia — marrow should at least kick out retics when B12 replaced unless marrow is exhausted or failing.
  - Bone marrow: trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores but no malignancy or dysplasia — points to ineffective erythropoiesis rather than pure peripheral destruction.
  - Multisystem fibrosis (lungs + liver) and premature ageing signs in a young adult — not typical for isolated nutritional deficiency.
  - DAT positive for IgG but no brisk hemolysis and low retic response — mixed signals; don’t overcall AIHA when marrow response is inadequate.
- When these discordances occur, expand the differential: inherited marrow failure syndromes (including TBDs), metabolic/infiltrative disorders, immune-mediated disease, occult malignancy. Genetic testing and telomere studies become reasonable next steps.

How telomere biology disorders present (practical patterns)
- Classic triad (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation) may be absent.
- Adult presentation commonly: unexplained cytopenias/aplastic anemia, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, premature greying/osteoporosis, malignancy risk.
- Lab patterns: cytopenias with low retic count; marrow may show hypocellularity or signs of ineffective erythropoiesis without clear dysplasia.
- Family history may be absent (de novo mutations, incomplete penetrance, variable expression).

What to do when you suspect a TBD
- Don’t delay: correct reversible causes (B12, folate, iron) but keep an eye on discordant findings after repletion.
- Basic workup: CBC with retic, peripheral smear, iron studies, B12/folate, LFTs, chest imaging if dyspnea or cough, elastography/US if hepatic disease suspected.
- Bone marrow exam to look for cellularity, dysplasia, infiltration.
- Specialized testing:
  - Telomere length measurement (flow-FISH) — useful adjunct though not always diagnostic alone.
  - Germline genetic testing with a telomere/TBD panel or clinical exome/genome sequencing. Expect VUS results; interpret in clinical context and with genetics input.
- Management and counseling:
  - Supportive care: transfusions, correct deficiencies, treat infections, bone health.
  - Avoid unnecessary cytotoxic agents (chemo) without hematology/genetics guidance — TBD patients tolerate cytotoxic therapy poorly.
  - Hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) can be curative for marrow failure but has higher toxicity in TBDs; transplant decisions need telomere-informed conditioning and genetics-informed donor selection.
  - Consider referral to hepatology/pulmonology for surveillance and transplant evaluation when fibrosis advances.
  - Genetic counseling for patient and family; discuss reproductive options and screening for at-risk relatives.
  - Experimental therapies (androgens like danazol) have some data for telomere elongation but carry risks and need specialist oversight.

Clinical pearls
- A very low reticulocyte count in anemia is a red flag for marrow underproduction/ineffective erythropoiesis — don’t assume peripheral causes.
- A positive DAT does not exclude marrow failure; interpret hemolysis labs (LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, retic) together.
- Young patients with cirrhosis or pulmonary fibrosis should trigger consideration of genetic causes (including TBDs), especially if there are hematologic abnormalities or premature ageing signs.
- Genetic results matter, but variant classification can be VUS — phenotype-driven interpretation and expert genetics input are essential.
- Early recognition of TBD changes management: surveillance for organ complications, avoidance of therapies that worsen outcomes, and timely transplant discussion.

Take-home messages
- Don’t anchor: correct nutritional deficiencies but re-evaluate if features persist or if multisystem premature-ageing signs are present.
- Low retic count with pancytopenia favors marrow-related problems or ineffective erythropoiesis — consider inherited marrow failure syndromes.
- Telomere biology disorders can masquerade as isolated B12-responsive anemia or idiopathic organ fibrosis; think TBD in young adults with cytopenias + pulmonary/hepatic fibrosis or premature greying/osteoporosis.
- Order telomere testing and germline genetic testing when suspicion is high, and involve hematology and genetic counseling early.
- Management is mainly supportive; HSCT is a potential cure for marrow failure but requires specialized planning in TBD patients.
