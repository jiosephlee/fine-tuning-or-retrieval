# Making Sense of a VUS: Using Phenotype to Move the Needle in Suspected TBD

What to do when a young patient presents with pancytopenia, premature greying, pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis — and genetic testing returns a monoallelic PARN missense variant labeled “Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)”? This is exactly the clinical dilemma from a late-20s woman who had progressive fatigue, severe B12 deficiency, dimorphic anaemia, hepatosplenomegaly with cirrhosis and radiographic pulmonary fibrosis. The sequencing hit — PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) — sat in the R3H domain, was extremely rare in population databases and predicted damaging by multiple in-silico tools, but remained a VUS on ACMG criteria.

Why this case is instructive
- VUS does not mean “clinically irrelevant.” In telomere biology disorders (TBDs) — where genotype–phenotype relationships are heterogeneous, incompletely penetrant and age-dependent — careful clinical correlation can meaningfully change management.
- A single monoallelic PARN change often gets less weight than biallelic PARN in classic dyskeratosis congenita, but monoallelic PARN has been associated with pulmonary fibrosis, liver fibrosis and bone marrow dysfunction. The phenotype here (multisystem premature ageing with organ fibrosis) strongly suggests a TBD despite the VUS label.
- The clinical question isn’t only “Is this variant pathogenic?” but “How should this patient and family be managed now, given the uncertainty?”

How to move from VUS to clinically useful information
1. Phenotype matching
   - Start with the full clinical picture. Telomere-related disease preferentially affects highly proliferative tissues: bone marrow (cytopenias, marrow failure), lung (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis), liver (fibrosis/cirrhosis), mucocutaneous features (sometimes absent), nails, hair (premature greying), and bone (osteoporosis).
   - The more the phenotype aligns with known manifestations of PARN/TBD, the more plausible pathogenicity becomes for a rare variant. In this case the combination of early-onset pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis plus marrow vulnerability fits the TBD spectrum.

2. Variant-level evidence
   - Domain and conservation: the variant affects a conserved residue in the R3H domain — a functionally important region for PARN activity. PM1 (critical domain) is supportive per ACMG.
   - In-silico predictions and absence from population databases (PM2, PP3) provide additional supporting evidence but are not determinative alone.
   - Compare to previously reported PARN missense variants (even if other variants are in different exons) — recurring domain or residue hotspots add weight.

3. Telomere length measurement
   - Telomere length testing (flow-FISH on peripheral blood leukocyte subtypes) is a pivotal functional correlate. Short telomeres for age strongly support a telomere biology disorder and increase the likelihood the variant is clinically relevant.
   - If telomeres are normal, consider timing (smoking, inflammation, chemotherapy), cell subset measured, and repeat or test alternative tissues; a normal result lowers the probability but doesn’t completely exclude functional impact.

4. Family studies and segregation
   - Test first-degree relatives (ideally with telomere length). Segregation with compatible phenotype or shortened telomeres supports pathogenicity. De novo occurrence (if neither parent carries the variant) is also informative.
   - Be mindful of incomplete penetrance: absence of disease in a carrier does not rule out pathogenicity but informs counseling.

5. Functional assays and expert review
   - Functional work (TERC maturation, PARN activity assays) is specialized but can be decisive. Consider referral to research labs or telomere-disease registries.
   - Submit variant and phenotype to ClinVar and specialist consortia; expert panels (gene-specific) can re-evaluate with collective evidence.

How this changes clinical management (acting despite uncertainty)
- Surveillance: treat the patient as at-risk for progressive marrow failure and organ fibrosis.
  - Regular CBCs with reticulocyte counts, bone marrow evaluation if cytopenias progress.
  - Pulmonary: baseline pulmonary function tests and high-resolution CT; interval surveillance tailored to findings and symptoms.
  - Hepatic: baseline elastography/imagery and liver function tests; monitor for portal hypertension and hepatocellular carcinoma per specialty guidance.
- Avoidance of iatrogenic hits: minimize exposures that accelerate telomere attrition — avoid cytotoxic/alkylating agents if possible; carefully consider immunosuppressive therapies. If chemotherapy or transplant is required, involve a specialist with TBD experience because standard conditioning increases toxicity.
- Transplant planning: hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) can be curative for marrow failure, but TBD patients need reduced-intensity conditioning and center expertise due to organ fibrosis and increased regimen-related toxicity.
- Therapeutic adjuncts: androgens (danazol, nandrolone) have shown telomere-lengthening signals in small studies but evidence is limited; weigh risks and specialist input is needed.
- Genetic counselling: explain uncertainty clearly — VUS today can be reclassified with new data. Offer family testing (variant + telomere length) and discuss reproductive options (preimplantation genetic testing if a pathogenic variant is later confirmed).
- Documentation and re-evaluation: enter the variant and clinical phenotype into databases, and plan periodic re-assessment of variant classification as new evidence emerges.

Communicating uncertainty with patients
- Be explicit: “The genetic change we found is currently of uncertain significance, but your clinical picture is strongly suggestive of a telomere biology disorder. We will therefore monitor you and your family closely, and act on problems early.”
- Offer concrete actions: surveillance plan, avoidances (e.g., smoking), discussion of potential therapies, and family testing.
- Reassure that VUS reclassification is possible; commit to recontact if variant gets upgraded/downgraded.

Clinical pearls
- A VUS in a telomere-related gene does not equal “no diagnosis.” Phenotype, telomere length, domain context and family data can tilt management decisions.
- Flow-FISH telomere testing is a high-yield next step when a VUS in a TBD gene is found and the phenotype is suggestive.
- Manage the patient’s organs now: surveillance, minimize toxic exposures, and coordinate early with transplant and liver/pulmonary specialists experienced in TBDs.
- Document and share: submit variant + phenotype to ClinVar and consider registry enrolment — collective data are how VUS become clarified.

Take-home messages
- Don’t dismiss a VUS in a telomere gene when the phenotype fits — use phenotype, domain knowledge, population rarity and telomere-length testing to inform probability.
- Short telomeres on flow-FISH substantially increase the clinical relevance of an uncertain variant and change management.
- Act on risk: institute surveillance, avoid telomere-toxic therapies, involve specialized transplant teams early and provide genetic counselling for the patient and family.
- Re-evaluate and re-contact: VUS status can change — contributing clinical and laboratory data to shared databases accelerates reclassification.

This case is a good reminder that genetics is not an endpoint but an integrating piece of the diagnostic puzzle. When clinical features scream “telomere disease,” a cautious but proactive approach — combining functional testing, family studies and organ-focused surveillance — is the right way to manage uncertainty for patients and families.
