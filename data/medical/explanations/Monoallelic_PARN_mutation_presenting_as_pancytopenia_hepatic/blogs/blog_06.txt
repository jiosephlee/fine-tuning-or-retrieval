Title: Blog about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

# ILD in the Young Adult: Genetics, Family Screening, and Transplant Nuances in Telomere Biology Disorders

What should make you sit up in the ED or clinic? A patient with progressive fibrotic interstitial lung disease (ILD) in their 20s–30s. Add unexplained cytopenias, premature greying, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis or a history suggesting marrow stress — and the usual “idiopathic” label starts to look dangerous. The case above — a woman in her late 20s with pancytopenia, hepatic cirrhosis, premature greying and CT-proven pulmonary fibrosis, ultimately found to carry a monoallelic PARN variant — is a classic reminder that telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can masquerade as multiple separate diseases and change management dramatically.

## Why telomere disorders matter for young ILD patients
- TBDs (mutations in TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, etc.) accelerate cellular senescence in high-turnover tissues: bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver, skin, mucosa. Phenotypes range from classic dyskeratosis congenita to isolated pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure.
- Young-onset fibrotic ILD (typically <50, often <40), especially when accompanied by cytopenia, liver disease, premature greying, nail/mucosal changes, early osteoporosis or a suggestive family history, should raise suspicion for a TBD.
- Nutritional or autoimmune explanations (severe B12 deficiency, a positive DAT, inflammatory markers) can coexist and distract — as in this case — but persistence or multisystem fibrosis despite correction of reversible insults should prompt genetic thinking.

## Practical approach: when to suspect and what to order
Start asking the “TBD screen” questions for ILD under 40:
- Age at onset <50 (especially <40)
- Unexplained cytopenia(s) or low reticulocyte response
- Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis without conventional cause
- Premature greying, nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, mucosal atrophy, osteoporosis or fractures
- Family history of early pulmonary fibrosis, aplastic anaemia, early liver disease, or premature death

Initial investigations to pursue early:
- CBC with reticulocyte count and peripheral smear (look for ineffective erythropoiesis)
- Liver elastography or imaging if LFTs/clinical exam suggest hepatopathy
- High-resolution chest CT for pattern (short-telomere ILD often fibrotic, sometimes UIP-like)
- Basic autoimmune and infectious workup to exclude common causes
- If suspicion remains high: refer for genetic evaluation and telomere length testing (flow-FISH or qPCR in specialized labs)

Clinical-genetic testing:
- Order multi-gene telomere/ILD panels (include TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1 and others) rather than single-gene tests when feasible.
- Telomere length testing (flow-FISH) provides functional evidence: very short telomeres for age support a TBD even when a variant is classified VUS.
- Expect VUS results — interpretation requires correlation with phenotype, family studies, and expert genetics input.
- Always involve genetic counselling early for patient and family cascade testing.

## How telomere status changes medical decisions
Antifibrotic therapy:
- Standard antifibrotic agents for fibrotic ILD (nintedanib, pirfenidone) remain the backbone for progressive fibrotic disease and are usually considered regardless of telomere status. There is no proven contraindication, but data in monogenic TBDs are limited.
- Telomere status does not exclude antifibrotic use; however, disease trajectory in TBD patients can be aggressive — act early.

Immunosuppression and cytotoxic drugs:
- Patients with short telomeres are exquisitely susceptible to cytotoxic and immunosuppressive toxicity (bone marrow suppression, hepatic toxicity). Classic teaching: avoid or use extreme caution with myelotoxic agents.
- Drugs such as azathioprine and high-dose cyclophosphamide (used historically in some ILD regimens) carry high risk; steroid-sparing or non-cytotoxic strategies should be preferred.
- In ILD, the move away from routine immunosuppression for fibrotic phenotypes (and the evidence of harm from azathioprine in some IPF cohorts) aligns well with a cautious approach in TBDs.

Lung transplant candidacy and planning:
- Short telomeres increase the risk of post-transplant bone marrow failure, poor wound healing, infections and extra-pulmonary organ complications.
- Standard myeloablative conditioning and routine immunosuppression protocols can produce catastrophic cytopenias and marrow failure in TBD patients.
- Key transplant considerations:
  - Early referral to transplant centers experienced with TBDs — do not wait until advanced respiratory failure.
  - Hematology co-management is essential pre- and post-transplant.
  - Consider reduced-intensity conditioning regimens; avoid full myeloablation and high-dose alkylators when possible.
  - Be prepared for an increased need for transfusion support and prolonged cytopenias; factor in liver disease for drug metabolism and candidacy.
  - Family genetic testing impacts donor selection — related donors may carry the same pathogenic variant or short telomeres; screen potential related donors before selecting them.
- Multidisciplinary discussion (pulmonology, transplant surgery, hematology, genetics, hepatology) changes candidacy thresholds and perioperative planning.

Hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) for marrow failure:
- HSCT can be curative for marrow failure but is high risk in TBDs because of lung and liver comorbidities and transplant-related toxicity.
- If HSCT is considered, reduced-intensity conditioning and expert center involvement are mandatory; outcomes are better when done before end-stage organ damage.

Experimental and adjunctive therapies:
- Androgen therapy (danazol, nandrolone) has shown telomere-lengthening effects in small studies and may improve counts in some patients, but long-term benefit and safety are uncertain.
- No targeted telomerase-restoring drug is standard of care; clinical trials and center expertise should guide off-label use.

## Communication and family implications
- Explain that a genetic diagnosis affects the patient’s transplant risk, treatment tolerability, surveillance for liver and hematologic disease, and family members’ risk.
- Offer cascade testing to at-risk relatives, and avoid using related donors for transplant until they are screened.
- Even when a variant is reported as a VUS (as in the PARN c.613T>C, p.Cys205Arg in the case), the phenotype and telomere measurements guide management — classification should not delay protective steps like family screening and transplant planning.

## Take-home clinical pearls
- Think TBD when ILD appears in a young adult, especially with cytopenias, liver disease, premature greying, mucocutaneous findings or early bone disease.
- Order telomere length testing (flow-FISH) and a multi-gene telomere panel; involve genetics early — VUS results require phenotype correlation.
- Avoid routine cytotoxic or myeloablative regimens where possible; immunosuppression can cause disproportionate marrow toxicity in short-telomere patients.
- For lung transplant candidates: refer early to experienced centers, involve hematology, consider reduced-intensity conditioning, and screen related donors for pathogenic variants or short telomeres.
- Family screening and genetic counselling are essential — a “sporadic” presentation in a young adult often has implications for relatives.

The case above highlights the diagnostic trap: severe B12 deficiency, hemolysis markers, and inflammatory signals can lead clinicians down familiar corridors. When multisystem fibrosis and marrow vulnerability co-exist in a young person, put TBDs back on the differential — it changes everything from drug choices to who can safely be your transplant donor.
