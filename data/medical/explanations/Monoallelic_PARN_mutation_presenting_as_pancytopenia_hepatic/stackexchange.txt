Clinical Pearls & Morning Report: Monoallelic_PARN_mutation_presenting_as_pancytopenia_hepatic

### [Clinical Pearls] In a young adult with pancytopenia, premature greying, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic fibrosis, and fibrotic lung disease that persist despite vitamin repletion, what unifying diagnostic category should be suspected?

Suspect a telomere biology disorder (TBD) — the dyskeratosis congenita (DC) spectrum — as the unifying diagnosis.

Clinical reasoning: this patient’s problems cluster in tissues with high replicative demand (bone marrow, hair, mucosa, liver, lung, bone), producing premature ageing features (early greying, nail/oral changes), persistent pancytopenia despite correction of nutritional deficits, and fibrotic involvement of lung and liver. That pattern is classic for disorders of telomere maintenance (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1 and others), in which germline telomerase/telomere pathway defects cause accelerated telomere shortening, stem‑cell exhaustion and organ fibrosis. Monoallelic variants (eg, in PARN) often present in young adults with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, bone‑marrow failure or mixed organ fibrosis and may lack the full mucocutaneous triad of classic DC.

Broader principles and next steps: when you see unexplained cytopenias and multi‑organ fibrosis in a young patient, think inherited telomere disease. Confirm with telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) and targeted next‑generation sequencing of telomere genes. Early recognition changes management — informs genetic counselling, organ‑specific surveillance (lung, liver, marrow), influences transplant timing and donor choice, and steers therapy away from standard cytotoxic regimens that worsen outcomes; experimental options (androgens) and hematopoietic stem‑cell transplant in progressive marrow failure may be considered.

### [Clinical Pearls] What elements comprise the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, and how can telomere biology disorders present in adults when the triad is absent?

The classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is oral leukoplakia, abnormal nails (nail dystrophy), and reticular (net‑like) skin hyperpigmentation. Historically the diagnosis rested on those three findings (or two plus bone‑marrow failure), because they reflect high‑turnover tissues most vulnerable to telomere shortening.

But telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are highly pleiotropic, show variable penetrance, and often present in adults without the full triad. In adults the common presentations are organ‑specific or marrow‑predominant: unexplained early‑onset idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or interstitial lung disease, otherwise unexplained bone‑marrow failure or cytopenias (aplastic anemia, myelodysplasia), and hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis. Milder “premature ageing” features—early greying/alopecia, mucosal atrophy, osteoporosis, avascular bone disease, and cryptic mucocutaneous findings—may be the only clues. Monoallelic mutations (eg, in TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1) frequently produce isolated IPF or marrow disease in adulthood; somatic rescue, environmental exposures, and genetic background determine which organ fails first.

Clinical reasoning point: when a young adult has unexplained early‑onset pulmonary fibrosis, cryptic cytopenias, liver fibrosis, or a cluster of premature‑ageing features (premature greying, mucosal atrophy, nail changes even if subtle), think TBD even if the classic triad is absent. The next steps are to measure telomere length (flow‑FISH) and perform targeted germline sequencing, because confirming a TBD changes surveillance, family counseling, and transplant planning (reduced‑intensity regimens, donor selection, and malignancy risk mitigation).

### [Clinical Pearls] Which clinical features in adults should prompt evaluation for a telomere biology disorder: list at least five that span hematologic, hepatic, pulmonary, and premature-ageing phenotypes?

Key teaching point: adult patients with unexplained disease of highly proliferative tissues — bone marrow, lung, liver, skin and hair — should prompt evaluation for a telomere‑biology disorder (TBD). Telomere defects preferentially cripple tissues with high replicative demand, so look for clusters of findings, early onset, or multisystem involvement that are out of proportion to common explanations.

Specific clinical features that should raise suspicion (at least five, spanning the requested domains):

1) Unexplained cytopenias or marrow failure in a young adult — isolated macrocytic anemia, refractory pancytopenia, aplastic anaemia, or transfusion‑dependent marrow failure without an acquired cause. Rationale: haemopoietic stem cells are highly telomere‑dependent; premature telomere attrition causes ineffective haematopoiesis and marrow exhaustion.

2) Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or early‑onset interstitial lung disease (especially onset <60 years) often with a family history of pulmonary fibrosis. Rationale: alveolar epithelial cells have limited replicative reserve; monallelic telomerase pathway mutations commonly present as familial or early IPF.

3) Non‑viral, non‑alcoholic hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis or unexplained portal hypertension in a young adult. Rationale: hepatocytes must regenerate after injury and show fibrosis when telomere maintenance is defective; cirrhosis can be an under‑recognised TBD manifestation.

4) Premature ageing features — premature greying or hair loss, early osteoporosis with low‑trauma fractures, and characteristic mucocutaneous findings of dyskeratosis congenita (nail dystrophy, reticular hyperpigmentation, oral leukoplakia) when present. Rationale: these reflect stem‑cell exhaustion in skin, hair follicles and bone.

5) Multisystem combination or unusually severe disease out of proportion to a single reversible cause — for example B12 deficiency with persistent cytopenia after correction, cytopenia plus pulmonary fibrosis, or marrow failure plus liver disease. Rationale: the co‑occurrence of organ‑specific fibrosis or failure suggests a unifying defect in telomere biology rather than isolated acquired disease.

Additional red flags that strengthen the case for evaluation: onset at a young age (often <50), family history of marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or early cirrhosis/cancer, unexplained mucocutaneous triad, unusually poor tolerance of cytotoxic therapy, and laboratory evidence of very short telomeres if available.

When to act: in any adult with one of the above features plus another supportive clue (young age, family history, multisystem involvement, or refractory disease), measure telomere length (flow‑FISH if available) and pursue targeted genetic testing for telomere‑related genes. Early recognition guides surveillance (pulmonary, hepatic, haematologic), genetic counselling, choice of therapies (HSCT planning, avoidance of genotoxic exposures), and donor selection.

### [Morning Report] In pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count, dimorphic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and elevated serum iron/ferritin, what is the most likely mechanism of cytopenias?

The pattern you describe (pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count, dimorphic red cells, hypersegmented neutrophils, basophilic stippling and Cabot rings, raised serum iron/ferritin and an erythroid‑hyperplastic marrow) points to ineffective hematopoiesis with intramedullary destruction of precursors rather than peripheral destruction.

Clinically: the low reticulocyte count despite marrow erythroid hyperplasia means newly produced red cells are being lost in the marrow. Hypersegmented neutrophils and dimorphic anemia indicate a major defect in DNA synthesis (severe B12/folate deficiency here) producing megaloblastic dyserythropoiesis. Basophilic stippling and Cabot rings are additional markers of disturbed erythropoiesis/dyserythropoiesis. Elevated serum iron and ferritin reflect failure to incorporate iron into hemoglobin (reduced utilization) and secondary iron loading of stores.

Pathophysiologically in this case: telomere dysfunction from a PARN-related telomere biology disorder reduces the replicative capacity of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells and predisposes erythroid precursors to premature senescence and apoptosis. Superimposed severe B12 deficiency further impairs DNA synthesis, magnifying intramedullary apoptosis and ineffective erythropoiesis. The net result is pancytopenia produced by marrow failure/ineffective hematopoiesis with intramedullary destruction rather than by peripheral hemolysis or immune cytopenia.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you reconcile a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG with laboratory and marrow findings favoring ineffective erythropoiesis rather than immune hemolysis, and what additional tests help distinguish these entities?

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) is an important clue, but it is not a standalone diagnosis of immune haemolysis. You reconcile a positive DAT for IgG with laboratory and marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis by integrating the whole haemolysis/erythropoiesis picture and asking whether there is evidence that red cells are being destroyed in the periphery and replaced appropriately.

In immune (warm) autoimmune haemolytic anaemia you expect: brisk haemolytic markers (high indirect bilirubin, very high LDH, low haptoglobin or free plasma haemoglobin), a high reticulocyte count or reticulocyte production index indicating a compensatory marrow response, and marrow that is erythroid‑hyperplastic but effective (maturing erythroid precursors). By contrast, ineffective erythropoiesis (for example due to severe B12/folate deficiency, sideroblastic processes or marrow failure) produces elevated indirect bilirubin and LDH to a variable degree from intramedullary destruction, but the reticulocyte count is low or inappropriately normal and the marrow shows erythroid hyperplasia with morphologic abnormalities and iron loading — exactly the pattern in this case. That discordance (positive DAT + low retic + marrow features of ineffective erythropoiesis and increased iron stores) argues that the DAT is an epiphenomenon rather than the primary cause of anaemia.

There are biologic reasons a DAT can be positive without clinically significant haemolysis: low‑affinity or low‑density IgG coating that does not trigger rapid splenic clearance, residual allo‑ or auto‑antibodies after prior transfusion, passively transferred antibodies, drug‑induced low‑grade IgG coating, or nonspecific binding in inflammatory states and marrow disorders. In telomere biology disorders and other chronic marrow illnesses the immune milieu may produce weak RBC coating without a fulminant haemolytic syndrome.

Tests and steps that help distinguish immune haemolysis from ineffective erythropoiesis in this situation

1) Quantify haemolysis and marrow response: repeat reticulocyte count and calculate the reticulocyte production index; measure haptoglobin, plasma free haemoglobin, urine hemosiderin and serial bilirubin/LDH. A brisk reticulocytosis with low haptoglobin supports peripheral immune haemolysis; a low retic with marrow erythroid hyperplasia points to ineffective erythropoiesis.

2) Eluate testing: perform an elution from the patient’s RBCs and test the eluate against reagent red cells. A positive eluate with a warm autoantibody specificity supports clinically significant IgG‑mediated haemolysis. A negative or nonreactive eluate argues against pathogenic antibody-mediated destruction.

3) Detailed immunohematology: repeat and expand DAT to include IgG subclass and complement (C3d), do an indirect antiglobulin test/antibody screen, test for alloantibodies or recent transfusion history, and perform thermal amplitude/cold agglutinin testing if indicated. Strong IgG and complement binding correlate better with haemolysis.

4) Corroborating marrow and iron studies: bone marrow morphology (megaloblastic changes, dyserythropoiesis), iron studies showing increased ferritin and marrow iron with ring sideroblasts if present, and measures of ineffective erythropoiesis (e.g., erythropoietin level, marrow iron staining) help attribute anaemia to intramedullary destruction or defective maturation.

5) Exclude other causes that can cause a positive DAT: medication review (penicillins, cephalosporins, rifampin, NSAIDs), recent IVIG or transfusion, and infectious triggers. Consider flow cytometry for PNH if haemolysis features are present.

6) Functional and longitudinal assessment: document clinical course and response to therapies. Immune haemolysis typically improves with immunosuppression (eg, corticosteroids) whereas correction of vitamin B12/folate or treating the underlying marrow disorder will correct ineffective erythropoiesis; a therapeutic trial can be informative when diagnostic testing is equivocal.

In short: a positive DAT requires correlation with evidence of peripheral haemolysis and an appropriate marrow reticulocyte response. In this patient the low reticulocyte count, marrow erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective maturation and iron loading point to ineffective erythropoiesis as the dominant process; the DAT likely reflects low‑grade or nonpathogenic IgG coating. The most decisive additional tests are an eluate study and a repeat, contemporaneous panel of haemolysis markers plus a reticulocyte production index to determine whether antibody‑mediated peripheral destruction is clinically significant.

### [Pathophysiology] Why are serum iron and ferritin often elevated in severe vitamin B12 deficiency with ineffective erythropoiesis?

Think of severe vitamin B12 (or folate) deficiency as a factory with a production-line defect: the marrow makes many erythroid precursors but they cannot complete maturation, so they die within the marrow. That intramedullary destruction (ineffective erythropoiesis or “intramedullary hemolysis”) has two linked consequences that raise serum iron and ferritin.

First, dying erythroblasts dump their haemoglobin-derived iron back into the marrow and circulation. That recycled iron increases serum iron and transferrin saturation even while the patient is anaemic and reticulocyte output is low — exactly what you see with a low retic count and erythroid hyperplasia on the marrow. Second, ineffective erythropoiesis suppresses hepcidin (via erythroid regulators such as erythroferrone), permitting greater iron release from macrophages and increased intestinal iron absorption; both actions raise circulating iron and load iron into stores. Ferritin therefore is often high because iron stores are actually increased, and ferritin is also an acute‑phase reactant that rises with inflammation or liver injury.

In this case the picture fits: marked B12 deficiency with erythroid hyperplasia, low retic, elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin (markers of intramedullary destruction), and marrow iron overload. Coexistent hepatic fibrosis and inflammation can further elevate ferritin independent of iron status. Clinically, this explains why a high ferritin/serum iron in a patient with anaemia does not exclude an iron‑restricted process — you must integrate reticulocyte response, transferrin saturation, marrow iron or soluble transferrin receptor, and the overall clinical context to distinguish true iron overload from ineffective erythropoiesis.

### [Morning Report] What are the gold standard investigations to confirm a suspected telomere biology disorder, and how are telomere length results typically interpreted?

In a patient like this — young, multisystem fibrosis with cytopenias and a PARN variant — the diagnostic approach has two complementary “gold‑standard” components: objective telomere length measurement and comprehensive germline genetic testing, interpreted in the context of age and clinical features.

Telomere length measurement
The gold‑standard laboratory test is flow‑FISH (flow cytometry combined with fluorescence in situ hybridisation) performed on peripheral blood leukocyte subsets. Flow‑FISH provides age‑adjusted telomere length distributions for total lymphocytes and for specific subsets (CD3+ T cells, CD4/8, NK cells, B cells) and is reproducible between centres when standardised. Quantitative PCR assays are widely used for screening but are less precise, poorly reproducible across labs, and should not replace flow‑FISH for diagnostic confirmation.

How results are interpreted
Telomere length must be read against age‑matched reference percentiles. In adults and children alike, telomere lengths below the first percentile for age (especially in lymphocyte subsets) are highly suggestive of a telomere biology disorder and support a diagnosis when coupled with compatible clinical features. Telomere lengths between the 1st and 10th percentiles are indeterminate but raise suspicion and warrant genetic testing and clinical correlation. Normal telomere length does not completely exclude a pathogenic telomere‑gene variant — partial loss‑of‑function variants, somatic mosaicism, or technical/sample issues (recent transfusion, bone marrow failure with marrow‑derived leukopenia, or post‑transplant samples) can confound results. When peripheral blood is unreliable (for example after transfusion or if marrow failure severely alters leukocyte populations), testing telomere length in nonhaematopoietic tissue (skin fibroblasts or buccal epithelial cells) can better reflect germline telomere length.

Genetic testing
Definitive confirmation rests on germline sequencing of telomere‑related genes. A targeted multigene panel (including TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, TINF2 and others) or broad approaches (clinical exome or genome sequencing with analysis for telomere genes) are the standard. Findings classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in a compatible gene establish the diagnosis. Variants of uncertain significance (as in this case) require integration of telomere length data, segregation analysis in family members, functional data when available, and expert variant curation. Because of reduced penetrance and variable expressivity, testing family members and considering non‑blood tissue for sequencing (to detect mosaicism) are often necessary.

Putting the two together
The most reliable diagnostic evidence is short telomeres by flow‑FISH together with a pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) germline variant in a telomere maintenance gene. Short telomeres alone in the appropriate clinical context are strongly supportive and justify genetic testing and management changes (surveillance, avoidance of genotoxic exposures, transplant planning). A monoallelic variant with borderline telomere shortening or normal telomeres requires careful longitudinal follow‑up, family studies, and, if available, functional assays or referral to a specialized telomere centre for adjudication.

In practice for this patient: obtain flow‑FISH on age‑matched lymphocyte subsets (and consider fibroblast telomere length if blood is confounded), pursue targeted germline panel or confirmatory testing of the PARN variant (and family segregation), and integrate those results to guide counselling, surveillance and therapy.

### [Pathophysiology] Summarize the role of PARN in telomere maintenance, including its impact on TERC maturation, and contrast the typical phenotypes of monoallelic versus biallelic PARN variants.

PARN is an essential nuclear deadenylase whose activity is critical for the biogenesis and stability of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). During TERC transcription some molecules acquire short oligo(A) tails that mark them for degradation; PARN removes these oligo(A) additions at the 3′ end, permitting proper 3′-end maturation and accumulation of stable, functional TERC. Loss of PARN activity therefore lowers mature TERC levels, reduces telomerase holoenzyme activity, and accelerates telomere shortening in highly proliferative tissues. Because telomere maintenance depends on adequate TERC, even partial loss of PARN can have outsized effects on stem-cell compartments (bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver) that rely on telomerase to preserve replicative capacity.

The clinical consequences reflect this molecular dose‑dependence. Biallelic (recessive) PARN pathogenic variants typically produce an early, severe telomere biology disorder phenotype — classically autosomal‑recessive dyskeratosis congenita or Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson–like presentations — with childhood onset, marked mucocutaneous stigmata, profound bone‑marrow failure, cerebellar or developmental abnormalities and very short telomeres. By contrast, monoallelic (heterozygous) PARN variants usually manifest later, with incomplete penetrance and a narrower, organ‑predominant phenotype: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and other fibrotic lung disease, adult‑onset bone‑marrow failure or cytopenias, and increasingly recognised hepatic fibrosis. Monoallelic disease is best understood as haploinsufficiency — reduced TERC and telomerase reserve that renders tissues vulnerable to additional stresses (nutritional deficiency, inflammation, environmental exposures), producing variable clinical expression as illustrated by the patient in this report.

Clinically this distinction matters because biallelic carriers are at high risk for early, progressive multisystem failure and require intensive surveillance and early consideration of curative therapy (for example, transplant strategies tailored to short‑telomere biology), whereas heterozygous carriers often present as adults with organ‑specific fibrosis or milder marrow disease, have variable penetrance, and benefit from targeted surveillance (pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic) and counselling about environmental risks and family testing. Measurement of telomere length and comprehensive genetic evaluation help place a given PARN variant in context and guide prognosis and management.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how defective telomere maintenance leads to organ fibrosis in the lung and liver, focusing on cellular senescence, impaired epithelial regeneration, and profibrotic signaling.

Think of telomere maintenance defects as a cellular “replicative frailty” that converts normal wound repair into a fibrotic, non‑resolving program. In this patient with a monoallelic PARN defect the root problem is insufficient telomerase function (PARN is required for maturation of the telomerase RNA component), so highly proliferative epithelial compartments exhaust telomere reserves early. That drives three linked pathophysiologic steps that produce organ fibrosis.

First, critically short telomeres trigger a persistent DNA‑damage response in epithelial and progenitor cells, inducing cellular senescence or apoptosis rather than productive proliferation. Senescent cells are not inert: they adopt a proinflammatory, profibrotic secretome (the SASP) that includes interleukins (IL‑6, IL‑1), chemokines and growth factors such as TGF‑β and PDGF. This milieu recruits and activates fibroblasts and immune cells and biases the tissue toward matrix deposition.

Second, loss of functional epithelial progenitors (alveolar type 2 cells in the lung; hepatocytes and cholangiocyte progenitors in the liver) impairs normal regeneration. When stem/progenitor cells cannot re‑epithelialize injured areas, repair defaults to a fibroblast‑dominated process. In the lung this manifests as honeycombing and progressive interstitial fibrosis; in the liver it promotes stellate‑cell activation, replacement of parenchyma with scar and architectural distortion (cirrhosis). The clinical consequence in a young patient is premature organ failure despite the absence of classic chronic insults.

Third, the SASP and dying epithelial cells amplify profibrotic signaling and alter cell–cell crosstalk so that fibroblasts differentiate into persistent myofibroblasts, resist apoptosis, and continuously lay down extracellular matrix. Telomere dysfunction also promotes low‑grade chronic inflammation and microvascular rarefaction, which sustain hypoxia and further fibrogenic signaling. Together these processes make fibrosis self‑perpetuating and poorly reversible.

Clinically, this pathophysiology explains why telomere biology disorders present with a combination of marrow failure (loss of hematopoietic reserve), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis in young adults: the same telomere defect compromises distinct epithelial stem cell pools, shifts healing toward fibrosis via SASP/TGF‑β pathways, and produces progressive organ scarring. Therapeutically and prognostically this matters because fibrosis in this setting is driven by an intrinsic regenerative failure rather than a removable external insult—surveillance for progressive dysfunction, avoidance of additional replicative stressors (cytotoxic drugs, smoking), early consideration of organ‑directed therapies (transplant) or experimental telomere‑directed approaches are the logical clinical corollaries.

### [Morning Report] In an adult with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis and cytopenias, outline the key steps of the initial workup to distinguish acquired from inherited causes before ordering genetic testing.

Start by pausing on the reflex to “just sequence” and do a focused, high‑yield clinical and laboratory triage that separates reversible or acquired explanations from true germline syndromes and that will also guide which genetic test (if any) is appropriate and which tissue to test.

First re‑ask the basic clinical questions and examine for syndromic clues: age at onset, tempo of cytopenias, family history of cytopenia, early greying, liver or pulmonary fibrosis, premature malignancy, or unexplained deaths. Look carefully for subtle mucocutaneous signs of telomere disease (nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, reticular pigmentation), non‑hematologic features (short stature, osteoporosis, oesophageal strictures, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis, pulmonary emphysema) and for evidence of premature ageing — these point toward an inherited telomere biology disorder (TBD) rather than an acquired process.

Concurrently exclude common and reversible acquired causes. Basic lab and non‑genetic testing should include a complete blood count with differential and peripheral smear, reticulocyte count, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, B12/folate/iron studies, thyroid function, viral serologies (HIV, hepatitis B/C, EBV/CMV as indicated), autoimmune serologies when clinically suggested, and a medication/toxin exposure review (chemotherapy, alcohol, alkylators, benzene, occupational exposures). Correct nutritional deficiencies and reassess marrow response before concluding an intrinsic marrow failure.

Obtain bone marrow evaluation early if cytopenias are persistent or unexplained: marrow cellularity, cytomorphology for dysplasia, iron stores, fibrosis, and assessment for infiltrative disease. Send conventional cytogenetics (karyotype) and single‑nucleotide polymorphism microarray/FISH panels that detect MDS‑defining abnormalities. Because acquired clonal hematopoiesis and myeloid malignancies mimic inherited marrow failure, add a targeted somatic mutation panel (myeloid NGS panel for TP53, DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, DDX41, RUNX1, etc.) — a clonal somatic mutation or MDS cytogenetic lesion points to an acquired or age‑related process rather than a straightforward germline syndrome.

Image and function test the affected organs to document the pattern and severity: high‑resolution CT chest and pulmonary function tests for interstitial lung disease, liver elastography/ultrasound and hepatic synthetic function tests for fibrosis/cirrhosis, and DEXA for bone disease. The combination of early pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis with marrow failure in a young adult makes a TBD much more likely than isolated acquired disease.

If a TBD is on the differential, perform a telomere length assay (flow‑FISH on leukocyte subsets or other validated method). Critically short telomeres for age (<1st percentile) strongly support a telomere disorder and justify germline genetic testing targeted to telomere genes; normal telomere length makes TBD unlikely and lowers the pretest probability for germline telomere gene sequencing. Flow‑FISH is a functional test that helps prioritize testing and interpret variants of uncertain significance.

When you move to genetic testing, choose the right test and specimen. Start with a focused germline gene panel for marrow failure and telomere biology genes rather than an unselected tumor panel or blood‑only sequencing, because blood may harbour somatic mutations that confound interpretation. If a potentially pathogenic germline variant is identified in blood, confirm germline status by testing a non‑hematopoietic tissue (cultured skin fibroblasts or buccal swab/saliva with caution) to exclude somatic mosaicism or clonal hematopoiesis. Negative or uncertain results on gene panels can be escalated to whole‑exome/genome sequencing with concurrent telomere testing and segregation studies in relatives when clinically indicated.

Finally, integrate the results into management: if acquired causes are identified, treat them; if telomere shortening and a germline mutation are confirmed, counsel about transplant risks (reduced‑intensity conditioning), malignancy surveillance, family testing, and organ surveillance. Throughout, involve haemato‑genetics or a specialized inherited bone marrow failure clinic early — their input helps pick the right tests and interpret VUS in the clinical context.

In short: targeted history/physical to look for syndromic features; exclude reversible acquired causes and treat deficiencies; marrow exam with cytogenetics and somatic NGS to look for acquired clonal disease; organ imaging and functional tests; flow‑FISH telomere length if suspicion persists; then germline testing with appropriate specimen choice and specialist genetic counselling. These steps maximize diagnostic yield and avoid misinterpretation of variants.

### [Morning Report] A monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) is reported as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. What additional evidence could support reclassification toward likely pathogenicity?

For a missense variant like PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) to be upgraded from VUS toward likely pathogenic, you need evidence that moves it from computational and positional support to direct biological and clinical causation. The most persuasive additional data would be a combination of well‑established functional evidence showing that the variant impairs PARN’s role in telomere biology, plus human genetic evidence that ties the variant to disease. Concretely:

1) Functional assays (ACMG PS3): Demonstrate that the variant reduces PARN function in assays that are accepted for this gene. Examples include reduced PARN protein expression/stability, decreased deadenylase activity, defective processing/maturation of TERC RNA (lower mature TERC levels), and impaired telomerase activity in patient cells or engineered cell lines. A complementation experiment in PARN‑deficient cells where wild‑type PARN rescues TERC maturation/telomerase activity but the p.Cys205Arg allele fails to rescue provides strong, specific evidence.

2) Telomere biology phenotype in patient cells (supporting PS3/PP4): Markedly short leukocyte telomere length measured by a validated method (flow‑FISH or Southern blot of terminal restriction fragments), ideally below the first percentile for age, supports a telomere biology disorder attributable to the variant. Demonstrating short telomeres in multiple affected tissues (blood, fibroblasts) strengthens causality.

3) RNA studies if relevant (PM4/PS3): If the variant affects splicing or RNA stability, show altered PARN transcript by RT‑PCR or reduced mRNA levels; if not splicing, showing that the substitution perturbs a known functional domain (R3H) with demonstrable molecular consequences is useful.

4) Segregation and de novo data (ACMG PM6/PP1): Co‑segregation of the variant with disease in multiple affected family members, or documentation of a de novo occurrence in the proband (with confirmed parentage), provides strong genetic evidence. For monoallelic variants in an incompletely penetrant adult‑onset disorder, multiple‑individual segregation or occurrence in unrelated affected individuals is particularly informative.

5) Case enrichment / recurrence (PS4): Identification of the same variant (or same residue alteration) in additional unrelated patients with compatible TBD phenotypes, and enrichment of rare PARN missense variants in case cohorts versus population controls, supports pathogenicity.

6) Absence / rarity in population databases + gene constraint (already present as PM2/PP2): Confirm and document the variant’s absence or extreme rarity in gnomAD and other population datasets, and place this in context of PARN’s intolerance to missense variation.

7) In vivo or biochemical corroboration (PS3/other supportive): Animal models or patient‑derived induced pluripotent stem cell models that recapitulate marrow or pulmonary phenotypes with the variant provide very strong mechanistic evidence.

In practice, the most actionable pathway is: measure telomere length (flow‑FISH), measure mature TERC levels and telomerase activity in patient leukocytes or fibroblasts, perform PARN protein/activity assays and complementation studies in cell lines, and pursue family testing to seek segregation or de novo status. If those studies show impaired PARN function, deficient TERC maturation/telomerase activity and very short telomeres in the proband (and the variant is absent in controls), the ACMG criteria would be met for likely pathogenic (PS3 + PM1/PM2 + PP1/PP3/PP4 depending on results).

### [Morning Report] Which ACMG/AMP evidence codes were applicable to the PARN variant in this case (PM1, PM2, PP2, PP3), and what types of data would provide moderate or strong evidence (e.g., PS3, PP1) to upgrade classification?

Short answer: the authors appropriately applied PM1, PM2, PP2 and PP3 to the PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) change. What would move this variant from VUS toward likely pathogenic or pathogenic are well‑validated functional or genetic data that meet ACMG moderate (PMx) or strong (PSx) criteria — in practice those are demonstrable loss of PARN function and robust segregation / case enrichment.

Why those four codes fit here: PM1 — the substitution lies in the R3H functional domain that is constrained for variation; PM2 — the change is absent/very rare in population databases; PP2 — PARN is intolerant of benign missense and missense is a known disease mechanism; PP3 — multiple in‑silico tools predict a deleterious effect. Those criteria are supportive but not sufficient without stronger evidence.

What would provide moderate or strong evidence (and examples):

1) Strong functional evidence (PS3): well‑validated, disease‑relevant assays showing a deleterious effect attributable to the variant. For PARN this would include reproducible reductions in PARN enzymatic activity or protein stability in patient cells or engineered systems, impaired 3′‑end maturation of TERC, reduced TERC levels, decreased telomerase activity (TRAP assay), demonstration of accelerated telomere shortening in vitro, or abnormal cellular phenotypes (senescence/apoptosis) rescued by wild‑type but not mutant PARN. Flow‑FISH telomere length alone is not definitive, but dramatically short telomeres in peripheral blood compared with age‑matched controls (confirmed by a validated assay) can be strong supportive evidence when linked to a functional deficit.

2) Strong genetic evidence from multiple unrelated affected individuals (PS4): significant case enrichment of the variant in well‑phenotyped TBD/IPF/BMFS cohorts compared with controls, or multiple unrelated probands with concordant phenotypes carrying the same rare variant.

3) Strong de novo evidence (PS2): confirmed de novo occurrence (paternity/maternity confirmed) in a patient with a compatible phenotype — for dominant/haploinsufficient mechanisms this is strong evidence.

4) Moderate to strong segregation evidence (PP1 → PM or PS level): familial co‑segregation of the variant with disease across multiple informative meioses. Under ACMG rules PP1 can be upgraded to moderate or strong depending on the number of segregations (several affected family members across meioses would be moderate/strong).

5) Moderate population/gene‑specific evidence (PM3 is for recessive loci and would not apply here): for a dominant/haploinsufficient gene, demonstration that this residue/domain is a mutational hotspot or that other pathogenic missense changes at the same residue exist (PM5) could add moderate weight.

In short, to upgrade this PARN missense variant you would want either a validated functional readout showing loss of PARN/TERC maturation/telomerase function (PS3), robust co‑segregation or confirmed de novo occurrence (PS2/PP1 upgraded), or case‑level enrichment across unrelated affected patients (PS4). Combining one strong line (e.g., PS3) with the existing moderate/supporting criteria would typically move a VUS to likely pathogenic.

### [Pathophysiology] List at least five telomere-related genes besides PARN that are commonly implicated in adult-onset telomere biology disorders, and indicate the most frequent inheritance pattern.

The common adult‑onset telomere biology disorders are most often caused by defects that impair telomerase or telomere maintenance. Besides PARN, five frequently implicated genes are TERT, TERC, RTEL1, DKC1 and TINF2. TERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase) and TERC (the telomerase RNA component) cause disease by haploinsufficiency of telomerase activity, RTEL1 (a helicase required for telomere replication) alters telomere stability, DKC1 (dyskerin) is required for telomerase RNA stability and ribonucleoprotein assembly, and TINF2 (a shelterin complex subunit) disrupts telomere protection. Clinically these defects produce short telomeres and predispose to pulmonary fibrosis, bone‑marrow failure and hepatic fibrosis.

The most frequent inheritance pattern across adult‑onset TBDs is autosomal dominant with variable penetrance (haploinsufficiency or dominant‑negative effects are common mechanisms). Important exceptions to bear in mind are DKC1, which is classically X‑linked recessive, and several genes such as CTC1 (associated with Coats plus) that more often give autosomal recessive disease. RTEL1 and some other genes can present with either dominant or recessive patterns depending on the variant. In practice, therefore, genetic counselling should consider autosomal dominant inheritance as the default for adult presentations, but pedigrees and specific gene/variant data are essential because of these notable exceptions.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What features differentiate bone marrow failure associated with telomere biology disorders from aplastic anemia due to other causes, and how might somatic genetic rescue modify the clinical phenotype?

When you see pancytopenia in a young adult, the first practical question is whether you are dealing with an acquired aplastic process (immune-mediated aplastic anemia, drugs, infection) or an inherited marrow failure syndrome such as a telomere biology disorder (TBD). The distinction matters because the diagnostic approach, response to therapy and transplant planning differ.

Bone marrow failure from telomere biology disorders is distinguished from other causes by a pattern of clinical, laboratory and genetic clues rather than a single pathognomonic test. Clinically, TBDs often present at a younger age than typical idiopathic aplastic anemia and commonly carry multisystem stigmata of premature cellular senescence: premature greying or hair loss, nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia (when present), unexplained pulmonary fibrosis or hepatic fibrosis, early-onset osteoporosis, and sometimes neurodevelopmental or vascular findings. A family history of early lung or liver disease, bone marrow failure, or premature death raises suspicion, but incomplete penetrance and de novo changes are common.

Laboratory and marrow features are also different in predictable ways. TBD-associated marrow disease can be heterogeneous — patients may have isolated cytopenias for years, macrocytosis or ineffective erythropoiesis rather than a uniformly hypocellular marrow. In other words, marrow cellularity can be hypocellular, normal or even hypercellular with dyspoiesis/ineffective haematopoiesis; this contrasts with classic immune aplastic anemia, where marrow hypocellularity and fat replacement are typical. Peripheral clues—low reticulocyte count out of proportion to marrow cellularity, macrocytosis, elevated mean corpuscular volume, or marrow erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective maturation—point toward stem‑cell replicative exhaustion as seen in TBDs and can be confounded by nutritional deficiencies (as in this case). Importantly, TBD patients are more likely to have extra‑hematologic organ fibrosis (lung, liver) and increased risk of clonal haematopoiesis, myeloid malignancy and mucocutaneous cancers.

Definitive distinction rests on specialized testing: measurement of telomere length (flow-FISH) showing very short age‑adjusted telomeres in leukocytes, and germline genetic testing for pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, DKC1, etc.). Because hematopoietic clones can be selected for or against, telomere length and sequencing performed on peripheral blood can be misleading; testing nonhematopoietic tissue (skin fibroblasts, buccal cells) is often necessary. Clinically relevant consequences follow: TBDs generally respond poorly to immunosuppression compared with idiopathic immune aplastic anemia, and hematopoietic stem cell transplant requires reduced‑intensity, telomere‑aware conditioning and careful donor selection because standard myeloablative regimens cause excessive toxicity (pulmonary and hepatic) in TBD patients.

Somatic genetic rescue complicates the picture and explains some surprising clinical courses. Hematopoietic cells can acquire somatic mutations that restore telomere maintenance or otherwise improve replicative fitness of a clone (for example, somatic reversion mutations in the mutant allele or activating mutations in telomerase pathway genes). When this happens, the patient may show spontaneous improvement or mosaic normalization of blood counts despite an underlying germline defect. That rescue can mask a germline diagnosis because blood telomere length and sequencing may look less abnormal; conversely, a rescued clone may dominate the hematopoietic compartment and carry additional somatic lesions that increase the risk of clonal hematopoiesis or myeloid neoplasia. Clinically, somatic rescue therefore produces three important implications: (1) transient or mosaic improvement of cytopenias should not exclude a germline TBD, (2) testing of nonhematopoietic tissue is required to unmask germline defects, and (3) rescued clones can have oncogenic potential, so surveillance for clonal evolution is essential.

Apply these principles to the case: a young patient with pancytopenia and multisystem fibrosis should trigger telomere length measurement and germline testing even when classical mucocutaneous signs are limited and when reversible causes (B12 deficiency) are present. A monoallelic PARN variant in blood may explain pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis and make the marrow vulnerable to stressors; but because somatic rescue or clonal selection can alter blood findings, confirmatory testing off‑blood tissue and cautious planning for transplant or immunosuppression (if considered) are required.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how severe vitamin B12 deficiency could unmask or exacerbate cytopenias in a person with an underlying telomere biology disorder.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency and a telomere biology disorder (TBD) can interact in a straightforward but clinically important way: the deficiency serves as an acute replicative and DNA‑synthetic stressor that unmasks the limited regenerative reserve of telomere‑shortened haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, precipitating or worsening cytopenias.

Physiology and pathophysiology tied together: telomeres set the replicative ceiling for stem cells. In TBDs (eg, PARN, TERT, TERC defects) haematopoietic stem cells have abnormally short telomeres and enter senescence or apoptosis after fewer divisions. Vitamin B12 deficiency produces a cell‑intrinsic block in DNA synthesis (impaired thymidine synthesis and defective S‑phase progression), leading to ineffective erythropoiesis, intramedullary apoptosis, and increased marrow demand for compensatory proliferation. In a person with normal telomere reserve the marrow can usually meet that demand; in someone with a TBD the extra proliferative burden rapidly exhausts a limited stem‑cell pool so that ineffective erythropoiesis becomes frank pancytopenia with a poor reticulocyte response.

There are several synergistic mechanisms that explain the clinical picture in this case. First, B12 deficiency increases DNA strand breaks, activates p53 pathways and raises intramedullary apoptosis — pathways that overlap with telomere‑shortening–induced senescence. Second, ineffective erythropoiesis releases iron and increases marrow iron stores (explaining high ferritin/serum iron despite anemia), which can further stress the marrow microenvironment. Third, the marrow’s blunted reticulocyte response reflects both the ineffective erythropoiesis caused by B12 lack and an exhausted progenitor compartment from telomere dysfunction. Finally, marrow findings that improve only partially or transiently after vitamin repletion suggest an underlying fixed deficit of stem‑cell reserve rather than purely nutritional disease.

Clinical implications and principles: always correct reversible contributors (B12, folate, drugs, infections) because they can be the proximate cause of cytopenias and may temporarily improve counts. But persistent, disproportionate, or recurrent cytopenias after correction should prompt evaluation for inherited marrow failure/telomere disorders (telomere length testing, targeted gene panels, genetic counselling). Recognize the “two‑hit” model: an acquired stressor unmasks a constitutional defect. This distinction matters for prognosis and management — patients with TBDs need long‑term surveillance for progressive bone marrow failure, fibrotic organ disease and special considerations for therapies (eg, reduced‑intensity conditioning if HSCT is contemplated).

### [Morning Report] In suspected pernicious anemia contributing to B12 deficiency, which serologic and histologic tests would you obtain to demonstrate autoimmune gastritis and intrinsic factor deficiency?

When you suspect pernicious anemia as the cause of B12 deficiency you want serologic evidence of autoimmune destruction of oxyntic mucosa and intrinsic-factor (IF) deficiency, and histologic confirmation of autoimmune (type A) gastritis in the gastric body/fundus.

Obtain serologic tests: anti‑parietal cell antibodies (APCA) and anti‑intrinsic‑factor antibodies (both blocking and binding types) — anti‑IF antibodies are more specific for pernicious anemia, APCA are more sensitive but less specific. Measure fasting serum gastrin (markedly elevated when parietal cells are lost) and serum pepsinogen I and the pepsinogen I/II ratio (low PG I and low ratio support gastric corpus atrophy). Test for Helicobacter pylori (breath, stool antigen, or biopsy) to exclude H. pylori–associated atrophic gastritis. Continue routine confirmation of B12 deficiency with serum B12 plus methylmalonic acid (and homocysteine as needed).

Obtain targeted histology from endoscopic biopsies of the gastric body and fundus (not just antral biopsies): expect loss/atrophy of oxyntic (parietal) mucosa with decreased/absent parietal cells, chronic lymphoplasmacytic (autoimmune) inflammation, glandular atrophy, intestinal or pseudopyloric metaplasia, and enterochromaffin‑like (ECL) cell hyperplasia (reflecting hypergastrinemia). Immunostains (H+/K+‑ATPase for parietal cells or chromogranin A for ECL cells) can be helpful in difficult cases.

Why these tests: anti‑IF and anti‑parietal cell antibodies demonstrate autoimmune targeting of IF and parietal cells; gastrin and pepsinogen are functional biomarkers of corpus atrophy; biopsy documents the anatomic substrate (parietal cell loss, atrophy, ECL hyperplasia) and excludes H. pylori or other pathology. Confirming autoimmune gastritis has implications for lifelong B12 replacement, surveillance for gastric neoplasia (adenocarcinoma and type I neuroendocrine tumors), and for evaluating other autoimmune disorders.

### [Management] What are key management priorities for a patient with suspected telomere biology disorder and evolving marrow failure, including transfusion strategy, infection prevention, and referral for transplant evaluation?

Key management priorities in a patient with a suspected telomere biology disorder (TBD) and evolving marrow failure are aimed at preserving marrow- and transplant‑related options, preventing infectious and transfusion‑related complications, and arranging prompt specialty evaluation. The reasoning is that TBDs are progressive, multisystem disorders in which supportive care can alter short‑term risk but definitive therapy (allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplant, HCT) is time‑sensitive and more successful when candidates are referred early and have had minimal sensitizing exposures or organ‑toxic therapies.

Transfusion strategy
Limit transfusions to what is necessary. Use a restrictive, symptom‑guided transfusion strategy (for stable patients, avoid routine RBC transfusion unless hemoglobin is clinically intolerable or <7–8 g/dL depending on symptoms and comorbidity). Each transfusion risks alloimmunization and iron overload and can complicate later HCT donor selection and engraftment; therefore minimize exposures whenever safely possible. When transfusions are required, give leukoreduced, irradiated blood products to reduce febrile reactions and prevent transfusion‑associated graft‑versus‑host disease in an at‑risk patient. Prefer CMV‑seronegative products and single‑donor platelet components when feasible (particularly if the patient is childbearing‑age and HCT is contemplated). Monitor and quantify cumulative iron burden (ferritin, MRI liver iron) and plan iron chelation if transfusion dependence develops.

Cytopenia‑specific thresholds and adjuncts
Follow standard prophylactic thresholds: prophylactic platelet transfusion for counts <10 × 10^9/L (raise threshold to ~20 × 10^9/L for fever, bleeding risk or invasive procedures); transfuse for active bleeding or procedural needs regardless of number. Use growth factors cautiously: G‑CSF may be used for severe neutropenia with infection but can have uncertain long‑term benefit and theoretical risks in TBDs; consult hematology. Consider erythropoiesis‑stimulating agents only after assessing marrow reserve and underlying causes (nutritional deficiency corrected in this case).

Infection prevention and vaccinations
Because marrow failure increases infection risk and many TBD patients later receive immunosuppression, institute standard prevention measures: rigorous oral/dental hygiene, skin care, and prompt evaluation/treatment of febrile neutropenia. In patients with neutrophils <500/µL consider antibacterial prophylaxis per local practice (eg, fluoroquinolone) and antifungal or antiviral prophylaxis guided by duration/severity of neutropenia and institutional protocols. Provide Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis if patient receives immunosuppressive therapy. Avoid live‑attenuated vaccines; give age‑appropriate inactivated vaccines (seasonal influenza, pneumococcal conjugate and polysaccharide series if not previously given, hepatitis B if seronegative). Ensure household contacts are immunized. Screen and treat latent viral infections (HBV, HCV, CMV) before HCT planning.

Multisystem assessment and optimization
Because pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis are common and strongly influence transplant candidacy and conditioning intensity, perform formal pulmonary testing (PFTs with DLCO, high‑resolution CT, cardiopulmonary evaluation) and hepatic staging (liver imaging, elastography, synthetic function, viral serologies). Optimize nutrition, correct vitamin deficiencies (as was done for B12/folate), treat osteoporosis, and manage portal hypertension or other liver complications pre‑emptively. These organ evaluations guide HCT timing, conditioning regimen and risk counseling.

Genetic evaluation and family/donor considerations
Obtain definitive genetic testing and telomere length measurement (flow‑FISH or equivalent) to confirm the TBD and to inform prognosis. Do not use related donors until they have been genetically tested and telomere length assessed; many affected genes are dominantly inherited or have variable penetrance, and using an apparently healthy related donor with an occult pathogenic variant risks poor graft durability and donor harm. Unrelated matched donors or cord blood are often preferable if a related carrier is identified. Early discussion with genetics for family testing and reproductive counseling is essential.

Early referral for transplant evaluation
Refer to a specialized HCT program experienced with telomere disorders early—before the patient accrues heavy transfusion exposure, progressive organ dysfunction, or intolerable infections. Timing matters: HCT can be curative for marrow failure but outcomes are strongly affected by pre‑transplant lung and liver status, prior transfusions, and the conditioning regimen. Centers experienced in TBD typically use reduced‑intensity, reduced‑toxicity conditioning that minimizes alkylator or radiation exposure, and they tailor regimens to pulmonary and hepatic reserve. The transplant team will also plan donor genetic testing and discuss alternative donor strategies.

Adjunctive and interim therapies
Consider androgen therapy (eg, danazol or nandrolone) only after specialist consultation; androgens have been reported to improve blood counts in some TBD patients but have uncertain long‑term effects and hepatic toxicity—particularly important with existing hepatic fibrosis. Avoid cytotoxic immunosuppressive agents or high‑dose alkylators outside of trial contexts. Monitor for and treat iron overload, and institute fertility preservation counseling if relevant.

Surveillance and counseling
Establish regular surveillance for progressive marrow failure, pulmonary function decline, hepatic decompensation, and cancer screening per TBD guidelines. Provide counseling on environmental risks (smoking, occupational exposures), and coordinate multidisciplinary care (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, transplant).

Takeaway
Be conservative with transfusions, use leukoreduced/irradiated/CMV‑appropriate blood products, prevent infections and vaccinate with inactivated vaccines, aggressively assess and optimize lung and liver function, avoid related donors without genetic/telomere testing, and refer early to an HCT center experienced in TBDs to plan donor selection and reduced‑toxicity conditioning. Early coordination preserves curative options and reduces transplant‑related risks.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which medications and exposures should be avoided or used with caution in patients with telomere biology disorders due to increased risk of myelotoxicity or organ toxicity?

Teachable pearl: patients with telomere biology disorders have limited hematopoietic and organ reserve because short/dysfunctional telomeres impair stem‑cell replication and DNA repair. That biologic vulnerability makes them unusually sensitive to agents or exposures that cause DNA damage, replicative stress, or direct organ toxicity — so several common drugs and environmental insults should be avoided or used only with extreme caution and specialist input.

Which agents/exposures to avoid or use with caution, and why
- Alkylating chemotherapy and high‑dose DNA‑damaging chemotherapy (eg, busulfan, cyclophosphamide, melphalan, chlorambucil): these agents produce profound and often irreversible marrow injury in TBDs. If cytotoxic therapy is unavoidable, consult hematology/genetics and use reduced‑intensity or non‑myeloablative regimens with dose reductions and close monitoring.
- Ionizing radiation and myeloablative radiotherapy: radiation causes DNA breaks and accelerates marrow failure and organ toxicity; avoid therapeutic radiation when possible and minimize exposures.
- Antimetabolites and classic marrow‑suppressing agents (eg, methotrexate, azathioprine, fludarabine, cytarabine): increased risk of prolonged cytopenias and delayed marrow recovery.
- Drugs with known profound idiosyncratic marrow toxicity (eg, chloramphenicol, certain sulfonamides in susceptible patients, prolonged or high‑dose trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole): these can precipitate severe cytopenias; use alternatives or careful monitoring.
- Amiodarone and other pulmonary‑toxic drugs (eg, bleomycin, nitrofurantoin long term): TBD patients already have predisposition to pulmonary fibrosis; avoid drugs that cause or worsen interstitial lung disease.
- Hepatotoxic drugs and alcohol: many TBDs have hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis at baseline; avoid or dose‑adjust hepatotoxic agents (eg, methotrexate, high‑dose anabolic steroids, certain antifungals, high‑dose acetaminophen) and counsel strict alcohol avoidance.
- Androgens and anabolic steroids: androgens (danazol, nandrolone) can increase telomerase activity and have been used therapeutically, but they carry risks of hepatotoxicity and may worsen liver disease; use only after specialist review and with hepatic monitoring.
- Smoking, occupational toxin exposures (eg, benzene), and other environmental genotoxins: these accelerate telomere attrition and promote marrow and pulmonary injury; strong counselling to avoid.
- Consider vaccine and infection precautions: severe cytopenias or planned immunosuppression may alter timing/type of vaccines; coordinate with specialists.

Clinical approach
Before prescribing any potentially myelotoxic, hepatotoxic, or pulmonary‑toxic therapy, obtain hematology/genetics input. Use lowest effective doses, prefer non‑DNA‑damaging alternatives when possible, institute vigilant laboratory and organ monitoring, and when transplant or chemotherapy is considered choose reduced‑intensity conditioning tailored to TBDs. Counsel patients to avoid alcohol, smoking and occupational/genotoxic exposures and to report new cytopenias, respiratory or hepatic symptoms promptly.

Rationale in one line: defective telomere maintenance means reduced stem‑cell reserve and impaired DNA‑damage responses — agents that damage DNA or target organs already affected by fibrosis result in disproportionate and often irreversible toxicity.

### [Management] When should hematopoietic stem cell transplantation be considered in telomere biology disorders, and what conditioning regimen modifications are recommended to minimize toxicity?

In telomere biology disorders the decision to pursue allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is driven by the marrow phenotype and by the balance between progressive haematologic failure and the very high non‑relapse mortality these patients suffer once organ fibrosis (especially pulmonary or hepatic) is established. Offer HSCT for patients with progressive, symptomatic bone‑marrow failure — persistent severe cytopenias (neutropenia, transfusion‑dependent anaemia or thrombocytopenia), rapid decline in blood counts despite supportive care, evolution to high‑risk myelodysplastic syndrome or frank marrow aplasia — after a period of careful observation and optimisation. Importantly, refer early: transplant outcomes are substantially worse once significant pulmonary fibrosis or cirrhosis is present, so candidacy should be assessed and transplantation planned before advanced end‑organ disease develops when marrow failure is inevitable or refractory to noncurative measures (androgens, growth factors, transfusions).

Conditioning modifications: because TBD patients have short telomeres in nonhaematopoietic tissues and exaggerated sensitivity to DNA‑damaging agents, use reduced‑intensity or nonmyeloablative approaches designed to minimize endothelial, pulmonary and hepatic toxicity rather than classic myeloablative regimens. Practical principles are:

• Avoid high‑dose alkylators and high‑dose total body irradiation when possible. Full‑intensity busulfan‑based regimens and high‑dose TBI are associated with excess pulmonary and hepatic failure in TBDs.

• Use fludarabine‑based reduced‑intensity conditioning with lower‑dose alkylator or treosulfan (which has a more favorable toxicity profile than busulfan in some series). Examples in the literature include fludarabine + low‑dose busulfan or fludarabine + treosulfan ± low‑dose melphalan.

• Incorporate serotherapy (alemtuzumab or rabbit ATG) to reduce graft‑versus‑host disease and permit lower intensity conditioning.

• Tailor drug dosing carefully (weight/BSA and organ function) and avoid agents known to cause pulmonary fibrosis or hepatic veno‑occlusive disease when feasible.

• Select an unaffected donor where possible; avoid related donors who may carry the same germline variant unless they are proven not to be carriers and have normal telomere length.

Finally, before HSCT perform comprehensive pre‑transplant evaluation and optimisation: objective pulmonary function testing including DLCO and high‑resolution CT, hepatic imaging and elastography, cardiopulmonary exercise testing as indicated, and correction of reversible problems. Discuss that HSCT can cure the haematologic manifestation but will not reverse established pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis and carries higher early mortality in TBDs — hence the emphasis on early referral and individualized, reduced‑toxicity conditioning.

### [Management] What pharmacologic options are available for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis in telomere biology disorders, and what special considerations apply to antifibrotic and immunosuppressive therapies?

In patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) arising from telomere biology disorders (TBDs) the therapeutic approach is guided by the same antifibrotic principles used in sporadic IPF, but with important disease‑specific caveats because of underlying marrow and hepatic vulnerability.

Antifibrotic therapy
Both approved antifibrotic agents—pirfenidone and nintedanib—are the mainstay to slow FVC decline and are indicated in IPF regardless of telomere‑gene status. Available data (including subgroup analyses) do not show lack of efficacy in patients with telomere‑related mutations, so antifibrotics should generally be offered early. Practical considerations specific to TBDs are that nintedanib commonly causes diarrhea and can raise liver enzymes and, through platelet/function effects, increase bleeding risk; pirfenidone causes photosensitivity, nausea and can also raise transaminases. Because TBD patients frequently have cytopenias and hepatic fibrosis, choose and dose agents with those risks in mind, monitor LFTs and blood counts closely after starting therapy, and favor the drug whose side‑effect profile best fits the patient’s comorbidities. Early initiation is reasonable because disease progresses faster in many TBD patients.

Androgen/telomerase‑directed therapies
Androgens (danazol, nandrolone) and other telomerase‑modulating strategies have shown biologic activity in TBDs: small studies demonstrated telomere lengthening and hematologic responses in bone‑marrow failure. Evidence that androgens improve pulmonary outcomes is limited and anecdotal. Consider androgen therapy primarily when progressive marrow failure is present and in the context of hematology oversight or a formal trial; if used, monitor liver function, lipids, and for virilizing effects. Experimental telomerase activators remain investigational and are not routine therapy for IPF.

Immunosuppressive therapy and cytotoxic agents
Standard immunosuppression regimens used previously in IPF (the combination of prednisone, azathioprine and N‑acetylcysteine) were shown to be harmful in IPF (PANTHER) and should be avoided. In TBD patients this principle is amplified: chronic immunosuppression and cytotoxic agents (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate) can precipitate or worsen marrow failure, increase infection risk, and aggravate hepatic injury. Use of high‑dose steroids for acute exacerbations remains controversial; if used, do so with awareness of infectious and metabolic risks and with close hematologic monitoring.

Transplant and post‑transplant immunosuppression
Referral for lung transplant should occur early because of younger age at presentation and rapid progression in some TBD patients. Pre‑transplant evaluation must include hematology and hepatology assessment—TBD patients have higher risks of post‑transplant cytopenias, poor wound healing, liver dysfunction and infections. Post‑transplant immunosuppression frequently needs tailoring: consider reduced‑intensity regimens, avoid myelosuppressive agents when possible, monitor counts vigilantly, and plan for multidisciplinary management (hematology for growth‑factor support or HSCT evaluation if marrow failure evolves).

Practical recommendations for this patient
Begin an antifibrotic (choice individualized: if significant thrombocytopenia or bleeding risk, favor pirfenidone; if severe photosensitivity or intolerance to pirfenidone, favor nintedanib), obtain baseline LFTs and full blood count and recheck early and frequently. Do not start chronic azathioprine/mycophenolate or routine corticosteroid maintenance for fibrosis. Engage hematology and hepatology prior to any cytotoxic or immunosuppressive therapy and before transplant listing. Discuss the limited, off‑label role of androgens for marrow disease only in a multidisciplinary setting with informed consent and monitoring.

In short: use approved antifibrotics early, avoid routine immunosuppression/cytotoxic agents because of proven harm in IPF and amplified risk in TBDs, consider androgens only under specialist care for hematologic indications, and coordinate care tightly with hematology/hepatology when contemplating transplant or any immunosuppressive strategy.

### [Management] Outline pre-transplant considerations for lung or liver transplantation in telomere biology disorders, including multi-organ involvement, bone marrow reserve, and post-transplant complications.

Key principle: telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are systemic, not organ-limited, and that systemic vulnerability drives both pre‑transplant risk and post‑transplant complications. When a patient with short‑telomere phenotype (as in this PARN case) is being considered for lung or liver transplant, management must shift from single‑organ thinking to staged, multidisciplinary risk assessment and mitigation focused on marrow reserve, extra‑hepatic/extra‑pulmonary disease, and the recognised idiosyncratic responses of TBD patients to conditioning, surgery and immunosuppression.

First, confirm the diagnosis and define disease biology. Genetic confirmation (targeted panel/clinical exome) and quantitative telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) are essential because they stratify risk: shorter telomeres and pathogenic variants in telomerase‑related genes predict higher peri‑operative and hematologic complications. In this case the monoallelic PARN variant and the clinical phenotype should prompt formal telomere length measurement and family screening prior to listing.

Second, comprehensive multisystem staging is mandatory. Characterize bone marrow reserve with serial CBCs, reticulocyte counts, bone marrow aspirate/biopsy (cellularity, dysplasia, fibrosis), cytogenetics and molecular studies; quantify transfusion dependence and response to hematinic therapy. Evaluate liver disease severity with labs, imaging, transient elastography (already done here), portal hypertension assessment, and hepatology review to determine isolated liver transplant versus combined transplant candidacy. Assess pulmonary physiology, pulmonary hypertension, and exercise capacity for transplant candidacy and timing. Screen for occult viral infections, HPV and dust‑related pathogens, and perform malignancy surveillance (skin, hematologic and aerodigestive tracts) because TBDs increase cancer risk.

Third, integrate marrow findings into decision making about organ transplant versus hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT). Significant marrow failure or progressive cytopenias may favour HSCT (potentially curative for marrow disease) before or in lieu of solid‑organ transplant, but HSCT conditioning is especially toxic in TBDs. If marrow reserve is marginal but not failing (as in this patient whose cytopenia improved with B12), accept that solid‑organ transplant may be feasible but the peri‑operative risk of progressive marrow failure, bleeding, and infection is higher. In practice this requires hematology input to define thresholds (e.g., platelet count targets, transfusion strategies) and contingency plans for post‑op marrow deterioration, including low‑intensity HSCT regimens if needed.

Fourth, tailor peri‑operative and immunosuppressive strategies to minimize marrow and hepatic toxicity. Avoid or reduce exposure to myelosuppressive agents and alkylators whenever possible. Use the least marrow‑toxic induction and maintenance regimens compatible with graft survival (for example, minimizing or avoiding thymoglobulin or high‑dose azathioprine; carefully weigh calcineurin inhibitor nephrotoxicity versus mTOR inhibitors which can impair wound healing). Anticipate and plan for prolonged cytopenias: line up transfusion support, consider prophylactic growth factors judiciously (G‑CSF in infection risk balanced against possible clonal selection), and maintain close drug‑level monitoring because altered protein binding or hepatic dysfunction may change pharmacokinetics.

Fifth, pre‑emptively manage infection and wound‑healing risk. TBD patients have impaired epithelial regenerative capacity; expect delayed wound healing, anastomotic complications, and higher risk of bronchial anastomotic failure after lung transplant. Optimize nutrition, correct vitamin deficiencies (B12, folate, vitamin D as in this patient), vaccinate pre‑transplant when possible, and plan for aggressive early prophylaxis for typical post‑transplant pathogens and opportunists. Lower thresholds for biopsy/culture and prolonged infectious disease follow‑up are prudent.

Sixth, plan for combined organ transplantation when indicated. Severe cirrhosis plus end‑stage lung disease may require combined liver–lung transplant in centres with experience, but combined procedures carry higher mortality and are rarely done in TBDs. Decisions should be individualized in a high‑volume centre after multidisciplinary review, accepting that outcomes are generally worse than for isolated organ disease.

Seventh, counsel about long‑term complications and monitoring. After transplant expect amplified risks: prolonged or progressive bone marrow failure with transfusion dependence, higher rates of cytopenias while on immunosuppression, accelerated incidence of malignancy (particularly squamous cell cancers and post‑transplant lymphoproliferative disease), and progressive fibrosis in non‑transplanted organs. Regular hematologic surveillance, dermatologic and ENT exams, cancer screening, telomere length/telomerase genetic follow‑up, and low‑threshold consideration of HSCT for progressive marrow failure are required. Reproductive and genetic counselling for family members should be arranged.

Finally, manage timing and goals of care with the patient. Discuss the tradeoffs: transplantation can prolong life and improve quality for organ failure, but in TBDs the systemic biology often limits long‑term success, increases peri‑operative morbidity, and may necessitate subsequent HSCT or additional organ support. Choose centres experienced with TBDs, and assemble an integrated team (transplant surgery, hepatology/pulmonology, hematology, genetics, infectious disease, nutrition, and rehabilitation) to individualize the plan.

In short, before listing a TBD patient for lung or liver transplant, obtain genetic and telomere testing, fully define marrow reserve and extra‑organ disease, involve hematology to decide sequencing of HSCT versus solid‑organ transplant, minimize marrow‑toxic peri‑operative immunosuppression, plan for heightened infection, wound‑healing and malignancy risk, and ensure shared decision making with comprehensive long‑term surveillance.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed mechanism by which androgens (e.g., danazol, nandrolone) may improve hematologic parameters in telomere biology disorders, and what are key risks and monitoring parameters?

Clinical pearl — why and how androgens can help in telomere biology disorders, and what to watch for.

Mechanism and clinical reasoning
Androgens such as danazol and (less commonly used) nandrolone appear to improve blood counts in some patients with telomere biology disorders by increasing telomerase activity and thereby stabilizing telomere length in haematopoietic stem/progenitor cells. The effect is thought to be mediated primarily through androgen receptor–dependent upregulation of TERT transcription (and possibly other telomerase complex components) and by improving maturation/stability of telomerase RNA in some contexts. There is also an immediate erythropoietic stimulus from androgens independent of telomerase (increased erythropoietin sensitivity and bone marrow stimulation), so the haematologic response can be partly non‑specific. In short, androgens may both raise telomerase activity (addressing the underlying telomere defect) and provide a direct marrow‑stimulatory effect; clinical trials (small, nonrandomized and one randomized pilot series) have shown telomere length increases and hematologic responses in some patients treated with danazol.

How that applies to this patient
In a patient with suspected PARN‑related TBD, an androgen could theoretically mitigate progressive marrow failure by improving telomerase function and boosting counts. However, PARN mutations are heterogeneous, and not all TBD genotypes respond equally; response depends on residual telomerase biology and marrow reserve. Thus androgen therapy is a temporizing, genotype‑informed, and closely monitored option — not a guaranteed cure.

Key risks and essential monitoring
The dominant concern that changes the risk–benefit calculus in this case is liver disease. Hepatotoxicity is the most important adverse effect of danazol and other anabolic/androgenic agents: cholestasis, hepatocellular injury, and de novo or worsening hepatic adenomas and peliosis have been described, and long‑term use can accelerate liver dysfunction. Given this patient’s established hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, androgens carry substantially increased risk and often are contraindicated or require extreme caution.

Other important risks to counsel and monitor for are virilization (especially in women), lipid abnormalities, fluid retention, mood/behavior changes, acne, decreased HDL, and increased thrombotic risk. Androgens may also impair fertility.

Monitoring strategy if androgens are considered
Before starting: document baseline CBC with reticulocyte count, comprehensive liver panel (AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin), hepatic synthetic function (albumin, INR), liver imaging or elastography and alpha‑fetoprotein as indicated, fasting lipids, pregnancy test (if applicable), and a thrombosis risk assessment. Consider genotype and multidisciplinary discussion (hematology + hepatology + genetics), and document informed consent about hepatic and reproductive risks.

During therapy: check CBC every 2–4 weeks initially until stable, then monthly; liver enzymes and bilirubin every 2–4 weeks initially and frequently thereafter (at least monthly for several months); monitor INR/albumin periodically; check lipids and screen for signs of virilization and thromboembolism. Reassess liver imaging and AFP periodically if long‑term therapy is continued. Consider repeating telomere length and marrow assessment at intervals to judge biological effect (often reported at 6–12 months in studies). Stop therapy promptly for substantial transaminase elevation, new hepatic decompensation, hepatic adenoma, or thrombotic events.

Practical dosing and evidence caveat
Published protocols most commonly used danazol 400–800 mg/day in adults; nandrolone and other androgens have less robust data. Hematologic responses and telomere elongation have been reported over months, but long‑term safety and durability remain uncertain. Bone marrow transplant remains the definitive therapy for progressive marrow failure and should be considered early in appropriate candidates; androgen therapy may be used as a bridge in selected patients.

Bottom line
Androgens can improve counts in TBDs by upregulating telomerase activity and by direct marrow stimulation, but they carry significant risks—most notably hepatotoxicity and thrombosis. In a patient with PARN‑associated disease and established hepatic fibrosis, the risks are substantial and require multidisciplinary evaluation, rigorous baseline assessment, and very close monitoring; in many cases hepatic disease will steer the team away from androgens and toward alternative strategies (supportive care, transplantation planning).

### [Clinical Pearls] Define the classic diagnostic criteria for dyskeratosis congenita and explain how broader telomere biology disorders are diagnosed when mucocutaneous features are limited or absent.

Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) was historically defined by a characteristic mucocutaneous triad—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy and reticular hyperpigmentation—together with bone marrow failure and other premature‑aging manifestations. For clinical and research purposes the “classic” diagnostic approach still uses that triad: either the presence of the three mucocutaneous signs, or two mucocutaneous signs plus bone‑marrow failure (pancytopenia, aplastic anaemia) or a known pathogenic variant in a telomere‑related gene. This clinical definition reflects the original phenotype that linked dyskerin and telomere shortening to an inherited syndrome.

In practice today, however, most telomere biology disorders (TBDs) present along a broad spectrum and often without the full mucocutaneous picture. When those classic signs are limited or absent, the diagnosis rests on a combination of pattern recognition (early‑onset, otherwise unexplained organ dysfunction in telomere‑sensitive tissues) and objective laboratory/genetic confirmation. Clinically, suspect a TBD in young adults with unexplained bone‑marrow failure or cytopenias, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis, premature greying or osteoporosis, avascular necrosis, or early malignancy—especially when multiple systems are involved or there is a family history suggestive of autosomal dominant inheritance with variable penetrance.

Two tests are most useful to move from suspicion to diagnosis. First, measure telomere length in peripheral blood leukocyte subsets by flow‑FISH: telomere length below the first percentile for age in lymphocytes and/or granulocytes is highly suggestive of a TBD in the appropriate clinical context. Second, perform targeted multi‑gene sequencing (or a TBD gene panel/exome) to look for pathogenic germline variants in telomere‑related genes (TERT, TERC, DKC1, PARN, RTEL1, TINF2, etc.). A pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant plus compatible clinical features establishes the diagnosis. If sequencing yields only a variant of uncertain significance (as in the PARN example), supportive evidence from very short telomeres, segregation in affected relatives, functional assays (where available), and careful phenotyping can reclassify the variant and guide management.

Two practical caveats guide real‑world decision making. First, monoallelic variants (particularly in TERT, TERC, RTEL1 and PARN) often present with organ‑specific phenotypes and incomplete penetrance—so absence of the classic triad does not exclude a genetically mediated TBD. Second, telomere length and genetic tests are complementary: very short telomeres with a compatible phenotype may suffice to proceed with monitoring, counselling and treatment planning even when a definitive genetic finding is lacking; conversely, a pathogenic variant with borderline telomere shortening in a young symptomatic patient still warrants diagnosis and surveillance because of variable expressivity.

In summary: the classic diagnostic construct for DC is the mucocutaneous triad (or two signs plus bone‑marrow failure), but contemporary diagnosis of TBDs relies on recognizing the multisystem, age‑atypical pattern of telomere‑sensitive organ disease and confirming it by telomere length measurement and germline genetic testing, interpreted in the context of family history, functional data and clinical course.

### [Management] What longitudinal surveillance would you implement for a young adult with a telomere biology disorder affecting marrow, liver, lung, and bone, and which red flags would prompt escalation of care?

Start by remembering the core principle: telomere biology disorders are multisystem, progressive conditions in which surveillance should be proactive, disease‑directed, and multidisciplinary so that organ‑specific decline is detected early and definitive therapies (transplantation, referral to specialty centres, clinical trials) can be timed before irreversible organ failure.

Initial baseline assessments to obtain now (if not already done): formal telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) and confirmation/clarification of the PARN variant with genetic counselling; full haematologic workup including marrow cellularity, cytogenetics and molecular myeloid panel; baseline pulmonary function tests (FVC, FEV1, DLCO) and six‑minute walk with oxygen saturation, and a baseline high‑resolution chest CT; baseline liver staging with LFTs, INR, platelet count, transient elastography (or MRI elastography) and hepatology evaluation including screening for portal hypertension; bone density (DEXA) and endocrine assessment (vitamin D, PTH); infection/immunization review; and a formal discussion with a combined bone‑marrow‑transplant and lung/liver transplant centre about future options and conditioning risks.

Longitudinal surveillance plan (practical, test frequency adjusted to trajectory):

Hematology: complete blood count with differential every 1–3 months when unstable or during active decline; every 3–6 months if stable. Repeat bone marrow evaluation and cytogenetics if new or progressive cytopenias, new blasts, rising transfusion needs, or unexplained marrow changes. Consider FISH/molecular reassessment if concern for clonal evolution. Low threshold for early transplant centre referral when persistent severe cytopenias, transfusion dependence, clonal evolution, or MDS/AML are suspected.

Pulmonary: outpatient PFTs including DLCO and 6‑minute walk every 3–6 months for the first year (more frequently if decline), then at least every 6–12 months if stable. Repeat HRCT if clinical change or every ~12 months if progressive disease is suspected. Home oximetry and prompt evaluation for new dyspnea, exercise desaturation or new cough. Early discussion with lung transplant program if FVC declines ≥10% or DLCO declines ≥15%, or if patient develops resting hypoxemia or increasing oxygen requirement — these are accepted markers of progression in fibrotic lung disease.

Hepatology: LFTs, INR, albumin and platelets every 3–6 months; transient elastography or MRI elastography annually (sooner if changes). Screen for complications of portal hypertension (clinical exam, ultrasound for splenomegaly/ascites); if cirrhosis is present, perform variceal screening (endoscopy per hepatology), and HCC surveillance with abdominal ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months. Refer early to liver transplant program if any sign of hepatic decompensation (ascites, encephalopathy, variceal bleed) or progressive synthetic dysfunction (rising INR, falling albumin, rising MELD).

Bone and endocrine: DEXA every 1–2 years and more often if fractures or rapid change. Monitor calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, PTH every 6–12 months and treat osteoporosis aggressively; avoid long gaps because fragility is common.

Cancer and mucocutaneous surveillance: annual skin inspection and oral cavity exam (dysplastic leukoplakia can progress). Maintain age‑appropriate cancer screening but with heightened suspicion—new weight loss, bleeding, new masses, or lymphadenopathy require urgent workup.

Infection prevention and general care: update pneumococcal and influenza vaccinations; avoid live vaccines if immunosuppression is required. Counsel on tobacco avoidance and minimize exposures that accelerate telomere attrition (smoking, excessive alcohol, cytotoxic agents when possible).

Medication and procedure cautions: avoid myeloablative chemotherapy and radiation when alternatives exist; if transplantation or chemotherapy is being considered, plan reduced‑intensity conditioning and discuss increased toxicity risk with transplant teams experienced in TBDs. Use hematopoietic growth factors and androgens only under specialist guidance and in context of clinical trials or expert opinion.

When to escalate care or refer urgently (red flags)

Hematologic red flags that mandate urgent transplant evaluation: progressive cytopenias with marrow hypocellularity or failure, new transfusion dependence for red cells or platelets, absolute neutrophil count <0.5 ×10^9/L with infections, development of clonal cytogenetic abnormalities or ≥5% blasts (MDS/AML evolution). Any rapid fall in hemoglobin, platelets or neutrophils that is unexplained.

Pulmonary red flags: acute or subacute worsening dyspnea, new hypoxemia at rest or with exertion, a fall in FVC ≥10% or DLCO ≥15% from baseline, increased supplemental oxygen requirement, or hospitalization for respiratory failure—prompt referral to pulmonary specialty and lung transplant centre.

Hepatic red flags: new ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, variceal hemorrhage, jaundice with rising bilirubin or INR, or a rapidly rising MELD score. These require urgent hepatology and liver transplant assessment.

Infectious/bleeding complications: recurrent, severe, or opportunistic infections; severe or uncontrollable bleeding in the setting of thrombocytopenia should lead to inpatient evaluation and escalation.

Other red flags: new or rapidly progressive cytopenia‑related symptoms (severe fatigue, syncope), new focal neurologic deficits, rapid bone loss with pathologic fracture, or any clinical concern for a new malignancy.

Final practical points: coordinate care through a multidisciplinary TBD clinic if available (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, transplant teams, infectious disease, endocrinology), document advance care planning and reproductive counseling early, and keep a low threshold to refer to specialized transplant centres because timing of HSCT or organ transplant in TBD is critical and outcomes are improved when done in experienced centres using tailored, reduced‑toxicity regimens. Surveillance intensity should be individualized to the patient’s trajectory, but the default stance is close, proactive monitoring given the risk of rapid, irreversible organ compromise.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with diffuse bone marrow FDG uptake on PET-CT, how do you differentiate reactive marrow changes (e.g., ineffective erythropoiesis) from infiltrative malignancy, and which corroborating tests are most useful?

When PET–CT shows diffusely increased FDG uptake throughout the axial and appendicular marrow the first clinical question is not “is this cancer?” but “is the marrow globally hypermetabolic (reactive) or is it diffusely infiltrated by malignancy?” Distinguishing the two requires integrating the PET pattern with the clinical picture and targeted laboratory and pathologic tests — PET alone cannot reliably make the call.

Start with the clinical context and simple labs. Diffuse, relatively uniform marrow uptake is classically seen with marrow stimulation or stress: recent growth‑factor use (G‑CSF), recent chemotherapy recovery, infection or inflammation, severe anaemia with ineffective erythropoiesis, haemolysis, and marrow reconversion from chronic anaemia or smoking. These states produce erythroid/myeloid hyperplasia and low‑grade but widespread glucose uptake. By contrast, malignant marrow involvement often produces focal or patchy areas of very high uptake (nodular foci), asymmetric distribution, or a combination of diffuse + focal; extremely high SUVs, rapidly progressive cytopenias, circulating blasts or systemic B symptoms should raise suspicion for haematologic malignancy.

Corroborating tests that are most useful — and in roughly the order you should use them — are peripheral blood indices and smear, targeted serum markers, and bone marrow examination with adjunct molecular/cytogenetic studies. A low reticulocyte count with macrocytosis, dimorphic red cells and features of ineffective erythropoiesis points to a reactive marrow hyperplasia driven by ineffective erythropoiesis (as in this case with severe B12 deficiency), whereas a rising blast percentage, leukoerythroblastic picture, or severe progressive pancytopenia favors infiltration. Serum LDH, ferritin, CRP/ESR and markers of haemolysis (indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin) help discriminate haemolysis/inflammation from neoplasm; exceptionally high ferritin and soluble IL‑2 receptor suggest HLH. A history of recent G‑CSF or transfusion also explains diffuse uptake.

Definitive discrimination rests on the bone marrow. Perform both aspiration and a trephine core: aspirate and flow cytometry detect abnormal blasts or immunophenotypic clones; the core shows architecture, cellularity, fibrosis, granulomas or focal infiltrates that can be missed by PET. Cytogenetics and targeted NGS panels for myeloid/lymphoid malignancies increase sensitivity for occult clonal disease. If biopsy shows trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia, increased iron stores and no dysplasia, cytogenetic abnormalities or clonal flow findings — as in this patient — the pattern is most consistent with reactive marrow change from ineffective erythropoiesis rather than infiltrative malignancy. If uncertainty persists, short‑interval clinical follow‑up, repeat PET after treating reversible causes (eg, correct B12, stop G‑CSF) or repeat marrow sampling are appropriate.

In summary: interpret diffuse marrow FDG uptake in clinical context; use CBC/retic and smear plus inflammatory and haemolysis markers to triage; and rely on marrow aspiration/core with flow cytometry, cytogenetics and molecular testing as the decisive workup to exclude infiltrative malignancy.
