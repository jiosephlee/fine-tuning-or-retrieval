Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

### [Clinical Pearls] In a young adult with pancytopenia, premature greying, osteoporosis, and unexplained hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis but without the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, what red-flag clinical features should prompt evaluation for a telomere biology disorder?

Answer (clinical pearl)

Think “young person with unexplained premature ageing + marrow failure or organ fibrosis” — those are the red flags that should trigger evaluation for a telomere biology disorder (TBD), even when the classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad is absent.

Red-flag clinical features
- Age: presentation in young adults (often <40–50 years) with findings usually seen in older patients.  
- Bone marrow abnormalities: unexplained cytopenias or bone- marrow failure (pancytopenia, persistent macrocytosis, low reticulocyte response, marrow failure out of proportion to a reversible cause). In the case, persistent pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count despite correction of B12 raised suspicion.  
- Early-onset pulmonary fibrosis: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or interstitial lung disease occurring at an unusually young age. The patient had diffuse fibrotic changes and ground‑glass opacities in her 20s.  
- Unexplained hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis in a young adult without usual causes (negative viral and autoimmune testing). This patient had cirrhosis on transient elastography despite exclusion of common etiologies.  
- Premature ageing stigmata: premature greying of hair, early osteoporosis/fragility fractures, nail dystrophy or mucosal changes (even if the full triad is absent). The case had premature greying and osteoporosis with fractures.  
- Multisystem involvement or disproportionate severity: two or more end‑organ manifestations that reflect high-turnover tissues (marrow, lung, liver, skin, hair, gut, gonads, eyes, nervous system). The combination of marrow failure + pulmonary + hepatic disease in this patient is characteristic.  
- Family history or clustering of related phenotypes (early pulmonary fibrosis, aplastic anaemia, liver disease, premature greying) or excessive toxicity to cytotoxic therapy or HSCT in relatives.

Why these features matter (pathophysiologic reasoning)
TBDs cause accelerated telomere shortening in highly proliferative tissues (haematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, alveolar epithelium, skin/hair), so they present as premature organ failure rather than isolated single-organ disease. Classic mucocutaneous signs of dyskeratosis congenita may be absent in adults; therefore, the combination of early‑onset fibrosis and marrow dysfunction is the practical clinical signal. In this case the young age plus pancytopenia, early greying, osteoporosis and both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis form a syndromic pattern typical of a TBD and supported the identification of a PARN variant.

Diagnostic approach and standards
- Measure telomere length (flow-FISH on lymphocytes is the reference standard in clinical practice; very short telomeres for age strongly support TBD).  
- Germline genetic testing with a targeted telomere gene panel or broad clinical exome/genome sequencing (include TERT, TERC, DKC1, PARN, RTEL1, TINF2 and others). The case found a monoallelic PARN variant on clinical exome — concordant with the suspected diagnosis.  
- Interpret genetics in context: incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity and monoallelic vs biallelic effects are common; a variant of uncertain significance (as here) may still be clinically meaningful when phenotype fits.  
- Evaluate family members and offer genetic counselling.

Immediate clinical implications
- Early recognition changes management: surveillance for progressive marrow failure, pulmonary and hepatic complications; avoidance/modification of cytotoxic exposure (conditioning regimens for HSCT should be tailored), and early referral to centres experienced with TBDs for HSCT planning if progressive bone marrow failure develops.  
- Consider telomere-directed therapies experimentally (androgens) only in specialised settings and discuss risks/benefits.  
- Smoking cessation, vaccination, and vigilance for malignancy and treatment toxicity.

Bottom line
In any young adult with unexplained cytopenias or bone‑marrow dysfunction plus early‑onset pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, premature greying/osteoporosis, or multisystem premature‑ageing features — even without the mucocutaneous triad — test telomere length and perform germline telomere‑gene sequencing. The presented case (late‑20s woman with pancytopenia, premature greying, osteoporosis, liver cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis who harboured a monoallelic PARN variant) exemplifies this pattern and the need for early genetic evaluation and specialized follow‑up.
