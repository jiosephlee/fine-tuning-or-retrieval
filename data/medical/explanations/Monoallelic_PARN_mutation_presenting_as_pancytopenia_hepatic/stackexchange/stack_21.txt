### [Management] Outline pre-transplant considerations for lung or liver transplantation in telomere biology disorders, including multi-organ involvement, bone marrow reserve, and post-transplant complications.

Key principle: telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are systemic, not organ-limited, and that systemic vulnerability drives both pre‑transplant risk and post‑transplant complications. When a patient with short‑telomere phenotype (as in this PARN case) is being considered for lung or liver transplant, management must shift from single‑organ thinking to staged, multidisciplinary risk assessment and mitigation focused on marrow reserve, extra‑hepatic/extra‑pulmonary disease, and the recognised idiosyncratic responses of TBD patients to conditioning, surgery and immunosuppression.

First, confirm the diagnosis and define disease biology. Genetic confirmation (targeted panel/clinical exome) and quantitative telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) are essential because they stratify risk: shorter telomeres and pathogenic variants in telomerase‑related genes predict higher peri‑operative and hematologic complications. In this case the monoallelic PARN variant and the clinical phenotype should prompt formal telomere length measurement and family screening prior to listing.

Second, comprehensive multisystem staging is mandatory. Characterize bone marrow reserve with serial CBCs, reticulocyte counts, bone marrow aspirate/biopsy (cellularity, dysplasia, fibrosis), cytogenetics and molecular studies; quantify transfusion dependence and response to hematinic therapy. Evaluate liver disease severity with labs, imaging, transient elastography (already done here), portal hypertension assessment, and hepatology review to determine isolated liver transplant versus combined transplant candidacy. Assess pulmonary physiology, pulmonary hypertension, and exercise capacity for transplant candidacy and timing. Screen for occult viral infections, HPV and dust‑related pathogens, and perform malignancy surveillance (skin, hematologic and aerodigestive tracts) because TBDs increase cancer risk.

Third, integrate marrow findings into decision making about organ transplant versus hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT). Significant marrow failure or progressive cytopenias may favour HSCT (potentially curative for marrow disease) before or in lieu of solid‑organ transplant, but HSCT conditioning is especially toxic in TBDs. If marrow reserve is marginal but not failing (as in this patient whose cytopenia improved with B12), accept that solid‑organ transplant may be feasible but the peri‑operative risk of progressive marrow failure, bleeding, and infection is higher. In practice this requires hematology input to define thresholds (e.g., platelet count targets, transfusion strategies) and contingency plans for post‑op marrow deterioration, including low‑intensity HSCT regimens if needed.

Fourth, tailor peri‑operative and immunosuppressive strategies to minimize marrow and hepatic toxicity. Avoid or reduce exposure to myelosuppressive agents and alkylators whenever possible. Use the least marrow‑toxic induction and maintenance regimens compatible with graft survival (for example, minimizing or avoiding thymoglobulin or high‑dose azathioprine; carefully weigh calcineurin inhibitor nephrotoxicity versus mTOR inhibitors which can impair wound healing). Anticipate and plan for prolonged cytopenias: line up transfusion support, consider prophylactic growth factors judiciously (G‑CSF in infection risk balanced against possible clonal selection), and maintain close drug‑level monitoring because altered protein binding or hepatic dysfunction may change pharmacokinetics.

Fifth, pre‑emptively manage infection and wound‑healing risk. TBD patients have impaired epithelial regenerative capacity; expect delayed wound healing, anastomotic complications, and higher risk of bronchial anastomotic failure after lung transplant. Optimize nutrition, correct vitamin deficiencies (B12, folate, vitamin D as in this patient), vaccinate pre‑transplant when possible, and plan for aggressive early prophylaxis for typical post‑transplant pathogens and opportunists. Lower thresholds for biopsy/culture and prolonged infectious disease follow‑up are prudent.

Sixth, plan for combined organ transplantation when indicated. Severe cirrhosis plus end‑stage lung disease may require combined liver–lung transplant in centres with experience, but combined procedures carry higher mortality and are rarely done in TBDs. Decisions should be individualized in a high‑volume centre after multidisciplinary review, accepting that outcomes are generally worse than for isolated organ disease.

Seventh, counsel about long‑term complications and monitoring. After transplant expect amplified risks: prolonged or progressive bone marrow failure with transfusion dependence, higher rates of cytopenias while on immunosuppression, accelerated incidence of malignancy (particularly squamous cell cancers and post‑transplant lymphoproliferative disease), and progressive fibrosis in non‑transplanted organs. Regular hematologic surveillance, dermatologic and ENT exams, cancer screening, telomere length/telomerase genetic follow‑up, and low‑threshold consideration of HSCT for progressive marrow failure are required. Reproductive and genetic counselling for family members should be arranged.

Finally, manage timing and goals of care with the patient. Discuss the tradeoffs: transplantation can prolong life and improve quality for organ failure, but in TBDs the systemic biology often limits long‑term success, increases peri‑operative morbidity, and may necessitate subsequent HSCT or additional organ support. Choose centres experienced with TBDs, and assemble an integrated team (transplant surgery, hepatology/pulmonology, hematology, genetics, infectious disease, nutrition, and rehabilitation) to individualize the plan.

In short, before listing a TBD patient for lung or liver transplant, obtain genetic and telomere testing, fully define marrow reserve and extra‑organ disease, involve hematology to decide sequencing of HSCT versus solid‑organ transplant, minimize marrow‑toxic peri‑operative immunosuppression, plan for heightened infection, wound‑healing and malignancy risk, and ensure shared decision making with comprehensive long‑term surveillance.
