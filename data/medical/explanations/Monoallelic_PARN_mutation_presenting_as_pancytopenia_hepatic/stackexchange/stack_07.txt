Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

### [Morning Report] What are the gold standard investigations to confirm a suspected telomere biology disorder, and how are telomere length results typically interpreted?

In a patient like this — young, multisystem fibrosis with cytopenias and a PARN variant — the diagnostic approach has two complementary “gold‑standard” components: objective telomere length measurement and comprehensive germline genetic testing, interpreted in the context of age and clinical features.

Telomere length measurement
The gold‑standard laboratory test is flow‑FISH (flow cytometry combined with fluorescence in situ hybridisation) performed on peripheral blood leukocyte subsets. Flow‑FISH provides age‑adjusted telomere length distributions for total lymphocytes and for specific subsets (CD3+ T cells, CD4/8, NK cells, B cells) and is reproducible between centres when standardised. Quantitative PCR assays are widely used for screening but are less precise, poorly reproducible across labs, and should not replace flow‑FISH for diagnostic confirmation.

How results are interpreted
Telomere length must be read against age‑matched reference percentiles. In adults and children alike, telomere lengths below the first percentile for age (especially in lymphocyte subsets) are highly suggestive of a telomere biology disorder and support a diagnosis when coupled with compatible clinical features. Telomere lengths between the 1st and 10th percentiles are indeterminate but raise suspicion and warrant genetic testing and clinical correlation. Normal telomere length does not completely exclude a pathogenic telomere‑gene variant — partial loss‑of‑function variants, somatic mosaicism, or technical/sample issues (recent transfusion, bone marrow failure with marrow‑derived leukopenia, or post‑transplant samples) can confound results. When peripheral blood is unreliable (for example after transfusion or if marrow failure severely alters leukocyte populations), testing telomere length in nonhaematopoietic tissue (skin fibroblasts or buccal epithelial cells) can better reflect germline telomere length.

Genetic testing
Definitive confirmation rests on germline sequencing of telomere‑related genes. A targeted multigene panel (including TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, TINF2 and others) or broad approaches (clinical exome or genome sequencing with analysis for telomere genes) are the standard. Findings classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in a compatible gene establish the diagnosis. Variants of uncertain significance (as in this case) require integration of telomere length data, segregation analysis in family members, functional data when available, and expert variant curation. Because of reduced penetrance and variable expressivity, testing family members and considering non‑blood tissue for sequencing (to detect mosaicism) are often necessary.

Putting the two together
The most reliable diagnostic evidence is short telomeres by flow‑FISH together with a pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) germline variant in a telomere maintenance gene. Short telomeres alone in the appropriate clinical context are strongly supportive and justify genetic testing and management changes (surveillance, avoidance of genotoxic exposures, transplant planning). A monoallelic variant with borderline telomere shortening or normal telomeres requires careful longitudinal follow‑up, family studies, and, if available, functional assays or referral to a specialized telomere centre for adjudication.

In practice for this patient: obtain flow‑FISH on age‑matched lymphocyte subsets (and consider fibroblast telomere length if blood is confounded), pursue targeted germline panel or confirmatory testing of the PARN variant (and family segregation), and integrate those results to guide counselling, surveillance and therapy.
