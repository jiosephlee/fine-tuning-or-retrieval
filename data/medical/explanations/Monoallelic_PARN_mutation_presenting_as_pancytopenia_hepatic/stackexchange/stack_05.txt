Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you reconcile a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG with laboratory and marrow findings favoring ineffective erythropoiesis rather than immune hemolysis, and what additional tests help distinguish these entities?

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) is an important clue, but it is not a standalone diagnosis of immune haemolysis. You reconcile a positive DAT for IgG with laboratory and marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis by integrating the whole haemolysis/erythropoiesis picture and asking whether there is evidence that red cells are being destroyed in the periphery and replaced appropriately.

In immune (warm) autoimmune haemolytic anaemia you expect: brisk haemolytic markers (high indirect bilirubin, very high LDH, low haptoglobin or free plasma haemoglobin), a high reticulocyte count or reticulocyte production index indicating a compensatory marrow response, and marrow that is erythroid‑hyperplastic but effective (maturing erythroid precursors). By contrast, ineffective erythropoiesis (for example due to severe B12/folate deficiency, sideroblastic processes or marrow failure) produces elevated indirect bilirubin and LDH to a variable degree from intramedullary destruction, but the reticulocyte count is low or inappropriately normal and the marrow shows erythroid hyperplasia with morphologic abnormalities and iron loading — exactly the pattern in this case. That discordance (positive DAT + low retic + marrow features of ineffective erythropoiesis and increased iron stores) argues that the DAT is an epiphenomenon rather than the primary cause of anaemia.

There are biologic reasons a DAT can be positive without clinically significant haemolysis: low‑affinity or low‑density IgG coating that does not trigger rapid splenic clearance, residual allo‑ or auto‑antibodies after prior transfusion, passively transferred antibodies, drug‑induced low‑grade IgG coating, or nonspecific binding in inflammatory states and marrow disorders. In telomere biology disorders and other chronic marrow illnesses the immune milieu may produce weak RBC coating without a fulminant haemolytic syndrome.

Tests and steps that help distinguish immune haemolysis from ineffective erythropoiesis in this situation

1) Quantify haemolysis and marrow response: repeat reticulocyte count and calculate the reticulocyte production index; measure haptoglobin, plasma free haemoglobin, urine hemosiderin and serial bilirubin/LDH. A brisk reticulocytosis with low haptoglobin supports peripheral immune haemolysis; a low retic with marrow erythroid hyperplasia points to ineffective erythropoiesis.

2) Eluate testing: perform an elution from the patient’s RBCs and test the eluate against reagent red cells. A positive eluate with a warm autoantibody specificity supports clinically significant IgG‑mediated haemolysis. A negative or nonreactive eluate argues against pathogenic antibody-mediated destruction.

3) Detailed immunohematology: repeat and expand DAT to include IgG subclass and complement (C3d), do an indirect antiglobulin test/antibody screen, test for alloantibodies or recent transfusion history, and perform thermal amplitude/cold agglutinin testing if indicated. Strong IgG and complement binding correlate better with haemolysis.

4) Corroborating marrow and iron studies: bone marrow morphology (megaloblastic changes, dyserythropoiesis), iron studies showing increased ferritin and marrow iron with ring sideroblasts if present, and measures of ineffective erythropoiesis (e.g., erythropoietin level, marrow iron staining) help attribute anaemia to intramedullary destruction or defective maturation.

5) Exclude other causes that can cause a positive DAT: medication review (penicillins, cephalosporins, rifampin, NSAIDs), recent IVIG or transfusion, and infectious triggers. Consider flow cytometry for PNH if haemolysis features are present.

6) Functional and longitudinal assessment: document clinical course and response to therapies. Immune haemolysis typically improves with immunosuppression (eg, corticosteroids) whereas correction of vitamin B12/folate or treating the underlying marrow disorder will correct ineffective erythropoiesis; a therapeutic trial can be informative when diagnostic testing is equivocal.

In short: a positive DAT requires correlation with evidence of peripheral haemolysis and an appropriate marrow reticulocyte response. In this patient the low reticulocyte count, marrow erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective maturation and iron loading point to ineffective erythropoiesis as the dominant process; the DAT likely reflects low‑grade or nonpathogenic IgG coating. The most decisive additional tests are an eluate study and a repeat, contemporaneous panel of haemolysis markers plus a reticulocyte production index to determine whether antibody‑mediated peripheral destruction is clinically significant.
