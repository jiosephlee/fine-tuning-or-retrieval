Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with diffuse bone marrow FDG uptake on PET-CT, how do you differentiate reactive marrow changes (e.g., ineffective erythropoiesis) from infiltrative malignancy, and which corroborating tests are most useful?

When PET–CT shows diffusely increased FDG uptake throughout the axial and appendicular marrow the first clinical question is not “is this cancer?” but “is the marrow globally hypermetabolic (reactive) or is it diffusely infiltrated by malignancy?” Distinguishing the two requires integrating the PET pattern with the clinical picture and targeted laboratory and pathologic tests — PET alone cannot reliably make the call.

Start with the clinical context and simple labs. Diffuse, relatively uniform marrow uptake is classically seen with marrow stimulation or stress: recent growth‑factor use (G‑CSF), recent chemotherapy recovery, infection or inflammation, severe anaemia with ineffective erythropoiesis, haemolysis, and marrow reconversion from chronic anaemia or smoking. These states produce erythroid/myeloid hyperplasia and low‑grade but widespread glucose uptake. By contrast, malignant marrow involvement often produces focal or patchy areas of very high uptake (nodular foci), asymmetric distribution, or a combination of diffuse + focal; extremely high SUVs, rapidly progressive cytopenias, circulating blasts or systemic B symptoms should raise suspicion for haematologic malignancy.

Corroborating tests that are most useful — and in roughly the order you should use them — are peripheral blood indices and smear, targeted serum markers, and bone marrow examination with adjunct molecular/cytogenetic studies. A low reticulocyte count with macrocytosis, dimorphic red cells and features of ineffective erythropoiesis points to a reactive marrow hyperplasia driven by ineffective erythropoiesis (as in this case with severe B12 deficiency), whereas a rising blast percentage, leukoerythroblastic picture, or severe progressive pancytopenia favors infiltration. Serum LDH, ferritin, CRP/ESR and markers of haemolysis (indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin) help discriminate haemolysis/inflammation from neoplasm; exceptionally high ferritin and soluble IL‑2 receptor suggest HLH. A history of recent G‑CSF or transfusion also explains diffuse uptake.

Definitive discrimination rests on the bone marrow. Perform both aspiration and a trephine core: aspirate and flow cytometry detect abnormal blasts or immunophenotypic clones; the core shows architecture, cellularity, fibrosis, granulomas or focal infiltrates that can be missed by PET. Cytogenetics and targeted NGS panels for myeloid/lymphoid malignancies increase sensitivity for occult clonal disease. If biopsy shows trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia, increased iron stores and no dysplasia, cytogenetic abnormalities or clonal flow findings — as in this patient — the pattern is most consistent with reactive marrow change from ineffective erythropoiesis rather than infiltrative malignancy. If uncertainty persists, short‑interval clinical follow‑up, repeat PET after treating reversible causes (eg, correct B12, stop G‑CSF) or repeat marrow sampling are appropriate.

In summary: interpret diffuse marrow FDG uptake in clinical context; use CBC/retic and smear plus inflammatory and haemolysis markers to triage; and rely on marrow aspiration/core with flow cytometry, cytogenetics and molecular testing as the decisive workup to exclude infiltrative malignancy. In this case the low reticulocyte index, peripheral smear features and a marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia with normal cytogenetics made reactive ineffective erythropoiesis the most likely explanation for diffuse FDG uptake.
