Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

### [Clinical Pearls] Which organ systems require routine surveillance in adults with telomere biology disorders, and what specific tests and intervals would you use to monitor for progression of pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, marrow failure, and malignancy risk?

Bottom line: Adults with telomere biology disorders require structured, lifelong surveillance of four principal domains—lungs, liver, bone marrow (haematopoiesis), and cancer risk—because telomere shortening preferentially damages highly proliferative tissues. In the presented case the patient already illustrates why: radiographic interstitial lung disease (diffuse ground‑glass with subpleural fibrosis), a FibroScan consistent with cirrhosis (18.1 kPa), and persistent cytopenias (pancytopenia with low reticulocyte count). Surveillance should be risk‑stratified to the patient’s current organ involvement and intensify with any clinical or laboratory change.

1) Pulmonary disease (monitoring for progression of pulmonary fibrosis)
- Rationale: monoallelic PARN and other TBD genes commonly present with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; progression determines timing of transplant referral.
- Baseline: high‑resolution chest CT (HRCT) and full pulmonary function testing (PFTs) including spirometry, lung volumes and DLCO; 6‑minute walk test (6MWT) with oximetry and resting pulse‑ox.
- Interval:
  - If lung involvement present but clinically stable: PFTs and DLCO every 6 months for the first year, then every 6–12 months thereafter; 6MWT and oximetry at similar intervals.
  - HRCT: repeat at 12 months or sooner if PFTs or symptoms worsen; thereafter every 12–24 months if stable (more frequent if clinical change).
  - If accelerated decline (FVC fall >10% or DLCO fall >15% in 12 months, or increasing oxygen requirement): refer promptly to an interstitial lung disease/transplant center.
- Triggers to escalate: new/worsening dyspnoea, ≥10% decline in FVC, ≥15% decline in DLCO, new resting hypoxemia or exercise desaturation.

2) Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- Rationale: TBDs commonly involve the liver and can progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The case had objective cirrhosis on transient elastography.
- Baseline: LFTs (AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, total bilirubin), albumin, INR, platelet count, serum AFP, transient elastography (FibroScan) and abdominal ultrasound.
- Interval:
  - For cirrhosis or high fibrosis (FibroScan ≥12–15 kPa or imaging evidence): LFTs, INR, albumin and platelets every 3–6 months; abdominal ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months for HCC surveillance.
  - For moderate fibrosis without cirrhosis: LFTs every 3–6 months and FibroScan annually; ultrasound annually (or sooner if FibroScan increases or labs worsen).
- Other actions: screen for portal hypertension (baseline endoscopy if cirrhosis per local variceal screening protocols), vaccinate against hepatitis A/B if nonimmune, avoid hepatotoxins (alcohol, hepatotoxic drugs), and engage hepatology early for management and transplant evaluation if decompensation or progressive fibrosis.

3) Bone marrow failure and haematologic surveillance
- Rationale: bone marrow is highly telomere‑sensitive; marrow failure, cytopenias and evolution to MDS/AML are risks. In this patient pancytopenia and ineffective erythropoiesis were prominent.
- Baseline: complete blood count (CBC) with differential and reticulocyte count, peripheral smear, iron studies, B12/folate (correct reversible causes), and baseline bone marrow biopsy if unexplained cytopenias persist.
- Interval:
  - For stable counts: CBC with differential and reticulocyte count every 1–3 months (3 months is reasonable for clinically stable mild cytopenias); more frequently (every 2–4 weeks) if symptomatic or transfusion dependent.
  - Repeat bone marrow biopsy and cytogenetics/NGS if there is new or progressive cytopenia, rising transfusion requirements, new blasts, dysplastic changes, or otherwise unexplained laboratory changes.
- Additional monitoring: ferritin/iron overload assessment if transfusion dependent (ferritin every 2–3 months; consider hepatic and cardiac iron MRI when indicated). Early referral to a haematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) centre is mandatory when progressive marrow failure or high‑risk clonal evolution is detected—note that HSCT regimens are modified for TBD patients because of increased toxicity.

4) Malignancy surveillance (haematologic and solid tumors)
- Rationale: TBDs carry increased risk of haematologic malignancies and selected solid tumors (oral cavity, skin, potentially HCC in cirrhosis).
- Practical approach:
  - Ongoing haematologic surveillance is principally by routine CBCs and bone marrow evaluation as above; low threshold for marrow workup if counts change.
  - For HCC risk in those with cirrhosis: abdominal ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months (as above).
  - Mucocutaneous and oral surveillance: periodic (at least annual) dermatologic and oral/oral‑medicine exams to inspect for leukoplakia, dysplasia or suspicious lesions; prompt biopsy of any lesions.
  - Lung cancer: counsel strongly against smoking; apply local LDCT screening guidelines if smoking history meets criteria, but management is individualized because of concurrent ILD.
  - Formal population‑wide cancer screening otherwise follows age‑ and risk‑appropriate guidelines but consider earlier or more frequent surveillance when family history or prior lesions exist.
- Counseling and family testing: offer genetic counselling and cascade testing for relatives; patients should be informed of elevated cancer risk and avoid carcinogenic exposures.

General practical pearls relevant to the case
- Tailor surveillance intensity to current organ involvement: the patient already needs semiannual HCC surveillance, at least 6‑monthly PFTs with annual HRCT, and CBCs every 1–3 months until stable.
- Minimize exposures that accelerate telomere attrition or organ injury: no smoking, avoid unnecessary cytotoxic chemotherapy or ionizing radiation when possible, and avoid hepatotoxins/alcohol.
- Coordinate care in a multidisciplinary clinic (haematology, hepatology, ILD/transplant, genetics, dermatology, ENT/dental) and consider early referral to transplant centers (lung and liver and HSCT) because timing and conditioning regimens require specialist planning in TBD.
- Document and correct reversible contributors to cytopenias (as in this case: treat B12/folate deficiency) but continue genetic‑guided surveillance because marrow insufficiency may progress despite correction of nutritional deficits.

In short: baseline comprehensive testing (HRCT + PFTs; FibroScan/ultrasound + liver labs; CBC with smear ± marrow; exam for mucocutaneous lesions) and then scheduled surveillance driven by severity—typically PFTs every 6–12 months and HRCT every 12–24 months if stable; liver labs every 3–6 months with FibroScan annually and ultrasound/AFP every 6 months if cirrhosis; CBC every 1–3 months with marrow reassessment for decline; and focused cancer surveillance (HCC in cirrhosis every 6 months; annual skin/oral exams), together with early multidisciplinary referral. These steps reflect the pathophysiology and the multisystem involvement illustrated in the case.
