### [Pathophysiology] Why are serum iron and ferritin often elevated in severe vitamin B12 deficiency with ineffective erythropoiesis?

Think of severe vitamin B12 (or folate) deficiency as a factory with a production-line defect: the marrow makes many erythroid precursors but they cannot complete maturation, so they die within the marrow. That intramedullary destruction (ineffective erythropoiesis or “intramedullary hemolysis”) has two linked consequences that raise serum iron and ferritin.

First, dying erythroblasts dump their haemoglobin-derived iron back into the marrow and circulation. That recycled iron increases serum iron and transferrin saturation even while the patient is anaemic and reticulocyte output is low — exactly what you see with a low retic count and erythroid hyperplasia on the marrow. Second, ineffective erythropoiesis suppresses hepcidin (via erythroid regulators such as erythroferrone), permitting greater iron release from macrophages and increased intestinal iron absorption; both actions raise circulating iron and load iron into stores. Ferritin therefore is often high because iron stores are actually increased, and ferritin is also an acute‑phase reactant that rises with inflammation or liver injury.

In this case the picture fits: marked B12 deficiency with erythroid hyperplasia, low retic, elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin (markers of intramedullary destruction), and marrow iron overload. Coexistent hepatic fibrosis and inflammation can further elevate ferritin independent of iron status. Clinically, this explains why a high ferritin/serum iron in a patient with anaemia does not exclude an iron‑restricted process — you must integrate reticulocyte response, transferrin saturation, marrow iron or soluble transferrin receptor, and the overall clinical context to distinguish true iron overload from ineffective erythropoiesis.
