# Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease and SIADH: A Teaching Case on Neuroinflammation, Hyponatremia, and Clinical Reasoning

## Case Vignette

An Asian man in his 60s presented with 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, arthralgia, and oral aphthae. Examination revealed folliculitis-like rashes; several days later a painful scrotal ulcer developed. He was tachycardic and febrile but otherwise hemodynamically stable and euvolemic. Laboratory testing showed marked hyponatremia (Na 124 mmol/L) with low serum osmolality and inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg; urine sodium 88 mmol/L), normal thyroid and adrenal function, and normal renal indices—fulfilling criteria for SIADH. Inflammatory markers were elevated (CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70 mm/h). Brain MRI was unremarkable; CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein with negative infectious studies, consistent with aseptic meningitis. He met International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) with oral aphthae, genital ulcer, and skin lesions (5 points). Colchicine monotherapy, topical agents for mucocutaneous disease, and fluid restriction for SIADH led to resolution of systemic, neurologic, and electrolyte abnormalities without corticosteroids or additional immunosuppression. There was no relapse over 4 years on colchicine.

This case illustrates concurrent new diagnoses of Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) complicated by SIADH, and it anchors key principles in the clinical approach to neuroinflammation and hyponatremia.

## Clinical Approach: Fever, Headache, Mucocutaneous Ulcers, and Hyponatremia

Fever and headache with a systemic inflammatory signal prompt consideration of CNS infection, autoimmune/inflammatory disorders, or neoplasm. Hyponatremia—especially euvolemic, hypo-osmolar—narrows the differential to conditions that impair free water excretion, including SIADH from CNS or pulmonary disease, drugs, endocrine disorders, and malignancy. The conjunction of mucocutaneous ulcers with systemic symptoms points toward BD as an unifying diagnosis; genital aphthosis is particularly specific.

The early priorities are to stabilize the patient, recognize and safely manage severe hyponatremia, and exclude immediately treatable CNS infections (eg, HSV, VZV, bacterial meningitis, tuberculosis, cryptococcus). Concomitant evaluation for systemic inflammatory disease proceeds in parallel. In suspected BD with neurologic complaints, neuro-BD is a consideration even when neuroimaging is normal, particularly in the first days of illness.

This case demonstrates several high-yield features:
- Neurologic symptoms in BD can precede or coincide with classic mucocutaneous findings, although this is uncommon.
- A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD; early disease may manifest as aseptic meningitis with subtle or delayed imaging changes.
- Euvolemic hyponatremia with high urine osmolality and sodium is typical of SIADH; CNS inflammation is a common precipitant.
- Seemingly unrelated findings—persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis—can be consequences of profound hyponatremia.

## Immunopathogenesis of Behçet’s Disease

BD is a chronic, relapsing, variable-vessel vasculitis with a predilection along the ancient Silk Road. Genetic susceptibility is strong; HLA-B*51 confers increased risk, while additional loci implicate antigen processing, T-cell costimulation, and innate immune pathways. The immunobiology features:
- Neutrophil hyperreactivity with enhanced chemotaxis, oxidative burst, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation, driving endothelial injury.
- A Th1/Th17-skewed adaptive response with excessive IL-17, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 production; regulatory T-cell function is relatively impaired.
- Monocyte/macrophage activation that perpetuates cytokine cascades and endothelial activation.
- Endothelial dysfunction with pan-vasculitic involvement: venules and veins are commonly affected, but arteries of any size can be involved.

Mucocutaneous lesions in BD reflect small-vessel vasculitis with neutrophilic infiltration and immune complex deposition. Neuro-BD arises when this aberrant immune response targets the CNS.

## Neuro-Behçet’s Disease: Phenotypes and Pathophysiology

Neuro-BD occurs in 10–20% of patients and typically manifests several years after systemic onset, though simultaneous presentation can occur, as in this case. International consensus recognizes two major phenotypes:

- Parenchymal neuro-BD: an inflammatory meningoencephalitis centered on the brainstem and diencephalon, with lesions often extending to basal ganglia, thalamus, and internal capsule. Histopathology shows perivascular neutrophilic and lymphocytic infiltrates with small-vessel vasculitis and focal necrosis. CSF exhibits pleocytosis (initially neutrophilic, then lymphocytic), elevated protein, and frequently high IL-6. Oligoclonal bands are uncommon compared with multiple sclerosis.

- Nonparenchymal neuro-BD: vascular complications including cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST), intracranial hypertension, and, less commonly, arterial aneurysms or ischemic events. Inflammatory changes predominate in venous walls; prothrombotic endotheliopathy contributes.

A subset of patients presents with aseptic meningitis without visible parenchymal lesions on MRI, particularly early in the disease. Although less common than classic brainstem lesions, this meningeal-predominant phenotype aligns with the patient’s CSF profile and normal imaging. Elevated CSF IL-6 (when measured) correlates with activity, severity, and relapse risk.

## SIADH and CNS Inflammation: Linking IL-6 to Water Homeostasis

Hyponatremia in this case was attributable to SIADH. Diagnostic features include hypotonic hyponatremia, inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg), urine sodium typically >30 mmol/L with normal salt and water intake, clinical euvolemia, and normal thyroid, adrenal, and renal function. Measurement of plasma ADH is not routinely required; values can be variable and are not part of standard criteria, but when obtained may be “inappropriately” elevated as here. Hyperglycemia lowers measured sodium; correcting for a glucose of approximately 14.2 mmol/L modestly raises sodium by only a few mmol/L, leaving true hyponatremia.

CNS inflammation can provoke nonosmotic ADH release via cytokine signaling. IL-6, prominent in BD and neuro-BD, can stimulate hypothalamic magnocellular neurons and augment pituitary ADH secretion. Inflammatory cytokines may also enhance renal collecting duct responsiveness to ADH via upregulation of aquaporin-2 channels, compounding water retention. The congruent improvement of hyponatremia and neurologic symptoms after anti-inflammatory therapy supports a functional link.

Clinically important consequences of severe hyponatremia include:
- Neurologic symptoms from cerebral edema: headache, nausea, confusion, seizures; persistent hiccups can be a subtle presenting sign.
- Rhabdomyolysis, likely from myocyte swelling and membrane disruption, with elevated CK and transaminases; correction of hyponatremia typically reverses.

Drug-induced SIADH must also be considered in BD (eg, cyclophosphamide, SSRIs, carbamazepine, vincristine), but no such triggers were present in this case.

## Diagnostic Workup

Establishing the correct diagnosis in overlapping neuroinflammatory and systemic syndromes requires parallel evaluation of infection, autoimmunity/vasculitis, and mimics, while treating life-threatening conditions.

Behçet’s disease
- Apply clinical criteria. The ICBD uses a point system: oral aphthosis (2), genital aphthosis (2), ocular lesions (2), skin lesions (1), neurologic involvement (1), vascular manifestations (1), and pathergy (1, optional). A score ≥4 indicates BD. This patient achieved 5 points with oral ulcers, genital ulcer, and skin lesions. HLA-B*51 supports susceptibility but is not diagnostic.
- Confirm breadth of involvement. A directed exam and ophthalmologic evaluation for uveitis are essential. Vascular assessment is indicated if symptoms suggest thrombosis or aneurysm.

Neuro-Behçet’s disease
- Brain MRI with contrast and high-resolution sequences focused on brainstem and diencephalon. Early in disease, MRI can be normal; repeating MRI after days to weeks may reveal evolving lesions. Include MR venography to exclude CVST in patients with headache or raised intracranial pressure.
- CSF analysis for cell count and differential, protein, glucose, Gram stain/cultures, and viral PCR panels (HSV, VZV, enteroviruses). In neuro-BD, CSF often shows pleocytosis and elevated protein. Oligoclonal bands are typically absent. CSF IL-6, when available, assists in assessing activity and prognosis.
- Exclude mimics: infectious (bacterial, TB, fungal, viral), neoplastic (CNS lymphoma), demyelinating disease, neurosarcoidosis, and autoimmune encephalitides. In this case, negative infectious studies, normal imaging, and CSF profile supported aseptic meningitis consistent with neuro-BD.

SIADH and hyponatremia
- Confirm hypotonic hyponatremia: serum osmolality <275 mOsm/kg.
- Establish inappropriately concentrated urine: urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg (often >300) and urine sodium >30 mmol/L in the absence of diuretics or hypovolemia.
- Exclude endocrine and renal causes: normal TSH and cortisol; assess renal function and uric acid (often low in SIADH).
- Review medications and obtain chest imaging to evaluate pulmonary causes; consider malignancy evaluation based on risk profile.

Practical considerations
- Correct serum sodium for hyperglycemia to avoid overestimating severity; this patient remained frankly hyponatremic after correction.
- Early MRV helps avoid missing CVST, a nonparenchymal neuro-BD manifestation with overlapping symptoms.
- Avoid indiscriminate serologic testing; pursue targeted evaluation when supported by clinical features, recognizing limited sensitivity and specificity of some markers (eg, ACE, sIL-2R).

## Management Principles

Treat the patient in front of you: address the life-threatening problem, control inflammation, and target the underlying disease.

Hyponatremia due to SIADH
- Initial strategy is fluid restriction (eg, 500 mL/day below 24-hour urine output, often 800–1000 mL/day) with careful monitoring of serum sodium, urine osmolality, and fluid balance. Increase dietary solute (salt and protein) as tolerated.
- Indications for hypertonic saline (3%): severe neurologic symptoms (seizures, coma) or impending herniation. Use small boluses (eg, 100 mL repeated) to rapidly raise sodium by 4–6 mmol/L to control symptoms, then slow correction.
- Consider urea, loop diuretics with salt tablets, or vasopressin receptor antagonists in refractory euvolemic hyponatremia when fluid restriction fails, after addressing the inciting cause. Vaptans require caution and close monitoring.
- Correction limits: generally ≤8 mmol/L in any 24 hours and ≤16–18 mmol/L over 48 hours to prevent osmotic demyelination, with even stricter limits in high-risk patients (alcohol use disorder, severe malnutrition, advanced liver disease, profound hyponatremia). If overcorrection occurs, relower with desmopressin and free water.
- Treat the driver: inflammation control in neuro-BD often normalizes ADH secretion and resolves hyponatremia.

Acute neuro-Behçet’s disease
- Parenchymal neuro-BD (meningoencephalitis) is usually treated with high-dose corticosteroids (eg, IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3–5 days), followed by a slow oral taper. Early steroid therapy shortens relapses and reduces neurologic sequelae.
- Add steroid-sparing immunosuppressants for severe attacks, incomplete responses, or relapsing disease. Evidence supports azathioprine, methotrexate, and—in more aggressive disease—TNF-α inhibitors (infliximab, adalimumab). Interferon-α is effective in some series. Cyclophosphamide is now used more selectively due to toxicity.
- Nonparenchymal disease (eg, CVST) is treated with corticosteroids and immunosuppression; anticoagulation may be added when no arterial aneurysm is present and bleeding risk is acceptable, balancing guideline variability and individual risk.
- Aseptic meningitis–predominant presentations can be self-limited; while corticosteroids remain standard, some mild cases improve without them. In this case, colchicine monotherapy temporally coincided with clinical resolution, although spontaneous improvement cannot be excluded.

Colchicine in BD and neuro-BD
- Colchicine is first-line for mucocutaneous and articular BD. Its mechanisms—inhibition of microtubule polymerization, neutrophil chemotaxis, and inflammasome activity—reduce IL‑1β and IL‑6 signaling.
- Systematic reviews suggest colchicine is generally ineffective as monotherapy for parenchymal neuro-BD; it is more often used as adjunctive therapy. Nevertheless, in milder meningeal presentations or when corticosteroids are contraindicated, a trial can be considered with vigilant monitoring and a low threshold to escalate therapy if neurologic disease persists or worsens.

Supportive and preventive care
- Treat mucocutaneous lesions with topical corticosteroids and antimicrobial creams for secondary infection as needed.
- Screen for ocular disease at baseline and during flares; sight-threatening uveitis requires urgent systemic therapy.
- Address comorbidities (eg, optimize diabetes control to lessen infection risk and steroid-associated hyperglycemia).
- Rehabilitation can mitigate deconditioning after prolonged hospitalization.

## Prognosis, Monitoring, and Follow-up

Acute neuro-BD often improves with therapy, but relapses and progression can occur. Risk factors for worse outcomes include male sex, brainstem/diencephalic involvement, high CSF IL‑6, early relapses, and poor steroid response. Long-term strategies include:
- Clinical surveillance for neurologic, ocular, vascular, and mucocutaneous relapses.
- Periodic MRI in patients with prior parenchymal disease or new neurologic symptoms; consider repeat MRV if headaches recur or intracranial hypertension is suspected.
- CSF IL‑6 (if available) can guide activity assessment and predict relapse risk.
- Maintenance immunosuppression (eg, azathioprine, TNF-α inhibitors) for relapsing or high-risk neuro-BD; colchicine maintenance is appropriate for mucocutaneous-dominant disease, as in this case.

In this patient, colchicine monotherapy was associated with sustained remission of both systemic and neurologic disease over 4 years, underscoring the heterogeneity of neuro-BD and the potential for milder meningeal-predominant phenotypes to resolve without high-dose steroids.

## Clinical Pearls and Pitfalls

- In patients with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous ulcers, think Behçet’s disease; genital aphthosis is highly specific and drives the diagnosis using ICBD.
- Neurologic symptoms can be the first manifestation of BD. A normal early MRI does not exclude neuro-BD; CSF often shows mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. Obtain MRV to exclude cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.
- SIADH is a common cause of euvolemic hyponatremia in CNS inflammation. Confirm hypotonic hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine and exclude thyroid, adrenal, and renal causes. Measuring ADH is rarely necessary.
- IL‑6–driven neuroinflammation may trigger nonosmotic ADH release; effective anti-inflammatory therapy often corrects hyponatremia.
- Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be underrecognized manifestations of severe hyponatremia; check CK and treat the electrolyte disturbance.
- When correcting hyponatremia, avoid overcorrection—generally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours—to prevent osmotic demyelination; be especially cautious in high-risk patients.
- Colchicine is reliable for mucocutaneous BD but is not standard monotherapy for parenchymal neuro-BD; corticosteroids and, when indicated, immunosuppressants or biologics remain first-line. Mild meningeal presentations may improve without steroids, but close monitoring is essential.
- Avoid shotgun serologic testing; target investigations to clinical suspicions and interpret nonspecific markers (eg, ACE, sIL‑2R) with caution.

## Take-Home Points

- Behçet’s disease is a variable-vessel vasculitis with neutrophil-driven and Th1/Th17-skewed inflammation; neuro-BD commonly involves the brainstem/diencephalon but may present with aseptic meningitis and a normal early MRI.
- Euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia with concentrated urine and normal thyroid/adrenal/renal function indicates SIADH; CNS inflammation is a frequent precipitant, likely via IL‑6–mediated ADH dysregulation.
- In BD with new headache or fever plus hyponatremia, consider neuro-BD and evaluate with MRI/MRV and CSF while excluding infection; treat hyponatremia safely and control inflammation.
- Corticosteroids are standard acute therapy for parenchymal neuro-BD; immunosuppression is added for severe or relapsing disease. Colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous disease and may help in mild meningeal presentations, as illustrated by this case.
