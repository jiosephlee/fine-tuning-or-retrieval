Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Morning Report] In a febrile, euvolemic patient with headache and hyponatraemia, which specific laboratory criteria establish SIADH after excluding hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency, and what thresholds would you expect for serum osmolality, urine osmolality, and urine sodium?

Answer — diagnostic criteria and expected thresholds

SIADH is a diagnosis of hypo‑osmolar, euvolaemic hyponatraemia made after excluding hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, renal failure and recent diuretic use. The key laboratory features are:

1. Hypotonic hyponatraemia
   - Serum sodium <135 mmol/L with low plasma/serum osmolality (classically <275 mOsm/kg; many labs use <270 mOsm/kg).  
   - In the reported case: serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg with Na 124 mmol/L.

2. Inappropriately concentrated urine for the plasma hypo-osmolality
   - Urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg indicates inability to maximally dilute; a urine osmolality >300 mOsm/kg is strong evidence of active ADH effect.  
   - In the case: urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg (markedly concentrated).

3. Elevated urine sodium (reflecting euvolaemia and intact renal salt handling)
   - Urine Na typically >20–30 mmol/L (many sources use >30–40 mmol/L) when the patient is euvolemic and eating a normal salt diet.  
   - In the case: urine Na 88 mmol/L.

4. Clinical euvolaemia on exam (no signs of hypovolaemia or oedema) and normal renal, thyroid and adrenal function
   - Exclude hypothyroidism (normal TSH, free T4 in the case) and adrenal insufficiency (baseline cortisol and/or stimulated cortisol; the patient’s cortisol was clearly not low).  
   - Also exclude recent diuretic use and other causes (renal failure, hyperglycaemia causing translocational hyponatraemia).

Additional points
- Direct ADH/AVP measurement is not required for diagnosis and is variably available; it may be elevated (as in this case: ADH 22.4 pg/mL) but is not necessary to confirm SIADH.
- Practical checklist: hypotonic hyponatraemia + urine osmolality inappropriately high (>100, usually >300) + urine Na >30 mmol/L + euvolaemia + exclusion of thyroid/adrenal/diuretic/renal causes = SIADH.

In short: expect low serum osmolality (<275 mOsm/kg, case 267), concentrated urine (urine osmolality often >300 mOsm/kg, case 900) and elevated urine sodium (>30 mmol/L, case 88) once hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency are excluded.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When acute neuro-Behçet’s disease is suspected but brain MRI is normal, which CSF findings and clinical features support the diagnosis over infectious meningoencephalitis, and how do these contrast with HSV/VZV or bacterial meningitis?

Bottom line
- When neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) is suspected but brain MRI is normal, the diagnosis rests on the clinical context (concurrent mucocutaneous BD findings) plus an “aseptic meningitis” CSF profile: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, moderately increased protein, normal glucose, and repeatedly negative bacterial cultures and viral PCRs. The presence of CNS‑related SIADH (hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated ADH) further supports an inflammatory CNS process like neuro‑BD rather than bacterial or herpetic infection.

Why these features favour neuro‑BD in this case
- Clinical context is decisive. This patient met criteria for definite systemic BD (oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, later genital ulcer), so new headache/vertigo with CSF inflammation is most parsimoniously acute neuro‑BD rather than a coincidental CNS infection.
- CSF pattern in the case: opening pressure mildly high (17 cmH2O), lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), elevated protein (0.97 g/L ≈ 97 mg/dL) and normal glucose; all cultures and PCRs for HSV, VZV and bacteria were negative. This is an aseptic meningo‑encephalitis pattern compatible with acute neuro‑BD, especially when infectious studies are repeatedly negative.
- Associated SIADH (serum hypo‑osmolality, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L, elevated ADH) is a known consequence of CNS inflammation (likely IL‑6–mediated) and in this patient paralleled clinical improvement after anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine), supporting a noninfectious inflammatory etiology.

How this contrasts with HSV/VZV meningoencephalitis
- HSV encephalitis: usually more prominent clinical encephalopathy, focal temporal lobe signs, MRI abnormalities in temporal lobes (or other parenchymal T2/FLAIR changes), higher CSF lymphocyte counts (often tens to hundreds/µL), frequently RBCs in CSF, and a positive HSV PCR. Empiric acyclovir is appropriate until PCR is negative. In this case PCR was negative and MRI was normal.
- VZV CNS disease: may show parenchymal lesions or vasculopathy on MRI, often higher CSF cell counts, and positive VZV PCR or intrathecal antibody. A dermatomal rash or zoster history can be a clue; neither was present here.

How this contrasts with bacterial meningitis
- Bacterial meningitis: rapid, more severe systemic toxicity, markedly elevated opening pressure, neutrophilic (polymorphonuclear) pleocytosis (hundreds to thousands/µL), very low CSF glucose (or low CSF/serum ratio), very high protein, and positive Gram stain/culture. The CSF here lacked neutrophilic predominance, had normal glucose, and cultures were negative — findings against bacterial meningitis.

Practical approach / clinical pearls
- In a patient with concurrent mucocutaneous BD signs and an aseptic CSF profile, consider acute neuro‑BD even if MRI is normal (early neuro‑BD can be radiologically silent).
- Continue empiric antimicrobial/antiviral therapy until bacterial causes and HSV/VZV are reasonably excluded (negative CSF PCR/cultures and clinical stability), then pivot to anti‑inflammatory treatment guided by the overall picture.
- The combination of aseptic CSF, negative pathogen testing, systemic BD features, and an SIADH pattern that improves with anti‑inflammatory therapy strongly favours neuro‑BD over HSV/VZV or bacterial meningitis, as illustrated by this case.

### [Clinical Pearls] Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, what score does a patient with recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and a folliculitis-like rash receive, and does HLA-B51 positivity alter the diagnostic classification?

Answer: Under the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) this patient scores 5 points — oral aphthae 2, genital aphthosis 2, and a folliculitis‑like skin lesion 1 — and a score ≥4 meets the threshold for “definite” Behçet’s disease. HLA‑B51 positivity does not change the ICBD score or the formal classification; it is a supportive genetic association (increasing disease likelihood in the appropriate clinical context) but is not part of the diagnostic scoring system and cannot substitute for the required clinical criteria.

Clinical reasoning linked to the case:
- The ICBD point system (oral 2, genital 2, ocular 2, skin 1, vascular 1, neurologic 1, pathergy 1) is a clinical, point‑based tool; this patient’s oral ulcers, scrotal ulcer and folliculitis‑type rash give 2+2+1 = 5 points (≥4 = definite BD), as the authors state.
- Although neurological involvement is recognized by ICBD (1 additional point), the patient already met “definite” criteria on mucocutaneous findings alone.
- HLA‑B51 is epidemiologically associated with BD and may raise pretest probability, but it is neither required nor scored by ICBD and should not be used in isolation to make the diagnosis.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic link between CNS inflammation in neuro-Behçet’s disease and SIADH, including the role of IL-6 and downstream effects on ADH release.

Direct answer
CNS inflammation in neuro‑Behçet’s disease can provoke SIADH through cytokine‑mediated stimulation of arginine vasopressin (AVP, ADH) release — most importantly via interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) produced within the inflamed CNS. IL‑6 (from activated monocytes/macrophages and microglia) acts on hypothalamic/osmoregulatory sites to increase AVP gene expression and secretion, lowering the osmotic threshold for release and producing the water retention and dilutional hyponatraemia that define SIADH.

Why this happens (pathophysiology)
- Source of IL‑6: Acute neuro‑BD is an inflammatory meningoencephalitic process. Aberrant monocyte activation in BD leads to spontaneous IL‑6 production; IL‑6 is also produced intrathecally by activated microglia/mononuclear cells during CNS inflammation. Although this patient’s CSF IL‑6 was not measured, the CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated CSF protein and marked systemic inflammatory markers (CRP 180 mg/L) are concordant with an IL‑6–driven inflammatory state described in neuro‑BD.
- How IL‑6 drives AVP release: IL‑6 can act at circumventricular organs (areas with an incomplete blood–brain barrier such as the organum vasculosum lamina terminalis) and directly on hypothalamic neurons (supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei). Through intracellular signaling (eg, JAK/STAT pathways), IL‑6 increases AVP gene transcription and neuronal excitability, and it can sensitize osmoreceptors so that AVP is secreted at lower plasma osmolality (non‑osmotic stimulation). Intrathecal IL‑6 therefore promotes inappropriate ADH secretion despite hypo‑osmolality.
- Downstream renal effects: Elevated AVP binds V2 receptors in the renal collecting duct, triggering aquaporin‑2 insertion and increased free water reabsorption → concentrated urine (high urine osmolality), natriuresis with continued urinary sodium excretion, expansion of total body water relative to solute and dilutional hyponatraemia.

Linking the mechanism to the case
- The patient had hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia (serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L) while clinically euvolemic — fulfilling SIADH criteria. Measured plasma ADH was elevated (22.4 pg/mL) and fell to 1.8 pg/mL as the clinical syndrome resolved, supporting an ADH‑mediated process.
- CNS inflammatory evidence—aseptic meningitis on CSF (lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein), systemic inflammation (high CRP), and clinical neuro symptoms—supports neuro‑BD as the inflammatory trigger. The temporal association of colchicine initiation, clinical improvement, normalization of sodium, and fall in ADH level favors a causal chain: neuroinflammation → cytokine (notably IL‑6) mediated ADH release → SIADH.
- Although direct IL‑6 measurements are lacking in this patient, the pathophysiologic model is consistent with published observations that CSF IL‑6 is elevated in neuro‑BD and that IL‑6 stimulates AVP release in neuroinflammatory states.

Practical diagnostic and management points (teaching pearls)
- Recognize the pattern: hypotonic hyponatraemia + inappropriately concentrated urine + high urine Na + euvolemia + normal thyroid/adrenal function = SIADH. In patients with neuro‑inflammatory symptoms, consider cytokine‑driven SIADH.
- Treat both problems: acute management of SIADH is guided by severity (fluid restriction for mild/moderate, hypertonic saline for severe symptomatic hyponatraemia). Definitive therapy requires treating the underlying CNS inflammation. In this case colchicine (which suppresses IL‑6 production among other effects) was temporally associated with resolution of neuro symptoms, fall in ADH and correction of sodium.
- Be vigilant for related complications (eg, hiccups, rhabdomyolysis) that may be manifestations of severe hyponatraemia in neuroinflammatory disease.

Take‑home summary
CNS inflammation in neuro‑Behçet’s can trigger SIADH via intrathecal and systemic IL‑6 production that stimulates hypothalamic AVP synthesis and release, lowering the osmotic set point and causing water retention with concentrated urine. Recognition depends on standard SIADH criteria and is important because controlling the inflammatory driver (as in this case) can normalize ADH levels and correct the hyponatraemia.

### [Management] What is the standard first-line treatment strategy for acute parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease, and in what scenarios might colchicine monotherapy be considered or deemed insufficient?

Short answer
- The standard first‑line therapy for acute parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s disease is prompt high‑dose corticosteroids (typically IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by an oral prednisone taper) with early consideration of a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant for parenchymal disease (eg, azathioprine, methotrexate, cyclophosphamide or anti‑TNF agents) to reduce relapse and prevent permanent neurologic damage.
- Colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous and some joint manifestations of Behçet’s and may be considered as adjunctive therapy or — rarely — as monotherapy in very mild, non‑parenchymal presentations (eg, isolated aseptic meningitis without focal deficits and with close monitoring), but it is generally insufficient for true parenchymal neuro‑BD, severe meningoencephalitis, brainstem involvement, focal neurologic deficits or progressive disease.

Teaching points and clinical reasoning
- Why aggressive therapy for parenchymal neuro‑BD? Parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s reflects inflammatory involvement of brain tissue (classically brainstem but may be other sites) and carries a risk of permanent deficits and relapse. The disease mechanism is inflammatory/vasculitic (neutrophil‑predominant inflammation with cytokine release including IL‑6), so rapid suppression of inflammation with high‑dose corticosteroids is the accepted initial approach to limit tissue injury.
- Typical regimen used in practice: IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3–5 days for severe attacks, then transition to oral prednisone (eg, 0.5–1 mg/kg/day) with a gradual taper. For parenchymal disease most experts add a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant early (eg, azathioprine 2–3 mg/kg/day, methotrexate, monthly cyclophosphamide pulses for life‑ or organ‑threatening disease) or escalate to biologics (eg, anti‑TNF agents) for refractory or severe cases.
- Role of colchicine: colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and cytokine production and is effective for oral/genital ulcers, erythema nodosum–type lesions and arthralgias. However, systematic reviews and clinical experience indicate colchicine alone is generally not effective for acute parenchymal neuro‑BD; it is most appropriately used as adjunctive therapy for mucocutaneous disease or when steroid exposure must be minimized.
- When colchicine monotherapy might be considered: the presented case is instructive — the patient had simultaneous systemic BD and CNS involvement limited to aseptic meningitis (CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal MRI, no focal deficits). He improved rapidly on colchicine alone and avoided steroids. In similarly mild, non‑parenchymal neuro‑BD (eg, aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, no focal signs, and careful inpatient monitoring), a trial of colchicine with close observation and a low threshold to escalate can be reasonable—especially if steroids are contraindicated.
- When colchicine is insufficient (and when to escalate): presence of parenchymal lesions on MRI, brainstem involvement, new or worsening focal neurologic deficits, rapid clinical deterioration, severe meningoencephalitis, or lack of clinical improvement within days of therapy should prompt immediate corticosteroids and early addition of immunosuppressive therapy. Given the morbidity of delayed treatment in parenchymal neuro‑BD, do not rely on colchicine alone in those scenarios.

Applying this to the case
- This report is a useful exception rather than a change in standard practice: colchicine monotherapy coincided with resolution of mucocutaneous findings, aseptic meningitis and SIADH in a patient with no MRI parenchymal lesions and no focal deficits. The authors and the literature caution that most acute parenchymal neuro‑BD patients require corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppression; therefore colchicine should not replace steroid‑based therapy in parenchymal disease but may be considered in selected mild, non‑parenchymal presentations with close follow‑up and a low threshold to escalate.

### [Management] Outline a stepwise management plan for SIADH in a patient with neuroinflammation: target fluid restriction, indications to escalate to hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists, and safe limits for sodium correction over 24 and 48 hours.

Bottom line
- In neuroinflammatory SIADH the immediate priorities are (1) confirm euvolemic hypotonic hyponatraemia and exclude adrenal/thyroid failure or diuretic use, (2) institute conservative measures (fluid restriction) and frequent monitoring, and (3) escalate promptly to hypertonic saline for acute neurologic symptoms or to pharmacologic therapy (vaptans) only if conservative care fails. In the reported Behçet’s case the patient was euvolemic with serum Na 124 mmol/L, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg and ADH elevated; he responded to strict fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) with gradual correction to 136 mmol/L by day 16 without hypertonic saline or vaptans.

Stepwise management plan

1. Confirm the diagnosis and initial risk stratification (first hour)
- Confirm true hypotonic, euvolemic hyponatraemia: low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine (urine Osm >100 mOsm/kg; in this case 900), urine Na typically >30 mmol/L.
- Exclude adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism (serum cortisol, TSH/fT4 were normal in the case) and review medications (SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide).
- Determine acuity and symptom severity:
  - Severe/acute neurological features (seizure, coma, obtundation, severe obtundation/respiratory arrest) → treat as emergency.
  - Moderate symptoms (confusion, lethargy, severe nausea, persistent vomiting) → expedite correction.
  - Mild or asymptomatic (headache, malaise, hiccups as in this case) → conservative first.

2. Immediate initial measures for most patients (first hours → days)
- Fluid restriction: usual starting target 800–1000 mL/day. In patients with very concentrated urine (urine Osm >>500 mOsm/kg) restrict further (often 500–800 mL/day) because free-water excretion will be poor. In the reported patient clinicians used <800 mL/day with daily sodium checks and clinical monitoring with success.
- Salt intake / urea / loop diuretic: consider oral sodium chloride or loop diuretics (to reduce urine osmolality and permit water excretion) if fluid restriction insufficient or poorly tolerated.
- Treat the underlying cause: in neuroinflammatory SIADH, control CNS inflammation (in the case, colchicine correlated with clinical and sodium improvement). In many neuro-BD cases corticosteroids or other immunosuppression are used if indicated.
- Monitoring: check serum Na at least every 6–12 hours initially for moderate cases; hourly to q2h during active hypertonic therapy or if patient is unstable.

3. Indications to escalate to hypertonic saline
- Immediate 3% hypertonic saline is indicated for severe symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, obtundation, impending herniation) or for very profound acute hyponatraemia (typically Na ≤120 mmol/L with neurologic signs).
- Recommended acute regimen: intermittent 100 mL boluses of 3% saline (some centers 150 mL) repeated up to 2 more times until clinical improvement, or continuous infusion titrated to target rise. Aim for a modest, rapid rise of 4–6 mmol/L over the first several hours to reverse life‑threatening cerebral oedema.
- After symptom control, slow further correction to stay within safe limits (see below).
- In the Behçet’s patient, absence of severe neurologic compromise allowed avoidance of hypertonic saline.

4. Indications to consider vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans)
- Consider tolvaptan or other vaptans for euvolemic SIADH that is chronic or refractory to fluid restriction and conservative measures, particularly when oral therapy is acceptable and rapid control of hyponatraemia is needed but there are no severe neurologic symptoms.
- Do NOT use vaptans as first-line for acute severe symptomatic hyponatraemia – hypertonic saline is preferred.
- Caveats: vaptans can produce rapid water diuresis and overcorrection → require close monitoring (Na q6–8 h at minimum); avoid in patients unable to respond to thirst or with liver disease (tolvaptan hepatotoxicity). Use short courses and specialist input.

5. Preventing and managing overcorrection
- Safe correction limits: standard pragmatic targets are
  - For most patients: ≤10–12 mmol/L increase in serum Na in the first 24 hours and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours.
  - For high-risk patients (chronic hyponatraemia, alcoholism, malnutrition, advanced liver disease, hypokalaemia): be more conservative — aim for ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours and ≤16 mmol/L in 48 hours.
- If overcorrection is occurring (rise faster than limits), intervene immediately: give IV dextrose 5% and/or desmopressin (DDAVP) to re-lower/“clamp” free-water loss and prevent osmotic demyelination. This is a standard rescue manoeuvre.
- Documentation: record baseline Na, target increment, and hourly goals; communicate clearly among the team.

6. Monitoring schedule (practical)
- Severe symptomatic or receiving hypertonic saline: Na every 1–2 hours until stable, then every 4–6 hours.
- Moderate symptoms / initiating vaptan: Na every 6–8 hours for first 24–48 hours.
- Mild/asymptomatic on fluid restriction alone: Na daily or q12h depending on urine Osm and clinical trajectory (in the case, daily monitoring sufficed and sodium normalized over 2 weeks).

Application to the presented case
- The patient was euvolemic with Na 124 mmol/L, urine Osm 900 mOsm/kg and no severe neurologic compromise. Team appropriately confirmed endocrine causes were excluded (normal cortisol, TSH), instituted strict fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), closely monitored Na, and treated the underlying inflammatory disease with colchicine. This conservative approach led to gradual, safe normalization of sodium to 136 mmol/L by day 16 without hypertonic saline or vaptans—an expected outcome when SIADH is driven by a reversible neuroinflammatory process and the patient is clinically stable.

Bottom-line practical rules
- Reserve hypertonic saline for life‑threatening or severely symptomatic hyponatraemia.
- Use fluid restriction as first-line in stable neuro-SIADH, tightened when urine is very concentrated.
- Consider vaptans only for refractory or chronic SIADH with specialist input and close sodium monitoring.
- Never exceed recommended correction limits (≤8–10 mmol/L/24 h for high-risk; ≤10–12 mmol/L/24 h for others; ≤18 mmol/L/48 h), and be ready to give DDAVP/dextrose if overcorrection threatens osmotic demyelination.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Differentiate SIADH from cerebral salt wasting and from hypovolaemic hyponatraemia in patients with CNS disease: which clinical and laboratory features (volume status, urine sodium/osmolality, uric acid/fractional excretion of urate, and response to isotonic saline) guide the diagnosis and how does management differ?

Bottom line
- In patients with CNS disease who are hyponatraemic, the key clinical distinction is between (1) SIADH (euvolemic, inappropriate ADH release), (2) cerebral salt wasting (CSW; primary renal salt loss with intravascular volume depletion), and (3) other hypovolaemic hyponatraemia (extrarenal or renal salt losses, diuretics). Treatment diverges: SIADH → fluid restriction ± measures to increase free water excretion; CSW/hypovolaemic states → restore intravascular volume with isotonic (or hypertonic if severe) saline and replace sodium.

How to differentiate — practical features and expected results

1) Clinical volume status
- SIADH: clinically euvolemic (no oedema, no orthostatic hypotension, no dry mucous membranes, no recent weight loss).  
- CSW: hypovolaemic (tachycardia, orthostasis, low JVP, dry mucosa, weight loss). Findings can be subtle in hospitalised patients, so serial weights, orthostatic vitals and fluid balance are helpful.  
- Other hypovolaemic hyponatraemia: hypovolaemic by exam; history points to extrarenal losses (vomiting, diarrhea, sweating) or diuretics.

Case tie‑in: the patient was assessed as euvolemic (no peripheral oedema, normotensive), arguing against CSW.

2) Urine osmolality and urine sodium
- SIADH: serum hypo‑osmolality with inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmol >100–300 mOsm/kg; often very high), and urine Na typically >30–40 mmol/L because kidney is not conserving sodium.  
- CSW: urine osmol also concentrated, urine Na generally high (>40 mmol/L) reflecting renal salt wasting.  
- Hypovolaemic extrarenal losses: urine Na low (<20 mmol/L) as the kidney avidly retains sodium (unless diuretics or intrinsic renal losses present).

Case tie‑in: urine osmol 900 mOsm/kg and urine Na 88 mmol/L — both compatible with either SIADH or CSW, not with extrarenal hypovolaemia.

3) Serum uric acid and fractional excretion of urate (FEurate)
- SIADH: hypouricaemia with increased FEurate (>10–12%) while hyponatraemic; FEurate typically normalises after correction of hyponatraemia.  
- CSW: hypouricaemia and elevated FEurate can be seen as well, but the key difference is that FEurate remains elevated after volume repletion/correction of sodium.  
- Hypovolaemic extrarenal: uric acid often elevated (prerenal retention) and FEurate low.

Practical point: measure serum urate and calculate FEurate at presentation and again after partial correction of volume/sodium — persistent elevated FEurate after correction points to CSW.

4) Response to isotonic saline
- SIADH: isotonic saline often fails to raise serum sodium and can worsen hyponatraemia if administered as the kidney excretes sodium while retaining free water (particularly when urine osmolality > serum osmolality). Fluid restriction or aquaretics are effective.  
- CSW/hypovolaemic hyponatraemia: isotonic saline corrects intravascular volume, suppresses ADH, and raises serum sodium; sodium improves with volume repletion.  
- Exception: if simultaneous SIADH and hypovolaemia exist, response may be mixed — interpret in clinical context.

Case tie‑in: the patient’s hyponatraemia responded to fluid restriction and recovered as CNS inflammation improved with colchicine; isotonic saline was not required — consistent with SIADH rather than CSW.

Other essential steps
- Exclude endocrine causes: check serum cortisol and thyroid function (normal in this case).  
- Review medications and recent treatments (e.g., diuretics, cyclophosphamide) that can cause SIADH or salt wasting.  
- In CNS disease, measure and follow ADH (or copeptin when available), serum urate/FEurate, and document weight/orthostatic changes and urine output.

Management differences — concise rules
- SIADH
  - First-line: fluid restriction (typical target <800–1000 mL/day depending on severity).  
  - If severe symptomatic hyponatraemia: hypertonic (3%) saline with careful correction to avoid osmotic demyelination.  
  - For chronic/resistant cases: oral salt + loop diuretic, urea, or vasopressin receptor antagonists (tolvaptan) in selected cases. Treat underlying cause (in CNS disease, treat the CNS process).  
- CSW / hypovolaemic hyponatraemia
  - Restore intravascular volume with isotonic saline (or hypertonic if symptomatic). Replace ongoing salt losses; consider fludrocortisone for refractory CSW. Stop offending diuretics; correct adrenal/renal causes.  
- In both, avoid overly rapid correction and monitor neurologic status and sodium trends closely.

Pathophysiology note (relevant to the case)
- CNS inflammation commonly triggers non‑osmotic ADH release (SIADH); inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑6 can stimulate ADH/vasopressin secretion. The reported patient with acute neuro‑Behçet’s had high inflammatory markers and elevated ADH; treating the CNS inflammation (colchicine) coincided with fall in ADH and normalization of sodium — a pattern characteristic of SIADH.

Takeaway for morning report
- When hyponatraemia accompanies CNS disease, start with careful bedside volume assessment, urine osmolality and urine sodium, and exclude adrenal/thyroid causes.  
- If euvolemic, urine osmol high and urine Na high → think SIADH (treat with fluid restriction, treat CNS inflammation).  
- If hypovolaemic with high urine Na → think CSW (treat with volume and sodium replacement).  
- Use serum urate/FEurate and the response to isotonic saline (and the FEurate after correction) to resolve cases where history/exam are ambiguous. In this case, the clinical euvolaemia, concentrated urine, high urine sodium, elevated ADH and response to conservative SIADH therapy with correction after anti‑inflammatory treatment all point to SIADH rather than CSW.

### [Clinical Pearls] What neuroanatomical regions are most commonly affected on MRI in parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease, and why can imaging be normal in the early phase despite clinical CNS involvement?

Direct answer: In parenchymal neuro–Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) the MRI lesions most classically involve the brainstem (especially the pons and midbrain), deep grey nuclei (thalamus, basal ganglia) and deep subcortical white matter — lesions are typically T2/FLAIR hyperintense, may enhance, and can be multiple or confluent. The hypothalamus and periventricular region can also be involved (and when they are, endocrine disturbances such as SIADH may follow), but hypothalamic lesions are less frequent than brainstem and deep‑structure lesions.

Why MRI can be normal early despite clear CNS involvement:
- Early neuro‑BD often presents as aseptic meningoencephalitis or perivascular inflammation that is microscopic and predominantly cellular (perivascular neutrophilic/lymphocytic infiltrates and small‑vessel vasculitis). These changes can produce symptoms and CSF abnormalities (pleocytosis, elevated protein) without producing a focal parenchymal lesion large enough to alter MRI signal.
- Functional disturbance of neuroendocrine pathways (for example dysregulated ADH release from hypothalamic neurons) can occur from inflammatory cytokines (IL‑6 and others) or subtle inflammatory changes that do not produce a visible structural lesion on routine MRI sequences.
- Technical and timing issues: small or early inflammatory lesions may be below the spatial resolution of standard MRI sequences or may not yet have developed the edema/necrosis that causes T2/FLAIR signal change. Contrast enhancement and repeat imaging (including targeted thin‑slice pituitary/hypothalamic sequences, FLAIR, diffusion, and gadolinium‑enhanced T1) performed later often reveal evolving lesions that were initially occult.

Link to the case: this patient had aseptic meningitis on CSF but a normal brain MRI (including the hypothalamic region) early in the course — a well‑recognized scenario in acute neuro‑BD. His hyponatraemia and elevated ADH could reflect hypothalamic dysfunction from inflammatory cytokines or microscopic inflammation not visualized on MRI. Thus, in suspected neuro‑BD a normal early MRI does not exclude CNS involvement; clinical assessment and CSF studies guide diagnosis, and repeat/dedicated imaging is indicated if symptoms persist or evolve.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which under-recognised manifestations in this case should prompt urgent evaluation for severe hyponatraemia, and what muscle complication can accompany profound sodium derangements?

Answer (clinical pearl)

In this case the two under‑recognised clues that should prompt urgent measurement of serum sodium are: persistent hiccups and otherwise unexplained systemic/neurologic symptoms (worsening headache, vertigo, malaise) in the setting of CNS inflammation. The patient had persistent hiccups on presentation and later developed marked hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L) from SIADH — hiccups are a well‑described but often overlooked manifestation of severe hyponatraemia, particularly when CNS inflammation is present.

The muscle complication to watch for is rhabdomyolysis. This patient had an elevated CK (1349 U/L) that normalised as the sodium corrected. Profound hypotonicity can cause muscle‑cell swelling, energy failure and intracellular Ca2+ overload (and seizures can also contribute), leading to myocyte breakdown and release of CK/myoglobin.

Clinical reasoning and practical points
- Why hiccups matter: hiccups reflect irritation of central or peripheral reflex arcs (brainstem, phrenic/vagal nerves) and have been reported as a clue to meningitis/CNS inflammation and to severe hyponatraemia/SIADH. In a patient with BD and new CNS symptoms, new persistent hiccups should trigger an urgent electrolyte check.
- Diagnosing the cause: in hyponatraemia evaluate serum osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium to identify SIADH (as in this case: low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality and urine Na). Assess volume status and exclude adrenal/thyroid causes.
- Management implications: treat life‑threatening/symptomatic hyponatraemia emergently (controlled hypertonic saline), otherwise initial management of SIADH is fluid restriction and addressing the underlying CNS inflammation (this patient improved with colchicine and fluid restriction). Monitor sodium correction carefully to avoid osmotic demyelination. If rhabdomyolysis is present, monitor CK, renal function, potassium and urine output and treat (typically with aggressive IV fluids and supportive care), balancing fluid needs against the fluid‑restriction strategy for SIADH under close monitoring.

Bottom line: new persistent hiccups (or unexplained worsening headache/neurologic symptoms) in a patient with CNS inflammation should prompt an urgent check of serum sodium — and remember that profound hyponatraemia can be complicated by rhabdomyolysis.

### [Morning Report] In the initial workup of fever, headache, and hyponatraemia in a patient with mucocutaneous lesions, which targeted tests are high-yield (e.g., CSF studies including viral PCRs, TB testing, cultures) and which screening markers (e.g., ACE, soluble IL-2 receptor, HLA-B51) have limited standalone diagnostic value and should be interpreted cautiously?

Short answer
- High‑yield, front‑line tests: focused CSF studies (opening pressure, cell count with differential, protein, glucose), Gram stain and bacterial cultures, CSF viral PCRs for HSV/VZV (and enterovirus when epidemiologically appropriate), CSF fungal tests/cryptococcal antigen and mycobacterial testing (AFB smear, TB PCR/culture) when risk factors exist, and CSF cytology if malignancy is a concern. Simultaneously obtain serum and urine studies to characterise the hyponatraemia (serum and urine osmolality, urine sodium), blood cultures, basic inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR), and brain MRI with contrast. These tests directly address the treatable infections and structural causes that must be excluded urgently.
- Limited standalone markers: serum ACE, serum soluble IL‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) and HLA‑B51 have only adjunctive value and should be interpreted cautiously in the clinical context — they are neither sensitive nor specific enough to establish neurosarcoidosis, lymphoma or Behçet’s disease on their own.

Teaching points and clinical reasoning (grounded in the case)
1) Start by ruling out the immediately treatable causes of fever, headache and hyponatraemia
- CNS infection (bacterial meningitis, HSV/VZV encephalitis, TB meningitis, cryptococcal disease) must be excluded promptly because management is time‑sensitive. The most informative tests are CSF cell count/differential, protein, glucose, opening pressure, Gram stain and bacterial cultures plus targeted pathogen testing (HSV/VZV PCR, TB PCR/AFB smear and culture when suspected, cryptococcal antigen/fungal culture). In the reported case these CSF studies (including viral PCRs, TB testing and cultures) were negative and helped safely stop empiric antimicrobials.
- Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics, and MRI brain with contrast early — negative imaging does not exclude early neuroinflammation (as in this case, MRI was normal despite aseptic meningitis).

2) Evaluate and manage the hyponatraemia in parallel
- Classify the hyponatraemia (serum osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium) and exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency. Measure volume status clinically. In SIADH expect hypo‑osmolar serum, inappropriately concentrated urine and natriuresis — exactly what this patient had. ADH assays are available but variable in utility; management should be guided by the biochemical pattern and clinical state (fluid restriction, avoid rapid correction).
- Remember CNS inflammation is a recognized cause of SIADH. Cytokines such as IL‑6 can stimulate ADH release; the case authors propose IL‑6–mediated ADH dysregulation as a plausible mechanism even though CSF IL‑6 was not measured.

3) Use targeted ancillary tests guided by pretest probability, not as screeners
- Serum ACE: can support suspicion for sarcoidosis when elevated, but has limited sensitivity and specificity (normal ACE does not exclude neurosarcoidosis). In this case ACE and lysozyme were normal and did not advance the diagnosis.
- sIL‑2R: may be raised in lymphoma and sarcoidosis, but mild elevations are non‑specific and occur in many inflammatory states; a solitary mildly elevated sIL‑2R should not drive a diagnosis of lymphoma without corroborating imaging, PET, CSF cytology or biopsy.
- HLA‑B51: associated with increased risk of Behçet’s disease but neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis. Positive HLA‑B51 in this patient was supportive but not diagnostic; the diagnosis depended on clinical mucocutaneous criteria and exclusion of other causes (the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease are clinical).

4) Practical algorithm for similar patients
- While awaiting results: empiric therapy when clinical suspicion for bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis is moderate/high (after cultures). In the case reported, empiric cefepime and later acyclovir were appropriately used and discontinued when tests were negative.
- Perform a focused CSF panel first. Add TB/fungal testing and CSF cytology when history, exposures, imaging or persistent abnormal CSF suggest them.
- Order ACE, sIL‑2R or HLA typing only when the clinical picture and imaging raise specific concern for sarcoidosis, lymphoma or Behçet’s disease, respectively; interpret them as supportive, not definitive.

Bottom line
In a patient with fever, headache, hyponatraemia and mucocutaneous lesions, prioritize CSF studies (including targeted viral PCRs, bacterial/fungal/mycobacterial tests and cytology), serum/urine osmolalities and early brain imaging — these are high‑yield and guide immediate management. Treat ACE, sIL‑2R and HLA‑B51 as adjunctive data points with limited standalone diagnostic value; rely on the clinical exam, imaging, CSF, and, when needed, tissue diagnosis to make the definitive call. This approach mirrors the case: targeted CSF testing excluded infection, broad screening markers were non‑diagnostic, and the diagnosis of acute neuro‑Behçet’s rested on characteristic mucocutaneous findings plus aseptic meningitis and exclusion of other causes.
