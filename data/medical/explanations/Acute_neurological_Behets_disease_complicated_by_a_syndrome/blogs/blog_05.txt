Title: Blog about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

# IL‑6, ADH, and the Inflamed Brain: Why SIADH Happens in Neuro‑BD

What makes this case interesting: a man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, mucocutaneous Behçet’s disease (BD) findings and hyponatraemia. He had aseptic meningitis on CSF, no MRI parenchymal lesions, and biochemical SIADH (low serum osmolality, concentrated urine, inappropriately high ADH). Everything — the ulcers, the neurologic symptoms and the hyponatraemia — settled after colchicine alone. This is a tidy clinical vignette linking neuroinflammation to non‑osmotic ADH release, and it’s a useful springboard to discuss mechanisms you can act on at the bedside.

Why inflammation begets hyponatraemia: the short version
- Central nervous system inflammation can trigger non‑osmotic ADH (vasopressin) release. The usual suspects for SIADH (CNS infection, malignancy, drugs) overlap with inflammatory disorders.
- Proinflammatory cytokines — particularly interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) — are implicated in driving ADH secretion from the hypothalamus/posterior pituitary and in sensitising osmoreceptors.
- Neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) is a prototypical CNS inflammatory disorder where CSF IL‑6 is often elevated. So when neuro‑BD presents acutely (meningoencephalitis/aseptic meningitis), SIADH is physiologically plausible.

Pathophysiology in a bit more detail (clinical focus)
- Cytokine‑mediated ADH release: IL‑6 can act centrally to increase ADH secretion independent of plasma osmolality. Experimental and clinical work across different CNS inflammatory conditions shows IL‑6 rises in CSF and accompanies inappropriate vasopressin release.
- Hypothalamic involvement not required: You don’t need a focal hypothalamic lesion on MRI to get SIADH. Diffuse meningeal inflammation or parenchymal cytokine spillover can be sufficient to provoke non‑osmotic ADH release.
- Systemic markers as practical proxies: Direct CSF IL‑6 measurement is not routinely available. Serum CRP (and clinical signs of systemic inflammation) may serve as a pragmatic surrogate flag for an active inflammatory milieu that could include elevated central IL‑6.
- Reverse causal thinking: Treating the inflammation — not just restricting water or antagonising ADH — often leads to sustained correction of sodium because the stimulus for inappropriate ADH disappears.

Putting it into practice: how this helps you manage a patient
- Think SIADH in any patient with fever/headache and hyponatraemia plus clinical or laboratory evidence of CNS inflammation. Don’t assume dilutional hyponatraemia from another source without considering CNS causes.
- Initial workup still follows standard hyponatraemia steps: confirm hypoosmolar hyponatraemia, assess volume status (many neuro‑inflammatory SIADH patients are clinically euvolemic), measure urine osmolality and urine sodium, exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency.
- Use inflammatory clues. High CRP, systemic mucocutaneous BD stigmata, headache/meningitic symptoms and CSF lymphocytosis/protein should raise your pre‑test probability that SIADH is inflammation‑driven.
- Treat the underlying inflammation promptly. In many CNS inflammatory states, definitive treatment (antibiotics/antivirals when indicated, steroids or disease‑specific immunotherapy where appropriate) removes the ADH stimulus and corrects sodium. This Behçet’s case is notable because colchicine (anti‑neutrophil and anti‑cytokine effects) alone coincided with recovery — suggesting that targeted anti‑inflammatory therapy can be enough.
- Manage sodium safely while you treat the inflammation. Fluid restriction is often firstline for mild–moderate SIADH. Avoid overly rapid correction; reserve hypertonic saline or vaptans for severe symptoms or refractory cases, and be cautious in inflammatory CNS disease where osmotic shifts can be risky.

Clinical pearls
- Neuro‑BD can present simultaneously with systemic BD and may lack MRI lesions early on — aseptic meningitis with CSF lymphocytosis is enough to suspect neuro involvement.
- SIADH does not require visible hypothalamic disease on imaging. Consider it when low serum osmolality coexists with high urine osmolality and urine sodium despite euvolemia.
- IL‑6 is a plausible mediator linking CNS inflammation to ADH secretion. You won’t usually measure CSF IL‑6 in routine care, but elevated CRP and CSF lymphocytosis increase the likelihood that inflammation is driving ADH.
- Treating the inflammation often treats the hyponatraemia. While fluid restriction buys time, addressing the inflammatory driver (e.g., corticosteroids or disease‑directed therapy) is key for durable correction.
- Colchicine has anti‑cytokine effects (including IL‑6 suppression) and is standard for mucocutaneous BD; while not generally recommended for neuro‑BD monotherapy, this case shows it may be effective in selected, milder inflammatory presentations. Interpret with caution — most neuro‑BD patients still require corticosteroids or stronger immunosuppression.

Take‑home messages
- Consider SIADH when hyponatraemia coexists with CNS inflammatory symptoms; MRI can be normal in early neuroinflammatory disease.
- IL‑6 is a plausible biological link between neuroinflammation (including neuro‑BD) and non‑osmotic ADH release; CRP and CSF findings can act as practical proxies.
- Manage the sodium safely (fluid restriction, avoid rapid correction) but prioritise treating the underlying inflammation — resolving the cytokine driver often normalises ADH and sodium.
- Use disease‑specific therapies based on severity; rare cases may respond to less aggressive anti‑inflammatory therapy (e.g., colchicine), but most neuro‑BD requires steroids/immunosuppression.

Bottom line: when the brain is inflamed, it can tell the pituitary to hoard water. Recognise the pattern, treat the inflammation, and correct the sodium carefully.
