Title: Blog about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

# When Behçet’s and Neuro‑Behçet’s Debut Together: Diagnosing the MRI‑Negative Presentation

What makes this case interesting? A man in his 60s presented with fever, headache and mucocutaneous lesions that met the International Criteria for Behçet’s disease — and simultaneously developed aseptic meningitis with marked hyponatraemia from SIADH. Brain MRI was normal. CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. He improved dramatically on colchicine alone (no systemic steroids), with sodium normalising over two weeks. This sequence highlights three clinically important points: neuro‑Behçet’s can debut concurrently with systemic BD, early neuro‑BD may be MRI‑negative, and CNS inflammation can trigger SIADH — a potentially overlooked clue.

## Quick case synopsis (stick to the facts)
- Patient: Asian man in his 60s, 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, persistent hiccups, arthralgia; oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rash on face/trunk on examination.  
- Course: Marked hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L), high CRP (180 mg/L), CK 1349 U/L. Serum osmolality low, urine osmolality high (900 mOsm/kg), urine Na 88 mmol/L, ADH elevated → SIADH.  
- Neuro workup: Head MRI normal. CSF: opening pressure 17 cm H2O, protein 0.97 g/L, lymphocytes 11/µL, cultures and viral PCRs negative → aseptic meningitis. HLA‑B51 positive. Genital ulcer appeared on day 5 — fulfilled International Criteria for BD (score ≥4).  
- Treatment & outcome: Empiric antimicrobials/acyclovir until infections excluded. Colchicine started on day 8; mucocutaneous, neurologic symptoms and hyponatraemia improved without steroids. Na normalised to 136 mmol/L by day 16. Colchicine continued for ~4 years with no recurrence.

## Recognising neuro‑Behçet’s when mucocutaneous features accrue over days
Neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) most commonly appears years after initial systemic disease, but concurrent presentation is possible. Key clinical clues that should raise your index of suspicion:
- New or worsening headache, fever, behavioral change, in a patient with oral/genital ulcers or characteristic skin lesions.
- New persistent hiccups or unexplained vertigo — these can accompany brainstem or meningeal inflammation and, as in this case, be seen with hyponatraemia.
- CSF demonstrating aseptic meningitis (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein) in the setting of mucocutaneous BD features.

Use the International Criteria for Behçet’s disease to classify systemic disease — it’s a clinical score; HLA‑B51 is supportive but not required and should not drive the diagnosis.

## MRI can be normal early — how to interpret a negative scan
Do not let a normal MRI exclude neuro‑BD. Early inflammatory meningoencephalitis may not yet produce focal parenchymal lesions. Practical imaging advice:
- A normal non‑contrast brain MRI early in the course is common in acute aseptic meningoencephalitis from BD.
- If clinical suspicion is high, repeat imaging. Consider:
  - Repeat MRI with dedicated brainstem sequences, high‑resolution T2/FLAIR, contrast enhancement, and diffusion‑weighted imaging.
  - Consider spinal MRI if myelopathic signs develop.
- Timing: repeat within days to 1–2 weeks if symptoms progress or persist; sooner if focal deficits or deterioration.
- Keep a low threshold to escalate the diagnostic workup (repeat MRI, repeat LP, PET/CT) if the clinical picture worsens or new signs appear — MRI sensitivity improves as parenchymal inflammation evolves.

## CSF interpretation — small pleocytosis matters
The CSF here showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11/µL) and elevated protein — a pattern compatible with aseptic meningitis and neuro‑BD. Important points:
- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein, negative cultures and viral PCRs, and an appropriate clinical context (mucocutaneous BD signs) should prompt consideration of neuro‑BD.
- Always exclude infections (HSV/VZV, TB, bacterial) and malignancy before labelling inflammatory neuro‑BD.
- If CSF is non‑diagnostic but suspicion persists, consider repeat LP after 48–72 hours, especially if clinical course evolves.

## SIADH as a clue — link to CNS inflammation
This case had clear SIADH: hypoosmolar hyponatraemia, concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), urine Na 88 mmol/L, euvolaemia, and elevated ADH. Clinical points:
- SIADH can be provoked by CNS inflammation. Inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑6 can stimulate ADH release; BD is a proinflammatory disease with documented CSF IL‑6 elevation in neuro‑BD.
- Presentation: persistent hiccups, malaise, and even rhabdomyolysis (CK 1349 U/L in this case) can be manifestations/complications of significant hyponatraemia — don’t miss them.
- Management: fluid restriction is first‑line for mild–moderate SIADH. Avoid rapid correction of sodium (risk osmotic demyelination). Hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists only when severe/symptomatic hyponatraemia or refractory to conservative management.
- Always assess volume status carefully and exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency (TSH, free T4, random cortisol were normal in this case).

## Treatment decision‑making in early neuro‑BD — steroids, colchicine, or watchful waiting?
- Standard teaching: acute neuro‑BD (especially parenchymal disease or severe deficits) is usually treated with high‑dose corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, anti‑TNF in some cases).
- However, some forms of acute meningoencephalitis associated with BD may be self‑limited. Colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous BD and has anti‑inflammatory effects (reduces neutrophil activation and cytokine release including IL‑6). Evidence for colchicine as monotherapy for neuro‑BD is limited.
- Practical approach:
  - If patient has focal deficits, rapidly progressive disease, or MRI/CSF strongly suggesting parenchymal neuro‑BD → start corticosteroids (and involve neuro/rheumatology) promptly.
  - If presentation is limited to aseptic meningitis without focal deficits, MRI negative, and the patient is clinically stable, discuss the option of colchicine and close monitoring; consider deferring steroids while excluding infectious causes. This case illustrates successful colchicine monotherapy, but spontaneous resolution cannot be excluded and this is not standard of care.
  - Individualise treatment after shared decision making and multidisciplinary input.

## Practical ED/inpatient checklist when you suspect neuro‑BD (MRI may be normal)
- Full clinical exam with careful search for mucocutaneous findings (oral/genital ulcers, erythema nodosum or folliculitis‑like lesions, ocular symptoms).
- Labs: CBC, electrolytes (focus on Na), glucose, creatinine, LFTs, CRP/ESR, CK (consider if hyponatraemia), thyroid function, random cortisol.
- Hyponatraemia workup: serum/urine osmolality, urine sodium, assess volume status, consider ADH level if available.
- Neuro workup: urgent head MRI with contrast and focused sequences; LP with cell count, protein, glucose, bacterial culture, TB testing when indicated, and viral PCR (HSV/VZV). Send CSF for cytology if malignancy suspected.
- Infectious exclusion: empiric antiviral/antibacterial therapy while awaiting tests if clinically warranted.
- Early specialty involvement: neurology, rheumatology, infectious disease, and endocrinology if SIADH present.
- Close monitoring: vitals, neuro exam, daily Na, and avoid rapid Na shifts.

## Clinical pearls
- Neuro‑Behçet’s can present at initial diagnosis of systemic BD; do not wait years for CNS involvement. Look for genital ulcers or evolving mucocutaneous signs over days.
- A normal early MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD — repeat imaging if clinical suspicion persists or symptoms progress.
- CSF with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in a patient with BD features is supportive of acute neuro‑BD after infections and malignancy are excluded.
- SIADH can be a manifestation of CNS inflammation from neuro‑BD; persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis should prompt electrolyte review.
- Colchicine treats mucocutaneous BD and may help early, mild neuro‑inflammatory presentations, but evidence for colchicine monotherapy in neuro‑BD is limited — steroids remain the standard for parenchymal disease or severe presentations.

## Take‑home messages
- Consider neuro‑Behçet’s when a patient with oral/genital ulcers or characteristic skin lesions presents with aseptic meningitis or unexplained headache — MRI can be normal early.
- Always exclude infection and malignancy before labelling neuro‑BD; use CSF analysis and repeat imaging when necessary.
- SIADH may accompany CNS inflammation from neuro‑BD; manage with fluid restriction and careful sodium monitoring, and watch for complications (hiccups, rhabdomyolysis).
- Treatment is individualized: start corticosteroids for parenchymal/severe disease; colchicine may be reasonable for mucocutaneous disease and potentially for mild aseptic neuro‑inflammation, but multidisciplinary discussion is advised.

This case is a useful reminder: scan the skin and mucosa, interpret CSF in context, don’t be reassured by a normal early MRI, and watch the sodium.
