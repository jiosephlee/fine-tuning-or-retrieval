Title: Blog about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

# When Behçet’s and Neuro‑Behçet’s Debut Together: Diagnosing the MRI‑Negative Presentation

What makes this case interesting? A man in his 60s presented with fever, headache and mucocutaneous lesions that met the International Criteria for Behçet’s disease — and simultaneously developed aseptic meningitis with marked hyponatraemia from SIADH. Brain MRI was normal. CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. He improved dramatically on colchicine alone (no systemic steroids), with sodium normalising over two weeks. This sequence highlights three clinically important points: neuro‑Behçet’s can debut concurrently with systemic BD, early neuro‑BD may be MRI‑negative, and CNS inflammation can trigger SIADH — a potentially overlooked clue.

## Quick case synopsis (stick to the facts)
- Patient: Asian man in his 60s, 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, persistent hiccups, arthralgia; oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rash on face/trunk on examination.  
- Course: Marked hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L), high CRP (180 mg/L), CK 1349 U/L. Serum osmolality low, urine osmolality high (900 mOsm/kg), urine Na 88 mmol/L, ADH elevated → SIADH.  
- Neuro workup: Head MRI normal. CSF: opening pressure 17 cm H2O, protein 0.97 g/L, lymphocytes 11/µL, cultures and viral PCRs negative → aseptic meningitis. HLA‑B51 positive. Genital ulcer appeared on day 5 — fulfilled International Criteria for BD (score ≥4).  
- Treatment & outcome: Empiric antimicrobials/acyclovir until infections excluded. Colchicine started on day 8; mucocutaneous, neurologic symptoms and hyponatraemia improved without steroids. Na normalised to 136 mmol/L by day 16. Colchicine continued for ~4 years with no recurrence.

## Recognising neuro‑Behçet’s when mucocutaneous features accrue over days
Neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) most commonly appears years after initial systemic disease, but concurrent presentation is possible. Key clinical clues that should raise your index of suspicion:
- New or worsening headache, fever, behavioral change, in a patient with oral/genital ulcers or characteristic skin lesions.
- New persistent hiccups or unexplained vertigo — these can accompany brainstem or meningeal inflammation and, as in this case, be seen with hyponatraemia.
- CSF demonstrating aseptic meningitis (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein) in the setting of mucocutaneous BD features.

Use the International Criteria for Behçet’s disease to classify systemic disease — it’s a clinical score; HLA‑B51 is supportive but not required and should not drive the diagnosis.

## MRI can be normal early — how to interpret a negative scan
Do not let a normal MRI exclude neuro‑BD. Early inflammatory meningoencephalitis may not yet produce focal parenchymal lesions. Practical imaging advice:
- A normal non‑contrast brain MRI early in the course is common in acute aseptic meningoencephalitis from BD.
- If clinical suspicion is high, repeat imaging. Consider:
  - Repeat MRI with dedicated brainstem sequences, high‑resolution T2/FLAIR, contrast enhancement, and diffusion‑weighted imaging.
  - Consider spinal MRI if myelopathic signs develop.
- Timing: repeat within days to 1–2 weeks if symptoms progress or persist; sooner if focal deficits or deterioration.
- Keep a low threshold to escalate the diagnostic workup (repeat MRI, repeat LP, PET/CT) if the clinical picture worsens or new signs appear — MRI sensitivity improves as parenchymal inflammation evolves.

## CSF interpretation — small pleocytosis matters
The CSF here showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11/µL) and elevated protein — a pattern compatible with aseptic meningitis and neuro‑BD. Important points:
- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein, negative cultures and viral PCRs, and an appropriate clinical context (mucocutaneous BD signs) should prompt consideration of neuro‑BD.
- Always exclude infections (HSV/VZV, TB, bacterial) and malignancy before labelling inflammatory neuro‑BD.
- If CSF is non‑diagnostic but suspicion persists, consider repeat LP after 48–72 hours, especially if clinical course evolves.

## SIADH as a clue — link to CNS inflammation
This case had clear SIADH: hypoosmolar hyponatraemia, concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), urine Na 88 mmol/L, euvolaemia, and elevated ADH. Clinical points:
- SIADH can be provoked by CNS inflammation. Inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑6 can stimulate ADH release; BD is a proinflammatory disease with documented CSF IL‑6 elevation in neuro‑BD.
- Presentation: persistent hiccups, malaise, and even rhabdomyolysis (CK 1349 U/L in this case) can be manifestations/complications of significant hyponatraemia — don’t miss them.
- Management: fluid restriction is first‑line for mild–moderate SIADH. Avoid rapid correction of sodium (risk osmotic demyelination). Hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists only when severe/symptomatic hyponatraemia or refractory to conservative management.
- Always assess volume status carefully and exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency (TSH, free T4, random cortisol were normal in this case).

## Treatment decision‑making in early neuro‑BD — steroids, colchicine, or watchful waiting?
- Standard teaching: acute neuro‑BD (especially parenchymal disease or severe deficits) is usually treated with high‑dose corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, anti‑TNF in some cases).
- However, some forms of acute meningoencephalitis associated with BD may be self‑limited. Colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous BD and has anti‑inflammatory effects (reduces neutrophil activation and cytokine release including IL‑6). Evidence for colchicine as monotherapy for neuro‑BD is limited.
- Practical approach:
  - If patient has focal deficits, rapidly progressive disease, or MRI/CSF strongly suggesting parenchymal neuro‑BD → start corticosteroids (and involve neuro/rheumatology) promptly.
  - If presentation is limited to aseptic meningitis without focal deficits, MRI negative, and the patient is clinically stable, discuss the option of colchicine and close monitoring; consider deferring steroids while excluding infectious causes. This case illustrates successful colchicine monotherapy, but spontaneous resolution cannot be excluded and this is not standard of care.
  - Individualise treatment after shared decision making and multidisciplinary input.

## Practical ED/inpatient checklist when you suspect neuro‑BD (MRI may be normal)
- Full clinical exam with careful search for mucocutaneous findings (oral/genital ulcers, erythema nodosum or folliculitis‑like lesions, ocular symptoms).
- Labs: CBC, electrolytes (focus on Na), glucose, creatinine, LFTs, CRP/ESR, CK (consider if hyponatraemia), thyroid function, random cortisol.
- Hyponatraemia workup: serum/urine osmolality, urine sodium, assess volume status, consider ADH level if available.
- Neuro workup: urgent head MRI with contrast and focused sequences; LP with cell count, protein, glucose, bacterial culture, TB testing when indicated, and viral PCR (HSV/VZV). Send CSF for cytology if malignancy suspected.
- Infectious exclusion: empiric antiviral/antibacterial therapy while awaiting tests if clinically warranted.
- Early specialty involvement: neurology, rheumatology, infectious disease, and endocrinology if SIADH present.
- Close monitoring: vitals, neuro exam, daily Na, and avoid rapid Na shifts.

## Clinical pearls
- Neuro‑Behçet’s can present at initial diagnosis of systemic BD; do not wait years for CNS involvement. Look for genital ulcers or evolving mucocutaneous signs over days.
- A normal early MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD — repeat imaging if clinical suspicion persists or symptoms progress.
- CSF with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in a patient with BD features is supportive of acute neuro‑BD after infections and malignancy are excluded.
- SIADH can be a manifestation of CNS inflammation from neuro‑BD; persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis should prompt electrolyte review.
- Colchicine treats mucocutaneous BD and may help early, mild neuro‑inflammatory presentations, but evidence for colchicine monotherapy in neuro‑BD is limited — steroids remain the standard for parenchymal disease or severe presentations.

## Take‑home messages
- Consider neuro‑Behçet’s when a patient with oral/genital ulcers or characteristic skin lesions presents with aseptic meningitis or unexplained headache — MRI can be normal early.
- Always exclude infection and malignancy before labelling neuro‑BD; use CSF analysis and repeat imaging when necessary.
- SIADH may accompany CNS inflammation from neuro‑BD; manage with fluid restriction and careful sodium monitoring, and watch for complications (hiccups, rhabdomyolysis).
- Treatment is individualized: start corticosteroids for parenchymal/severe disease; colchicine may be reasonable for mucocutaneous disease and potentially for mild aseptic neuro‑inflammation, but multidisciplinary discussion is advised.

This case is a useful reminder: scan the skin and mucosa, interpret CSF in context, don’t be reassured by a normal early MRI, and watch the sodium.



# Hyponatraemia Pointing to the Brain: SIADH as a Signpost in Acute Neuro-Behçet’s

What makes this case sing is the chain of clues: a man in his 60s with new oral and later genital ulcers, fever and headache, marked euvolaemic hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L) with low serum osmolality, very concentrated urine and high urine sodium — meeting SIADH criteria — and CSF lymphocytic meningitis but a normal brain MRI. The simultaneous diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) is uncommon, and here SIADH appears to be the earliest—and reversible—marker of CNS inflammation. Colchicine alone (no steroids) coincided with rapid clinical and biochemical recovery, including normalization of ADH.

If you see unexplained euvolaemic hyponatraemia in a patient with mucocutaneous lesions or non‑specific CNS symptoms, think beyond the common pulmonary and drug causes — the brain (including neuroinflammation from BD) can be the culprit.

## Recognising SIADH in the ED/ward — the practical checklist
SIADH explains hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine. In this case the pattern was classic:
- Serum Na low (124 mmol/L), serum osmolality low (267 mOsm/kg)
- Urine osmolality high (900 mOsm/kg) and urine Na high (88 mmol/L)
- Clinically euvolaemic, normal renal, adrenal and thyroid function
- No diuretics

Remember the diagnostic steps you must do to label SIADH:
- Confirm hypotonic hyponatraemia (measure serum osmolality).
- Assess volume status (euvolaemia points to SIADH).
- Check urine osmolality (>100 mOsm/kg, typically >300 when ADH is inappropriately high).
- Check urine sodium (>30–40 mmol/L suggests renal salt wasting is not the cause).
- Exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency (TSH, free T4, cortisol).
- Review medications and look for pulmonary disease or ectopic ADH‑secreting tumours.

Measuring ADH is not required for diagnosis and often not available; here it was measured (22.4 pg/mL) and fell with clinical recovery — a nice confirmatory datapoint, but not essential.

## Don’t forget to correct sodium for hyperglycaemia
Hyperglycaemia causes osmotic water shift and dilutes serum sodium. Correct measured Na before interpreting hyponatraemia:

Common correction (practical):
- For every 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) glucose above ~100 mg/dL, add ≈1.6 mEq/L to the serum Na.
- In this case the patient’s glucose was ~256 mg/dL (14.2 mmol/L). Correction increases Na only a few mmol/L (still hyponatraemic), so SIADH remains the likely diagnosis.

(There are alternative correction factors proposed — 1.6 vs 2.4 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL — but the goal is to confirm whether hyperglycaemia alone explains the hyponatraemia. If corrected Na is still low, pursue SIADH workup.)

## When neuroinflammation causes SIADH — mechanism & clues
CNS inflammation can trigger ADH release. Key points:
- IL‑6 and other proinflammatory cytokines stimulate ADH secretion via hypothalamic pathways.
- Neuroinflammatory disorders (meningitis, encephalitis, neurosarcoid, neuro‑Behçet’s) have been linked to SIADH.
- BD, especially neuro‑BD, is associated with elevated IL‑6 in CSF. Although this case didn’t measure IL‑6, the marked systemic inflammation (CRP) and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis fit the pattern.
- MRI can be normal in early neuro‑BD, so normal imaging does not exclude CNS involvement — CSF testing and clinical context matter.

Clinical clues that hyponatraemia is neuro‑related include new headache, confusion, persistent hiccups, vomiting, or focal neurologic signs. This patient had headache, worsening vertigo, hiccups and CSF pleocytosis.

## Exclude the usual suspects in parallel
While thinking “brain first” in a patient with BD and hyponatraemia, still exclude common causes simultaneously:
- Drugs: SSRIs, SNRIs, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, tramadol, opioids, some chemotherapies (e.g., cyclophosphamide), and several others can cause SIADH. In BD specifically, cyclophosphamide has been implicated.
- Pulmonary disease: pneumonia, bronchiectasis, TB can trigger SIADH.
- Malignancy: small‑cell lung cancer (ectopic ADH) and other tumours.
- Endocrine: hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency (check TSH, cortisol).
- Diuretics: thiazides cause hyponatraemia but the volume/urine profile differs from classic SIADH.
Work these up in parallel because timing matters — treat severe hyponatraemia promptly while pursuing the aetiology.

## Management principles in this case and in general
Treat the hyponatraemia according to severity and chronicity, and treat the underlying cause:

- Symptomatic or severe acute hyponatraemia (seizures, obtundation, severe vomiting, respiratory arrest): hypertonic saline, with careful boluses and frequent Na checks.
- Asymptomatic or mild–moderate: fluid restriction (800–1000 mL/day or less as tolerated) is first‑line for SIADH; monitor Na closely.
- Vaptans (tolvaptan) can be used selectively but need careful supervision and are usually not first‑line in acute inflammatory SIADH.
- Avoid over‑rapid correction. Aim for ≤8 mmol/L rise in 24 hours (many recommend ≤6–8 in high‑risk patients). Chronic hyponatraemia requires even more cautious correction to prevent osmotic demyelination.
- Address and remove offending drugs; treat pulmonary infection or malignancy if present.

In this case, conservative management with fluid restriction plus treatment directed at BD (colchicine) coincided with clinical and biochemical recovery. Corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants are standard for many neuro‑BD presentations, but some patients with meningitic presentations may improve without steroids; colchicine’s anti‑inflammatory effects (inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis and cytokine release including IL‑1 and IL‑6) may have helped here. Do not take this single case to mean steroids are unnecessary for all neuro‑BD: treat based on severity and established guidance.

## Pearls from the case — what to remember at the bedside
- Hyponatraemia in a euvolaemic patient with low serum osmolality, concentrated urine and high urine Na = SIADH until proven otherwise.
- In a patient with mucocutaneous lesions or known BD, SIADH can be an early sign of neuro‑inflammation even when brain MRI is normal.
- Correct measured sodium for significant hyperglycaemia before concluding the degree of hyponatraemia.
- Look for unusual but telling features: persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be manifestations/complications of profound hyponatraemia.
- Measure TSH and cortisol to exclude endocrine mimics; review medications and chest imaging to screen for pulmonary or paraneoplastic causes.
- ADH measurement can be supportive but is not required for diagnosis and often not practical.

## Take‑home bullets
- Unexplained hypotonic hyponatraemia with euvolaemia + high urine osmolality/Na = SIADH — search for CNS inflammation as a cause, especially when mucocutaneous BD features are present.
- Correct sodium for hyperglycaemia before interpreting severity; hyperglycaemia rarely fully explains marked hyponatraemia.
- Normal brain MRI does not exclude neuro‑Behçet’s — CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and clinical context are key.
- Manage hyponatraemia based on symptoms: fluid restriction for mild/moderate SIADH; hypertonic saline for severe/symptomatic cases; avoid over‑rapid correction.
- Treat the underlying neuroinflammation — in this case colchicine coincided with recovery, but usual neuro‑BD care commonly requires corticosteroids ± other immunosuppressants.

This case is a useful reminder: when the sodium drops and the mind is involved, the brain is often the place to look.



# The Hiccup, the CK, and the Fluid: Navigating SIADH’s Hidden Complications

This case surprises in three little findings that don’t often show up together: persistent hiccups, marked hyponatraemia from SIADH, and an elevated CK consistent with rhabdomyolysis — all in a patient with newly diagnosed systemic Behçet’s disease and aseptic meningitis (acute neuro‑BD). That constellation forced a practical bedside tension: fluid restriction for SIADH versus aggressive IV fluids to protect the kidney from myoglobin injury. The good news: recognising the pattern and tailoring management avoided both neurologic catastrophe and renal failure.

## Why this matters (and why you should care)
- Hyponatraemia is common and often straightforward. But when it’s driven by CNS inflammation (neuro‑BD in this case), SIADH can present with odd clues — hiccups, refractory nausea, or subtle neurologic worsening — and can be complicated by rhabdomyolysis.
- Rhabdomyolysis from profound hyponatraemia is underappreciated. If missed, the usual treatment for rhabdo (large-volume isotonic fluids) can worsen the SIADH‑related hyponatraemia; conversely, strict fluid restriction to treat SIADH can allow CK to climb and precipitate myoglobinuric AKI.
- The case highlights the importance of early CK measurement in hyponatraemic patients with neuroinflammatory features or compatible symptoms, and of a pragmatic, monitored plan when the goals for sodium and renal protection conflict.

Brief recap of the case (stick-to-facts):
An Asian man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, persistent hiccups and mucocutaneous lesions. Labs showed serum Na 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality low (267 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L, ADH elevated — diagnostic of SIADH. CSF had aseptic meningitis; MRI was non‑diagnostic. CK was 1,349 U/L (rhabdomyolysis), renal function was preserved. Painful genital ulcer and folliculitis‑like rash led to a diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease with acute neuro‑BD. Colchicine and fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) were used; hyponatraemia and CK normalized without hypertonic saline or immunosuppression.

## Pathophysiologic snapshot (why hiccups, SIADH and rhabdo can cluster)
- CNS inflammation can trigger inappropriate ADH release. In neuroinflammatory conditions IL‑6 and other cytokines promote ADH secretion; ADH elevation leads to water retention and dilutional hyponatraemia (SIADH phenotype: low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, high urine Na, euvolemic exam).
- Severe hyponatraemia can injure skeletal muscle: cell swelling, calcium influx and protease activation may precipitate muscle necrosis and CK release. Seizures, prolonged immobility or metabolic derangements amplify that risk.
- Hiccups are a useful, often-overlooked clue. Persistent hiccups have been reported with meningitis and with significant hyponatraemia and may reflect brain‑stem or meningeal irritation — flagging potential neuro‑SIADH.

## The bedside tension: fluid restriction vs fluid resuscitation
Two incompatible goals can collide:
- SIADH → primary therapy is free water restriction (and in some cases hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms), because giving water or unmonitored isotonic fluids may worsen hyponatraemia.
- Rhabdomyolysis → mainstay is early, adequate IV isotonic fluids to maintain urine output and prevent myoglobinuria/AKI.

So how do you reconcile them? The approach is pragmatic and driven by what is most time‑sensitive: neurologic risk (severe hyponatraemia with seizures/altered mental status) and evidence of evolving renal threat from rhabdo (rapidly rising CK, dark urine, oliguria, rising creatinine).

## Practical, stepwise approach (what I’d do at the bedside)

1. Rapid assessment and categorisation
   - Neurologic status first: seizures, severe obtundation, or focal deficits → treat as severe symptomatic hyponatraemia.
   - Rhabdomyolysis risk: CK >1,000 U/L, muscle pain/weakness, dark urine, or rising creatinine demand early renal protection.

2. Immediate actions based on dominant problem
   - If severe neurologic symptoms from hyponatraemia:
     - Give boluses of hypertonic saline (e.g., 100 mL of 3% saline IV over 10 minutes, repeat as needed) to acutely raise serum Na enough to relieve life‑threatening cerebral edema.
     - Simultaneously monitor urine output, Na and CK closely. Get nephrology involved early if CK is elevated.
   - If no severe neuro features but significant rhabdomyolysis risk:
     - Start isotonic saline to protect kidneys — but do it cautiously and under strict monitoring of serum sodium (q2–4 h).
     - Aim for urine output to prevent myoglobin accumulation; consider loop diuretic if volume overload or if the urine is not clearing myoglobin despite fluids.
   - If both conditions are moderate (e.g., Na 120–130, CK 1,000–5,000, patient clinically stable):
     - Tailor a middle path: modest IV isotonic saline (to protect kidney) while intensively monitoring Na and urine osmolality, and be prepared to intervene (hypertonic saline or desmopressin) if rapid Na shifts or neurologic deterioration occur.
     - Consider using a loop diuretic with isotonic saline to increase free water excretion if the urine remains too concentrated (this can help prevent further dilutional hyponatraemia while maintaining renal perfusion).

3. Monitoring and safety nets
   - Serum sodium checks every 2–4 hours while actively correcting or giving fluids.
   - Target safe correction: generally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours (many aim for ≤6–8 mmol/L in high‑risk patients); avoid rapid overcorrection.
   - If a spontaneous water diuresis causes too-rapid rise in Na, stop interventions and give desmopressin (and D5W if needed) to relower/slow correction.
   - Check CK, creatinine, K+, phosphate and calcium frequently; monitor urine color/volume and urine myoglobin if available.
   - Early nephrology consult when CK is high, urine output falls, or if you need aggressive fluids that risk worsening hyponatraemia.

4. Therapies to consider (and pitfalls)
   - Hypertonic saline: life‑saving in severe neurologic hyponatraemia; must be used with strict monitoring to avoid osmotic demyelination.
   - Isotonic saline: mainstay of rhabdo care, but in SIADH it can be ineffective or worsen hyponatraemia if not paired with strategies to enhance free water excretion.
   - Loop diuretics: can be used to induce free water diuresis when combined with isotonic saline (helps clear urine and increase Na), but may also shift electrolytes — monitor K+ and Mg2+.
   - Vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans): effective aquaretics for chronic SIADH or persistent euvolemic hyponatraemia, but are not first‑line for acute severe hyponatraemia and can cause rapid Na rises — use only with specialist input.
   - Desmopressin: paradoxically useful to prevent or treat overly rapid correction (i.e., “clamp” the kidney when water diuresis causes too fast a Na rise).

5. Treat the underlying cause
   - In this case, colchicine for Behçet’s-related inflammation correlated with clinical and sodium recovery. For neuroinflammatory SIADH, treating the inflammation can be curative for the hyponatraemia.

## Monitoring specifics (practical order set)
- Baseline: Na, K, Cl, HCO3, creatinine, CK, phosphate, Ca, Mg, serum/urine osmolality, urine Na, urine output.
- While intervening: serum Na q2–4 h, urine output hourly, CK daily (or more often if rising), creatinine daily or more if trending up.
- Document neurologic exam and urine color hourly in unstable patients.

## Red flags that demand escalation
- Seizure or decreased consciousness → urgent hypertonic saline.
- Rising creatinine or oliguria with rising CK → escalate fluids and nephrology input.
- Rapid spontaneous rise in Na (water diuresis) → give desmopressin to slow correction.
- Na correction >8 mmol/L in 24 hours (or if approaching that threshold) → consider measures to re‑lower Na.

## Clinical pearls
- Persistent hiccups in a febrile patient with headaches should raise the index of suspicion for CNS inflammation and hyponatraemia; don’t shrug them off as benign.
- Check CK in patients with moderate–severe hyponatraemia who have neuro symptoms, muscle pain, a history of immobility, seizures, or dark urine. Rhabdomyolysis may be present even without classic pain.
- In SIADH, the urine is concentrated. Isotonic saline can be counterproductive unless you simultaneously promote free water excretion (loop diuretic) or you need to protect the kidney from myoglobin injury — choose strategy based on which organ is at immediate risk.
- When in doubt, prioritise what’s immediately life‑threatening: severe cerebral edema and seizures > preventing potential AKI. But anticipate the other problem and preemptively involve nephrology to manage fluid and electrolyte tradeoffs.
- Treating the underlying neuroinflammation may reverse SIADH — point illustrated by rapid improvement after colchicine in this Behçet’s case.

## Take‑home messages
- Persistent hiccups and hyponatraemia in a patient with meningitic features should trigger evaluation for SIADH due to CNS inflammation.
- Always check CK in hyponatraemic patients with neuro signs, myalgias, immobility or dark urine — rhabdomyolysis can coexist and change management.
- The bedside tension between fluid restriction (SIADH) and aggressive IV fluids (rhabdomyolysis) requires individualized prioritisation, frequent sodium monitoring (q2–4 h during active management), early nephrology consult, and the use of adjuncts (loop diuretics, desmopressin, or hypertonic saline) as appropriate.
- Treat the underlying inflammatory cause when possible — resolving the driver of SIADH can remove the dilemma.

# Colchicine for Acute Neuro-Behçet’s: Outlier or Option?

What makes this case interesting
A man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, mucocutaneous Behçet’s signs (oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, then a scrotal ulcer), aseptic CSF pleocytosis, MRI without parenchymal lesions, and significant SIADH-related hyponatraemia. After starting colchicine (no systemic steroids or other immunosuppressants were used), his mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia rapidly improved and he remained well on colchicine long‑term. Neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) is typically treated with high‑dose corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression; colchicine is usually reserved for mucocutaneous disease. So: is this an important exception, or a teachable outlier?

Why this matters clinically
Neuro‑BD is heterogeneous. Parenchymal disease (classically brainstem) is treated aggressively because it can leave permanent deficits. But acute neuro‑BD also can present as a self‑limited aseptic meningoencephalitis with normal MRI early on. This case highlights three practical points we should notice at the bedside:
- neuro‑BD may present concurrently with first‑time systemic BD and with only mild, non‑focal neurologic signs,
- CNS inflammation can cause SIADH (and related symptoms such as hiccups and even rhabdomyolysis from severe hyponatraemia), and
- colchicine—an inexpensive, long‑used anti‑neutrophil agent—was temporally associated with resolution of both neurological and endocrine derangements.

Is there mechanistic plausibility for colchicine working in neuro‑BD (and SIADH)?
Yes — plausibility is moderate but indirect.
- Colchicine inhibits microtubule polymerization, impairing neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation. BD pathogenesis is strongly linked to neutrophil hyperactivity and dysregulated innate immunity.
- Colchicine downregulates inflammasome activity and reduces pro‑inflammatory cytokines (IL‑1β, and indirectly IL‑6). IL‑6 is implicated in CNS inflammation and can stimulate ADH release—offering a possible explanation for improvement in SIADH when CNS inflammation is blunted.
- The above provides a biologic rationale but not proof. The report did not measure CSF or serum IL‑6 levels, and published controlled data for colchicine in neuro‑BD are lacking.

What the evidence says (briefly)
- Colchicine has proven benefit for mucocutaneous BD manifestations in randomized and observational studies, and is guideline‑supported for mucocutaneous disease.
- For parenchymal neuro‑BD, the literature supports corticosteroids ± steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, anti‑TNF agents) because of risk of progression and permanent disability. Systematic reviews conclude colchicine is ineffective for neuro‑BD when used alone; historically it has been an adjunct.
- This case is a single, well‑described instance of apparent colchicine monotherapy success in MRI‑negative, aseptic meningoencephalitis‑type neuro‑BD. Single cases can generate hypotheses but cannot change standard care.

So when, if ever, is a steroid‑sparing trial with colchicine reasonable?
Be conservative. Consider a carefully supervised colchicine trial only in highly selected situations, and always with low threshold to escalate to steroids/immunosuppression:
- Candidate clinical scenario:
  - Clear, recent‑onset mucocutaneous BD signs supporting a systemic BD diagnosis, AND
  - Neurologic presentation limited to meningoencephalitic symptoms (headache, fever, malaise, mild vertigo, hiccups) without focal deficits, seizures, decreased consciousness, visual loss, or progressive neurological decline, AND
  - CSF shows mild lymphocytic pleocytosis/protein elevation consistent with aseptic meningitis, AND
  - Brain MRI without parenchymal lesions (especially no brainstem lesions), AND
  - Patient is inpatient or has guaranteed close outpatient follow‑up for daily clinical checks and labs (including sodium), AND
  - Multidisciplinary agreement (neurology, rheumatology/infectious diseases) that risks of immediate immunosuppression outweigh benefits.
- If you try colchicine‑first:
  - Ensure rigorous monitoring: neurological exam at least daily initially, frequent serum sodium checks (especially if SIADH present), and low threshold to give high‑dose steroids if new focal signs, deterioration, or worsening MRI/CSF.
  - Document baseline and serial assessments, and involve the patient in shared decision‑making about risks.
- Avoid a colchicine‑first approach if:
  - Any focal neurological deficit, cognitive decline, seizures, decreased level of consciousness, severe hyponatraemia with neurologic symptoms, optic neuritis/uveitis, or clear parenchymal brainstem involvement — these situations warrant prompt high‑dose corticosteroids ± additional immune therapy.

Practical safety and logistics
- Colchicine adverse effects are mostly GI (diarrhoea, nausea) and dose‑related. Serious toxicity (myopathy, bone‑marrow suppression) can occur especially with renal impairment, advanced age, or interacting drugs (CYP3A4 or P‑glycoprotein inhibitors such as clarithromycin, cyclosporine, some azoles, certain statins). Adjust dose in renal or hepatic dysfunction and check for drug interactions.
- The case report did not specify the colchicine dose used. Typical BD dosing for mucocutaneous disease in practice is often in the range of ~0.5–1 mg daily (split dosing), adjusted per tolerance and renal function; confirm local dosing protocols and pharmaco‑toxicity limits.
- SIADH management in the case was conservative: fluid restriction, close sodium monitoring, avoidance of overly rapid correction. This is the appropriate initial approach for moderate SIADH without severe neurologic symptoms.

Bottom line / balanced recommendation
This single case suggests colchicine may have a role in very select, mild, MRI‑negative neuro‑BD presentations and may even help reverse SIADH driven by CNS inflammation. However, it does not overturn current practice: corticosteroids remain first‑line for most acute neuro‑BD, and immunosuppressive therapy is often required for parenchymal disease. Colchicine‑first can be considered only in a tightly controlled, closely monitored setting for patients with mild, non‑focal disease and a clear BD phenotype, and only after multidisciplinary discussion and explicit contingency plans to escalate treatment.

Clinical pearls
- Neuro‑BD can present with aseptic meningitis and a normal brain MRI early on — diagnosis is clinical and supported by CSF and mucocutaneous findings.
- SIADH may complicate CNS inflammatory diseases (including neuro‑BD) — look for hyponatraemia, concentrated urine, elevated urine sodium, euvolemia, and consider SIADH in patients with migraine‑like symptoms, persistent hiccups, or unexplained hyponatraemia.
- Colchicine has biological plausibility for modulating BD inflammation (neutrophils, IL‑1/IL‑6 pathways), but evidence for efficacy in neuro‑BD is limited; use as monotherapy in neuro‑BD should be exceptional and closely supervised.
- Always have a low threshold to start corticosteroids for progressive or focal neurological disease to reduce risk of permanent deficits.

Key take‑home messages
- Colchicine may help in highly selected, mild MRI‑negative neuro‑Behçet’s presentations, but this is not standard care.
- Rapid improvement on colchicine in a single case is hypothesis‑generating — not proof. Treat neuro‑BD aggressively when there are focal signs, MRI parenchymal lesions, or clinical deterioration.
- If attempting a steroid‑sparing strategy, ensure inpatient monitoring, daily neurologic assessments and labs (especially sodium), and clear escalation thresholds with neurology/rheumatology input.



# Don’t Let the Labs Lead You: Smarter Testing in Suspected Neuro‑Behçet’s

This case is a great teaching vignette because it shows how a patient with fever, headache and hyponatraemia can lure you into broad, low‑yield testing — ACE, sIL‑2R, broad autoantibody panels — before the clinical picture clarifies. An Asian man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, hiccups, mucocutaneous lesions and marked hyponatraemia. Early MRI was normal, CSF showed mild lymphocytic meningitis, and a few non‑specific labs (CRP high, sIL‑2R mildly up) were available. The diagnosis only became obvious when genital ulcers appeared and the patient met criteria for definite Behçet’s disease; SIADH resolved as his inflammatory disease improved on colchicine. The lesson: start with the right tests at the right time, reassess frequently, and avoid letting low pretest‑probability panels create noise.

## What made this case tricky
- Presentation was non‑specific: fever, headache and malaise are classic for infectious meningoencephalitis but also occur in many inflammatory disorders.
- MRI was unhelpful (normal) — early neuro‑Behçet’s can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions.
- Hyponatraemia with euvolemia and high urine osmolality pointed to SIADH, which is common in CNS inflammation and can produce additional clues (hiccups, rhabdomyolysis).
- Mucocutaneous findings were present, but the genital ulcer — a highly specific clue for Behçet’s — appeared later, after a broad diagnostic workup had already been started.

## Why broad “shotgun” panels can hurt more than help
- Tests like serum ACE, sIL‑2R and broad autoantibody panels are often ordered reflexively in CNS inflammation workups. Their sensitivity and specificity are limited and results can be misleading when pretest probability is low.
- Mild elevations (eg, sIL‑2R) may reflect nonspecific systemic inflammation — not lymphoma or sarcoid. Normal ACE does not exclude neurosarcoidosis.
- False reassurance from “normal” or equivocal panels, or chasing mildly abnormal nonspecific results, delays diagnosis and wastes resources.
- Every test should be ordered with a diagnostic question in mind: How will this result change management now?

## A practical, stepwise approach to CNS inflammation (use the case as a guide)

1. Immediate priorities (first hours)
   - Stabilize the patient, check airway/breathing/circulation.
   - Rapidly identify life threats: obtain electrolytes (hyponatraemia can be dangerous), glucose, bedside assessment for focal deficits/altered mental status.
   - If infection is possible, start empiric antimicrobials/antivirals after sending blood cultures and CSF (don’t delay LP unless contraindicated).

2. Focused initial diagnostic set (first 24 hours)
   - Brain imaging (CT head for safety, MRI brain as soon as feasible); remember normal MRI does not exclude early inflammatory meningitis/neuro‑Behçet’s.
   - CSF studies: cell count with differential, protein, glucose, bacterial culture, PCR for HSV/VZV, and other targeted tests if clinical suspicion.
   - Basic labs: CBC, CRP/ESR, renal and liver function, TSH, cortisol, serum osmolality, urine osmolality and sodium (to evaluate hyponatraemia/volume status).
   - If SIADH suspected: confirm hypoosmolality + inappropriately concentrated urine + euvolemia + exclusion of adrenal/thyroid causes.

3. Don’t reflex to broad autoimmune/oncologic panels unless guided
   - Ask: what diagnoses are plausible given the history, exam and early tests?
   - If sarcoidosis, lymphoma or autoimmune encephalitis is suspected clinically (eg, pulmonary findings, B symptoms, focal MRI lesions, subacute cognitive decline, seizures), then order ACE, chest imaging, sIL‑2R, specific antibody panels targeted to the suspicion.
   - If those clues are absent, defer broad testing and re‑evaluate serially. In this case, genital ulcers — a highly specific BD feature — only appeared on day 5 and obviated much of the earlier speculation.

4. Re‑calibrate diagnosis as new data emerge
   - Keep a dynamic differential. When mucocutaneous BD features appeared, the team shifted from “broad CNS inflammatory workup” to focused BD management.
   - Avoid retroactive overinterpretation of earlier nonspecific results (eg, “sIL‑2R was mildly high” — that fits inflammation, not necessarily lymphoma).

## Interpreting nonspecific markers in context
- sIL‑2R: can be elevated in lymphoma, but also in many inflammatory states. A mild rise without supportive imaging/CSF findings should not drive invasive workup.
- ACE: limited sensitivity for sarcoid, especially in isolated neurosarcoidosis. Normal ACE is not strongly reassuring.
- HLA‑B51: supportive but not diagnostic for Behçet’s; use clinical criteria to make the call.
- CRP/ESR: markers of inflammation — helpful to trend for response to therapy but nonspecific as to cause.

## Management implications illustrated by the case
- Empiric antimicrobials/antivirals were reasonable given fever and meningitic presentation; they were stopped when tests were negative.
- SIADH was managed conservatively (fluid restriction, monitoring) — no hypertonic saline or vaptans were needed. Watch for signs like persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis as markers of significant hyponatraemia.
- Colchicine for mucocutaneous BD is standard; its apparent benefit in this patient’s aseptic meningitis is interesting but not definitive — steroids/immunosuppression remain mainstays for many neuro‑Behçet’s presentations. Still, the rapid clinical response supports the importance of treating the underlying inflammatory driver rather than chasing nonspecific lab abnormalities.

## Clinical pearls
- In CNS inflammation, confirm the diagnosis stepwise: stabilize → exclude/cover infection → targeted diagnostics guided by evolving clinical clues.
- Don’t let low pretest‑probability panels (ACE, sIL‑2R, large autoantibody screens) become the diagnostic tail that wags the patient care dog. Ask: how will this test change management now?
- Reassess frequently; a new sign (eg, genital ulcer in this case) can dramatically elevate the pretest probability for a specific disease and change your workup.
- SIADH is a common companion of CNS inflammation. Check urine osmolality and urine sodium early when hyponatraemia is present, and look for atypical clues such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis.
- Normal MRI does not rule out neuro‑Behçet’s; CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and a compatible clinical syndrome may be the key.

Take‑home messages
- Focus testing on questions that will change immediate management; stabilize and treat potential infections first.
- Avoid reflexive “shotgun” ordering of ACE, sIL‑2R and broad autoantibody panels unless clinical features point toward sarcoid, lymphoma or autoimmune encephalitis.
- Re‑calibrate your differential as new, high‑yield clinical findings appear (eg, genital ulcers in Behçet’s).
- Interpret nonspecific markers (sIL‑2R, ACE) with caution — mild abnormalities more often reflect inflammation than a specific disease.
- In CNS disease with hyponatraemia, consider SIADH early and manage according to severity while treating the underlying inflammatory process.



# Empiric Now, De‑escalate Fast: Antimicrobial Strategy in Aseptic Meningitis Mimics

Why this case catches your eye: a man in his 60s arrives with fever, headache, persistent hiccups, and marked hyponatraemia. Early management followed the usual emergency playbook — cefepime was started on admission and acyclovir added empirically when CNS inflammation was suspected. CSF showed an aseptic meningitis picture (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, raised protein), brain MRI was normal, HSV/VZV testing was negative, and an alternative diagnosis — systemic Behçet’s disease with acute neuro‑Behçet’s — became evident after mucocutaneous lesions (including a scrotal ulcer) and HLA‑B51 positivity. The patient improved on colchicine alone and hyponatraemia from SIADH resolved without hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists.

This is a clean example of three things we face all the time in the ED/ward:
- Start broad when the differential includes bacterial meningitis or viral encephalitis.
- Know the limits and timing of diagnostic tests (especially viral PCR).
- Have clear, defensible off‑ramps so you can stop antimicrobials when an inflammatory, non‑infectious diagnosis is more likely.

## Empiric principles for suspected meningitis/encephalitis — fast and practical
When a patient presents with fever plus headache or altered neuro status, time matters. Empiric antimicrobials should not be delayed if bacterial meningitis is on the table.

Key pragmatic points:
- Collect blood cultures and perform lumbar puncture before antibiotics if it’s safe and will not cause dangerous delays. If LP must be delayed (increased ICP risk, need for CT), give empiric antibiotics immediately.
- Empiric regimens vary by age/risk and local epidemiology. A common adult empiric approach is: vancomycin + a third‑generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone or cefotaxime). Add ampicillin for Listeria coverage in older adults (>50–60), neonates or immunocompromised hosts.
- Add empiric acyclovir when encephalitis is a possibility (fever + focal neuro signs, seizures, behavioral change), because untreated HSV encephalitis has a high morbidity and acyclovir is relatively safe.
- Cefepime was used in this reported patient as empiric antibacterial therapy. Note: not all cephalosporins (and certainly not all empiric choices) reliably cover Listeria — tailor agents to patient age/risk factors.

Start early. Reassess early.

## Pitfalls with early viral PCRs — know the window
Viral PCR from CSF (HSV, VZV) is highly specific and sensitive in established disease, but timing matters.

What to remember:
- An early lumbar puncture (very shortly after symptom onset) can yield a false‑negative HSV PCR. If clinical suspicion for HSV encephalitis remains high despite an initial negative PCR, repeat LP (or repeat testing) at 24–72 hours is reasonable.
- A negative HSV/VZV PCR plus a non‑suspicious CSF picture (lymphocytic pleocytosis, modest protein elevation, normal glucose) and a benign clinical trajectory lowers the likelihood of HSV encephalitis.
- In the reported case acyclovir was stopped after CSF testing ruled out HSV/VZV — appropriate provided clinical suspicion had fallen and testing was reliable/timed appropriately.

Bottom line: don’t stop acyclovir too early if you still strongly suspect HSV; but do stop it promptly when testing and the clinical course point away from it.

## Safe off‑ramps — when it’s reasonable to stop antibiotics/antivirals
Stopping empiric antimicrobials is often harder than starting them — fear of missing an occult infection keeps therapy going. Use an evidence‑based checklist to de‑escalate safely.

Consider stopping antimicrobials when all of the following apply:
- The patient is clinically improving or stable without signs of systemic sepsis.
- CSF Gram stain is negative and cell counts/chemistry are inconsistent with bacterial meningitis (for example: lymphocytic predominance, modest protein rise, normal glucose).
- CSF bacterial cultures are negative at the appropriate interval (usually 48 hours for many pathogens).
- Viral PCRs (HSV/VZV) are negative and either timed appropriately or repeated if early LP suggested false negativity, and there is no evolving focal encephalitic picture.
- An alternative, plausible non‑infectious diagnosis is established that explains the clinical and laboratory findings (here: systemic Behçet’s disease with aseptic meningitis and SIADH).
- There is clear documentation and communication (with neurology/ID as needed) and a monitoring plan for clinical deterioration after stopping therapy.

In short: ensure microbiology, CSF picture and the clinical trajectory line up before stopping. When in doubt, discuss with Infectious Disease or Neurology.

## Applying this to the Behçet’s case — why de‑escalation was defensible
This patient had:
- CSF consistent with aseptic meningitis (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, raised protein), no pathogens identified, and negative targeted viral testing.
- A normal brain MRI (early neuro‑Behçet’s can be MRI‑normal).
- Emerging mucocutaneous findings diagnostic for Behçet’s (oral and later scrotal ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash), which provided a unifying non‑infectious explanation for the CNS syndrome.
- Clinical improvement after initiation of colchicine and conservative SIADH management, with normalization of sodium and resolution of symptoms.

Given the above, discontinuing acyclovir after negative HSV/VZV CSF tests and de‑escalating antibiotics once cultures remained negative and the alternative diagnosis gained support was appropriate and aligned with a risk‑balanced approach.

## Clinical pearls from the case
- Hyponatraemia (SIADH) can be a clue to CNS inflammation, not just pulmonary disease or drugs — persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis in this patient were linked to hyponatraemia and helped complete the clinical picture.
- Normal neuroimaging does not exclude neuro‑Behçet’s — acute neuro‑BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions.
- Colchicine, while a mainstay for mucocutaneous Behçet’s, is not usually considered adequate monotherapy for neuro‑BD in textbooks — this patient improved without corticosteroids or other immunosuppression. That said, spontaneous resolution can occur and management should be individualized.
- Always document the rationale for stopping antimicrobials and set a safety plan (what to watch for, who to call, when to reassess).

## Take‑home messages
- In suspected meningitis/encephalitis: collect blood cultures and LP if safe, start empiric antimicrobials promptly when bacterial disease is possible, and add acyclovir when encephalitis is a concern.
- Know the limitations of early viral PCRs — consider repeat testing if early LP is negative but clinical suspicion for HSV remains high.
- De‑escalate aggressively but safely: negative CSF testing, a compatible CSF profile, clinical improvement and an alternative diagnosis allow stopping antimicrobials — but do it with clear documentation and follow‑up.
- Non‑infectious neuroinflammatory diseases (eg, neuro‑Behçet’s) can mimic infectious meningitis and may present with SIADH; reassess the differential as new diagnostic clues (eg, mucocutaneous lesions) appear.

This case is a tidy lesson: start broad, pursue a focused diagnostic workup, and — when the data and clinical evolution point away from infection — de‑escalate without hesitation.
