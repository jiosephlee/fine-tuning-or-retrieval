# Hyponatremia, Headache, and Hiccups: Spotting SIADH from Neuroinflammation

What makes this case interesting? A man in his 60s presents with fever, headache, persistent hiccups and new mucocutaneous lesions. Labs show moderate hypotonic hyponatremia (Na 124 mmol/L), very concentrated urine (Uosm 900 mOsm/kg), urine Na 88 mmol/L, and an inappropriately high ADH level. CSF shows aseptic meningitis and—after the appearance of genital ulcers and a positive HLA-B51—Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s is diagnosed. The hyponatraemia resolves with conservative SIADH management and colchicine that treated the underlying inflammatory disease, without steroids or hypertonic therapy.

This is a neat clinical vignette because it reminds us that CNS inflammation can drive SIADH, that non‑specific symptoms like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis may be clues to hyponatraemia, and that the initial approach to hypotonic hyponatraemia must be methodical: confirm hypotonic hyponatraemia, demonstrate impaired water excretion, exclude endocrine causes, and decide urgently whether the patient needs rapid correction.

## Quick diagnostic approach when CNS symptoms + hyponatraemia show up

1. Confirm it’s true hyponatraemia and hypotonic:
   - Serum Na low
   - Serum osmolality low (or calculated hypotonic hyponatraemia)

2. Is the kidney appropriately conserving water?
   - Urine osmolality >100–300 mOsm/kg with hypotonic serum suggests impaired water excretion. In SIADH it’s typically high (>300–900), like 900 in this case.

3. Check urine sodium:
   - Urine Na >30 mmol/L in euvolemic hyponatraemia supports SIADH (renal salt handling intact).

4. Exclude thyroid and adrenal insufficiency:
   - TSH and free T4 normal in the case.
   - Cortisol should be adequate (random cortisol useful; consider ACTH stimulation when in doubt). The case had normal cortisol.

5. Assess volume status — but be cautious:
   - SIADH = clinically euvolemic. However, volume exam is notoriously unreliable. Use the labs above (Uosm, UNa) and the clinical context (no oedema, normal orthostatics) rather than bedside assessment alone.

6. Look for triggers:
   - CNS processes (aseptic meningitis, encephalitis, stroke), pulmonary disease, malignancy, and drugs.
   - In this case the aseptic meningitis from neuro-Behçet’s is the likely driver.

## Why hiccups and rhabdomyolysis matter

- Hiccups can be an unusual but important clue to irritation of the central pathways (brainstem/hypothalamus) or to metabolic disturbances. In severe hyponatraemia hiccups are reported and may precede obvious neurological decline.
- Rhabdomyolysis may complicate severe hyponatraemia and can be overlooked. Elevated CK in the setting of hyponatraemia should raise suspicion for muscle injury from osmotic shifts or associated seizures/immobility. This case had high CK that normalised as sodium corrected.

Takeaway: don’t dismiss hiccups or unexplained CK elevations—check electrolytes.

## When is SIADH the working diagnosis?
Put it together:
- Hypotonic hyponatraemia
- Inappropriately concentrated urine (Uosm >100–300; often >300)
- Elevated urine sodium (>30 under normal dietary intake)
- Clinically euvolemic
- Normal thyroid and adrenal function
- No diuretics or other obvious pharmacologic cause

If all the above are met, SIADH is the most likely explanation. In the Behçet’s case ADH was measurably elevated, CSF showed aseptic meningitis, and other causes were excluded—consistent with SIADH driven by neuroinflammation.

## Treatment principles — who needs immediate active correction?

Decide on severity and acuity:
- Severe symptoms (seizure, coma, severe obtundation, respiratory arrest): treat immediately with boluses of 3% hypertonic saline.
  - A common initial bolus: 100 mL 3% saline IV over 10 minutes, repeat up to 2 times until acute symptoms improve (local protocols vary).
- Moderate symptoms (confusion, severe vomiting, significant gait disturbance): consider hypertonic saline and close monitoring.
- Mild or asymptomatic hyponatraemia: start conservative measures.

Goals for correction:
- Acute hyponatraemia (<48 h): more aggressive correction tolerated, but still avoid overcorrection.
- Chronic hyponatraemia (>48 h or unknown): correct slowly. Avoid >8–10 mmol/L rise in plasma Na in any 24-hour period (some guidelines recommend ≤8 mmol/L/24h; some use 10 mmol/L), and ≤18 mmol/L/48h. In high-risk patients (alcoholism, malnutrition, hypokalemia, liver disease), even slower correction is safer.

Monitoring:
- Check serum Na every 2–4 hours during active correction; every 6–12 hours once stable.
- Watch for osmotic demyelination risk when correcting chronic hyponatraemia too quickly.
- If overcorrection occurs, re-lower Na with hypotonic fluids or desmopressin and free water.

Fluid restriction and when it’s appropriate:
- First-line for mild-moderate SIADH: fluid restriction (typical goal <800–1000 mL/day, though <800 mL/day was used in this case).
- Works best when urine osmolality is not extremely high. If Uosm is very high (>500–600) and sodium very low, restriction alone may be ineffective or slow.

Other options if fluid restriction fails:
- Salt tablets + loop diuretics (to promote free water excretion) in some patients.
- Demeclocycline (slow onset, nephrotoxic risk—avoid in renal insufficiency).
- Vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans):
  - Tolvaptan can raise Na effectively but carries hepatotoxicity concerns, is costly, and risks rapid overcorrection; use with caution and in appropriate settings.
  - Generally reserved for euvolemic or hypervolemic hyponatraemia when other measures fail and close monitoring is available.
- Treat the underlying cause: in this case colchicine to control neuroinflammation correlated with resolution of SIADH.

## Putting it into practice — a quick roadmap

1. Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia.
2. Urgent assessment for severe symptoms (seizures, coma). If present → give hypertonic saline immediately.
3. Measure urine osmolality and urine sodium; check TSH and cortisol; review meds.
4. If SIADH likely and patient stable → start fluid restriction, monitor Na frequently.
5. If fluid restriction fails or patient has moderate-severe symptoms → consider hypertonic saline (for symptoms) or pharmacologic options (vaptan) with careful monitoring.
6. Search for and treat underlying cause. In CNS inflammation, treating the inflammation often corrects the SIADH.

## Why this case teaches us something practical
- CNS inflammation (here neuro-Behçet’s presenting concurrently with systemic BD) can cause SIADH even when brain MRI is normal.
- Clinical volume assessment is unreliable—rely on urine studies and endocrine exclusion.
- Watch for non‑specific clues (hiccups, rhabdomyolysis) that point to significant hyponatraemia.
- Conservative management (fluid restriction + treating the underlying neuroinflammation) can be effective; not every SIADH needs hypertonic saline or vaptans.
- When in doubt, treat severe symptoms emergently; when stable, correct carefully to avoid osmotic demyelination.

Key take-home messages:
- Confirm hypotonic hyponatraemia, then use urine osmolality and urine sodium to diagnose SIADH; exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency.
- Volume status exam is unreliable—interpret in the context of labs.
- Hiccups and elevated CK can be clues to significant hyponatraemia from CNS processes.
- Treat life‑threatening symptoms with hypertonic saline; otherwise start fluid restriction and treat the underlying cause. Avoid overcorrection (generally ≤8–10 mmol/L/24 h).
- In SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, controlling inflammation (as in this Behçet’s case) can resolve the electrolyte disorder.

If you see a patient with headache, hyponatraemia and odd symptoms like persistent hiccups—think CNS inflammation → check urine osmolality and sodium, exclude endocrine causes, and tailor therapy to the severity and tempo of the illness.



# Neuro‑Behçet’s with a Normal MRI: Don’t Be Reassured by Clean Imaging

What makes this case interesting: an older man presents with fever, headache, hiccups and hyponatraemia. He has oral aphthae and, later, genital ulcers and folliculitis‑like skin lesions. Brain MRI is unremarkable, but lumbar puncture shows aseptic meningitis, and he fulfills criteria for definite Behçet’s disease. He also develops SIADH that resolves as the inflammatory picture and mucocutaneous lesions improve on colchicine alone. The lesson? A normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) — and mucocutaneous clues plus CSF matter.

Why this should matter to you
- Neuro‑BD can present early in the disease course and sometimes as isolated meningitic/meningoencephalitic syndrome without classic parenchymal lesions. MRI — especially early — can be normal.
- Missing neuro‑BD leads to delays in appropriate immunomodulatory therapy and missed recognition of complications (eg, SIADH) that can be life‑threatening if unrecognized.
- Clinical context — oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions, fever and unexplained CNS symptoms — should override false reassurance from a clean scan.

How this patient taught us to think

1) Don’t over‑rely on MRI timing
- Early neuro‑BD may lack the classic brainstem or basal ganglia lesions. MRI sensitivity is lower in the first days of CNS inflammation.
- If suspicion is high and MRI is normal, that is not the end of the workup. Consider repeat MRI if symptoms evolve or fail to improve (commonly 1–2 weeks, or sooner if focal signs develop).
- Use MRI to look for alternative causes (abscess, stroke, mass) but don’t let a normal scan exclude inflammatory CNS disease.

2) CSF is often the clincher in aseptic presentations
- In this case CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein with mildly raised opening pressure — a pattern compatible with acute neuro‑BD.
- Typical CSF profile in neuro‑BD: low‑to‑moderate lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, no pathogens on culture/PCR and no malignant cells.
- Remember: CSF studies also let you exclude treatable infections (HSV, VZV, TB, cryptococcus) and guide safe de‑escalation of empiric antimicrobials.

3) Balance empiric antimicrobial/antiviral therapy with timely autoimmune evaluation
- Initial empiric coverage for bacterial meningitis/encephalitis and acyclovir are appropriate while awaiting CSF PCR/culture when presentation is febrile and there are neurologic symptoms.
- But rapid negative CSF PCRs and cultures, combined with mucocutaneous BD features, should shift you toward inflammatory causes and away from prolonged antimicrobials.
- Don’t withhold immunomodulatory therapy out of fear of missing infection — rather, use the diagnostic window from CSF and microbiology to make a reasoned decision.

4) Look for mucocutaneous clues — they are often the decisive piece
- Oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rashes: in this case the genital ulcer appeared after admission but clinched the diagnosis.
- Behçet’s is a clinical diagnosis. When mucocutaneous findings are present, they should raise the pretest probability of neuro‑BD even if imaging is normal.
- HLA‑B51 may support the diagnosis but is neither necessary nor sufficient.

5) SIADH is an important and under‑recognized complication
- CNS inflammation can drive inappropriate ADH release; IL‑6 is a plausible mediator.
- In this report hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L), low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, high urine sodium and elevated ADH were consistent with SIADH.
- Clinical clues: persistent hiccups, malaise, and even rhabdomyolysis can be downstream consequences of severe hyponatraemia.
- Manage SIADH with fluid restriction and careful sodium monitoring; hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists are reserved for severe or symptomatic cases. Avoid too‑rapid correction.

6) Therapeutic approach — corticosteroids are typical, but context matters
- Acute neuro‑BD is often treated with corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression. However, some meningoencephalitic forms may improve without systemic steroids.
- In this case colchicine monotherapy (used for mucocutaneous BD) coincided with rapid clinical improvement and correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine has anti‑inflammatory effects (neutrophil inhibition, cytokine suppression), though systematic data do not support it as standard monotherapy for neuro‑BD.
- Use clinical judgement: if symptoms are severe, progressive, or there are focal deficits/parenchymal lesions — treat promptly with steroids and consult rheumatology/neurology. If the picture is mild, CSF favors aseptic inflammation, and mucocutaneous disease is present, a trial of less aggressive therapy may be reasonable with close monitoring.

Practical workflow from this case (how I’d manage a similar patient)
- Rapid clinical assessment: volume status, neurologic exam, look for mucocutaneous lesions.
- Baseline labs: electrolytes (spot for hyponatraemia), serum osmolality, urine osmolality and sodium.
- Urgent CT head if concern for mass/bleed before LP; otherwise LP with CSF cell count, protein, glucose, Gram stain, bacterial culture, HSV/VZV PCR, TB studies as indicated and cytology if cancer suspected.
- Start empiric antibiotics ± acyclovir while tests pending if febrile/CNS signs.
- If CSF shows aseptic meningitis and mucocutaneous features are present: involve rheumatology/neurology early; consider Behçet’s criteria.
- Manage SIADH conservatively (fluid restriction, monitor Na daily). Treat severe or symptomatic hyponatraemia per standard protocols.
- Repeat MRI if clinical course worsens or fails to improve; timing individualized (commonly within 1–2 weeks).
- Escalate to steroids/immunosuppression for worsening neurologic function or parenchymal disease.

Key take‑home messages
- A normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑Behçet’s — CSF and clinical context are critical.
- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein in CSF in the setting of mucocutaneous BD features should raise strong suspicion for neuro‑BD.
- Start empiric antimicrobials/antivirals when appropriate, but re‑focus management rapidly when infectious testing is negative.
- Watch for SIADH in neuroinflammatory disease; hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be clues to severe hyponatraemia.
- Consult rheumatology/neurology early; consider steroids for severe disease, but recognize that some cases may improve with colchicine and supportive care — close monitoring is essential.

Bottom line: If the clinical picture points to neuro‑Behçet’s, don’t be falsely reassured by a clean MRI. Use CSF, mucocutaneous signs and thoughtful serial assessment to guide diagnosis and management.



# When Behçet’s and Neuro‑Behçet’s Present Together: Building the Diagnosis at the Bedside

What makes this case jump off the page? A man in his 60s with fever, headache and mucocutaneous lesions was diagnosed simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) — a rare presentation — and developed SIADH with hyponatraemia. Brain MRI was normal, CSF showed aseptic meningitis, genital ulcers appeared during the admission, HLA‑B51 was positive, and colchicine alone led to clinical and biochemical resolution. This is a great reminder that bedside diagnosis, serial exams and focused thinking beat shotgun testing every time.

Why this matters
- Neuro‑BD usually shows up years after mucocutaneous disease; seeing systemic BD and acute neuro‑BD at the same time is uncommon and easy to miss.
- Neuro‑BD can present as aseptic meningitis with normal neuroimaging early on — don’t over‑rely on MRI to exclude it.
- Hyponatraemia in CNS inflammation may be SIADH; in neuro‑BD this can resolve with control of inflammation.
- The diagnostic pivot in this case was the emergence of a genital ulcer — a high‑weight finding in the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD).

A practical bedside framework for suspected BD with CNS symptoms
1. Start with history and rapid mucocutaneous exam. In the ICBD, oral aphthae, genital aphthosis and ocular lesions are the big tickets. Genital ulcers carry particularly strong diagnostic weight; if present, they push you towards a BD diagnosis even when other data are equivocal.
2. Look for evolution. BD lesions can appear sequentially. If the diagnosis is uncertain on arrival, re‑examine daily — new oral or genital ulcers, pustular/folliculitis‑like skin lesions, or ocular signs can change your diagnosis rapidly.
3. Evaluate CNS symptoms pragmatically. Headache, meningitic symptoms, hiccups, behavioural changes and focal deficits may reflect neuro‑BD. If MRI is normal but CSF shows aseptic lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, neuro‑BD remains on the table.
4. Interpret HLA‑B51 in context. A positive HLA‑B51 supports the diagnosis but is neither necessary nor sufficient; ICBD is clinical. Don’t let a positive or negative HLA test dictate care.
5. Reserve broad autoimmune/oncologic screens for when clinical features point that way. Tests like serum ACE or sIL‑2R can be misleading (non‑specific elevations with inflammation). Use them when you have a targeted suspicion (e.g., sarcoid phenotype, persistent MRI abnormalities, systemic B symptoms, lymphadenopathy).
6. Manage the endocrine complication early. Hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality and inappropriately concentrated urine = SIADH until proven otherwise. In the setting of CNS inflammation, conservative management (fluid restriction, close monitoring, avoid rapid correction) is often effective. Consider inflammatory control as part of treatment for SIADH related to neuroinflammation.
7. Treatment needs to be individualized. While high‑dose corticosteroids and immunosuppression are standard for many neuro‑BD cases, some presentations—particularly aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions—may improve with less aggressive therapy (colchicine was effective in this report). Still, be ready to escalate for severe or progressive disease.

Why avoid shotgun testing
- Non‑specific markers (ACE, sIL‑2R, many autoantibodies) can generate false leads that delay the diagnosis of BD and the focused management of neuroinflammation.
- In this case, those tests added little; the diagnosis became clear once genital aphthosis appeared and CSF showed aseptic meningitis. Serial clinical assessment was decisive.
- Target tests to answer a specific clinical question. If you suspect neurosarcoidosis because of imaging or systemic features, then ACE and chest imaging are useful. If not, they are low‑yield.

A few pathophysiologic notes to keep in mind
- CNS inflammation can drive SIADH via cytokine‑mediated ADH release; IL‑6 is a plausible mediator in neuroinflammatory states and is elevated in neuro‑BD in other reports. Treat the inflammation and SIADH may correct.
- Normal early MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD. Parenchymal lesions, especially brainstem, may be delayed or absent with meningoencephalitic presentations.
- Rhabdomyolysis and persistent hiccups can be clues to severe hyponatraemia — look beyond the obvious.

Clinical pearls
- Genital ulcers are high‑yield: their appearance should immediately raise your pretest probability for BD and push you to integrate mucocutaneous findings with CNS data.
- Don’t use HLA‑B51 as a diagnostic shortcut — it’s supportive but not decisive.
- Serial physical exams matter. Re‑examine the skin and mucosa daily in undifferentiated febrile CNS syndromes.
- If CSF shows aseptic meningitis and MRI is normal, think neuro‑BD when mucocutaneous features are present; early neuroimaging negativity does not rule it out.
- Avoid broad, non‑targeted autoimmune/oncologic screening unless there’s a clinical signal — these tests can distract and are often non‑specific.
- Treat SIADH conservatively first in euvolemic, stable patients and focus on controlling CNS inflammation; ADH‑mediated hyponatraemia can resolve as inflammation subsides.

Take‑home points
- New genital ulcers in a patient with oral ulcers and aseptic meningitis should make you think Behçet’s — and change your working diagnosis quickly.
- Normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑Behçet’s; CSF and serial clinical exams are crucial.
- HLA‑B51 is supportive but not diagnostic; the ICBD is a clinical tool — use it at the bedside.
- Avoid indiscriminate ACE/sIL‑2R/autoimmune screening without clinical indications.
- Consider SIADH in neuroinflammatory presentations; manage hyponatraemia carefully and treat the underlying inflammation.

Bottom line: When faced with fever, headache and mucocutaneous lesions, do a focused clinical exam, re‑examine often, and let evolving bedside findings guide testing and treatment. Behçet’s and neuro‑Behçet’s can present together — and the diagnosis is often made at the bedside, not the lab.



# Colchicine Alone for Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s? A Steroid‑Sparing Curveball

What makes this case interesting: a man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, mucocutaneous Behçet’s features and aseptic meningitis consistent with acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD). He also had SIADH with hyponatraemia. Instead of the usual high‑dose corticosteroids (± additional immunosuppression) he was started on colchicine alone and improved quickly — mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms and the hyponatraemia resolved without steroids. Colchicine monotherapy is not the standard teaching for neuro‑BD, so this case forces us to ask: when — if ever — is steroid avoidance reasonable, and how should we pick patients?

Why this matters
- Neuro‑BD is traditionally treated aggressively with corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppression because parenchymal CNS disease can cause lasting deficits. Guidelines and case series generally favour steroids for acute neuroinflammation.
- At the same time, real patients sometimes have reasons to avoid steroids (poor diabetes control, active infection risk, severe steroid side‑effect profile). Knowing the limits of a less aggressive approach — and how to monitor it — is clinically useful.
- This case also highlights that CNS inflammation from neuro‑BD can produce SIADH and notable electrolyte and systemic complications that need independent management.

Short summary of the case (grounded facts)
- New diagnosis of definite systemic Behçet’s (oral + genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash). Acute CNS symptoms: fever, headache, vertigo, hiccups.
- MRI normal; CSF: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein (aseptic meningitis pattern).
- Euvolemic hypoosmolar hyponatraemia, high urine osmolality and urine sodium, elevated ADH → SIADH. No infectious agent identified.
- Colchicine started (patient refused or clinicians chose steroid avoidance owing to diabetes and clinical picture). Rapid clinical improvement within 24–48 hours; sodium normalized over two weeks. No steroids or other immunosuppressants used; no relapse over follow‑up on colchicine.

What the literature and common teaching say (brief, pragmatic)
- Most reviews and series treat acute neuro‑BD with high‑dose corticosteroids; parenchymal disease commonly requires steroids plus steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, TNF inhibitors) when severe or recurrent.
- Colchicine is well established for mucocutaneous and arthritic Behçet’s manifestations, but evidence supporting colchicine as sole therapy for acute neuro‑BD is limited/absent. Systematic reviews generally find colchicine insufficient for major organ (CNS) disease.
- Spontaneous improvement of aseptic meningitis‑type presentations has been reported; some neuro‑BD presentations are self‑limited. Distinguishing a mild, self‑limited episode from one that will progress is the clinical challenge.

When colchicine monotherapy might be considered (clinical caveats)
Colchicine alone is not the default. Consider it only in a carefully selected, closely monitored patient when:
- Presentation suggests mild, non‑parenchymal neuro‑BD (aseptic meningitis picture) rather than parenchymal brainstem/hemispheric involvement.
- Neurological exam is nonfocal: no motor weakness, cranial nerve palsy, altered consciousness, seizures or progressive cognitive decline.
- Brain MRI is normal or shows no definitive parenchymal lesion in typical BD locations (brainstem, basal ganglia, etc).
- CSF shows only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and modest protein elevation (not massive pleocytosis or neutrophilic predominance).
- There are strong reasons to avoid steroids (uncontrolled diabetes, severe steroid contraindication, patient preference) and the treating team is prepared to watch closely and escalate if needed.
- The treating team can perform frequent neurologic checks, daily sodium and basic labs, and repeat imaging/CSF if deterioration occurs.

When to escalate immediately to steroids or further immunosuppression
Escalate without delay when any of the following are present:
- Focal neurological deficits (new weakness, CN palsies), rapidly progressive symptoms, impaired consciousness or seizures.
- Radiological evidence of parenchymal lesions, especially in brainstem or deep structures.
- Marked CSF pleocytosis, high opening pressure, or features suggesting bacterial/viral encephalitis not yet excluded.
- Worsening hyponatraemia causing significant symptoms despite conservative measures, or other systemic deterioration.
- Clinical deterioration after a trial period (see monitoring below).

Colchicine dosing, safety and monitoring — practical pointers
- Typical adult colchicine dosing for mucocutaneous Behçet’s is in the range of 0.5 mg twice daily (some regimens 0.5–1.0 mg/day depending on tolerance and local practice). Dosing for neuro‑BD is not standardized because it’s not a recommended monotherapy — use established mucocutaneous doses if choosing this route.
- Renal and hepatic impairment require dose adjustment. Elderly patients tolerate lower doses less well.
- Watch for GI side effects (nausea, diarrhea, vomiting) — these are the most common dose‑limiting issues.
- Major safety interactions: strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (macrolides like clarithromycin, some azoles) and P‑glycoprotein inhibitors increase colchicine toxicity risk. Avoid co‑administration or reduce dose and monitor closely.
- Rare but serious: myopathy and rhabdomyolysis (especially with statins, renal impairment), bone marrow suppression with chronic high exposure — monitor CK, CBC if clinical suspicion or chronic use.
- In this case, CK was elevated on admission (rhabdomyolysis possibly related to severe hyponatraemia) and normalized as sodium corrected — monitor CK when indicated.

Monitoring plan if attempting colchicine first‑line in a selected patient
- Neurological checks at least daily initially (more frequently if any change).
- Daily serum sodium and basic metabolic panel until stable; more frequent if hyponatraemia is severe.
- Repeat MRI if new or progressive focal signs, or if no improvement within 48–72 hours.
- Consider repeat CSF if clinical course worsens or diagnosis remains uncertain.
- Low threshold to start high‑dose corticosteroids if any sign of deterioration.

Clinical pearls
- MRI can be normal early in neuro‑Behçet’s, and neuro‑BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions — diagnosis is clinical and requires a high index of suspicion when mucocutaneous features coexist.
- Neuroinflammation can trigger SIADH; treat the hyponatraemia independently (fluid restriction, careful Na monitoring) while addressing the underlying cause.
- Colchicine has anti‑inflammatory effects relevant to BD (neutrophil inhibition, cytokine modulation), but evidence does not support routine colchicine monotherapy for major organ involvement — this case is an exception, not a new standard.
- When steroid avoidance is necessary, a tightly supervised trial of colchicine may be reasonable in a very selected patient with mild, non‑parenchymal neuro‑BD — but have a clear escalation plan.

Take‑home messages
- Colchicine monotherapy for acute neuro‑Behçet’s is an intriguing option only in highly selected, mild aseptic meningitis‑type presentations with no focal deficits and normal imaging, particularly when steroids are undesirable.
- Most cases of neuro‑BD still warrant prompt corticosteroids (± steroid‑sparing agents) — do not withhold standard therapy from patients with parenchymal disease, focal deficits, seizures or deterioration.
- If attempting colchicine first, monitor closely (neurological exam, sodium, CK, repeat imaging) and be ready to escalate immediately.
- Manage SIADH and other metabolic complications aggressively and independently of immunomodulatory choices.
- This case reminds us there’s nuance in treating neuroinflammation: individual patient factors matter, but exceptions don’t replace established evidence‑based practice.

If you’re considering colchicine as part of a neuro‑BD plan for a patient: discuss the risks and benefits with the patient, document the rationale and monitoring/escape strategy, and liaise with neurology/rheumatology early.



# IL‑6, ADH, and the Inflamed Brain: Why SIADH Happens in Neuro‑BD

What makes this case interesting: a man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, mucocutaneous Behçet’s disease (BD) findings and hyponatraemia. He had aseptic meningitis on CSF, no MRI parenchymal lesions, and biochemical SIADH (low serum osmolality, concentrated urine, inappropriately high ADH). Everything — the ulcers, the neurologic symptoms and the hyponatraemia — settled after colchicine alone. This is a tidy clinical vignette linking neuroinflammation to non‑osmotic ADH release, and it’s a useful springboard to discuss mechanisms you can act on at the bedside.

Why inflammation begets hyponatraemia: the short version
- Central nervous system inflammation can trigger non‑osmotic ADH (vasopressin) release. The usual suspects for SIADH (CNS infection, malignancy, drugs) overlap with inflammatory disorders.
- Proinflammatory cytokines — particularly interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) — are implicated in driving ADH secretion from the hypothalamus/posterior pituitary and in sensitising osmoreceptors.
- Neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) is a prototypical CNS inflammatory disorder where CSF IL‑6 is often elevated. So when neuro‑BD presents acutely (meningoencephalitis/aseptic meningitis), SIADH is physiologically plausible.

Pathophysiology in a bit more detail (clinical focus)
- Cytokine‑mediated ADH release: IL‑6 can act centrally to increase ADH secretion independent of plasma osmolality. Experimental and clinical work across different CNS inflammatory conditions shows IL‑6 rises in CSF and accompanies inappropriate vasopressin release.
- Hypothalamic involvement not required: You don’t need a focal hypothalamic lesion on MRI to get SIADH. Diffuse meningeal inflammation or parenchymal cytokine spillover can be sufficient to provoke non‑osmotic ADH release.
- Systemic markers as practical proxies: Direct CSF IL‑6 measurement is not routinely available. Serum CRP (and clinical signs of systemic inflammation) may serve as a pragmatic surrogate flag for an active inflammatory milieu that could include elevated central IL‑6.
- Reverse causal thinking: Treating the inflammation — not just restricting water or antagonising ADH — often leads to sustained correction of sodium because the stimulus for inappropriate ADH disappears.

Putting it into practice: how this helps you manage a patient
- Think SIADH in any patient with fever/headache and hyponatraemia plus clinical or laboratory evidence of CNS inflammation. Don’t assume dilutional hyponatraemia from another source without considering CNS causes.
- Initial workup still follows standard hyponatraemia steps: confirm hypoosmolar hyponatraemia, assess volume status (many neuro‑inflammatory SIADH patients are clinically euvolemic), measure urine osmolality and urine sodium, exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency.
- Use inflammatory clues. High CRP, systemic mucocutaneous BD stigmata, headache/meningitic symptoms and CSF lymphocytosis/protein should raise your pre‑test probability that SIADH is inflammation‑driven.
- Treat the underlying inflammation promptly. In many CNS inflammatory states, definitive treatment (antibiotics/antivirals when indicated, steroids or disease‑specific immunotherapy where appropriate) removes the ADH stimulus and corrects sodium. This Behçet’s case is notable because colchicine (anti‑neutrophil and anti‑cytokine effects) alone coincided with recovery — suggesting that targeted anti‑inflammatory therapy can be enough.
- Manage sodium safely while you treat the inflammation. Fluid restriction is often firstline for mild–moderate SIADH. Avoid overly rapid correction; reserve hypertonic saline or vaptans for severe symptoms or refractory cases, and be cautious in inflammatory CNS disease where osmotic shifts can be risky.

Clinical pearls
- Neuro‑BD can present simultaneously with systemic BD and may lack MRI lesions early on — aseptic meningitis with CSF lymphocytosis is enough to suspect neuro involvement.
- SIADH does not require visible hypothalamic disease on imaging. Consider it when low serum osmolality coexists with high urine osmolality and urine sodium despite euvolemia.
- IL‑6 is a plausible mediator linking CNS inflammation to ADH secretion. You won’t usually measure CSF IL‑6 in routine care, but elevated CRP and CSF lymphocytosis increase the likelihood that inflammation is driving ADH.
- Treating the inflammation often treats the hyponatraemia. While fluid restriction buys time, addressing the inflammatory driver (e.g., corticosteroids or disease‑directed therapy) is key for durable correction.
- Colchicine has anti‑cytokine effects (including IL‑6 suppression) and is standard for mucocutaneous BD; while not generally recommended for neuro‑BD monotherapy, this case shows it may be effective in selected, milder inflammatory presentations. Interpret with caution — most neuro‑BD patients still require corticosteroids or stronger immunosuppression.

Take‑home messages
- Consider SIADH when hyponatraemia coexists with CNS inflammatory symptoms; MRI can be normal in early neuroinflammatory disease.
- IL‑6 is a plausible biological link between neuroinflammation (including neuro‑BD) and non‑osmotic ADH release; CRP and CSF findings can act as practical proxies.
- Manage the sodium safely (fluid restriction, avoid rapid correction) but prioritise treating the underlying inflammation — resolving the cytokine driver often normalises ADH and sodium.
- Use disease‑specific therapies based on severity; rare cases may respond to less aggressive anti‑inflammatory therapy (e.g., colchicine), but most neuro‑BD requires steroids/immunosuppression.

Bottom line: when the brain is inflamed, it can tell the pituitary to hoard water. Recognise the pattern, treat the inflammation, and correct the sodium carefully.



# CK Through the Roof: Rhabdomyolysis as a Consequence of Severe Hyponatremia

What’s surprising about the case that kicked this post off? A man in his 60s with newly diagnosed Behçet’s disease developed SIADH and profound hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L) and—unexpectedly—markedly elevated CK consistent with rhabdomyolysis. His rhabdo resolved as sodium normalized with conservative SIADH care and colchicine for the underlying inflammation. The take-home: severe hypotonic hyponatraemia can injure skeletal muscle, and that injury can create a clinical tug-of-war between treating SIADH (fluid restriction) and treating rhabdomyolysis (aggressive IV fluids). You need to recognise the complication early and manage both safely.

Why hyponatraemia causes rhabdomyolysis (brief physiology)
- Acute hypotonicity causes water influx into cells. Muscle cells swell, intracellular calcium handling is disrupted, and cell membranes become more fragile—this can trigger muscle necrosis.
- Severe and/or rapid-onset hyponatraemia is most commonly implicated.
- CNS inflammation (meningitis, encephalitis, neuro-Behçet) → SIADH → sustained hypotonicity increases the risk.
- Cytokine-mediated muscle injury may contribute in inflammatory states.

When to check CK in hyponatraemic patients
Think CK in any patient with:
- Profound hyponatraemia (Na <125 mmol/L), especially if acute or symptomatic.
- Myalgias, muscle tenderness, weakness, dark urine, or unexplained high CK‑sensitive symptoms (malaise, severe fatigue, arthralgia).
- Extra clues: persistent hiccups, altered level of consciousness, seizures—features associated with CNS inflammation and more severe hyponatraemia.
- High CK is also more likely if there were prolonged immobility, seizures, or vigorous muscle activity before presentation.

How to recognise rhabdomyolysis
- CK >5× upper limit of normal is typical trigger for concern; many cases have CK in the thousands (the case above had CK ≈ 1,349 U/L).
- Check serum creatinine, potassium, phosphate, calcium, urine dipstick for blood (myoglobinuria), and urine output.
- Rhabdo requires early intervention to avoid acute kidney injury (AKI).

The management dilemma: SIADH wants fluid restriction; rhabdo needs IV fluids
This is the clinical knife-edge. Fluid restriction is first-line for most SIADH patients, but a patient with CK rise and risk of myoglobinuric AKI needs renal-protective IV fluids. Strategies to reconcile both aims:

1) Tailor the priority to the immediate threat
- If CK is only mildly elevated and renal function is normal, you can start conservative SIADH measures (fluid restriction, monitor).
- If CK is high (thousands) or rising, or urine output/creatinine is deteriorating, preventing AKI takes precedence—start IV fluids to drive myoglobin clearance.

2) Use isotonic saline carefully (and monitor sodium closely)
- Give isotonic (0.9% NaCl) boluses or controlled infusion to protect the kidney while checking sodium frequently (q2–4 hours initially).
- In true SIADH, isotonic saline may be retained and paradoxically worsen hyponatraemia; but with rhabdo and AKI risk, renal protection can outweigh modest worsening of sodium—provided monitoring and safeguards are in place.

3) Prevent rapid sodium overcorrection
- Target correction limits: ideally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours (many guidelines recommend ≤8), and certainly avoid >10–12 mmol/L/24h in high-risk patients (some allow up to 8–10). Aim for conservative correction, especially if hyponatraemia is chronic or duration unknown.
- If a water diuresis starts (sudden rise in urine output) and Na is climbing fast, give small boluses of D5W or desmopressin to slow correction (“desmopressin clamp” strategy).
- If overcorrection occurs, re-lower sodium promptly with D5W and/or desmopressin.

4) Use adjuncts for SIADH when fluid restriction is insufficient or contraindicated
- Oral urea (if available) or loop diuretics + oral salt can be considered to increase free water excretion.
- Vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) work but carry a high risk of overly rapid correction—use only with expertise and inpatient monitoring.
- Demeclocycline has delayed onset and nephrotoxic potential—avoid in rhabdo/AKI risk.

Monitoring plan (practical)
- Na, K, creatinine, CK, phosphate, calcium: baseline, then q2–4 hours during active correction or fluid shifts, then daily when stable.
- Urine output hourly if IV fluids are running; aim for urine output >0.5–1 mL/kg/hr for rhabdo protection.
- ECG monitoring for hyperkalemia.
- If sodium target is being reached safely and CK is downtrending, transition to oral/less intensive monitoring.

Specific clinical pearls from the Behçet’s/SIADH case that generalise well
- Don’t assume hyponatraemia = SIADH that can be managed only with water restriction. Look for complications: rhabdomyolysis, seizures, other end-organ effects.
- Hiccups, persistent nausea, or diffuse myalgias in a hyponatraemic patient are red flags—check CK.
- In inflammatory CNS disease causing SIADH, cytokines (IL-6) may drive ADH release—treating the underlying inflammation can fix both sodium and the root cause.
- If treating the underlying inflammatory disease (e.g., steroids, colchicine, immunosuppressant) is underway and hyponatraemia is modest, conservative SIADH management may be reasonable.
- Always have a plan to prevent overcorrection: frequent labs, desmopressin availability, and use of D5W to lower Na if necessary.

Putting it together — a pragmatic approach
1. Initial evaluation: Na, osmolality, urine osmolality/sodium, CK, creatinine, electrolytes, urine output. Look for myalgias, weakness, dark urine.
2. If CK elevated but kidney function preserved and hyponatraemia mild-moderate: consider close observation, fluid restriction, treat underlying cause; repeat labs frequently.
3. If CK markedly elevated or creatinine rising: prioritise renal protection—start isotonic IV fluids, monitor sodium every 2–4 hours, correct electrolytes, consider bicarbonate if acidotic and uric acid/myoglobin risk.
4. Prevent rapid Na swings: have desmopressin and D5W ready; use desmopressin clamp if free-water diuresis occurs.
5. Treat the underlying driver of SIADH (infection, CNS inflammation). In the Behçet’s case, anti-inflammatory therapy (colchicine here) correlated with resolution of SIADH and CK—so treat the cause where appropriate.

Concise take-home messages
- Severe hypotonic hyponatraemia can cause rhabdomyolysis; check CK when hyponatraemia is profound or patients have muscle symptoms.
- Rhabdomyolysis may force you to give IV fluids despite SIADH—balance renal protection against risk of overcorrection with close monitoring.
- Prevent overly rapid Na correction: frequent Na checks, desmopressin availability, and willingness to give D5W to re-lower Na if needed.
- Treat underlying cause of SIADH (e.g., CNS inflammation) when possible—this can resolve both hyponatraemia and muscle injury.
- Have a clear monitoring and escalation plan: serial labs, urine output targets, ECG, and thresholds for nephrology/endocrinology consultation.

Bottom line: don’t miss the “CK through the roof” in hyponatraemic patients. Early recognition, frequent monitoring, and a tailored plan that balances renal protection with safe sodium correction will keep you out of the osmotic demyelination and AKI trouble zones.



# Hyponatremia in the Hyperglycemic Patient: Correct the Sodium, Not the Patient

What’s striking: diabetics (or anyone hyperglycemic) frequently arrive with “low” sodium. Jumping to the label “hyponatraemia” without considering tonicity and glucose-driven water shifts leads to misdiagnosis, unnecessary treatments, and danger — especially when CNS symptoms are present. The recent case of acute neuro‑Behçet’s complicated by SIADH reminds us to do the math and interpret osmolality before treating sodium. In that patient, serum Na was 124 mmol/L with plasma glucose ≈14.2 mmol/L (~256 mg/dL). Even after glucose correction the Na remained low — a true hypotonic problem — and the workup (urine Osm 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L, elevated ADH) supported SIADH. But you should always ask: is this translocational (hypertonic) hyponatraemia from hyperglycaemia, true hypotonic hyponatraemia, or pseudohyponatraemia?

## Physiology in one line
Hyperglycaemia is an effective osmole — it pulls water out of cells into the ECF, diluting plasma Na and lowering measured serum sodium (apparent hyponatraemia). That is a hypertonic (translocational) hyponatraemia. Conversely, true hypotonic hyponatraemia = excess free water relative to solute (and is where SIADH lives). Pseudohyponatraemia is an assay artifact from high lipids or proteins with indirect ion‑selective electrode methods.

## Stepwise bedside approach (fast)
1. Check plasma glucose immediately on any patient with low Na.
2. Calculate corrected (glucose-adjusted) sodium before labeling hypotonic hyponatraemia.
3. If corrected Na is still low -> evaluate tonicity (measured plasma osmolality) and proceed with standard hyponatraemia workup (urine Osm, urine Na, volume status).
4. If corrected Na normalizes → treat the hyperglycaemia; usually no need for hypertonic saline.

## Correction formulas — pick one and use it
- Older, commonly used: corrected Na = measured Na + 1.6 × [(glucose mg/dL − 100) / 100]
- Alternative (for marked hyperglycaemia): use 2.0–2.4 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100 mg/dL (some data suggest 2.4 is closer with very high glucose).
- Quick SI units conversion: 1 mmol/L glucose ≈ 18 mg/dL. So for glucose in mmol/L:
  - corrected Na ≈ measured Na + 1.6 × [(glucose mmol/L − 5.6) / 5.6]
Example (from the case): glucose 14.2 mmol/L ≈ 256 mg/dL. Correction using 1.6 rule:
 corrected Na ≈ 124 + 1.6 × ((256 − 100)/100) ≈ 124 + 2.5 ≈ 126–127 mmol/L.
So the hyponatraemia was not fully explained by hyperglycaemia → continue evaluation for true hypotonic causes.

## Osmolality — the arbiter
Calculate measured or estimated plasma osmolality:
- Estimated plasma Osm (mOsm/kg) = 2 × Na (mmol/L) + glucose (mg/dL) / 18 + BUN (mg/dL) / 2.8
- Tonicity (effective osmolality) = 2 × Na + glucose/18  (BUN is ineffective osmole)
Interpretation:
- Low measured/estimated osmolality (<275 mOsm/kg): hypotonic hyponatraemia → pursue volume status and urine studies (SIADH, hypovolaemia, adrenal, hypothyroid).
- Normal/high osmolality with low Na:
  - High osmolality → translocational/hypertonic hyponatraemia (e.g., hyperglycaemia, mannitol).
  - Normal osmolality → check for pseudohyponatraemia (hyperlipidaemia, hyperproteinaemia) or lab artifact.

## Urine studies and next steps if corrected Na remains low
- Urine Osm:
  - >100 mOsm/kg and inappropriately concentrated in the face of hyponatraemia → impaired water excretion (SIADH, cortisol/thyroid deficiency, etc.).
  - <100 mOsm/kg → primary polydipsia or low solute intake.
- Urine Na and clinical volume status refine differential (e.g., SIADH typically euvolemic, urine Na >30–40 mmol/L).
- Always exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency before labeling SIADH.
- For CNS disease suspects: get CSF, MRI as indicated — CNS inflammation can drive ADH via IL‑6 and other cytokines.

## Common pitfalls
- Treating apparent hyponatraemia from hyperglycaemia with hypertonic saline: can cause dangerous hypernatremia after glucose correction.
- Ignoring osmolality and relying only on Na.
- Forgetting pseudohyponatraemia in severe hypertriglyceridemia or hyperproteinaemia (check direct ISE method or repeat with plasma water measurement).
- Failing to correlate clinical signs (volume status, CNS findings) with labs.

## Practical management tips
- If hyperglycaemia explains most/ all of the Na drop: correct glucose (insulin + fluids) and recheck Na frequently; do not give hypertonic saline solely for corrected sodium.
- If true hypotonic hyponatraemia (corrected Na remains low) and patient is euvolemic with concentrated urine → manage as SIADH (fluid restriction, treat underlying cause, consider demeclocycline/vasopressin antagonists or hypertonic saline only if severe/symptomatic).
- Monitor sodium closely when treating either glucose or hyponatraemia — both shifts can produce rapid changes in brain tonicity.
- Be cautious with over-correction: aim for safe correction rates (≤8–10 mmol/L in 24 h for most patients; slower if chronic).

## Clinical pearl from the Behçet’s case
The patient had both hyperglycaemia and true hypotonic hyponatraemia from SIADH driven by CNS inflammation. Correcting for glucose moved sodium only modestly; urine Osm was very high and urine Na elevated, and ADH was high — all consistent with SIADH. Colchicine and fluid restriction (not hypertonic saline) were sufficient as hyponatraemia resolved with treatment of the underlying inflammatory process. Bottom line: do the glucose correction, check osmolality, then treat the patient, not the uncorrected lab number.

Take-home messages
- Always check glucose and calculate corrected Na before labeling hypotonic hyponatraemia.
- Use plasma osmolality (measured or estimated) to classify hyponatraemia: hypotonic vs hypertonic (translocational) vs isotonic/pseudohyponatraemia.
- If corrected Na remains low → proceed with urine Osm/Na and endocrine screens to identify SIADH or other causes.
- Treat the underlying cause first (e.g., lower glucose, manage CNS inflammation) and avoid unnecessary or dangerous sodium corrections.



# Headache, Fever, and a Rash: The High‑Yield Skin Exam that Changes Your Neuro Workup

What makes this case memorable? A man in his 60s arrives with fever, headache, malaise and hyponatraemia — the kind of presentation that makes you think infection first (meningoencephalitis), then malignancy, then autoimmune. Neuroimaging is bland. CSF shows mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and raised protein. Then on day 5 a painful genital ulcer appears, and the history of oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like lesions suddenly reframes everything: definite Behçet’s disease with acute neuro‑Behçet’s (aseptic meningitis) complicated by SIADH. Colchicine alone produced rapid clinical and biochemical recovery without steroids or other immunosuppression.

This is a great teaching case because it shows how a targeted skin and mucosal exam (and timed re‑exams) can pivot your differential, streamline investigations, and avoid unnecessary therapies.

## Why the skin and mucosa matter in a neuro workup
Behçet’s is a multisystem vasculitis whose diagnostic power lies largely in mucocutaneous findings. The International Criteria for Behçet’s scores oral aphthae, genital aphthosis and skin lesions — and in practice these are often the clues that convert a broad, unfocused neuro workup into a specific disease pathway. Neuro‑Behçet’s can present as aseptic meningitis or meningoencephalitis, sometimes with normal MRI early on. If you treat every fever + headache + hyponatraemia as infection until proven otherwise, you may end up exposing patients to unnecessary antimicrobials, delayed correct therapy, or inappropriate invasive testing.

Key practical points from the case:
- Oral aphthae were present at presentation but initially not linked to BD because genital ulcers appeared later. Early mucosal lesions may be overlooked if not actively sought.
- Cutaneous findings were folliculitis‑like (face/trunk) — not classic "textbook" lesions, but contributory when combined with aphthae.
- MRI can be normal in acute neuro‑Behçet’s; CSF may show mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein (aseptic meningitis pattern).
- SIADH can complicate CNS inflammation from neuro‑Behçet’s; look for hyponatraemia, high urine osmolality and urine sodium despite a euvolemic exam. Symptoms like persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis/stat CK elevations can be clues to significant hyponatraemia.
- Rapid clinical and sodium recovery after colchicine supports an inflammatory (not infectious) driver in this case — though spontaneous resolution could not be fully excluded.

## ED/inpatient approach — a stepwise, skin‑forward pathway
1. Initial assessment (ED): ABCs, sepsis bundle if indicated, quick neuro exam.
   - If fever + headache + altered features: obtain basic labs (CBC, CMP including Na), blood cultures, consider CT head if focality or to guide LP safety.
   - Check serum osmolality and basic endocrine screen (TSH, cortisol if unstable) when hyponatraemia present.

2. Targeted skin/mucosa exam at first contact:
   - Look inside the mouth (oral aphthae), inspect scrotum/perineum for genital ulcers, and examine face/trunk/limbs for acneiform or folliculitis‑like lesions.
   - Ask specifically about recurrent oral ulcers and prior unexplained skin lesions; many patients do not volunteer this spontaneously.

3. Early investigations when CNS infection is on the table:
   - LP for cell count, protein, glucose, Gram stain, culture, HSV/VZV PCR; send CSF for cytology if malignancy suspected.
   - MRI brain — but remember normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑Behçet’s in the early phase.

4. Narrowing the differential with new mucocutaneous findings:
   - If oral + genital ulcers ± typical skin lesions → apply Behçet criteria; check HLA‑B51 (supportive but not diagnostic).
   - Reconsider empiric antimicrobials: if CSF and PCRs are negative and the clinical picture fits Behçet’s, broaden thought to neuroinflammation rather than infection.

5. Management priorities:
   - Treat life‑threatening infection until excluded.
   - Manage hyponatraemia per standard SIADH protocols: fluid restriction, monitor Na closely; hypertonic saline only for severe neurologic symptoms or very low Na. Vasopressin receptor antagonists not first‑line in acute inflammatory SIADH.
   - Treat Behçet’s: mucocutaneous disease often responds to colchicine; neuro‑Behçet’s classically responds to corticosteroids and sometimes additional immunosuppression. This case responded to colchicine alone, but most guidance still supports corticosteroids for parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s.

6. Re‑examination timing:
   - If the initial exam is non‑revealing but the diagnosis is unclear, schedule systematic re‑exams every 24–48 hours for the first 3–5 days. Genital ulcers and skin lesions can evolve or appear later (as in this case on day 5).
   - Document serial skin/mucosa findings and photographs when feasible to track evolving clues.

## When to suspect SIADH in neuroinflammatory disease
- Euvolemic hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine (high urine osmolality and urine Na >20 mmol/L), normal adrenal and thyroid function.
- CNS inflammation (aseptic meningitis/encephalitis) is a classic cause; Behçet’s‑associated CNS inflammation is a plausible trigger via cytokine‑mediated ADH release (IL‑6 implicated).
- Watch for non‑specific manifestations of hyponatraemia: hiccups, malaise, lethargy, weakness, and complications like rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK).

## Clinical pearls
- Always inspect the mouth and perineum in patients with fever + headache + neurological symptoms — a single missed aphthous ulcer can change the diagnosis.
- Neuro‑Behçet’s can present with normal MRI early; CSF findings of aseptic meningitis + mucocutaneous features can be enough to make the diagnosis.
- Re‑examine daily for evolving mucocutaneous signs. Genital ulcers may appear days after presentation.
- SIADH may be the first lab clue to CNS inflammation; treat the hyponatraemia according to severity but look for CNS causes.
- Colchicine is first‑line for mucocutaneous Behçet’s and may help systemic inflammation, but don’t reflexively skip corticosteroids when parenchymal CNS disease or severe neuro features are present.

Key takeaways:
- A focused skin and mucosal exam can pivot a broad neuroinfectious/malignant workup to a specific inflammatory diagnosis — Behçet’s.
- Normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑Behçet’s; combine serial exams, CSF analysis and clinical context.
- Re‑examination at 24–48 hour intervals for several days is high‑yield when early mucocutaneous features are absent or subtle.
- Consider SIADH in neuroinflammatory disease; manage hyponatraemia carefully and treat the underlying inflammation.
- Use colchicine for mucocutaneous disease; for neuro‑Behçet’s, corticosteroids remain the standard for most cases, but individual responses (as in this report) can vary.
