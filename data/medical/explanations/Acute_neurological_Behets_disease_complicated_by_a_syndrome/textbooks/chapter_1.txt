Chapter 1: ## Clinical Approach to Concurrent Behçet’s Disease, Neuro-Behçet’s, and Hyponatremia

Patients who present with fever, headache or other nonspecific CNS symptoms together with mucocutaneous lesions (oral and genital aphthae, acneiform or folliculitis‑like rash) and hyponatraemia should prompt early consideration of three linked problems: a systemic inflammatory disorder such as Behçet’s disease (BD), neurologic involvement of BD (neuro‑BD), and an electrolyte disturbance most commonly due to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The case just presented is a useful model for a bedside‑first, safety‑prioritizing approach: (1) stabilise the patient and identify life‑threatening hyponatraemia or CNS infection; (2) rapidly characterise the hyponatraemia and volemic status; (3) pursue a focused diagnostic workup for CNS infection and neuro‑inflammation; and (4) treat the underlying inflammatory disorder while supporting and monitoring sodium correction.

Initial stabilisation and urgency. The first priority is to assess the acuity of neurologic compromise and the severity of hyponatraemia. Severe or life‑threatening manifestations of hyponatraemia—seizures, obtundation or deep coma, severe respiratory compromise, or rapid neurologic deterioration—mandate prompt treatment with hypertonic saline (eg, bolus 3% NaCl) even before a definitive diagnosis is established. For patients who are symptomatic but not critically ill, cease hypotonic fluids, institute close neurologic monitoring and proceed with investigations. Be mindful that the clinical picture may be mixed: fever, neck stiffness or altered behaviour may reflect infection; persistent hiccups, as in this case, can be an early clue to profound hyponatraemia or brainstem irritation.

Rapid characterisation of hyponatraemia at the bedside. A systematic biochemical assessment allows differentiation of pseudohyponatraemia and the principal pathophysiologies:

- Measure plasma glucose, serum osmolality, renal function, TSH and cortisol. Hyperglycaemia can produce a translocational hyponatraemia and should be corrected for when interpreting sodium. Hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency must be excluded because their treatments differ.
- If hypotonic hyponatraemia is confirmed (low serum osmolality), obtain urine osmolality and urine sodium. SIADH typically shows inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality >100–300 mOsm/kg, often >300 mOsm/kg) with elevated urine sodium (>30 mmol/L) in a euvolemic patient.
- Assess volume status carefully—most patients with SIADH are clinically euvolemic. Use orthostatic vital signs, mucous membranes, skin turgor, presence/absence of peripheral oedema or raised JVP; these clinical findings help distinguish hypovolaemic, euvolaemic and hypervolaemic hyponatraemia and guide therapy. Remember that the physical exam is imperfect; integrate biochemical data and clinical context.

Diagnosing SIADH in the setting of suspected neuro‑inflammation. When you have hypotonic hyponatraemia with concentrated urine and high urine sodium in a euvolemic patient, normal thyroid and adrenal function, and no recent diuretic use, the diagnosis of SIADH is most likely. Measurement of plasma ADH is rarely necessary for routine clinical care and results are often not timely; the biochemical pattern plus clinical context is usually sufficient. In patients with CNS inflammation (meningoencephalitis, meningitis, brainstem disease) SIADH is a well‑recognised mechanism — inflammatory cytokines (eg, interleukin‑6) can stimulate ADH secretion — so the presence of aseptic meningitis or neuro‑BD should raise suspicion that SIADH is secondary to CNS disease.

Empiric management while workup proceeds. Because fever and focal neurologic symptoms can signal treatable CNS infections, empiric antimicrobial coverage is often appropriate until infection is excluded. Obtain blood cultures and perform a lumbar puncture promptly if there are no contraindications; if increased intracranial pressure or focal mass lesion is suspected, obtain a head CT or MRI first. Empiric regimens should cover likely pathogens and include acyclovir when viral encephalitis is a consideration. Empiric antibiotics/antivirals can be discontinued when cultures and PCRs are negative and clinical picture supports inflammatory disease rather than infection.

Recognising neuro‑Behçet’s disease even with normal imaging. Neuro‑BD commonly presents with meningoencephalitis, headache and behavioural change, and less commonly with focal deficits or brainstem signs. Crucially, early in the disease MRI can be normal or nonspecific; therefore a normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑BD. Cerebrospinal fluid in neuro‑BD often shows mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein without an identified pathogen, as in this case. The diagnosis of neuro‑BD in a patient with mucocutaneous signs should be clinical: use the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease to establish systemic BD, then interpret CNS findings in that context after excluding infection, neoplasm and other inflammatory disorders (eg, sarcoid). HLA‑B51, when present, supports the diagnosis but is not diagnostic.

Therapeutic priorities for SIADH in this setting. For SIADH due to CNS inflammation, two principles apply: treat the hyponatraemia to prevent acute neurologic complications, and treat the underlying inflammatory disease to remove the stimulus for inappropriate ADH release.

- Initial, conservative measures for mild to moderate, asymptomatic SIADH include free water restriction (often to <800–1000 mL/day), correction of precipitants (eg, discontinue offending drugs), and close monitoring of serum sodium and neurologic status. In many patients with reversible CNS inflammation, these measures and treatment of the CNS disease lead to gradual correction.
- For severe symptoms (seizures, coma, severe obtundation) or very low sodium, administer hypertonic saline promptly. Use modest boluses of 3% NaCl and aim for a small, rapid rise (eg, 4–6 mmol/L) to control life‑threatening symptoms, then slow the correction. Avoid overcorrection: plan to limit the rise to ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours (some guidelines suggest ≤6–8 mmol/L in high‑risk patients for osmotic demyelination), and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours. If overcorrection occurs, re‑lower sodium with hypotonic fluid or desmopressin as indicated.
- Pharmacologic agents (demeclocycline, urea, vasopressin receptor antagonists) have a role in chronic or refractory SIADH but are generally not first‑line in the acute neuroinflammatory setting. Vaptans can correct sodium but carry risks and do not treat the underlying disease.

Treating the underlying neuro‑inflammatory disorder. Corticosteroids are the mainstay of therapy for acute neuro‑BD and most patients respond to high‑dose steroids, often supplemented by other immunosuppressants for more severe or relapsing disease. However, as demonstrated by the present case, some patients with meningoencephalitis‑type neuro‑BD may improve with less intensive therapy — colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous BD and has anti‑inflammatory effects (inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and suppression of cytokine production) that may, in selected cases, ameliorate systemic and neurologic inflammation. This response is not the norm for neuro‑BD, and the decision to withhold systemic corticosteroids should be individualized after careful evaluation and close monitoring; empiric steroids are appropriate when neuro‑BD is suspected and infection has been reasonably excluded. Multidisciplinary involvement (neurology, rheumatology, infectious disease) is important for tailoring immunosuppression.

Avoiding common diagnostic and management pitfalls. Do not be reassured by a normal MRI early in the disease course; rely on CSF analysis and clinical pattern recognition. Avoid indiscriminate testing — for example, ACE and sIL‑2R have limited sensitivity and specificity and should be directed by clinical suspicion (eg, features of sarcoid or lymphoma). Always check for drug‑induced SIADH (eg, cyclophosphamide, antipsychotics, SSRIs) and correct for serum sodium in marked hyperglycaemia. Watch for less obvious manifestations of hyponatraemia such as persistent hiccups, nausea, or unexplained rhabdomyolysis; measure CK when symptoms or severe electrolyte disturbance raise concern.

Monitoring and follow‑up. In the inpatient setting, monitor serum sodium every 4–6 hours during active correction and daily once stable. Reassess neurologic status frequently. If SIADH resolves with treatment of neuro‑inflammation, the patient can often transition from fluid restriction to ad lib intake as appropriate; persistent or recurrent SIADH may require longer‑term therapy. Arrange ophthalmologic evaluation for uveitis, outpatient rheumatology/neurology follow‑up for ongoing management of BD, and patient education about recognising flares and medication‑related side effects.

Takeaway clinical checklist for the triad presentation
- Recognise the syndrome: new mucocutaneous lesions + nonspecific CNS symptoms + hyponatraemia should raise immediate suspicion for neuro‑BD complicated by SIADH.
- Stabilise first: rapidly identify life‑threatening hyponatraemia or CNS infection and treat empirically as needed.
- Characterise hyponatraemia: measure serum osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium; exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency; assess volume status.
- Cover infection while you investigate: obtain blood cultures and CSF studies; start empiric antimicrobials/acyclovir when clinically warranted.
- Look for neuro‑BD even with normal imaging: CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in the context of BD mucocutaneous features support the diagnosis.
- Treat the hyponatraemia safely (fluid restriction, limited use of hypertonic saline for severe symptoms, strict limits on correction rate) while addressing CNS inflammation (usually corticosteroids; colchicine may help mucocutaneous disease and, in select cases, systemic inflammation).

Applying this framework to the presented case explains the successful course: early recognition of SIADH and careful sodium management while empirically covering infection, combined with focused clinical diagnosis of BD and targeted anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine with close observation), led to resolution of CNS symptoms and normalization of sodium without the need for aggressive immunosuppression.
