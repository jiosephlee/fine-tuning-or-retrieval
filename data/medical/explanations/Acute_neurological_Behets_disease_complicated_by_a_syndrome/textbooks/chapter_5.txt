Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Chapter 5: ## Management

Management of a patient with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) complicated by SIADH requires two parallel and coordinated strategies: control the CNS inflammation that is driving the disease, and safely correct and monitor the hyponatraemia. Each problem influences the other—successful suppression of neuroinflammation is often the definitive treatment for SIADH in this context, while inappropriate or overly rapid correction of sodium can cause catastrophic neurologic injury. Treatment choices should therefore balance the urgency of controlling inflammation against the risks of sodium correction, drug toxicity, and infectious complications from immunosuppression.

Controlling neuroinflammation: first-line and escalation
- First-line therapy for acute neuro-BD is high‑dose corticosteroids. For patients with suspected or proven parenchymal CNS involvement or meningoencephalitis causing significant symptoms, the usual initial regimen is intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days, followed by an oral prednisone taper (typical starting dose ~1 mg/kg/day, maximum ~60–80 mg/day) with gradual tapering over weeks to months guided by clinical response and relapse risk. Rapid initiation of steroids is frequently necessary to prevent permanent neurologic deficit.
- For most patients, a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant is added when there is relapsing disease, high risk of recurrence, or need for prolonged steroid exposure. Common options with evidence in BD include azathioprine (generally 2–3 mg/kg/day, after TPMT testing when available) and methotrexate (typically 15–25 mg weekly, subcutaneously or intramuscularly, with folic acid supplementation). Mycophenolate mofetil is an alternative in some practices (up to 2–3 g/day), although comparative trial data are limited.
- For fulminant, refractory or progressive parenchymal neuro-BD despite steroids and conventional immunosuppressants, escalation to biologic therapy is recommended. Anti‑TNF agents have the strongest evidence in refractory neuro‑BD: infliximab (5 mg/kg IV at weeks 0, 2, 6, then every 6–8 weeks) and adalimumab (often an 80 mg loading dose followed by 40 mg every other week) are commonly used and can induce durable control. Prior to TNF blockade, screen for latent tuberculosis, hepatitis B and other infections, and counsel patients about infection risk.
- IL‑6 blockade (e.g., tocilizumab) has been used in selected refractory cases and when TNF inhibitors fail or are contraindicated; evidence is limited to case series and reports. Cyclophosphamide may be used for life‑ or function‑threatening disease in some centres, but cyclosporine A should be avoided in patients with neuro‑BD or who are at risk for CNS involvement because of a well‑described association with neurotoxicity and precipitating neurologic symptoms.
- Role of colchicine: colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous and some joint manifestations of BD (typical dosing for mucocutaneous disease is 0.5 mg to 1 mg total daily, often given as 0.5 mg twice daily), but randomized data and reviews do not support colchicine as a reliable monotherapy for established parenchymal neuro‑BD. Nevertheless, in milder phenotypes—patients with aseptic meningitis, minimal deficits, or when corticosteroids are contraindicated—it may be reasonable to trial colchicine while maintaining a low threshold for escalation. When used, monitor for gastrointestinal intolerance, cytopenias and drug interactions (especially renal impairment and concomitant P‑glycoprotein or CYP3A4 inhibitors).

Treating the SIADH: immediate and staged measures
- The initial steps in SIADH focus on assessing severity and symptoms, instituting simple measures, and preventing overly rapid sodium shifts. For asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients with chronic or subacute SIADH, conservative therapy—fluid restriction to approximately 500–1,000 mL/day (individualized by insensible losses and ongoing outputs)—is first‑line, together with removal of offending drugs and treatment of the underlying cause (here, suppression of CNS inflammation).
- In more severe or symptomatic hyponatraemia (e.g., seizures, severe confusion, obtundation) prompt hypertonic saline is indicated. The commonly used initial approach for severe symptoms is a 100 mL bolus of 3% saline over 10 minutes, repeated up to two additional times as needed to produce a modest and rapid rise in serum sodium (typically 4–6 mmol/L) or clear clinical improvement. After acute stabilization, slow controlled correction is essential: target an increase no greater than 6–8 mmol/L in the first 24 hours (some guidelines allow up to 8 mmol/L), and no more than ~18 mmol/L over 48 hours. Patients with risk factors for osmotic demyelination (malnutrition, alcoholism, advanced liver disease, hypokalemia) should be treated more conservatively.
- For euvolemic SIADH that is fluid‑restrictive‑refractory or where restriction is impractical, several options exist: oral salt tablets combined with a loop diuretic (to reduce medullary concentrating ability), oral urea (an effective osmotic agent widely used in Europe and increasingly used elsewhere; typical doses 15–30 g/day), and demeclocyline (rarely used because of nephrotoxicity and slow onset). Vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans such as tolvaptan) produce aquaresis and rapid sodium correction and can be effective in selected hospitalized patients; however, they are costly, require careful inpatient monitoring to avoid overcorrection, and have been associated with hepatotoxicity with longer‑term use. In practice, vaptans are reserved for short‑term use under specialist guidance when other measures fail or are contraindicated.
- Close monitoring is mandatory: serum sodium should be checked every 4–6 hours during active correction or hypertonic therapy, with concomitant tracking of urine output, urine osmolality and urine sodium to assess ongoing ADH activity. Stop or slow hypertonic infusion once sodium has risen sufficiently (typically by 4–6 mmol/L) or symptoms improve. Be vigilant for “autocorrection” when the inciting cause (neuroinflammation) begins to resolve—urine osmolality may fall and prompt rapid sodium rises; frequent monitoring allows early detection and measures (free water or desmopressin) to blunt overcorrection if necessary.

Practical sequencing and integration in the case of neuro‑BD with SIADH
- Start with a simultaneous but prioritized plan: stabilize serum sodium and neurologic status, and treat the presumed inflammatory driver. In patients with severe neurologic compromise from neuro‑BD, immediate high‑dose IV steroids should not be delayed while investigating hyponatraemia; at the same time, institute appropriate measures to prevent dangerous sodium shifts (e.g., controlled hypertonic saline for severe symptoms, fluid restriction for milder cases).
- Because CNS inflammation commonly drives SIADH, effective anti‑inflammatory treatment often corrects the inappropriate ADH secretion. Expect improvement in serum sodium as disease activity falls; this temporal relationship should guide expectations and monitoring frequency.
- Avoid medications that may aggravate SIADH (certain antidepressants, carbamazepine, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, some antipsychotics) unless they are essential; review the medication list carefully before starting immunosuppressive therapy.

Safety considerations and monitoring on immunosuppression
- Screen for latent infections (tuberculosis with interferon‑gamma release assay and chest X‑ray, hepatitis B surface antigen/core antibody and hepatitis C testing) prior to starting biologics or other long‑term immunosuppressants. Vaccinate appropriately (influenza, pneumococcal, others) before immunosuppression where feasible.
- Monitor laboratory parameters routinely: complete blood counts and liver enzymes for azathioprine (and TPMT testing where available), methotrexate (LFTs, renal function, and provide folic acid), and for colchicine when used chronically (renal function, particularly in the elderly).
- Be aware that cyclosporine A carries a risk of neurotoxicity and is best avoided in patients with known or suspected neuro‑BD; if cyclosporine is used for other BD manifestations, monitor closely and reconsider alternatives if neurologic symptoms arise.
- In patients with hyponatraemia, correct co‑morbid metabolic contributors: correct hypokalemia (which will increase serum sodium when replaced), and account for hyperglycaemia—serum sodium should be “corrected” for hyperglycaemia when interpreting values and planning therapy. In the index case, poor glycaemic control likely affected sodium measurements and should be optimized.

Topical and supportive measures
- While systemic therapy is instituted, treat mucocutaneous lesions symptomatically: topical corticosteroids or analgesic mouth rinses for oral aphthae, topical antiseptics or silver sulfadiazine for genital ulcers as needed, and supportive measures for pain and nutrition. Address complications such as rhabdomyolysis (as in the presented patient) with careful fluid management balancing the need for renal protection and the risk of worsening hyponatraemia.
- Provide rehabilitation and assess for cognitive or focal deficits before discharge; arrange close outpatient follow‑up with neurology, rheumatology or a multidisciplinary BD clinic and regular sodium monitoring until stabilization is confirmed.

When colchicine alone may be considered
- The reported case responded to colchicine monotherapy, which is informative but not generalizable. Colchicine is an appropriate first‑line agent for mucocutaneous BD and may be considered in patients with very mild, self‑limited neuro‑inflammatory presentations (aseptic meningitis without focal deficits and normal or stable imaging) when corticosteroids are undesirable or contraindicated. However, clinicians should maintain a low threshold to escalate to corticosteroids and immunosuppressants if neurologic symptoms progress or do not improve rapidly, because delaying effective therapy risks permanent neurologic injury.

Summary
- Manage neuro‑BD aggressively when parenchymal involvement or significant neurologic symptoms are present, using high‑dose IV corticosteroids followed by an appropriate steroid‑sparing agent; escalate to anti‑TNF biologics or other agents for refractory disease. Avoid cyclosporine in patients at risk for CNS disease.
- Treat SIADH with a staged approach: fluid restriction and removal of reversible contributors for mild cases; hypertonic saline for severe or symptomatic hyponatraemia with strict limits on the rate of correction (≤6–8 mmol/L in 24 h, ≤18 mmol/L in 48 h); consider salt + loop diuretic, urea, or vaptans in refractory situations under specialist guidance.
- Coordinate care so that therapy for the underlying neuroinflammation and sodium management proceed in parallel, with frequent monitoring (serum sodium every 4–6 hours during active correction) and attention to drug safety, infection screening and comorbidity optimisation.
