Chapter 6: ## Prognosis, Follow-up, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis after an episode of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) is heterogeneous and depends largely on the pattern and severity of CNS involvement and the rapidity with which inflammation is controlled. Neuro-BD may present as a monophasic aseptic meningitis-like illness that resolves completely, as in the current case, but more commonly it follows a relapsing–remitting course. When parenchymal lesions occur—particularly in the brainstem, thalamus or hemispheres—patients are at increased risk for permanent neurological deficits, cumulative disability and cognitive decline with recurrent attacks. Early recognition and suppression of CNS inflammation reduce the likelihood of irreversible damage; therefore timeliness of immunomodulatory therapy is a major determinant of long-term outcome.

Clinical vigilance is required even when early imaging is normal. Brain MRI can be normal in the first days of an inflammatory episode of neuro-BD, particularly when the phenotype is predominantly meningeal or when lesions are small. A normal early MRI does not exclude neuro-BD; if clinical suspicion remains, repeat imaging (with and without contrast, using thin slices and dedicated brainstem sequences) should be obtained within 1–4 weeks or sooner if the clinical picture worsens. The development of new focal deficits, prolonged or progressive symptoms, or new cognitive impairment should prompt re-imaging and reconsideration of escalation of immunotherapy. In practice, patients with an initial self-limited presentation warrant close outpatient follow-up at short intervals (weeks) to detect relapse early; those with parenchymal disease typically require more intensive follow-up and longer-term immunosuppression.

Hyponatraemia and SIADH in the context of neuro-BD deserve particular attention because they both affect management and can produce misleading clinical features. When a patient with suspected or established BD presents with headache, fever or other CNS symptoms plus hyponatraemia, maintain a high index of suspicion for SIADH related to CNS inflammation. The diagnosis of SIADH should be based on the standard biochemical and clinical criteria (hypotonic hyponatraemia, inappropriately concentrated urine, elevated urine sodium in the setting of euvolemia, and exclusion of hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency and recent diuretic use), rather than reliance on plasma ADH measurements. ADH assays are technically difficult, variably reproducible, and often unhelpful for clinical decision-making; they can be misleading, as illustrated by the elevated ADH in this case. In the acute setting, manage SIADH according to severity: mild or asymptomatic cases respond to fluid restriction and addressing the underlying inflammatory driver; severe symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, obtundation) requires controlled administration of hypertonic saline to raise serum sodium promptly enough to relieve cerebral edema while avoiding excessive correction. Aim for a modest acute rise sufficient to reverse severe symptoms (for example, several mmol/L), then limit the total correction to a safe rate—generally no more than about 8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours—to minimize the risk of osmotic demyelination. Monitor serum sodium frequently (every 2–4 hours while actively correcting), reassess volume status, and treat the inflammatory disorder that is driving inappropriate ADH release. Recognize that non-neurological clues—persistent hiccups, anorexia, nausea, or an unexpected rise in creatine kinase/rhabdomyolysis—can be manifestations of significant hyponatraemia and should prompt measurement of serum sodium.

Follow-up after an episode of neuro-BD should be structured and multidisciplinary. Early post‑discharge contact is important to confirm clinical improvement and sodium stability. Neurology follow-up with periodic examinations and cognitive screening is indicated; repeat MRI is reasonable if symptoms persist or recur. Ophthalmology evaluation is essential because ocular involvement can progress silently and strongly influences morbidity; schedule regular eye exams even if none were present at the index episode. Because BD is a multisystem vasculitis, screen and monitor for vascular involvement (venous thrombosis and arterial aneurysms) guided by symptoms and physical findings—utilise vascular imaging selectively rather than as routine indiscriminate testing. Rehabilitation services should be engaged for any residual motor, vestibular or cognitive deficits.

Decisions about long-term immunosuppression should be individualized according to phenotype, severity and relapse risk. Colchicine is appropriate and often effective for mucocutaneous and arthritic manifestations and—as in this patient—can be associated with resolution of a self-limited neuro-meningitic presentation. However, the standard approach to acute parenchymal neuro-BD commonly includes high-dose corticosteroids to rapidly control inflammation, usually followed by a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant (azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide for severe cases) or a biologic agent (anti-TNF agents such as infliximab or adalimumab) for refractory or relapsing disease. Avoid medications known to worsen neurological disease: cyclosporine is associated with neurotoxicity and has been linked to new or worsening neuro-BD and should be avoided in patients with CNS involvement. Prior to initiation of long-term immunosuppression, perform appropriate baseline screening (TB testing, hepatitis serologies, vaccinations where appropriate) and institute monitoring protocols for drug toxicity (periodic CBCs, liver and renal function tests, and vigilance for infectious complications).

Preventing iatrogenic harm is a consistent theme in follow-up care. Avoid indiscriminate testing that is unlikely to alter management; use targeted investigations driven by the clinical picture. Correct hyponatraemia cautiously and under close monitoring to prevent osmotic demyelination. When instituting immunosuppression, use agents appropriate to the neuro-BD phenotype and avoid cyclosporine when CNS disease is present. Educate patients about relapse symptoms—new focal neurological signs, persistent severe headache, altered cognition, visual changes or recurrence of mucocutaneous lesions—and ensure rapid access to care because early treatment of relapse preserves neurologic function.

Clinical pearls
- Acute neuro-BD may be monophasic but has a meaningful risk of relapse and cumulative disability; early and appropriate control of CNS inflammation improves outcomes.  
- A normal brain MRI early in the course does not exclude neuro-BD; repeat imaging and close clinical follow-up are essential.  
- In BD patients with headache or fever plus hyponatraemia, think about SIADH from CNS inflammation; use standard biochemical criteria and exclude mimics rather than relying on ADH assays.  
- Persistent hiccups, unexplained anorexia or an unexpected rise in CK can be clues to clinically important hyponatraemia.  
- Correct hyponatraemia slowly and under frequent monitoring; treat severe neurologic symptoms with controlled hypertonic saline but limit total correction rates to reduce risk of osmotic demyelination.  
- Tailor immunosuppression to the neuro-BD phenotype: use steroids and steroid‑sparing agents for parenchymal disease; avoid cyclosporine if CNS involvement is present; consider biologics for refractory or recurrent cases.  
- Long‑term follow-up should include neurologic and ophthalmologic surveillance, assessment for vascular disease, monitoring for treatment toxicity and rehabilitation for residual deficits.

Taken together, these principles will help clinicians recognise and manage the short‑term complications (such as SIADH), reduce the risk of long‑term neurological morbidity, and limit iatrogenic harm through judicious investigation and appropriately targeted therapy.
