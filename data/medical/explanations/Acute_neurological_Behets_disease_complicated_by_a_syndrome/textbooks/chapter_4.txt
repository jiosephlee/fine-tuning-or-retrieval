Chapter 4: ## Diagnostic Workup

When a patient presents with mucocutaneous lesions and new central nervous system (CNS) symptoms, a structured, stepwise diagnostic approach is essential to (1) establish whether the patient meets criteria for Behçet’s disease (BD), (2) determine whether the CNS process represents neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) or an alternative cause, and (3) identify and classify the cause of hyponatraemia if present (for example, syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, SIADH). The case above illustrates how these three threads must be woven together: the mucocutaneous findings led to classification as definite BD, aseptic meningitis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pointed toward acute neuro‑BD despite a normal early MRI, and laboratory testing confirmed SIADH. Below is a practical diagnostic framework, with attention to common pitfalls.

1) Establishing the clinical diagnosis of Behçet’s disease
- Use the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) as the initial classificatory tool. Points are allocated as follows: oral aphthae (2), genital aphthae (2), ocular involvement (2), skin lesions (1), vascular lesions (1), neurologic involvement (1), and a positive pathergy test (1). A total score of ≥4 supports a diagnosis of BD. In practice, careful bedside documentation of oral and genital ulcers, the timing and appearance of skin lesions, and a targeted ophthalmologic examination (to detect current or prior uveitis) are high‑yield.
- Ancillary tests such as HLA‑B51 can support but not establish the diagnosis; its presence increases disease probability in the appropriate clinical context but is neither necessary nor sufficient. Pathergy testing has variable sensitivity by region/ethnicity and should be interpreted accordingly.
- Avoid over-reliance on non‑specific serologic biomarkers (eg, ACE, soluble IL‑2 receptor) unless there is a specific clinical suspicion for an alternate diagnosis such as sarcoidosis or lymphoma; these tests can produce false leads if applied indiscriminately.

2) Evaluating suspected neuro‑Behçet’s disease
- Neuro‑BD is a clinical diagnosis that rests on compatible neurological features in the setting of BD and exclusion of mimics. Typical presentations include meningoencephalitis, brainstem syndromes, and parenchymal lesions with subacute progression. Headache is common but non‑specific.
- Brain MRI with and without gadolinium is the cornerstone of evaluation. Sequences should include T1, T2, FLAIR, diffusion‑weighted imaging, and post‑contrast images with attention to the brainstem and diencephalic/hypothalamic regions. However, MRI can be normal in early or purely meningeal/aseptic presentations — a normal early MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD. If clinical suspicion remains high, repeat MRI (often within 1–2 weeks) or follow‑up imaging is appropriate.
- Lumbar puncture is mandatory when CNS infection is in the differential or when neuro‑BD is suspected. CSF studies should include opening pressure, cell count with differential, protein, glucose, Gram stain, bacterial cultures, and targeted PCR testing for HSV, VZV, enteroviruses and, where indicated, mycobacterial studies and cryptococcal antigen. CSF cytology is useful if malignancy is being considered.
- Typical CSF findings in acute neuro‑BD are a lymphocytic (or mixed) pleocytosis and moderately elevated protein; glucose is usually normal. Oligoclonal IgG bands are typically absent (which can help distinguish neuro‑BD from multiple sclerosis), although this is not absolute. CSF interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) can be elevated and may be informative as an adjunct marker of CNS inflammation in specialized centers, but it is not required for diagnosis.
- Interpretation requires integration: CSF pleocytosis and elevated protein with negative infectious studies in a patient who meets ICBD criteria increases the likelihood of acute neuro‑BD.

3) Workup and confirmation of SIADH as the cause of hyponatraemia
- Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia before labeling SIADH: measure serum sodium and serum osmolality. SIADH requires a low serum osmolality (typically <275 mOsm/kg; some laboratories use <270) in the setting of hyponatraemia.
- Demonstrate inappropriate concentration of urine: urine osmolality that is inappropriately high for the plasma hypo‑osmolal state (generally >100 mOsm/kg and commonly >300 mOsm/kg) indicates impaired water excretion.
- Urine sodium is usually >30 mmol/L when the patient is euvolemic and dietary sodium intake is not severely restricted. Low urine sodium suggests volume depletion or recent diuretic use.
- Confirm euvolemia clinically (no signs of hypovolemia such as orthostasis or tachycardia, and no signs of hypervolemia such as pulmonary edema or peripheral edema). Be aware that clinical assessment of volume status can be challenging and requires integration of history, exam, and laboratory data.
- Exclude other causes of hyponatraemia: normal thyroid function tests (TSH and free T4), normal adrenal function (random cortisol may suffice in many settings, with stimulation testing if there is concern for insufficiency), and normal renal function. A careful medication review is essential because thiazide diuretics, some antidepressants, anticonvulsants and chemotherapeutic agents are frequent culprits. Pulmonary disease and malignancy must also be considered as potential causes of SIADH.
- Adjust measured serum sodium for severe hyperglycaemia before making the diagnosis. Hyperglycaemia causes osmotic translocation of water from intracellular to extracellular compartments and lowers measured sodium. A practical correction is: add approximately 1.6 mmol/L to the measured sodium for every 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) increase of glucose above ~5.6 mmol/L. Example from the case: plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L (≈256 mg/dL) represents ≈8.6 mmol/L above 5.6 mmol/L; corrected Na would increase modestly (~+2–3 mmol/L). Correcting for glucose avoids misclassification of hyponatraemia and prevents inappropriate treatment.
- Additional laboratory clues: low serum uric acid and low BUN are common in SIADH but are non‑specific.

4) Excluding infections, malignancy and other mimics
- Because CNS infection can mimic neuro‑BD and cause SIADH, it must be actively excluded. Empiric antimicrobial or antiviral therapy is reasonable while results are pending if the pretest probability of infection is significant.
- Evaluate for malignancy (particularly small cell lung cancer) as a cause of SIADH when indicated by clinical features or imaging; chest radiography or CT chest is appropriate when pulmonary symptoms or risk factors exist.
- If neurosarcoidosis, CNS lymphoma, or other inflammatory disorders are part of the differential, target tests should be ordered only when clinical or imaging clues exist (eg, suspicious systemic findings, lymphadenopathy, focal MRI lesions). Non‑specific biomarkers such as ACE and sIL‑2R have limited sensitivity and specificity and commonly give false leads if used in an undifferentiated workup; interpret them in the clinical context.

5) Practical integration and common pitfalls
- Do not exclude neuro‑BD solely because early MRI is normal. A normal MRI early in the disease course can occur with aseptic meningitis or limited parenchymal involvement; rely on clinical findings and CSF studies and consider repeat imaging if symptoms persist or worsen.
- Avoid over‑testing with low‑yield assays that are unlikely to change management. Targeted testing guided by the history, exam, and basic imaging/CSF findings is more productive than broad panels that generate incidental abnormalities.
- Always correct sodium for severe hyperglycaemia and review medications before diagnosing SIADH. Diuretics, recent intravenous fluids, and osmotic agents can confound urine sodium and osmolality measurements.
- Measure and monitor electrolytes frequently when treating hyponatraemia. Rapid over‑correction can cause osmotic demyelination; while this is a management concern, it underscores the importance of an accurate initial diagnosis and careful monitoring.
- Recognize that laboratory measurements of ADH are rarely required for routine diagnosis of SIADH; elevated ADH, when measured, may support the diagnosis but is not necessary. Similarly, CSF IL‑6 can be informative in academic centers but is not required for routine diagnosis of neuro‑BD.

In summary, the diagnostic workup of a patient with suspected BD, new neurologic symptoms and hyponatraemia is an exercise in synthesis: apply the ICBD to classify BD, obtain MRI and lumbar puncture to define CNS inflammation and to exclude infection, and use standard biochemical criteria (with correction for hyperglycaemia and exclusion of endocrine and renal causes) to diagnose SIADH. The case presented exemplifies this approach: detailed mucocutaneous inspection and application of the ICBD established BD; CSF showed aseptic meningitis consistent with acute neuro‑BD despite a normal early MRI; and a focused endocrine and laboratory assessment met criteria for SIADH after correcting for hyperglycaemia and excluding thyroid/adrenal disease and diuretics.
