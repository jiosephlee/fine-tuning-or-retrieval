Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology: From Behçet’s Inflammation to Neuroinvolvement and SIADH

Behçet’s disease (BD) is best understood as an autoinflammatory vasculitis in which a genetically susceptible host mounts an excessive innate and adaptive immune response to poorly defined triggers. At the cellular level this state is characterised by neutrophil hyperactivity and an adaptive T‑cell skewing towards Th1 and Th17 phenotypes with increased production of interferon‑γ, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), IL‑1 and IL‑6. HLA‑B51 is the strongest genetic risk marker identified to date and increases the probability of developing BD, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis; clinical criteria remain paramount. The downstream effect of this immune milieu is a propensity for neutrophil‑driven endothelial injury of small and medium vessels that manifests clinically as recurrent mucosal ulceration, skin pustules, ocular inflammation and, in a subset of patients, involvement of major organs including the central nervous system.

Neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) arises when the inflammatory process involves the brain, meninges, venous sinuses or cerebral vessels. Two broad phenotypes are recognised. The parenchymal form typically presents as an acute or subacute meningoencephalitis with a predilection for brainstem and diencephalic structures; patients may have headache, fever, behavioural change, cranial nerve dysfunction or focal deficits. The non‑parenchymal phenotype comprises cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, intracranial hypertension and, less commonly, isolated meningeal disease. Importantly, early parenchymal neuro‑BD may produce only inflammatory changes in the cerebrospinal fluid or microscopic perivascular infiltration that are not immediately visible on conventional MRI. Thus a normal MRI does not exclude active CNS inflammation; the case patient illustrates this point—he had CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and raised protein consistent with aseptic meningitis despite an unremarkable brain MRI early in the course.

How does CNS inflammation in BD promote inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion? The connection lies in cytokine‑mediated modulation of hypothalamic and circumventricular structures that regulate arginine vasopressin (AVP, often referred to clinically as ADH) release. Proinflammatory cytokines produced in the periphery or intrathecally—most notably IL‑6—can stimulate hypothalamic neurons directly or indirectly via glial activation to increase AVP secretion. In several CNS inflammatory states (meningitis, encephalitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage) elevated CSF and serum IL‑6 levels correlate with increased ADH/AVP activity and the development of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). In BD there is evidence of elevated IL‑6 in CSF during acute neuro‑BD, and the disease’s intrinsic ability to provoke robust IL‑6 production through activated monocytes and neutrophils provides a plausible mechanistic link to ADH dysregulation. In the present patient the temporal association—CNS symptoms and CSF inflammation concurrent with elevated ADH (22.4 pg/mL), low serum osmolality and concentrated urine—supports this pathophysiologic sequence, although direct measurement of CSF cytokines would be required to prove causality.

The pathobiology of SIADH is straightforward in physiological terms but has important clinical consequences. Excess circulating ADH acts at V2 receptors in the renal collecting duct to insert aquaporin‑2 channels into the apical membrane, increasing free‑water reabsorption independent of sodium transport. The result is retention of electrolyte‑free water, expansion of total body water that is small enough to maintain an apparently euvolemic state on clinical exam, and a fall in serum sodium concentration (dilutional hyponatraemia). Laboratory hallmarks are hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality often >300 mOsm/kg) and elevated urine sodium (>30 mmol/L) in the absence of adrenal, thyroid or renal failure and in the absence of diuretic use. Clinically, the spectrum of manifestations ranges from subtle neurocognitive symptoms (fatigue, headache, mild confusion, gait disturbance) to severe cerebral oedema with seizures, obtundation and coma when hyponatraemia develops rapidly or becomes profound. Less commonly appreciated are downstream complications such as rhabdomyolysis, which can occur in the setting of severe hyponatraemia and was manifest in the patient described by an elevated CK; persistent hiccups are another sometimes‑reported and under‑recognized manifestation that can reflect diaphragmatic irritability in the setting of electrolyte disturbance.

Several features of BD and its treatment can contribute to SIADH. Central nervous system inflammation—even when radiologically occult—can directly stimulate ADH release via cytokine signalling as above. Additionally, certain medications used in BD (eg, cyclophosphamide) have been implicated in SIADH through direct effects on renal concentrating mechanisms or by enhancing ADH secretion. In the present case no offending medication was identified and the timing favoured SIADH as a consequence of the inflammatory CNS process.

Therapeutic implications follow from understanding these mechanisms. Acute neuro‑BD is typically treated with high‑dose corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppression; these agents reduce meningeal and parenchymal inflammation and would be expected to lower cytokine levels and hence ADH drive. Colchicine, which was used successfully in this patient as monotherapy, has a mechanistic rationale: it inhibits microtubule polymerisation in neutrophils, reduces neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation, and interferes with inflammasome activation and downstream production of IL‑1β and IL‑6. By attenuating the upstream inflammatory cascade, colchicine could plausibly reduce intrathecal cytokine concentrations and secondarily lower ADH stimulation. However, evidence that colchicine alone can consistently control acute parenchymal neuro‑BD is limited; most series support corticosteroids and steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants for moderate or severe CNS disease. The favorable response in this patient may reflect a relatively mild, self‑limited inflammatory episode, a prompt anti‑inflammatory effect of colchicine, or both. It is also worth noting practical limitations in validating the inflammation→IL‑6→ADH pathway in individual patients: AVP/ADH assays are technically challenging and pulse‑dependent, and copeptin (the stable C‑terminal portion of the vasopressin precursor) is now frequently used as a surrogate; similarly, CSF cytokine assays are not routinely available and lack standardisation.

In summary, BD sets the stage for neuro‑involvement by creating a neutrophil‑ and cytokine‑rich inflammatory environment that preferentially targets small vessels and perivascular regions in the CNS. When this inflammatory activity reaches hypothalamic or periventricular regulatory centres—or when inflammatory mediators diffuse into the CSF—it can provoke inappropriate ADH release, leading to the laboratory and clinical picture of SIADH. Recognising this pathophysiologic link is important because hyponatraemia may be a presenting or complicating feature of neuro‑BD and requires targeted management (fluid restriction, cautious use of hypertonic saline when indicated, and treatment of the underlying inflammation) to prevent neurologic deterioration and other complications.
