### [Differential Diagnosis] Differentiate SIADH from cerebral salt wasting in the setting of CNS inflammation: what are the key differences in volume status, urine sodium/excretion patterns, and management implications?

When a patient with CNS inflammation develops hyponatraemia you must distinguish two fundamentally different pathophysiologies because the treatments are opposite.

SIADH is a water‑retention problem caused by inappropriate ADH release from the CNS (or other triggers). Patients are clinically euvolemic (no orthostatic hypotension, no tachycardia or decreased skin turgor, no peripheral edema), serum osmolality is low, urine osmolality is inappropriately high (>100–300–900 mOsm/kg depending on severity), and urine sodium is typically >30–40 mmol/L because the kidney is excreting sodium normally while retaining free water. Biochemical clues include low serum uric acid and low BUN from dilution/renal urate wasting. Importantly, giving isotonic saline usually fails to raise serum sodium (and can worsen it) because the infused sodium is excreted while the retained water remains; the correct initial management is fluid restriction, cautious use of hypertonic saline for severe or symptomatic hyponatraemia, and later consideration of salt tablets, loop diuretics or vasopressin‑receptor antagonists if needed.

Cerebral salt wasting (CSW), by contrast, is a primary renal loss of sodium driven by natriuretic mechanisms in the setting of CNS disease. The key distinguishing feature is true intravascular volume depletion: patients have signs of hypovolaemia (orthostasis, tachycardia, reduced JVP, weight loss, sometimes increased hematocrit), urine sodium is high (>40 mmol/L) and urine output may be large, and serum osmolality is low because sodium and water are both lost with a net negative sodium balance. Biochemically you may see a higher BUN and hemoconcentration reflecting volume loss. In CSW, isotonic (or hypertonic) saline and salt replacement correct both the hypovolaemia and the hyponatraemia; fludrocortisone can be helpful to promote sodium retention.

Because both disorders can present with low serum sodium and high urine sodium in CNS disease, clinical assessment of volume status and the patient’s response to isotonic saline are the decisive tests. If the patient is euvolemic, has low uric acid and the hyponatraemia improves with fluid restriction (as in this Behçet’s neuro‑inflammation case), SIADH is most likely. If the patient is hypovolaemic with orthostatic changes and hyponatraemia improves promptly with volume repletion, CSW is likely and salt/volume repletion is required.

Finally, remember practical pitfalls: clinical volume assessment can be difficult in elderly or edematous patients, and early in illness both processes can coexist or be misread. Use serial weights, orthostatic vitals, urine output trends, serum uric acid/BUN/hematocrit, and an observed trial of isotonic saline when safe to help distinguish them. In the case you read, the patient was clinically euvolemic, had concentrated urine with high urine sodium, low serum osmolality and low uric acid, and his sodium normalized with fluid restriction and treatment of the underlying neuro‑Behçet’s inflammation — a classic pattern for SIADH rather than CSW.
