### [Morning Report] Define the diagnostic criteria for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), and identify the specific serum and urine osmolality and urine sodium thresholds that support the diagnosis.

Think of SIADH as a diagnosis of inappropriate, persistent ADH activity in a patient with hypotonic hyponatraemia after you exclude hypovolaemia, adrenal/thyroid failure and diuretic use. The diagnostic reasoning is: is the plasma hypotonic, is the kidney concentrating urine when it should be dilute, is the patient clinically euvolemic, and are other endocrine causes excluded?

Formal diagnostic criteria (clinical teaching version)
- Hypotonic hyponatraemia: serum sodium <135 mmol/L with low serum osmolality (classically <275 mOsm/kg).  
- Inappropriately concentrated urine for the hypo‑osmolar state: urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg (and typically substantially elevated — often >300 mOsm/kg) rather than maximally dilute.  
- Urinary sodium consistent with ongoing natriuresis: urine Na generally >30 mmol/L (often >40 mmol/L) when the patient is on a normal salt intake.  
- Clinical euvolaemia on examination (no signs of volume depletion or overload).  
- Normal adrenal and thyroid function (exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency).  
- No recent use of diuretics or other drugs that cause hyponatraemia, and no renal failure that would impair free‑water excretion.  
- Supporting finding: measurable or elevated ADH (vasopressin) in the setting of hypo‑osmolarity is “inappropriate,” although ADH assays are not required for routine diagnosis.

Apply this to the case: the patient had serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg (low), urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg (markedly inappropriately high), urine sodium 88 mmol/L (high), clinical euvolemia, and normal TSH/cortisol — all satisfying the SIADH criteria. Management follows from the diagnosis (fluid restriction as first line, avoid rapid correction).
