### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with hypo-osmolar hyponatremia and euvolemia, which tests are essential to rule out endocrine mimics of SIADH (e.g., adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism), and why?

When you see hypotonic hyponatraemia in an apparently euvolemic patient and are considering SIADH, the first essential step is to exclude endocrine causes that can produce the same biochemical picture — primarily adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism — because treating these conditions (especially giving glucocorticoids) is urgent and because true SIADH should be a diagnosis of exclusion.

Practically, obtain a morning serum cortisol and simultaneous serum ACTH, plus thyroid function tests (TSH and free T4). A random early‑morning cortisol >18 µg/dL (≈500 nmol/L) makes adrenal insufficiency unlikely and supports SIADH; a cortisol <3 µg/dL is strongly suggestive of adrenal failure and mandates prompt glucocorticoid replacement. Values between ~3 and 18 µg/dL are indeterminate and require dynamic testing (standard 250‑µg cosyntropin stimulation test) or measurement of nadir/serial cortisol/ACTH to distinguish primary from secondary adrenal insufficiency. Measuring ACTH is important because secondary (central) adrenal insufficiency can present with hyponatraemia without hyperkalemia and with a low or inappropriately normal ACTH.

For thyroid disease, measure TSH and free T4 together because primary hypothyroidism produces an elevated TSH with low free T4, but central hypothyroidism can have a low/normal TSH with low free T4. Overt hypothyroidism with low free T4 can impair free water clearance and mimic SIADH; therefore a normal free T4 excludes clinically meaningful hypothyroidism as the cause.

Remember to interpret these tests in the clinical context: check for glucocorticoid use that can suppress the HPA axis, severe illness that alters cortisol levels, and pituitary disease if central causes are suspected. Only after excluding adrenal insufficiency and clinically significant hypothyroidism should you confidently label the patient as having SIADH and proceed with SIADH‑directed management.
