Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Management] What is the recommended safe rate of sodium correction in chronic or indeterminate-duration hyponatremia, and what are the clinical indications for initiating hypertonic (3%) saline?

Safe correction of chronic or hyponatremia of indeterminate duration must balance reversing cerebral oedema (when present) against the risk of osmotic demyelination from overly rapid correction. The widely accepted targets are to increase serum sodium by no more than 8 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and no more than 18 mmol/L in the first 48 hours. In patients at particularly high risk for osmotic demyelination (malnourished, chronic alcoholism, advanced liver disease, severe hypokalemia), a more conservative limit of about 4–6 mmol/L in 24 hours is prudent. If overcorrection occurs, the antidote is to promptly re-lower sodium (give free water or D5W and/or desmopressin) to bring the rise back within recommended limits.

Hypertonic (3%) saline is indicated when hyponatremia is accompanied by clinically significant cerebral or cardiorespiratory compromise, or when symptoms are progressive despite conservative measures. Typical indications are severe or moderately severe neurological symptoms such as coma, obtundation, seizure, severe focal deficit, or signs of increased intracranial pressure. It is also appropriate in true acute hyponatremia (<48 hours) with severe symptoms because the brain has had little time to adapt. By contrast, asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic chronic hyponatremia is managed with cause‑directed therapy (fluid restriction, salt tablets, address ADH stimulus) rather than bolus hypertonic saline.

When hypertonic saline is given, the practical approach is to raise serum sodium just enough to relieve dangerous cerebral oedema—typically an initial target rise of 4–6 mmol/L (often achieved by 100 mL of 3% saline over 10 minutes, repeat once or twice as needed) — then slow further correction so the 24‑hour limit is not exceeded. Monitor serum sodium frequently (every 2–4 hours during active correction), track urine output and urine osmolality, and be prepared to give desmopressin and free water if sodium rises too quickly.

In the case presented, the patient was euvolemic with sodium 124 mmol/L from SIADH but without life‑threatening neurologic compromise; conservative management with fluid restriction and close monitoring was appropriate and avoided the risks of unnecessary rapid correction.
