### [Clinical Pearls] Why is direct measurement of plasma ADH not required for the diagnosis of SIADH, and how should an elevated ADH level be interpreted if obtained?

You do not need to directly measure plasma antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin) to make the diagnosis of SIADH because the diagnosis is a physiologic one based on the clinical and laboratory pattern of water handling, not on a single hormone level. In practice SIADH is diagnosed when there is hypotonic hyponatraemia with clinical euvolemia, concentrated urine (inappropriately high urine osmolality), natriuresis (inappropriately high urine sodium), and exclusion of adrenal, thyroid disease and recent diuretics or other clear causes. Those features demonstrate that free water excretion is impaired despite hypo‑osmolality — which is the essence of “inappropriate” ADH effect — without needing to measure the peptide itself.

There are several practical and biologic reasons ADH measurement is not required and is often unhelpful. ADH is unstable in blood, its assays are technically difficult and not standardized, and the hormone is secreted in pulses and is strongly affected by many non‑osmotic stimuli (nausea, pain, hypotension, stress, certain drugs, inflammation). Because of these issues, a single plasma ADH value has limited sensitivity and specificity for the clinical syndrome. Copeptin (the more stable C‑terminal fragment of the preprohormone) is a better laboratory surrogate in research settings but still adds little to routine, bedside diagnostic judgment.

If an ADH (or copeptin) level is obtained, interpret it only in the context of serum osmolality and the clinical situation. An absolute elevation can support the diagnosis when it is higher than expected for the concurrently measured plasma osmolality (i.e., relative inappropriateness), and a fall in ADH/copeptin with correction of hyponatraemia or treatment of the underlying cause lends causal support. However, an elevated ADH is not specific for SIADH because hypovolaemia, pain, nausea, stress, some malignancies, pulmonary disease, drugs and CNS inflammation (as in this Behçet’s case) also raise ADH. Conversely, a normal or low ADH does not exclude SIADH because of assay and timing issues.

Apply the principle: use the pattern of hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia plus concentrated urine, natriuresis and exclusion of other causes to make the diagnosis; if ADH/copeptin is measured, use it as supportive, not definitive, evidence and always interpret it against serum osmolality and the clinical context (e.g., CNS inflammation, nausea, medications). In the reported patient, the elevated ADH that fell with clinical improvement is consistent with neuroinflammation‑driven inappropriate ADH release, but it would not have been required to establish SIADH.
