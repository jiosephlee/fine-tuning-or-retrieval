Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Management] When, if ever, should vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) be used in SIADH secondary to neuroinflammation, and what are the main risks and monitoring requirements?

Short answer: Vaptans are rarely first‑line for SIADH from neuroinflammation. They should be reserved as a short‑term rescue therapy when (a) the patient has clinically significant or worsening neurologic symptoms from hyponatraemia despite conservative measures, or (b) fluid restriction and treatment of the underlying CNS inflammation have failed or are impractical and the patient remains at imminent risk of complications (seizure, profound encephalopathy, severe rhabdomyolysis). In most cases of neuro‑inflammatory SIADH — including the reported Behçet’s case — treating the underlying inflammation and careful fluid restriction with close monitoring is preferred.

Why: In SIADH the pathophysiology is inappropriate vasopressin activity and vaptans (oral tolvaptan, IV conivaptan) directly antagonize the V2 receptor to produce aquaresis and raise serum sodium. That makes them mechanistically attractive, but clinical experience and guidelines caution their routine use because the risks (particularly overly rapid correction) are substantial and because correcting the underlying CNS cause often normalizes ADH secretion.

Main risks and how to monitor
1) Over‑rapid correction → osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS). This is the principal danger. Target correction rates of serum Na should be conservative (aim ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours, certainly avoid >10–12 mmol/L in 24 h; many experts prefer ≤8). If overcorrection occurs, promptly re‑lower sodium with hypotonic fluids and/or desmopressin. Monitor serum sodium every 2–4 hours while initiating vaptan therapy until stable, then at least daily.

2) Excessive aquaresis, hypovolaemia and hypotension. Monitor urine output hourly initially, daily weights, orthostatic signs and intravascular volume. Adjust fluids if volume depletion develops.

3) Hypernatremia. Check sodium frequently and stop the drug if sodium approaches upper safe limit or rises too quickly.

4) Hepatotoxicity (tolvaptan). Tolvaptan has been associated with idiosyncratic liver injury with prolonged use. Avoid long courses; obtain baseline liver tests and monitor LFTs if therapy continues beyond a few days. Do not use prolonged (>30 days) therapy unless specialist oversight and monitoring are in place.

5) Drug interactions and contraindications. Tolvaptan is a CYP3A4 substrate; avoid strong inhibitors. Vaptans are contraindicated in hypovolaemic hyponatraemia and in end‑stage liver disease (conivaptan IV has additional vascular effects).

Practical approach in neuroinflammatory SIADH
Start with diagnosis confirmation (euvolemic hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia, high urine osmolality and urine Na), exclude hypovolaemia/other causes, and immediately institute targeted management of the neurologic disorder plus fluid restriction and frequent monitoring. Reserve vaptans for hospitalized patients when: severe symptoms persist despite initial measures, fluid restriction is ineffective/unfeasible, or ongoing hyponatraemia is causing complications and more rapid but controlled correction is needed. If used, give vaptan in the hospital, monitor serum Na q2–4 h for the first 24–48 h, watch urine output and volume status, check baseline LFTs and avoid prolonged outpatient use. Have a plan to treat overcorrection (desmopressin + hypotonic fluid).

Applied to the presented case: the patient improved rapidly with anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine) and fluid restriction; that is the preferred course. A vaptan would not have been indicated unless he had worsening neurologic compromise or failed conservative therapy — and if one had been started, it should have been short, inpatient, and closely monitored to avoid rapid correction and hepatic toxicity.
