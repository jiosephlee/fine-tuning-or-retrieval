### [Morning Report] In a patient with suspected neuro-Behçet’s but a normal initial brain MRI, what additional studies (e.g., MR venography, spinal MRI, repeat MRI, CSF IL-6) would you consider and why?

When you suspect neuro‑Behçet’s but the first brain MRI is normal, treat the imaging result as a snapshot rather than a definitive exclusion. Neuro‑BD commonly produces brainstem/hypothalamic/parenchymal lesions that can be subtle or absent early, and Behçet’s also confers a high risk of venous thrombosis — so plan investigations that (1) look for occult parenchymal disease in locations MRI may miss initially, (2) look for venous causes that mimic meningitis/headache and cause SIADH, and (3) document inflammatory activity in the CSF.

Specifically, obtain a contrast‑enhanced brain MRI with thin slices focused on the brainstem and hypothalamic region (and include diffusion and FLAIR sequences), and repeat it if clinical suspicion persists — commonly within 7–14 days or sooner if new focal signs develop — because lesions can evolve after presentation. Add MR venography at the same time to exclude cerebral venous thrombosis/dural sinus thrombosis, which is relatively common in BD, can present with headache and hyponatraemia and may be missed on routine non‑venous sequences.

If there are spinal symptoms (sensory level, radicular pain, bladder dysfunction) or if parenchymal brain imaging remains uninformative, obtain spinal MRI with contrast to look for myelitis — neuro‑BD classically involves brainstem and spinal cord as well as meninges. If the clinical picture is dominated by aseptic meningitis (as in this case), repeat lumbar puncture and broaden CSF testing: cell count, glucose/protein, microbiologic PCRs already done, cytology if malignancy remains a concern, and consider measuring CSF inflammatory markers such as IL‑6. CSF IL‑6 (or other cytokines) is not diagnostic but can demonstrate CNS inflammatory activity, may support a neuroinflammatory mechanism for SIADH, and can be useful for monitoring response to therapy in select cases — keeping in mind limited availability and lack of disease‑specific cutoffs.

If imaging and CSF remain indeterminate but suspicion is high, consider whole‑body FDG‑PET or targeted CT to exclude systemic malignancy or sarcoidosis (which can have MRI‑negative neurosarcoid early) and, when available and relevant, dedicated pituitary MRI if central endocrine causes for SIADH are suspected. Always integrate serial clinical findings — mucocutaneous lesions, HLA‑B51, CSF pleocytosis and response to therapy — because neuro‑BD is a clinical diagnosis and early imaging can be falsely normal.
