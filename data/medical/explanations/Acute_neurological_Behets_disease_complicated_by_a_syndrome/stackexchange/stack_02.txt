### [Clinical Pearls] List the two major neurologic phenotypes of neuro-Behçet’s disease (parenchymal vs non-parenchymal) and give one representative clinical/radiographic example of each.

Neuro‑Behçet’s has two distinct clinicopathologic patterns that determine presentation, imaging and management: the parenchymal form and the non‑parenchymal (vascular/meningeal) form.

The parenchymal phenotype reflects direct inflammatory involvement of brain tissue, most classically the brainstem, thalami and basal ganglia. Clinically this presents with subacute meningoencephalitis or focal brainstem signs — for example progressive brainstem syndrome with dysarthria, vertical gaze palsy, ataxia or hemiparesis. MRI typically shows T2/FLAIR hyperintense, often asymmetric lesions in the brainstem or deep hemispheric nuclei; these lesions are the hallmark of parenchymal neuro‑BD and are managed primarily with high‑dose corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppression.

The non‑parenchymal phenotype includes venous and meningeal complications rather than focal parenchymal inflammation. The classic representative example is cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST): a patient with severe headache, papilledema and raised intracranial pressure, with MR venography demonstrating thrombosis of the transverse or superior sagittal sinus. Management centers on anticoagulation together with treatment of the underlying inflammatory disease. Aseptic meningitis with normal brain parenchymal MRI also falls in the non‑parenchymal spectrum and can explain presentations like the case reported, where CSF pleocytosis and SIADH accompanied normal brain MRI.

Recognizing which phenotype predominates matters because it guides urgent therapy (immunosuppression for parenchymal disease; anticoagulation plus immunomodulation for CVST/non‑parenchymal disease) and has prognostic implications.
