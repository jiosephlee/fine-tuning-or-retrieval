Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic link between CNS inflammation in neuro-Behçet’s disease and SIADH, including the role of IL-6 and downstream effects on ADH release.

Direct answer
CNS inflammation in neuro‑Behçet’s disease can provoke SIADH through cytokine‑mediated stimulation of arginine vasopressin (AVP, ADH) release — most importantly via interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) produced within the inflamed CNS. IL‑6 (from activated monocytes/macrophages and microglia) acts on hypothalamic/osmoregulatory sites to increase AVP gene expression and secretion, lowering the osmotic threshold for release and producing the water retention and dilutional hyponatraemia that define SIADH.

Why this happens (pathophysiology)
- Source of IL‑6: Acute neuro‑BD is an inflammatory meningoencephalitic process. Aberrant monocyte activation in BD leads to spontaneous IL‑6 production; IL‑6 is also produced intrathecally by activated microglia/mononuclear cells during CNS inflammation. Although this patient’s CSF IL‑6 was not measured, the CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated CSF protein and marked systemic inflammatory markers (CRP 180 mg/L) are concordant with an IL‑6–driven inflammatory state described in neuro‑BD.
- How IL‑6 drives AVP release: IL‑6 can act at circumventricular organs (areas with an incomplete blood–brain barrier such as the organum vasculosum lamina terminalis) and directly on hypothalamic neurons (supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei). Through intracellular signaling (eg, JAK/STAT pathways), IL‑6 increases AVP gene transcription and neuronal excitability, and it can sensitize osmoreceptors so that AVP is secreted at lower plasma osmolality (non‑osmotic stimulation). Intrathecal IL‑6 therefore promotes inappropriate ADH secretion despite hypo‑osmolality.
- Downstream renal effects: Elevated AVP binds V2 receptors in the renal collecting duct, triggering aquaporin‑2 insertion and increased free water reabsorption → concentrated urine (high urine osmolality), natriuresis with continued urinary sodium excretion, expansion of total body water relative to solute and dilutional hyponatraemia.

Linking the mechanism to the case
- The patient had hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia (serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L) while clinically euvolemic — fulfilling SIADH criteria. Measured plasma ADH was elevated (22.4 pg/mL) and fell to 1.8 pg/mL as the clinical syndrome resolved, supporting an ADH‑mediated process.
- CNS inflammatory evidence—aseptic meningitis on CSF (lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein), systemic inflammation (high CRP), and clinical neuro symptoms—supports neuro‑BD as the inflammatory trigger. The temporal association of colchicine initiation, clinical improvement, normalization of sodium, and fall in ADH level favors a causal chain: neuroinflammation → cytokine (notably IL‑6) mediated ADH release → SIADH.
- Although direct IL‑6 measurements are lacking in this patient, the pathophysiologic model is consistent with published observations that CSF IL‑6 is elevated in neuro‑BD and that IL‑6 stimulates AVP release in neuroinflammatory states.

Practical diagnostic and management points (teaching pearls)
- Recognize the pattern: hypotonic hyponatraemia + inappropriately concentrated urine + high urine Na + euvolemia + normal thyroid/adrenal function = SIADH. In patients with neuro‑inflammatory symptoms, consider cytokine‑driven SIADH.
- Treat both problems: acute management of SIADH is guided by severity (fluid restriction for mild/moderate, hypertonic saline for severe symptomatic hyponatraemia). Definitive therapy requires treating the underlying CNS inflammation. In this case colchicine (which suppresses IL‑6 production among other effects) was temporally associated with resolution of neuro symptoms, fall in ADH and correction of sodium.
- Be vigilant for related complications (eg, hiccups, rhabdomyolysis) that may be manifestations of severe hyponatraemia in neuroinflammatory disease.

Take‑home summary
CNS inflammation in neuro‑Behçet’s can trigger SIADH via intrathecal and systemic IL‑6 production that stimulates hypothalamic AVP synthesis and release, lowering the osmotic set point and causing water retention with concentrated urine. Recognition depends on standard SIADH criteria and is important because controlling the inflammatory driver (as in this case) can normalize ADH levels and correct the hyponatraemia.
