Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Clinical Pearls] What neuroanatomical regions are most commonly affected on MRI in parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease, and why can imaging be normal in the early phase despite clinical CNS involvement?

Direct answer: In parenchymal neuro–Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) the MRI lesions most classically involve the brainstem (especially the pons and midbrain), deep grey nuclei (thalamus, basal ganglia) and deep subcortical white matter — lesions are typically T2/FLAIR hyperintense, may enhance, and can be multiple or confluent. The hypothalamus and periventricular region can also be involved (and when they are, endocrine disturbances such as SIADH may follow), but hypothalamic lesions are less frequent than brainstem and deep‑structure lesions.

Why MRI can be normal early despite clear CNS involvement:
- Early neuro‑BD often presents as aseptic meningoencephalitis or perivascular inflammation that is microscopic and predominantly cellular (perivascular neutrophilic/lymphocytic infiltrates and small‑vessel vasculitis). These changes can produce symptoms and CSF abnormalities (pleocytosis, elevated protein) without producing a focal parenchymal lesion large enough to alter MRI signal.
- Functional disturbance of neuroendocrine pathways (for example dysregulated ADH release from hypothalamic neurons) can occur from inflammatory cytokines (IL‑6 and others) or subtle inflammatory changes that do not produce a visible structural lesion on routine MRI sequences.
- Technical and timing issues: small or early inflammatory lesions may be below the spatial resolution of standard MRI sequences or may not yet have developed the edema/necrosis that causes T2/FLAIR signal change. Contrast enhancement and repeat imaging (including targeted thin‑slice pituitary/hypothalamic sequences, FLAIR, diffusion, and gadolinium‑enhanced T1) performed later often reveal evolving lesions that were initially occult.

Link to the case: this patient had aseptic meningitis on CSF but a normal brain MRI (including the hypothalamic region) early in the course — a well‑recognized scenario in acute neuro‑BD. His hyponatraemia and elevated ADH could reflect hypothalamic dysfunction from inflammatory cytokines or microscopic inflammation not visualized on MRI. Thus, in suspected neuro‑BD a normal early MRI does not exclude CNS involvement; clinical assessment and CSF studies guide diagnosis, and repeat/dedicated imaging is indicated if symptoms persist or evolve.
