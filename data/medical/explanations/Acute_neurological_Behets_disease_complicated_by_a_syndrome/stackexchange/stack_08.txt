### [Pathophysiology] Explain how neuroinflammation—particularly interleukin-6 (IL-6) signaling—can promote non-osmotic release of antidiuretic hormone and lead to SIADH.

In acute CNS inflammation the usual osmotic control of arginine vasopressin (AVP, or ADH) secretion can be overridden by inflammatory signalling — and interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is a key mediator of that process. The mechanistic chain is straightforward and explains the findings in this case.

When the meninges or brain parenchyma are inflamed, activated immune cells in blood and CSF release cytokines (notably IL‑6). IL‑6 reaches the hypothalamus and posterior pituitary by several routes: through leaky regions of the blood–brain barrier, across circumventricular organs, and via afferent neural pathways (e.g., vagal and other brainstem inputs triggered by meningeal irritation). IL‑6 acts on hypothalamic neurons in two complementary ways that produce non‑osmotic AVP release. First, it directly stimulates AVP‑producing magnocellular neurons in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, increasing AVP gene expression and exocytosis independent of plasma osmolality. Second, IL‑6 sensitises and/or activates the central integrative pathways that set the AVP secretory “set point” (inflammation‑driven prostaglandin and nitric oxide signalling are intermediary messengers), so that AVP is secreted at lower serum osmolality and in response to non‑osmotic stimuli such as pain, nausea, fever or hypotension. The net effect is persistent, inappropriate AVP release with concentrated urine, natriuresis, and hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia — the biochemical signature of SIADH.

There are also peripheral and renal effects that amplify hyponatraemia. IL‑6–mediated inflammation increases renal sensitivity to AVP and can up‑regulate aquaporin channels in the collecting duct, reducing free‑water clearance for any given AVP concentration. Finally, systemic inflammation and cytokine‑induced sympathetic activation or baroreceptor reflexes can provide additional non‑osmotic stimuli to AVP secretion.

Clinically this explains the case: aseptic meningeal inflammation produced elevated ADH (measured), very concentrated urine with high urine sodium, and euvolemic hyponatraemia despite a normal-looking MRI. Resolution of inflammation (and likely falling IL‑6 activity) with anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine in this patient, which suppresses cytokine production including IL‑6) led to a fall in AVP and correction of sodium without the need for AVP antagonists. Measuring CSF or serum IL‑6 would have strengthened causality, but is not required to apply the pathophysiologic principle.

Takeaway for practice: any CNS inflammatory process can drive non‑osmotic AVP release via cytokine (especially IL‑6) pathways; when you see hyponatraemia with concentrated urine and clinical euvolemia in a patient with meningoencephalitic signs, treat the underlying inflammation and restrict free water while avoiding rapid correction of sodium.
