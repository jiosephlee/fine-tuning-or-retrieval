### [Clinical Pearls] What are the limitations of serum ACE and lysozyme as screening tests for neurosarcoidosis, and how should normal values be interpreted in a patient with CNS inflammation?

Serum ACE and lysozyme are commonly ordered as “screening” blood tests when sarcoidosis is on the differential, but their performance characteristics and biology impose important limitations — especially when the question is isolated CNS inflammation.

First, neither test is sensitive enough to rule out neurosarcoidosis. ACE is produced by epithelioid cells in granulomas, so serum ACE rises when there is a substantial systemic granulomatous burden. In patients with disease confined to the CNS or with an early/limited inflammatory focus, serum ACE is often normal. Lysozyme behaves similarly: it can be elevated with granulomatous inflammation but is neither sensitive nor specific for intracranial disease. In practice a normal serum ACE or lysozyme is common in neurosarcoidosis and therefore does not exclude it.

Second, both tests lack specificity. Elevations occur with many other inflammatory states, hepatic disease, some infections and certain malignancies; laboratory assays vary between centers; and medications (for example systemic corticosteroids) and patient factors (age, ACE polymorphisms) can alter results. Conversely, ACE inhibitors and some lab methods can suppress measured ACE, producing false negatives. CSF ACE or lysozyme measurements have been proposed, but they also have limited sensitivity and are not definitive.

What this means for clinical interpretation: treat serum ACE and lysozyme as adjuncts that can increase or decrease your suspicion when the pre-test probability is moderate to high, but do not use a normal value to exclude neurosarcoidosis in a patient with CNS inflammation. In a patient like the one in this case — meningitic CSF, normal brain MRI, strong alternative clinical clues (mucocutaneous Behçet’s findings) and no systemic sarcoid features — normal serum ACE and lysozyme simply lower the likelihood of systemic sarcoidosis but do not reliably rule out isolated neurosarcoid. The correct approach is to integrate these labs with imaging, CSF studies, targeted searches for systemic sarcoidosis (chest imaging, PET if indicated), and, when necessary and feasible, tissue diagnosis from the most accessible site. In short: use ACE/lysozyme to support a diagnosis when positive, but never to exclude neurosarcoidosis when they are normal.
