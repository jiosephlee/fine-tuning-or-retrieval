# Right-Sided Hydropneumothorax in a Child? Think Retroperitoneum-to-Hiatus Fistula

What looks like a straightforward right-sided hydropneumothorax or recurrent empyema in a child can sometimes have a surprising abdominal origin: a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) tracking from the retroperitoneum through a diaphragmatic hiatus into the chest. The case above is a classic “pulmonary mask” — repeated large, dark red pleural effusions and temporary clinical improvement after drainage — but the pathology was a pancreatic duct disruption after blunt abdominal trauma. The chest X‑ray and even CT findings (hydropneumothorax, air–fluid levels, loculations) can easily steer you toward empyema and thoracic-only management. That’s the trap.

Why the right side (and sometimes bilaterality)?
- Pancreatic fluid that leaks posteriorly into the retroperitoneum follows the path of least resistance. It can travel superiorly and enter the mediastinum via the aortic or oesophageal hiatus. The path taken and the side of least resistance frequently determine which pleural space is involved.
- The right pleural space is commonly affected because pancreatic collections that track posteriorly and cross the midline may preferentially enter the right posterior mediastinum or right paraoesophageal channel before breaching the pleura.
- Bilateral communication is possible — a dominant right fistula with a narrow left communication (as in this child) explains asymmetric but sometimes bilateral effusions.

Why hydropneumothorax or air–fluid levels can mislead
- A retroperitoneal-to-hiatus fistula can carry not only enzyme-rich fluid but also gas from the gut or from secondary infection in a pancreatic collection/pseudocyst, producing hydropneumothorax or air–fluid levels that look like empyema.
- Loculations and consolidation on chest imaging add to the mimicry of complicated parapneumonic effusion or empyema.

What should shift your thinking from empyema → PPF?
- Recurrent large unilateral pleural effusions that rapidly re-accumulate after drainage, especially without a convincing pulmonary source.
- Pleural fluid that is markedly amylase- and lipase-rich. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L should prompt pancreatic evaluation (and amylase isoenzyme typing if needed).
- History elements that may be subtle: recent blunt abdominal trauma (even an apparently minor bike-handle injury), pancreatitis symptoms (abdominal pain, vomiting) that may be absent or overlooked in children, or features suggesting chronic/recurrent pancreatitis (family history, steatorrhea).
- Pleural fluid appearance: hemorrhagic or dark red fluid is common in PPF and can be mistaken for hemothorax or malignant effusion.

Imaging strategy — what helps and what doesn’t
- Chest X‑ray: shows large effusion, sometimes air–fluid level or hydropneumothorax. Useful to recognize a recurring collection but nonspecific.
- Chest CT: great for defining pleural collections, consolidation, and tracking planes; it often shows a retroperitoneal collection or a tract but only depicts the fistulous tract in ~33–47% of cases.
- MRCP: best noninvasive test for ductal anatomy and fistula visualization (sensitivity ~70–80%). In kids with suspected PPF, MRCP is high yield.
- ERCP: diagnostic and therapeutic (stent); visualizes fistula directly in many cases and allows transpapillary stenting to reduce ductal pressure.
- Always correlate imaging with pleural fluid biochemistry — a high pleural amylase is the biochemical red flag.

Practical chest-tube and acute-care pearls
- Don’t reflexively stop the workup after placing a drain. A chest tube buys respiratory relief but is often only a temporizing measure in PPF.
- Send pleural fluid for routine studies AND amylase/lipase (and consider amylase isoenzymes if available), cytology, Gram stain/culture, ADA and TB testing if indicated — but interpret ADA in context (mildly raised ADA can mislead).
- If you see dark red, recurrent pleural fluid, add pleural amylase to your STAT panel.
- Drain large effusions to relieve respiratory compromise. But remember: ongoing high output should prompt immediate abdominal imaging (CT abdomen/MRCP) and GI/pancreatic consultation rather than repeated thoracic-only procedures.
- Early multidisciplinary coordination (pediatrics/pediatric surgery/gastroenterology/interventional endoscopy/radiology) improves outcomes.

Therapeutic approach (practical roadmap)
- Initial conservative management (especially when no major ductal disruption identified): NPO, bowel rest, TPN, somatostatin analogue (octreotide), pancreatic enzyme replacement, and pleural drainage. In children this approach can succeed but has a modest reported success rate.
- If conservative measures fail within 2–3 weeks or ductal anatomy shows a targetable lesion: ERCP with transpapillary stenting and/or sphincterotomy — less invasive and effective at reducing intraductal pressure and promoting fistula closure.
- If endoscopic therapy is not feasible or fails (distal obstruction, complex ductal injury, or persistent leakage): surgical options (distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy, cystenteric drainage) offer high success rates.
- In pediatric patients, trauma and genetic causes (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) are important etiologies — consider genetic testing when pancreatitis is recurrent.

Key clinical pearls and take-home points
- Recurrent unilateral (often right-sided) large pleural effusion in a child — always add pleural amylase/lipase to the initial fluid panel.
- Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is a powerful clue for PPF; consider amylase isoenzyme typing to distinguish pancreatic from salivary sources.
- Hydropneumothorax and air–fluid levels do not equal empyema — they can come from retroperitoneal pancreatic collections tracking through a hiatus.
- Chest tube is for symptom control and diagnosis — don’t let it stop you from searching for an abdominal source.
- MRCP is your noninvasive imaging workhorse for suspected PPF; ERCP is both diagnostic and often therapeutic.
- In children, think trauma and genetic causes rather than alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis.

Bottom line: in kids with recurrent, large, bloody or malodorous pleural effusions — especially right-sided or with hydropneumothorax — expand your differential beyond pulmonary disease. Send pleural amylase/lipase early, get MRCP if suspicion exists, and use chest drainage as a bridge while you hunt down the pancreatic source. Early recognition keeps kids off unnecessary pulmonary treatments (and TB meds), shortens hospital stays, and directs life‑changing interventions like endoscopic stenting or surgery when needed.



## The Recurrent Effusion That Wasn’t TB: Diagnostic Bias in Endemic Settings

What makes this case stick in your head? A late-childhood boy with large, recurrent, dark‑red right pleural effusions and minimal systemic illness—repeated thoracenteses gave symptomatic relief, pleural ADA was modestly raised, there was household TB contact, and empiric anti‑TB therapy was started. Only after persistence and revisiting the history did the team uncover a bicycle handlebar abdominal injury and, ultimately, pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) confirmed on MRCP with strikingly elevated pleural amylase/lipase. The diagnosis changed management completely: nil per os, TPN, octreotide, chest drainage —and clinical cure.

Why this matters
- In TB‑endemic places it’s easy to anchor on TB when you see an exudative pleural effusion plus an epidemiologic link. That anchoring can delay diagnosis of non‑pulmonary causes that present with a “pulmonary mask.” PPF is rare in children but presents classically with recurrent, massive, often haemorrhagic effusions and very high pleural amylase. Missing it leads to unnecessary TB treatment, repeated thoracenteses, prolonged morbidity and potential complications.
- This case is a great reminder to marry pattern recognition with a simple, pragmatic diagnostic checklist before committing to long courses of anti‑tubercular therapy.

A quick cognitive debrief: anchoring + availability
- Anchoring: initial focus on TB because of family contact and a mildly raised ADA—early tests influenced downstream thinking.
- Availability: in endemic areas TB is a common cause of pleural effusion, so it comes to mind first—even when features are atypical.
- Solution: pause, reframe the problem when the presentation is atypical (large, recurrent, haemorrhagic, afebrile), broaden the differential, and order a focused panel of tests to rule in/out alternative etiologies before starting long therapy.

When to suspect a non‑tuberculous cause (think pancreatopleural fistula or other alternatives)
- Large or massive pleural effusion, especially unilateral and recurrent after drainage
- Dark red or haemorrhagic/straw‑coloured high‑volume fluid
- Relatively few systemic symptoms (no prolonged fever, no weight loss)
- Recent abdominal trauma or known pancreatitis (but absence of abdominal pain does not exclude pancreatic source)
- Rapid reaccumulation after thoracentesis

Rational test order for a large, recurrent pleural effusion before committing to empiric TB therapy
- Pleural fluid cytology (malignancy) and routine biochemistry (protein, LDH)
- Pleural fluid cell count with differential (neutrophil vs lymphocyte predominance)
- Pleural fluid pH and glucose (low pH/glucose suggest empyema/rupture)
- Pleural fluid amylase and lipase — essential if effusion is large, haemorrhagic, or recurrent
- Gram stain and culture, plus aerobic/anaerobic cultures if purulent or febrile
- GeneXpert (or other rapid MTB test) on pleural fluid — high specificity though sensitivity is limited
- ADA — interpret with caution (moderately raised ADA can be seen in other conditions)
- Triglycerides (if milky effusion) for chylothorax
- Consider CT chest/abdomen or MRCP if pleural fluid amylase or history suggests pancreatic source
- Consider ERCP if imaging shows ductal disruption—diagnostic and therapeutic

A practical checklist to avoid premature anti‑TB therapy in pleural effusion
- Is the effusion large, recurrent, or rapidly reaccumulating after drainage? If yes → broaden workup.
- Is the fluid bloody/haemorrhagic or unusually high volume? If yes → add pleural amylase/lipase and cytology.
- Are systemic TB features absent (no prolonged fever, weight loss, night sweats)? If absent and presentation atypical → pause before TB Rx.
- Is there a history of abdominal trauma, pancreatitis, alcohol, or hereditary pancreatitis risk factors? Ask specifically.
- Have you sent GeneXpert on pleural fluid (and sputum if possible) before starting therapy? If not, consider waiting for rapid tests unless clinical instability mandates empiric Rx.
- Is ADA only modestly elevated (borderline)? Remember false positives; correlate with cell differential and other tests.
- Has cross‑sectional imaging (CT/MRCP) been considered to look for pancreatic leaks, pseudocysts, malignancy, or diaphragmatic defects? If suspicion for non‑pulmonary source exists, get imaging early.
- If pleural fluid amylase >1000 IU/L or lipase markedly elevated → prioritize pancreatic investigations (MRCP/CT) and involve gastroenterology/pediatric surgery.

Clinical pearls
- Pleural fluid amylase >1,000 IU/L is highly suggestive of pancreatic origin (PPF) but other causes (eg, oesophageal rupture, some malignancies) can elevate amylase—amylase isoenzyme typing (pancreatic vs salivary) helps when needed.
- MRCP has good sensitivity (70–80%) to visualize fistulae and ductal anatomy; CT is useful for collections/pseudocysts but less sensitive for the fistula itself. ERCP is both diagnostic and therapeutic.
- Conservative management (NPO, TPN, somatostatin analogues like octreotide, chest drainage) is reasonable first line in children without major ductal disruption; add ERCP stenting or surgery when conservative therapy fails or anatomy requires intervention.
- A mildly raised ADA is not a gatekeeper for TB therapy—interpret in clinical context.

Take‑home messages
- Don’t let endemic disease prevalence blind you: anchoring on TB can delay diagnosis of treatable non‑pulmonary causes.
- In large, recurrent, haemorrhagic pleural effusions—always send pleural amylase and lipase early.
- Use a short, focused checklist and targeted imaging (MRCP/CT) before starting prolonged empiric anti‑TB therapy, unless the patient is clinically unstable.
- Remember: PPF may present with little or no abdominal pain in children; ask about trauma and subtle abdominal history repeatedly.

This boy’s story teaches a simple lesson: pause for atypical features, order a few high‑yield pleural tests (amylase/lipase, cytology, GeneXpert), and think anatomically — sometimes the lung is merely showing the fistula’s destination, not its origin.
