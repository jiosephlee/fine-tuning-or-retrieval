# The Pulmonary Mask: When Recurrent ‘Hemorrhagic’ Effusions Are Pancreatic

We see a lot of pleural effusions in the ED and wards. Most get worked up for infection, malignancy, TB, or heart failure — not the pancreas. That’s exactly why pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is dangerous: it wears a “pulmonary mask.” The case here — a late-childhood boy with recurrent, dark red, right-sided effusions who finally disclosed a bicycle-handlebar injury — is classic for how a pancreatic leak can present as a respiratory problem and be repeatedly misdirected into pulmonary pathways (including empiric TB treatment, multiple thoracenteses and an intercostal drain).

Why this case is interesting
- The child’s dominant complaints were chest pain and respiratory distress; abdominal signs were minimal. That’s common: most pediatric PPFs present with thoracic symptoms, not abdominal pain.
- Pleural fluid was dark red and large-volume, repeatedly totaling around a litre per tap — a pattern that should make you think beyond empyema and TB.
- Simple, high-yield test (pleural fluid amylase) and targeted imaging (MRCP) clinched the diagnosis and averted further inappropriate pulmonary workup. Conservative pancreatic-directed care (NPO, TPN, octreotide, drainage) resolved the fistula.

Pathophysiology in one line
- A disrupted pancreatic duct or ruptured pseudocyst tracks retroperitoneally through hiatuses or erodes the diaphragm, allowing enzyme-rich fluid to enter the mediastinum/pleural space and create an internal pancreatic-pleural fistula.

How PPF masquerades as a lung problem
- Large unilateral pleural effusion with respiratory compromise dominates the clinical picture.
- Abdominal pain is often absent or mild in children.
- Recurrent rapid re-accumulation after drainage is common because the pancreatic source keeps leaking.
- If trauma (handlebar injury) or pancreatitis history is not elicited, attention stays on thoracic causes.

Red flags that should trigger a search for PPF
- Recurrent, large-volume unilateral pleural effusions despite initial drainage.
- Pleural fluid that is serosanguinous/dark red (not purulent) and re-accumulates rapidly.
- Pleural fluid analysis showing very high amylase (commonly >1000 U/L).
- Recent blunt abdominal trauma (especially handlebar injuries) or known pancreatitis.
- Lack of systemic infection signs (no fever, sterile cultures) and negative TB workup.
- Pleural effusion with minimal pulmonary parenchymal disease on imaging.

Practical approach at the bedside
1. Re-ask the history: blunt abdominal trauma, prior pancreatitis, alcohol/family history of hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR in kids).
2. When you drain bloody or serosanguinous large effusions that re-accumulate, send pleural fluid for amylase and lipase immediately — this is cheap, quick, and often diagnostic.
3. Interpret amylase: pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 U/L is strongly suggestive of PPF (but remember other causes like esophageal perforation, malignancy, TB can raise amylase — check amylase isoforms if needed).
4. Imaging:
   - CT chest/abdomen: looks for collections, tracks, pseudocysts; identifies hydropneumothorax and retroperitoneal collections but may miss the fistula itself.
   - MRCP: best noninvasive study for duct anatomy and fistulous tracts (70–80% sensitivity).
   - ERCP: diagnostic and therapeutic — visualizes fistula and allows transpapillary stenting.
5. Management ladder:
   - Initial conservative trial (especially in children without major ductal disruption): NPO, TPN or enteral rest, somatostatin analogue (octreotide), pancreatic enzyme replacement as indicated, and adequate pleural drainage (thoracostomy/ICD).
   - If conservative measures fail (usually 2–3 weeks) or ductal anatomy shows obstruction/favorable target: ERCP with stent to reduce intraductal pressure.
   - If ERCP/stenting not feasible or ductal disease is irreparable: surgical options (distal pancreatectomy, pancreaticojejunostomy, cystenteric drainage).
6. Avoid knee-jerk empiric treatments for pulmonary causes (e.g., prolonged ATT or repeated thoracenteses) once PPF is reasonably suspected: they don’t fix the source and expose the patient to morbidity.

Pitfalls and differentiators
- TB: especially in endemic areas, ADA can be mildly raised — but very high pleural amylase argues against isolated TB. GeneXpert/culture and clinical context matter.
- Esophageal rupture (Boerhaave): can also elevate pleural amylase but usually has vomiting and mediastinitis features; clinical context and imaging help.
- Malignancy: can produce amylase-rich effusions (usually in adults) — cytology, imaging, and clinical picture help differentiate.
- Amylase isoform testing (pancreatic vs salivary) can be useful when the diagnosis is unclear.

Clinical pearls
- “Pleural fluid amylase” — send it early when you see recurrent, large, serosanguinous effusions. It’s a high-yield test that can change the whole diagnostic pathway.
- In children, consider trauma and hereditary pancreatitis as important causes; in adults alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis dominates.
- Conservative pancreatic therapy (NPO + octreotide + drainage) can close many fistulae, but have a low success rate in kids compared with adults — escalate to ERCP or surgery when indicated.
- MRCP is the noninvasive imaging study of choice for demonstrating the fistula and ductal anatomy before ERCP or operative planning.
- A “pulmonary mask” pleural effusion that keeps coming back deserves a pancreatic workup.

Key takeaways
- Recurrent, large-volume unilateral pleural effusions with dark red/serosanguinous fluid should prompt pleural fluid amylase/lipase testing.
- Don’t be misled by predominant respiratory symptoms — think retroperitoneal sources (pancreas), especially after abdominal trauma.
- MRCP and ERCP are complementary: MRCP for diagnosis and anatomy; ERCP for diagnosis and endoscopic therapy (stenting).
- Initial therapy is often conservative (NPO, TPN, octreotide, drainage); escalate to ERCP or surgery if the leak persists or ductal anatomy requires intervention.
- Early recognition prevents unnecessary pulmonary interventions and expedites definitive care.

Bottom line: when a child (or adult) has rapid, recurrent, “hemorrhagic” pleural effusions that don’t behave like empyema or TB, broaden your differential to include the pancreas — and send pleural amylase before you assume it’s purely pulmonary.
