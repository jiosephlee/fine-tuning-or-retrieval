### [Pathophysiology] Describe the mechanism by which octreotide aids in the closure of a pancreatopleural fistula.

Octreotide promotes closure of a pancreatopleural fistula by physiologically “switching off” the pancreas and thereby reducing the driving force that keeps the fistula open. Mechanistically, octreotide — a long‑acting somatostatin analogue — binds somatostatin receptors on pancreatic acinar cells and on the neuroendocrine control pathways, suppressing release of cholecystokinin and other stimulatory hormones and directly inhibiting exocrine pancreatic secretion. The result is a marked reduction in pancreatic enzyme and fluid output and a fall in intraductal pressure. With lower secretory flow through the disrupted duct or pseudocyst, ongoing leakage into the retroperitoneum and pleural space decreases, permitting the tract to thrombose and the inflammation to resolve. In clinical practice this effect is augmented by bowel rest and TPN (which remove enteral stimulation of the gland) and by pleural drainage (which prevents accumulation and infection), so octreotide is used as part of conservative management to convert a high‑flow leak into a low‑flow situation that can heal without endoscopic or surgical intervention. Evidence is largely from case series and observational reports in children and adults, so the intervention is best viewed as a temporizing measure for 2–3 weeks (the window in which many leaks heal) or as adjunct to endoscopic stenting if conservative measures fail.
