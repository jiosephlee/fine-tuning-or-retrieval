### [Clinical Pearls] Summarize the key red flags that should prompt consideration of pancreatopleural fistula in a pediatric patient with recurrent pleural effusions.

Think “pancreas behind the chest” whenever a child has recurrent, large pleural effusions that don’t behave like routine pulmonary disease. In practice the key red flags are a pattern of recurrent, often massive unilateral (but sometimes bilateral) pleural effusions that promptly reaccumulate after drainage; dark‑red or blood‑stained pleural fluid; pleural fluid that is exudative with very high amylase and/or lipase (classically pleural amylase ≥1,000 U/L and markedly elevated lipase); a history of pancreatitis, unexplained abdominal pain, or recent blunt abdominal trauma (even minor, e.g., bicycle handlebar injury); paucity of systemic or pulmonary infectious signs despite large effusions and negative TB/microbiology; and chest or cross‑sectional imaging that suggests a retroperitoneal/pancreatic collection tracking superiorly through a hiatus or shows ductal abnormality or pseudocyst. These findings together point away from primary thoracic causes and toward a pancreatopleural fistula.

When you see these red flags the next steps are biochemical confirmation (pleural and serum amylase/lipase and, if available, amylase isoenzyme typing), then cross‑sectional imaging with MRCP (best noninvasive test to define ductal anatomy and fistula) and CT for anatomic detail; ERCP is used both diagnostically and therapeutically. Early recognition matters because management shifts from empiric pulmonary therapies to bowel rest, drainage, somatostatin analogue and nutritional support, with endoscopic stenting or surgery reserved when conservative measures fail.
