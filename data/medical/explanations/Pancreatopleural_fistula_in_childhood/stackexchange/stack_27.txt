### [Pathophysiology] Which two diaphragmatic hiatuses most commonly serve as routes for pancreatic fluid to reach the thoracic cavity, and how might this influence the side of the effusion?

The two diaphragmatic hiatuses most commonly implicated are the aortic and the esophageal hiatus. A posterior pancreatic duct disruption or leaking pseudocyst in the retroperitoneum will follow the path of least resistance upward into the posterior mediastinum and enter the chest through one of those natural openings.

How that determines the side of the effusion is a matter of anatomy and the route the fluid takes. The esophageal hiatus lies a little left of midline and communicates readily with left para‑esophageal and mediastinal spaces, so leaks that track through it (and leaks arising from the pancreatic tail, which is left‑sided) more commonly produce left pleural effusions. The aortic hiatus is more midline and central; collections that ascend via the aortic space can spread bilaterally or produce right‑sided, left‑sided, or midline mediastinal collections depending on local fascial planes and the “path of least resistance.” Thus while left‑sided effusions are classically described, right‑sided or dominant right pleural effusions (as in this child) occur when the fluid tracks along right para‑esophageal or retroesophageal planes, through an enlarged/right‑predominant hiatus, or when the pancreatic injury is located more medially or to the right. In short: esophageal hiatus → favors left effusion; aortic (or central retroperitoneal) route → variable, often bilateral or right‑predominant depending on local anatomy and pressure gradients.
