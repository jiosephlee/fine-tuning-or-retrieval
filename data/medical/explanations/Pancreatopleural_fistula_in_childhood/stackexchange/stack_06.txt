### [Differential Diagnosis] Name at least four non-pancreatic causes of amylase-rich pleural effusion and one distinguishing clinical or laboratory feature for each.

Think of pleural fluid amylase as coming from two different sources — pancreatic-type amylase (suggests pancreatic leak) and salivary-type amylase (suggests oesophageal or salivary tract sources). Non‑pancreatic causes to remember, with a distinguishing clinical or lab feature for each:

1) Boerhaave’s syndrome (spontaneous oesophageal rupture). Classically preceded by severe retching/vomiting and chest pain; chest imaging often shows mediastinal emphysema or pleural air and the pleural fluid is foul, very acidic (low pH), and contains food debris — amylase is high and of salivary isoenzyme type.

2) Oesophageal perforation from other causes (instrumentation, malignancy). History of recent endoscopy, caustic ingestion or progressive dysphagia/weight loss; imaging may show localized mediastinal fluid/air and pleural fluid may have high salivary‑type amylase with positive Gram stain/culture if contaminated.

3) Malignancy (especially lung, oesophageal, or metastatic tumours that produce amylase). Look for systemic signs (weight loss, cachexia), suspicious radiographic mass, and positive pleural fluid cytology; the amylase is often salivary isoenzyme and cytology may demonstrate malignant cells.

4) Salivary gland injury or infection (parotitis, ductal injury after trauma or surgery). Relevant history of head/neck trauma or recent surgery, local salivary gland swelling/pain, and pleural fluid amylase that is predominantly the salivary isoenzyme.

5) Empyema or complicated parapneumonic effusion. The pleural fluid is frankly purulent, strongly neutrophil predominant, low pH and very low glucose; modestly elevated amylase can occur from swallowed/contaminating saliva or local cellular release, but the clinical picture and positive culture distinguish it from pancreatic leak.

In practice, enzyme isoform testing (pancreatic vs salivary amylase), the clinical history (vomiting, endoscopy, head/neck surgery, trauma), pleural fluid pH, glucose and cytology/culture, and chest/abdomen imaging help separate pancreatic pleural effusion from these non‑pancreatic causes.
