Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

### [Morning Report] In the context of traumatic pancreatic injury, what ductal features on MRCP/ERCP predict failure of conservative therapy and favor endoscopic or surgical intervention?

The key question is whether the pancreatic duct is essentially intact and amenable to transpapillary decompression, or is structurally interrupted so that conservative measures (bowel rest, octreotide, drainage) cannot stop ongoing leakage. On cross‑sectional and endoscopic pancreatograms, the features that predict failure of conservative therapy and push you toward endoscopic or surgical therapy are those that indicate a major ductal disruption, obstruction, or a disconnected segment.

Specifically, findings that favor intervention are: clear ductal discontinuity or complete transection (a gap between upstream and downstream duct segments or non‑opacification of the distal duct on ERCP — the “disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome”); persistent extravasation of contrast from the main pancreatic duct on ERCP; a large‑caliber leak or pseudocyst that communicates with the duct; upstream ductal dilation with a downstream obstructing stricture that prevents transpapillary drainage; and multifocal or complex ductal injury. Clinically important adjuncts are high and persistent fistula output (continued ICD or chest‑tube drainage despite 1–3 weeks of conservative care), evidence of infected collections, or progressive nutritional/respiratory compromise.

How that guides management: partial ductal injuries, focal strictures, or small leaks often respond to ERCP with transpapillary stent placement (to lower intraductal pressure and bridge the leak). Complete ductal transection or a disconnected tail usually cannot be cured by stenting and most often require surgical drainage/resection (distal pancreatectomy or internal drainage procedures) once the patient is optimized. In practice, attempt conservative therapy for 1–3 weeks when ductal anatomy is favorable; proceed to ERCP for both diagnosis and possible stenting if a transpapillary route is feasible; plan surgery sooner when imaging shows ductal discontinuity, large communicating pseudocyst/collection, or when endoscopic therapy is not possible or fails.
