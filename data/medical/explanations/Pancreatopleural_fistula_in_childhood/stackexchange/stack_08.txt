Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

### [Clinical Pearls] Which genetic variants are most commonly implicated in pediatric chronic pancreatitis that can predispose to pancreatopleural fistula, and in what clinical scenarios should genetic testing be pursued?

Direct answer
- The common genetic contributors to pediatric chronic pancreatitis cited in the report are PRSS1 and SPINK1, with CFTR and CTRC also important contributors. The report notes that PRSS1 mutations (classically R122H and the N29 variant) and SPINK1 mutations “feature prominently,” together accounting for up to ~30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cases.  

Why these genes matter (concise pathophysiology)
- PRSS1 (cationic trypsinogen): gain‑of‑function mutations (eg, R122H, N29 variants) make trypsinogen resistant to inactivation or prone to inappropriate activation → intrapancreatic autodigestion, recurrent inflammation, ductal injury and early chronic pancreatitis.  
- SPINK1: loss or reduction of the pancreatic trypsin inhibitor lowers the threshold for autodigestion once trypsin is activated.  
- CFTR: mutations impair ductal bicarbonate and fluid secretion, producing inspissated secretions, ductal obstruction and recurrent injury.  
- CTRC (chymotrypsin C): variants impair degradation of prematurely activated trypsinogen, increasing intrapancreatic active trypsin.

How this predisposes to pancreatopleural fistula (clinical link)
- Recurrent/chronic pancreatitis produces ductal strictures, upstream hypertension, pseudocysts or duct disruptions. Posterior ductal leaks can track retroperitoneally through the oesophageal or aortic hiatus into the mediastinum and pleural space and form a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF). Thus hereditary forms that produce early, recurrent or chronic ductal injury increase the lifetime risk of complications such as PPF.

When to pursue genetic testing (practical clinical scenarios)
- Genetic testing should be pursued in children and adolescents with:  
  - Recurrent acute pancreatitis (two or more well‑documented attacks), or  
  - Early‑onset or imaging‑proven chronic pancreatitis, or  
  - Idiopathic pancreatitis (no alcohol, trauma, metabolic, obstructive or infectious cause identified), or  
  - A family history of pancreatitis or early pancreatic disease, or  
  - Severe or unusual complications (eg, PPF, early pseudocyst formation) or when test results would affect long‑term counseling/management.  

Application to this case
- Although this child’s PPF followed blunt abdominal trauma, the report correctly emphasizes that genetic causes predominate in paediatric chronic pancreatitis. If the patient develops recurrent pancreatitis or chronic ductal changes, or if there is any family history, a targeted pancreatitis gene panel (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC at minimum) is appropriate for diagnosis, prognostic counseling and cascade family testing.

Practical pearl
- Order a pancreatitis genetic panel (including PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) in children with recurrent or early chronic pancreatitis or unexplained severe complications—because identifying a pathogenic variant explains the mechanism of ductal injury, informs surveillance and family counselling, and can alter timing of interventions.
