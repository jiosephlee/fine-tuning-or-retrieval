Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the anatomic pathway by which posterior pancreatic duct disruption can lead to a pleural effusion, and contrast it with the consequence of an anterior disruption.

When the pancreatic duct is breached posteriorly the disruption empties pancreatic juice into the retroperitoneal space rather than the peritoneal cavity. The retroperitoneum communicates cranially with the posterior mediastinum along pre‑existing fascial planes, and fluid will follow the path of least resistance through natural diaphragmatic openings — most commonly the aortic and oesophageal hiatuses — into the mediastinum and then through the pleura to collect in a pleural space. Enzyme‑rich pancreatic fluid is proteolytic and lipolytic, so it both irritates and digests tissues en route; that explains the often large, haemorrhagic, amylase‑rich pleural effusions and why a pseudocyst or a discrete tract (pancreatopleural fistula) may form tracking from the pancreas to the chest.

By contrast, an anterior duct disruption spills directly into the peritoneal cavity. There the same enzyme‑rich fluid produces pancreatic ascites and local chemical peritonitis rather than a thoracic collection. Whether a retroperitoneal leak produces a right, left or bilateral pleural effusion depends on the site of the pancreatic injury (head versus tail), the side of retroperitoneal tracking, and which hiatus or weak point the fluid exploits; the tail and body more often track leftward, but transhiatal spread can reach either hemithorax, as in this case where fluid tracked through the oesophageal hiatus to the right pleural space.

Putting this together clinically: recurrent, large, amylase‑rich, often bloody pleural effusions with a history of pancreatitis or pancreatic trauma should prompt imaging (MRCP/CT/ERCP) to look for a posterior duct disruption with retroperitoneal tracking rather than primary thoracic disease; anterior disruptions present with ascites and intra‑abdominal signs.
