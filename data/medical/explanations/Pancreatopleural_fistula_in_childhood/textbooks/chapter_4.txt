Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Chapter 4: ## Diagnostic Workup and Imaging Strategy

When a child presents with a large, recurrent, or otherwise unexplained pleural effusion, the diagnostic workup must be broadened beyond common pulmonary causes to include intra‑abdominal sources such as pancreatitis with an internal pancreatic fistula. The clinical course of the case presented — repeated large right‑sided, dark‑red pleural effusions that improved transiently after thoracentesis, a delayed disclosure of prior blunt abdominal trauma, and ultimately markedly elevated pleural amylase with MRCP demonstration of a tract from the pancreatic tail through the esophageal hiatus — illustrates how a pancreatic source can masquerade as primary thoracic disease. A pragmatic, pediatric‑focused algorithm balances rapid assessment and drainage for respiratory compromise with early laboratory and imaging steps that can identify pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) before unnecessary or prolonged pulmonary treatments are pursued.

Initial laboratory testing should be broad but targeted. In any child with a large or recurrent pleural effusion obtain serum amylase and lipase early; elevations support a pancreatic source but may be absent in some cases. Routine baseline studies — CBC with differential, CRP or ESR, liver and renal function tests, and fasting triglycerides — are useful to identify alternative causes (for example, hypertriglyceridemia and chylothorax) and to guide general management. If a child has recurrent or chronic pancreatitis or onset at a young age, arrange genetic evaluation (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) and sweat chloride testing to assess for cystic fibrosis; these investigations are particularly important when there is no clear traumatic or alcoholic etiology.

Pleural fluid analysis is pivotal and should be performed at the first diagnostic thoracentesis. Fluid must be characterized by Light’s criteria (protein and LDH) to distinguish transudate from exudate, and cell count with differential, Gram stain and aerobic/anaerobic cultures should be sent. Pancreatic disease should be suspected when pleural fluid is an exudate and either grossly hemorrhagic or serosanguinous, and when pleural fluid amylase is markedly elevated. A pleural fluid amylase concentration in excess of 1,000 IU/L is highly suggestive of a pancreatic source; when available, amylase isoenzyme typing (to distinguish pancreatic from salivary amylase) can help differentiate pancreatic fistula from other causes of amylase‑rich effusions (eg, esophageal rupture or some malignancies). Also measure pleural fluid pH and glucose: low pH and very low glucose point to complicated parapneumonic effusion or empyema but may also be seen in long‑standing pancreatic effusions; triglycerides should be checked if chylothorax is in the differential. Adenosine deaminase (ADA) can be elevated with tuberculous pleuritis but must be interpreted cautiously: mild elevations do not confirm TB and can mislead the clinician, as in this case where empiric antitubercular therapy was initiated before pancreatic testing ruled it out. Cytology should be sent when malignancy is a concern.

Imaging should follow a stepwise approach that reflects urgency, diagnostic yield, and pediatric considerations about radiation and sedation. A frontal chest radiograph and bedside ultrasound are the initial imaging modalities of choice. They rapidly confirm the presence, laterality and approximate volume of effusion and help to identify loculations; ultrasound is the best bedside tool to guide safe diagnostic thoracentesis or chest‑tube placement and avoids radiation exposure. In the presented case, bedside ultrasound quantified an approximately one‑litre effusion and facilitated safe aspiration, which temporarily improved the child’s respiratory status.

Cross‑sectional imaging is the next step when pleural fluid analysis suggests a pancreatic origin (eg, high pleural amylase), when there is a history of abdominal trauma, or when recurrent effusions prompt a search for an extrathoracic cause. Contrast‑enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen is widely available and excellent at detecting pancreatitis complications — pseudocysts, pancreatic calcifications, ductal dilation, and retroperitoneal or pleural collections — and is particularly helpful for operative planning or when MRCP is not immediately available. However, CT detects the actual pancreatic‑to‑pleural fistulous tract in only a minority of cases (reported around one‑third), so its inability to show a tract does not exclude a PPF. In the case described, CT raised suspicion by demonstrating a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail and a hydropneumothorax, which prompted dedicated pancreatobiliary imaging.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the preferred noninvasive ductal imaging modality in children when technical resources and pediatric anesthesia support permit. MRCP has substantially greater sensitivity than CT for delineating pancreatic duct anatomy and visualizing a fistulous trajectory through diaphragmatic hiatuses (esophageal or aortic), with reported sensitivities of roughly 70–80%. When available, secretin‑enhanced MRCP further increases sensitivity by transiently stimulating pancreatic exocrine secretions and mapping ductal leaks. MRCP in our patient confirmed a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail communicating with the distal pancreatic duct and tracking superiorly into the right pleural cavity, providing the anatomical diagnosis that redirected therapy away from pulmonary etiologies.

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the diagnostic and therapeutic gold standard for ductal disruption: it can directly demonstrate leak sites and allows transpapillary interventions such as stent placement or dilatation that lower intraductal pressure and divert pancreatic secretions to promote fistula closure. In pediatrics ERCP has been used successfully, but it requires careful weighing of benefits and risks (notably post‑ERCP pancreatitis) and should be performed by experienced pediatric interventional endoscopists. Indications for early ERCP include persistent high volume pleural drainage despite conservative measures, demonstrable ductal obstruction or stricturing on MRCP/CT, or when MRCP suggests a stentable site. If endoscopic therapy is not feasible or fails, surgical options (eg, distal pancreatectomy with or without pancreaticojejunostomy, cystogastrostomy/cystojejunostomy) are the definitive alternatives.

A practical diagnostic algorithm based on the preceding considerations is as follows and can be applied in most pediatric settings. For any child with a large, unilateral, hemorrhagic or recurrent pleural effusion: obtain chest radiograph and bedside ultrasound, perform diagnostic thoracentesis and send a comprehensive pleural fluid panel that explicitly includes amylase (and triglycerides, pH, glucose, LDH, protein, cell count, Gram stain/culture, cytology and ADA as indicated). If pleural amylase is markedly elevated (eg, >1,000 IU/L) or serum amylase/lipase are raised, obtain targeted pancreatic imaging rather than repeating blind thoracenteses: MRCP is preferred when available and feasible for children; contrast CT chest/abdomen is useful to detect collections, calcifications and guide percutaneous interventions. If MRCP or CT demonstrates ductal disruption or a pseudocyst communicating with the chest, early multidisciplinary discussion (pediatric gastroenterology/interventional endoscopy, thoracic surgery, interventional radiology, and intensive care) should occur to plan conservative therapy (NPO, bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide) versus earlier ERCP with transpapillary stenting. Conservative measures may be attempted for a limited period (generally 2–3 weeks) when the fistula is small and drainage is controlled; failure to improve or persistent high output favors endoscopic or surgical intervention.

Pediatric‑specific considerations must be woven through the diagnostic and imaging strategy. Minimize ionizing radiation in children: preferentially use ultrasound and MRCP when clinically appropriate; when CT is necessary, limit the field of view and use pediatric dose reduction protocols. Plan for sedation or general anesthesia for MRCP and ERCP in young children, including airway management and post‑procedure monitoring. Coordinate early with pediatric specialists — pediatric gastroenterology/hepatology, pulmonology, radiology, surgery, anesthesia and genetics — so imaging, endoscopy and possible operative interventions proceed efficiently. Ensure that laboratory requests specify pancreatic amylase isoenzyme typing if available, and that pleural fluid is sent promptly and processed to avoid delays that can lead to repeated, non‑diagnostic thoracenteses. Finally, interpret tests like ADA in clinical context; mild ADA elevations or an apparent “tuberculosis contact” history (as in our patient) should not delay consideration of a pancreatic source when the pleural fluid is hemorrhagic and amylase‑rich.

In summary, the diagnostic approach to suspected pancreatopleural fistula in children is based on early pleural fluid analysis including amylase, judicious use of bedside ultrasound and non‑ionizing ductal imaging (MRCP when possible), and early multidisciplinary planning for endoscopic or surgical therapy when indicated. Applying this strategy to the presented case would have appropriately prioritized pancreatic testing after the first hemorrhagic effusion and might have shortened the interval to definitive diagnosis and therapy, sparing repeated empirical pulmonary treatments.
