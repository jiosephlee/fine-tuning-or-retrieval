Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis of Amylase-Rich or Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusions

When confronted with a child who has recurrent, large-volume, dark red (hemorrhagic) pleural effusions, the clinician must maintain a broad differential and use pleural fluid analysis plus targeted imaging and history to triage between thoracic and extrathoracic sources. The presented case illustrates how a pancreatic source can masquerade as a primary pulmonary disease and how routine assumptions (for example, empiric therapy for tuberculosis) can delay the correct diagnosis. Below I review the major competing diagnoses for amylase-rich or hemorrhagic pleural effusions, describe the characteristic pleural fluid and clinical features that distinguish them, and give practical diagnostic “decision cues” to prioritize further testing.

Tuberculous pleuritis
- Typical presentation: subacute to chronic symptoms—fever, night sweats, weight loss, pleuritic chest pain; often unilateral, small-to-moderate effusion. In children contact history can be present and clinicians are right to consider TB.
- Pleural fluid: lymphocyte-predominant exudate, elevated protein and LDH, and often raised ADA (adenosine deaminase). ADA is sensitive but not specific; moderate elevations can occur with other exudative processes.
- Diagnostic confirmation: microbiology from pleural fluid (AFB smear and culture, GeneXpert MTB/RIF) has limited sensitivity. If suspicion remains, pleural biopsy for histology and culture increases yield.
- Pitfall illustrated by the case: a mildly raised ADA plus exposure history led to empiric anti‑TB treatment despite a hemorrhagic, recurrent effusion. In a recurrent large hemorrhagic effusion, consider alternative causes; do not rely on ADA alone.

Parapneumonic effusion and empyema
- Typical presentation: acute febrile illness with productive cough and radiographic consolidation; patient is often toxic.
- Pleural fluid: neutrophil-predominant exudate, low pH (<7.2) and low glucose (<60 mg/dL) in complicated parapneumonic effusions/empyema, positive Gram stain or culture in many cases.
- Hemorrhagic pleural effusions can occur with necrotising infection, but markedly elevated pleural amylase is uncommon.
- Management: requires antibiotics and drainage. In the absence of systemic infection, look elsewhere.

Malignancy (primary pleural malignancy, lymphoma, leukemia, metastatic disease)
- Typical presentation: systemic “B” symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss) may be present; persistent or recurrent unilateral effusions; in children, lymphoma/leukemia are more relevant than primary lung carcinoma.
- Pleural fluid: exudate; cytology may reveal malignant cells (sensitivity varies). Some tumors produce salivary-type amylase, causing elevated pleural amylase.
- Diagnostic steps: pleural fluid cytology, peripheral blood smear, bone marrow studies if hematologic malignancy suspected, and cross-sectional imaging. Persistent bloody effusions raise concern for malignancy—investigate aggressively.

Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave syndrome)
- Typical presentation: acute severe chest pain after forceful vomiting, subcutaneous emphysema, rapid deterioration with mediastinitis.
- Pleural fluid: can be bloody or serosanguineous; typically low pH, low glucose, presence of food particles or bile, and markedly elevated amylase that is salivary-type rather than pancreatic.
- Imaging and diagnosis: contrast esophagram with water-soluble contrast (followed by barium if negative) or CT showing mediastinal air/fluid and pleural collections.
- Distinguishing features: history of violent emesis and rapid systemic toxicity distinguish Boerhaave’s from pancreatic fistulas.

Hepatic hydrothorax
- Typical presentation: right-sided effusion in the setting of portal hypertension and cirrhosis, with ascites.
- Pleural fluid: usually a transudate (low protein, low LDH) unless secondary infection or malignancy is present; amylase is not elevated.
- Clues: liver disease on exam/labs and imaging, and resolution with management of portal hypertension.

Chylothorax
- Typical presentation: milky or opalescent pleural fluid after thoracic surgery/trauma, or in lymphatic obstruction.
- Pleural fluid: high triglycerides (>110 mg/dL), chylomicrons on lipoprotein electrophoresis.
- Distinguishing features: appearance and triglyceride/chylomicron testing; amylase not typically elevated.

Diaphragmatic hernia
- Typical presentation: breathlessness and bowel sounds in the chest, sometimes gastrointestinal symptoms.
- Pleural fluid: depends on associated visceral injury; imaging usually shows abdominal viscera in the thorax.
- Diagnosis: plain radiograph, chest/abdomen CT.

Pancreatitis-related pleural effusion vs pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)
- Pancreatitis-associated pleural effusion: small-to-moderate, usually left-sided, often transient and occurring during or shortly after an episode of pancreatitis.
- Pancreatopleural fistula: tends to produce recurrent, large-volume (often massive), unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions that can be hemorrhagic and may predominantly produce thoracic symptoms with minimal abdominal complaints—particularly in children. A history of pancreatitis, blunt abdominal trauma, or genetic predisposition (e.g., PRSS1, SPINK1 mutations) increases the pretest probability.
- Pleural fluid: hallmark is very high amylase (commonly >1,000 IU/L). Pleural fluid lipase is often elevated and can be more specific for a pancreatic source. Amylase isoenzyme analysis (pancreatic vs salivary type) helps distinguish pancreatic leak from salivary-type amylase produced by some tumors or from esophageal leaks.
- Imaging: CT may show pseudocysts, retroperitoneal collections, or tracking fluid but often misses the fistula itself. MRCP has better sensitivity (approximately 70–80%) for visualizing fistulous tracts and duct anatomy. ERCP is both diagnostic and therapeutic—visualizes the duct and allows transpapillary stenting.
- Clinical cue from the case: recurrent, large right-sided hemorrhagic effusions with pleural amylase of 6,118 U/L and elevated pleural lipase pointed to a pancreatic origin confirmed on MRCP.

Practical diagnostic approach and “red flags”
- Always send a full pleural fluid panel on the first thoracentesis: cell count and differential, protein, LDH, pH, glucose, Gram stain and culture, AFB/GeneXpert when TB suspected, cytology, triglycerides, amylase and lipase, and ADA if TB in differential.
- Consider the following features as decision cues favoring a pancreatic source/PPF:
  - Very high pleural fluid amylase (commonly >1,000 IU/L), especially when pleural fluid lipase is also elevated.
  - Recurrent large-volume or “massive” pleural effusion that rapidly reaccumulates despite drainage.
  - Poor clinical response to conventional pulmonary therapies (antibiotics, anti‑TB drugs) and lack of systemic features of infection.
  - History of pancreatitis, alcohol abuse (in adults), blunt abdominal trauma, or family history of hereditary pancreatitis.
  - Imaging demonstrating retroperitoneal collections tracking through the diaphragmatic hiatus, or a pseudocyst adjacent to the diaphragm.
- Use amylase isoenzyme testing when the source is unclear (pancreatic vs salivary). A high pleural lipase strengthens the pancreatic diagnosis.
- If PPF is suspected, obtain cross-sectional imaging (contrast-enhanced CT chest/abdomen and MRCP) early; defer empiric, prolonged anti-TB therapy until pancreatic and malignant causes are excluded when the clinical picture is atypical for TB.

Summary
Hemorrhagic or amylase-rich pleural effusions have a focused differential—infectious (parapneumonic/empyema, TB), traumatic, malignant, esophageal perforation, chylothorax, hepatic hydrothorax, and pancreatic sources (including PPF). Distinguishing features include the pleural fluid cell type, pH and glucose, ADA, triglycerides, and especially pleural amylase and lipase with isoenzyme typing. Recurrent, massive pleural effusions with very high pleural amylase (and elevated lipase), particularly after abdominal trauma or in the setting of pancreatitis, should trigger early abdominal imaging (MRCP/CT) and consideration of ERCP—this approach could have avoided prolonged empiric anti-TB therapy in the presented child and led to earlier definitive management.
