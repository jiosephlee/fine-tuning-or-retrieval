# The Pulmonary Mask: When Recurrent ‘Hemorrhagic’ Effusions Are Pancreatic

We see a lot of pleural effusions in the ED and wards. Most get worked up for infection, malignancy, TB, or heart failure — not the pancreas. That’s exactly why pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is dangerous: it wears a “pulmonary mask.” The case here — a late-childhood boy with recurrent, dark red, right-sided effusions who finally disclosed a bicycle-handlebar injury — is classic for how a pancreatic leak can present as a respiratory problem and be repeatedly misdirected into pulmonary pathways (including empiric TB treatment, multiple thoracenteses and an intercostal drain).

Why this case is interesting
- The child’s dominant complaints were chest pain and respiratory distress; abdominal signs were minimal. That’s common: most pediatric PPFs present with thoracic symptoms, not abdominal pain.
- Pleural fluid was dark red and large-volume, repeatedly totaling around a litre per tap — a pattern that should make you think beyond empyema and TB.
- Simple, high-yield test (pleural fluid amylase) and targeted imaging (MRCP) clinched the diagnosis and averted further inappropriate pulmonary workup. Conservative pancreatic-directed care (NPO, TPN, octreotide, drainage) resolved the fistula.

Pathophysiology in one line
- A disrupted pancreatic duct or ruptured pseudocyst tracks retroperitoneally through hiatuses or erodes the diaphragm, allowing enzyme-rich fluid to enter the mediastinum/pleural space and create an internal pancreatic-pleural fistula.

How PPF masquerades as a lung problem
- Large unilateral pleural effusion with respiratory compromise dominates the clinical picture.
- Abdominal pain is often absent or mild in children.
- Recurrent rapid re-accumulation after drainage is common because the pancreatic source keeps leaking.
- If trauma (handlebar injury) or pancreatitis history is not elicited, attention stays on thoracic causes.

Red flags that should trigger a search for PPF
- Recurrent, large-volume unilateral pleural effusions despite initial drainage.
- Pleural fluid that is serosanguinous/dark red (not purulent) and re-accumulates rapidly.
- Pleural fluid analysis showing very high amylase (commonly >1000 U/L).
- Recent blunt abdominal trauma (especially handlebar injuries) or known pancreatitis.
- Lack of systemic infection signs (no fever, sterile cultures) and negative TB workup.
- Pleural effusion with minimal pulmonary parenchymal disease on imaging.

Practical approach at the bedside
1. Re-ask the history: blunt abdominal trauma, prior pancreatitis, alcohol/family history of hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR in kids).
2. When you drain bloody or serosanguinous large effusions that re-accumulate, send pleural fluid for amylase and lipase immediately — this is cheap, quick, and often diagnostic.
3. Interpret amylase: pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 U/L is strongly suggestive of PPF (but remember other causes like esophageal perforation, malignancy, TB can raise amylase — check amylase isoforms if needed).
4. Imaging:
   - CT chest/abdomen: looks for collections, tracks, pseudocysts; identifies hydropneumothorax and retroperitoneal collections but may miss the fistula itself.
   - MRCP: best noninvasive study for duct anatomy and fistulous tracts (70–80% sensitivity).
   - ERCP: diagnostic and therapeutic — visualizes fistula and allows transpapillary stenting.
5. Management ladder:
   - Initial conservative trial (especially in children without major ductal disruption): NPO, TPN or enteral rest, somatostatin analogue (octreotide), pancreatic enzyme replacement as indicated, and adequate pleural drainage (thoracostomy/ICD).
   - If conservative measures fail (usually 2–3 weeks) or ductal anatomy shows obstruction/favorable target: ERCP with stent to reduce intraductal pressure.
   - If ERCP/stenting not feasible or ductal disease is irreparable: surgical options (distal pancreatectomy, pancreaticojejunostomy, cystenteric drainage).
6. Avoid knee-jerk empiric treatments for pulmonary causes (e.g., prolonged ATT or repeated thoracenteses) once PPF is reasonably suspected: they don’t fix the source and expose the patient to morbidity.

Pitfalls and differentiators
- TB: especially in endemic areas, ADA can be mildly raised — but very high pleural amylase argues against isolated TB. GeneXpert/culture and clinical context matter.
- Esophageal rupture (Boerhaave): can also elevate pleural amylase but usually has vomiting and mediastinitis features; clinical context and imaging help.
- Malignancy: can produce amylase-rich effusions (usually in adults) — cytology, imaging, and clinical picture help differentiate.
- Amylase isoform testing (pancreatic vs salivary) can be useful when the diagnosis is unclear.

Clinical pearls
- “Pleural fluid amylase” — send it early when you see recurrent, large, serosanguinous effusions. It’s a high-yield test that can change the whole diagnostic pathway.
- In children, consider trauma and hereditary pancreatitis as important causes; in adults alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis dominates.
- Conservative pancreatic therapy (NPO + octreotide + drainage) can close many fistulae, but have a low success rate in kids compared with adults — escalate to ERCP or surgery when indicated.
- MRCP is the noninvasive imaging study of choice for demonstrating the fistula and ductal anatomy before ERCP or operative planning.
- A “pulmonary mask” pleural effusion that keeps coming back deserves a pancreatic workup.

Key takeaways
- Recurrent, large-volume unilateral pleural effusions with dark red/serosanguinous fluid should prompt pleural fluid amylase/lipase testing.
- Don’t be misled by predominant respiratory symptoms — think retroperitoneal sources (pancreas), especially after abdominal trauma.
- MRCP and ERCP are complementary: MRCP for diagnosis and anatomy; ERCP for diagnosis and endoscopic therapy (stenting).
- Initial therapy is often conservative (NPO, TPN, octreotide, drainage); escalate to ERCP or surgery if the leak persists or ductal anatomy requires intervention.
- Early recognition prevents unnecessary pulmonary interventions and expedites definitive care.

Bottom line: when a child (or adult) has rapid, recurrent, “hemorrhagic” pleural effusions that don’t behave like empyema or TB, broaden your differential to include the pancreas — and send pleural amylase before you assume it’s purely pulmonary.



# Pleural Fluid Amylase 101: Ordering, Interpreting, and Avoiding TB Traps

What makes this case interesting
A previously well child with repeated, large, dark‑red right pleural effusions had multiple thoracic-focused workups and even empiric anti‑tubercular therapy started — until pleural amylase and MRCP exposed a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) from blunt abdominal trauma. This “pulmonary mask” is classic for PPF: respiratory symptoms dominate, the thorax looks like the problem, but the leak originates in the pancreas. The key missed clue in many cases is a high pleural amylase (often with very high pleural lipase) that points you out of the chest and into the abdomen.

Why pleural amylase matters
Pleural fluid amylase is a simple, inexpensive test that changes the diagnostic pathway when used in the right context. High pleural amylase should trigger consideration of three main causes: pancreatopleural fistula/pancreatitis, oesophageal perforation (Boerhaave’s), and some malignancies. Distinguishing pancreatic versus salivary sources (and excluding TB or empyema) is critical because management differs dramatically — from conservative pancreatic rest and octreotide to endoscopy/surgery, versus urgent surgical repair for Boerhaave’s, or chemotherapy for malignancy.

When to order pleural amylase
Think pleural amylase when any of the following apply:
- Large or recurrent unilateral pleural effusion (especially haemorrhagic/dark red) without clear infective or cardiac cause.
- Exudative effusion with very high pleural fluid output despite drainage.
- Recent history of pancreatitis, pancreatic trauma, or abdominal symptoms (even if mild or delayed).
- Pleural fluid with extremely high protein/LDH but sterile cultures and no empyema.
- Atypical lab pattern for TB (e.g., neutrophil predominance or very bloody fluid) despite endemic exposure.

How to interpret results — practical thresholds
- Normal pleural amylase: similar to serum normal; values vary by lab.
- Mild elevation (up to a few hundred U/L): nonspecific — could be oesophageal disease, pancreatitis, malignancy.
- Strongly suggestive of pancreatic origin: pleural amylase ≥1,000 U/L. Many PPF reports show pleural amylase in the thousands (case above: 6,118 U/L).
- Very high pleural lipase, when available, supports pancreatic source (lipase is more pancreas‑specific).
- Pleural/serum amylase ratio: not routinely used, but a pleural amylase substantially higher than serum suggests local production/leak.

Pancreatic vs salivary amylase: avoid the false certainty trap
Total amylase doesn’t tell you the isoenzyme. Two useful approaches if available:
- Pleural fluid amylase isoenzyme analysis or electrophoresis: separates pancreatic (P‑type) from salivary (S‑type) amylase. P‑type predominance → pancreatic leak. S‑type predominance → oesophageal or salivary source.
- Measure pleural fluid lipase: elevation supports pancreatic origin (oesophageal rupture rarely gives high lipase).
If isoenzyme testing isn’t available, integrate the lab numbers with clinical features and imaging: extremely high amylase/lipase + MRCP/CT findings clinches PPF.

Distinguishing PPF from other causes of amylase‑rich effusions
- Pancreatopleural fistula / pancreatitis
  - Often massive, recurrent, unilateral (or bilateral) pleural effusion.
  - Very high pleural amylase (often >1,000 U/L), high pleural lipase.
  - May follow abdominal trauma or pancreatitis; abdominal pain may be absent.
  - MRCP or CT may show a disrupted duct or pseudocyst tracking to the mediastinum/pleura; ERCP is both diagnostic and therapeutic.
- Oesophageal perforation (Boerhaave’s)
  - Typically sudden severe chest pain after vomiting, with mediastinitis features.
  - Pleural fluid amylase usually raised but of salivary (S‑type) origin; lipase typically not elevated.
  - Pleural fluid may be food‑contaminated, with low pH and high neutrophils.
  - Contrast esophagography or CT with oral contrast for diagnosis.
- Malignancy
  - Some cancers (lung, pancreas, ovary) can have elevated pleural amylase.
  - Cytology, imaging and clinical context are essential; isoenzyme is usually salivary type if metastatic salivary‑type tumours present.
- Tuberculosis
  - TB effusion classically lymphocyte‑predominant, high ADA; but ADA is not specific and can be mildly elevated in other exudates.
  - TB does not typically produce enormous pleural amylase/lipase; a very high pleural amylase should lower the probability of TB.

Pitfalls in TB‑endemic settings — ADA and the “TB trap”
- ADA can be a useful marker for tuberculous pleuritis, but it is not pathognomonic. ADA is often elevated (>40 U/L) in TB but can be mildly or moderately raised in empyema, lymphoma, rheumatoid disease, and even some pancreatic effusions.
- Relying on a single mildly elevated ADA or a history of TB contact (as in the reported case) risks anchoring bias and unnecessary anti‑tubercular therapy.
- Practical caution: do not start ATT solely on mild ADA elevation without microbiologic confirmation (GeneXpert/culture) or tissue diagnosis when the clinical picture is atypical (haemorrhagic fluid, very high amylase, neutrophil predominance, recurrent large effusions).

A stepwise laboratory and diagnostic strategy to avoid misdiagnosis
1. Baseline pleural studies for any exudative effusion: cell count, differential, protein, LDH, glucose, pH, Gram stain, culture, cytology.
2. Add pleural amylase and lipase when the effusion is:
   - Haemorrhagic or unusually large/recurrent; OR
   - Exudative with sterile cultures and atypical features; OR
   - Any history of pancreatitis, abdominal trauma, vomiting or unexplained abdominal symptoms.
3. If pleural amylase is elevated:
   - Send pleural lipase and compare with serum amylase/lipase.
   - Request amylase isoenzyme typing if available.
   - Obtain cross‑sectional imaging: MRCP preferred for duct anatomy and fistula visualization; CT chest/abdomen for collections/tracking.
   - Do microbiologic tests relevant to TB (GeneXpert, AFB culture) and cytology — but interpret in light of amylase/lipase and imaging.
4. Before starting empiric anti‑TB therapy:
   - Ensure pleural findings are consistent with TB (lymphocytic, low glucose, high ADA, typical imaging, positive microbiology).
   - If high pleural amylase/lipase or imaging suggests PPF/Boerhaave’s, prioritize pancreatic/esophageal workup and consult surgery/GI. Stop and reassess ATT if PPF confirmed.
5. If PPF is likely: early GI consult for MRCP/ERCP and consider conservative management first (NPO, TPN/enteral strategy, octreotide, chest drainage), then ERCP stent or surgery if conservative measures fail.

Clinical pearls
- Very high pleural amylase (often >1,000 U/L) and especially high pleural lipase strongly point to a pancreatic source — don’t anchor on TB just because of endemic exposure or mildly raised ADA.
- Pleural fluid that is dark red or haemorrhagic with recurrent large volumes should trigger amylase/lipase testing.
- Isoenzyme testing or lipase in pleural fluid helps distinguish pancreatic from salivary sources (PPF vs oesophageal perforation).
- MRCP is the noninvasive imaging test of choice to define fistula anatomy; ERCP is diagnostic and therapeutic.
- Conservative therapy (NPO, somatostatin analogues, nutritional support, chest drainage) can succeed — especially in children — but require close follow‑up and consideration of endoscopic/surgical options if no improvement.

Key take‑home messages
- Order pleural amylase (± lipase) for large, recurrent, bloody or otherwise unexplained exudative effusions, or when history suggests pancreatitis/abdominal trauma.
- Pleural amylase ≥1,000 U/L and elevated pleural lipase are highly suggestive of pancreatopleural fistula.
- Use isoenzyme typing or lipase to distinguish pancreatic versus salivary sources (PPF vs oesophageal rupture).
- Don’t start anti‑TB treatment solely on mild ADA elevation or TB contact — integrate amylase results, imaging, microbiology, and clinical context.
- If pleural amylase is high, get MRCP/ERCP and involve GI/surgery early — the right diagnosis saves the patient from unnecessary TB therapy and directs appropriate pancreatic care.

Keep this test in your pleural toolbox — it’s cheap, fast, and in the right case, diagnostic.



# Handlebar to Hemithorax: Blunt Abdominal Trauma as a Hidden Cause of PPF in Kids

This boy’s story is the sort of case that makes you rethink the limits of “thoracic” presentations. Recurrent, massive, dark‑red right pleural effusions sent him down a pulmonary workup (including empiric TB therapy) until a repeat history and cross‑sectional imaging revealed the real culprit: a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) tracking from the tail of the pancreas through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space — all after a seemingly minor bicycle handlebar injury.

Why this case is worth your attention
- PPF is rare in children (≈47 published pediatric cases) and commonly presents with respiratory — not abdominal — complaints. That makes it a classic “pulmonary mask.”
- Blunt abdominal trauma, such as handlebar injuries, is an under‑appreciated trigger for pancreatic duct disruption or pseudocyst formation in kids. Trauma accounted for a large slice of pediatric series.
- The pleural fluid here was dark red and massively elevated in amylase/lipase (pleural amylase >6,000 U/L) — a diagnostic clue that points away from primary lung disease and toward pancreatic origin.
- The child initially withheld the trauma history — remember that kids hide events like bike falls or bullying — so a second‑pass, nonjudgmental history can be diagnostic.

How a pancreatic injury reaches the chest
Pancreatic juice follows the path of least resistance:
- A posterior ductal disruption or ruptured pseudocyst allows enzyme‑rich fluid to escape into the retroperitoneum.
- Fluid can ascend through natural diaphragmatic hiatuses — especially the aortic or esophageal hiatus — into the mediastinum and then perforate the pleura.
- The fistula may track to either hemithorax; right‑sided collections are well described (as in this case), and bilateral communications can occur.
- The resulting pleural effusion is typically large, often recurrent, and characteristically exudative with very high amylase and lipase.

Key diagnostic steps (think pancreas when the chest story doesn’t add up)
- Pleural fluid analysis: very high amylase (>1,000 U/L is suspicious; levels in thousands are strongly supportive). Consider amylase isoenzyme or pancreatic‑type amylase to differentiate from salivary causes.
- Cross‑sectional imaging:
  - CT chest/abdomen: good for pseudocysts, collections and tracking fluid; direct visualization of the fistula is variable.
  - MRCP: better sensitivity (≈70–80%) for fistula and duct anatomy; noninvasive and very helpful in kids.
- ERCP: diagnostic and therapeutic — gold standard for ductal imaging and allows transpapillary stent placement when indicated.
- Don’t forget to reassess the history for prior abdominal trauma, recurrent pancreatitis, or family history pointing to hereditary pancreatitis.

Management principles — escalate from conservative to endoscopic to surgical
- Initial conservative approach (appropriate for many children without severe ductal disruption):
  - NPO, bowel rest; TPN if prolonged.
  - Somatostatin analogue (octreotide) to reduce pancreatic exocrine secretion.
  - Pancreatic enzyme replacement as needed.
  - Adequate pleural drainage (thoracentesis or chest tube) to relieve respiratory compromise.
  - Many pediatric cases respond to this bundle over 2–3 weeks — as in this patient, whose drainage decreased over 10 days.
- Endoscopic therapy (ERCP ± transpapillary stent): reduces intraductal pressure and promotes fistula closure; good success rates in children when anatomy allows.
- Surgery (distal pancreatectomy, cystojejunostomy, Puestow) reserved for failed conservative/endoscopic therapy or when ductal anatomy or distal fistula location precludes stenting.

Pitfalls and pearls from this case
- A “pulmonary” picture with recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion should trigger measurement of pleural amylase and a focused abdominal evaluation.
- Always re‑ask for trauma history in pediatric respiratory presentations; children often minimize or hide injuries.
- Dark red pleural fluid does not always equal TB, malignancy, or empyema — enzyme‑rich pancreatic fluid can be bloody.
- MRCP is an excellent noninvasive next step in kids; ERCP remains therapeutic when intervention is needed.
- Conservative therapy (NPO + octreotide + chest drainage) can work quickly — monitor drain output and biochemical markers.

Take‑home messages
- Consider pancreatopleural fistula in children with recurrent, large, high‑amylase pleural effusions, especially if there’s any history (even remote or initially denied) of abdominal trauma or pancreatitis.
- Ask about handlebar or other blunt abdominal injuries — they can cause pancreatic duct disruption with delayed chest presentations.
- Test pleural fluid amylase early; get cross‑sectional imaging (CT/MRCP) to look for retroperitoneal collections tracking into the chest.
- Start conservative management (NPO, drainage, octreotide, TPN/PERT) and escalate to ERCP or surgery only if necessary.

This case is a neat reminder: in kids, the chest sometimes whispers the story of an injured pancreas. Reopen the history, check the amylase, and let imaging follow the fluid — you might spare the child months of misdirected investigations and treatments.



# Imaging the Invisible Leak: CT, MRCP, or ERCP for Suspected Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF)?

What makes this case worth pausing over? A late-childhood boy with repeated, large right-sided, dark-red pleural effusions that kept improving after taps — until a CT showed a retroperitoneal collection tracking to the tail of the pancreas and MRCP confirmed fistulous communication. The chest was the loudest clinical problem but the pancreas was the culprit. That “pulmonary mask” is exactly why imaging selection and sequencing matter: one modality will pick up complications, another will map the duct, and a third lets you intervene.

Below is a pragmatic, FOAM-style playbook for choosing and interpreting imaging in suspected PPF — especially useful when you’re caring for a deteriorating child.

## What each modality is good for (and not good for)

CT (contrast-enhanced chest/abdomen)
- Strengths: fast, widely available, excellent at showing pleural complications (large effusion, hydropneumothorax, lung consolidation), pancreatic collections/pseudocysts, fat stranding, retroperitoneal fluid tracking and hemorrhage. In trauma-era presentations it also identifies associated injuries.
- Limitations: limited sensitivity for direct visualization of the actual fistulous tract — reported fistula detection ~33–47%. Ionizing radiation is a bigger concern in children.
- What to look for: massive unilateral pleural effusion (often hemorrhagic), hydropneumothorax, multiloculated hypodense collections extending from retroperitoneum into mediastinum/pleural space, discontinuity or leak near pancreatic tail/body, pseudocyst abutting the diaphragm or esophageal hiatus.

MRCP (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography)
- Strengths: non‑invasive ductal imaging; no ionizing radiation — a major advantage in kids. Sensitivity for visualizing fistula/duct anatomy ~70–80%. Excellent for seeing communication between pancreatic duct/pseudocyst and mediastinal/pleural collections.
- Limitations: availability, need for child cooperation or sedation, longer acquisition time. May be less acute-illness friendly if the patient cannot tolerate study.
- What to look for: tubular cystic collections tracking superiorly through the retroperitoneum into mediastinum/pleura, direct connection between distal pancreatic duct and collection, pancreatic duct narrowing/stricture, pseudocyst location and size. MRCP can show bilateral involvement and small communicating tracts better than CT.

ERCP (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography)
- Strengths: gold standard for therapeutic planning. ERCP can visualize the duct directly and allows transpapillary stenting/balloon dilation to decompress the duct and close fistula — essentially both diagnostic and therapeutic.
- Limitations: invasive, requires anesthesia, risk of procedure-induced pancreatitis or complications, operator-dependent. Visualization rates vary (in pediatric series 38–78% for fistula), but therapeutic success can be high when feasible.
- What to look for/perform: leak delineation on pancreatogram, site of duct disruption (main duct vs branch), strictures or stones, opportunity for stent placement across the leak or into pseudocyst.

## How to sequence imaging in a sick child with recurrent pleural effusion and suspicion for PPF

1. Initial response and chest imaging
   - Chest X‑ray and bedside lung ultrasound first for immediate assessment (effusion size, hydropneumothorax) — guide whether drainage is urgent.
   - If large symptomatic effusion → therapeutic/diagnostic thoracentesis (send pleural amylase/lipase, cell count, culture, cytology). Very high pleural amylase (>>1000 IU/L) should trigger pancreatic workup.

2. Cross-sectional imaging — CT vs MRCP first?
   - If the child is hemodynamically unstable, signs of sepsis, recent trauma, or you need rapid whole‑chest/abdomen evaluation → CT chest ± abdomen with IV contrast. CT will reveal collections, bleeding, and complications quickly and will guide immediate drainage or chest tube placement.
   - If the child is stable and you can obtain MRCP without delay or anesthesia concerns → MRCP is preferable as the next test because it maps the duct and fistula without radiation and changes management (conservative vs endoscopic vs surgical).
   - Practical hybrid approach: CT first for acute care (as in the presented case: CT showed a tracking collection and hydropneumothorax), then MRCP to confirm fistula anatomy and ductal involvement.

3. When to call GI/interventional endoscopy (ERCP)
   - Consider ERCP early if MRCP demonstrates a ductal disruption amenable to endoscopic therapy, or if conservative therapy fails (persistent high output from chest tube after 2–3 weeks), or if there is ductal obstruction/stricture.
   - In children, plan ERCP in centers experienced with pediatric pancreatobiliary ERCP and with pediatric anesthesia support.

4. When to involve surgery
   - Failing medical + endoscopic therapy, inaccessible distal duct leaks, or complicated ductal anatomy → surgical options (distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy) are effective but carry more morbidity.

## Practical interpretation tips (what not to miss)

- Dark‑red, high‑amylase pleural fluid is a red flag for PPF — don’t let a positive TB contact or mildly elevated ADA distract you. Confirm with serum and pleural fluid amylase/lipase.
- On CT, follow any retroperitoneal fluid collection upward — a continuous hypodense track to the mediastinum or pleural space is strong circumstantial evidence even if you can’t see a discrete fistula.
- On MRCP, look for a communicating neck between the cyst/collection and the pancreatic duct — even a narrow connection can sustain a high-volume leak.
- If MRCP is equivocal but clinical suspicion is high (recurrent large effusions, very high pleural amylase), consider early ERCP instead of waiting for prolonged conservative therapy.

## Clinical pearls from the case
- Pediatric PPF often presents with respiratory symptoms and massive unilateral effusion; abdominal history (trauma, pancreatitis) may be initially concealed — keep asking and re‑asking.
- Pleural fluid amylase >1000 IU/L is highly suggestive of pancreatic origin; perform amylase typing when available to distinguish pancreatic vs salivary sources.
- Use CT for speed and to detect complications; use MRCP to map the duct noninvasively; use ERCP when you need to treat the leak or when MRCP/CT do not give a clear therapeutic plan.
- In children, prioritize radiation sparing — MRCP should be obtained when feasible and safe.

## Take-home messages
- Suspect PPF in recurrent, large, amylase‑rich pleural effusions — especially with a history of pancreatitis or blunt abdominal trauma.
- CT = rapid, picks up pleural complications and collections; poor at directly showing small fistulae.
- MRCP = best noninvasive ductal mapping (70–80% sensitivity) and favored in children to avoid radiation.
- ERCP = diagnostic + therapeutic gold standard when endoscopic stenting is feasible; bring pediatric endoscopy expertise.
- Sequence imaging pragmatically: stabilize → chest imaging/ultrasound → CT if unstable or trauma/other chest pathology suspected → MRCP for ductal anatomy when stable → ERCP for therapy if indicated.

Keep a low threshold to send pleural fluid amylase/lipase early. In the right clinical context, imaging the “invisible leak” will change management — and save the child from repeated thoracic procedures and unnecessary treatments.



# Closing the Leak: A Management Pathway for Pediatric Pancreatopleural Fistula

What’s striking about pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in kids is how well it wears a “pulmonary mask.” Massive, recurrent pleural effusions — sometimes blood‑stained — dominate the presentation and lead teams down thoracic pathways (TB workups, empyema, malignancy) while the pancreatic source is missed. In the case above a previously healthy child with blunt abdominal trauma had recurrent right pleural effusions with dark red fluid that repeatedly improved after drainage — until CT/MRCP and very high pleural amylase/lipase (pleural amylase 6118 U/L, pleural lipase 7211 U/L) pointed to PPF. He responded to conservative management (NPO, TPN, octreotide, PERT) with ICD drainage and recovered without need for endoscopy or surgery.

Here’s a concise, pragmatic bedside-to-ward pathway for children with suspected or confirmed PPF, synthesized from the case and available pediatric series.

## Recognize it early — the red flags
- Recurrent, large unilateral pleural effusion (often massive) without clear pulmonary cause.
- Pleural fluid markedly elevated amylase/lipase (pleural amylase ≥1000 IU/L strongly suggests PPF).
- History of pancreatitis, pancreatic trauma, or risk factors for pediatric chronic pancreatitis (genetic causes).
- Pleural fluid that is serous/serosanguinous/dark red and repeatedly reaccumulates after thoracentesis.

Tip: consider pleural amylase typing (pancreatic vs salivary) if other causes of high pleural amylase are possible (eg Boerhaave’s, malignancy).

## Initial diagnostics — what to order
- Chest X‑ray and bedside ultrasound to quantify effusion and guide drainage.
- Diagnostic thoracentesis with pleural fluid studies: cell count, Gram stain/culture, cytology, amylase/lipase, ADA if TB suspected.
- Serum amylase/lipase.
- Cross‑sectional imaging: contrast CT chest/abdomen to look for collections/pseudocysts and track. CT detects associated collections well but shows the fistula in only ~33–47%.
- MRCP to define ductal anatomy and visualize fistula — reported sensitivity ~70–80% for fistula visualization.
- ERCP remains the therapeutic gold standard for ductal mapping and stenting, with high reported pediatric success rates in small series.

## Management pathway (practical bedside algorithm)

1. Immediate stabilization (ED/PICU)
   - Oxygen as needed; analgesia.
   - Ultrasound‑guided thoracentesis for symptomatic relief.
   - If recurrent/large ongoing drainage → controlled thoracic drainage (chest tube/ICD) rather than repeated blind taps.

2. Diagnostic confirmation
   - Send pleural fluid amylase/lipase early. Markedly elevated levels clinch the diagnosis and shift focus to the pancreas.
   - Obtain MRCP (or CT + MRCP when possible) to delineate collections and ductal communication.

3. First‑line (conservative) therapy — attempt for 2–3 weeks if no urgent indication to intervene
   - NPO (bowel rest).
   - Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) to reduce pancreatic stimulation and provide calories.
   - Somatostatin analogue — octreotide infusion 10–40 mcg/kg/day (case used 30 mcg/kg/day). Purpose: suppress pancreatic exocrine output and aid fistula closure.
   - Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) as supportive therapy as per local practice.
   - Controlled thoracic drainage (ICD) to evacuate pleural collection and prevent trapped lung; monitor output daily.
   - Close clinical and radiologic reassessment (drain output trend, inflammatory markers, imaging).

   Expectation: conservative success in pediatric cases is limited — reported around 12% — but some children (like the presented case) do close the fistula within days–weeks.

4. When to escalate to endoscopy (ERCP with transpapillary stent)
   - Failure of conservative measures after 2–3 weeks (persistent high ICD output, ongoing symptoms, no radiologic improvement).
   - Demonstrated ductal disruption amenable to transpapillary stenting on MRCP/ERCP.
   - Ductal strictures, pseudocysts amenable to transpapillary therapy.
   - ERCP benefits: reduces intraductal pressure and diverts flow, often closing fistula in 2–3 weeks. Small pediatric series report high technical and clinical success (some reports cite near‑complete success rates in selected pediatric cohorts).

5. Surgical options — when endoscopy not possible or fails
   - Indications: inaccessible ductal disruption for ERCP/stent, distal ductal disruption with large pseudocyst, failure of endoscopic therapy.
   - Procedures include distal pancreatectomy +/- splenectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow), or cystojejunostomy depending on anatomy.
   - Pediatric surgical series report high success (~93% in one small group), low operative mortality (<5%), but recurrence (~11%).

## Key clinical pearls
- Think pancreas in any unexplained recurrent pleural effusion — especially if pleural amylase is very high. The chest can be a “pulmonary mask.”
- Pleural amylase ≥1000 IU/L is strongly suggestive of PPF; amylase typing (pancreatic vs salivary) can help differentiate causes.
- MRCP is the noninvasive test of choice for mapping fistula/ductal anatomy (70–80% sensitivity). CT is useful for detecting collections and surgical planning but less sensitive for the fistula tract.
- Conservative therapy — NPO, TPN, octreotide (10–40 mcg/kg/day), PERT, and adequate chest drainage — is first-line, but pediatric success rates are modest (~12%). Set expectations early and plan for timely escalation if no response.
- ERCP with transpapillary stent is effective and often definitive; in small pediatric series it has excellent success when technically feasible.
- Surgery is highly successful when indicated but reserved for endoscopic failure or unfavorable ductal anatomy.

## Bottom line
PPF in children is rare but important to catch early because the treatment target is the pancreas — not the lung. Rapid pleural drainage for symptoms, early pleural fluid amylase testing, MRCP for anatomy, and an organized trial of conservative therapy (NPO/TPN + octreotide + PERT + chest tube) give many children a chance to heal without surgery. If the leak persists after a monitored 2–3 week conservative window, escalate to ERCP with transpapillary stent; reserve surgery for refractory cases or unfavorable anatomy. The case above is a nice reminder that a high index of suspicion and a focused pancreatic workup can close the leak — and spare the child prolonged thoracic interventions or unnecessary TB/malignancy workups.

Takeaway bullets
- Recurrent massive pleural effusion + very high pleural amylase → think pancreatopleural fistula.
- First‑line conservative bundle: NPO, TPN, octreotide 10–40 mcg/kg/day, PERT + controlled thoracic drainage; reassess at 2–3 weeks.
- If conservative fails → ERCP with transpapillary stent (high success in selected pediatric cases).
- Surgery (distal pancreatectomy, Puestow, cystojejunostomy) reserved for endoscopic failure or unfavorable anatomy; outcomes in small pediatric series are good.



# Right-Sided Hydropneumothorax in a Child? Think Retroperitoneum-to-Hiatus Fistula

What looks like a straightforward right-sided hydropneumothorax or recurrent empyema in a child can sometimes have a surprising abdominal origin: a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) tracking from the retroperitoneum through a diaphragmatic hiatus into the chest. The case above is a classic “pulmonary mask” — repeated large, dark red pleural effusions and temporary clinical improvement after drainage — but the pathology was a pancreatic duct disruption after blunt abdominal trauma. The chest X‑ray and even CT findings (hydropneumothorax, air–fluid levels, loculations) can easily steer you toward empyema and thoracic-only management. That’s the trap.

Why the right side (and sometimes bilaterality)?
- Pancreatic fluid that leaks posteriorly into the retroperitoneum follows the path of least resistance. It can travel superiorly and enter the mediastinum via the aortic or oesophageal hiatus. The path taken and the side of least resistance frequently determine which pleural space is involved.
- The right pleural space is commonly affected because pancreatic collections that track posteriorly and cross the midline may preferentially enter the right posterior mediastinum or right paraoesophageal channel before breaching the pleura.
- Bilateral communication is possible — a dominant right fistula with a narrow left communication (as in this child) explains asymmetric but sometimes bilateral effusions.

Why hydropneumothorax or air–fluid levels can mislead
- A retroperitoneal-to-hiatus fistula can carry not only enzyme-rich fluid but also gas from the gut or from secondary infection in a pancreatic collection/pseudocyst, producing hydropneumothorax or air–fluid levels that look like empyema.
- Loculations and consolidation on chest imaging add to the mimicry of complicated parapneumonic effusion or empyema.

What should shift your thinking from empyema → PPF?
- Recurrent large unilateral pleural effusions that rapidly re-accumulate after drainage, especially without a convincing pulmonary source.
- Pleural fluid that is markedly amylase- and lipase-rich. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L should prompt pancreatic evaluation (and amylase isoenzyme typing if needed).
- History elements that may be subtle: recent blunt abdominal trauma (even an apparently minor bike-handle injury), pancreatitis symptoms (abdominal pain, vomiting) that may be absent or overlooked in children, or features suggesting chronic/recurrent pancreatitis (family history, steatorrhea).
- Pleural fluid appearance: hemorrhagic or dark red fluid is common in PPF and can be mistaken for hemothorax or malignant effusion.

Imaging strategy — what helps and what doesn’t
- Chest X‑ray: shows large effusion, sometimes air–fluid level or hydropneumothorax. Useful to recognize a recurring collection but nonspecific.
- Chest CT: great for defining pleural collections, consolidation, and tracking planes; it often shows a retroperitoneal collection or a tract but only depicts the fistulous tract in ~33–47% of cases.
- MRCP: best noninvasive test for ductal anatomy and fistula visualization (sensitivity ~70–80%). In kids with suspected PPF, MRCP is high yield.
- ERCP: diagnostic and therapeutic (stent); visualizes fistula directly in many cases and allows transpapillary stenting to reduce ductal pressure.
- Always correlate imaging with pleural fluid biochemistry — a high pleural amylase is the biochemical red flag.

Practical chest-tube and acute-care pearls
- Don’t reflexively stop the workup after placing a drain. A chest tube buys respiratory relief but is often only a temporizing measure in PPF.
- Send pleural fluid for routine studies AND amylase/lipase (and consider amylase isoenzymes if available), cytology, Gram stain/culture, ADA and TB testing if indicated — but interpret ADA in context (mildly raised ADA can mislead).
- If you see dark red, recurrent pleural fluid, add pleural amylase to your STAT panel.
- Drain large effusions to relieve respiratory compromise. But remember: ongoing high output should prompt immediate abdominal imaging (CT abdomen/MRCP) and GI/pancreatic consultation rather than repeated thoracic-only procedures.
- Early multidisciplinary coordination (pediatrics/pediatric surgery/gastroenterology/interventional endoscopy/radiology) improves outcomes.

Therapeutic approach (practical roadmap)
- Initial conservative management (especially when no major ductal disruption identified): NPO, bowel rest, TPN, somatostatin analogue (octreotide), pancreatic enzyme replacement, and pleural drainage. In children this approach can succeed but has a modest reported success rate.
- If conservative measures fail within 2–3 weeks or ductal anatomy shows a targetable lesion: ERCP with transpapillary stenting and/or sphincterotomy — less invasive and effective at reducing intraductal pressure and promoting fistula closure.
- If endoscopic therapy is not feasible or fails (distal obstruction, complex ductal injury, or persistent leakage): surgical options (distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy, cystenteric drainage) offer high success rates.
- In pediatric patients, trauma and genetic causes (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) are important etiologies — consider genetic testing when pancreatitis is recurrent.

Key clinical pearls and take-home points
- Recurrent unilateral (often right-sided) large pleural effusion in a child — always add pleural amylase/lipase to the initial fluid panel.
- Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is a powerful clue for PPF; consider amylase isoenzyme typing to distinguish pancreatic from salivary sources.
- Hydropneumothorax and air–fluid levels do not equal empyema — they can come from retroperitoneal pancreatic collections tracking through a hiatus.
- Chest tube is for symptom control and diagnosis — don’t let it stop you from searching for an abdominal source.
- MRCP is your noninvasive imaging workhorse for suspected PPF; ERCP is both diagnostic and often therapeutic.
- In children, think trauma and genetic causes rather than alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis.

Bottom line: in kids with recurrent, large, bloody or malodorous pleural effusions — especially right-sided or with hydropneumothorax — expand your differential beyond pulmonary disease. Send pleural amylase/lipase early, get MRCP if suspicion exists, and use chest drainage as a bridge while you hunt down the pancreatic source. Early recognition keeps kids off unnecessary pulmonary treatments (and TB meds), shortens hospital stays, and directs life‑changing interventions like endoscopic stenting or surgery when needed.



## The Recurrent Effusion That Wasn’t TB: Diagnostic Bias in Endemic Settings

What makes this case stick in your head? A late-childhood boy with large, recurrent, dark‑red right pleural effusions and minimal systemic illness—repeated thoracenteses gave symptomatic relief, pleural ADA was modestly raised, there was household TB contact, and empiric anti‑TB therapy was started. Only after persistence and revisiting the history did the team uncover a bicycle handlebar abdominal injury and, ultimately, pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) confirmed on MRCP with strikingly elevated pleural amylase/lipase. The diagnosis changed management completely: nil per os, TPN, octreotide, chest drainage —and clinical cure.

Why this matters
- In TB‑endemic places it’s easy to anchor on TB when you see an exudative pleural effusion plus an epidemiologic link. That anchoring can delay diagnosis of non‑pulmonary causes that present with a “pulmonary mask.” PPF is rare in children but presents classically with recurrent, massive, often haemorrhagic effusions and very high pleural amylase. Missing it leads to unnecessary TB treatment, repeated thoracenteses, prolonged morbidity and potential complications.
- This case is a great reminder to marry pattern recognition with a simple, pragmatic diagnostic checklist before committing to long courses of anti‑tubercular therapy.

A quick cognitive debrief: anchoring + availability
- Anchoring: initial focus on TB because of family contact and a mildly raised ADA—early tests influenced downstream thinking.
- Availability: in endemic areas TB is a common cause of pleural effusion, so it comes to mind first—even when features are atypical.
- Solution: pause, reframe the problem when the presentation is atypical (large, recurrent, haemorrhagic, afebrile), broaden the differential, and order a focused panel of tests to rule in/out alternative etiologies before starting long therapy.

When to suspect a non‑tuberculous cause (think pancreatopleural fistula or other alternatives)
- Large or massive pleural effusion, especially unilateral and recurrent after drainage
- Dark red or haemorrhagic/straw‑coloured high‑volume fluid
- Relatively few systemic symptoms (no prolonged fever, no weight loss)
- Recent abdominal trauma or known pancreatitis (but absence of abdominal pain does not exclude pancreatic source)
- Rapid reaccumulation after thoracentesis

Rational test order for a large, recurrent pleural effusion before committing to empiric TB therapy
- Pleural fluid cytology (malignancy) and routine biochemistry (protein, LDH)
- Pleural fluid cell count with differential (neutrophil vs lymphocyte predominance)
- Pleural fluid pH and glucose (low pH/glucose suggest empyema/rupture)
- Pleural fluid amylase and lipase — essential if effusion is large, haemorrhagic, or recurrent
- Gram stain and culture, plus aerobic/anaerobic cultures if purulent or febrile
- GeneXpert (or other rapid MTB test) on pleural fluid — high specificity though sensitivity is limited
- ADA — interpret with caution (moderately raised ADA can be seen in other conditions)
- Triglycerides (if milky effusion) for chylothorax
- Consider CT chest/abdomen or MRCP if pleural fluid amylase or history suggests pancreatic source
- Consider ERCP if imaging shows ductal disruption—diagnostic and therapeutic

A practical checklist to avoid premature anti‑TB therapy in pleural effusion
- Is the effusion large, recurrent, or rapidly reaccumulating after drainage? If yes → broaden workup.
- Is the fluid bloody/haemorrhagic or unusually high volume? If yes → add pleural amylase/lipase and cytology.
- Are systemic TB features absent (no prolonged fever, weight loss, night sweats)? If absent and presentation atypical → pause before TB Rx.
- Is there a history of abdominal trauma, pancreatitis, alcohol, or hereditary pancreatitis risk factors? Ask specifically.
- Have you sent GeneXpert on pleural fluid (and sputum if possible) before starting therapy? If not, consider waiting for rapid tests unless clinical instability mandates empiric Rx.
- Is ADA only modestly elevated (borderline)? Remember false positives; correlate with cell differential and other tests.
- Has cross‑sectional imaging (CT/MRCP) been considered to look for pancreatic leaks, pseudocysts, malignancy, or diaphragmatic defects? If suspicion for non‑pulmonary source exists, get imaging early.
- If pleural fluid amylase >1000 IU/L or lipase markedly elevated → prioritize pancreatic investigations (MRCP/CT) and involve gastroenterology/pediatric surgery.

Clinical pearls
- Pleural fluid amylase >1,000 IU/L is highly suggestive of pancreatic origin (PPF) but other causes (eg, oesophageal rupture, some malignancies) can elevate amylase—amylase isoenzyme typing (pancreatic vs salivary) helps when needed.
- MRCP has good sensitivity (70–80%) to visualize fistulae and ductal anatomy; CT is useful for collections/pseudocysts but less sensitive for the fistula itself. ERCP is both diagnostic and therapeutic.
- Conservative management (NPO, TPN, somatostatin analogues like octreotide, chest drainage) is reasonable first line in children without major ductal disruption; add ERCP stenting or surgery when conservative therapy fails or anatomy requires intervention.
- A mildly raised ADA is not a gatekeeper for TB therapy—interpret in clinical context.

Take‑home messages
- Don’t let endemic disease prevalence blind you: anchoring on TB can delay diagnosis of treatable non‑pulmonary causes.
- In large, recurrent, haemorrhagic pleural effusions—always send pleural amylase and lipase early.
- Use a short, focused checklist and targeted imaging (MRCP/CT) before starting prolonged empiric anti‑TB therapy, unless the patient is clinically unstable.
- Remember: PPF may present with little or no abdominal pain in children; ask about trauma and subtle abdominal history repeatedly.

This boy’s story teaches a simple lesson: pause for atypical features, order a few high‑yield pleural tests (amylase/lipase, cytology, GeneXpert), and think anatomically — sometimes the lung is merely showing the fistula’s destination, not its origin.



# Beyond the Effusion: Genetics and the Pediatric Pancreas

What makes this case interesting
A previously well child presents with recurrent, massive, blood‑stained pleural effusions and respiratory distress — several thoracic procedures and a tuberculosis workup later you discover a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) tracking from the tail of the pancreas. Imaging and very high pleural amylase/lipase clinch the diagnosis, and conservative management works. That’s already uncommon. What should trigger a pause in your mental checklist is the context: a child (not an adult with alcohol use), early onset pancreatitis/ductal injury, recurrence, and — often overlooked — a relatively minor blunt abdominal trauma that seems to “unmask” a pancreas that behaves badly. In pediatrics, genetics often underlies pancreatitis, and recognising that changes counseling, follow‑up and sometimes management.

Why genetics matters in pediatric pancreatitis and PPF
- The etiologic landscape in children is different from adults. Alcohol is a dominant driver in adults but rare in children. Genetic predisposition (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC and others) accounts for a substantial share of pediatric chronic or recurrent pancreatitis.
- A single blunt force can rupture a duct or trigger a pseudocyst, but a genetically vulnerable pancreas is more likely to develop complications (persistent leaks, fistulae, chronic changes) after an otherwise modest insult.
- Identifying a pathogenic variant matters beyond a label — it affects recurrence risk estimates, drives cascade testing of family members, influences surveillance for chronic pancreatitis complications, informs lifestyle advice and can change thresholds for interventions such as ERCP, stenting or surgery.

When to consider genetic testing (practical triggers)
In a child with pancreatitis or PPF, consider genetics if any of the following are present:
- Recurrent acute pancreatitis (≥2 distinct attacks) or early‑onset pancreatitis (especially <18 years)
- Evidence of chronic pancreatitis (ductal changes, calcifications, pseudocysts)
- Complications disproportionate to the triggering event (eg, PPF after minor trauma)
- Family history of pancreatitis or unexplained early pancreatic disease
- Idiopathic pancreatitis after standard evaluation (metabolic, autoimmune, medication causes excluded)
- Need for major interventions or if results would change management or inform surgery planning

Which genes to test and what to expect
- Core genes: PRSS1 (causative gain‑of‑function mutations — hereditary pancreatitis, high penetrance), SPINK1 and CTRC (predisposing, modify risk), CFTR (variable effect, consider pancreatic‑associated variants), and less commonly CPA1, CEL, and others.
- Most centers use targeted pancreatitis gene panels (next‑generation sequencing) rather than single‑gene testing.
- Yield: panels identify pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in a substantial minority of pediatric cases; yield is higher with earlier onset and recurrent disease.
- Interpretation caveats: variants of uncertain significance (VUS) are common; not all positive tests predict a definite course. Genetics needs correlation with phenotype and family history.

How results change care
- Positive, actionable result (pathogenic/likely pathogenic):
  - Counsel family on recurrence risk, natural history, and need for cascade testing of relatives.
  - Increase vigilance for recurrent attacks and progression to chronic pancreatitis; earlier consideration of endoscopic therapy (ERCP/stenting) if ductal disease is present.
  - Lifestyle and medication counseling: avoid tobacco, alcohol when age‑appropriate, certain drugs that predispose to pancreatitis; optimize nutrition.
  - Consider referral to multidisciplinary care: pediatric gastroenterology, genetics, nutrition, pain management.
- Negative or VUS:
  - Continue standard workup and management; reassess if phenotype evolves.
  - VUS should prompt periodic reinterpretation as databases grow.
- In all cases, genetic counselling before and after testing is important to explain implications, limitations and psychosocial issues.

When to test (timing and logistics)
- Avoid acute confounders: testing can be sent during or after the acute admission; results won’t change immediate management of the fistula but will guide medium‑term plans and family counselling.
- Use a validated pancreatitis gene panel. Involve clinical genetics or a pancreatology service to select test and interpret results.
- Consider pancreatic imaging and ERCP/ MRCP concurrently to define anatomy — genetics + ductal anatomy together better inform decisions (eg, stenting vs surgery).

Why trauma can unmask an underlying predisposition
- A genetically abnormal ductal system or impaired pancreatic protease regulation (eg, PRSS1/SPINK1 variants) reduces the threshold for ductal disruption, pseudocyst formation and persistent leakage.
- In that setting, a relatively modest blunt force (bicycle handlebar in the case above) can produce a leak that would otherwise heal in a normal pancreas, and pancreatic secretions track via mediastinal hiatuses to produce a PPF.
- Clinically: a history of seemingly minor abdominal trauma preceding unusual or recurrent thoracic presentations should raise the index of suspicion for underlying pancreatic disease and for genetic predisposition.

Clinical pearls from the case and literature
- Think pancreas for unexplained, recurrent, massive or unilateral pleural effusions — test pleural fluid amylase. Values ≥1000 IU/L should make PPF very likely.
- In children, genetic causes are common enough that recurrent/early pancreatitis or complicated presentations should prompt a genetics workup.
- MRCP has good sensitivity for fistula and ductal anatomy and is noninvasive; ERCP can be diagnostic and therapeutic, but has higher procedural risk.
- Conservative therapy (NPO, TPN, octreotide, chest drainage) can work, but success rates in children are limited — early discussion about endoscopic options is appropriate when drainage persists or ductal abnormalities are present.
- Always involve clinical genetics for testing strategy and family counseling; anticipate VUS and the need for cascade testing.

Take‑home messages
- Consider a genetic predisposition (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC, etc.) whenever a child presents with early, recurrent, chronic or complicated pancreatitis (including PPF), or when a minor trauma precipitates disproportionate pancreatic injury.
- Order a targeted pancreatitis gene panel with genetics input; positive results change counseling, family testing, surveillance and sometimes management.
- Combine genetic results with ductal imaging (MRCP/ERCP) to guide decisions about endoscopic stenting vs longer conservative attempts vs surgery.
- High pleural fluid amylase in a child with recurrent effusion is a red flag — look for pancreatic origin and think about genetics sooner rather than later.

If you see a kid with recurrent pleural effusions and a history of abdominal trauma or earlier pancreatitis — stop and ask about family history, send pleural amylase, get MRCP, and loop in genetics. Finding a variant won’t always change acute care, but it will change the roadmap for that child and their family.
