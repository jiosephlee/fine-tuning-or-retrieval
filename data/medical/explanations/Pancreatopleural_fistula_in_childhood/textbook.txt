Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment of Recurrent Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion in a Child

The child presented with a week of progressive dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. On arrival he had mild tachypnea (respiratory rate ~20/min for age) and hypoxemia with oxygen saturation 88%–89% on room air; heart rate was 110/min. Physical examination localized the problem to the right hemithorax: reduced chest excursion on the right, markedly diminished tactile fremitus, stony‑dull percussion note over the right subscapular and infra‑axillary regions, and decreased breath sounds in the same areas. These findings are classic for a large pleural effusion and explain the child’s respiratory compromise.

Initial stabilization focused on respiratory support and empirical treatment while investigations were arranged. He was transferred to the PICU and given supplemental oxygen (mask at 5 L/min), and blood cultures were sent before empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin–tazobactam) were started—an appropriate early strategy when a significant pleural collection raises concern for infected fluid or empyema pending results. A chest X‑ray confirmed a large right pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle, and bedside lung ultrasound quantified a large anechoic collection estimated at about a litre; point‑of‑care ultrasound is valuable for real‑time volume estimation and for guiding safe thoracentesis.

Therapeutic and diagnostic thoracentesis produced almost 1 L of dark red (blood‑stained) pleural fluid and produced rapid clinical improvement: tachypnea settled and oxygen saturation rose to 95%–98%, permitting discharge after initial stabilization. The marked symptomatic response after drainage is typical when respiratory compromise is driven by mechanical lung compression. However, recurrent reaccumulation of a large unilateral effusion within days—necessitating repeat thoracentesis with similar hemorrhagic fluid—should immediately broaden the differential and prompt reconsideration of the working diagnosis.

Several clinical red flags in this case pointed away from routine pulmonary causes and toward an extracavitary source. First, the fluid was consistently hemorrhagic. Second, the effusion was large, unilateral (right‑sided) and rapidly recurrent despite adequate initial drainage. Third, systemic signs were minimal: there was no sustained fever, weight loss, or marked inflammatory response to indicate fulminant pulmonary infection or malignancy. These features should prompt targeted pleural fluid studies beyond routine cell counts and cultures—specifically measurement of pleural fluid amylase and lipase, cytology, and cross‑sectional imaging to look for an extrapulmonary origin such as pancreatitis or a fistulous tract.

This case also illustrates an anchoring pitfall that commonly delays correct diagnosis. A positive household history of tuberculosis and a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA biased clinicians toward tubercular pleuritis and led to empiric antitubercular therapy even though GeneXpert was negative and constitutional features of TB were absent. The initial focus on thoracic causes consumed much of the early workup until repeat admissions prompted re‑interviewing the child and eliciting a previously undisclosed history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle. That detail, together with the hemorrhagic recurrent effusion, ultimately led to cross‑sectional imaging and pleural fluid amylase testing that confirmed a pancreatopleural fistula.

Practical takeaways for initial assessment: in any child with a large, recurrent, unilateral hemorrhagic pleural effusion and relatively few systemic signs, always include extra‑thoracic causes in the differential. Early diagnostic steps that would have expedited diagnosis in this case are careful repeated history (including blunt abdominal trauma), prompt pleural fluid amylase/lipase measurement, and cross‑sectional imaging (contrast CT or MRCP) to identify pancreatic collections or fistulous tracts. Recognizing these red flags avoids inappropriate anchoring (for example, on tuberculosis) and guides the clinician to the correct, often non‑pulmonary, source of the effusion.

## Pathophysiology and Epidemiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula in Children

A pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is an abnormal communication between the pancreatic ductal system or a pancreatic pseudocyst and the pleural cavity. Mechanistically it follows rupture or disruption of the pancreatic duct or of an associated pseudocyst. When the disruption is posterior, pancreatic juice leaks into the retroperitoneal space and then follows lines of least resistance superiorly: it may track along the retroperitoneum into the mediastinum via the aortic or oesophageal hiatus and then enter the pleural cavity. Anterior ruptures tend to spill into the peritoneal cavity producing pancreatic ascites rather than a pleural effusion. Direct transdiaphragmatic erosion of a pseudocyst is another, less common route.

The fluid that accumulates in the pleural space in PPF is characteristically enzyme‑rich. Active pancreatic secretions contain very high concentrations of amylase and lipase; when these reach the pleura they produce large-volume, often rapidly recurrent effusions. Enzymatic proteolysis and local inflammation can damage pleural and vascular tissues, which helps explain why pleural fluid is frequently serosanguinous or frankly hemorrhagic, as seen in the presented child. A commonly used diagnostic threshold is a pleural fluid amylase greatly in excess of serum values — values ≥1,000 IU/L are strongly suggestive of pancreatic origin (in this case the pleural amylase was >6,000 IU/L).

Laterality of the effusion is determined by the route the leak takes and by local anatomy. Because the pancreatic tail lies on the left, left-sided effusions are more commonly reported; however, fluid can cross the midline in the retroperitoneum, preferentially track along one hiatus, or be directed by local adhesions or collections, producing right-sided or bilateral effusions. In the present case the collection tracked from the pancreatic tail via the para‑oesophageal route to the right pleural cavity, accounting for a right‑predominant effusion despite the pancreatic source.

PPF is rare in children. Published experience is limited (on the order of a few dozen pediatric reports), whereas many more cases have been described in adults. Overall PPF complicates a small fraction of pancreatitis cases (approximately 0.4% in mixed‑age series). The spectrum of causes differs by age: in adults chronic alcoholic pancreatitis predominates, while in children blunt abdominal trauma and hereditary or genetic forms of pancreatitis are important contributors. Blunt pancreatic injury (for example from bicycle handlebar trauma) is a well‑recognized trigger of duct disruption and was the likely initiating event in this child. Genetic predispositions to recurrent or chronic pancreatitis that increase the later risk of ductal disruption include PRSS1 and SPINK1 mutations and variants in CFTR and CTRC; such etiologies should be suspected when pancreatitis is recurrent or when there is a relevant family history.

An additional diagnostic nuance relates to amylase isoenzymes. Elevated pleural fluid amylase can occur in other conditions (for example oesophageal rupture, some malignancies) but the source differs: PPF yields predominantly pancreatic (P‑type) amylase, whereas salivary (S‑type) amylase predominates in other causes. Distinguishing isoenzymes can help confirm pancreatic origin when the clinical picture is unclear.

Clinically, PPF in children often wears a “pulmonary mask.” Large recurrent pleural effusions produce dyspnoea, chest pain and radiographic opacities that direct attention to the thorax, while abdominal symptoms may be absent or mild. This mismatch commonly delays recognition of the pancreatic source unless a high index of suspicion is maintained, especially when pleural fluid is hemorrhagic and amylase‑rich or when there is antecedent abdominal trauma. Recognizing these pathophysiologic and epidemiologic patterns helps prioritize appropriate biochemical testing (pleural and serum amylase/lipase) and targeted imaging (MRCP/CT, with ERCP reserved for diagnostic/therapeutic ductal evaluation), as illustrated in this case.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning: From Thoracic Symptoms to a Pancreatic Source

Recurrent, large unilateral pleural effusions in a child should prompt systematic reconsideration of the source when initial thoracic workup is unrevealing. In this case the clinical pattern — repeated massive right-sided effusions that were dark red, prompt symptomatic improvement after drainage, and little evidence of systemic infection — provided the first clues that the pleural problem might arise outside the chest. Key clinical triggers that should raise suspicion for a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) are: (1) recurrent, often massive pleural effusions (frequently unilateral), (2) hemorrhagic or highly enzyme‑rich fluid rather than purulent fluid, (3) only minimal systemic or pulmonary inflammatory signs, and (4) a history of pancreatitis or abdominal trauma (the latter was elicited here only after repeated admissions).

Pleural fluid analysis is central to shifting diagnostic thinking toward a pancreatic origin. A very high pleural fluid amylase — classically cited as ≥1000 U/L — is strongly suggestive of a pancreatic source; pleural fluid lipase is typically elevated as well and supports the diagnosis. In this child the pleural fluid amylase was 6,118 U/L and lipase 7,211 U/L, values that are far above infectious or other non‑pancreatic causes and effectively confirm pancreatic leakage into the pleural space when interpreted with clinical and imaging data. Cytology was negative for malignancy, and the absence of a predominantly neutrophilic exudate argued against empyema. It is also important to recognize limitations of individual tests: ADA can be mildly elevated in hemorrhagic or exudative effusions and is therefore not specific for tuberculosis in this context; the case illustrates this pitfall because the child initially received empirical antitubercular therapy despite a later negative GeneXpert and an overall clinical picture inconsistent with TB.

Imaging should progress from bedside to targeted cross‑sectional studies guided by evolving suspicion. A chest radiograph and point‑of‑care ultrasound are appropriate first steps to confirm and quantify effusion and guide safe thoracentesis or chest tube placement. When pleural fluid characteristics or the clinical course suggest an extrapulmonary or complex process, contrast CT is the next practical test: CT reliably delineates pleural collections, hydropneumothorax, consolidation and — importantly — can identify collections tracking into the mediastinum or retroperitoneum. CT demonstrates the pancreatic fistulous tract itself less often (fistula visualization rates quoted around 33–47%), but it is useful for showing pseudocysts or multiloculated collections and their anatomic course. In this patient CT demonstrated a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal/pleural space toward the pancreatic tail, which shifted the diagnostic focus to the pancreas.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the noninvasive imaging test best suited to define ductal anatomy and to visualize fistulous tracts; reported sensitivity is about 70–80% for fistula visualization. In this case MRCP identified a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail that tracked superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity with a narrow communication to the left pleural space and communication with the distal pancreatic duct — imaging findings that corroborated the biochemical evidence of pancreatic leakage and provided the anatomic explanation for recurrent right‑sided hemorrhagic effusions. When MRCP is inconclusive or when therapeutic endoscopic intervention is contemplated, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) serves as both a diagnostic and therapeutic gold standard; ERCP allows direct opacification of the ductal system, possible visualization of the fistulous tract, and placement of transpapillary stents to reduce ductal pressure and promote fistula closure.

A structured differential diagnosis remains necessary because other conditions can produce enzyme‑rich or bloody pleural effusions. In this child tuberculosis was considered because of family contact and mildly elevated ADA, but GeneXpert was negative and the overall presentation lacked systemic TB features. Empyema was unlikely owing to absence of fever and neutrophil‑predominant fluid. Malignancy was excluded by normal blood counts and negative cytology. Boerhaave’s syndrome was unlikely given the absence of forceful vomiting, severe chest pain, or alimentary symptoms and normal gastrointestinal transit. Diaphragmatic hernia, hepatic hydrothorax and renal causes were excluded by imaging and organ‑specific tests. The combination of extremely high pleural amylase/lipase, imaging that demonstrated a retroperitoneal/pancreatic collection tracking into the chest, and the later‑obtained history of blunt abdominal trauma provided a coherent explanation and allowed discontinuation of inappropriate antitubercular therapy.

Two diagnostic lessons from this case are practical and generalizable. First, measure pleural fluid amylase (and lipase) early when pleural effusion is recurrent, hemorrhagic, or discordant with respiratory infection; a very high value shifts the workup toward the pancreas. Second, maintain a low threshold for cross‑sectional abdominal and mediastinal imaging (contrast CT and MRCP) when pleural fluid biochemistry or clinical history suggest an extrapulmonary source. Eliciting a remote history of abdominal trauma — the critical inflection point in this case — and integrating biochemical and imaging findings allowed prompt diagnosis of PPF and appropriate conservative management.

## Management of Pancreatopleural Fistula: Conservative, Endoscopic, and Surgical Strategies

Acute care of a child who presents with a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) focuses first on respiratory stabilization and source control of the pleural collection. Provide supplemental oxygen and analgesia as needed and reserve antibiotics for cases with clear evidence of infection. Large, symptomatic or recurrent pleural effusions are drained promptly: ultrasound-guided diagnostic and therapeutic thoracentesis is appropriate for initial relief, but persistent or reaccumulating output usually requires placement of an intercostal drain (ICD) to allow continuous drainage and facilitate monitoring. In the case presented, repeated one‑litre, blood‑stained effusions and recurrence led to ICD insertion, which both controlled symptoms and provided a quantifiable measure of ongoing fistula output.

Conservative medical therapy is the usual first-line approach when there is no clear indication for immediate endoscopic or surgical intervention. The goals are to reduce pancreatic exocrine secretion and intraductal pressure so the ductal disruption or pseudocyst can heal, while maintaining nutrition and preventing complications. Key measures are bowel rest (nil per os), provision of nutrition by total parenteral nutrition (TPN) if enteral feeding would maintain pancreatic stimulation, and administration of somatostatin analogues to suppress exocrine pancreatic secretion. Octreotide dosing used in pediatric reports and in this case is in the range 10–40 µg/kg/day (the team used 30 µg/kg/day), typically given as a continuous infusion; the drug reduces pancreatic secretions and can lower intraductal pressure to promote fistula closure. Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) is often given concurrently to reduce the stimulus for endogenous secretion and aid nutrition. Thoracic drainage is continued during conservative care until output declines and the lung re-expands.

Conservative therapy is monitored closely and given a defined trial period. Most series propose attempting non‑operative therapy for about 2–3 weeks unless the patient’s condition worsens. During this period, follow chest tube output (volume and character) daily, monitor respiratory status and oxygen requirements, repeat chest radiography or ultrasound to document re-expansion, and track nutritional and laboratory parameters (weight, electrolytes, serum albumin, inflammatory markers, and trends in serum and pleural fluid amylase/lipase). Chest tube removal is considered when drainage has become minimal or ceased, the lung is radiographically re‑expanded, and there is no evidence of ongoing air leak or infection. In the reported child, output steadily declined over 10 days on NPO, TPN, octreotide and PERT until the ICD could be removed and the patient discharged.

Conservative therapy in children has a relatively low reported success rate; pediatric series describe successful nonsurgical closure in only about 12% of cases, and failure is more likely when there is ductal obstruction or an established ductal stricture. Therefore, lack of progressive improvement—persistent high-volume drainage, ongoing symptoms, or imaging that demonstrates a persistent ductal disruption—should prompt escalation to endoscopic therapy.

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) serves both diagnostic and therapeutic roles and is the logical next step when conservative therapy fails or imaging identifies a ductal leak or stenosis amenable to instrumentation. The principal endoscopic maneuvers are transpapillary stent placement across a leaking duct segment (to bridge the disruption and lower intraductal pressure) and balloon dilation of strictures when present; sphincterotomy may be performed per operator judgment. Stent therapy typically promotes fistula closure within 2–3 weeks; reported pediatric series show high technical and clinical success (case reports and small series have reported excellent outcomes, even up to 100% in selected cohorts), and stents are usually left in place for a period ranging from several weeks to a few months (commonly 4 weeks to 6 months). ERCP carries procedure‑related risks (post‑ERCP pancreatitis, infection, perforation, stent migration), so timing and candidacy should be individualized and performed by experienced pediatric endoscopists.

Surgical intervention is reserved for patients who fail endoscopic therapy, have a ductal disruption not amenable to stenting (for example a distal leak inaccessible by ERCP), or have other surgical indications such as infected pancreatic necrosis or complex pseudocysts. Procedures include distal pancreatectomy for disease confined to the tail, longitudinal pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow) for dilated ducts with long‑segment disease, or cystojejunostomy for large pseudocysts. Published pediatric surgical series report high success rates (approximately 93% in one review), operative mortality under 5%, and recurrence rates around 11%. As with endoscopy, risks and potential benefits must be weighed carefully and surgery should be performed at centers with pediatric pancreatic experience.

Anticipate and prevent complications during any management pathway: infection of the pleural space or bloodstream (particularly with TPN and central lines), nutritional deficits and micronutrient abnormalities, catheter problems (dislodgement, blockage), and recurrence of the fistula after apparent closure. Mitigation strategies include strict aseptic care of drains and central lines, monitoring and correcting electrolytes and protein status, early involvement of nutrition teams, and timely escalation from conservative to endoscopic or surgical therapy when indicated to avoid prolonged morbidity.

Applying these principles to the presented case: after recurrent symptomatic effusions and CT/MRCP evidence of a fistulous track to the pancreatic tail, the team placed an ICD for continuous drainage and initiated conservative management—NPO, TPN, pancreatic enzyme replacement and octreotide at 30 µg/kg/day. Close monitoring showed steadily falling drainage and clinical improvement such that the ICD could be removed after ten days and the child discharged without requiring ERCP or surgery. This outcome illustrates the role of early source control with drainage, a defined conservative trial with objective monitoring, and readiness to escalate if the fistula fails to close—an approach that balances the low but real chances of spontaneous closure in children with the significant morbidity of prolonged leakage.

## Outcome, Follow-up, and Key Clinical Lessons

Outcome in this child was excellent and straightforward: with prompt recognition of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) and institution of conservative measures (nil per os, total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogue, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy and intercostal drainage), pleural drainage fell to zero over ten days, the chest tube was removed and he was discharged symptom‑free. On outpatient follow‑up he remained asymptomatic, resumed full activities including school and play, and has had no recurrent pleural collections to date. This course illustrates that a well‑timed conservative strategy can be effective for select pediatric PPFs, especially when the ductal anatomy and clinical course permit healing without immediate intervention.

Follow‑up priorities after hospital discharge focus on early detection of recurrence, nutritional recovery and addressing modifiable risk factors. Clinically, the patient should be reviewed frequently in the early post‑discharge period (for example, monthly as in this case) with attention to any new respiratory symptoms (dyspnoea, chest pain, cough) or recurrent abdominal pain, nausea or vomiting. Basic surveillance may include a chest radiograph or bedside lung ultrasound if symptoms recur, and measurement of serum amylase/lipase when clinically indicated; repeat cross‑sectional imaging (MRCP or CT) is reserved for persistent or recurrent symptoms to delineate ductal anatomy or persistent collections. Nutritional follow‑up should monitor weight, growth parameters and nutritional repletion after TPN; pancreatic enzyme replacement should be reviewed for adequacy and adherence. If pancreatitis or fistula recurs, consider a genetic evaluation (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC and other relevant panels), because hereditary or structural causes are more likely to underlie recurrent pediatric pancreatitis and influence long‑term management.

Lifestyle and preventive counseling are important in this case because the child had severe obesity. Counsel families on a structured program of dietary improvement, portion control and graded physical activity tailored to the child’s age and abilities, with referral to a pediatric dietitian and to programs for weight management as appropriate. Weight reduction and a healthier diet improve overall metabolic risk and may reduce some triggers for pancreatic pathology in children (and are important for long‑term cardiometabolic health). Equally important in trauma‑associated cases is education about prompt reporting of abdominal injury and seeking medical attention early after blunt abdominal trauma, which can allow earlier identification of pancreatic injury and prevent delayed complications such as PPF.

Several clinical pearls emerge directly from this case that help prevent missed or delayed diagnoses. First, measure pleural fluid amylase whenever a pleural effusion is recurrent, large, haemorrhagic, or unexplained by initial thoracic workup; a markedly elevated pleural amylase (often >1,000 U/L) strongly suggests a pancreatic source. Second, actively re‑elicit a history of recent abdominal trauma or abdominal symptoms—children (and their families) may under‑report blunt injuries or abdominal pain, and the “pulmonary mask” of PPF commonly directs attention away from the abdomen. Third, do not anchor on tuberculosis or other thoracic diagnoses based on a single mildly elevated pleural ADA value or family exposure alone; ADA and epidemiologic clues must be integrated with pleural fluid biochemistry, microbiology (e.g., GeneXpert), and imaging. Once a pancreatic etiology is established, stop unnecessary anti‑tubercular or prolonged empiric pulmonary therapies to avoid harms from inappropriate treatment.

When PPF is suspected, pursue diagnostic efficiency with appropriate imaging: MRCP is the preferred noninvasive test to visualize pancreatic duct anatomy and the fistulous tract and should be prioritized when pleural fluid amylase is high or when trauma/acute pancreatitis is suspected. CT can identify collections, pseudocysts and other complications but is less sensitive for delineating the tract itself. ERCP remains the gold standard when therapeutic ductal intervention (transpapillary stenting) is being considered, but because ERCP is invasive it is typically used after MRCP or when conservative measures fail or when stenting is planned.

Finally, adopt a stepwise, escalation strategy for management. Conservative care—bowel rest, nutritional support (TPN when necessary), somatostatin analogue (eg, octreotide as used in this case), pancreatic enzyme replacement and adequate pleural drainage—should be attempted first in stable patients and monitored closely for 2–3 weeks. If high output persists, if ductal disruption or stenosis amenable to endoscopic therapy is demonstrated, or if conservative therapy fails, ERCP with transpapillary stent placement is the next step because it lowers intraductal pressure and promotes fistula closure. Surgical options (distal pancreatectomy, pancreaticojejunostomy, cystojejunostomy) are reserved for failed endoscopic therapy, inaccessible ductal anatomy, or complications that require definitive drainage or resection; surgery in pediatric series has had high success but carries greater morbidity. Tailor escalation to the child’s anatomy, clinical response and local expertise.

In short, this case highlights that timely consideration of a pancreatic source for recurrent, haemorrhagic pleural effusions—combined with targeted pleural fluid testing and early MRCP—can lead to efficient, nonoperative resolution and avoid unnecessary pulmonary treatments. Regular clinical follow‑up, attention to nutrition and obesity management, and a low threshold for genetic testing if pancreatitis recurs will optimize long‑term outcomes.
