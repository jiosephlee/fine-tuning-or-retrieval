# Pancreatopleural Fistula in Childhood: Teaching Through a Case of Recurrent Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion

## The Case

A boy in late childhood presented with progressive right-sided chest pain, dyspnea, and recurrent large pleural effusions that temporarily improved after thoracenteses. The pleural fluid was consistently dark red. He had no fever, weight loss, or chronic cough, though he had a family contact with treated pulmonary tuberculosis. On examination there was hypoxia, decreased right-sided breath sounds, and stony dullness to percussion; the abdomen was soft and non-tender. His body habitus was notable for severe obesity. Initial chest radiography and bedside ultrasonography confirmed a large right pleural effusion, and thoracentesis yielded approximately one liter of blood-stained exudate, with symptomatic relief.

He re-presented twice with similar symptoms and repeat effusions. A more detailed history disclosed blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar one month prior. Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest demonstrated right hydropneumothorax, patchy consolidation, and a multiloculated collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region into the upper left retroperitoneum toward the pancreatic tail. MR cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) revealed pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection arising from the distal pancreatic duct and extending superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow communication to the left pleura. Both serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase were markedly elevated (pleural amylase >6000 U/L; lipase >7000 U/L). An intercostal chest tube was placed. The patient was managed with bowel rest (nil per os), total parenteral nutrition, octreotide infusion, and pancreatic enzyme replacement. Over 10 days, pleural drainage diminished and ceased; the chest tube was removed, and he was discharged well, with no recurrence on follow-up.

This case illustrates an uncommon but important cause of recurrent pleural effusion in children—pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)—and underscores the hazard of anchoring on thoracic diagnoses when the pathology lies below the diaphragm.

## Initial Clinical Approach to Recurrent Pleural Effusion in a Child

Recurrent unilateral pleural effusion in a child should prompt a systematic approach:

1) Stabilize and relieve respiratory compromise. Large effusions warrant therapeutic drainage to improve oxygenation and alleviate work of breathing. Ultrasound guidance optimizes safety and yield.

2) Classify the effusion. Apply Light’s criteria to distinguish exudate from transudate. In children, most recurrent unilateral effusions are exudative and demand a search for infection, malignancy, autoimmune disease, or less common entities such as chylothorax, hemothorax, and amylase-rich effusions.

3) Let the fluid lead the way. Bedside fluid appearance (frank pus, milky, serosanguineous, frankly bloody) and targeted tests guide the differential:
- Cell count and differential; protein, LDH; glucose and pH; Gram stain and culture
- Triglyceride and cholesterol for suspected chylothorax/pseudochylothorax
- Hematocrit for hemothorax (pleural fluid hematocrit ≥50% of peripheral suggests hemothorax)
- Cytology for malignancy
- Adenosine deaminase (ADA) and mycobacterial testing in TB-endemic settings, recognizing that ADA may be falsely elevated in empyema, rheumatoid pleuritis, and hemorrhagic effusions
- Amylase and lipase if clinical clues suggest pancreatic or esophageal source, especially in recurrent, large-volume, or hemorrhagic effusions

4) Revisit the history and expand the field. Abdominal pain, trauma (particularly bicycle handlebar injuries), prior pancreatitis, alcohol exposure (rare in children), gallstones, hypertriglyceridemia, or cystic fibrosis should be queried. Dysphagia, forceful emesis, and chest pain raise concern for esophageal perforation (Boerhaave syndrome). Constitutional symptoms, chronic cough, and TB exposure warrant consideration of tuberculous pleurisy.

In this case, repeated large, hemorrhagic, right-sided effusions with dark red fluid, absence of fever or neutrophilic exudate, and elevated pleural amylase/lipase redirected attention from the pleura to the pancreas. The subsequently elicited history of blunt abdominal trauma was pivotal.

## Differential Diagnosis of Amylase-Rich or Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion

Marked pleural fluid amylase elevation (>1000 IU/L) is highly suggestive of pancreatic pleural effusion but is not pathognomonic. Causes include:

- Pancreatopleural fistula from acute or chronic pancreatitis, pseudocyst rupture, pancreatic duct disruption, or trauma. Pleural amylase is typically pancreatic P-type; lipase is also elevated.

- Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave syndrome). Amylase is usually salivary S-type; lipase is not elevated. Clinical context includes forceful emesis, severe chest pain, mediastinal emphysema, and systemic toxicity.

- Malignancy (especially lung adenocarcinoma, pancreatic carcinoma, metastatic disease). Both salivary and pancreatic isoenzymes have been reported; cytology and imaging aid diagnosis.

- Rarely, tuberculous pleuritis can present with mildly elevated amylase but typically not to the levels seen in pancreatic leaks; ADA may be high.

In hemorrhagic effusions, differentials include malignancy, pulmonary embolism, trauma, and occasionally inflammatory exudates. In this case, absence of malignant cells, nondiagnostic TB testing, and the imaging-defined pancreatic–pleural communication supported PPF.

## Pathophysiology: How the Pancreas Floods the Pleura

Understanding PPF requires an appreciation of pancreatic ductal anatomy, pancreatic injury patterns, and the potential spaces linking abdomen to thorax.

The main pancreatic duct drains exocrine secretions rich in proenzymes and bicarbonate. Disruption can occur due to:
- Acute pancreatitis with enzymatic autodigestion
- Chronic pancreatitis with ductal strictures and pseudocyst formation
- Blunt abdominal trauma compressing the pancreas against the vertebral column, often injuring the neck/body or tail; handlebar injuries in children are classic

When ductal integrity is compromised, secretions escape. The direction of egress determines the clinical syndrome:
- Anterior ductal rupture releases fluid into the peritoneal cavity, causing pancreatic ascites.
- Posterior rupture releases fluid into the retroperitoneum. The retroperitoneal collection follows fascial planes along the path of least resistance cranially. Through the diaphragmatic hiatuses—usually the esophageal or aortic hiatus—the collection can extend into the posterior mediastinum and rupture into the pleural space, establishing an internal fistula between the pancreatic ductal system and the pleural cavity.

Once established, a PPF behaves like a low-resistance diversion for pancreatic juice. Ongoing exocrine secretion maintains high-volume effusion, which can be unilateral (classically left, but right-sided or bilateral occur depending on the path of extension). High amylase and lipase concentrations in pleural fluid reflect active leakage. The effusion can be hemorrhagic due to enzymatic irritation of pleural capillaries and associated inflammation. Persistent losses lead to dyspnea, nutritional compromise, and risk of superinfection.

Two anatomic patterns have therapeutic implications:
- Partial duct disruption or a leak associated with a pseudocyst that still communicates with the main duct: often amenable to endoscopic decompression and stenting.
- Disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome (DPDS), where the upstream pancreas is severed from the main duct (e.g., after necrosis or transection): durable endoscopic control is less likely, and surgery may be required.

In pediatrics, genetic predispositions to pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) are important in recurrent or chronic cases; trauma is a frequent nonhereditary trigger. Obesity can worsen pancreatitis severity and complicate respiratory mechanics during large effusions.

## Diagnostic Workup

Pleural fluid analysis
- Expect an exudate by Light’s criteria with very high amylase (often >1000 IU/L) and elevated lipase. Glucose and pH may be low if secondary inflammation is present, but not to the degree seen in empyema.
- Cytology helps exclude malignancy; cultures rule out infection.
- If esophageal perforation is a concern, contrast esophagography and evaluation for mediastinal air are indicated and pleural fluid often shows a low pH and food particles; amylase is salivary type and lipase is not elevated.

Serum tests
- Serum amylase and lipase may be variably elevated; paradoxically, levels can be modest if pancreatic juice preferentially drains away via the fistula, decompressing the ductal system.
- Evaluate for pancreatitis etiologies as indicated: triglycerides, calcium, liver chemistries, autoimmune markers, and genetic testing for recurrent/chronic presentations.

Imaging
- Chest radiography and ultrasonography confirm effusion and guide drainage; ultrasonography can demonstrate septations but has limited sensitivity for fistulous tracts.
- Contrast-enhanced CT of chest and abdomen helps identify pancreatic inflammation, pseudocysts, ductal dilation, and retroperitoneal tracks; direct visualization of a fistula is limited but suggestive tracking collections, as in this case, are highly informative.
- MRCP provides noninvasive delineation of pancreatic duct anatomy and can visualize fistulous tracts in 70–80% of cases. Secretin-enhanced MRCP increases sensitivity for subtle leaks and strictures by stimulating pancreatic secretion.
- ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning by defining the site of duct disruption, ductal strictures, and allowing intervention. In children, cannulation success and visualization rates vary by expertise (fistula visualized in roughly 40–80%). ERCP is typically reserved for failure of initial conservative therapy or when MRCP suggests a targetable duct abnormality.

Special studies
- Amylase isoenzyme typing (P-type vs S-type) can distinguish pancreatic from salivary sources but is not universally available; clinical context and imaging usually suffice.
- In children with recurrent pancreatitis or an unclear etiology, consider genetic testing (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) and sweat chloride testing to inform long-term management.

Diagnostic pearls from the case
- Mildly elevated pleural ADA with hemorrhagic fluid and negative molecular TB testing should not anchor the diagnosis on tuberculosis; ADA is an adjunctive test with known false positives.
- Right-sided predominance does not exclude PPF; retroperitoneal collections can ascend through the esophageal hiatus and favor the right pleural space.

## Management Principles

Goals are to:
- Stabilize the patient and drain the pleural space to relieve respiratory compromise.
- Reduce pancreatic exocrine secretion and intraductal pressure to promote closure of the fistula.
- Treat or prevent infection.
- Address the underlying ductal pathology when needed.

Initial conservative therapy
- Pleural drainage: Large effusions warrant chest tube drainage when respiratory distress, hypoxemia, or rapid reaccumulation occurs. Serial therapeutic thoracentesis may suffice in selected stable patients but is often impractical given high recurrence.
- Pancreatic rest: Bowel rest reduces pancreatic stimulation. In children, early enteral nutrition via nasojejunal feeding distal to the ligament of Treitz is preferred when feasible, as it minimizes pancreatic secretion and preserves gut integrity; total parenteral nutrition is appropriate when enteral feeding is not tolerated or not available.
- Somatostatin analogues: Octreotide reduces pancreatic exocrine output. Pediatric dosing ranges from 1–4 mcg/kg/hour by continuous infusion or 2–4 mcg/kg subcutaneously every 8 hours; in this case, 10–40 mcg/kg/day continuous infusion was used with success. Monitor for hyperglycemia, gallbladder sludge, and GI side effects.
- Analgesia and supportive care: Multimodal pain management, careful fluid resuscitation, and monitoring for systemic inflammatory response.
- Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy: Indicated for exocrine insufficiency or to reduce postprandial pancreatic stimulation once advancing the diet.

Antibiotics
- Not routinely required for sterile pancreatic collections or sterile pleural effusions. Initiate when there is evidence of infected collections or empyema (fever, neutrophilic pleocytosis, low pleural fluid glucose/pH, positive cultures), or if the patient is clinically septic.

Assess response and decide when to escalate
- Many pediatric PPFs will not close with conservative care alone; reported success is modest. Nevertheless, a trial of 1–2 weeks is reasonable if there is no major ductal obstruction and the patient is clinically improving.
- Consider earlier escalation to endoscopic therapy when:
  - Persistent high-output pleural drainage (>150–200 mL/day in children) beyond several days
  - Failure of drainage to decline with appropriate conservative measures after 1–2 weeks
  - MRCP suggests a dominant ductal stricture or leak that is likely to respond to decompression
  - Recurrent effusion after initial resolution

Endoscopic therapy (ERCP)
- Pancreatic sphincterotomy and transpapillary stent placement bridge the leak, reduce intraductal pressure, and redirect flow into the duodenum. Stents (typically 5–7 Fr in children) are left in place for weeks to months and exchanged as needed.
- Success rates in mixed-age series are high (>80–90%) and are excellent in pediatric case series when a stent can be placed across the disruption. Post-ERCP pancreatitis and anesthesia risks are important considerations in children.

Surgical options
- Indications include failed endoscopic therapy, non-bridgable duct disruptions (especially DPDS), distal ductal obstruction, or complications (infected/organized collections).
- Procedures are tailored to anatomy:
  - Distal pancreatectomy for tail/body disruption beyond the neck
  - Longitudinal pancreaticojejunostomy (Puestow) for dilated duct with multiple strictures
  - Cystogastrostomy or cystojejunostomy for mature pseudocysts communicating with the duct
- Operative mortality is low in specialized centers, and success in closing fistulas is high.

Follow-up and prevention
- Gradual advancement of diet, reassessment for exocrine insufficiency, and continuation of enzyme supplementation as needed.
- Address modifiable risk factors: weight management, avoidance of abdominal trauma in sports until healing, and evaluation for genetic predispositions in recurrent disease.
- Imaging follow-up is individualized; clinical resolution and absence of recurrent effusion often suffice. Persistent or recurrent symptoms warrant repeat MRCP or ERCP evaluation.

Application to the case
- The child’s effusion was controlled with chest tube drainage, pancreatic rest, TPN, and octreotide, obviating the need for ERCP or surgery. The imaging suggested a leak communicating with the distal duct and no severe proximal obstruction, a scenario in which conservative therapy may succeed. Close monitoring ensured that escalation would be available if drainage persisted.

## Special Considerations in Children

- Etiologies differ: in pediatrics, genetic mutations (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) and trauma are more prominent than alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis.
- Nutritional vulnerability: prioritize enteral nutrition when possible; consider micronutrient supplementation and monitor growth parameters.
- Radiation and anesthesia: favor MRCP over repeated CT when feasible; ERCP requires pediatric anesthesia expertise.
- Psychosocial factors: delayed disclosure of trauma may occur; a supportive environment facilitates accurate history-taking.

## Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

- Anchoring on thoracic disease. Recurrent, large, unilateral effusions—especially hemorrhagic and unaccompanied by systemic infection—should prompt measurement of pleural amylase and consideration of abdominal sources.

- Overreliance on ADA for TB diagnosis. Mildly elevated ADA in hemorrhagic or inflammatory effusions can mislead; integrate ADA with clinical, microbiologic, and imaging data.

- Missing the trauma history. Bicycle handlebar injuries are classic for pancreatic injury; eliciting this history may require sensitive, repeated questioning.

- Delayed escalation. Prolonged conservative therapy without declining drainage may prolong hospitalization; early MRCP to define duct anatomy helps plan timely ERCP or surgery.

- Inadequate nutritional support. Pancreatic leaks can persist if enteral stimulation continues inadvertently; ensure true pancreatic rest early, transitioning thoughtfully to jejunal feeds or TPN.

## Clinical Pearls

- Think below the diaphragm in a child with recurrent, massive, unilateral pleural effusions—especially if the fluid is hemorrhagic or amylase-rich.

- Pleural fluid amylase >1000 IU/L with elevated lipase strongly suggests a pancreatic source; salivary-type amylase with normal lipase points toward esophageal perforation or malignancy.

- MRCP noninvasively maps fistulas and ductal anatomy; secretin-enhanced studies improve sensitivity.

- Conservative therapy—drainage, pancreatic rest, and octreotide—can close selected fistulas within 1–3 weeks, but many pediatric cases will ultimately require ERCP with pancreatic duct stenting.

- In disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome, durable fistula closure usually requires surgery.

- ADA is an adjunct, not a diagnosis; hemorrhagic effusions can yield falsely elevated values.

- Handlebar injuries are a red flag for pancreatic trauma; an early CT abdomen with pancreatic protocol is warranted when suspected.

## Take-Home Points

- Pancreatopleural fistula is a rare but important cause of recurrent pleural effusion in children; presentation is often respiratory, with abdominal symptoms absent or subtle.

- Pathophysiology involves ductal disruption with retroperitoneal tracking of pancreatic secretions through diaphragmatic hiatuses into the pleural space.

- Diagnosis rests on recognizing an amylase-rich exudative effusion, confirming fistulous anatomy with MRCP/CT, and, when needed, delineating the duct with ERCP.

- Management is stepwise: stabilize and drain, rest the pancreas with nutritional support and octreotide, and escalate to endoscopic or surgical interventions based on response and ductal anatomy.

- A meticulous history, including recent abdominal trauma, and a broad differential prevent misdiagnosis and unnecessary treatments.
