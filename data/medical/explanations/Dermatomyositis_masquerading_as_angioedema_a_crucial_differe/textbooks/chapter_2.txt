Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology and Serologic Phenotypes of Dermatomyositis (SAE1-Positive Focus)

Dermatomyositis (DM) is best understood as an immune-mediated microangiopathy of skin and muscle in which humoral and innate immune mechanisms converge on the endothelium of endomysial and dermal capillaries. A central histopathologic hallmark is endothelial injury with loss of capillaries and deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) on endomysial capillaries. This complement activation—thought to be triggered by immune complexes and autoantibody–antigen interactions at the microvascular surface—produces focal ischemia of muscle fibers that is most conspicuous in the perifascicular region and manifests as perifascicular atrophy, necrosis, and regeneration on muscle biopsy. The biopsy in this case, with foci of perifascicular necrotic and regenerating fibers and demonstrable C5b‑9 deposition, is a classic illustration of this complement‑mediated microvascular injury pathway and distinguishes DM from other inflammatory myopathies in which necrosis is prominent but capillary deposition of MAC is absent or minimal (for example, immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy).

Overlaying the complement pathway, type I interferon (IFN‑α/β) signalling is a molecular signature of DM that links vascular injury, myofiber dysfunction, and the striking cutaneous manifestations. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells and other innate immune elements produce type I IFNs in affected tissue, upregulating interferon‑stimulated genes (MxA is the prototypic marker used on immunohistochemistry). Type I IFNs promote endothelial activation and dysfunction, increase expression of MHC and adhesion molecules, and recruit inflammatory cells; they also contribute to keratinocyte and mucocutaneous changes that produce heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, shawl and V‑neck signs, and periungual telangiectasia. The prominence of skin disease in many DM patients therefore reflects this interferon‑driven cutaneous tropism in tandem with microvascular injury.

The serologic landscape of DM is heterogeneous and clinically informative. Myositis‑specific antibodies (MSAs) stratify patients into phenotypic subgroups with differing risks for interstitial lung disease (ILD), malignancy, cutaneous severity, and pattern/timing of muscle involvement. Anti–small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti‑SAE1) antibodies target the heterodimeric SAE E1 enzyme required for sumoylation, a post‑translational modification that regulates nuclear transport and transcriptional responses. The exact pathogenic role of anti‑SAE1 is not fully established; hypotheses include direct interference with sumoylation pathways in skin and muscle cells or, more likely, that SAE1 autoimmunity marks a distinct immunologic milieu characterized by prominent type I IFN activity and B‑cell–driven autoantibody responses. Clinically, SAE1‑positive DM often presents with early, conspicuous cutaneous disease and a relatively high frequency of extramuscular complications: dysphagia (reported in roughly 40–50% of series), ILD (approximately 40%), and a measurable association with malignancy (estimates commonly in the mid‑teens percent range). Importantly, myopathy in SAE1 disease may be delayed or variable in severity relative to the skin findings, so a high index of suspicion is required when skin features dominate.

Subcutaneous oedema or “pseudoangioedema” is a recognized but uncommon manifestation across DM serotypes and can be the presenting feature when it is localized to the face or neck. The pathophysiology of this oedema is distinct from histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema: inflammatory capillary leak from microvascular injury, increased vascular permeability driven by cytokines (including type I IFNs), and local myofasciitis or muscle inflammation with interstitial fluid accumulation are the dominant mechanisms. Lymphatic dysfunction related to perimuscular inflammation may further impede fluid removal. Although classic reports associate facial/neck oedema more frequently with anti‑MDA5 and anti‑TIF1‑γ antibodies, SAE1‑positive patients can and do develop significant soft‑tissue swelling, as the present case demonstrates. Recognizing this helps avoid misdiagnosis (for example, Ludwig’s angina or idiopathic angioedema) and prompts evaluation for myopathy and immunologic markers.

Extramuscular involvement in DM follows from the same vascular and inflammatory processes. Dysphagia results from weakness of pharyngeal constrictors and upper oesophageal striated musculature or from cricopharyngeal dysfunction, and it carries important sequelae—aspiration pneumonitis and nutritional compromise—requiring early assessment with swallowing studies. ILD in DM reflects inflammatory injury of the pulmonary interstitium; its prevalence and severity vary with MSA subtype (notably high with anti‑MDA5 and not infrequent with anti‑SAE1) and dramatically influence prognosis and therapeutic choices. Cardiac involvement—myocarditis, conduction disturbances, and arrhythmias—occurs less commonly but merits surveillance because of its potential morbidity. Malignancy screening is guided by age, specific risk factors, and serology: dermatomyositis overall is a paraneoplastic syndrome in a subset of patients, and SAE1 positivity confers an intermediate to high risk in many published series, prompting directed cancer evaluation and longitudinal surveillance.

Laboratory and biopsy features reflect these mechanisms. Serum muscle enzymes (CK, AST, ALT, LDH) can range from near normal to markedly elevated; CK often correlates with active myonecrosis, and extreme elevations—as in the case described—signal severe muscle injury but do not alone determine prognosis. Autoantibody panels (often performed by immunoblot/line assay or immunoprecipitation) that identify MSAs—anti‑SAE among them—provide specificity for DM and help predict phenotype. Muscle histology in DM classically demonstrates perifascicular atrophy and necrosis, perivascular and perimysial inflammatory infiltrates (often with histiocytes and B cells), and complement deposition in capillaries. These features help differentiate DM from IMNM (pronounced necrosis with sparse inflammation and association with SRP/HMGCR antibodies) and from inclusion body myositis (endomysial CD8+ T‑cell invasion, rimmed vacuoles, and progressive distal/proximal pattern in older patients).

Therapeutically, an appreciation of DM pathophysiology informs practice. High‑dose glucocorticoids remain first‑line for rapidly progressive muscle or airway‑threatening disease because they suppress broad inflammatory cascades. Because B cells and autoantibodies participate in complement activation, B‑cell depletion (rituximab) and IVIG—effective in blocking Fc‑mediated processes and modulating complement—are rational choices for severe or refractory disease and for complications such as dysphagia. Steroid‑sparing agents (mycophenolate, azathioprine, methotrexate) are selected according to comorbidities (for example, avoiding methotrexate in active ILD), and targeted trials of interferon pathway inhibitors are an active area of research that directly addresses the IFN‑driven biology of DM. Finally, recognizing that subcutaneous oedema in DM is inflammatory rather than histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated prevents unnecessary trials of antihistamines or C1‑inhibitor therapies and directs clinicians to immunosuppression and supportive measures (airway protection, nutrition, aspiration prevention).

In summary, dermatomyositis is a complement‑ and interferon‑driven microangiopathy with distinct serologic phenotypes. Anti‑SAE1 positivity defines a syndrome with prominent cutaneous disease, frequent dysphagia and ILD, variable timing and severity of myositis, and a modestly increased cancer risk. The clinical and histologic features seen in the presented patient—marked CK elevation, perifascicular necrosis, C5b‑9 deposition, diffuse inflammatory muscle uptake on PET/MRI, dysphagia and neck soft‑tissue oedema—are coherent with the underlying vascular and interferon‑mediated mechanisms and explain both the presentation and the rationale for aggressive, multimodal immunotherapy.
