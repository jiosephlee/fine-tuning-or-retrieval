Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Chapter 4: ## Management: Acute Stabilization and Disease Control

The immediate priorities in this patient were to suppress the intense immune-mediated muscle inflammation threatening airway and swallowing function, prevent complications of severe myositis and its treatment, and provide supportive care for nutrition and aspiration risk. Because his neck swelling reflected active cervical myositis with bulbar involvement rather than histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, management focused on rapid immunomodulation combined with airway surveillance and multidisciplinary supportive measures.

High‑dose intravenous corticosteroids were used for rapid control of inflammation: the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days, followed by an oral prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg daily and weaned by 5 mg every 2–3 weeks guided by clinical strength and CK. This “pulse” strategy is standard for severe dermatomyositis with threatened airway or marked weakness because it produces a rapid anti‑inflammatory effect; the subsequent high-dose oral phase maintains disease suppression while allowing gradual steroid reduction as objective (strength, CK) and subjective improvement are demonstrated.

IVIG was employed early in this case because of the severity of dysphagia and widespread myositis. A loading regimen of 2 g/kg total given over 5 days (split doses) followed by monthly cycles is a commonly used approach in refractory or severe dermatomyositis, particularly when bulbar dysfunction places the patient at high risk of aspiration. IVIG has multiple immunomodulatory actions (neutralizing pathogenic antibodies, Fc receptor modulation, complement inhibition) and is supported by clinical experience for rapidly improving bulbar symptoms and steroid‑resistant disease. In this patient IVIG was continued monthly as a steroid‑sparing adjunct while other maintenance immunotherapies were initiated.

For steroid‑sparing long‑term disease control the team selected mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) at 1 g twice daily. MMF is effective and generally well tolerated for dermatomyositis and was chosen here for patient preference and safety considerations. Methotrexate—another common option—was avoided because the patient had abnormal pulmonary function and radiological evidence compatible with aspiration pneumonitis; methotrexate can exacerbate or be associated with drug‑related pulmonary toxicity and was therefore not considered optimal. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not started because the patient tolerated MMF and was already receiving multiple agents; choice of steroid‑sparing therapy should be individualized to comorbidity, tolerability and concomitant lung disease.

Because the disease was antibody‑positive and clinically severe, rituximab (1 g intravenously, repeated at 2 weeks) was added as B‑cell depletion therapy. Rituximab is commonly used as an add‑on in refractory or severe myositis and in seropositive patients; clinicians should counsel that its therapeutic onset may be delayed (weeks to months) and that clinical improvement often accrues over time, so it is usually combined with other faster‑acting modalities (steroids, IVIG) during the initial period.

Airway and swallowing management were integral throughout admission and follow‑up. ENT performed flexible nasendoscopy to assess upper airway anatomy and secretions; vigilant monitoring for evolving obstruction is necessary because severe cervical myositis can impair bulbar and strap muscle function. Speech‑language pathology guided diet modifications, swallowing therapy and recommended enteral feeding when aspiration risk was high. This patient required nasogastric feeding during the inpatient stay and for approximately 2 months afterwards because of severe pharyngeal dysphagia and documented aspiration on barium swallow. Enteral access should be escalated to percutaneous gastrostomy if prolonged feeding is anticipated.

Before and during intensive immunosuppression the team completed standard infectious screening and arranged prophylaxis. Baseline tests included hepatitis B and C serology, HIV, VZV and CMV serology and an assessment for latent tuberculosis (QuantiFERON and sputum AFB when indicated). Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis was started—trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX) is first line—but was stopped when the patient developed hemolytic anaemia and prophylaxis was switched to nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks. Clinicians should weigh risks and benefits of PJP prophylaxis and choose alternatives (aerosolized pentamidine, atovaquone) if TMP‑SMX is contraindicated or not tolerated; monitor blood counts and renal function while on immunosuppressants.

Response and safety monitoring combined clinical, laboratory and functional measures. Serial quantitative assessments of proximal muscle strength and CK were used to track disease activity (CK fell from 16,184 U/L to normal within weeks in this case). Liver enzymes and LDH were followed because of muscle breakdown and drug toxicity risks; full blood counts and renal function were monitored for mycophenolate and IVIG adverse effects. When rituximab is used, immunoglobulin levels and infection surveillance should be serially assessed because hypogammaglobulinaemia can develop. For IVIG, monitor for infusion reactions, thrombotic risk and renal impairment. Pulmonary function tests and chest imaging were repeated to assess aspiration sequelae and to guide the decision to avoid methotrexate; these also inform longer‑term interstitial lung disease surveillance.

Given the established association between dermatomyositis and malignancy, a targeted cancer surveillance plan was instituted in line with IMACS guidance. Because this patient had major risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and intermediate risk features (male sex, SAE1 antibody), an initial malignancy screen included CT chest and PET (both negative for occult malignancy), tumour markers and plans for gastrointestinal endoscopy (gastroscopy and colonoscopy) for occult lesions. Ongoing surveillance is symptom‑driven and includes interval history and examination, age‑ and risk‑appropriate imaging and endoscopy as indicated; frequency is individualized based on initial findings and evolving risk.

Finally, multidisciplinary coordination underpinned successful care: rheumatology/immunology led immunosuppression decisions, ENT provided airway surveillance, neurology and pathology confirmed diagnostic studies (muscle biopsy), pulmonology managed restrictive/aspiration issues and PFTs, speech pathology and dietetics managed dysphagia and nutrition, and infectious diseases advised screening and prophylaxis. Regular communication between teams facilitated timely de‑escalation of steroids as CK and strength improved and guided adjustment of IVIG frequency and MMF dosing during recovery. This coordinated approach allowed recovery of strength, resolution of cervical swelling and progressive restoration of swallowing over the ensuing months.
