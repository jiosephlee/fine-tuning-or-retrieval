Chapter 5: ## Management of Dermatomyositis with Severe Oropharyngeal Involvement

Severe oropharyngeal involvement in dermatomyositis — manifesting as marked cervical soft‑tissue swelling, dysphagia, aspiration and potential airway compromise — is an uncommon but clinically important presentation that changes priorities in management. The therapeutic strategy must address three parallel goals: rapidly suppress active immune‑mediated inflammation to preserve airway and swallowing musculature; secure airway protection and nutrition; and prevent or mitigate complications of extensive immunosuppression. The case presented above illustrates these principles: prompt recognition of myositis as the driver of neck swelling and dysphagia, early high‑dose immunosuppression (pulse steroids and IVIG) and coordinated supportive care produced clinical remission and recovery of swallowing function.

Initial disposition and multidisciplinary coordination
Patients with significant dysphagia, evidence of aspiration, or any concern for airway compromise should be admitted. Early involvement of a multidisciplinary team is essential: rheumatology/immunology or neuromuscular medicine to direct immunotherapy; ENT to evaluate the airway (flexible nasendoscopy, assessment for upper airway obstruction), anaesthesia/intensive care for airway monitoring and potential intubation/tracheostomy; speech‑language pathology (SLP) and dietetics for swallow assessment and nutritional planning; pulmonology if aspiration pneumonitis or interstitial lung disease (ILD) is suspected; and infectious disease for prophylaxis and screening. In patients with progressive neck edema, ICU monitoring is appropriate if there is evolving stridor, hypoxia, rapidly progressive edema, or need for frequent airway reassessment.

Rapid immunomodulation for severe disease
When myositis threatens airway or bulbar function, early aggressive immunomodulation is indicated to halt inflammation and limit irreversible muscle injury.

- Glucocorticoids: For life‑ or function‑threatening disease, give intravenous methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg daily for 3 days (pulse therapy), then convert to high‑dose oral prednisone (approximately 1 mg/kg/day, often capped around 60 mg/day) with a gradual taper guided by clinical response and laboratory markers. The pulse dose helps reduce inflammation quickly; the oral taper should be individualized and cautious because relapse is common with overly rapid tapering. Monitor for steroid adverse effects (hyperglycaemia, hypertension, infection, steroid myopathy) and institute prophylactic measures discussed below.

- Early steroid‑sparing therapy: Because prolonged high‑dose steroids carry substantial toxicity, a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant should usually be started early once infection is excluded. Choice depends on comorbidities and the clinical phenotype:
  - Mycophenolate mofetil is widely used for muscle and lung involvement and was selected in the case above because of tolerability and daily oral dosing.
  - Methotrexate is effective for muscle disease but should be used cautiously or avoided when ILD is present or when pulmonary fibrosis risk is high.
  - Azathioprine and tacrolimus are alternatives based on tolerance and organ‑specific disease.
  - Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, cyclosporine) have a role, particularly for refractory disease or when steroid‑sparing is required.
  - Cyclophosphamide is reserved for severe, progressive ILD or vasculopathic disease because of its toxicity profile.

- Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG): IVIG has proven efficacy in dermatomyositis and can be particularly beneficial for refractory muscle disease and dysphagia. A common regimen is an initial loading dose of 2 g/kg total (often given over 2–5 days), followed by maintenance cycles of 2 g/kg given every 4–6 weeks as clinically indicated. IVIG is often used early in severe bulbar disease because of its relatively rapid effect and steroid‑sparing potential.

- B‑cell–directed therapy (rituximab): Rituximab is used for refractory cases or when rapid steroid reduction is needed. Regimens include 1 g intravenously on day 0 and 14, or 375 mg/m2 weekly for 4 doses. Evidence is mixed but many patients with refractory inflammatory myopathies respond, and rituximab is a reasonable option when first‑line agents and IVIG are inadequate or contraindicated.

Choice and sequencing of agents should be individualized, and combining modalities (eg, steroids + steroid‑sparing agent + IVIG ± rituximab) is common in severe presentations. In the case above, pulse steroids followed by oral prednisone, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab were used successfully.

Airway and dysphagia management
Bulbar muscle weakness and pharyngeal inflammation raise immediate concerns for aspiration and malnutrition.

- Assessment: Perform bedside swallow screening, and if impairment is suspected, proceed to instrumental evaluation (videofluoroscopic swallow study or flexible endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES)). These studies define physiology (oral vs pharyngeal phase dysfunction), risk of aspiration, and guide rehabilitation strategies.

- Nutrition: If the patient is unsafe for oral intake or aspiration risk is high, institute enteral feeding. A nasogastric tube (NGT) is appropriate for short‑term support (typically weeks). If prolonged dysphagia is expected (>4–6 weeks) or repeated NGT placement is impractical, consider percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG). Early provision of adequate calories and protein supports muscle recovery and prevents weight loss.

- Aspiration precautions and rehabilitation: Implement aspiration precautions (upright feeding, thickened liquids when indicated, oral hygiene) and engage SLP for compensatory strategies, postural maneuvers, texture modification, and targeted swallow exercises. In some centres neuromuscular electrical stimulation is considered adjunctive therapy for chronic dysphagia, but evidence is mixed; its use should be individualized.

- Airway protection: If there is progressive airway obstruction from soft‑tissue edema or bulbar failure with inability to protect the airway, intubation (elective when possible) or tracheostomy (if prolonged ventilatory support or airway protection anticipated) should be performed in conjunction with ENT/anesthesiology. Note that inflammatory edema from myositis does not respond to antihistamines or epinephrine as histaminergic angioedema would—immune suppression is the effective treatment.

Pulmonary considerations
Pulmonary disease in dermatomyositis ranges from aspiration pneumonitis to ILD. Management must be coordinated with pulmonology.

- Aspiration pneumonitis: Treat clinically when suspected; antibiotic choice is guided by local flora and aspiration risk, culture results when available, and clinical severity. Address the source of aspiration with swallowing rehabilitation and enteral feeding as above.

- Interstitial lung disease: If ILD is present or suspected, choose immunosuppression that addresses both muscle and lung disease (eg, mycophenolate, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide for severe cases). Baseline and serial pulmonary function tests (including diffusion capacity) and high‑resolution CT imaging are used to monitor progression.

Infection screening and prophylaxis
Before initiating intense immunosuppression, perform screening for latent or chronic infections and institute appropriate prophylaxis.

- Baseline screening: test for hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti‑HBc), hepatitis C, HIV, and latent tuberculosis (IGRA and consider chest radiography). Determine VZV immune status. CMV serology and other tests may be relevant in certain settings.

- Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis: Indicated for patients on prolonged high‑dose steroids (eg, prednisone ≥20 mg/day for ≥4 weeks) or combination immunosuppression. First‑line prophylaxis is trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX); common regimens include one single‑strength tablet (400/80 mg) daily or one double‑strength tablet (800/160 mg) three times weekly. Alternatives for intolerance or adverse events include inhaled pentamidine (300 mg monthly), atovaquone (1500 mg daily), or dapsone (100 mg daily) after G6PD testing. In the presented case, TMP‑SMX caused haemolysis and inhaled pentamidine was used.

- Vaccination and timing: Administer indicated inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, SARS‑CoV‑2) before initiation of B‑cell depleting therapy or IVIG when possible to maximize response. Live vaccines are contraindicated during significant immunosuppression. For patients seronegative for varicella, immunisation should ideally occur before immunosuppression.

- Reactivation risk: For patients with evidence of prior hepatitis B infection (anti‑HBc positive), consult hepatology for antiviral prophylaxis when using rituximab or other B‑cell depleting agents.

Supportive care and complication prevention
High‑dose steroids and multimodal immunosuppression require proactive supportive measures.

- Bone health: Start calcium and vitamin D and consider bisphosphonates for patients expected to be on prolonged (>3 months) high‑dose steroids or who have other osteoporosis risk factors.

- Gastroprotection: Consider PPI/H2 blocker for patients at high risk of steroid‑related peptic disease.

- Thromboprophylaxis: Hospitalized, immobilized patients should receive pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis unless contraindicated.

- Metabolic monitoring: Regularly monitor blood glucose and blood pressure while on high‑dose steroids; modify therapy as needed.

- Laboratory surveillance: Baseline and periodic CBC, renal and liver function tests (because many immunosuppressants are hepatically metabolized or myelosuppressive), creatine kinase and other muscle enzymes (aldolase, LDH), and drug‑specific monitoring (eg, TPMT activity if considering thiopurines). Monitor immunoglobulin levels during repeated IVIG and B‑cell depletion therapy.

- Skin care: Sun protection is important to limit cutaneous flares; consider antimalarial agents (hydroxychloroquine) for refractory cutaneous disease in appropriate patients, with baseline ophthalmologic assessment.

Rehabilitation and return to function
Begin physiotherapy and occupational therapy early, even during the acute phase, to limit deconditioning. Exercise programs in inflammatory myopathies emphasize submaximal, gradually progressive, and monitored strengthening once disease activity is controlled; overly aggressive exercise during active inflammation can worsen muscle injury. Swallowing rehabilitation (SLP‑led) should be an ongoing part of recovery until safe oral intake returns.

Assessment of response and steroid tapering
Treatment response is judged clinically (improvement in strength, swallow function, resolution of edema), and by laboratory and imaging markers (falling CK, normalization of liver enzymes, improvement on MRI or PET where used). In bulbar disease, clinical improvement in swallowing and protective airway reflexes is a priority even if CK lags.

Taper steroids slowly once improvement is sustained and steroid‑sparing agents are active. A pragmatic approach is to reduce prednisone by 5–10 mg every 1–2 weeks until reaching 20 mg/day, then more slowly (eg, 2.5–5 mg decrements) while reassessing for relapse. If relapse occurs during taper, escalate therapy and re‑evaluate adherence and infection.

Cancer screening and long‑term surveillance
Because dermatomyositis confers an increased risk of malignancy, comprehensive baseline malignancy screening is recommended and should be individualized based on age, sex, risk factors, and specific autoantibody profile. Ongoing vigilance for new symptoms and routine surveillance (appropriate imaging and endoscopies guided by risk) is part of longitudinal care.

Putting it together — an approach illustrated by the case
The patient in the case presented with subacute neck swelling, dysphagia and very high CK. Recognizing that neck edema was inflammatory rather than histaminergic led to hospital admission, rapid diagnostic evaluation (nailfold capillaroscopy, CK, MRI and PET muscle imaging, muscle biopsy) and aggressive therapy. He received pulse methylprednisolone, early IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab, with coordinated ENT assessment, swallow evaluation, nasogastric feeding and PJP prophylaxis (later modified due to intolerance). The combined approach reversed life‑threatening inflammation, allowed recovery of bulbar function, and illustrates the core principles above: early aggressive immunomodulation for threatened bulbar or airway function, careful airway and nutritional support, infection prevention, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation.

Key practical points
- Admit and monitor patients with severe dysphagia, aspiration or evolving airway compromise; involve ENT, ICU, SLP, nutrition and pulmonology early.
- Use pulse IV methylprednisolone (500–1000 mg daily ×3) for severe disease, convert to high‑dose oral prednisone, and add a steroid‑sparing agent early.
- IVIG is effective for refractory disease and dysphagia; typical dosing is 2 g/kg total with maintenance cycles every 4–6 weeks.
- Consider rituximab for refractory or severe disease requiring rapid steroid sparing.
- Protect the airway and provide enteral nutrition (NGT or PEG based on expected duration); intensify swallow rehabilitation.
- Screen for and prevent opportunistic infections (PJP prophylaxis, vaccinate with inactivated vaccines pre‑treatment), and monitor for treatment toxicity.
- Start rehabilitation early and taper immunosuppression based on clinical and laboratory response, with careful long‑term surveillance for relapse and malignancy.
