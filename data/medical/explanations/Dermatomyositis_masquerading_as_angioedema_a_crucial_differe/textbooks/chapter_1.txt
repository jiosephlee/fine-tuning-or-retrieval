Chapter 1: ## Initial Clinical Approach: Acute Neck Swelling with Subtle Myopathy Clues

Acute cervical soft-tissue swelling is a frequent emergency presentation and, rightly, triggers rapid evaluation for life‑threatening ENT causes such as deep neck space infection, Ludwig’s angina or airway-compromising angioedema. The case above illustrates a less common but important diagnostic pitfall: inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis, in this patient anti‑SAE1 antibody positive) producing subcutaneous and muscular edema that can masquerade as angioedema. A systematic approach that separates true histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema and structural infectious processes from inflammatory and neuromuscular mimics will shorten time to the correct diagnosis and appropriate immune‑directed therapy, while also ensuring airway safety.

Differentiate true angioedema from mimics
Begin by categorising angioedema into histaminergic (allergic), bradykinin‑mediated (eg, C1‑esterase inhibitor deficiency or ACE inhibitor–related) and non‑mediated causes (eg, inflammatory myopathies, cellulitis, venous congestion). Key historical and clinical discriminators are timing, triggers and associated features. Histaminergic angioedema typically has an acute onset over minutes to hours, is often pruritic, may be associated with urticaria and responds to antihistamines, glucocorticoids and epinephrine. Bradykinin‑mediated episodes tend to develop more gradually (hours), are non‑pruritic and non‑urticarial, are not responsive to antihistamines/epinephrine, and are often associated with ACE inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitor exposure, or inherited/acquired C1‑esterase inhibitor deficiency. By contrast, pseudoangioedema from an inflammatory myopathy is usually subacute over days to weeks, frequently painless or only mildly discomforting, non‑pitting and non‑pruritic, and associated with other myopathic features (proximal weakness, dysphagia) or characteristic cutaneous stigmata. In the case above, a 3‑week progressive, non‑tender neck swelling without urticaria, no medication triggers and associated proximal weakness should prompt consideration beyond primary angioedema.

Red flags that point to inflammatory myopathy
Certain “red flags” should lower the threshold to evaluate for myositis when screening for angioedema seems unrevealing:
- Subacute, progressive neck/face swelling that is non‑pitting and non‑pruritic.
- Proximal muscle weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, hip flexor weakness) or new dysphagia/hoarseness, particularly if swallowing secretions or cough is evident on bedside nasendoscopy.
- Cutaneous clues: facial erythema that involves nasolabial folds, shawl or V‑neck distributions, heliotrope change, Gottron’s papules, Holster sign, periungual erythema or nailfold microhemorrhages.
- Laboratory hints: disproportionately elevated muscle enzymes (CK markedly raised, with AST/ALT and LDH elevations that mirror CK rather than transaminase patterns from liver disease).

Focused physical examination: look beyond the neck
When primary ENT causes are excluded, perform a meticulous dermatologic and neuromuscular exam. Inspect the face, eyelids and extensor surfaces for heliotrope rash and Gottron’s papules; palpate for firmness of subcutaneous tissues versus fluctuance that would suggest abscess. Assess periungual areas and, if available, perform nailfold capillaroscopy—periungual telangiectasia, capillary drop‑out or microhemorrhages are highly suggestive of dermatomyositis and were pivotal in the case presented. Test proximal muscle strength (hip flexors, shoulder abduction) and screen bulbar function (swallowing, cough, phonation), as oropharyngeal muscle weakness carries immediate aspiration and airway implications.

Initial laboratory testing and interpretation
In any atypical “angioedema” case add muscle injury labs early. A suggested initial panel includes serum creatine kinase (CK), aldolase (if available), AST/ALT, LDH, basic metabolic panel with creatinine and electrolytes, and urinalysis for myoglobin. CK is the most sensitive readily available test for active myositis; values can range from mildly to extremely elevated (our patient had CK >16,000 U/L). When CK is high, AST and LDH elevations may reflect muscle release rather than hepatic injury; AST often rises out of proportion to ALT in muscle disease. Because very high CK and myoglobinuria can cause acute kidney injury, monitor renal function and urine output, and consider aggressive hydration if myoglobinuria is present. Also order autoimmune serology including ANA and a myositis‑specific antibody panel (eg, anti‑SAE1, anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑TIF1‑γ), and measure complement (C4) and C1‑esterase inhibitor levels and function when bradykinin‑mediated angioedema is in the differential—normal C4 and C1 function (as in this patient) argue against hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency.

Imaging and airway assessment
Exclude ENT emergencies with appropriate imaging and endoscopic assessment. Contrast‑enhanced CT neck is indicated to rule out deep space infection, abscess or vascular causes; diffuse soft‑tissue swelling and muscle inflammation without abscess should raise suspicion for a non‑infectious inflammatory process. Flexible nasoendoscopy is essential to evaluate supraglottic and glottic patency, pooling of secretions and vocal cord function—findings of increased secretions and poor clearance, as in this case, are consistent with bulbar muscle involvement rather than primary angioedema.

Pulmonary testing and swallowing evaluation
Pulmonary function testing demonstrating an extrathoracic restrictive pattern or reduced maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures suggests respiratory or bulbar muscle involvement and indicates need for close respiratory monitoring. Because dysphagia is common in dermatomyositis and can be severe (aspiration risk), arrange early evaluation by speech‑language pathology and objective testing such as a video fluoroscopic swallow (barium) study to characterise the severity and guide nutrition and airway precautions.

Avoid anchoring bias; think broad and remember response to therapy
A useful cognitive check is to reassess expected response to standard angioedema therapies. Rapid improvement with epinephrine/antihistamines supports histaminergic causes; lack of response should prompt reconsideration. Normal C4/C1‑INH function and negative medication history for ACEi/DPP‑4 inhibitors argue against bradykinin‑mediated angioedema and should prompt muscle enzyme testing early rather than continuing only antihistamine/steroid trials. Inflammatory neck swelling from myositis will not respond to antihistamines and often requires immunosuppression.

Ancillary testing and definitive diagnosis
If initial testing suggests myositis, obtain confirmatory studies: MRI of involved muscle groups (T2/STIR sequences show oedema), electromyography if diagnosis uncertain, and ultimately muscle biopsy when needed; biopsy in dermatomyositis often shows perifascicular atrophy, perimysial and perivascular inflammation and complement deposition in capillaries (C5b‑9). Simultaneously screen for interstitial lung disease with CT chest and consider malignancy screening per dermatomyositis guidelines, particularly in adult patients with risk factors.

Multidisciplinary management and disposition
Early involvement of ENT (airway), immunology/rheumatology (diagnosis, immunosuppression), neurology/myositis specialists (diagnostic workup), speech‑language pathology (dysphagia management) and ICU teams (if respiratory compromise) improves safety and expedites therapy. Airway protection should be prioritised—progressive dysphagia, pooling secretions, hypoventilation or stridor warrant low threshold for controlled airway management. When active myositis with bulbar or respiratory muscle involvement is confirmed or strongly suspected, prompt initiation of high‑dose glucocorticoids (often intravenous pulses) and early steroid‑sparing agents (eg, IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab) is frequently required; the risks of immunosuppression should be balanced against the urgency of treating life‑threatening muscle inflammation and guided by infectious workup.

Practical summary
When faced with unexplained neck or facial swelling: (1) rapidly exclude airway‑threatening infections and abscess with imaging and endoscopy; (2) differentiate histaminergic versus bradykinin angioedema by history, associated urticaria/pruritus and complement/C1‑INH testing; (3) if presentation is subacute, non‑pruritic and accompanied by weakness, dysphagia or cutaneous signs, immediately check CK/aldolase and AST/ALT and examine nailfolds and typical dermatomyositis rashes; (4) involve multidisciplinary teams early for airway, swallowing and immunosuppressive management. The case presented exemplifies how recognising subtle myopathic and cutaneous clues and ordering a CK early avoided diagnostic delay and enabled timely, disease‑directed therapy.
