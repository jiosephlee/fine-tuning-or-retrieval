Chapter 6: ## Prognosis, Malignancy Surveillance, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis in dermatomyositis (DM) is highly variable and largely determined by the extent and rapidity of organ involvement and by how quickly immuno-inflammatory activity is identified and controlled. Inflammatory features that denote high disease activity — severe proximal weakness, frank myonecrosis with markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), extensive subcutaneous or facial/neck oedema, progressive dysphagia and clinically significant interstitial lung disease (ILD) — are associated with increased short‑term morbidity and a greater need for aggressive, combination immunosuppression. These same features, however, are often the manifestations that prompt admission and early treatment; when treated promptly and intensively, many patients achieve clinical remission or marked improvement, as illustrated by this case, in which profound CK elevation, prominent cervical oedema and dysphagia all improved after high‑dose steroids, IVIG and steroid‑sparing agents. Conversely, delayed recognition — for example, when neck swelling is misclassified as idiopathic angioedema or an isolated ENT problem — risks complications such as aspiration pneumonitis, nutritional compromise and respiratory failure, and worsens long‑term functional outcome.

Autoantibody profile provides useful prognostic information. SAE1‑antibody–positive DM is classically associated with prominent cutaneous disease and a relatively high frequency of dysphagia and ILD. Published cohorts suggest an appreciable association with malignancy (reported rates vary by series, with one review noting ~18% in SAE cohorts), and therefore these patients warrant careful cancer surveillance at baseline and during follow‑up. The presence of subcutaneous oedema, significant bulbar involvement or early ILD in any DM subtype signals high inflammatory burden and should lower the threshold for inpatient care, early multidisciplinary assessment (rheumatology/immunology, neurology, ENT/speech pathology, respiratory medicine) and combination therapy rather than prolonged single‑agent steroid tapering.

Malignancy surveillance in DM must be structured and multimodal. No single tumour marker or test is sufficiently sensitive to exclude an occult cancer. At diagnosis, risk stratification should consider age, sex, cutaneous phenotype, antibody status (e.g., anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑NXP2, SAE1), presence of dysphagia or rapid weight loss, and smoking history. Practical baseline evaluation often includes: thorough history and physical exam focused on common DM‑associated cancers; age‑ and sex‑appropriate standard screening (mammography, cervical screening, colonoscopy as indicated, PSA where appropriate); cross‑sectional imaging — either CT chest/abdomen/ pelvis or FDG‑PET/CT depending on availability, local practice and pretest probability; and targeted investigations based on symptoms (e.g., upper and lower endoscopy for GI symptoms or if risk factors suggest GI malignancy). For SAE1‑positive disease, many centres will add PET/CT and consider gastrointestinal endoscopy because of the reported associations and because tumour markers alone are insensitive. Surveillance intensity should be highest in the first 1–3 years after diagnosis when paraneoplastic associations are most likely to present; a reasonable pragmatic approach is repeat focused imaging or reassessment at 6–12 months and again at 12–36 months if the initial screen is negative but risk remains elevated, with symptom‑triggered re‑evaluation thereafter. These intervals should be individualized — for example, shorter intervals for older patients, those with high‑risk antibodies or unexplained constitutional symptoms. Importantly, tumor markers (CEA, CA‑125, CA19‑9, PSA) can supplement but should not replace structured imaging and endoscopic evaluation.

Long‑term monitoring must address both disease activity and complications of the disease and its treatment. Disease activity can be followed clinically (manual muscle testing, patient‑reported function, dysphagia assessment) and biochemically (serial CK, aldolase, transaminases, LDH), recognizing that CK may normalize despite ongoing weakness in some chronic cases. For suspected or established ILD obtain baseline spirometry with DLCO and high‑resolution CT chest; repeat testing every 3–6 months initially for progressive disease or every 6–12 months for stable ILD, with earlier reassessment for new respiratory symptoms. Bulbar dysfunction mandates objective swallow assessment (video fluoroscopy or fibreoptic evaluation), early involvement of speech and language therapy, and nutritional planning including temporary enteral feeding when aspiration risk or malnutrition is significant, as in this patient.

Surveillance for complications and treatment toxicities is mandatory. Aspiration pneumonia and malnutrition are frequent and should be anticipated in patients with dysphagia; preventive measures include swallow therapy, dietary modification, and consideration of enteral feeding until safe swallow is restored. Cardiopulmonary complications include myocarditis, arrhythmia and pulmonary hypertension; screen with ECG and consider cardiac biomarkers, echocardiography or cardiac MRI if there are suggestive symptoms or unexplained troponin/BNP elevation. Venous thromboembolism risk is increased in systemic inflammatory states and during periods of immobility or hospitalization; assess and provide prophylaxis per usual thromboprophylaxis guidelines. Chronic corticosteroid exposure requires monitoring for hyperglycaemia, hypertension, osteoporosis (baseline DXA and commence bone protection early for prolonged high‑dose therapy), weight gain, mood changes and infection risk.

Medication‑specific monitoring and counselling reduce iatrogenic harm. Baseline infectious screening before significant immunosuppression should include hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti‑HBc), hepatitis C, and HIV serology, and consideration of TB screening. For rituximab, check baseline immunoglobulins and hepatitis B markers (anti‑HBc is critical) because B‑cell depletion can reactivate HBV; vaccinate against pneumococcus and seasonal influenza and, where possible, complete immunizations before B‑cell depleting therapy because vaccine responses are blunted post‑rituximab. Periodic CBC and LFTs are required for azathioprine, methotrexate and mycophenolate — the latter requires regular full blood counts and liver function checks and counselling on contraception (mycophenolate is teratogenic). Azathioprine should be preceded by TPMT testing where available. IVIG therapy requires monitoring of renal function, vigilance for infusion reactions and thrombotic events, and attention to fluid status. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis should be considered for patients on prolonged high‑dose steroids combined with other immunosuppressants — TMP‑SMX is first choice but alternatives (aerosolized pentamidine, atovaquone) are available for those intolerant, as occurred in our patient. Finally, counsel patients about live vaccines: generally avoid live attenuated vaccines while on significant immunosuppression; use inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, SARS‑CoV‑2) and time administration relative to B‑cell–depleting agents when possible.

Patient education is a cornerstone of long‑term management. Teach patients to recognize early warning signs of a flare — new or worsening proximal weakness, increasing difficulty with swallowing or breathing, new or expanding rash, unexplained fevers or weight loss — and to seek prompt review. Photoprotection (broad‑spectrum sunscreen, protective clothing) is important because ultraviolet exposure commonly worsens cutaneous disease. Discuss contraception and pregnancy planning early for people of childbearing potential: methotrexate and mycophenolate are teratogenic and must be stopped well in advance of conception; for men and women, plan immunosuppressive regimens in collaboration with obstetrics where pregnancy is desired. Reinforce infection vigilance and adherence to prophylactic strategies when indicated.

Clinical pearls to avoid missed diagnoses and optimize outcomes
- In any case of “idiopathic angioedema” with a subacute course, absent urticaria, normal C1‑INH/C4 and lack of response to antihistamines, measure CK and perform a careful skin and nailfold examination. Pseudoangioedema from underlying myositis is an uncommon but critical mimic.  
- Nailfold capillaroscopy, periungual changes, Holster or shawl signs and subtle limb rashes may be the clues that convert a diagnostic cul‑de‑sac into the correct systemic diagnosis.  
- Tumour markers alone are insufficient to rule out occult malignancy; use a structured approach (history, examination, cross‑sectional imaging or PET, age‑appropriate cancer screening and targeted endoscopy) and maintain vigilance for the first 1–3 years after DM diagnosis.  
- Severe dysphagia and significant neck/facial oedema identify patients at risk for aspiration and nutritional compromise and should prompt early multidisciplinary intervention (ENT, speech pathology, nutrition, respiratory) and aggressive immunomodulation.  
- Before initiating rituximab or other lymphocyte‑depleting therapies, screen for HBV/ HCV and ensure vaccination where feasible; monitor immunoglobulins during follow‑up.  
- Inpatients with active, severe DM benefit from early combination therapy (high‑dose corticosteroids plus a steroid‑sparing agent ± IVIG ± biologic therapy) rather than prolonged mono‑therapy in order to limit morbidity from ongoing inflammation.

In summary, the clinical course of DM can be favorable when high disease activity is recognized early and treated aggressively; SAE1‑positive patients require particular vigilance for dysphagia, ILD and occult malignancy. A structured surveillance plan for cancer, pulmonary and cardiac complications, together with careful monitoring for immunosuppressive toxicities and an emphasis on patient education, will optimize long‑term outcomes and reduce preventable morbidity — lessons underscored by the successful recovery in this case.
