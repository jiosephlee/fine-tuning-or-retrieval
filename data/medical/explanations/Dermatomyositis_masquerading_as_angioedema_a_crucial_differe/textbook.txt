Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

## Initial Clinical Approach: Acute Neck Swelling with Subtle Myopathy Clues

Acute cervical soft-tissue swelling is a frequent emergency presentation and, rightly, triggers rapid evaluation for life‑threatening ENT causes such as deep neck space infection, Ludwig’s angina or airway-compromising angioedema. The case above illustrates a less common but important diagnostic pitfall: inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis, in this patient anti‑SAE1 antibody positive) producing subcutaneous and muscular edema that can masquerade as angioedema. A systematic approach that separates true histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema and structural infectious processes from inflammatory and neuromuscular mimics will shorten time to the correct diagnosis and appropriate immune‑directed therapy, while also ensuring airway safety.

Differentiate true angioedema from mimics
Begin by categorising angioedema into histaminergic (allergic), bradykinin‑mediated (eg, C1‑esterase inhibitor deficiency or ACE inhibitor–related) and non‑mediated causes (eg, inflammatory myopathies, cellulitis, venous congestion). Key historical and clinical discriminators are timing, triggers and associated features. Histaminergic angioedema typically has an acute onset over minutes to hours, is often pruritic, may be associated with urticaria and responds to antihistamines, glucocorticoids and epinephrine. Bradykinin‑mediated episodes tend to develop more gradually (hours), are non‑pruritic and non‑urticarial, are not responsive to antihistamines/epinephrine, and are often associated with ACE inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitor exposure, or inherited/acquired C1‑esterase inhibitor deficiency. By contrast, pseudoangioedema from an inflammatory myopathy is usually subacute over days to weeks, frequently painless or only mildly discomforting, non‑pitting and non‑pruritic, and associated with other myopathic features (proximal weakness, dysphagia) or characteristic cutaneous stigmata. In the case above, a 3‑week progressive, non‑tender neck swelling without urticaria, no medication triggers and associated proximal weakness should prompt consideration beyond primary angioedema.

Red flags that point to inflammatory myopathy
Certain “red flags” should lower the threshold to evaluate for myositis when screening for angioedema seems unrevealing:
- Subacute, progressive neck/face swelling that is non‑pitting and non‑pruritic.
- Proximal muscle weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, hip flexor weakness) or new dysphagia/hoarseness, particularly if swallowing secretions or cough is evident on bedside nasendoscopy.
- Cutaneous clues: facial erythema that involves nasolabial folds, shawl or V‑neck distributions, heliotrope change, Gottron’s papules, Holster sign, periungual erythema or nailfold microhemorrhages.
- Laboratory hints: disproportionately elevated muscle enzymes (CK markedly raised, with AST/ALT and LDH elevations that mirror CK rather than transaminase patterns from liver disease).

Focused physical examination: look beyond the neck
When primary ENT causes are excluded, perform a meticulous dermatologic and neuromuscular exam. Inspect the face, eyelids and extensor surfaces for heliotrope rash and Gottron’s papules; palpate for firmness of subcutaneous tissues versus fluctuance that would suggest abscess. Assess periungual areas and, if available, perform nailfold capillaroscopy—periungual telangiectasia, capillary drop‑out or microhemorrhages are highly suggestive of dermatomyositis and were pivotal in the case presented. Test proximal muscle strength (hip flexors, shoulder abduction) and screen bulbar function (swallowing, cough, phonation), as oropharyngeal muscle weakness carries immediate aspiration and airway implications.

Initial laboratory testing and interpretation
In any atypical “angioedema” case add muscle injury labs early. A suggested initial panel includes serum creatine kinase (CK), aldolase (if available), AST/ALT, LDH, basic metabolic panel with creatinine and electrolytes, and urinalysis for myoglobin. CK is the most sensitive readily available test for active myositis; values can range from mildly to extremely elevated (our patient had CK >16,000 U/L). When CK is high, AST and LDH elevations may reflect muscle release rather than hepatic injury; AST often rises out of proportion to ALT in muscle disease. Because very high CK and myoglobinuria can cause acute kidney injury, monitor renal function and urine output, and consider aggressive hydration if myoglobinuria is present. Also order autoimmune serology including ANA and a myositis‑specific antibody panel (eg, anti‑SAE1, anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑TIF1‑γ), and measure complement (C4) and C1‑esterase inhibitor levels and function when bradykinin‑mediated angioedema is in the differential—normal C4 and C1 function (as in this patient) argue against hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency.

Imaging and airway assessment
Exclude ENT emergencies with appropriate imaging and endoscopic assessment. Contrast‑enhanced CT neck is indicated to rule out deep space infection, abscess or vascular causes; diffuse soft‑tissue swelling and muscle inflammation without abscess should raise suspicion for a non‑infectious inflammatory process. Flexible nasoendoscopy is essential to evaluate supraglottic and glottic patency, pooling of secretions and vocal cord function—findings of increased secretions and poor clearance, as in this case, are consistent with bulbar muscle involvement rather than primary angioedema.

Pulmonary testing and swallowing evaluation
Pulmonary function testing demonstrating an extrathoracic restrictive pattern or reduced maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures suggests respiratory or bulbar muscle involvement and indicates need for close respiratory monitoring. Because dysphagia is common in dermatomyositis and can be severe (aspiration risk), arrange early evaluation by speech‑language pathology and objective testing such as a video fluoroscopic swallow (barium) study to characterise the severity and guide nutrition and airway precautions.

Avoid anchoring bias; think broad and remember response to therapy
A useful cognitive check is to reassess expected response to standard angioedema therapies. Rapid improvement with epinephrine/antihistamines supports histaminergic causes; lack of response should prompt reconsideration. Normal C4/C1‑INH function and negative medication history for ACEi/DPP‑4 inhibitors argue against bradykinin‑mediated angioedema and should prompt muscle enzyme testing early rather than continuing only antihistamine/steroid trials. Inflammatory neck swelling from myositis will not respond to antihistamines and often requires immunosuppression.

Ancillary testing and definitive diagnosis
If initial testing suggests myositis, obtain confirmatory studies: MRI of involved muscle groups (T2/STIR sequences show oedema), electromyography if diagnosis uncertain, and ultimately muscle biopsy when needed; biopsy in dermatomyositis often shows perifascicular atrophy, perimysial and perivascular inflammation and complement deposition in capillaries (C5b‑9). Simultaneously screen for interstitial lung disease with CT chest and consider malignancy screening per dermatomyositis guidelines, particularly in adult patients with risk factors.

Multidisciplinary management and disposition
Early involvement of ENT (airway), immunology/rheumatology (diagnosis, immunosuppression), neurology/myositis specialists (diagnostic workup), speech‑language pathology (dysphagia management) and ICU teams (if respiratory compromise) improves safety and expedites therapy. Airway protection should be prioritised—progressive dysphagia, pooling secretions, hypoventilation or stridor warrant low threshold for controlled airway management. When active myositis with bulbar or respiratory muscle involvement is confirmed or strongly suspected, prompt initiation of high‑dose glucocorticoids (often intravenous pulses) and early steroid‑sparing agents (eg, IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab) is frequently required; the risks of immunosuppression should be balanced against the urgency of treating life‑threatening muscle inflammation and guided by infectious workup.

Practical summary
When faced with unexplained neck or facial swelling: (1) rapidly exclude airway‑threatening infections and abscess with imaging and endoscopy; (2) differentiate histaminergic versus bradykinin angioedema by history, associated urticaria/pruritus and complement/C1‑INH testing; (3) if presentation is subacute, non‑pruritic and accompanied by weakness, dysphagia or cutaneous signs, immediately check CK/aldolase and AST/ALT and examine nailfolds and typical dermatomyositis rashes; (4) involve multidisciplinary teams early for airway, swallowing and immunosuppressive management. The case presented exemplifies how recognising subtle myopathic and cutaneous clues and ordering a CK early avoided diagnostic delay and enabled timely, disease‑directed therapy.

## Pathophysiology and Serologic Phenotypes of Dermatomyositis (SAE1-Positive Focus)

Dermatomyositis (DM) is best understood as an immune-mediated microangiopathy of skin and muscle in which humoral and innate immune mechanisms converge on the endothelium of endomysial and dermal capillaries. A central histopathologic hallmark is endothelial injury with loss of capillaries and deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) on endomysial capillaries. This complement activation—thought to be triggered by immune complexes and autoantibody–antigen interactions at the microvascular surface—produces focal ischemia of muscle fibers that is most conspicuous in the perifascicular region and manifests as perifascicular atrophy, necrosis, and regeneration on muscle biopsy. The biopsy in this case, with foci of perifascicular necrotic and regenerating fibers and demonstrable C5b‑9 deposition, is a classic illustration of this complement‑mediated microvascular injury pathway and distinguishes DM from other inflammatory myopathies in which necrosis is prominent but capillary deposition of MAC is absent or minimal (for example, immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy).

Overlaying the complement pathway, type I interferon (IFN‑α/β) signalling is a molecular signature of DM that links vascular injury, myofiber dysfunction, and the striking cutaneous manifestations. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells and other innate immune elements produce type I IFNs in affected tissue, upregulating interferon‑stimulated genes (MxA is the prototypic marker used on immunohistochemistry). Type I IFNs promote endothelial activation and dysfunction, increase expression of MHC and adhesion molecules, and recruit inflammatory cells; they also contribute to keratinocyte and mucocutaneous changes that produce heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, shawl and V‑neck signs, and periungual telangiectasia. The prominence of skin disease in many DM patients therefore reflects this interferon‑driven cutaneous tropism in tandem with microvascular injury.

The serologic landscape of DM is heterogeneous and clinically informative. Myositis‑specific antibodies (MSAs) stratify patients into phenotypic subgroups with differing risks for interstitial lung disease (ILD), malignancy, cutaneous severity, and pattern/timing of muscle involvement. Anti–small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti‑SAE1) antibodies target the heterodimeric SAE E1 enzyme required for sumoylation, a post‑translational modification that regulates nuclear transport and transcriptional responses. The exact pathogenic role of anti‑SAE1 is not fully established; hypotheses include direct interference with sumoylation pathways in skin and muscle cells or, more likely, that SAE1 autoimmunity marks a distinct immunologic milieu characterized by prominent type I IFN activity and B‑cell–driven autoantibody responses. Clinically, SAE1‑positive DM often presents with early, conspicuous cutaneous disease and a relatively high frequency of extramuscular complications: dysphagia (reported in roughly 40–50% of series), ILD (approximately 40%), and a measurable association with malignancy (estimates commonly in the mid‑teens percent range). Importantly, myopathy in SAE1 disease may be delayed or variable in severity relative to the skin findings, so a high index of suspicion is required when skin features dominate.

Subcutaneous oedema or “pseudoangioedema” is a recognized but uncommon manifestation across DM serotypes and can be the presenting feature when it is localized to the face or neck. The pathophysiology of this oedema is distinct from histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema: inflammatory capillary leak from microvascular injury, increased vascular permeability driven by cytokines (including type I IFNs), and local myofasciitis or muscle inflammation with interstitial fluid accumulation are the dominant mechanisms. Lymphatic dysfunction related to perimuscular inflammation may further impede fluid removal. Although classic reports associate facial/neck oedema more frequently with anti‑MDA5 and anti‑TIF1‑γ antibodies, SAE1‑positive patients can and do develop significant soft‑tissue swelling, as the present case demonstrates. Recognizing this helps avoid misdiagnosis (for example, Ludwig’s angina or idiopathic angioedema) and prompts evaluation for myopathy and immunologic markers.

Extramuscular involvement in DM follows from the same vascular and inflammatory processes. Dysphagia results from weakness of pharyngeal constrictors and upper oesophageal striated musculature or from cricopharyngeal dysfunction, and it carries important sequelae—aspiration pneumonitis and nutritional compromise—requiring early assessment with swallowing studies. ILD in DM reflects inflammatory injury of the pulmonary interstitium; its prevalence and severity vary with MSA subtype (notably high with anti‑MDA5 and not infrequent with anti‑SAE1) and dramatically influence prognosis and therapeutic choices. Cardiac involvement—myocarditis, conduction disturbances, and arrhythmias—occurs less commonly but merits surveillance because of its potential morbidity. Malignancy screening is guided by age, specific risk factors, and serology: dermatomyositis overall is a paraneoplastic syndrome in a subset of patients, and SAE1 positivity confers an intermediate to high risk in many published series, prompting directed cancer evaluation and longitudinal surveillance.

Laboratory and biopsy features reflect these mechanisms. Serum muscle enzymes (CK, AST, ALT, LDH) can range from near normal to markedly elevated; CK often correlates with active myonecrosis, and extreme elevations—as in the case described—signal severe muscle injury but do not alone determine prognosis. Autoantibody panels (often performed by immunoblot/line assay or immunoprecipitation) that identify MSAs—anti‑SAE among them—provide specificity for DM and help predict phenotype. Muscle histology in DM classically demonstrates perifascicular atrophy and necrosis, perivascular and perimysial inflammatory infiltrates (often with histiocytes and B cells), and complement deposition in capillaries. These features help differentiate DM from IMNM (pronounced necrosis with sparse inflammation and association with SRP/HMGCR antibodies) and from inclusion body myositis (endomysial CD8+ T‑cell invasion, rimmed vacuoles, and progressive distal/proximal pattern in older patients).

Therapeutically, an appreciation of DM pathophysiology informs practice. High‑dose glucocorticoids remain first‑line for rapidly progressive muscle or airway‑threatening disease because they suppress broad inflammatory cascades. Because B cells and autoantibodies participate in complement activation, B‑cell depletion (rituximab) and IVIG—effective in blocking Fc‑mediated processes and modulating complement—are rational choices for severe or refractory disease and for complications such as dysphagia. Steroid‑sparing agents (mycophenolate, azathioprine, methotrexate) are selected according to comorbidities (for example, avoiding methotrexate in active ILD), and targeted trials of interferon pathway inhibitors are an active area of research that directly addresses the IFN‑driven biology of DM. Finally, recognizing that subcutaneous oedema in DM is inflammatory rather than histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated prevents unnecessary trials of antihistamines or C1‑inhibitor therapies and directs clinicians to immunosuppression and supportive measures (airway protection, nutrition, aspiration prevention).

In summary, dermatomyositis is a complement‑ and interferon‑driven microangiopathy with distinct serologic phenotypes. Anti‑SAE1 positivity defines a syndrome with prominent cutaneous disease, frequent dysphagia and ILD, variable timing and severity of myositis, and a modestly increased cancer risk. The clinical and histologic features seen in the presented patient—marked CK elevation, perifascicular necrosis, C5b‑9 deposition, diffuse inflammatory muscle uptake on PET/MRI, dysphagia and neck soft‑tissue oedema—are coherent with the underlying vascular and interferon‑mediated mechanisms and explain both the presentation and the rationale for aggressive, multimodal immunotherapy.

## Differential Diagnosis: Cervical Swelling, Dysphagia, and Weakness

Cervical soft‑tissue swelling accompanied by oropharyngeal symptoms and weakness is a diagnostically broad presentation that spans infectious, allergic/angioedema, neuromuscular, inflammatory myopathies, endocrine/metabolic, structural and neoplastic processes. Timely distinction among these entities is critical because management and immediate risks differ markedly — for example, airway compromise from angioedema or myositis‑related pharyngeal weakness, sepsis from deep neck infection, versus the need for urgent immunosuppression and cancer screening in dermatomyositis. The case presented illustrates common pitfalls: an apparently localized “angioedema” that lacked typical allergic features, imaging without a drainable collection, and subtle cutaneous signs and proximal weakness that pointed away from the usual otolaryngology diagnoses and toward an inflammatory myopathy.

Angioedema — histaminergic versus bradykinin‑mediated — is often the first diagnostic consideration for acute neck and facial swelling. Histaminergic angioedema tends to have rapid onset, associated urticaria or pruritus, and responsiveness to antihistamines, corticosteroids or epinephrine. By contrast, bradykinin‑mediated angioedema (eg, ACE inhibitor–related, hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency) is usually non‑pruritic, longer lasting, and refractory to antihistamines and steroids. Laboratory discriminators include low C4 and low or dysfunctional C1 esterase inhibitor for hereditary/acquired C1‑INH deficiency. A careful drug history (ACE inhibitors, neprilysin inhibitors) and family history are essential. In the present case the absence of urticaria or pruritus, lack of culprit medications or family history, and normal C4 and C1‑INH testing argued strongly against bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.

Infectious and inflammatory causes of neck swelling — Ludwig angina, deep neck space infection or abscess, bacterial sialadenitis, cellulitis/erysipelas — typically present with fever, focal pain, elevated white cell count and imaging evidence of an abscess or focal inflammatory collection. Clinical features such as trismus, severe odynophagia, toxic appearance, and CT findings of loculated fluid with rim enhancement point toward a surgically managed deep space infection. Diffuse, non‑tender swelling without a discrete collection and an afebrile, clinically well patient (as in this case) should prompt reconsideration of a noninfectious etiology.

Neuromuscular junction disorders and cranial neuropathies can present predominantly with bulbar symptoms and neck involvement. Myasthenia gravis classically causes fluctuating, fatigable bulbar weakness (dysarthria, dysphagia, ptosis) without a marked rise in serum creatine kinase (CK); electrophysiology and acetylcholine receptor or MuSK antibody testing are diagnostic. Botulism and certain cranial neuropathies produce prominent autonomic signs (eg, dry mouth, dilated pupils) and descending weakness; their time course and exposure history usually provide clues. In contrast, the case patient had fixed proximal limb weakness and markedly elevated CK, findings that disfavor a primary neuromuscular junction disorder.

Inflammatory myopathies form a key differential when weakness and neck or facial swelling coexist. Dermatomyositis (DM) may present with cutaneous signs (heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, shawl or V‑sign, periungual capillary changes) and proximal muscle weakness; Importantly, DM can cause prominent subcutaneous edema of the face and neck — a less common but well‑described manifestation — and dysphagia from pharyngeal and upper esophageal muscle involvement. Characteristic laboratory clues are substantially elevated CK, aldolase, transaminases and LDH; myositis‑specific autoantibodies (eg, anti‑SAE1, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑MDA‑5, antisynthetase antibodies) can direct phenotype and cancer risk. Imaging with MRI shows muscle edema; PET or FDG uptake may demonstrate diffuse myositis; definitive diagnosis is supported by muscle biopsy, with perifascicular atrophy and complement (C5b‑9) deposition in dermatomyositis. In this patient the constellation of markedly raised CK, nailfold capillary abnormalities, positive ANA and anti‑SAE1 antibody, PET/MRI muscle uptake and classic biopsy findings established DM as the cause of the “angioedema,” attributable to inflammatory myositis and microvascular injury rather than histaminergic or bradykinin processes.

Other inflammatory myopathies have distinguishing features. Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (often anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP positive) presents with very high CK and severe weakness but typically minimal cutaneous signs; biopsies show necrosis with scarce inflammatory infiltrate. Inclusion body myositis (IBM) affects older patients, has a characteristic distribution (finger flexors, quadriceps), is slowly progressive, and may cause dysphagia; CK is usually only mildly elevated and muscle biopsy reveals rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates. Antisynthetase syndrome (eg, anti‑Jo‑1) features mechanic’s hands, myositis, interstitial lung disease and arthritis; the presence of ILD and mechanic’s hands helps differentiate it from other entities.

Endocrine, metabolic and toxic causes can mimic myositis or cause rhabdomyolysis with neck swelling and dysphagia. Hypothyroid myopathy typically causes myalgias, slow reflexes and modest CK elevation and will be accompanied by hypothyroid clinical signs and TSH abnormalities. Drug‑related rhabdomyolysis (statins, fibrates, certain antipsychotics) often has an identifiable exposure and markedly raised CK; history is critical. Electrolyte disturbances (severe hypokalemia) may produce acute weakness and should be screened.

Allergic and drug reactions — DRESS, acute contact dermatitis, severe cellulitic dermatitis — can produce facial and neck swelling. These are usually accompanied by pain, pruritus, systemic features such as fever, eosinophilia (DRESS) and an evocative temporal relationship with a new drug or contact exposure. Skin morphology and mucosal involvement can be diagnostic.

Structural and vascular causes are less inflammatory but important to consider. Superior vena cava syndrome produces facial swelling, plethora and venous distension; a history of malignancy, indwelling central venous devices or mediastinal mass suggests this diagnosis and CT venography confirms it. Thyroid enlargement/goiter, lymphatic obstruction from malignancy, benign orofacial granulomatosis, amyloidosis or sarcoidosis may cause chronic, unilateral or bilateral neck swelling; granulomatous diseases often have systemic features and imaging or biopsy establishes diagnosis.

How to discriminate: practical testing and interpretation
- Bedside and early laboratory tests: CBC, CRP/ESR, CK, liver enzymes, creatinine, urinalysis for myoglobin, serum TSH — a very high CK points toward myositis/rhabdomyolysis; leukocytosis and high CRP with focal neck pain favor bacterial infection.
- Angioedema workup: C4 and quantitative/function assay for C1‑INH, drug history for ACE inhibitors; normal complement studies and no response to antihistamines argue against bradykinin‑mediated or histaminergic angioedema, respectively.
- Autoantibodies: ANA and myositis‑specific panels (eg, anti‑SAE1, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑Mi‑2, antisynthetase, anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) guide diagnosis, phenotype and associated cancer risk.
- Imaging: contrast CT neck is essential to exclude abscess or gas‑forming infection and to define the pattern of soft‑tissue swelling. MRI of muscle identifies edema and guides biopsy; FDG‑PET can both highlight active myositis and screen for occult malignancy.
- Functional testing: video fluoroscopic swallow study quantifies pharyngeal dysphagia and guides aspiration risk/feeding decisions; pulmonary function tests and chest imaging evaluate for aspiration pneumonitis or myositis‑related ILD.
- Electrophysiology and biopsy: EMG can differentiate myopathic from neuromuscular junction disorders; muscle biopsy remains diagnostic in many inflammatory myopathies, revealing pattern‑specific findings (perifascicular atrophy and capillary complement deposition in DM, necrotizing features in IMNM, rimmed vacuoles in IBM).

Applying these discriminators to the case: the patient’s afebrile state, normal white cell count, CT neck without an abscess, markedly raised CK, periungual capillary changes, positive anti‑SAE1 antibody and MRI/PET evidence of diffuse muscle inflammation all pointed away from infection or classic angioedema and toward dermatomyositis. Normal C4 and C1‑INH function excluded hereditary/acquired C1‑INH deficiency. The presence of severe pharyngeal dysphagia and FDG uptake in head and neck musculature highlighted the immediate airway and aspiration risks and justified rapid institution of high‑dose corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive therapies.

Practical takeaways for clinicians
- Do not assume all facial or neck swelling is allergic or infectious. Whenever the presentation is atypical for angioedema (no urticaria, non‑pruritic, poor response to antihistamines/steroids) or when CT shows diffuse soft tissue edema without a drainable collection, broaden the differential.
- Early CK measurement, thorough skin and nailfold examination (capillaroscopy where available), and a targeted autoantibody panel can rapidly distinguish dermatomyositis and other inflammatory myopathies from ENT and allergic diagnoses.
- When myositis is suspected, assess swallowing and airway function promptly; dysphagia may require tube feeding and severe pharyngeal weakness may demand urgent immunosuppression.
- Use a combination of serology, imaging, EMG and muscle biopsy to reach a definitive diagnosis, and initiate malignancy screening according to myositis subtype and risk profile.

In summary, cervical swelling with dysphagia and weakness demands a systematic approach that integrates clinical pattern recognition with directed laboratory and imaging studies. Dermatomyositis should be included in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neck or facial edema — particularly when accompanied by proximal weakness, markedly elevated muscle enzymes, nailfold and cutaneous findings — because early recognition alters both acute management and long‑term surveillance, including cancer screening.

## Diagnostic Workup of Suspected Dermatomyositis with Neck Edema

Neck swelling that resembles angioedema can be a clinical red herring. When it is persistent, non-pruritic, non-fluctuating with antihistamines, and accompanied by even subtle cutaneous or neuromuscular findings, clinicians should broaden the differential to include inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis (DM). The case presented illustrates this point: a patient referred for presumed deep neck infection or idiopathic angioedema was found to have markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), periungual nailfold changes and proximal weakness, and ultimately a myositis-specific autoantibody (anti‑SAE1) and muscle biopsy features diagnostic of DM. The diagnostic evaluation in this clinical scenario must therefore be systematic: confirm the myopathic process, define its phenotype and severity, exclude mimics that would change management (especially infectious or bradykinin-mediated angioedema), assess organ involvement (particularly lung and pharyngeal musculature), and screen for associated malignancy before committing to prolonged immunosuppression.

Initial laboratory assessment should focus on objective evidence of muscle injury and the complications that may follow. Serum CK is the most sensitive and widely available biomarker of active myositis; very high values (as in this case, >16,000 U/L) strongly support a myopathic process and alert clinicians to the risk of myoglobinuria and acute kidney injury. Complementary tests that are helpful include aldolase, AST/ALT and LDH, which can all be elevated from muscle sources; a urinalysis for myoglobin and routine renal function tests should be obtained to detect or monitor rhabdomyolysis. Basic inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) are nonspecific but useful adjuncts for the overall clinical picture and to track response to therapy.

Autoantibody testing plays two roles: it helps confirm an autoimmune myopathy and often defines a more precise phenotype with prognostic and therapeutic implications. A screening antinuclear antibody (ANA) is often positive in DM and may display a speckled pattern. More specific testing is with a comprehensive myositis panel that includes myositis-specific and myositis-associated antibodies: antisynthetase antibodies (anti‑Jo‑1 and other tRNA synthetases), anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑MDA5, anti‑NXP2, anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, and, in this case, anti‑SAE1. The pattern of antibodies guides clinicians because associations exist (for example, antisynthetase antibodies associate with interstitial lung disease and mechanic’s hands; anti‑TIF1‑γ and anti‑NXP2 are linked to higher malignancy risk in adults; anti‑MDA5 often presents with clinically amyopathic disease and rapidly progressive ILD; anti‑SAE1 is associated with prominent cutaneous disease, dysphagia and variable malignancy risk). Importantly, a negative panel does not exclude inflammatory myopathy; seronegative disease occurs.

When the initial presentation mimics angioedema, concurrent testing to exclude true histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema should be performed when clinically appropriate. Measurements of complement C4 and quantitative and functional C1 esterase inhibitor (C1‑INH) levels distinguish hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency (bradykinin-mediated) from other causes. Serum tryptase can be measured acutely if an anaphylactic mechanism is suspected. Normal complement and C1‑INH results, together with absent exposure to common culprits (ACE inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitors) and lack of a clear allergic trigger, support the concept of pseudoangioedema from inflammatory soft tissue swelling rather than a primary angioedema disorder.

Imaging has a central role in localizing disease and guiding invasive testing. MRI of affected muscle groups is the preferred modality to identify active muscle edema, fatty replacement, and areas of inflammation that should be targeted for biopsy. In the presented patient, MRI changes in pelvic and thigh muscles helped define active myositis. FDG‑PET can serve a dual purpose: it quantifies systemic inflammatory burden—sometimes highlighting otherwise occult muscle involvement such as prominent head and neck muscle uptake that explains cervical swelling—and simultaneously surveys for occult malignancy. However, clinicians must remember FDG uptake is nonspecific and may reflect inflammation as well as neoplasia. Neck and soft‑tissue CT imaging is useful to exclude alternative local pathology (abscesses, deep neck space infection) and, when combined with high‑resolution CT of the chest, provides essential screening for interstitial lung disease (ILD). Pulmonary function testing with spirometry, lung volumes and DLCO further characterizes respiratory involvement; an extrathoracic or restrictive pattern may point to upper airway or respiratory muscle involvement rather than primary parenchymal ILD, as occurred in this case.

Dysphagia is common in DM and may be clinically occult. Formal assessment with a videofluoroscopic swallow study is the standard to quantify pharyngeal dysfunction and aspiration risk and to guide decisions on nutritional support (e.g., nasogastric feeding) and airway protection. Early recognition of severe pharyngeal weakness is essential because aspiration pneumonitis and respiratory compromise may necessitate urgent interventions, including hospitalization and high‑dose corticosteroids.

Muscle biopsy remains the histopathologic gold standard when the diagnosis is uncertain or when subtype clarification will influence management. Classic dermatomyositis features include perifascicular atrophy and necrosis, perimysial/perivascular inflammation, and complement-mediated capillary damage with membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition in endomysial capillaries. These findings differentiate DM from immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (which has prominent necrosis with comparatively little inflammation) and inclusion body myositis (which has rimmed vacuoles and distinctive inflammatory patterns). Biopsy interpretation should be integrated with clinical, serologic and imaging data.

Before initiating potent immunosuppression, an infection and immunization screen is mandatory. Test for hepatitis B and C, HIV, and assess latent tuberculosis with interferon‑gamma release assays or chest imaging; check varicella zoster and CMV serostatus if relevant to planned therapy. Where agents such as rituximab or high‑dose steroids are planned, ensure appropriate prophylaxis and management plans (e.g., HBV antiviral prophylaxis if HBsAg or HBcAb positive). Update non‑live immunizations (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID‑19, recombinant zoster) where possible prior to immunosuppression. Consider Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis when prolonged high‑dose steroids or lymphocyte‑depleting agents are used.

Malignancy screening in adult DM should be risk‑stratified: the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group recommends intensive baseline screening for patients with high‑risk clinical features—dermatomyositis phenotype, older age, dysphagia, certain autoantibodies and male sex among others. Practical screening commonly includes CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET/CT, age‑appropriate cancer screens (mammography, cervical screening, colonoscopy, prostate evaluation), targeted endoscopy if GI symptoms or high risk, and directed laboratories if indicated. Tumor markers alone lack sensitivity and should not supplant imaging and endoscopic evaluation. Repeat surveillance should be individualized, especially in those with high‑risk antibody profiles or new systemic symptoms.

Finally, establish baseline and serial monitoring to document disease activity and detect treatment toxicity. Quantitative muscle testing (for example, MMT‑8), CK and other muscle enzymes, skin activity scores, periodic pulmonary function tests and repeat imaging when indicated (HRCT for ILD progression, MRI for persistent weakness) form the backbone of follow‑up. Regular laboratory monitoring for cytopenias, liver and renal function is required for most steroid‑sparing agents. In the presented patient, this structured approach allowed early diagnosis, targeted therapy (including IV methylprednisolone, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab), close monitoring of swallowing and respiratory function, and a coordinated malignancy screen—measures that together led to clinical remission and recovery of function.

In summary, when neck edema is unexplained by common otolaryngologic or allergic causes, integrate directed muscle enzyme testing and careful skin/musculoskeletal examination, pursue a full myositis panel and muscle imaging, exclude true angioedema with complement studies where appropriate, assess pulmonary and swallowing involvement, and perform histopathologic confirmation and malignancy screening before prolonged immunosuppression. This stepwise, multidisciplinary evaluation optimizes diagnostic accuracy and guides safe, effective treatment.

## Management of Dermatomyositis with Severe Oropharyngeal Involvement

Severe oropharyngeal involvement in dermatomyositis — manifesting as marked cervical soft‑tissue swelling, dysphagia, aspiration and potential airway compromise — is an uncommon but clinically important presentation that changes priorities in management. The therapeutic strategy must address three parallel goals: rapidly suppress active immune‑mediated inflammation to preserve airway and swallowing musculature; secure airway protection and nutrition; and prevent or mitigate complications of extensive immunosuppression. The case presented above illustrates these principles: prompt recognition of myositis as the driver of neck swelling and dysphagia, early high‑dose immunosuppression (pulse steroids and IVIG) and coordinated supportive care produced clinical remission and recovery of swallowing function.

Initial disposition and multidisciplinary coordination
Patients with significant dysphagia, evidence of aspiration, or any concern for airway compromise should be admitted. Early involvement of a multidisciplinary team is essential: rheumatology/immunology or neuromuscular medicine to direct immunotherapy; ENT to evaluate the airway (flexible nasendoscopy, assessment for upper airway obstruction), anaesthesia/intensive care for airway monitoring and potential intubation/tracheostomy; speech‑language pathology (SLP) and dietetics for swallow assessment and nutritional planning; pulmonology if aspiration pneumonitis or interstitial lung disease (ILD) is suspected; and infectious disease for prophylaxis and screening. In patients with progressive neck edema, ICU monitoring is appropriate if there is evolving stridor, hypoxia, rapidly progressive edema, or need for frequent airway reassessment.

Rapid immunomodulation for severe disease
When myositis threatens airway or bulbar function, early aggressive immunomodulation is indicated to halt inflammation and limit irreversible muscle injury.

- Glucocorticoids: For life‑ or function‑threatening disease, give intravenous methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg daily for 3 days (pulse therapy), then convert to high‑dose oral prednisone (approximately 1 mg/kg/day, often capped around 60 mg/day) with a gradual taper guided by clinical response and laboratory markers. The pulse dose helps reduce inflammation quickly; the oral taper should be individualized and cautious because relapse is common with overly rapid tapering. Monitor for steroid adverse effects (hyperglycaemia, hypertension, infection, steroid myopathy) and institute prophylactic measures discussed below.

- Early steroid‑sparing therapy: Because prolonged high‑dose steroids carry substantial toxicity, a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant should usually be started early once infection is excluded. Choice depends on comorbidities and the clinical phenotype:
  - Mycophenolate mofetil is widely used for muscle and lung involvement and was selected in the case above because of tolerability and daily oral dosing.
  - Methotrexate is effective for muscle disease but should be used cautiously or avoided when ILD is present or when pulmonary fibrosis risk is high.
  - Azathioprine and tacrolimus are alternatives based on tolerance and organ‑specific disease.
  - Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, cyclosporine) have a role, particularly for refractory disease or when steroid‑sparing is required.
  - Cyclophosphamide is reserved for severe, progressive ILD or vasculopathic disease because of its toxicity profile.

- Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG): IVIG has proven efficacy in dermatomyositis and can be particularly beneficial for refractory muscle disease and dysphagia. A common regimen is an initial loading dose of 2 g/kg total (often given over 2–5 days), followed by maintenance cycles of 2 g/kg given every 4–6 weeks as clinically indicated. IVIG is often used early in severe bulbar disease because of its relatively rapid effect and steroid‑sparing potential.

- B‑cell–directed therapy (rituximab): Rituximab is used for refractory cases or when rapid steroid reduction is needed. Regimens include 1 g intravenously on day 0 and 14, or 375 mg/m2 weekly for 4 doses. Evidence is mixed but many patients with refractory inflammatory myopathies respond, and rituximab is a reasonable option when first‑line agents and IVIG are inadequate or contraindicated.

Choice and sequencing of agents should be individualized, and combining modalities (eg, steroids + steroid‑sparing agent + IVIG ± rituximab) is common in severe presentations. In the case above, pulse steroids followed by oral prednisone, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab were used successfully.

Airway and dysphagia management
Bulbar muscle weakness and pharyngeal inflammation raise immediate concerns for aspiration and malnutrition.

- Assessment: Perform bedside swallow screening, and if impairment is suspected, proceed to instrumental evaluation (videofluoroscopic swallow study or flexible endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES)). These studies define physiology (oral vs pharyngeal phase dysfunction), risk of aspiration, and guide rehabilitation strategies.

- Nutrition: If the patient is unsafe for oral intake or aspiration risk is high, institute enteral feeding. A nasogastric tube (NGT) is appropriate for short‑term support (typically weeks). If prolonged dysphagia is expected (>4–6 weeks) or repeated NGT placement is impractical, consider percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG). Early provision of adequate calories and protein supports muscle recovery and prevents weight loss.

- Aspiration precautions and rehabilitation: Implement aspiration precautions (upright feeding, thickened liquids when indicated, oral hygiene) and engage SLP for compensatory strategies, postural maneuvers, texture modification, and targeted swallow exercises. In some centres neuromuscular electrical stimulation is considered adjunctive therapy for chronic dysphagia, but evidence is mixed; its use should be individualized.

- Airway protection: If there is progressive airway obstruction from soft‑tissue edema or bulbar failure with inability to protect the airway, intubation (elective when possible) or tracheostomy (if prolonged ventilatory support or airway protection anticipated) should be performed in conjunction with ENT/anesthesiology. Note that inflammatory edema from myositis does not respond to antihistamines or epinephrine as histaminergic angioedema would—immune suppression is the effective treatment.

Pulmonary considerations
Pulmonary disease in dermatomyositis ranges from aspiration pneumonitis to ILD. Management must be coordinated with pulmonology.

- Aspiration pneumonitis: Treat clinically when suspected; antibiotic choice is guided by local flora and aspiration risk, culture results when available, and clinical severity. Address the source of aspiration with swallowing rehabilitation and enteral feeding as above.

- Interstitial lung disease: If ILD is present or suspected, choose immunosuppression that addresses both muscle and lung disease (eg, mycophenolate, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide for severe cases). Baseline and serial pulmonary function tests (including diffusion capacity) and high‑resolution CT imaging are used to monitor progression.

Infection screening and prophylaxis
Before initiating intense immunosuppression, perform screening for latent or chronic infections and institute appropriate prophylaxis.

- Baseline screening: test for hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti‑HBc), hepatitis C, HIV, and latent tuberculosis (IGRA and consider chest radiography). Determine VZV immune status. CMV serology and other tests may be relevant in certain settings.

- Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis: Indicated for patients on prolonged high‑dose steroids (eg, prednisone ≥20 mg/day for ≥4 weeks) or combination immunosuppression. First‑line prophylaxis is trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX); common regimens include one single‑strength tablet (400/80 mg) daily or one double‑strength tablet (800/160 mg) three times weekly. Alternatives for intolerance or adverse events include inhaled pentamidine (300 mg monthly), atovaquone (1500 mg daily), or dapsone (100 mg daily) after G6PD testing. In the presented case, TMP‑SMX caused haemolysis and inhaled pentamidine was used.

- Vaccination and timing: Administer indicated inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, SARS‑CoV‑2) before initiation of B‑cell depleting therapy or IVIG when possible to maximize response. Live vaccines are contraindicated during significant immunosuppression. For patients seronegative for varicella, immunisation should ideally occur before immunosuppression.

- Reactivation risk: For patients with evidence of prior hepatitis B infection (anti‑HBc positive), consult hepatology for antiviral prophylaxis when using rituximab or other B‑cell depleting agents.

Supportive care and complication prevention
High‑dose steroids and multimodal immunosuppression require proactive supportive measures.

- Bone health: Start calcium and vitamin D and consider bisphosphonates for patients expected to be on prolonged (>3 months) high‑dose steroids or who have other osteoporosis risk factors.

- Gastroprotection: Consider PPI/H2 blocker for patients at high risk of steroid‑related peptic disease.

- Thromboprophylaxis: Hospitalized, immobilized patients should receive pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis unless contraindicated.

- Metabolic monitoring: Regularly monitor blood glucose and blood pressure while on high‑dose steroids; modify therapy as needed.

- Laboratory surveillance: Baseline and periodic CBC, renal and liver function tests (because many immunosuppressants are hepatically metabolized or myelosuppressive), creatine kinase and other muscle enzymes (aldolase, LDH), and drug‑specific monitoring (eg, TPMT activity if considering thiopurines). Monitor immunoglobulin levels during repeated IVIG and B‑cell depletion therapy.

- Skin care: Sun protection is important to limit cutaneous flares; consider antimalarial agents (hydroxychloroquine) for refractory cutaneous disease in appropriate patients, with baseline ophthalmologic assessment.

Rehabilitation and return to function
Begin physiotherapy and occupational therapy early, even during the acute phase, to limit deconditioning. Exercise programs in inflammatory myopathies emphasize submaximal, gradually progressive, and monitored strengthening once disease activity is controlled; overly aggressive exercise during active inflammation can worsen muscle injury. Swallowing rehabilitation (SLP‑led) should be an ongoing part of recovery until safe oral intake returns.

Assessment of response and steroid tapering
Treatment response is judged clinically (improvement in strength, swallow function, resolution of edema), and by laboratory and imaging markers (falling CK, normalization of liver enzymes, improvement on MRI or PET where used). In bulbar disease, clinical improvement in swallowing and protective airway reflexes is a priority even if CK lags.

Taper steroids slowly once improvement is sustained and steroid‑sparing agents are active. A pragmatic approach is to reduce prednisone by 5–10 mg every 1–2 weeks until reaching 20 mg/day, then more slowly (eg, 2.5–5 mg decrements) while reassessing for relapse. If relapse occurs during taper, escalate therapy and re‑evaluate adherence and infection.

Cancer screening and long‑term surveillance
Because dermatomyositis confers an increased risk of malignancy, comprehensive baseline malignancy screening is recommended and should be individualized based on age, sex, risk factors, and specific autoantibody profile. Ongoing vigilance for new symptoms and routine surveillance (appropriate imaging and endoscopies guided by risk) is part of longitudinal care.

Putting it together — an approach illustrated by the case
The patient in the case presented with subacute neck swelling, dysphagia and very high CK. Recognizing that neck edema was inflammatory rather than histaminergic led to hospital admission, rapid diagnostic evaluation (nailfold capillaroscopy, CK, MRI and PET muscle imaging, muscle biopsy) and aggressive therapy. He received pulse methylprednisolone, early IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab, with coordinated ENT assessment, swallow evaluation, nasogastric feeding and PJP prophylaxis (later modified due to intolerance). The combined approach reversed life‑threatening inflammation, allowed recovery of bulbar function, and illustrates the core principles above: early aggressive immunomodulation for threatened bulbar or airway function, careful airway and nutritional support, infection prevention, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation.

Key practical points
- Admit and monitor patients with severe dysphagia, aspiration or evolving airway compromise; involve ENT, ICU, SLP, nutrition and pulmonology early.
- Use pulse IV methylprednisolone (500–1000 mg daily ×3) for severe disease, convert to high‑dose oral prednisone, and add a steroid‑sparing agent early.
- IVIG is effective for refractory disease and dysphagia; typical dosing is 2 g/kg total with maintenance cycles every 4–6 weeks.
- Consider rituximab for refractory or severe disease requiring rapid steroid sparing.
- Protect the airway and provide enteral nutrition (NGT or PEG based on expected duration); intensify swallow rehabilitation.
- Screen for and prevent opportunistic infections (PJP prophylaxis, vaccinate with inactivated vaccines pre‑treatment), and monitor for treatment toxicity.
- Start rehabilitation early and taper immunosuppression based on clinical and laboratory response, with careful long‑term surveillance for relapse and malignancy.

## Prognosis, Malignancy Surveillance, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis in dermatomyositis (DM) is highly variable and largely determined by the extent and rapidity of organ involvement and by how quickly immuno-inflammatory activity is identified and controlled. Inflammatory features that denote high disease activity — severe proximal weakness, frank myonecrosis with markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), extensive subcutaneous or facial/neck oedema, progressive dysphagia and clinically significant interstitial lung disease (ILD) — are associated with increased short‑term morbidity and a greater need for aggressive, combination immunosuppression. These same features, however, are often the manifestations that prompt admission and early treatment; when treated promptly and intensively, many patients achieve clinical remission or marked improvement, as illustrated by this case, in which profound CK elevation, prominent cervical oedema and dysphagia all improved after high‑dose steroids, IVIG and steroid‑sparing agents. Conversely, delayed recognition — for example, when neck swelling is misclassified as idiopathic angioedema or an isolated ENT problem — risks complications such as aspiration pneumonitis, nutritional compromise and respiratory failure, and worsens long‑term functional outcome.

Autoantibody profile provides useful prognostic information. SAE1‑antibody–positive DM is classically associated with prominent cutaneous disease and a relatively high frequency of dysphagia and ILD. Published cohorts suggest an appreciable association with malignancy (reported rates vary by series, with one review noting ~18% in SAE cohorts), and therefore these patients warrant careful cancer surveillance at baseline and during follow‑up. The presence of subcutaneous oedema, significant bulbar involvement or early ILD in any DM subtype signals high inflammatory burden and should lower the threshold for inpatient care, early multidisciplinary assessment (rheumatology/immunology, neurology, ENT/speech pathology, respiratory medicine) and combination therapy rather than prolonged single‑agent steroid tapering.

Malignancy surveillance in DM must be structured and multimodal. No single tumour marker or test is sufficiently sensitive to exclude an occult cancer. At diagnosis, risk stratification should consider age, sex, cutaneous phenotype, antibody status (e.g., anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑NXP2, SAE1), presence of dysphagia or rapid weight loss, and smoking history. Practical baseline evaluation often includes: thorough history and physical exam focused on common DM‑associated cancers; age‑ and sex‑appropriate standard screening (mammography, cervical screening, colonoscopy as indicated, PSA where appropriate); cross‑sectional imaging — either CT chest/abdomen/ pelvis or FDG‑PET/CT depending on availability, local practice and pretest probability; and targeted investigations based on symptoms (e.g., upper and lower endoscopy for GI symptoms or if risk factors suggest GI malignancy). For SAE1‑positive disease, many centres will add PET/CT and consider gastrointestinal endoscopy because of the reported associations and because tumour markers alone are insensitive. Surveillance intensity should be highest in the first 1–3 years after diagnosis when paraneoplastic associations are most likely to present; a reasonable pragmatic approach is repeat focused imaging or reassessment at 6–12 months and again at 12–36 months if the initial screen is negative but risk remains elevated, with symptom‑triggered re‑evaluation thereafter. These intervals should be individualized — for example, shorter intervals for older patients, those with high‑risk antibodies or unexplained constitutional symptoms. Importantly, tumor markers (CEA, CA‑125, CA19‑9, PSA) can supplement but should not replace structured imaging and endoscopic evaluation.

Long‑term monitoring must address both disease activity and complications of the disease and its treatment. Disease activity can be followed clinically (manual muscle testing, patient‑reported function, dysphagia assessment) and biochemically (serial CK, aldolase, transaminases, LDH), recognizing that CK may normalize despite ongoing weakness in some chronic cases. For suspected or established ILD obtain baseline spirometry with DLCO and high‑resolution CT chest; repeat testing every 3–6 months initially for progressive disease or every 6–12 months for stable ILD, with earlier reassessment for new respiratory symptoms. Bulbar dysfunction mandates objective swallow assessment (video fluoroscopy or fibreoptic evaluation), early involvement of speech and language therapy, and nutritional planning including temporary enteral feeding when aspiration risk or malnutrition is significant, as in this patient.

Surveillance for complications and treatment toxicities is mandatory. Aspiration pneumonia and malnutrition are frequent and should be anticipated in patients with dysphagia; preventive measures include swallow therapy, dietary modification, and consideration of enteral feeding until safe swallow is restored. Cardiopulmonary complications include myocarditis, arrhythmia and pulmonary hypertension; screen with ECG and consider cardiac biomarkers, echocardiography or cardiac MRI if there are suggestive symptoms or unexplained troponin/BNP elevation. Venous thromboembolism risk is increased in systemic inflammatory states and during periods of immobility or hospitalization; assess and provide prophylaxis per usual thromboprophylaxis guidelines. Chronic corticosteroid exposure requires monitoring for hyperglycaemia, hypertension, osteoporosis (baseline DXA and commence bone protection early for prolonged high‑dose therapy), weight gain, mood changes and infection risk.

Medication‑specific monitoring and counselling reduce iatrogenic harm. Baseline infectious screening before significant immunosuppression should include hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti‑HBc), hepatitis C, and HIV serology, and consideration of TB screening. For rituximab, check baseline immunoglobulins and hepatitis B markers (anti‑HBc is critical) because B‑cell depletion can reactivate HBV; vaccinate against pneumococcus and seasonal influenza and, where possible, complete immunizations before B‑cell depleting therapy because vaccine responses are blunted post‑rituximab. Periodic CBC and LFTs are required for azathioprine, methotrexate and mycophenolate — the latter requires regular full blood counts and liver function checks and counselling on contraception (mycophenolate is teratogenic). Azathioprine should be preceded by TPMT testing where available. IVIG therapy requires monitoring of renal function, vigilance for infusion reactions and thrombotic events, and attention to fluid status. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis should be considered for patients on prolonged high‑dose steroids combined with other immunosuppressants — TMP‑SMX is first choice but alternatives (aerosolized pentamidine, atovaquone) are available for those intolerant, as occurred in our patient. Finally, counsel patients about live vaccines: generally avoid live attenuated vaccines while on significant immunosuppression; use inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, SARS‑CoV‑2) and time administration relative to B‑cell–depleting agents when possible.

Patient education is a cornerstone of long‑term management. Teach patients to recognize early warning signs of a flare — new or worsening proximal weakness, increasing difficulty with swallowing or breathing, new or expanding rash, unexplained fevers or weight loss — and to seek prompt review. Photoprotection (broad‑spectrum sunscreen, protective clothing) is important because ultraviolet exposure commonly worsens cutaneous disease. Discuss contraception and pregnancy planning early for people of childbearing potential: methotrexate and mycophenolate are teratogenic and must be stopped well in advance of conception; for men and women, plan immunosuppressive regimens in collaboration with obstetrics where pregnancy is desired. Reinforce infection vigilance and adherence to prophylactic strategies when indicated.

Clinical pearls to avoid missed diagnoses and optimize outcomes
- In any case of “idiopathic angioedema” with a subacute course, absent urticaria, normal C1‑INH/C4 and lack of response to antihistamines, measure CK and perform a careful skin and nailfold examination. Pseudoangioedema from underlying myositis is an uncommon but critical mimic.  
- Nailfold capillaroscopy, periungual changes, Holster or shawl signs and subtle limb rashes may be the clues that convert a diagnostic cul‑de‑sac into the correct systemic diagnosis.  
- Tumour markers alone are insufficient to rule out occult malignancy; use a structured approach (history, examination, cross‑sectional imaging or PET, age‑appropriate cancer screening and targeted endoscopy) and maintain vigilance for the first 1–3 years after DM diagnosis.  
- Severe dysphagia and significant neck/facial oedema identify patients at risk for aspiration and nutritional compromise and should prompt early multidisciplinary intervention (ENT, speech pathology, nutrition, respiratory) and aggressive immunomodulation.  
- Before initiating rituximab or other lymphocyte‑depleting therapies, screen for HBV/ HCV and ensure vaccination where feasible; monitor immunoglobulins during follow‑up.  
- Inpatients with active, severe DM benefit from early combination therapy (high‑dose corticosteroids plus a steroid‑sparing agent ± IVIG ± biologic therapy) rather than prolonged mono‑therapy in order to limit morbidity from ongoing inflammation.

In summary, the clinical course of DM can be favorable when high disease activity is recognized early and treated aggressively; SAE1‑positive patients require particular vigilance for dysphagia, ILD and occult malignancy. A structured surveillance plan for cancer, pulmonary and cardiac complications, together with careful monitoring for immunosuppressive toxicities and an emphasis on patient education, will optimize long‑term outcomes and reduce preventable morbidity — lessons underscored by the successful recovery in this case.
