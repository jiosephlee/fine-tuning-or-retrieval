# Dermatomyositis Presenting as Cervical “Angioedema”: Recognizing Pseudoangioedema and Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis

## Case-Based Introduction and Clinical Approach

A man in his 40s presented with a 3-week history of progressive neck swelling and new proximal lower extremity weakness. Initial evaluation focused on deep neck space infection and “idiopathic angioedema,” given diffuse cervical soft tissue edema on CT and elevated inflammatory markers. Flexible nasendoscopy revealed no structural airway lesions, and infectious workup was unrevealing. Closer inspection demonstrated periungual capillary changes and subtle rash; creatine kinase (CK) returned at 16,184 U/L. MRI and FDG-PET showed widespread myositis, and muscle biopsy revealed perifascicular injury with capillary complement deposition. A myositis antibody line blot was strongly positive for anti–small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1 (SAE1) antibodies. The working diagnosis shifted decisively to dermatomyositis (DM) with prominent cervical pseudoangioedema and pharyngeal dysphagia, and treatment with high-dose glucocorticoids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab achieved remission.

This case illustrates a practical imperative: unexplained cervicofacial edema—especially when nonpainful, nonpruritic, and refractory to typical angioedema therapies—demands consideration of inflammatory myopathy. Early bedside clues (periungual erythema/telangiectasias, photodistributed rash, proximal weakness, dysphagia) and a simple laboratory test (CK) can redirect the workup from otolaryngology and allergy pathways to rheumatology and neuromuscular evaluation, averting delays in immunosuppression.

## Building the Differential Diagnosis of Cervical Swelling

Cervical soft tissue swelling spans several domains:

- Infectious/inflammatory: cellulitis, erysipelas, bacterial sialadenitis, Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space infection, necrotizing fasciitis.
- Vascular/venous: superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome, internal jugular thrombosis.
- Allergic/bradykinin-mediated angioedema: histaminergic (with urticaria/pruritus; rapid response to antihistamines/epinephrine), bradykinin-mediated (ACE inhibitor–induced, hereditary or acquired C1-inhibitor deficiency; often lacks urticaria; C4 low in many cases).
- Infiltrative/granulomatous: orofacial granulomatosis, sarcoidosis.
- Neoplastic: lymphoma or other malignancies causing lymphatic obstruction.
- Myopathic/autoimmune: dermatomyositis with pseudoangioedema due to subcutaneous and muscle edema, often associated with dysphagia and proximal weakness.

In this patient, several features de-emphasized classic angioedema or deep neck infection: afebrile status; normal airway examination aside from secretions; persistent, non-tender, non-pruritic neck fullness; normal complement/C1 inhibitor parameters; and lack of an offending medication. Conversely, findings that elevate suspicion for inflammatory myopathy included proximal weakness, dysphagia, nailfold capillary abnormalities, a photodistributed rash, markedly elevated CK, and imaging consistent with myositis. The teaching point is straightforward: add CK to the initial panel for idiopathic cervicofacial swelling, especially when atypical for histamine/bradykinin-mediated angioedema and when cutaneous or neuromuscular signs are present.

## Dermatomyositis: Pathophysiology and Disease Mechanisms

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by a systemic microangiopathy with distinctive cutaneous manifestations and skeletal muscle involvement. Modern concepts integrate two interlocking processes:

- Complement-mediated microvascular injury: Activation of the classical complement pathway results in membrane attack complex (C5b-9) deposition on endomysial capillaries. Capillary dropout leads to ischemia in muscle fascicles, especially at the periphery, producing perifascicular fiber atrophy and necrosis—the histologic signature of DM. Similar microvascular injury in skin underlies the photosensitive rash, periungual capillary changes, and characteristic edema.

- Type I interferon signaling: DM tissues display a robust type I interferon “signature,” with upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes (e.g., MxA). Interferons amplify endothelial and myofiber injury, promote antigen presentation, and sustain autoimmunity. The interferon milieu likely contributes to the profound cutaneous features and some systemic complications (e.g., interstitial lung disease in selected serotypes).

Autoantibodies partition DM into clinically meaningful subsets (myositis-specific autoantibodies, MSA). Anti-SAE1 targets the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme that initiates “sumoylation,” a post-translational modification influencing protein stability and transcriptional control. The mechanistic link between anti-SAE1 and tissue injury is incompletely defined, but the phenotype has distinctive tendencies: prominent cutaneous disease (heliotrope, Gottron’s, shawl/V/holster signs), frequent dysphagia, and a nontrivial prevalence of interstitial lung disease; malignancy risk appears intermediate relative to the highest-risk DM serotypes (e.g., anti–TIF1-γ, anti–NXP2).

Why “pseudoangioedema”? In DM, edema stems from microvascular leakage and inflammation in the dermis/subcutis and, when present in the neck, from inflamed strap and pharyngeal musculature. The swelling is neither histamine- nor bradykinin-mediated; therefore, complement studies (C4, C1-inhibitor) are normal, and responses to antihistamines/bradykinin antagonists are poor. Dysphagia reflects involvement of striated pharyngeal and upper esophageal muscles and impaired laryngeal elevation, predisposing to microaspiration and aspiration pneumonitis, as observed in this case. Pulmonary findings in DM can arise from aspiration, respiratory muscle weakness, and, in serotype-specific contexts, primary interstitial lung disease.

## Clinical Presentation and Examination

Classic DM couples a pathognomonic or characteristic rash with proximal limb-girdle weakness. Heliotrope eruption and Gottron’s papules/erythema over extensor surfaces are classic; shawl and V-signs denote photosensitive patches over the shoulders, upper back, and anterior chest. Holster sign (lateral thigh plaques) and periungual telangiectasias or microhemorrhages reflect microangiopathy. Edema of face and neck is less common but may dominate the early picture and mislead initial triage.

Muscle symptoms range from mild myalgia to profound weakness; neck flexors are often involved. Oropharyngeal dysphagia is common and correlates with poor outcomes due to aspiration. Interstitial lung disease may present with cough/dyspnea and restrictive physiology; however, pulmonary function can also reflect respiratory muscle weakness or extrathoracic dysfunction in pharyngeal/laryngeal weakness. Cardiac involvement (arrhythmias, myocarditis) is underrecognized and warrants vigilance.

In this patient, subtle cutaneous and nailfold findings, hip flexor weakness, and dysphagia coexisted with striking cervical edema—an atypical but instructive presentation.

## Diagnostic Workup: From Bedside Clues to Confirmatory Studies

1) Screening laboratories
- Muscle enzymes: CK is the most sensitive initial marker in classic DM; aldolase, LDH, AST, and ALT often accompany CK elevations. Notably, AST and ALT are present in muscle; disproportionate transaminase elevation may be misattributed to hepatic disease unless CK is checked.
- Inflammatory markers: CRP/ESR may be normal or mildly elevated; marked elevation suggests complications (infection, extensive tissue injury) but does not exclude DM.
- Autoantibodies: ANA is commonly positive with a speckled pattern. Myositis panels detect MSAs (e.g., SAE1, TIF1-γ, Mi-2, MDA5, NXP2, antisynthetase such as Jo-1/OJ/PL-7/PL-12) and myositis-associated antibodies (MAA) seen with overlap syndromes. Commercial line blots have variable specificity; correlation with phenotype and, when needed, orthogonal confirmation is prudent.

2) Imaging
- MRI of symptomatic muscle groups (STIR/T2 fat-suppressed sequences) demonstrates edema consistent with active myositis, aids biopsy site selection, and monitors treatment response. In the case presented, thigh MRI localized active inflammation across pelvic and thigh muscles.
- FDG-PET can show diffuse muscular uptake in active myositis and simultaneously screen for occult malignancy; however, PET-negative studies do not exclude cancer and should complement, not replace, age- and risk-appropriate screening.
- CT neck/chest can delineate soft tissue swelling, aspiration-related opacities, and ILD; HRCT is preferred when ILD is suspected.

3) Electrophysiology
- EMG can support a myopathic process and identify active denervation/reinnervation but is not mandatory when clinical, serologic, imaging, and histologic evidence is compelling.

4) Tissue diagnosis
- Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for confirming DM in ambiguous cases. Perifascicular fiber atrophy/necrosis and capillary C5b-9 deposition are characteristic. Interferon-inducible markers (e.g., MxA) and capillary pathology strengthen the diagnosis. The biopsy in this patient demonstrated perifascicular injury with membrane attack complex deposition—classic for DM.

5) Dysphagia evaluation
- Videofluoroscopic swallow study quantifies aspiration risk, guides diet texture modification, and informs decisions regarding enteral access. Findings of severe pharyngeal phase dysfunction warrant early nasogastric or percutaneous feeding to prevent aspiration and malnutrition.

6) Pulmonary assessment
- Pulmonary function testing in DM may show a restrictive pattern with reduced TLC and low maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures in respiratory muscle weakness; flow-volume loops may show extrathoracic features with laryngeal/pharyngeal dysfunction. HRCT evaluates ILD.

7) Malignancy screening
- DM confers an elevated cancer risk, highest in the first 3 years after onset and modulated by age, sex, and autoantibody profile. Contemporary approaches incorporate IMACS guidance: stratify risk by DM subtype and clinical features (e.g., dysphagia, age >40–50, male sex, certain autoantibodies). High-risk patients merit thorough baseline screening (history/physical, targeted labs, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, and additional organ-specific studies such as colonoscopy/gastroscopy based on age/region) and consideration of PET/CT. Continue vigilant surveillance during the early years following diagnosis. In the present case, the combination of dermatomyositis, dysphagia, male sex, and SAE1 seropositivity prompted comprehensive baseline imaging and serial follow-up.

8) Excluding mimics of cervicofacial edema
- For suspected angioedema: assess for urticaria, pruritus, episodic pattern, medication triggers (ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, neprilysin inhibitors, NSAIDs), family history. Obtain C4 and C1 inhibitor quantity/function when bradykinin-mediated angioedema is plausible. In this patient, normal complement and the clinical picture argued strongly against bradykinin-mediated disease.

## Distinguishing Dermatomyositis Pseudoangioedema from True Angioedema

- Timing and triggers: Histaminergic angioedema has rapid onset, often with urticaria and pruritus; bradykinin-mediated angioedema can be insidious but often involves lips/tongue and recurs, with gastrointestinal colic. DM edema evolves subacutely and coexists with photosensitive rash and systemic myopathic features.
- Response to therapy: Histaminergic angioedema responds to antihistamines, corticosteroids, epinephrine; bradykinin-mediated angioedema may respond to C1-INH concentrate, bradykinin B2 antagonists, or kallikrein inhibitors. DM pseudoangioedema improves with immunosuppression directed at myositis, not anti-bradykinin strategies.
- Laboratory pattern: Angioedema due to C1-INH deficiency features low C4 and abnormal C1-INH quantity/function. DM shows elevated CK/aldolase and myositis-specific autoantibodies; complement studies are typically normal.
- Associated signs: Nailfold capillary changes, heliotrope/Gottron’s/shawl/V/holster signs, proximal weakness, and dysphagia favor DM.

## Management Principles

Treatment goals are to quell immune-mediated injury, restore strength and swallowing, protect vital organs (airway, lungs, heart), prevent infections and steroid toxicity, and address cancer risk.

1) Initial immunosuppression
- Glucocorticoids: Prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day is standard for active myositis. In severe presentations—bulbar involvement, rapidly progressive weakness, impending respiratory compromise—initiate intravenous methylprednisolone (e.g., 500–1,000 mg daily for 3 days) before transitioning to oral therapy. Taper is guided by strength, CK trends, and extramuscular disease.
- IVIG: Robust evidence supports IVIG in active DM, including for refractory skin disease and dysphagia. It is appropriate up front in severe bulbar involvement or steroid-intolerant patients. Typical dosing is 2 g/kg per cycle administered every 4 weeks initially.
- Early steroid-sparing agents: Because prolonged high-dose steroids are morbid and relapses are common, add a second-line agent early. Options include methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, and calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus/cyclosporine). Choice is individualized based on comorbidities, ILD status, patient preference, and tolerability. In this case, mycophenolate was favored given pulmonary considerations and patient preferences.

2) Escalation for refractory disease or specific phenotypes
- Rituximab can be effective in steroid/DMARD-refractory DM, including severe dysphagia and ILD in overlap settings.
- Calcineurin inhibitors are particularly useful when ILD coexists, especially in antisynthetase or MDA5 phenotypes.
- Cyclophosphamide may be reserved for severe, progressive ILD not controlled with other agents.
- JAK inhibitors (e.g., tofacitinib) are emerging for refractory cutaneous DM and selected ILD phenotypes, leveraging the interferon pathway; use remains off-label and specialist-driven.

3) Supportive and preventive care
- Airway and swallowing: Early speech-language therapy, videofluoroscopic guidance, dietary modification, and timely nasogastric or percutaneous feeding prevent aspiration and maintain nutrition. Head-of-bed elevation and oral hygiene reduce pneumonia risk.
- Pulmonary rehabilitation and respiratory muscle training can support recovery when weakness contributes to dyspnea.
- Infection prevention: PJP prophylaxis should be considered with high-dose steroids and additional immunosuppression; alternatives (e.g., inhaled pentamidine) are acceptable when TMP-SMX is not tolerated. Update vaccinations (influenza, pneumococcal, zoster where appropriate) before or early during therapy when feasible.
- Bone and metabolic health: Calcium/vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, and bisphosphonates for high fracture risk; monitor glucose, blood pressure, and lipids during steroid therapy.
- Dermatologic care: Sun protection and topical agents complement systemic therapy for cutaneous disease.
- Physical therapy: Supervised, progressive resistance and aerobic training enhances strength recovery and function and is safe in controlled disease.
- Cardiac monitoring: Baseline ECG and troponin, with echocardiography as indicated; maintain a low threshold to evaluate new dyspnea, chest pain, or palpitations.

4) Malignancy surveillance
- Implement a risk-adapted baseline evaluation and maintain heightened surveillance in the first 3 years. Partner with primary care and oncology as needed, and ensure age-appropriate cancer screening (e.g., colonoscopy, Pap test; prostate evaluation; in women, pelvic ultrasound/mammography as indicated) and organ-directed imaging when clinically suspected.

5) Monitoring and tapering
- Track patient-reported function and objective strength, CK/aldolase, cutaneous activity, and extramuscular involvement (swallow function, pulmonary parameters). Steroid taper proceeds slowly once strength and enzymes stabilize; background agents are continued for maintenance and only tapered after sustained remission. Relapses often present first in skin or CK; adjust therapy preemptively.

In the presented case, aggressive induction with IV methylprednisolone and IVIG, followed by prednisone taper, mycophenolate, and rituximab, rapidly controlled bulbar disease and normalized CK. Dysphagia required months of targeted rehabilitation and enteral support but resolved fully with disease control.

## Prognosis and Outcomes

Outcomes in DM vary by serotype and organ involvement. Major determinants include:

- Malignancy: Highest risk around diagnosis and within 3 years thereafter; TIF1-γ and NXP2 confer the greatest association. Vigilant screening can enable early detection and improve outcomes.
- ILD: Particularly in MDA5- and antisynthetase-associated disease; early recognition and combination immunosuppression improve survival.
- Dysphagia and aspiration: Bulbar disease increases morbidity; prompt therapy and multidisciplinary care are critical.
- Cardiac involvement: Underappreciated contributor to morbidity; early detection is key.

Anti-SAE1 DM often responds to immunosuppression; dysphagia is common but, as in this patient, may recover with timely, intensive therapy. Most strength gains occur in the first few months; cutaneous disease may lag and require ongoing management. Long-term maintenance with a steroid-sparing agent and/or IVIG reduces relapse risk.

## Clinical Pearls and Pitfalls

- In any “idiopathic” cervicofacial swelling, check CK and look for nailfold capillary changes and photodistributed rashes. Pseudoangioedema from DM can mimic otolaryngologic emergencies.
- DM edema is not histamine- or bradykinin-mediated; normal C4 and C1-inhibitor parameters and poor response to angioedema therapies are clues to an alternative diagnosis.
- AST and ALT elevations can be myogenic; always interpret transaminases alongside CK and aldolase in suspected myopathy.
- Perifascicular atrophy/necrosis and capillary C5b-9 deposition on muscle biopsy are characteristic of DM. MRI localizes active disease and helps target biopsy; FDG-PET can both map myositis and screen for malignancy.
- Dysphagia is common in DM and portends aspiration risk; early videofluoroscopic assessment, enteral nutrition, and aggressive immunosuppression are lifesaving.
- IVIG improves muscle and skin disease in DM and is particularly valuable for severe bulbar involvement or steroid-refractory cases.
- Choose steroid-sparing agents based on phenotype and comorbidities. Mycophenolate and calcineurin inhibitors are favored in many ILD scenarios; methotrexate and azathioprine remain effective in muscle and skin-predominant disease when pulmonary toxicity risk is low.
- Malignancy risk is real; apply risk-stratified, time-sensitive cancer screening and maintain surveillance for at least 3 years after diagnosis.
- Rehabilitation is treatment: structured exercise augments immunotherapy, accelerates strength recovery, and mitigates steroid toxicity.
- Early, correct attribution of “angioedema” to dermatomyositis in atypical cases prevents delays that can cost swallow function, lung health, and life.

## Take-Home Points

- Dermatomyositis can present with prominent neck edema that mimics angioedema; subtle skin findings and proximal weakness are critical clues.
- A markedly elevated CK should immediately pivot the workup toward inflammatory myopathy; normal complement studies argue against bradykinin-mediated angioedema.
- Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis often features striking cutaneous disease, frequent dysphagia, and a measurable malignancy risk; biopsy shows perifascicular injury with complement deposition.
- Severe bulbar involvement warrants high-dose glucocorticoids, early IVIG, and prompt initiation of a steroid-sparing agent; rituximab and other agents are added for refractory disease.
- Multidisciplinary care—ENT, rheumatology, neurology, pulmonology, speech-language pathology, nutrition, and primary care—optimizes safety, accelerates recovery, and addresses cancer surveillance.
