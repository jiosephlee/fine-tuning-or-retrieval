# The Dangerous Swallow: Recognizing and Managing Myositis Dysphagia

What’s striking about the case that prompted this post is how a potentially life‑threatening myositis complication — severe dysphagia with aspiration risk — presented not with classic proximal limb weakness or Gottron’s papules, but as a “neck swelling” misinterpreted initially as angioedema or deep neck infection. The patient turned out to have SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with marked myositis of the head and neck on PET/MRI, severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy, aspiration pneumonitis on CT, and profound CK elevation. This is a great reminder: when neck fullness, dysphagia, cough with secretions, or unexplained “pseudo‑angioedema” show up, think myositis — and act early to protect the airway, prevent aspiration, and start targeted therapy.

## Why dysphagia in inflammatory myopathy matters
Dysphagia occurs in many dermatomyositis and other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM). It may be subtle at first, progress rapidly, and reflect involvement of oropharyngeal and upper esophageal striated muscle — not a primary ENT lesion. Consequences include aspiration (silent or overt), aspiration pneumonitis, weight loss, malnutrition, and need for prolonged enteral support. In SAE1‑positive disease dysphagia and interstitial lung disease are not uncommon.

In the case above, the patient had increased secretions and coughing on nasendoscopy, a severe pharyngeal residue and penetration on videofluoroscopy, CT chest changes consistent with aspiration pneumonitis, and restrictive pulmonary function abnormalities reflecting weakness of extrathoracic respiratory muscles. The team moved quickly: CK measurement, imaging, VFSS, early SLP input, NG feeding and aggressive immunosuppression — and avoided emergent airway catastrophe.

## How to spot myositis dysphagia at the bedside
Be suspicious when dysphagia co‑exists with any of the following:
- proximal limb weakness (even mild), myalgias, markedly elevated CK, or subtle cutaneous features of dermatomyositis (periungual changes, shawl/V‑neck sign, heliotrope, Holster sign).
- neck fullness/swelling that is non‑tender, non‑infective in pattern, or accompanies voice/airway secretions and coughing.
- cough during swallow testing or frequent clearing, and history of recurrent chest infections.

Clinical pearls:
- Dysphagia can be silent. Absence of overt choking does not rule out aspiration risk.
- Early CK testing is cheap, fast and high yield when ENT/ED findings don’t explain the problem.
- Nailfold capillaroscopy/periungual changes can be the dermatologic clue that changes the whole differential.

## Early multidisciplinary steps — don’t wait
1) Call speech‑language pathology (SLP) early. A focused bedside swallow screen by SLP helps decide urgency of formal assessment and need for NPO status.
2) If there is any concern on bedside assessment (coughing, wet voice, pooling, weak cough) go straight to instrumental evaluation — videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) depending on availability and patient stability.
3) Put the patient NPO until SLP/instrumental evaluation unless you have unequivocal bedside evidence of safe oral intake.
4) Secure nutrition: temporary nasogastric tube feeding is often the first line for reversible inflammatory myopathy causing dysphagia; PEG only if prolonged (>4–6 weeks) or if long‑term enteral access is anticipated.
5) Manage secretions aggressively — they cause aspiration and airway compromise:
   - Anticholinergic agents (glycopyrrolate) can reduce drooling in selected patients; consider risks.
   - Frequent suctioning, repositioning, and oral hygiene reduce bacterial load.
   - If secretion burden is severe and unresponsive, consult ENT for consideration of more invasive airway/airway protection procedures.

Grounding in the case: this patient required nasogastric feeding for two months while immunotherapy worked — a pragmatic, reversible approach.

## Imaging and pulmonary testing — interpret with the myositis lens
- CT chest clues consistent with aspiration pneumonitis: peribronchial consolidation, dependent ground‑glass or patchy airspace opacities—especially in mid‑to‑lower zones and with a peribronchial distribution — should raise suspicion for aspiration rather than primary fibrosing ILD.
- FDG‑PET and MRI can localize active muscle inflammation in head/neck and pharyngeal musculature; useful when the clinical picture is unclear.
- Pulmonary function testing: an "extrathoracic restrictive" pattern or reduced vital capacity that is worse in supine position suggests respiratory muscle weakness (bulbar/diaphragmatic involvement). This pattern differs from classical parenchymal restriction seen with fibrosis and should prompt assessment of respiratory muscle strength and swallowing safety.

In the case, PFTs showed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern without significant bronchodilator response — supporting muscle weakness rather than airway disease. CT showed peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration.

## When to escalate airway and respiratory support
Escalate early if any of the following exist:
- Signs of impaired airway protection: ineffective cough, accumulation of secretions, recurrent desaturation, stridor, or progressive bulbar weakness.
- Radiological progression of aspiration pneumonia or hypoxaemia despite oxygen and conservative measures.
- Rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness including neck flexors, with evidence of declining vital capacity or inability to clear secretions.

Escalation options:
- Admit to a monitored setting; frequent respiratory observation and serial vital capacity measurements.
- Consider elective intubation for airway protection when bulbar failure is progressive or secretions cannot be managed noninvasively.
- In prolonged ventilatory failure or recurrent aspiration, discuss tracheostomy for airway protection and pulmonary toilet with the multidisciplinary team.
- Early immunomodulatory therapy (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG ± steroid‑sparing agents/biologics) is essential — treating the underlying myositis is what ultimately improves swallowing function.

In our reported patient, prompt high‑dose IV methylprednisolone, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab were used because of significant airway muscle involvement — and the patient recovered swallowing over weeks to months.

## Practical SLP and medical management tips
- Use VFSS to define the physiology: oral vs pharyngeal phase impairment, residue, penetration/aspiration, and need for compensatory strategies (chin tuck, head turn, thickened fluids).
- FEES is invaluable at bedside when transport to radiology is unsafe or to directly visualize secretions, pooling, and laryngeal sensation.
- Implement compensatory strategies only if VFSS/FEES show they are effective; do not rely on maneuvers alone if aspiration is significant.
- Enteral route: NG tube for short‑term use; consider PEG only when recovery is uncertain or prolonged.
- Maintain excellent oral hygiene and reduce oral bacterial load to limit aspiration pneumonia severity.
- Coordinate immunotherapy early with rheumatology/immunology — dysphagia often improves with disease control, but recovery can lag weeks to months.

## Take‑home messages
- Myositis (including dermatomyositis) can present with severe dysphagia and head/neck “pseudo‑angioedema”; suspect it when neck swelling is unexplained or accompanied by weakness, elevated CK or cutaneous signs.
- Early SLP involvement and instrumental assessment (VFSS or FEES) are mandatory when aspiration risk is suspected.
- Protect the airway: keep NPO until assessed, use NG feeding for short‑term nutrition, manage secretions aggressively, and escalate to intubation/tracheostomy if airway protection fails.
- Interpret CT chest and PFTs with the myositis context — peribronchial consolidation suggests aspiration pneumonitis and an extrathoracic restrictive pattern supports respiratory muscle weakness.
- Timely immunosuppression matters: treating the myositis is the definitive step to recovering swallow function, but expect a weeks‑to‑months timeline.

Seen a patient with unexplained neck swelling and cough with secretions? Check a CK, look for dermatomyositis skin signs, call SLP, and think about aspiration — the dangerous swallow is often silent until it’s not.
