# Imaging the Puffy Neck: Infection, Angioedema, or Inflamed Muscle?

What looks like “Ludwig’s angina” or idiopathic angioedema isn’t always infection or histamine/bradykinin‑mediated swelling. This case of anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis presenting with progressive neck swelling shows how inflammatory myopathy can masquerade as ENT disease — with CT that screams deep neck oedema, but no abscess, and MRI/PET that point to active myositis. If you’re the ED, ENT or radiologist facing a puffy neck, knowing the imaging patterns (and the clinical checks) can shortcut delays to immunosuppression and avoid unnecessary surgery or antibiotics.

## Why this case is interesting
- Subacute, painful‑appearing cervical swelling evolved over weeks and prompted transfer for possible Ludwig’s angina, yet there was no drainable collection or source.
- CT showed diffuse strap muscle and retropharyngeal edema, but flexible nasendoscopy and labs were not consistent with a deep neck space infection.
- Muscle‑directed testing (very high CK), MRI thigh changes, FDG‑PET muscle uptake, and muscle biopsy confirmed dermatomyositis (SAE1 antibody positive) — a rare but documented cause of “pseudo‑angioedema.”
- The imaging pattern shifted management from surgical/antibiotic pathways to urgent immunosuppression, with good clinical recovery.

## Radiology pearls — how to separate the big three

1) CT neck: infection (Ludwig’s/abscess) vs inflammatory myositis
- Suggests infection (Ludwig’s angina/deep neck space abscess)
  - Focal fluid collection with rim enhancement
  - Gas locules, adjacent odontogenic source, asymmetric fascial plane involvement
  - Significant fat stranding, rapid progression, possible systemic sepsis features
- Suggests myositis/inflammatory neck swelling
  - Diffuse enlargement and edema of strap muscles (symmetric or patchy) without a discrete rim‑enhancing collection
  - Retropharyngeal soft tissue thickening driven by inflamed muscle and soft tissue, but no focal abscess
  - Less pronounced fat stranding or overlying cellulitis signs compared with bacterial infection
  - Lack of gas and absence of a clear odontogenic or tonsillar source

2) CT neck: angioedema (histaminergic/bradykinin)
- Classical imaging: diffuse soft‑tissue swelling of subcutaneous tissues and mucosal/submucosal thickening (larynx, pharynx), generally without enhancement or muscle‑centered edema
- No strap‑muscle T2/STIR pattern of myositis and no muscle necrosis
- Clinical context matters: rapid onset, triggers (ACEi, allergic exposure), urticaria, pruritus (histaminergic) or prior angioedema history

3) MRI muscle: the myositis signature
- Active inflammatory myositis shows muscle enlargement with:
  - T2/STIR or fat‑suppressed sequences: diffuse or perifascicular hyperintensity (edema)
  - T1: may show fatty atrophy chronically; acute disease less T1 change
  - Distribution can be patchy or perifascicular (typical in dermatomyositis), and can involve neck/shoulder girdle muscles when clinically relevant
- Important: MRI localizes active disease for biopsy and correlates with symptoms (eg, neck muscle involvement causing swelling/dysphagia)

4) FDG‑PET: inflammation and opportunistic malignancy screen
- Diffuse, symmetric increased FDG uptake in multiple muscle groups — points toward systemic inflammatory myopathy rather than localized infection
- PET doubles as malignancy screen in dermatomyositis (given paraneoplastic risk), so it can answer two questions: is uptake muscle‑predominant (inflammatory) and is there occult tumor?
- Focal nodal or visceral hypermetabolism would raise concern for occult malignancy and prompt targeted investigation

## Pitfalls that mislead clinicians (and how to avoid them)
- Pitfall: Seeing a swollen neck with elevated CRP and defaulting to antibiotics/surgery.
  - Avoidance: Look for drainage signs, gas, rim‑enhancing collections on CT and correlate with exam (fever, odonto/tonsillar source). If imaging lacks a drainable abscess, pause for alternative causes.
- Pitfall: Attributing neck swelling to angioedema without measuring CK or examining skin/nailfolds.
  - Avoidance: In subacute or atypical presentations, always check CK and inspect for cutaneous stigmata (heliotrope, Gottron’s, periungual changes, Holster sign). Ask about muscle weakness/dysphagia.
- Pitfall: Interpreting soft tissue swelling on CT as cellulitis when muscle‑centric edema is present.
  - Avoidance: Review muscle planes — strap muscle enlargement and retropharyngeal edema without superficial fascial necrosis favors myositis.
- Pitfall: Missing myositis when ENT endoscopy is “normal.”
  - Avoidance: Swallowing dysfunction may be due to pharyngeal muscle weakness; get a barium swallow if dysphagia is present and consider MRI of neck/thighs.

## Practical workflow for the patient with unexplained neck swelling
- First pass (ED/ENT/radiology)
  - Rapid airway assessment: any signs of compromise → secure airway.
  - Urgent contrast CT neck if infection suspected. Look specifically for abscess vs diffuse muscle edema.
- If CT lacks abscess or surgical source, and presentation is subacute or associated with weakness/dysphagia:
  - Check CK, AST/ALT, LDH
  - Full skin exam (heliotrope, Gottron’s, periungual changes)
  - Consider ANA/myositis panel and nailfold capillaroscopy when available
  - MRI (neck or thighs) to document muscle edema and guide biopsy
  - FDG‑PET if systemic myositis suspected and malignancy screening is indicated
- Management pivots
  - If myositis likely: early rheumatology/immunology input and prompt immunosuppression (steroids ± steroid‑sparing agents, IVIG, rituximab as indicated)
  - Avoid unnecessary surgical exploration or repeated antibiotics when imaging and labs point away from infection

## Clinical pearls
- Strapped muscle enlargement and retropharyngeal soft tissue edema on CT without rim‑enhancing collection is a red flag for non‑infectious causes — think myositis.
- Very high CK in the face of neck swelling is diagnostic gold: if CK is elevated, the differential shifts strongly toward inflammatory myopathy.
- MRI T2/STIR hyperintensity in muscle and perifascicular changes is characteristic of dermatomyositis and guides biopsy.
- FDG‑PET often shows diffuse muscle uptake in inflammatory myopathies and provides concurrent malignancy screening — a high‑yield single test in suspicious dermatomyositis.
- Don’t conflate “angioedema” with any neck swelling — history timeframe, triggers, urticaria, and imaging distribution distinguish histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema from myositis‑related subcutaneous edema.

## Take‑home messages
- Neck swelling without a drainable collection on CT should prompt consideration of inflammatory myopathy, especially when accompanied by weakness, dysphagia, or subtle cutaneous signs.
- Always check a CK early in unexplained neck swelling; it’s cheap, fast, and can be decisive.
- Use MRI for muscle characterization and FDG‑PET to document systemic muscle inflammation while screening for occult malignancy.
- Early recognition avoids unnecessary surgery and expedites immunosuppressive therapy, which can be lifesaving when airway or swallowing muscles are involved.

If you’re the radiologist putting eyes on a swollen neck CT: before you sign off “no drainable abscess,” add a line — “consider inflammatory myopathy if clinical context and labs (CK) support” — and you may change a patient’s trajectory from OR to rheumatology.
