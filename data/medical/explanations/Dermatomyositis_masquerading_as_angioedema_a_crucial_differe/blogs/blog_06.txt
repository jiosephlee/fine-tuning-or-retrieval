# Hitting Dermatomyositis Early and Hard: Steroids, IVIG, and Beyond

What makes this case worth your attention? A previously well man in his 40s walks into the ED with progressive neck swelling — initially worked up as Ludwig’s angina or angioedema — and ends up diagnosed with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis with prominent neck/subcutaneous oedema, severe dysphagia and very high CK (16,184 U/L). The “angioedema” turned out to be inflammatory myositis of the neck/airway muscles manifesting as non-histaminergic, non-bradykinin swelling. The team treated him aggressively with pulse steroids, early IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab and achieved clinical remission.

If you see unexplained neck swelling with any muscle weakness, dysphagia, skin/nail findings or raised inflammatory markers — check a CK. This case crystallises a pragmatic approach for severe dermatomyositis with airway/swallow involvement.

## How I think about initial severity and the need to be aggressive
Severe dermatomyositis features that push me to “hit hard” early:
- Airway or bulbar muscle involvement (dysphagia, aspiration risk) — high morbidity.
- Very high CK with clinical weakness.
- Rapid progression over days–weeks.
- Radiologic/functional evidence of lung involvement or aspiration.
- Autoantibodies associated with aggressive phenotype (eg, SAE1 in this case, MDA5/TIF1 associations can influence risk).

When airway/bulbar muscles are involved you’re treating both inflammatory myositis and protecting airway/nutrition — early, potent immunosuppression is justified.

## Practical induction regimen (what worked in this case)
From the report — and what I would mirror in similar presentations:
1. Pulse IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days (for severe/bulbar involvement), then transition to high-dose oral prednisone (eg, 0.5–1 mg/kg/day) with a defined taper.
   - Rationale: rapid suppression of inflammation to protect airway and muscles.
2. Early IVIG (total 2 g/kg, split over 3–5 days as a loading course), then maintenance cycles (eg, monthly) as clinically indicated.
   - In this case: loading 2 g/kg over 5 days, then monthly cycles — correlated with fast improvement in swelling and eventual recovery of strength.
   - IVIG is especially useful for severe, refractory, or bulbar disease and when rapid improvement is needed.
3. Start a steroid-sparing immunosuppressant early (chosen to match pulmonary status and patient factors).
   - Options:
     - Mycophenolate mofetil (used in this case: 1 g twice daily) — good GI/tolerance profile, used widely when there's concern about methotrexate and ILD.
     - Methotrexate — effective but use cautiously if interstitial lung disease or pulmonary fibrosis is a concern.
     - Azathioprine, tacrolimus are alternatives depending on comorbidities and response.
4. Consider rituximab for severe disease, refractory cases, or specific antibody profiles.
   - In this patient they used two 1,000 mg doses two weeks apart — reasonable for severe disease needing B-cell targeted therapy.
   - Rituximab can be considered early in life‑threatening or steroid‑refractory cases, or when combination therapy is preferred.

Tailor the combination to disease severity, pulmonary status, comorbidities and infection risk. In this case the combination (pulse steroids + IVIG + mycophenolate + rituximab) produced remission.

## Timelines for response — what to expect and monitor
- Symptom improvement:
  - Bulbar/airway swelling and pain: often first to respond over days–weeks after pulse steroids/IVIG.
  - Strength (proximal muscles): measurable improvement often within 2–6 weeks; more complete recovery may take months.
- CK trends:
  - Expect CK to fall quickly with effective therapy — in this case CK normalized within weeks (from 16,184 to ~37 U/L by about 3 weeks).
  - CK is useful but not the whole story — correlate with function and symptoms.
- Functional monitoring:
  - Serial bedside strength testing (hip flexors, deltoids), timed functional tests when possible.
  - Objective measures: MRC scale, timed up-and-go, 30-second sit-to-stand in follow-up.
- Respiratory/bulbar monitoring:
  - Regular swallow assessment (safety of oral intake), consider nasogastric/PEG feeding if aspiration risk persists.
  - Pulmonary function tests and imaging for ILD surveillance.

Expect early biochemical improvement (CK) and progressive functional gains; don’t stop close monitoring even if CK normalises.

## Where rituximab fits
- Role: B-cell depletion for refractory disease, severe presentations, or certain autoantibody-driven phenotypes.
- Timing: can be given early in severe cases or added if inadequate response to steroids + steroid-sparing agent +/‑ IVIG.
- Dosing: common regimens include 1,000 mg IV x2 (2 weeks apart) — same as used here.
- Caveat: screen for infectious risks pre-rituximab (HBV, TB, HIV), and plan vaccination/ prophylaxis strategies.

## Choosing a steroid-sparing agent when ILD is on the table
- If ILD or pulmonary fibrosis is present or strongly suspected, favour:
  - Mycophenolate mofetil (frequently used; used in this case) or
  - Tacrolimus in certain contexts.
- Use methotrexate with caution if ILD or baseline abnormal lung function exists — risk profile and patient preferences matter.
- Azathioprine remains an option when other choices aren’t suitable.

## PJP prophylaxis — options when TMP‑SMX isn’t tolerated
Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole is first-line for Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis, but adverse reactions can occur (as in this case: haemolytic anaemia).
Alternatives:
- Aerosolized pentamidine 300 mg nebulised every 4 weeks — used successfully for this patient.
- Oral atovaquone (750 mg twice daily with food) — suitable when TMP‑SMX not tolerated and G6PD deficiency precludes dapsone.
- Dapsone (100 mg daily) — an option if G6PD normal; check G6PD first.
Pick the alternative based on tolerance, G6PD status, absorption concerns and logistics.

## What else to remember (practical pearls)
- Don’t anchor on “angioedema” if swelling is atypical: check for skin signs (periungual changes, rash), proximal weakness, dysphagia and order a CK early.
- Neck/facial swelling in dermatomyositis is rare but real — it may be myositis-related oedema rather than histamine/bradykinin-mediated angioedema.
- Early IVIG can be a game changer in bulbar/airway involvement.
- Malignancy screening is mandatory in adult dermatomyositis — tailor investigations to risk factors and antibody profile; continue symptom-driven surveillance.
- Multidisciplinary care: involve rheumatology/immunology, ENT (airway assessment), speech/swallow therapy, respiratory and nutrition teams early.

## Take-home messages
- Check CK when neck/facial “angioedema” is unexplained or accompanied by weakness/dysphagia/skin signs.
- For severe dermatomyositis with airway or bulbar involvement: pulse IV methylprednisolone, early IVIG, and an early steroid‑sparing agent are reasonable — escalate to rituximab for severe/refractory disease.
- Monitor clinical function and CK serially; expect biochemical improvement quickly but functional recovery may take weeks–months.
- If TMP‑SMX is not tolerated for PJP prophylaxis, consider aerosolized pentamidine, atovaquone, or dapsone (if G6PD clear).
- Keep a low threshold for multidisciplinary care and cancer screening.

This case is a reminder: dermatomyositis can hide in plain sight — sometimes masquerading as ENT disease — and when it threatens the airway or swallow, treat early and treat aggressively.



## Don’t Miss the Nails: Bedside Cutaneous Clues in Systemic Myositis

A guy in his 40s shows up with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling — everyone thinks Ludwig’s angina, ENT scopes him, CT shows diffuse neck soft-tissue oedema, no abscess. He’s afebrile, not stridulous. He’s sent to immunology for “angioedema” workup. The small-but-mighty clue that changed everything? Nailfold microhaemorrhages and periungual changes on bedside capillaroscopy, plus subtle violaceous facial erythema (including the nasolabial folds) and a Holster sign on the thigh. A stat CK confirms massive myositis, autoantibodies (anti-SAE1) and muscle biopsy clinch dermatomyositis. What looked like ENT disease and angioedema was actually inflammatory myositis with soft‑tissue oedema and dysphagia requiring urgent immunosuppression.

Why this matters
- Neck/facial swelling is usually worked up as infection, venous congestion, histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema. But dermatomyositis (and other connective tissue diseases) can produce prominent subcutaneous oedema and mimic ENT pathology.
- The difference is not academic. Dermatomyositis often needs high-dose steroids and rapid steroid‑sparing therapy (IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab, etc.). Delaying treatment risks airway compromise, aspiration and progressive muscle damage.
- Low-tech skin and nail signs at the bedside are high-yield: they can reframe the entire diagnostic pathway before expensive imaging or invasive procedures.

What to look for at the bedside
- Periungual telangiectasias and nailfold microhaemorrhages — early, sometimes the only cutaneous clue.
- Violaceous facial erythema that can include the nasolabial folds (don’t dismiss facial erythema as rosacea or sunburn when other clues are present).
- Holster sign (violaceous rash on the lateral thigh), shawl or V‑neck signs, heliotrope eyelid rash, Gottron’s papules — classic but not always present.
- Subacute proximal muscle weakness (hip flexors, shoulder abductors), dysphagia, or subtle cough/aspiration on nasendoscopy — think inflammatory myopathy.

How to do quick nailfold capillaroscopy in ED/clinic (one-minute technique)
- Use what you have: a dermatoscope, magnifying glass (10×), or even the camera + flashlight on a smartphone. A patient-friendly nailfold view is enough.
- Warm hands first (cold causes vasoconstriction). Have the patient rest palms up for a minute.
- Place the magnifier/dermatoscope at the proximal nailfold (just proximal to the cuticle) of the 2nd–5th fingers.
- Look for dilated/enlarged capillary loops, capillary dropouts/avascular areas, and microhaemorrhages. Periungual erythema is visible without magnification.
- Record photos if available (helps follow-up and specialist referral).

Interpreting the findings (practical points)
- Microhaemorrhages, capillary dilatation and avascular areas are suggestive of microangiopathy seen in dermatomyositis and systemic sclerosis spectrum disorders.
- These changes are not diagnostic by themselves but should raise suspicion for myositis in the right clinical context.
- Normal nailfold exam does not exclude myositis — absence of signs doesn’t rule it out.

When to pivot the workup (clinical triggers)
- Neck/facial swelling without airway compromise and no focal infectious source on CT.
- Normal inflammatory markers and WBC do not exclude inflammatory myositis; CK can be massively elevated even with modest CRP.
- Any proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, aspiration on swallow study, or unexplained transaminitis — check a CK early.
- Cutaneous signs described above — do a bedside nailfold exam; if positive, add CK, myositis antibody panel, and early rheumatology/immunology consult.

Common mimics and how to keep yourself honest
- Histaminergic angioedema: often itchy, transient, responds to antihistamines/epinephrine; usually rapid onset/offset.
- Bradykinin-mediated angioedema (ACEi): look for medication history, normal complement/C1 esterase function.
- Cellulitis/deep neck infection: fever, leukocytosis, focal tenderness, fluctuance/abscess on imaging.
- Superior vena cava syndrome: facial/neck swelling with venous congestion signs, positional worsening.
- Hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis: painful urticarial lesions, low complement; look for systemic features.
- If in doubt, combine signs: ENT exam + imaging + bedside skin/nail exam + CK will separate these quickly.

Key diagnostic and practical tips
- CK is cheap, fast, and game-changing. If clinical suspicion exists (nailfold changes, proximal weakness, neck swelling with no clear ENT cause), send CK STAT.
- Early myositis antibody testing (including less common antibodies like SAE1) can help phenotype disease and triage malignancy screening.
- Dysphagia or bulbar muscle involvement raises urgency — these patients aspirate and often require early IV steroids/IVIG.
- If you suspect dermatomyositis, involve rheumatology/immunology early and consider inpatient monitoring for airway or swallowing compromise.

Take-home messages
- Bedside nailfold capillaroscopy is a low-tech, high-yield exam that can redirect workups away from ENT/infectious causes toward systemic myositis.
- In unexplained neck/facial oedema, look at the nails and skin; if you find periungual telangiectasias/microhaemorrhages or violaceous rashes, get a CK and call rheum/immunology.
- Don’t assume “angioedema” without a compatible history or response to standard therapies — dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema and needs very different treatment.

Keep your eyes on the nails — they often tell the systemic story before the CT does.



## Cancer Screening in Dermatomyositis: A Pragmatic IMACS Walkthrough

What makes this worth your attention? Because dermatomyositis (DM) is not just a muscle-and-skin problem — it’s a paraneoplastic red flag. The case that sparked this post is classic in how it forces you to think beyond the obvious: a middle‑aged man with SAE1‑positive DM, dysphagia and neck swelling that wasn’t angioedema. Those features together pushed this patient into a high‑risk cancer category and triggered an aggressive but pragmatic malignancy screen that found no occult tumour — for now. This is exactly the clinical moment IMACS guidance aims to address: identify who needs what testing, when, and how often.

Here’s a bedside-friendly approach to translating IMACS into action.

Why screen at all (brief rationale)
- Multiple studies show an increased incidence of cancer in patients with DM, especially within the first 3 years of disease onset. Certain clinical features (dermatomyositis phenotype itself, dysphagia) and specific myositis autoantibodies increase the pretest probability. Early detection matters because malignancy‑associated DM can respond to tumour therapy and has distinct management and prognostic implications.

Risk stratification — the practical checklist
Use readily available clinical features to group patients into low / intermediate / high malignancy risk. IMACS highlights several risk factors; pragmatically prioritize these at the bedside:
- High‑risk features (any one, but risk accumulates): dermatomyositis phenotype, dysphagia, older age (typically >40–50), male sex in some series, certain autoantibodies (eg, anti‑TIF1‑γ, SAE1 has been reported as an intermediate/higher risk marker in some cohorts).
- Intermediate risk: single atypical antibody, milder cutaneous disease without dysphagia.
- Low risk: polymyositis phenotype without cutaneous features, younger patients, absence of risk antibodies.

A staged, pragmatic screening plan to initiate during the index admission
Stage 1 — bedside and baseline tests you should order now
- Thorough history and focused exam: review weight loss, constitutional symptoms, smoking, family history, directed lymph node and pelvic/genital exam, ENT/oral exam if dysphagia.
- Basic bloods: FBC, LFTs, renal function, inflammatory markers.
- Tumour markers (adjunctive, not diagnostic): CEA, CA‑125 (women), CA19‑9, PSA (men) — use to complement imaging and follow trends if suspicious, not as lone screening tests.
- Myositis antibody panel results (if not already available) — they change pretest probability and may guide intensity of screening.

Stage 2 — cross‑sectional imaging (baseline)
- For high‑risk patients (eg, DM + dysphagia, positive high‑risk antibody such as TIF1‑γ or SAE1 with other risk features, older age): obtain whole‑body PET‑CT if available, or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast. Rationale: PET‑CT has high sensitivity for occult malignancy and can also map inflammatory muscle disease; CT is a reasonable alternative where PET isn’t accessible.
- For intermediate risk: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis is a reasonable baseline.
- For low risk: age‑appropriate imaging/screening; consider CT/PET if clinical concern emerges.

Stage 3 — targeted investigations
- Upper and lower endoscopy: consider early endoscopy (gastroscopy ± colonoscopy) if high‑risk features include dysphagia, GI symptoms, or if PET/CT or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis raises concern. IMACS and multiple series suggest GI malignancies can be missed on routine imaging; the recent case appropriately planned endoscopy despite negative imaging.
- Age/gender‑specific: mammography, pelvic ultrasound/gynecologic review (women), testicular/urogenital evaluation (men) depending on findings and risk.
- ENT/ORL evaluation if head/neck symptoms or PET/CT uptake.
- Referral to oncology/gastroenterology/urology/gynecology as indicated.

Timing and pragmatic surveillance intervals
- Highest yield window: first 3 years after DM onset; most cancers present within this period.
- Suggested schedule (practical, risk‑adjusted):
  - High risk: baseline CT or PET‑CT + targeted endoscopy where indicated during index admission; repeat imaging at 6–12 months, then annually up to 3 years if negative. Maintain a low threshold for earlier re‑imaging if new symptoms arise.
  - Intermediate risk: baseline CT chest/abdomen/pelvis; repeat at 12 months and then annually for 2 years if no clinical concern.
  - Low risk: ensure adherence to age‑appropriate population screening; review clinically every 3–6 months for symptoms during the first 2 years.
- Individualize frequency: frailty, comorbidities, and patient preference matter; discuss the balance of diagnostic yield versus radiation/cost.

Red flags that mandate expedited re‑workup
- New or progressive weight loss, iron‑deficiency anaemia, unexplained lymphadenopathy, change in respiratory symptoms, new focal pain or neurological deficits, or new/progressive skin changes.
- New PET‑avid lesions, rising tumour markers, or abnormalities on interval imaging.

Practical tips for workflow in the hospital
- Start the basic labs, myositis antibody panel and tumor markers on admission; these are cheap and fast.
- Arrange CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (or PET‑CT) early — logistically easier from inpatient than trying to expedite outpatient bookings.
- If dysphagia is present, coordinate early gastroenterology / ENT / speech and swallow teams and plan endoscopy when safe.
- Document discussion with the patient about malignancy risk and plan for surveillance — medico‑legal and important for shared decision‑making.
- Link care: set up outpatient oncology/rheumatology follow‑up and a clear surveillance timeline in discharge paperwork.

Counselling points for patients
- Explain that DM carries an increased but not inevitable risk of associated cancer, particularly early after diagnosis.
- Describe the planned tests, why they’re being done now, and what surveillance will look like.
- Reassure that negative baseline tests aren’t a lifetime guarantee — that’s why targeted follow‑up for 2–3 years is important.

How the case ties it together
The presented SAE1‑positive DM patient had multiple high/intermediate risk features (DM phenotype, dysphagia, SAE1 antibody, male sex). The team appropriately escalated: aggressive baseline imaging (CT + PET), tumour markers, targeted endoscopic plans, and a structured follow‑up schedule. This is exactly the pragmatic IMACS approach in action: risk‑stratify, do targeted, timely testing during the index admission, and surveil over the high‑yield first 3 years.

Key take‑home messages
- Dermatomyositis is a paraneoplastic red flag — the highest malignancy risk is within the first 3 years after diagnosis.
- Risk‑stratify at the bedside: DM phenotype, dysphagia, age, sex and autoantibodies (eg, TIF1‑γ, SAE1) drive how aggressive your screen should be.
- High‑risk patients: do baseline PET‑CT (or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis if PET not available) and consider early endoscopy; repeat imaging at 6–12 months and then annually up to 3 years.
- Intermediate risk: baseline CT and clinical surveillance; consider repeat imaging at 12 months.
- Low risk: ensure age‑appropriate cancer screening and close clinical follow‑up; escalate testing if new symptoms develop.
- Start tests early during the index admission when possible (labs, myositis antibodies, imaging) and document a clear surveillance plan with specialist follow‑up.
