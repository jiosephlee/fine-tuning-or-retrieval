Title: Blog about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

# CK Is the New C4: Lab Clues in the Swollen Neck

What if the “angioedema” you’re called for isn’t histamine or bradykinin at all, but muscle inflammation pushing up into the neck? That’s exactly what happened in a man in his 40s who arrived with progressive neck swelling, subtle proximal weakness and dysphagia — initially worked up for Ludwig’s angina and idiopathic angioedema — and turned out to have SAE1–positive dermatomyositis with a CK of 16,184 U/L. The take-home punchline: when idiopathic neck swelling doesn’t behave like angioedema, reflex a CK. CK (plus a few other labs and a quick bedside look at the nailfolds) will often outscore C4 in pointing toward myositis.

Why this is surprising and why it matters
- Neck/facial soft-tissue oedema can be an unusual manifestation of dermatomyositis. It can mimic primary ENT disease or idiopathic angioedema and delay immunosuppression.
- Misclassifying this swelling as histaminergic/bradykinin-mediated angioedema risks missed airway muscle involvement, progressive dysphagia and delayed treatment.
- A very high CK with the right clinical context almost immediately changes your differential and management: activate myositis workup, protect the airway, start immunosuppression with input from rheumatology/immunology.

The lab constellation that points toward myositis (vs true angioedema)
- CK: massive elevation (often thousands — case CK 16,184 U/L) — the single most useful reflex test when angioedema is unexplained and accompanied by weakness, dysphagia, or cutaneous clues.
- AST > ALT: AST can be disproportionately high with muscle injury (AST 396 vs ALT 143 in the case) — don’t automatically attribute transaminitis to liver disease.
- LDH: often elevated with muscle breakdown.
- Inflammatory markers: can be mildly raised (CRP not always normal) — presence of inflammatory response should not reassure you that this is infectious only.
- Complement/C1 esterase: usually normal in myositis-related “pseudoangioedema.” In true complement-mediated or hereditary angioedema you may find low C4, abnormal C1 esterase function/levels.
- Urine myoglobin/renal function: check to screen for rhabdomyolysis, but absence of myoglobinuria doesn’t rule out severe myositis.
- Myositis-specific/associated antibodies: request a panel when CK elevation and clinical features point to inflammatory myopathy (see below).

Red flags that should trigger CK now (not later)
- “Angioedema” without urticaria, pruritus, clear exposure history or ACEi/medication trigger.
- Progressive neck/facial swelling with dysphagia, dysphonia, odynophagia or signs of bulbar/airway muscle involvement.
- Any proximal muscle weakness (even subtle) or myalgias.
- Cutaneous clues: heliotrope, Gottron’s papules, shawl/V-neck signs, periungual changes, unexplained violaceous facial erythema.
- Unexplained transaminitis with AST > ALT or elevated LDH.

When to send a myositis panel and what to include
- Indications: marked CK elevation, clinical suspicion after bedside exam (weakness + rash/nailfold changes), or severe neck/face oedema unexplained by angioedema/infection.
- Practical panel: ANA and myositis antibody panel including Mi-2, TIF1-γ, MDA5, Jo-1 and SAE1 where available. Note that SAE1 positivity is associated with cutaneous features, dysphagia and risk of ILD/malignancy.
- Don’t wait for antibody results to act if the patient has airway compromise, severe dysphagia, high CK or progressive weakness.

Quick bedside nailfold capillaroscopy — how it helps
- Technique: use a dermatoscope or magnifying glass and oil on the periungual area to visualise capillary loops at the nailfold.
- What to look for: dilated/giant capillary loops, haemorrhages, avascular areas or dropout — findings that point to an inflammatory connective tissue process rather than isolated angioedema.
- It’s rapid, non-invasive and can clinch suspicion at the bedside, prompting immediate CK and specialist consultation.

Immediate management implications when myositis is likely
- Airway risk: bulbar/neck muscle involvement can progress rapidly. Low threshold for airway assessment and early ENT/anesthesia involvement.
- Early immunosuppression: high-dose steroids are commonly started emergently for severe disease; steroid-sparing agents/IVIG/rituximab are considered based on severity and specialist guidance.
- Swallowing assessment and aspiration risk: do a bedside swallow test and consider formal swallow study; treat aspiration aggressively.
- Malignancy screening and ILD surveillance: certain myositis subtypes (including SAE1) carry cancer and lung disease risk — initiate age- and risk-appropriate screening.

Clinical pearls
- “Normal C4” does not rule out a dangerous cause of neck swelling — consider CK when the history/exam don’t fit classic angioedema.
- AST > ALT with high LDH should make you think muscle, not liver, until proven otherwise.
- Nailfold capillary changes are an underused bedside tool that can point you toward dermatomyositis in minutes.
- Myositis can present without classic skin signs (heliotrope/Gottron’s) and still cause dramatic soft-tissue oedema — keep a low threshold for CK if the picture is atypical.
- Early rheumatology/immunology and ENT involvement is crucial when there is neck swelling plus dysphagia or bulbar signs.

Bottom line (the quick checklist)
- If “idiopathic” angioedema of the neck/face + any red flag → send CK STAT.
- Check AST/ALT, LDH and complement/C1 esterase concurrently.
- Do a bedside nailfold capillaroscopy and thorough neuromuscular exam.
- If CK is high or clinical suspicion strong → urgent myositis workup, airway assessment and early immunosuppression with specialist input.

Make CK the new reflex in unexplained neck swelling. It’s fast, inexpensive, and in the right clinical context it’s the test that changes everything.
