# SAE1-Positive Dermatomyositis: A Phenotype to Know

What’s interesting here? A previously well man in his 40s presents with progressive neck “angioedema” and mild myalgia — initially worked up for Ludwig’s angina and angioedema — but the diagnosis turned out to be SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis with severe myositis, dysphagia and inflammatory neck swelling. This isn’t classic histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema: the swelling tracked to muscle and soft‑tissue inflammation, CK was sky high (16,184 U/L), nailfold changes and subtle rash clues were present, and muscle biopsy + SAE1 antibody confirmed dermatomyositis. The case is a great reminder that myositis can masquerade as ENT or allergy disease and that timely recognition alters management and outcomes.

Why SAE1 matters at the bedside
- SAE1 (anti‑SUMO activating enzyme subunit 1) defines a recognizable clinical pattern. In the literature, SAE1‑positive disease commonly shows prominent cutaneous features and significant bulbar involvement — dysphagia is frequent (reported ~46%). Interstitial lung disease (ILD) also occurs (reported ~43%), and malignancy has been reported in a minority (~18%). The phenotype sits distinct from other myositis autoantibodies and knowing these patterns helps prioritize assessment, screening and treatment intensity.

Key contrasts to other myositis phenotypes (practical bedside flavor)
- SAE1
  - Prominent cutaneous disease (heliotrope/shawl/V‑sign may be seen), frequent dysphagia, variable ILD risk, measurable malignancy signal (~18%).
  - Can present with frank muscle inflammation and oedema causing soft‑tissue swelling (face/neck) and aspiration risk.
- TIF1‑γ
  - Stronger association with cancer (classically highest malignancy risk among dermatomyositis antibodies).
  - Marked cutaneous findings; muscle disease variable.
  - Malignancy screening should be aggressive when TIF1‑γ positive.
- MDA5
  - Often amyopathic or paucimyopathic with striking skin ulcerations, palmar papules, and rapidly progressive ILD (high morbidity/mortality).
  - Lower malignancy signal compared with TIF1‑γ but much higher pulmonary risk — prioritize pulmonary assessment and early aggressive therapy.

How this changes what you do in the ED/ENT clinic/clinic visit
- Don’t anchor on “idiopathic angioedema” when the swelling is atypical:
  - Non‑tender, persistent neck/face swelling without clear allergic trigger, normal C1 esterase function and complements, absent ACE inhibitor use → think myositis/inflammatory causes.
- Look for subtle dermatologic and neuromuscular clues:
  - Cutaneous signs (heliotrope, shawl, Holster sign, periungual erythema or nailfold haemorrhages), proximal weakness (ask about hip flexors, climbing stairs), dysphagia or cough with secretions.
- Rapidly obtain CK (and AST/ALT/LDH): an extremely elevated CK shifts the differential away from edema/angioedema and toward myositis/necrotising myopathy.
- Early airway and swallow assessment: dysphagia and pharyngeal weakness are common in SAE1 disease — aspiration pneumonitis and need for nasogastric feeding or airway protection are real concerns.

Investigation and malignancy screening — pragmatic steps
- Basic labs: CBC, CRP, CK, AST/ALT, LDH; myoglobin/renal function if very high CK.
- Autoantibodies: ANA and a myositis panel (including SAE1, TIF1‑γ, MDA5, antisynthetase antibodies) — results guide prognosis and cancer/ILD screening intensity.
- Structural and functional testing: CT neck/chest as indicated, videofluoroscopic swallow if dysphagia, chest CT and PFTs for ILD evaluation.
- Malignancy screen tailored to risk: IMACS guidance — patients with dermatomyositis + dysphagia and SAE1 positivity may merit CT/PET and age/sex‑appropriate endoscopic evaluation; TIF1‑γ positivity pushes you even harder to look for occult cancer.

Treatment/prioritization pearls
- Treat the myositis (not histamine/bradykinin pathways) when diagnosis is established:
  - High‑dose corticosteroids for induction; consider pulse IV methylprednisolone for life/airway‑threatening disease.
  - IVIG, rituximab, mycophenolate, azathioprine, methotrexate are used as steroid‑sparing or adjunctive agents — choice depends on ILD risk, comorbidities and antibody profile (e.g., methotrexate may be avoided if ILD concern).
- Monitor for complications of severe myositis: aspiration, respiratory muscle weakness, rhabdomyolysis, renal injury, and infection from immunosuppression.
- Multidisciplinary follow‑up (rheumatology/immunology, ENT/speech pathology, pulmonology, oncology when indicated).

Clinical pearls — what to remember tomorrow morning
- Persistent, non‑tender neck/face swelling without clear allergic/infectious cause — think myositis, especially if there are cutaneous signs or weakness.
- CK is cheap, fast and informative — get it when angioedema doesn’t behave like angioedema.
- SAE1 disease: cutaneous prominence + dysphagia common; ILD risk present; malignancy risk exists (not as high as TIF1‑γ but non‑trivial) — screen accordingly.
- Airway and swallow assessment are essential early — dysphagia can be covert and lead to aspiration pneumonitis.
- Antibody phenotype informs counseling, cancer screening intensity, and anticipatory monitoring for lungs and swallowing.

Take‑home messages
- Consider inflammatory myopathy when neck or facial “angioedema” is atypical (non‑tender, persistent, no trigger, normal C1/C4).
- Check CK and examine for subtle skin/nailfold findings and proximal weakness; send a myositis panel including SAE1.
- SAE1 dermatomyositis: think prominent cutaneous disease, frequent dysphagia, ILD risk and a modest malignancy signal — screen and manage accordingly.
- Early multidisciplinary care (airway/swallow, immunosuppression, malignancy screening) changes outcomes.

This case is a neat diagnostic pivot: from ENT/angioedema pathway to systemic autoimmune disease. Keep your differential broad, use CK liberally, and let the antibody profile steer your screening and follow‑up.
