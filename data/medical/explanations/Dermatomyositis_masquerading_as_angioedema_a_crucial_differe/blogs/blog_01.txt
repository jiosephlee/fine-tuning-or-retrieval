# When “Angioedema” Isn’t: Dermatomyositis of the Neck

We see a fair bit of “angioedema” in the ED — swollen faces, tight throats, anxious patients. Most are histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated and follow familiar pathways. But sometimes the face/neck swelling isn’t true angioedema at all. This case: a man in his 40s with progressive neck swelling over weeks, dysphagia and subtle skin/nailfold changes was initially worked up for Ludwig’s angina and idiopathic angioedema — until a CK came back sky-high and the diagnosis flipped to anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis with prominent cervical myositis and pseudo‑angioedema. Important lesson: think inflammatory myopathy when neck/facial swelling is atypical for classic angioedema.

Why this is surprising
- Angioedema is acute, dramatic and often mediated by histamine or bradykinin. Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy — it’s not the first thing most ED clinicians will think of when faced with isolated neck swelling.
- Subcutaneous oedema (facial/neck swelling) is an uncommon manifestation of dermatomyositis — reported in only a small minority — so it can masquerade as ENT disease or idiopathic angioedema.
- Missing the underlying myositis delays immunosuppression and risks airway and bulbar-muscle complications (dysphagia, aspiration) and other organ involvement.

Red flags that this is NOT classic angioedema
- Subacute tempo: progressive over days–weeks rather than minutes–hours.
- Lack of urticaria/pruritus and poor or no response to antihistamines, epinephrine or standard angioedema measures.
- Normal complement and C1 esterase function (C4/C1-INH) — argues against hereditary or acquired bradykinin-mediated angioedema.
- Concurrent muscle symptoms or subtle weakness (eg, hip flexor weakness, fatigability, dysphagia) even if mild.
- Subtle cutaneous clues: periungual erythema/telangiectasia, heliotrope/violaceous facial erythema, shawl/V-neck/holster signs, Gottron’s papules (may be absent).
- Systemic features: elevated CK, transaminases, LDH, and raised inflammatory markers without sepsis picture.
- Imaging showing diffuse strap muscle oedema or retropharyngeal/neck soft-tissue inflammation without abscess.

A practical, rapid ED approach to unexplained neck/facial swelling
Start with the airway and life‑threats as always. If the airway looks threatened — stridor, hypoxia, rapidly progressing obstruction, severe dysphagia with pooling secretions — call airway/ENT/ICU early and prepare for definitive airway.

If airway stable, use this focused triage:

1) History and exam
- Tempo: minutes–hours (think histamine/bradykinin) vs days–weeks (think inflammatory, infectious, neoplastic).
- Meds/hx: ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, recent transfusions, family history of hereditary angioedema.
- Systemic features: fevers, odynophagia, sore throat, muscle weakness, dysphagia, skin rash (look carefully at periungual folds, elbows/knees, face/eyelids, V/shawl areas).
- ENT exam: look for odontogenic source, sialadenitis, fluctuance, trismus, airway compromise.

2) Immediate tests in ED
- Point-of-care: pulse ox, ECG, bedside airway assessment.
- Labs: CBC, CRP, CK (this is critical if presentation atypical or muscle symptoms present), basic metabolic panel, LFTs.
- Complement studies (C4) and C1-INH can be ordered if angioedema suspected — but these return later; do not delay CK.
- Bedside imaging: contrast CT neck if concern for deep neck space infection or to evaluate strap muscle/retropharyngeal edema vs abscess.

3) Response to initial therapy
- If you suspect histaminergic angioedema: antihistamines, steroids, epinephrine as indicated.
- If no improvement and features above present (subacute, CK high, subtle myositis signs): stop chasing histamine/bradykinin treatments as sole explanation — broaden differential.

4) When to think dermatomyositis / inflammatory myopathy
- Elevated CK (often markedly), raised AST/ALT/LDH with muscle weakness/dysphagia.
- Nailfold capillary changes (periungual haemorrhages or telangiectasia) or violaceous facial rash.
- CT/MRI showing diffuse muscle inflammation of the neck/shoulder girdle.
- Myositis autoantibodies (eg, SAE1, MDA5, TIF1-γ etc) — useful but not required to start treatment if clinical picture severe.

Airway considerations when dysphagia / bulbar muscle involvement is suspected
- Dysphagia and bulbar weakness can progress to aspiration and failure to protect the airway even without classic external neck swelling.
- Watch for increased secretions, cough during flexible nasendoscopy, pooled secretions, voice changes, and post‑swallow aspiration on bedside testing.
- Early involvement of speech pathology for swallow assessment; consider bedside swallow or early formal video fluoroscopic swallow if available.
- Low threshold for nasogastric feeding if aspiration risk high.
- Low threshold for early ICU transfer and elective intubation if progressive bulbar weakness, worsening dysphagia or inability to handle secretions — securing the airway in a controlled setting is safer than emergency cricothyrotomy later.
- Discuss with ENT/anesthesia for potential difficult airway — neck edema from myositis can distort anatomy.

Therapeutic implications — why it matters in the ED
- Dermatomyositis is treated with high‑dose steroids and steroid‑sparing immunosuppression (IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab, methotrexate depending on phenotype and lung disease). Early recognition expedites appropriate immunotherapy and reduces complications (aspiration, progressive myositis, interstitial lung disease).
- If suspected in ED with severe bulbar/airway involvement, start systemic steroids after baseline infection and viral screens as appropriate — but do not delay airway protection.
- Coordinate admission to medical/rheumatology/immunology and arrange early CK, autoantibodies and imaging (MRI/CT/PET as needed).

Clinical pearls
- Always get a CK in unexplained neck/face swelling that is subacute or has atypical features for angioedema.
- Absence of urticaria, normal complements, lack of response to antihistamines/epinephrine and a subacute course = time to think beyond histamine/bradykinin.
- Look for subtle skin signs (periungual changes, violaceous facial erythema, Holster/shawl signs) — they may be the only dermatologic clue.
- Neck swelling from dermatomyositis is due to muscle inflammation and interstitial oedema — not mast cell or bradykinin mediated — so usual angioedema drugs won’t help.
- Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, aspiration) is common with some myositis subtypes (in SAE1-positive dermatomyositis dysphagia and ILD are not uncommon) — early swallow assessment and airway planning are vital.

Key take-home messages
- If neck/facial swelling is subacute, without urticaria, unresponsive to usual angioedema therapy, or accompanied by weakness/dysphagia — order a CK and look for dermatomyositis/myositis.
- Early airway assessment, swallow evaluation and low threshold for ICU/ENT involvement are essential when dysphagia or bulbar signs are present.
- Dermatomyositis can mimic ENT infections and angioedema; recognition in the ED directs prompt immunosuppression and organ‑specific surveillance (including malignancy work-up).

Bottom line: not every “angioedema” is histamine or bradykinin driven. Keep dermatomyositis in your differential for unexplained neck/face swelling — a quick CK and an eye for subtle skin/nailfold findings can change the diagnosis and the patient’s trajectory.
