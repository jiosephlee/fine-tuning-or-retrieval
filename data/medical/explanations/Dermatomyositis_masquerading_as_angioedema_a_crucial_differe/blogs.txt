Title: Blog about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

# When “Angioedema” Isn’t: Dermatomyositis of the Neck

We see a fair bit of “angioedema” in the ED — swollen faces, tight throats, anxious patients. Most are histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated and follow familiar pathways. But sometimes the face/neck swelling isn’t true angioedema at all. This case: a man in his 40s with progressive neck swelling over weeks, dysphagia and subtle skin/nailfold changes was initially worked up for Ludwig’s angina and idiopathic angioedema — until a CK came back sky-high and the diagnosis flipped to anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis with prominent cervical myositis and pseudo‑angioedema. Important lesson: think inflammatory myopathy when neck/facial swelling is atypical for classic angioedema.

Why this is surprising
- Angioedema is acute, dramatic and often mediated by histamine or bradykinin. Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy — it’s not the first thing most ED clinicians will think of when faced with isolated neck swelling.
- Subcutaneous oedema (facial/neck swelling) is an uncommon manifestation of dermatomyositis — reported in only a small minority — so it can masquerade as ENT disease or idiopathic angioedema.
- Missing the underlying myositis delays immunosuppression and risks airway and bulbar-muscle complications (dysphagia, aspiration) and other organ involvement.

Red flags that this is NOT classic angioedema
- Subacute tempo: progressive over days–weeks rather than minutes–hours.
- Lack of urticaria/pruritus and poor or no response to antihistamines, epinephrine or standard angioedema measures.
- Normal complement and C1 esterase function (C4/C1-INH) — argues against hereditary or acquired bradykinin-mediated angioedema.
- Concurrent muscle symptoms or subtle weakness (eg, hip flexor weakness, fatigability, dysphagia) even if mild.
- Subtle cutaneous clues: periungual erythema/telangiectasia, heliotrope/violaceous facial erythema, shawl/V-neck/holster signs, Gottron’s papules (may be absent).
- Systemic features: elevated CK, transaminases, LDH, and raised inflammatory markers without sepsis picture.
- Imaging showing diffuse strap muscle oedema or retropharyngeal/neck soft-tissue inflammation without abscess.

A practical, rapid ED approach to unexplained neck/facial swelling
Start with the airway and life‑threats as always. If the airway looks threatened — stridor, hypoxia, rapidly progressing obstruction, severe dysphagia with pooling secretions — call airway/ENT/ICU early and prepare for definitive airway.

If airway stable, use this focused triage:

1) History and exam
- Tempo: minutes–hours (think histamine/bradykinin) vs days–weeks (think inflammatory, infectious, neoplastic).
- Meds/hx: ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, recent transfusions, family history of hereditary angioedema.
- Systemic features: fevers, odynophagia, sore throat, muscle weakness, dysphagia, skin rash (look carefully at periungual folds, elbows/knees, face/eyelids, V/shawl areas).
- ENT exam: look for odontogenic source, sialadenitis, fluctuance, trismus, airway compromise.

2) Immediate tests in ED
- Point-of-care: pulse ox, ECG, bedside airway assessment.
- Labs: CBC, CRP, CK (this is critical if presentation atypical or muscle symptoms present), basic metabolic panel, LFTs.
- Complement studies (C4) and C1-INH can be ordered if angioedema suspected — but these return later; do not delay CK.
- Bedside imaging: contrast CT neck if concern for deep neck space infection or to evaluate strap muscle/retropharyngeal edema vs abscess.

3) Response to initial therapy
- If you suspect histaminergic angioedema: antihistamines, steroids, epinephrine as indicated.
- If no improvement and features above present (subacute, CK high, subtle myositis signs): stop chasing histamine/bradykinin treatments as sole explanation — broaden differential.

4) When to think dermatomyositis / inflammatory myopathy
- Elevated CK (often markedly), raised AST/ALT/LDH with muscle weakness/dysphagia.
- Nailfold capillary changes (periungual haemorrhages or telangiectasia) or violaceous facial rash.
- CT/MRI showing diffuse muscle inflammation of the neck/shoulder girdle.
- Myositis autoantibodies (eg, SAE1, MDA5, TIF1-γ etc) — useful but not required to start treatment if clinical picture severe.

Airway considerations when dysphagia / bulbar muscle involvement is suspected
- Dysphagia and bulbar weakness can progress to aspiration and failure to protect the airway even without classic external neck swelling.
- Watch for increased secretions, cough during flexible nasendoscopy, pooled secretions, voice changes, and post‑swallow aspiration on bedside testing.
- Early involvement of speech pathology for swallow assessment; consider bedside swallow or early formal video fluoroscopic swallow if available.
- Low threshold for nasogastric feeding if aspiration risk high.
- Low threshold for early ICU transfer and elective intubation if progressive bulbar weakness, worsening dysphagia or inability to handle secretions — securing the airway in a controlled setting is safer than emergency cricothyrotomy later.
- Discuss with ENT/anesthesia for potential difficult airway — neck edema from myositis can distort anatomy.

Therapeutic implications — why it matters in the ED
- Dermatomyositis is treated with high‑dose steroids and steroid‑sparing immunosuppression (IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab, methotrexate depending on phenotype and lung disease). Early recognition expedites appropriate immunotherapy and reduces complications (aspiration, progressive myositis, interstitial lung disease).
- If suspected in ED with severe bulbar/airway involvement, start systemic steroids after baseline infection and viral screens as appropriate — but do not delay airway protection.
- Coordinate admission to medical/rheumatology/immunology and arrange early CK, autoantibodies and imaging (MRI/CT/PET as needed).

Clinical pearls
- Always get a CK in unexplained neck/face swelling that is subacute or has atypical features for angioedema.
- Absence of urticaria, normal complements, lack of response to antihistamines/epinephrine and a subacute course = time to think beyond histamine/bradykinin.
- Look for subtle skin signs (periungual changes, violaceous facial erythema, Holster/shawl signs) — they may be the only dermatologic clue.
- Neck swelling from dermatomyositis is due to muscle inflammation and interstitial oedema — not mast cell or bradykinin mediated — so usual angioedema drugs won’t help.
- Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, aspiration) is common with some myositis subtypes (in SAE1-positive dermatomyositis dysphagia and ILD are not uncommon) — early swallow assessment and airway planning are vital.

Key take-home messages
- If neck/facial swelling is subacute, without urticaria, unresponsive to usual angioedema therapy, or accompanied by weakness/dysphagia — order a CK and look for dermatomyositis/myositis.
- Early airway assessment, swallow evaluation and low threshold for ICU/ENT involvement are essential when dysphagia or bulbar signs are present.
- Dermatomyositis can mimic ENT infections and angioedema; recognition in the ED directs prompt immunosuppression and organ‑specific surveillance (including malignancy work-up).

Bottom line: not every “angioedema” is histamine or bradykinin driven. Keep dermatomyositis in your differential for unexplained neck/face swelling — a quick CK and an eye for subtle skin/nailfold findings can change the diagnosis and the patient’s trajectory.



# CK Is the New C4: Lab Clues in the Swollen Neck

What if the “angioedema” you’re called for isn’t histamine or bradykinin at all, but muscle inflammation pushing up into the neck? That’s exactly what happened in a man in his 40s who arrived with progressive neck swelling, subtle proximal weakness and dysphagia — initially worked up for Ludwig’s angina and idiopathic angioedema — and turned out to have SAE1–positive dermatomyositis with a CK of 16,184 U/L. The take-home punchline: when idiopathic neck swelling doesn’t behave like angioedema, reflex a CK. CK (plus a few other labs and a quick bedside look at the nailfolds) will often outscore C4 in pointing toward myositis.

Why this is surprising and why it matters
- Neck/facial soft-tissue oedema can be an unusual manifestation of dermatomyositis. It can mimic primary ENT disease or idiopathic angioedema and delay immunosuppression.
- Misclassifying this swelling as histaminergic/bradykinin-mediated angioedema risks missed airway muscle involvement, progressive dysphagia and delayed treatment.
- A very high CK with the right clinical context almost immediately changes your differential and management: activate myositis workup, protect the airway, start immunosuppression with input from rheumatology/immunology.

The lab constellation that points toward myositis (vs true angioedema)
- CK: massive elevation (often thousands — case CK 16,184 U/L) — the single most useful reflex test when angioedema is unexplained and accompanied by weakness, dysphagia, or cutaneous clues.
- AST > ALT: AST can be disproportionately high with muscle injury (AST 396 vs ALT 143 in the case) — don’t automatically attribute transaminitis to liver disease.
- LDH: often elevated with muscle breakdown.
- Inflammatory markers: can be mildly raised (CRP not always normal) — presence of inflammatory response should not reassure you that this is infectious only.
- Complement/C1 esterase: usually normal in myositis-related “pseudoangioedema.” In true complement-mediated or hereditary angioedema you may find low C4, abnormal C1 esterase function/levels.
- Urine myoglobin/renal function: check to screen for rhabdomyolysis, but absence of myoglobinuria doesn’t rule out severe myositis.
- Myositis-specific/associated antibodies: request a panel when CK elevation and clinical features point to inflammatory myopathy (see below).

Red flags that should trigger CK now (not later)
- “Angioedema” without urticaria, pruritus, clear exposure history or ACEi/medication trigger.
- Progressive neck/facial swelling with dysphagia, dysphonia, odynophagia or signs of bulbar/airway muscle involvement.
- Any proximal muscle weakness (even subtle) or myalgias.
- Cutaneous clues: heliotrope, Gottron’s papules, shawl/V-neck signs, periungual changes, unexplained violaceous facial erythema.
- Unexplained transaminitis with AST > ALT or elevated LDH.

When to send a myositis panel and what to include
- Indications: marked CK elevation, clinical suspicion after bedside exam (weakness + rash/nailfold changes), or severe neck/face oedema unexplained by angioedema/infection.
- Practical panel: ANA and myositis antibody panel including Mi-2, TIF1-γ, MDA5, Jo-1 and SAE1 where available. Note that SAE1 positivity is associated with cutaneous features, dysphagia and risk of ILD/malignancy.
- Don’t wait for antibody results to act if the patient has airway compromise, severe dysphagia, high CK or progressive weakness.

Quick bedside nailfold capillaroscopy — how it helps
- Technique: use a dermatoscope or magnifying glass and oil on the periungual area to visualise capillary loops at the nailfold.
- What to look for: dilated/giant capillary loops, haemorrhages, avascular areas or dropout — findings that point to an inflammatory connective tissue process rather than isolated angioedema.
- It’s rapid, non-invasive and can clinch suspicion at the bedside, prompting immediate CK and specialist consultation.

Immediate management implications when myositis is likely
- Airway risk: bulbar/neck muscle involvement can progress rapidly. Low threshold for airway assessment and early ENT/anesthesia involvement.
- Early immunosuppression: high-dose steroids are commonly started emergently for severe disease; steroid-sparing agents/IVIG/rituximab are considered based on severity and specialist guidance.
- Swallowing assessment and aspiration risk: do a bedside swallow test and consider formal swallow study; treat aspiration aggressively.
- Malignancy screening and ILD surveillance: certain myositis subtypes (including SAE1) carry cancer and lung disease risk — initiate age- and risk-appropriate screening.

Clinical pearls
- “Normal C4” does not rule out a dangerous cause of neck swelling — consider CK when the history/exam don’t fit classic angioedema.
- AST > ALT with high LDH should make you think muscle, not liver, until proven otherwise.
- Nailfold capillary changes are an underused bedside tool that can point you toward dermatomyositis in minutes.
- Myositis can present without classic skin signs (heliotrope/Gottron’s) and still cause dramatic soft-tissue oedema — keep a low threshold for CK if the picture is atypical.
- Early rheumatology/immunology and ENT involvement is crucial when there is neck swelling plus dysphagia or bulbar signs.

Bottom line (the quick checklist)
- If “idiopathic” angioedema of the neck/face + any red flag → send CK STAT.
- Check AST/ALT, LDH and complement/C1 esterase concurrently.
- Do a bedside nailfold capillaroscopy and thorough neuromuscular exam.
- If CK is high or clinical suspicion strong → urgent myositis workup, airway assessment and early immunosuppression with specialist input.

Make CK the new reflex in unexplained neck swelling. It’s fast, inexpensive, and in the right clinical context it’s the test that changes everything.



# SAE1-Positive Dermatomyositis: A Phenotype to Know

What’s interesting here? A previously well man in his 40s presents with progressive neck “angioedema” and mild myalgia — initially worked up for Ludwig’s angina and angioedema — but the diagnosis turned out to be SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis with severe myositis, dysphagia and inflammatory neck swelling. This isn’t classic histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema: the swelling tracked to muscle and soft‑tissue inflammation, CK was sky high (16,184 U/L), nailfold changes and subtle rash clues were present, and muscle biopsy + SAE1 antibody confirmed dermatomyositis. The case is a great reminder that myositis can masquerade as ENT or allergy disease and that timely recognition alters management and outcomes.

Why SAE1 matters at the bedside
- SAE1 (anti‑SUMO activating enzyme subunit 1) defines a recognizable clinical pattern. In the literature, SAE1‑positive disease commonly shows prominent cutaneous features and significant bulbar involvement — dysphagia is frequent (reported ~46%). Interstitial lung disease (ILD) also occurs (reported ~43%), and malignancy has been reported in a minority (~18%). The phenotype sits distinct from other myositis autoantibodies and knowing these patterns helps prioritize assessment, screening and treatment intensity.

Key contrasts to other myositis phenotypes (practical bedside flavor)
- SAE1
  - Prominent cutaneous disease (heliotrope/shawl/V‑sign may be seen), frequent dysphagia, variable ILD risk, measurable malignancy signal (~18%).
  - Can present with frank muscle inflammation and oedema causing soft‑tissue swelling (face/neck) and aspiration risk.
- TIF1‑γ
  - Stronger association with cancer (classically highest malignancy risk among dermatomyositis antibodies).
  - Marked cutaneous findings; muscle disease variable.
  - Malignancy screening should be aggressive when TIF1‑γ positive.
- MDA5
  - Often amyopathic or paucimyopathic with striking skin ulcerations, palmar papules, and rapidly progressive ILD (high morbidity/mortality).
  - Lower malignancy signal compared with TIF1‑γ but much higher pulmonary risk — prioritize pulmonary assessment and early aggressive therapy.

How this changes what you do in the ED/ENT clinic/clinic visit
- Don’t anchor on “idiopathic angioedema” when the swelling is atypical:
  - Non‑tender, persistent neck/face swelling without clear allergic trigger, normal C1 esterase function and complements, absent ACE inhibitor use → think myositis/inflammatory causes.
- Look for subtle dermatologic and neuromuscular clues:
  - Cutaneous signs (heliotrope, shawl, Holster sign, periungual erythema or nailfold haemorrhages), proximal weakness (ask about hip flexors, climbing stairs), dysphagia or cough with secretions.
- Rapidly obtain CK (and AST/ALT/LDH): an extremely elevated CK shifts the differential away from edema/angioedema and toward myositis/necrotising myopathy.
- Early airway and swallow assessment: dysphagia and pharyngeal weakness are common in SAE1 disease — aspiration pneumonitis and need for nasogastric feeding or airway protection are real concerns.

Investigation and malignancy screening — pragmatic steps
- Basic labs: CBC, CRP, CK, AST/ALT, LDH; myoglobin/renal function if very high CK.
- Autoantibodies: ANA and a myositis panel (including SAE1, TIF1‑γ, MDA5, antisynthetase antibodies) — results guide prognosis and cancer/ILD screening intensity.
- Structural and functional testing: CT neck/chest as indicated, videofluoroscopic swallow if dysphagia, chest CT and PFTs for ILD evaluation.
- Malignancy screen tailored to risk: IMACS guidance — patients with dermatomyositis + dysphagia and SAE1 positivity may merit CT/PET and age/sex‑appropriate endoscopic evaluation; TIF1‑γ positivity pushes you even harder to look for occult cancer.

Treatment/prioritization pearls
- Treat the myositis (not histamine/bradykinin pathways) when diagnosis is established:
  - High‑dose corticosteroids for induction; consider pulse IV methylprednisolone for life/airway‑threatening disease.
  - IVIG, rituximab, mycophenolate, azathioprine, methotrexate are used as steroid‑sparing or adjunctive agents — choice depends on ILD risk, comorbidities and antibody profile (e.g., methotrexate may be avoided if ILD concern).
- Monitor for complications of severe myositis: aspiration, respiratory muscle weakness, rhabdomyolysis, renal injury, and infection from immunosuppression.
- Multidisciplinary follow‑up (rheumatology/immunology, ENT/speech pathology, pulmonology, oncology when indicated).

Clinical pearls — what to remember tomorrow morning
- Persistent, non‑tender neck/face swelling without clear allergic/infectious cause — think myositis, especially if there are cutaneous signs or weakness.
- CK is cheap, fast and informative — get it when angioedema doesn’t behave like angioedema.
- SAE1 disease: cutaneous prominence + dysphagia common; ILD risk present; malignancy risk exists (not as high as TIF1‑γ but non‑trivial) — screen accordingly.
- Airway and swallow assessment are essential early — dysphagia can be covert and lead to aspiration pneumonitis.
- Antibody phenotype informs counseling, cancer screening intensity, and anticipatory monitoring for lungs and swallowing.

Take‑home messages
- Consider inflammatory myopathy when neck or facial “angioedema” is atypical (non‑tender, persistent, no trigger, normal C1/C4).
- Check CK and examine for subtle skin/nailfold findings and proximal weakness; send a myositis panel including SAE1.
- SAE1 dermatomyositis: think prominent cutaneous disease, frequent dysphagia, ILD risk and a modest malignancy signal — screen and manage accordingly.
- Early multidisciplinary care (airway/swallow, immunosuppression, malignancy screening) changes outcomes.

This case is a neat diagnostic pivot: from ENT/angioedema pathway to systemic autoimmune disease. Keep your differential broad, use CK liberally, and let the antibody profile steer your screening and follow‑up.



# The Dangerous Swallow: Recognizing and Managing Myositis Dysphagia

What’s striking about the case that prompted this post is how a potentially life‑threatening myositis complication — severe dysphagia with aspiration risk — presented not with classic proximal limb weakness or Gottron’s papules, but as a “neck swelling” misinterpreted initially as angioedema or deep neck infection. The patient turned out to have SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with marked myositis of the head and neck on PET/MRI, severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy, aspiration pneumonitis on CT, and profound CK elevation. This is a great reminder: when neck fullness, dysphagia, cough with secretions, or unexplained “pseudo‑angioedema” show up, think myositis — and act early to protect the airway, prevent aspiration, and start targeted therapy.

## Why dysphagia in inflammatory myopathy matters
Dysphagia occurs in many dermatomyositis and other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM). It may be subtle at first, progress rapidly, and reflect involvement of oropharyngeal and upper esophageal striated muscle — not a primary ENT lesion. Consequences include aspiration (silent or overt), aspiration pneumonitis, weight loss, malnutrition, and need for prolonged enteral support. In SAE1‑positive disease dysphagia and interstitial lung disease are not uncommon.

In the case above, the patient had increased secretions and coughing on nasendoscopy, a severe pharyngeal residue and penetration on videofluoroscopy, CT chest changes consistent with aspiration pneumonitis, and restrictive pulmonary function abnormalities reflecting weakness of extrathoracic respiratory muscles. The team moved quickly: CK measurement, imaging, VFSS, early SLP input, NG feeding and aggressive immunosuppression — and avoided emergent airway catastrophe.

## How to spot myositis dysphagia at the bedside
Be suspicious when dysphagia co‑exists with any of the following:
- proximal limb weakness (even mild), myalgias, markedly elevated CK, or subtle cutaneous features of dermatomyositis (periungual changes, shawl/V‑neck sign, heliotrope, Holster sign).
- neck fullness/swelling that is non‑tender, non‑infective in pattern, or accompanies voice/airway secretions and coughing.
- cough during swallow testing or frequent clearing, and history of recurrent chest infections.

Clinical pearls:
- Dysphagia can be silent. Absence of overt choking does not rule out aspiration risk.
- Early CK testing is cheap, fast and high yield when ENT/ED findings don’t explain the problem.
- Nailfold capillaroscopy/periungual changes can be the dermatologic clue that changes the whole differential.

## Early multidisciplinary steps — don’t wait
1) Call speech‑language pathology (SLP) early. A focused bedside swallow screen by SLP helps decide urgency of formal assessment and need for NPO status.
2) If there is any concern on bedside assessment (coughing, wet voice, pooling, weak cough) go straight to instrumental evaluation — videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) depending on availability and patient stability.
3) Put the patient NPO until SLP/instrumental evaluation unless you have unequivocal bedside evidence of safe oral intake.
4) Secure nutrition: temporary nasogastric tube feeding is often the first line for reversible inflammatory myopathy causing dysphagia; PEG only if prolonged (>4–6 weeks) or if long‑term enteral access is anticipated.
5) Manage secretions aggressively — they cause aspiration and airway compromise:
   - Anticholinergic agents (glycopyrrolate) can reduce drooling in selected patients; consider risks.
   - Frequent suctioning, repositioning, and oral hygiene reduce bacterial load.
   - If secretion burden is severe and unresponsive, consult ENT for consideration of more invasive airway/airway protection procedures.

Grounding in the case: this patient required nasogastric feeding for two months while immunotherapy worked — a pragmatic, reversible approach.

## Imaging and pulmonary testing — interpret with the myositis lens
- CT chest clues consistent with aspiration pneumonitis: peribronchial consolidation, dependent ground‑glass or patchy airspace opacities—especially in mid‑to‑lower zones and with a peribronchial distribution — should raise suspicion for aspiration rather than primary fibrosing ILD.
- FDG‑PET and MRI can localize active muscle inflammation in head/neck and pharyngeal musculature; useful when the clinical picture is unclear.
- Pulmonary function testing: an "extrathoracic restrictive" pattern or reduced vital capacity that is worse in supine position suggests respiratory muscle weakness (bulbar/diaphragmatic involvement). This pattern differs from classical parenchymal restriction seen with fibrosis and should prompt assessment of respiratory muscle strength and swallowing safety.

In the case, PFTs showed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern without significant bronchodilator response — supporting muscle weakness rather than airway disease. CT showed peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration.

## When to escalate airway and respiratory support
Escalate early if any of the following exist:
- Signs of impaired airway protection: ineffective cough, accumulation of secretions, recurrent desaturation, stridor, or progressive bulbar weakness.
- Radiological progression of aspiration pneumonia or hypoxaemia despite oxygen and conservative measures.
- Rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness including neck flexors, with evidence of declining vital capacity or inability to clear secretions.

Escalation options:
- Admit to a monitored setting; frequent respiratory observation and serial vital capacity measurements.
- Consider elective intubation for airway protection when bulbar failure is progressive or secretions cannot be managed noninvasively.
- In prolonged ventilatory failure or recurrent aspiration, discuss tracheostomy for airway protection and pulmonary toilet with the multidisciplinary team.
- Early immunomodulatory therapy (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG ± steroid‑sparing agents/biologics) is essential — treating the underlying myositis is what ultimately improves swallowing function.

In our reported patient, prompt high‑dose IV methylprednisolone, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab were used because of significant airway muscle involvement — and the patient recovered swallowing over weeks to months.

## Practical SLP and medical management tips
- Use VFSS to define the physiology: oral vs pharyngeal phase impairment, residue, penetration/aspiration, and need for compensatory strategies (chin tuck, head turn, thickened fluids).
- FEES is invaluable at bedside when transport to radiology is unsafe or to directly visualize secretions, pooling, and laryngeal sensation.
- Implement compensatory strategies only if VFSS/FEES show they are effective; do not rely on maneuvers alone if aspiration is significant.
- Enteral route: NG tube for short‑term use; consider PEG only when recovery is uncertain or prolonged.
- Maintain excellent oral hygiene and reduce oral bacterial load to limit aspiration pneumonia severity.
- Coordinate immunotherapy early with rheumatology/immunology — dysphagia often improves with disease control, but recovery can lag weeks to months.

## Take‑home messages
- Myositis (including dermatomyositis) can present with severe dysphagia and head/neck “pseudo‑angioedema”; suspect it when neck swelling is unexplained or accompanied by weakness, elevated CK or cutaneous signs.
- Early SLP involvement and instrumental assessment (VFSS or FEES) are mandatory when aspiration risk is suspected.
- Protect the airway: keep NPO until assessed, use NG feeding for short‑term nutrition, manage secretions aggressively, and escalate to intubation/tracheostomy if airway protection fails.
- Interpret CT chest and PFTs with the myositis context — peribronchial consolidation suggests aspiration pneumonitis and an extrathoracic restrictive pattern supports respiratory muscle weakness.
- Timely immunosuppression matters: treating the myositis is the definitive step to recovering swallow function, but expect a weeks‑to‑months timeline.

Seen a patient with unexplained neck swelling and cough with secretions? Check a CK, look for dermatomyositis skin signs, call SLP, and think about aspiration — the dangerous swallow is often silent until it’s not.



# Imaging the Puffy Neck: Infection, Angioedema, or Inflamed Muscle?

What looks like “Ludwig’s angina” or idiopathic angioedema isn’t always infection or histamine/bradykinin‑mediated swelling. This case of anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis presenting with progressive neck swelling shows how inflammatory myopathy can masquerade as ENT disease — with CT that screams deep neck oedema, but no abscess, and MRI/PET that point to active myositis. If you’re the ED, ENT or radiologist facing a puffy neck, knowing the imaging patterns (and the clinical checks) can shortcut delays to immunosuppression and avoid unnecessary surgery or antibiotics.

## Why this case is interesting
- Subacute, painful‑appearing cervical swelling evolved over weeks and prompted transfer for possible Ludwig’s angina, yet there was no drainable collection or source.
- CT showed diffuse strap muscle and retropharyngeal edema, but flexible nasendoscopy and labs were not consistent with a deep neck space infection.
- Muscle‑directed testing (very high CK), MRI thigh changes, FDG‑PET muscle uptake, and muscle biopsy confirmed dermatomyositis (SAE1 antibody positive) — a rare but documented cause of “pseudo‑angioedema.”
- The imaging pattern shifted management from surgical/antibiotic pathways to urgent immunosuppression, with good clinical recovery.

## Radiology pearls — how to separate the big three

1) CT neck: infection (Ludwig’s/abscess) vs inflammatory myositis
- Suggests infection (Ludwig’s angina/deep neck space abscess)
  - Focal fluid collection with rim enhancement
  - Gas locules, adjacent odontogenic source, asymmetric fascial plane involvement
  - Significant fat stranding, rapid progression, possible systemic sepsis features
- Suggests myositis/inflammatory neck swelling
  - Diffuse enlargement and edema of strap muscles (symmetric or patchy) without a discrete rim‑enhancing collection
  - Retropharyngeal soft tissue thickening driven by inflamed muscle and soft tissue, but no focal abscess
  - Less pronounced fat stranding or overlying cellulitis signs compared with bacterial infection
  - Lack of gas and absence of a clear odontogenic or tonsillar source

2) CT neck: angioedema (histaminergic/bradykinin)
- Classical imaging: diffuse soft‑tissue swelling of subcutaneous tissues and mucosal/submucosal thickening (larynx, pharynx), generally without enhancement or muscle‑centered edema
- No strap‑muscle T2/STIR pattern of myositis and no muscle necrosis
- Clinical context matters: rapid onset, triggers (ACEi, allergic exposure), urticaria, pruritus (histaminergic) or prior angioedema history

3) MRI muscle: the myositis signature
- Active inflammatory myositis shows muscle enlargement with:
  - T2/STIR or fat‑suppressed sequences: diffuse or perifascicular hyperintensity (edema)
  - T1: may show fatty atrophy chronically; acute disease less T1 change
  - Distribution can be patchy or perifascicular (typical in dermatomyositis), and can involve neck/shoulder girdle muscles when clinically relevant
- Important: MRI localizes active disease for biopsy and correlates with symptoms (eg, neck muscle involvement causing swelling/dysphagia)

4) FDG‑PET: inflammation and opportunistic malignancy screen
- Diffuse, symmetric increased FDG uptake in multiple muscle groups — points toward systemic inflammatory myopathy rather than localized infection
- PET doubles as malignancy screen in dermatomyositis (given paraneoplastic risk), so it can answer two questions: is uptake muscle‑predominant (inflammatory) and is there occult tumor?
- Focal nodal or visceral hypermetabolism would raise concern for occult malignancy and prompt targeted investigation

## Pitfalls that mislead clinicians (and how to avoid them)
- Pitfall: Seeing a swollen neck with elevated CRP and defaulting to antibiotics/surgery.
  - Avoidance: Look for drainage signs, gas, rim‑enhancing collections on CT and correlate with exam (fever, odonto/tonsillar source). If imaging lacks a drainable abscess, pause for alternative causes.
- Pitfall: Attributing neck swelling to angioedema without measuring CK or examining skin/nailfolds.
  - Avoidance: In subacute or atypical presentations, always check CK and inspect for cutaneous stigmata (heliotrope, Gottron’s, periungual changes, Holster sign). Ask about muscle weakness/dysphagia.
- Pitfall: Interpreting soft tissue swelling on CT as cellulitis when muscle‑centric edema is present.
  - Avoidance: Review muscle planes — strap muscle enlargement and retropharyngeal edema without superficial fascial necrosis favors myositis.
- Pitfall: Missing myositis when ENT endoscopy is “normal.”
  - Avoidance: Swallowing dysfunction may be due to pharyngeal muscle weakness; get a barium swallow if dysphagia is present and consider MRI of neck/thighs.

## Practical workflow for the patient with unexplained neck swelling
- First pass (ED/ENT/radiology)
  - Rapid airway assessment: any signs of compromise → secure airway.
  - Urgent contrast CT neck if infection suspected. Look specifically for abscess vs diffuse muscle edema.
- If CT lacks abscess or surgical source, and presentation is subacute or associated with weakness/dysphagia:
  - Check CK, AST/ALT, LDH
  - Full skin exam (heliotrope, Gottron’s, periungual changes)
  - Consider ANA/myositis panel and nailfold capillaroscopy when available
  - MRI (neck or thighs) to document muscle edema and guide biopsy
  - FDG‑PET if systemic myositis suspected and malignancy screening is indicated
- Management pivots
  - If myositis likely: early rheumatology/immunology input and prompt immunosuppression (steroids ± steroid‑sparing agents, IVIG, rituximab as indicated)
  - Avoid unnecessary surgical exploration or repeated antibiotics when imaging and labs point away from infection

## Clinical pearls
- Strapped muscle enlargement and retropharyngeal soft tissue edema on CT without rim‑enhancing collection is a red flag for non‑infectious causes — think myositis.
- Very high CK in the face of neck swelling is diagnostic gold: if CK is elevated, the differential shifts strongly toward inflammatory myopathy.
- MRI T2/STIR hyperintensity in muscle and perifascicular changes is characteristic of dermatomyositis and guides biopsy.
- FDG‑PET often shows diffuse muscle uptake in inflammatory myopathies and provides concurrent malignancy screening — a high‑yield single test in suspicious dermatomyositis.
- Don’t conflate “angioedema” with any neck swelling — history timeframe, triggers, urticaria, and imaging distribution distinguish histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema from myositis‑related subcutaneous edema.

## Take‑home messages
- Neck swelling without a drainable collection on CT should prompt consideration of inflammatory myopathy, especially when accompanied by weakness, dysphagia, or subtle cutaneous signs.
- Always check a CK early in unexplained neck swelling; it’s cheap, fast, and can be decisive.
- Use MRI for muscle characterization and FDG‑PET to document systemic muscle inflammation while screening for occult malignancy.
- Early recognition avoids unnecessary surgery and expedites immunosuppressive therapy, which can be lifesaving when airway or swallowing muscles are involved.

If you’re the radiologist putting eyes on a swollen neck CT: before you sign off “no drainable abscess,” add a line — “consider inflammatory myopathy if clinical context and labs (CK) support” — and you may change a patient’s trajectory from OR to rheumatology.



# Hitting Dermatomyositis Early and Hard: Steroids, IVIG, and Beyond

What makes this case worth your attention? A previously well man in his 40s walks into the ED with progressive neck swelling — initially worked up as Ludwig’s angina or angioedema — and ends up diagnosed with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis with prominent neck/subcutaneous oedema, severe dysphagia and very high CK (16,184 U/L). The “angioedema” turned out to be inflammatory myositis of the neck/airway muscles manifesting as non-histaminergic, non-bradykinin swelling. The team treated him aggressively with pulse steroids, early IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab and achieved clinical remission.

If you see unexplained neck swelling with any muscle weakness, dysphagia, skin/nail findings or raised inflammatory markers — check a CK. This case crystallises a pragmatic approach for severe dermatomyositis with airway/swallow involvement.

## How I think about initial severity and the need to be aggressive
Severe dermatomyositis features that push me to “hit hard” early:
- Airway or bulbar muscle involvement (dysphagia, aspiration risk) — high morbidity.
- Very high CK with clinical weakness.
- Rapid progression over days–weeks.
- Radiologic/functional evidence of lung involvement or aspiration.
- Autoantibodies associated with aggressive phenotype (eg, SAE1 in this case, MDA5/TIF1 associations can influence risk).

When airway/bulbar muscles are involved you’re treating both inflammatory myositis and protecting airway/nutrition — early, potent immunosuppression is justified.

## Practical induction regimen (what worked in this case)
From the report — and what I would mirror in similar presentations:
1. Pulse IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days (for severe/bulbar involvement), then transition to high-dose oral prednisone (eg, 0.5–1 mg/kg/day) with a defined taper.
   - Rationale: rapid suppression of inflammation to protect airway and muscles.
2. Early IVIG (total 2 g/kg, split over 3–5 days as a loading course), then maintenance cycles (eg, monthly) as clinically indicated.
   - In this case: loading 2 g/kg over 5 days, then monthly cycles — correlated with fast improvement in swelling and eventual recovery of strength.
   - IVIG is especially useful for severe, refractory, or bulbar disease and when rapid improvement is needed.
3. Start a steroid-sparing immunosuppressant early (chosen to match pulmonary status and patient factors).
   - Options:
     - Mycophenolate mofetil (used in this case: 1 g twice daily) — good GI/tolerance profile, used widely when there's concern about methotrexate and ILD.
     - Methotrexate — effective but use cautiously if interstitial lung disease or pulmonary fibrosis is a concern.
     - Azathioprine, tacrolimus are alternatives depending on comorbidities and response.
4. Consider rituximab for severe disease, refractory cases, or specific antibody profiles.
   - In this patient they used two 1,000 mg doses two weeks apart — reasonable for severe disease needing B-cell targeted therapy.
   - Rituximab can be considered early in life‑threatening or steroid‑refractory cases, or when combination therapy is preferred.

Tailor the combination to disease severity, pulmonary status, comorbidities and infection risk. In this case the combination (pulse steroids + IVIG + mycophenolate + rituximab) produced remission.

## Timelines for response — what to expect and monitor
- Symptom improvement:
  - Bulbar/airway swelling and pain: often first to respond over days–weeks after pulse steroids/IVIG.
  - Strength (proximal muscles): measurable improvement often within 2–6 weeks; more complete recovery may take months.
- CK trends:
  - Expect CK to fall quickly with effective therapy — in this case CK normalized within weeks (from 16,184 to ~37 U/L by about 3 weeks).
  - CK is useful but not the whole story — correlate with function and symptoms.
- Functional monitoring:
  - Serial bedside strength testing (hip flexors, deltoids), timed functional tests when possible.
  - Objective measures: MRC scale, timed up-and-go, 30-second sit-to-stand in follow-up.
- Respiratory/bulbar monitoring:
  - Regular swallow assessment (safety of oral intake), consider nasogastric/PEG feeding if aspiration risk persists.
  - Pulmonary function tests and imaging for ILD surveillance.

Expect early biochemical improvement (CK) and progressive functional gains; don’t stop close monitoring even if CK normalises.

## Where rituximab fits
- Role: B-cell depletion for refractory disease, severe presentations, or certain autoantibody-driven phenotypes.
- Timing: can be given early in severe cases or added if inadequate response to steroids + steroid-sparing agent +/‑ IVIG.
- Dosing: common regimens include 1,000 mg IV x2 (2 weeks apart) — same as used here.
- Caveat: screen for infectious risks pre-rituximab (HBV, TB, HIV), and plan vaccination/ prophylaxis strategies.

## Choosing a steroid-sparing agent when ILD is on the table
- If ILD or pulmonary fibrosis is present or strongly suspected, favour:
  - Mycophenolate mofetil (frequently used; used in this case) or
  - Tacrolimus in certain contexts.
- Use methotrexate with caution if ILD or baseline abnormal lung function exists — risk profile and patient preferences matter.
- Azathioprine remains an option when other choices aren’t suitable.

## PJP prophylaxis — options when TMP‑SMX isn’t tolerated
Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole is first-line for Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis, but adverse reactions can occur (as in this case: haemolytic anaemia).
Alternatives:
- Aerosolized pentamidine 300 mg nebulised every 4 weeks — used successfully for this patient.
- Oral atovaquone (750 mg twice daily with food) — suitable when TMP‑SMX not tolerated and G6PD deficiency precludes dapsone.
- Dapsone (100 mg daily) — an option if G6PD normal; check G6PD first.
Pick the alternative based on tolerance, G6PD status, absorption concerns and logistics.

## What else to remember (practical pearls)
- Don’t anchor on “angioedema” if swelling is atypical: check for skin signs (periungual changes, rash), proximal weakness, dysphagia and order a CK early.
- Neck/facial swelling in dermatomyositis is rare but real — it may be myositis-related oedema rather than histamine/bradykinin-mediated angioedema.
- Early IVIG can be a game changer in bulbar/airway involvement.
- Malignancy screening is mandatory in adult dermatomyositis — tailor investigations to risk factors and antibody profile; continue symptom-driven surveillance.
- Multidisciplinary care: involve rheumatology/immunology, ENT (airway assessment), speech/swallow therapy, respiratory and nutrition teams early.

## Take-home messages
- Check CK when neck/facial “angioedema” is unexplained or accompanied by weakness/dysphagia/skin signs.
- For severe dermatomyositis with airway or bulbar involvement: pulse IV methylprednisolone, early IVIG, and an early steroid‑sparing agent are reasonable — escalate to rituximab for severe/refractory disease.
- Monitor clinical function and CK serially; expect biochemical improvement quickly but functional recovery may take weeks–months.
- If TMP‑SMX is not tolerated for PJP prophylaxis, consider aerosolized pentamidine, atovaquone, or dapsone (if G6PD clear).
- Keep a low threshold for multidisciplinary care and cancer screening.

This case is a reminder: dermatomyositis can hide in plain sight — sometimes masquerading as ENT disease — and when it threatens the airway or swallow, treat early and treat aggressively.



## Don’t Miss the Nails: Bedside Cutaneous Clues in Systemic Myositis

A guy in his 40s shows up with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling — everyone thinks Ludwig’s angina, ENT scopes him, CT shows diffuse neck soft-tissue oedema, no abscess. He’s afebrile, not stridulous. He’s sent to immunology for “angioedema” workup. The small-but-mighty clue that changed everything? Nailfold microhaemorrhages and periungual changes on bedside capillaroscopy, plus subtle violaceous facial erythema (including the nasolabial folds) and a Holster sign on the thigh. A stat CK confirms massive myositis, autoantibodies (anti-SAE1) and muscle biopsy clinch dermatomyositis. What looked like ENT disease and angioedema was actually inflammatory myositis with soft‑tissue oedema and dysphagia requiring urgent immunosuppression.

Why this matters
- Neck/facial swelling is usually worked up as infection, venous congestion, histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema. But dermatomyositis (and other connective tissue diseases) can produce prominent subcutaneous oedema and mimic ENT pathology.
- The difference is not academic. Dermatomyositis often needs high-dose steroids and rapid steroid‑sparing therapy (IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab, etc.). Delaying treatment risks airway compromise, aspiration and progressive muscle damage.
- Low-tech skin and nail signs at the bedside are high-yield: they can reframe the entire diagnostic pathway before expensive imaging or invasive procedures.

What to look for at the bedside
- Periungual telangiectasias and nailfold microhaemorrhages — early, sometimes the only cutaneous clue.
- Violaceous facial erythema that can include the nasolabial folds (don’t dismiss facial erythema as rosacea or sunburn when other clues are present).
- Holster sign (violaceous rash on the lateral thigh), shawl or V‑neck signs, heliotrope eyelid rash, Gottron’s papules — classic but not always present.
- Subacute proximal muscle weakness (hip flexors, shoulder abductors), dysphagia, or subtle cough/aspiration on nasendoscopy — think inflammatory myopathy.

How to do quick nailfold capillaroscopy in ED/clinic (one-minute technique)
- Use what you have: a dermatoscope, magnifying glass (10×), or even the camera + flashlight on a smartphone. A patient-friendly nailfold view is enough.
- Warm hands first (cold causes vasoconstriction). Have the patient rest palms up for a minute.
- Place the magnifier/dermatoscope at the proximal nailfold (just proximal to the cuticle) of the 2nd–5th fingers.
- Look for dilated/enlarged capillary loops, capillary dropouts/avascular areas, and microhaemorrhages. Periungual erythema is visible without magnification.
- Record photos if available (helps follow-up and specialist referral).

Interpreting the findings (practical points)
- Microhaemorrhages, capillary dilatation and avascular areas are suggestive of microangiopathy seen in dermatomyositis and systemic sclerosis spectrum disorders.
- These changes are not diagnostic by themselves but should raise suspicion for myositis in the right clinical context.
- Normal nailfold exam does not exclude myositis — absence of signs doesn’t rule it out.

When to pivot the workup (clinical triggers)
- Neck/facial swelling without airway compromise and no focal infectious source on CT.
- Normal inflammatory markers and WBC do not exclude inflammatory myositis; CK can be massively elevated even with modest CRP.
- Any proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, aspiration on swallow study, or unexplained transaminitis — check a CK early.
- Cutaneous signs described above — do a bedside nailfold exam; if positive, add CK, myositis antibody panel, and early rheumatology/immunology consult.

Common mimics and how to keep yourself honest
- Histaminergic angioedema: often itchy, transient, responds to antihistamines/epinephrine; usually rapid onset/offset.
- Bradykinin-mediated angioedema (ACEi): look for medication history, normal complement/C1 esterase function.
- Cellulitis/deep neck infection: fever, leukocytosis, focal tenderness, fluctuance/abscess on imaging.
- Superior vena cava syndrome: facial/neck swelling with venous congestion signs, positional worsening.
- Hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis: painful urticarial lesions, low complement; look for systemic features.
- If in doubt, combine signs: ENT exam + imaging + bedside skin/nail exam + CK will separate these quickly.

Key diagnostic and practical tips
- CK is cheap, fast, and game-changing. If clinical suspicion exists (nailfold changes, proximal weakness, neck swelling with no clear ENT cause), send CK STAT.
- Early myositis antibody testing (including less common antibodies like SAE1) can help phenotype disease and triage malignancy screening.
- Dysphagia or bulbar muscle involvement raises urgency — these patients aspirate and often require early IV steroids/IVIG.
- If you suspect dermatomyositis, involve rheumatology/immunology early and consider inpatient monitoring for airway or swallowing compromise.

Take-home messages
- Bedside nailfold capillaroscopy is a low-tech, high-yield exam that can redirect workups away from ENT/infectious causes toward systemic myositis.
- In unexplained neck/facial oedema, look at the nails and skin; if you find periungual telangiectasias/microhaemorrhages or violaceous rashes, get a CK and call rheum/immunology.
- Don’t assume “angioedema” without a compatible history or response to standard therapies — dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema and needs very different treatment.

Keep your eyes on the nails — they often tell the systemic story before the CT does.



## Cancer Screening in Dermatomyositis: A Pragmatic IMACS Walkthrough

What makes this worth your attention? Because dermatomyositis (DM) is not just a muscle-and-skin problem — it’s a paraneoplastic red flag. The case that sparked this post is classic in how it forces you to think beyond the obvious: a middle‑aged man with SAE1‑positive DM, dysphagia and neck swelling that wasn’t angioedema. Those features together pushed this patient into a high‑risk cancer category and triggered an aggressive but pragmatic malignancy screen that found no occult tumour — for now. This is exactly the clinical moment IMACS guidance aims to address: identify who needs what testing, when, and how often.

Here’s a bedside-friendly approach to translating IMACS into action.

Why screen at all (brief rationale)
- Multiple studies show an increased incidence of cancer in patients with DM, especially within the first 3 years of disease onset. Certain clinical features (dermatomyositis phenotype itself, dysphagia) and specific myositis autoantibodies increase the pretest probability. Early detection matters because malignancy‑associated DM can respond to tumour therapy and has distinct management and prognostic implications.

Risk stratification — the practical checklist
Use readily available clinical features to group patients into low / intermediate / high malignancy risk. IMACS highlights several risk factors; pragmatically prioritize these at the bedside:
- High‑risk features (any one, but risk accumulates): dermatomyositis phenotype, dysphagia, older age (typically >40–50), male sex in some series, certain autoantibodies (eg, anti‑TIF1‑γ, SAE1 has been reported as an intermediate/higher risk marker in some cohorts).
- Intermediate risk: single atypical antibody, milder cutaneous disease without dysphagia.
- Low risk: polymyositis phenotype without cutaneous features, younger patients, absence of risk antibodies.

A staged, pragmatic screening plan to initiate during the index admission
Stage 1 — bedside and baseline tests you should order now
- Thorough history and focused exam: review weight loss, constitutional symptoms, smoking, family history, directed lymph node and pelvic/genital exam, ENT/oral exam if dysphagia.
- Basic bloods: FBC, LFTs, renal function, inflammatory markers.
- Tumour markers (adjunctive, not diagnostic): CEA, CA‑125 (women), CA19‑9, PSA (men) — use to complement imaging and follow trends if suspicious, not as lone screening tests.
- Myositis antibody panel results (if not already available) — they change pretest probability and may guide intensity of screening.

Stage 2 — cross‑sectional imaging (baseline)
- For high‑risk patients (eg, DM + dysphagia, positive high‑risk antibody such as TIF1‑γ or SAE1 with other risk features, older age): obtain whole‑body PET‑CT if available, or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast. Rationale: PET‑CT has high sensitivity for occult malignancy and can also map inflammatory muscle disease; CT is a reasonable alternative where PET isn’t accessible.
- For intermediate risk: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis is a reasonable baseline.
- For low risk: age‑appropriate imaging/screening; consider CT/PET if clinical concern emerges.

Stage 3 — targeted investigations
- Upper and lower endoscopy: consider early endoscopy (gastroscopy ± colonoscopy) if high‑risk features include dysphagia, GI symptoms, or if PET/CT or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis raises concern. IMACS and multiple series suggest GI malignancies can be missed on routine imaging; the recent case appropriately planned endoscopy despite negative imaging.
- Age/gender‑specific: mammography, pelvic ultrasound/gynecologic review (women), testicular/urogenital evaluation (men) depending on findings and risk.
- ENT/ORL evaluation if head/neck symptoms or PET/CT uptake.
- Referral to oncology/gastroenterology/urology/gynecology as indicated.

Timing and pragmatic surveillance intervals
- Highest yield window: first 3 years after DM onset; most cancers present within this period.
- Suggested schedule (practical, risk‑adjusted):
  - High risk: baseline CT or PET‑CT + targeted endoscopy where indicated during index admission; repeat imaging at 6–12 months, then annually up to 3 years if negative. Maintain a low threshold for earlier re‑imaging if new symptoms arise.
  - Intermediate risk: baseline CT chest/abdomen/pelvis; repeat at 12 months and then annually for 2 years if no clinical concern.
  - Low risk: ensure adherence to age‑appropriate population screening; review clinically every 3–6 months for symptoms during the first 2 years.
- Individualize frequency: frailty, comorbidities, and patient preference matter; discuss the balance of diagnostic yield versus radiation/cost.

Red flags that mandate expedited re‑workup
- New or progressive weight loss, iron‑deficiency anaemia, unexplained lymphadenopathy, change in respiratory symptoms, new focal pain or neurological deficits, or new/progressive skin changes.
- New PET‑avid lesions, rising tumour markers, or abnormalities on interval imaging.

Practical tips for workflow in the hospital
- Start the basic labs, myositis antibody panel and tumor markers on admission; these are cheap and fast.
- Arrange CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (or PET‑CT) early — logistically easier from inpatient than trying to expedite outpatient bookings.
- If dysphagia is present, coordinate early gastroenterology / ENT / speech and swallow teams and plan endoscopy when safe.
- Document discussion with the patient about malignancy risk and plan for surveillance — medico‑legal and important for shared decision‑making.
- Link care: set up outpatient oncology/rheumatology follow‑up and a clear surveillance timeline in discharge paperwork.

Counselling points for patients
- Explain that DM carries an increased but not inevitable risk of associated cancer, particularly early after diagnosis.
- Describe the planned tests, why they’re being done now, and what surveillance will look like.
- Reassure that negative baseline tests aren’t a lifetime guarantee — that’s why targeted follow‑up for 2–3 years is important.

How the case ties it together
The presented SAE1‑positive DM patient had multiple high/intermediate risk features (DM phenotype, dysphagia, SAE1 antibody, male sex). The team appropriately escalated: aggressive baseline imaging (CT + PET), tumour markers, targeted endoscopic plans, and a structured follow‑up schedule. This is exactly the pragmatic IMACS approach in action: risk‑stratify, do targeted, timely testing during the index admission, and surveil over the high‑yield first 3 years.

Key take‑home messages
- Dermatomyositis is a paraneoplastic red flag — the highest malignancy risk is within the first 3 years after diagnosis.
- Risk‑stratify at the bedside: DM phenotype, dysphagia, age, sex and autoantibodies (eg, TIF1‑γ, SAE1) drive how aggressive your screen should be.
- High‑risk patients: do baseline PET‑CT (or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis if PET not available) and consider early endoscopy; repeat imaging at 6–12 months and then annually up to 3 years.
- Intermediate risk: baseline CT and clinical surveillance; consider repeat imaging at 12 months.
- Low risk: ensure age‑appropriate cancer screening and close clinical follow‑up; escalate testing if new symptoms develop.
- Start tests early during the index admission when possible (labs, myositis antibodies, imaging) and document a clear surveillance plan with specialist follow‑up.
