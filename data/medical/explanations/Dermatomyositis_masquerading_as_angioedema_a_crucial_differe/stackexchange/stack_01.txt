Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

### [Morning Report] A man in his 40s presents with 3 weeks of progressive, non-tender neck swelling initially presumed to be angioedema; exam reveals subtle periungual erythema and mild proximal hip flexor weakness without urticaria. What single laboratory test should be ordered urgently, and how would a marked abnormality redirect the diagnostic workup?

Order a serum creatine kinase (CK) immediately.

Why: the combination of progressive, non‑tender neck “angioedema,” subtle periungual/skin changes and proximal hip‑flexor weakness should raise suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis) rather than histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema. CK is the single fastest, most informative test to detect active muscle injury. In the reported case a CK of 16,184 U/L (many‑fold above normal) was the pivotal result that changed the working diagnosis.

How a marked CK elevation redirects the workup (practical steps and rationale)
- Diagnostic pivot: a very high CK points to myositis/rhabdomyolysis → stop treating this as isolated angioedema or primary ENT infection and begin a myositis workup.
- Immediate additional labs to obtain/monitor: renal function, electrolytes, AST/ALT, LDH, and urine myoglobin (to assess and manage rhabdomyolysis risk).
- Focused autoimmunity testing: order myositis‑specific/‑associated autoantibodies (eg, SAE1, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, anti‑Jo‑1) and ANA — these may identify dermatomyositis subtypes and cancer risk (as in this case, SAE1 positivity).
- Imaging and tissue diagnosis: obtain MRI of affected muscles and consider FDG‑PET for extent/malignancy screening; plan muscle biopsy if needed to confirm diagnosis (the case had typical biopsy with C5b‑9 deposition).
- Evaluate complications and organ involvement: formal swallowing evaluation (videofluoroscopic swallow) for dysphagia/aspiration; pulmonary assessment with PFTs and chest CT to screen for interstitial lung disease; malignancy screening per IMACS guidance (age‑ and risk‑appropriate imaging/endoscopy).
- Urgent management implications: marked CK with bulbar/airway involvement warrants early aggressive immunosuppression (high‑dose IV methylprednisolone ± IVIG and steroid‑sparing agents) and supportive measures (airway protection, NG feeding) — as was done in the reported patient.
- Continued monitoring: serial CKs to follow treatment response and ongoing surveillance for ILD or occult malignancy given dermatomyositis associations.

Bottom line: In this scenario, a serum CK is the single urgent test. A markedly elevated CK redirects evaluation from angioedema/infectious causes toward inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis), triggers autoimmune serologies, muscle imaging/biopsy, aspiration and pulmonary assessment, malignancy screening, and often urgent immunosuppressive therapy.
