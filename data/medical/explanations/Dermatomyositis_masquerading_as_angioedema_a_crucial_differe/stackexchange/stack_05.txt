Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

### [Clinical Pearls] In inflammatory myopathy with severe oropharyngeal dysphagia, how do an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on pulmonary function testing and a videofluoroscopic swallow study guide diagnosis of respiratory complications and immediate management to prevent aspiration-related lung injury?

Clinical pearl — how these two tests change diagnosis and immediate management

When an inflammatory myopathy presents with severe oropharyngeal dysphagia, combine pulmonary function testing and a videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) to (1) determine the mechanism of respiratory compromise, (2) establish aspiration as the cause of lung injury, and (3) trigger immediate protective interventions.

Why each test matters
- Pulmonary function testing: a restrictive pattern in this setting points to respiratory muscle involvement (diaphragm and accessory muscles) or extrathoracic/upper‑airway compromise from inflamed neck musculature and soft tissues. Documented falls in FVC (especially supine), low maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) or evidence of extrathoracic flow limitation imply reduced cough and impaired ability to protect the airway — physiologic risk factors for aspiration and for rapid respiratory decompensation. In the case, an extrathoracic restrictive pattern raised concern for neck/airway muscle dysfunction rather than primary obstructive lung disease.

- Videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS): the VFSS is the diagnostic standard for oropharyngeal dysphagia and aspiration risk. Findings of severe pharyngeal dysphagia, pyriform sinus residue and post‑swallow penetration to the vocal cords (as in this patient) objectively confirm impaired bolus clearance and high risk of micro‑aspiration and aspiration pneumonitis.

How the tests guide diagnosis of respiratory complications
- Correlation of VFSS (aspiration risk) with PFTs (reduced respiratory reserve and ineffective cough) makes aspiration the likely mechanism for the patient’s pulmonary findings. In the report, CT chest showed peribronchial consolidation in mid–lower zones consistent with aspiration pneumonitis; combined with VFSS and restrictive PFTs, this established micro‑aspiration from pharyngeal dysfunction as the cause rather than primary interstitial lung disease.

Immediate management priorities prompted by these findings
1. Protect the airway and prevent further aspiration
   - Make patient NPO immediately when VFSS shows high‑risk aspiration.
   - Institute enteral feeding by nasogastric tube (or consider early PEG if prolonged) — this was used in the case.
   - Positioning (upright during/after feeds), oral hygiene, and suctioning of secretions.
   - Early speech and language pathology (swallow therapy) and ENT input.

2. Treat respiratory compromise and monitor closely
   - Supplemental oxygen, chest physiotherapy, aggressive secretion management.
   - Monitor for respiratory muscle fatigue (serial FVC, MIP/MEP, overnight oximetry) — low reserve may prompt noninvasive ventilation or early intubation if protective reflexes fail or gas exchange deteriorates.
   - Repeat chest imaging to document evolving aspiration pneumonitis versus secondary infection.

3. Treat the underlying inflammatory myopathy urgently
   - Early high‑dose corticosteroids and additional immunotherapy (IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents or biologics) to reverse bulbar and respiratory muscle inflammation; this case received IV methylprednisolone, IVIG and steroid‑sparing therapy with clinical and radiologic improvement.
   - As swallowing recovers, repeat VFSS before reintroducing oral intake.

4. Avoid unnecessary antibiotics unless secondary bacterial pneumonia is suspected
   - Aspiration pneumonitis is often chemical and managed supportively; treat with antibiotics only when clinical, radiologic, or biomarker evidence suggests bacterial infection.

Practical monitoring and disposition
- Keep the patient in a setting where airway/respiratory status can be rapidly escalated.
- Reassess swallow function serially; remove enteral feeding only after a repeat VFSS shows safe swallow.
- Expect improvement in lung imaging and PFTs as muscle inflammation is treated — as happened in this patient, whose CT changes and PFTs improved over months.

Bottom line: In inflammatory myopathy with dysphagia, a restrictive/extrathoracic pattern on PFTs signals reduced respiratory reserve and airway protection, while VFSS documents the anatomic/functional risk of aspiration. Together they diagnose aspiration‑related lung injury and mandate immediate airway protection (NPO, enteral feeding), targeted respiratory monitoring/support, early swallow rehabilitation and prompt immunotherapy to reverse the process.
