### [Morning Report] Which elements of the 2017 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for idiopathic inflammatory myopathies support a diagnosis of dermatomyositis in a patient with elevated CK, compatible rash, MRI evidence of myositis, and characteristic biopsy features?

Think of the 2017 EULAR/ACR classification system as a weighted checklist: features are given points and the total score (with or without muscle biopsy) determines “probable” or “definite” idiopathic inflammatory myopathy and then subtypes (including dermatomyositis) are assigned based on patterns of findings.

In this patient the elements that carry the greatest weight in favour of dermatomyositis are the characteristic cutaneous features together with objective muscle disease and the biopsy pattern.

First, the cutaneous findings. The presence of a compatible dermatomyositis rash (facial violaceous erythema/heliotrope‑type change, shawl/V‑neck/Holster sign, periungual changes) is one of the most specific clinical inputs in the EULAR/ACR model for classifying dermatomyositis rather than other myopathies. In the scoring algorithm those pathognomonic rashes substantially increase the probability that the myopathy is dermatomyositis.

Second, objective muscle involvement. Markedly elevated creatine kinase documents active myofibre injury and is one of the items that adds score in the model. Imaging evidence of active myositis on MRI (muscle edema) is also an accepted objective marker of muscle involvement and contributes to the total score (and, in modern practice, increases confidence in the diagnosis when biopsy is concordant).

Third, the muscle biopsy. The 2017 criteria give particularly high discriminatory value to biopsy features typical of dermatomyositis — especially perifascicular atrophy/degeneration, perifascicular necrosis/regeneration and complement-mediated capillary injury (C5b‑9 deposition). Those histologic patterns distinguish dermatomyositis from immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis and, when present, push the classification to “definite” dermatomyositis even more readily than non‑specific inflammatory changes.

Finally, supportive but not required findings. The detection of a myositis‑specific autoantibody (here anti‑SAE1) is not a scored item in the original 2017 algorithm but is biologically and clinically supportive of a dermatomyositis phenotype (and helps with prognosis and cancer screening). MRI and PET evidence of multisite muscle inflammation and the clinical feature of dysphagia further strengthen the case clinically.

Putting it together: this patient has the combination of a compatible dermatomyositis rash + very high CK + MRI evidence of myositis + a muscle biopsy showing perifascicular myofibre necrosis/regeneration with capillary C5b‑9 deposition. Those elements, when entered into the 2017 EULAR/ACR scoring system, generate a score consistent with dermatomyositis (indeed “definite” DM when biopsy is included). Clinically, the biopsy pattern and rash are the most specific features that distinguish dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies; CK and MRI confirm muscle involvement and raise the score to meet classification thresholds.
