Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical phenotype is classically associated with anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, including typical cutaneous signs and its associations with dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and malignancy risk?

Anti‑SAE1 (small ubiquitin‑like modifier‑activating enzyme 1) dermatomyositis classically presents as a cutaneous‑predominant phenotype with frequent, but variably timed, muscle involvement and a propensity for dysphagia, interstitial lung disease (ILD) and an elevated—though not highest—malignancy risk.

Key clinical features and reasoning (grounded in the case):
- Cutaneous phenotype: patients typically have the canonical dermatomyositis rashes—heliotrope/periorbital violaceous erythema, Gottron’s papules, shawl/V‑neck (and Holster) signs and periungual changes/telangiectasias. The reported patient exemplified this with periungual erythema and a Holster sign even though classic heliotrope/Gottron’s were not prominent early.
- Muscle disease: SAE1 disease is often described as skin‑predominant with myositis that can be mild or develop later, but significant myonecrosis and very high CK (as in this patient: CK >16,000 U/L) and clinically important weakness (including bulbar/airway muscles) do occur and may require aggressive immunosuppression.
- Dysphagia: common in SAE1 dermatomyositis—about 46% in the large series cited in the case—and clinically important because of aspiration risk. The case had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue and penetration on swallow study and aspiration‑related consolidation on chest imaging.
- Interstitial lung disease: frequent (≈43% in the review cited) and may range from subclinical PFT abnormalities to radiographic ILD. In this patient PFTs showed a restrictive pattern and CT chest showed peribronchial consolidation (attributed mainly to aspiration), highlighting the need to evaluate respiratory involvement.
- Malignancy risk: intermediate to elevated (the literature review in the report found malignancy in ≈18% of SAE1 cases). Because dermatomyositis itself and features such as dysphagia increase cancer risk, SAE1 positivity contributes to the concern—hence the patient’s comprehensive malignancy screening per IMACS guidance (clinical review, imaging, tumour markers, planned endoscopies).

Practical points for learners:
- Consider SAE1 testing when dermatomyositis has prominent cutaneous findings with or without pronounced myositis, especially if dysphagia or respiratory symptoms are present.
- Recognize that subcutaneous/face or neck oedema can occur and may mimic angioedema or infection; lack of histaminergic/bradykinin features and concurrent skin/nail changes or muscle weakness should prompt CK and myositis antibody testing (as in this case).
- Perform baseline ILD assessment (history, PFTs, chest imaging) and age‑ and risk‑appropriate malignancy screening in SAE1 dermatomyositis—escalate screening when additional risk factors (dermatomyositis itself, dysphagia, male sex in this case) are present.
- Management often requires early, aggressive immunosuppression (glucocorticoids plus steroid‑sparing agents/IVIG/rituximab as indicated), and attention to aspiration risk from dysphagia.

Bottom line: anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis is classically a skin‑predominant dermatomyositis with frequent dysphagia (≈46%) and ILD (≈43%) and a modestly increased malignancy risk (≈18%); it can, however, present with severe myositis and unusual features such as neck/face oedema that mimic angioedema, so maintain a high index of suspicion and screen appropriately.
