Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Sepsis, DIC, or TMA? Untangling the Coagulopathy Web

## The Dog Bite That Led to Dialysis

We all know the drill with dog bites: clean the wound, maybe give some penicillin, and send the patient home. But what happens when a simple bite on the upper lip spirals into septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and eventually dialysis dependence? This case report from Fredriksen et al. presents a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who turned out to be a textbook example of how infection can unmask a hidden hematologic catastrophe.

The pathogen was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a gram-negative rod lurking in the oropharynx of dogs. While *C. canimorsus* is known for causing fulminant sepsis, particularly in asplenic patients, this patient was immunocompetent. What makes this case a must-read for the emergency and critical care clinician is the diagnostic whiplash. She presented with a classic sepsis picture that looked like DIC, only to develop a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) once the initial storm seemed to pass. It forces us to ask: when does sepsis-induced coagulopathy stop being just sepsis and become something requiring complement blockade?

## The Clinical Timeline: When Sepsis Isn’t the Whole Story

The patient arrived with fever, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and coagulopathy. Her initial workup showed metabolic acidosis, hypoglycemia, and mottled extremities. The clinical team rightly suspected *C. canimorsus* early on and started broad-spectrum antibiotics. She was transferred to the ICU, managed for septic shock, and initially showed improvement. By day 8, she was stable enough to move to the nephrology ward, though she remained anuric and dialysis-dependent.

Here is where the case pivots. Despite the sepsis resolving and inflammatory markers improving, her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without bleeding. Her platelets stayed stubbornly low, and LDH skyrocketed. A peripheral smear revealed 15–20% schistocytes. This was the "red flag" that the initial diagnosis of sepsis/DIC was incomplete. The hemolysis was microangiopathic, suggesting a TMA process that had been obscured by the initial inflammatory storm.

This timeline is critical. In pure DIC, coagulation parameters usually normalize as the infection clears. In this patient, the coagulopathy resolved, but the hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persisted and worsened. This divergence suggests a second mechanism at play—in this case, complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) triggered by the infection.

## Differentiating DIC from TMA in the ICU

Distinguishing between sepsis-induced coagulopathy and TMA is one of the trickier challenges in the ICU. Both present with thrombocytopenia and renal failure. Both can show schistocytes on a smear, though they are often more abundant in TMA. The confusion is compounded by the fact that severe sepsis itself can trigger a TMA-like picture.

In this case, the team had to systematically rule out the other big players in the TMA differential. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was unlikely given an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5% (TTP is typically <10%). Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS) was ruled out via negative stool PCR. The diagnosis ultimately rested on the exclusion of these causes, the presence of MAHA, and a kidney biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and acute tubular necrosis consistent with TMA.

A major pitfall in diagnosing CM-HUS is relying too heavily on standard complement levels. In this patient, C3 and C4 were within normal limits. This is a crucial teaching point: normal complement levels do not exclude CM-HUS. The alternative pathway activity was actually reduced, but this was likely due to treatment effects rather than baseline depletion. The diagnosis of CM-HUS remains a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion, heavily reliant on recognizing the clinical phenotype when standard sepsis treatment fails to halt the hemolysis.

## The Therapeutic Dilemma: Complement Blockade in Infection

Once CM-HUS was suspected, the treatment decision became complex. The standard of care for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits the terminal complement component C5. However, giving a drug that blocks the complement system during an active bacterial infection is risky. Terminal complement is essential for killing encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria* and, ironically, *Capnocytophaga*.

The team navigated this by ensuring blood cultures were negative before starting eculizumab. They also administered meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero) and provided antibiotic prophylaxis. While plasma exchange (PEX) was used as a bridge, the patient’s sustained hematological and renal recovery only occurred after complement blockade was initiated. This highlights that while PEX is lifesaving in TTP, it offers only temporary benefit in CM-HUS. The key to renal recovery in CM-HUS appears to be the early initiation of eculizumab, even if it requires careful management of infection risks.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Watch the Timeline:** If a patient with sepsis and DIC shows persistent or worsening hemolysis and thrombocytopenia after the infection is controlled, suspect a superimposed TMA.
*   **Normal Complement Doesn’t Rule Out CM-HUS:** Standard C3 and C4 levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course; do not dismiss CM-HUS based on normal complement screening alone.
*   **Schistocytes Are a Clue, Not a Diagnosis:** While schistocytes can occur in DIC, a high percentage (e.g., >15–20%) combined with persistent thrombocytopenia and renal failure warrants a TMA workup.
*   **Rule Out the Big Two:** Always check ADAMTS13 activity to exclude TTP and stool PCR to exclude STEC-HUS before diagnosing CM-HUS.
*   **Infection Risk with Eculizumab:** When treating CM-HUS triggered by infection, ensure the infection is cleared and provide appropriate vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis before initiating complement blockade.
*   **Zoonotic Triggers:** Consider *Capnocytophaga* in any septic patient with a history of animal bites, even if they are immunocompetent, as it can trigger rare complications like CM-HUS.
