Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Normal Complement Levels Do Not Rule Out HUS: The CM-HUS Diagnostic Trap

Dog bites are a common presentation in the emergency department, often dismissed as minor soft tissue injuries requiring tetanus prophylaxis and perhaps a course of antibiotics. But what happens when a routine dog bite spirals into fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and dialysis-dependent kidney failure? Even more surprisingly, what happens when the patient stabilizes from sepsis, only to crash again with a new hematologic catastrophe? This case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection triggering complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) highlights a critical diagnostic pitfall that can leave clinicians staring at normal lab values while a patient’s kidneys shut down.

## The Clinical Course: From Sepsis to TMA

Our patient was a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented four days after sustaining a dog bite to her upper lip. She had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin but failed to fill the prescription. Her presentation was dramatic, featuring high fever, malaise, and numbness in her feet. On arrival, she was septic with mottled extremities, severe hypoglycemia, and metabolic acidosis. The initial workup revealed pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and coagulopathy consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

Blood cultures eventually confirmed *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod found in dog oropharyngeal flora. She was treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics and transferred to the ICU. Initially, the clinical picture seemed dominated by sepsis and DIC. However, the true complexity of her case emerged after the septic phase began to resolve. Despite improving inflammatory markers and recovering platelets, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. This was accompanied by undetectable haptoglobin and rising lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), signaling active hemolysis. A peripheral smear showed 15% to 20% schistocytes, raising the alarm for a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

## The Diagnostic Maze and the Complement Trap

When you see microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and AKI, your differential diagnosis narrows quickly to thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC-HUS), and complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). In this case, TTP was effectively ruled out with an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%, well above the <10% threshold required for diagnosis. STEC-HUS was excluded via negative stool PCR and the absence of prodromal enteric illness.

This left CM-HUS as the primary suspect, but here is where the diagnostic trap springs shut. Standard complement testing, specifically C3 and C4 levels, was within normal limits. In many clinical algorithms, normal complement levels are used to rule out atypical HUS, leading clinicians to stop the investigation prematurely. However, as this case demonstrates, normal C3/C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS. Complement levels can remain within reference ranges during the disease course, often because they are acute phase reactants that rise in response to inflammation, masking the consumption occurring at the endothelial level.

Further complicating the picture, the patient initially had a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), suggesting autoimmune hemolytic anemia. This was likely a false positive due to penicillin exposure or prior transfusions, as repeat testing was negative. The definitive diagnosis was secured through a renal biopsy, which revealed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation, consistent with TMA. Extended complement testing later showed reduced alternative pathway activity, but this was performed after treatment had begun, reinforcing that functional assays can be tricky to interpret in the acute setting.

## Management Challenges and Therapeutic Strategy

Treating CM-HUS in the context of active infection presents a unique therapeutic dilemma. The standard of care for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal complement component C5. While this halts the complement-mediated endothelial damage, it also impairs the host defense against encapsulated bacteria, including *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. In this patient, eculizumab was initiated only after blood cultures became negative to minimize the risk of uncontrolled infection.

Plasma exchange (PEX) was used as a bridging therapy. While PEX is life-saving in TTP, it offers only temporary benefit in CM-HUS by removing circulating inflammatory mediators without correcting the underlying complement dysfunction. The patient’s spontaneous diuresis returned after PEX, but sustained hematological and renal recovery occurred only after the initiation of complement blockade. Crucially, meningococcal vaccination (Menveo and Bexsero) and antibiotic prophylaxis were administered before starting eculizumab to mitigate the risk of opportunistic infections. The patient achieved hematological remission and partial renal recovery, though she remains on maintenance eculizumab with ongoing monitoring for relapse.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Normal C3/C4 Does Not Rule Out CM-HUS:** Complement levels can remain within reference ranges during the disease course due to their nature as acute phase reactants; do not rely on standard panels to exclude the diagnosis.
*   **Infection Can Trigger CM-HUS:** Even in immunocompetent adults without known genetic complement defects, severe infections like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* can act as the "second hit" required to trigger complement-mediated TMA.
*   **Biopsy Confirms TMA:** When laboratory markers are equivocal, a renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and glomerular ischemia is critical for confirming TMA and guiding therapy.
*   **Rule Out Mimics First:** Always exclude TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC-HUS (stool PCR) before diagnosing CM-HUS, as management differs significantly.
*   **Vaccinate Before Blocking:** If eculizumab is required in the setting of recent infection, ensure meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are in place to prevent life-threatening encapsulated bacterial infections.
