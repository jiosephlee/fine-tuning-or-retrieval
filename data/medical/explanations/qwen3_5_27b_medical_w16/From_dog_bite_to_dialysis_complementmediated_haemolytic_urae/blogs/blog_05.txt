Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Refractory Hypoglycemia in Sepsis: A Red Flag for Capnocytophaga

## The Bite That Went Wrong

Dog bites are a common complaint in the emergency department. Most are minor, requiring local wound care and perhaps a tetanus booster. But every now and then, a bite signals the start of a fulminant, life-threatening cascade. A recent case report highlights a healthy woman in her late 40s who presented four days after a dog bite to her upper lip. What started as a missed prescription for prophylactic penicillin evolved into septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and eventually a rare form of kidney failure.

What makes this case particularly instructive for the emergency clinician is a specific metabolic derangement that appeared early in her course: severe, refractory hypoglycemia. While sepsis-associated hypoglycemia is not unheard of, the severity and resistance to treatment in this patient served as a critical warning sign for a specific, fastidious pathogen lurking in the dog’s mouth.

## The Clinical Course

The patient arrived at the emergency department with a rapidly worsening condition. She had a high fever exceeding 40 °C, malaise, and reduced oral intake. Physical examination revealed mottled and cool lower extremities, suggesting poor perfusion. Her initial laboratory workup showed marked systemic derangement, including severe inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy.

The metabolic picture was alarming. Blood gas analysis revealed metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 and an elevated lactate of 5.7 mmol/L. More concerning was the blood glucose. She was found to have hypoglycemia that was refractory to oral glucose and glucagon, with a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L. This profound hypoglycemia, occurring in the context of sepsis, prompted urgent transfer to the hospital.

Given the constellation of fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and AKI, the initial working diagnosis was DIC secondary to bacteraemia. The medical team suspected *Capnocytophaga* species from the onset due to the history of the dog bite. Broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was started immediately to cover dog-associated pathogens. Seven days later, blood cultures confirmed *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia.

## The Pathogen: Capnocytophaga canimorsus

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod found in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. While human infections are rare, transmission can occur via bites, scratches, or even close contact. The clinical presentation is highly variable, ranging from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis and multiorgan failure.

Despite broad antibiotic susceptibility, mortality can approach 30%. Diagnosis is often delayed because the organism is fastidious and grows slowly in cultures. This delay can be critical, as seen in our patient, who did not initiate the prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin prophylaxis on the day of the bite.

In this case, the infection presented with septic shock, severe leukopaenia, and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. However, the clinical picture did not stabilize after antibiotic initiation. The patient became completely anuric with rapidly worsening renal function, requiring continuous renal replacement therapy.

## The Twist: Infection-Triggered HUS

The diagnostic complexity deepened after the initial sepsis phase. On day 3 in the ICU, the patient developed diffuse skin bleeding and distal cyanotic changes. After eight days of hospitalisation, she was transferred to the nephrology ward. While inflammatory markers improved and coagulation parameters normalized, she developed a sudden isolated drop in haemoglobin without evidence of bleeding.

Platelets remained persistently low, haptoglobin became undetectable, and LDH levels rose, indicating ongoing haemolysis. A peripheral blood smear revealed 15%–20% schistocytes, consistent with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). The differential diagnosis included thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*-associated HUS (STEC-HUS).

ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP, and stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative. A kidney biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis and ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The final diagnosis was complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS).

This highlights a crucial teaching point: CM-HUS may be obscured during the early sepsis and DIC phase. In this patient, haemolysis appeared only after sepsis began improving, suggesting infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a pre-existing disease. Notably, basic complement tests (C3/C4) were within normal limits, which does not exclude CM-HUS, as levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course.

## Management and Outcomes

Management of CM-HUS in the context of active infection presents unique challenges. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component 5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS. However, using complement blockade during active bacteraemia raises concerns, as terminal complement components are critical for defence against encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*.

In this case, eculizumab was initiated after blood cultures became negative to minimize the risk of uncontrolled infection. The patient received meningococcal vaccines and prophylactic antibiotics alongside the complement inhibitor. Plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy prior to the first dose of eculizumab.

Following the therapeutic intervention, the patient achieved haematological remission. Her kidney function partially recovered, though she remains dialysis-independent with reduced renal function (serum creatine 175 µmol/L). She continues maintenance eculizumab therapy with planned reassessment. Extended complement genetic testing did not identify any pathogenic variants, supporting the diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS without a demonstrable inherited complement predisposition.

## Clinical Pearls

This case underscores the importance of recognizing specific metabolic red flags in sepsis and maintaining a high index of suspicion for zoonotic pathogens. The refractory hypoglycemia was an early marker of severity that should have triggered immediate broad-spectrum coverage. Furthermore, clinicians must remain vigilant for superimposed thrombotic microangiopathies in patients recovering from severe sepsis, even if initial complement levels appear normal.

*   **Refractory hypoglycemia in sepsis:** Severe, refractory hypoglycemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L) is a specific and often overlooked marker of severe *Capnocytophaga* sepsis and should prompt immediate broad-spectrum coverage in bite wound patients.
*   **Capnocytophaga canimorsus:** This gram-negative rod is found in dog and cat oropharyngeal flora. Infections are rare but carry a mortality rate of up to 30%. Diagnosis can be delayed due to slow growth in cultures.
*   **TMA after Sepsis:** Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be triggered by infection. It may be obscured during the early sepsis and DIC phase, presenting later with MAHA and thrombocytopenia.
*   **Normal Complement Levels:** Normal C3/C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS. Diagnosis is clinicopathological and requires exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC-HUS.
*   **Eculizumab Safety:** When using complement blockade, ensure active bacteraemia is treated and cultures are negative to reduce the risk of overwhelming infection with encapsulated bacteria. Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are essential.
