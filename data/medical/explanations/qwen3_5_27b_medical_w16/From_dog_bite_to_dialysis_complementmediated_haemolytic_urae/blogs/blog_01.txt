Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# The Dog Bite That Almost Killed: Recognizing Capnocytophaga Sepsis

We all know dog bites are common in the ED. Most are minor, requiring a quick wash, a tetanus shot, and maybe some oral antibiotics. But occasionally, a bite delivers a pathogen that turns a routine visit into a fight for life. A recent case report highlights a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented four days after a dog bite to her lip. She didn’t fill her penicillin prescription. By the time she hit our doors, she was in septic shock with severe hypoglycaemia, DIC, and acute kidney injury. She ended up on dialysis. This isn’t just a story about a bite; it’s a lesson on *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a rare but fulminant zoonosis that demands immediate recognition.

## The Pathogen: Capnocytophaga canimorsus

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod found in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. While human infections are rare, they are notorious for their severity. Transmission occurs via bites, scratches, or even close contact with saliva. The clinical spectrum is wide, ranging from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and multiorgan failure. Despite being susceptible to broad antibiotics, mortality can approach 30%.

Diagnosis is often delayed because the organism is fastidious and grows slowly in culture. In the case we’re discussing, *C. canimorsus* was suspected early due to the dog bite history, but confirmation took seven days via aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. This delay underscores the need for high clinical suspicion and the increasing utility of molecular methods like real-time nanopore sequencing when standard cultures lag.

## The Clinical Picture: Sepsis and the Red Flags

Our patient presented with a constellation of symptoms that should raise immediate alarm bells. Four days post-bite, she developed a high fever over 40°C, malaise, and numbness with discolouration of her feet. In the emergency department, she was septic with mottled, cool lower extremities.

One of the most striking features in this case was severe, refractory hypoglycaemia. Her glucose nadir was 0.6 mmol/L, which was resistant to oral glucose and glucagon. While hypoglycaemia can occur in severe sepsis, profound hypoglycaemia in the context of a dog bite is a specific red flag for *Capnocytophaga* infection. Her initial labs showed marked systemic derangement: severe inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy.

The initial interpretation was DIC secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis. Surgical causes like necrotising fasciitis were ruled out. Broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was started immediately to cover dog-associated pathogens. This is a critical teaching point: empiric coverage for bite wounds must include penicillin or piperacillin/tazobactam to target *Capnocytophaga*. In this case, the patient had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin initially but was unable to collect it, leaving her unprotected during the critical window.

## The Twist: When Sepsis Turns to HUS

Here is where the case gets complex. After transfer to the ICU, the patient became anuric and required continuous renal replacement therapy. She received supportive care and antibiotics, and initially, her inflammatory profile began to improve. However, on the nephrology ward, the clinical picture shifted again.

She developed a sudden, isolated drop in haemoglobin without bleeding, alongside persistent thrombocytopenia and rising LDH. A peripheral blood smear revealed schistocytes, consistent with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). This suggested a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The differential diagnosis for TMA in this setting is broad, including Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC-HUS).

TTP was ruled out as ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (TTP typically presents below 10%). STEC-HUS was excluded via negative stool PCR. The diagnosis landed on complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). This is a rare complication where the infection triggers dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, leading to endothelial damage. It is worth noting that her basic complement levels (C3/C4) were within normal limits. This is a crucial pearl: normal complement levels do not exclude CM-HUS, as they may remain in reference ranges during the disease course.

## Management and The Eculizumab Decision

Managing CM-HUS in the context of active or recent infection presents a therapeutic dilemma. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component 5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS and improves outcomes. However, terminal complement components are critical for defence against encapsulated bacteria, including *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*.

In this patient, eculizumab was initiated only after blood cultures became negative to minimize the risk of uncontrolled infection. Before starting the drug, she received meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero) and prophylactic antibiotics. Plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy, which provided transient stabilisation, but sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade.

The patient achieved haematological remission and partial renal recovery, though she remains on maintenance eculizumab. Extended genetic testing did not identify pathogenic variants, supporting a diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a hereditary complement defect.

## Take-Home Pearls

*   **Don't ignore the bite:** In any patient presenting with severe sepsis and a history of animal contact, especially dogs or cats, consider *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.
*   **Hypoglycaemia is a clue:** Profound, refractory hypoglycaemia in septic patients with animal exposure is a specific warning sign for this pathogen.
*   **Cover early:** Ensure empiric antibiotic coverage for bite wounds includes penicillin or piperacillin/tazobactam. Non-adherence to prophylaxis can be fatal.
*   **Watch for the second hit:** Even after sepsis appears controlled, monitor for delayed complications like TMA. New onset haemolysis or worsening renal function warrants investigation for HUS.
*   **Normal complement doesn't rule out HUS:** In suspected CM-HUS, do not be reassured by normal C3/C4 levels; they may not reflect the underlying dysregulation.
*   **Vaccinate before blocking:** If eculizumab is required, ensure meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are in place to prevent overwhelming infection.
