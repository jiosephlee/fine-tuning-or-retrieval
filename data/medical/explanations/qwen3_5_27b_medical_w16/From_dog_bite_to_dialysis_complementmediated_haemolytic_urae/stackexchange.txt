Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] A previously healthy adult presents with fulminant sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) four days after a dog bite. Which fastidious Gram-negative rod is the most likely pathogen, and what is a key diagnostic challenge associated with its identification in standard blood cultures?

The most likely pathogen is *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious, encapsulated Gram-negative rod that resides in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. In this case, the patient’s presentation of fulminant sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) four days after a dog bite is highly characteristic of invasive *Capnocytophaga* infection, which can progress rapidly to multiorgan failure even in immunocompetent adults.

The key diagnostic challenge is the organism’s slow growth in standard culture media, which frequently delays identification. As illustrated in this case, although *C. canimorsus* was suspected early based on the clinical history, confirmation by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures took seven days. This delay highlights the limitation of relying solely on standard microbiological workflows for this fastidious pathogen. Consequently, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with sepsis following animal exposure, even if initial cultures are negative. The case report notes that this diagnostic lag is prompting increasing use of molecular methods, such as real-time nanopore sequencing, to expedite identification. Early empiric coverage with penicillin-class antibiotics is critical, as mortality can approach 30% if treatment is delayed.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient presenting with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and thrombocytopenia, what specific ADAMTS13 activity level is required to effectively rule out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and guide further workup for other thrombotic microangiopathies?

In a patient presenting with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and thrombocytopenia, an ADAMTS13 activity level greater than 10% is required to effectively rule out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). As highlighted in this case, TTP is defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, typically presenting with activity below 10%. Our patient’s ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, which effectively excluded TTP and guided the diagnostic workup toward other thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs), specifically complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS).

The diagnostic priority in adults with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury is to exclude TTP, given its high mortality and requirement for urgent plasma exchange. In this case, despite the confounding presence of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation, the persistence of hemolysis and thrombocytopenia necessitated TMA evaluation. The ADAMTS13 result of 43.5% was the critical discriminator; it confirmed the absence of severe deficiency, allowing the team to rule out TTP and investigate alternative causes such as Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC) or complement dysregulation. Following the exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS, the diagnosis of CM-HUS was established based on renal biopsy findings and clinical response to eculizumab. Thus, an ADAMTS13 activity above 10% serves as the threshold to redirect management from TTP protocols to other TMA etiologies.

### [Clinical Pearls] In the context of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), why might standard complement assays (C3/C4) be misleading, and what does a normal result imply regarding the exclusion of this diagnosis?

In the context of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), standard complement assays such as C3 and C4 are often misleading because levels may remain within normal reference ranges during the active disease course. Therefore, a normal result does not exclude the diagnosis.

This case underscores that CM-HUS is fundamentally a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion. In this patient, despite the presence of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) confirmed by renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and acute tubular necrosis, locally available standard complement tests (C3/C4) and functional assays of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were initially within normal limits. The authors explicitly state that complement levels may be normal during the disease course, and relying on them alone risks missing the diagnosis.

The pathophysiology involves dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, often triggered by infection. While extended complement testing performed later in this patient’s hospitalization showed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50), the report attributes this reduction to treatment effects from plasma exchange and eculizumab rather than intrinsic disease activity at that time. This further highlights the diagnostic complexity.

Clinically, the diagnosis should be pursued based on the constellation of persistent haemolysis, schistocytosis, and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury after excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (ADAMTS13 activity >10%) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (negative stool PCR). As demonstrated by this patient’s rapid haematological remission following eculizumab initiation, early recognition and treatment are critical, even in the setting of normal initial complement levels.

### [Pathophysiology] Pathophysiologically, how does an acute infection like Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia trigger complement-mediated HUS in an immunocompetent host without a pre-existing genetic complement defect?

**Answer:**

In this case, the acute *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection acted as the primary environmental trigger for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) by inducing dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, even in the absence of a detectable genetic defect.

Pathophysiologically, *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated, gram-negative rod that inherently stimulates the complement system as a critical component of host defense. While complement activation is necessary to clear the pathogen, this case illustrates a clinical paradox: in certain hosts, this immune response becomes uncontrolled. The resulting complement overactivity leads to endothelial injury, microthrombi formation, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Although 50% to 70% of CM-HUS patients harbour genetic variants in complement regulatory proteins (such as factors H, I, or B), this patient’s extended genetic testing identified no pathogenic variants. This supports the concept that severe infection can overwhelm regulatory mechanisms alone, serving as a sufficient "second hit" or even primary precipitant for TMA in phenotypically normal individuals.

Several features in this case support infection-triggered CM-HUS without hereditary predisposition:
1.  **Timing:** Haemolysis and worsening thrombocytopenia developed *after* the initial septic phase began to improve, suggesting the TMA was a distinct complement-mediated process unmasked once the acute inflammatory storm of sepsis subsided.
2.  **Complement Levels:** Standard complement levels (C3/C4) were within normal limits initially. It is crucial to recognize that normal levels do not exclude CM-HUS, as consumption may not be reflected in static levels during the disease course.
3.  **Therapeutic Response:** The patient achieved sustained haematological remission only after the initiation of eculizumab, a terminal complement inhibitor, confirming the central role of complement dysregulation in her pathology.

The teaching point is that clinicians must consider CM-HUS in patients presenting with TMA following severe infections, even without a family history or known genetic complement defect. Early recognition and complement blockade are essential to prevent irreversible renal injury.

### [Management] Before initiating eculizumab therapy for CM-HUS, what specific prophylactic measures are mandatory to mitigate the risk of life-threatening opportunistic infections associated with terminal complement blockade?

Before initiating eculizumab therapy for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), mandatory prophylactic measures include vaccination against *Neisseria meningitidis* and antimicrobial prophylaxis.

Eculizumab blocks the terminal complement component C5, which is critical for host defense against encapsulated bacteria. Consequently, blockade significantly increases the risk of life-threatening invasive meningococcal disease. In this case, the patient received first doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B)—prior to eculizumab induction. However, because protective immunity typically requires two weeks to develop, and the vaccines were not administered at least two weeks before starting complement blockade, the patient was concurrently given prophylactic penicillin (Apocillin) to bridge this gap.

This case underscores that vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis are essential prerequisites for safe complement inhibition. While eculizumab is the standard of care for CM-HUS and markedly improves renal outcomes, it must be balanced against the risk of opportunistic infections. In this patient, eculizumab was initiated only after blood cultures became negative, further mitigating the risk of uncontrolled infection during the period of complement blockade.

### [Management] How does the therapeutic role of plasma exchange differ between TTP and CM-HUS, and why is it often considered a bridging therapy rather than definitive treatment in the latter?

**Answer:**

The therapeutic role of plasma exchange (PEX) differs fundamentally between thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). In TTP, PEX is the definitive, lifesaving treatment because it replaces the deficient ADAMTS13 enzyme. In CM-HUS, however, PEX serves only as a bridging therapy; it provides temporary stabilization but does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation.

In our patient, the distinction was critical. While TTP was a primary differential given the microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and thrombocytopenia, her ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP, which typically presents with activity below 10%. Consequently, the pathophysiology driving her disease was dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, likely triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia.

PEX was initiated in this case while awaiting confirmatory testing and the first dose of eculizumab. The text notes that PEX provides only temporary benefit in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators, without correcting the intrinsic complement system dysfunction. Consistent with this, our patient experienced a return of spontaneous diuresis following plasma exchange sessions, reflecting transient stabilisation. However, sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after the initiation of eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component 5.

Therefore, PEX is considered bridging therapy in CM-HUS because it cannot prevent the ongoing endothelial damage caused by uncontrolled complement activation. Definitive management requires complement blockade. Furthermore, as highlighted in this case, initiating eculizumab requires caution during active infection, as terminal complement components are vital for defence against encapsulated bacteria like *Capnocytophaga*. In this patient, eculizumab was started only after blood cultures became negative and vaccination was administered, illustrating why PEX is often employed to maintain stability until it is safe to begin definitive complement inhibition.

### [Morning Report] In this case, the patient developed isolated hemolysis and worsening renal function after initial clinical improvement from sepsis. What does this temporal relationship suggest about the etiology of the thrombotic microangiopathy compared to primary sepsis-induced coagulopathy?

The temporal dissociation between resolving sepsis and worsening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) features is the critical diagnostic clue in this case. In primary sepsis-induced disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), the coagulopathy and organ injury typically parallel the infectious burden; as antibiotics control the infection and inflammatory markers decline, coagulation parameters and renal function should stabilize or improve.

In this patient, however, the clinical course diverged. While the inflammatory profile improved and coagulation parameters normalized—indicating resolving DIC—the patient developed sudden, isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening renal failure requiring dialysis. This progression despite clinical improvement from sepsis suggests a distinct pathophysiological mechanism superimposed on the infection rather than a direct continuation of septic coagulopathy.

The case report highlights that the hemolysis appeared only after sepsis began improving, supporting a diagnosis of infection-triggered complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). In this mechanism, the bacterial infection acts as an external trigger that activates the alternative complement pathway, leading to endothelial injury and TMA independent of the initial consumptive coagulopathy. The subsequent rapid haematological remission following eculizumab therapy, a complement inhibitor, further confirms that the etiology was complement-mediated rather than sepsis-driven. Clinically, this underscores the necessity of monitoring for TMA even after sepsis control is achieved, particularly when renal function or hemoglobin levels deteriorate despite resolving inflammatory markers.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the most critical determinant of renal prognosis in patients with CM-HUS, and how does delayed initiation of eculizumab therapy impact long-term outcomes?

The most critical determinant of renal prognosis in patients with complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) is the time to initiation of complement blockade with eculizumab. As explicitly stated in the conclusion of this case, renal function and the time-to-initiation of eculizumab therapy are the main determinants of prognosis.

Delayed initiation of eculizumab is associated with incomplete renal recovery and a consequently higher risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease. In this case, the patient presented with a diagnostic challenge where the TMA was obscured by an initial phase of severe sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation. Although plasma exchange provided transient stabilisation and returned spontaneous diuresis, sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after the initiation of eculizumab. Despite achieving haematological remission, the patient’s kidney function only partially recovered, with a serum creatinine remaining at 175 µmol/L. This outcome underscores the principle that while complement blockade is the standard of care, the extent of renal injury sustained prior to treatment—often exacerbated by diagnostic delays—limits the potential for full recovery. Therefore, early recognition of TMA, particularly when haemolysis persists after sepsis resolves, and prompt initiation of eculizumab are the most important predictors of favourable long-term renal outcomes.
