Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis of TMA

The diagnostic challenge in this case centered on distinguishing complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) from other causes of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) amidst a background of severe sepsis. The patient initially presented with septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), characterized by pancytopenia and coagulopathy. However, the clinical picture evolved after the initial septic phase, marked by a sudden isolated drop in haemoglobin, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI). This constellation of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and AKI constitutes the classic TMA triad. Recognizing this shift was critical, as the management of TMA differs fundamentally from supportive care for sepsis-induced organ dysfunction. In this patient, the emergence of schistocytes on peripheral blood smear and undetectable haptoglobin confirmed MAHA, necessitating a rigorous workup to identify the specific aetiology of the TMA to guide targeted therapy.

The first priority in the differential diagnosis of TMA in adults is excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), a condition requiring urgent plasma exchange. In this case, the clinical team utilized the PLASMIC score to assess the pretest probability of TTP while awaiting confirmatory testing. The patient’s score was 4/7, indicating a low probability of TTP. This was corroborated by ADAMTS13 activity levels, which returned at 43.5%. Since TTP is defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, typically presenting with activity below 10%, this result effectively ruled out TTP. This diagnostic distinction was vital because, while plasma exchange is the treatment of choice for TTP, it offers only temporary benefit in CM-HUS. The exclusion of TTP allowed the clinical team to pivot toward investigating other causes of TMA, specifically focusing on complement dysregulation.

Following the exclusion of TTP, the team evaluated for Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*-associated HUS (STEC-HUS), the most common cause of HUS in children but less frequent in adults. STEC-HUS is typically preceded by a prodromal diarrheal illness, which was absent in this patient’s history. Furthermore, stool polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis for Shiga toxin was negative. The combination of negative microbiological testing and the lack of enteric symptoms argued strongly against STEC-HUS. This step was essential to narrow the differential diagnosis toward non-infectious or infection-triggered complement-mediated processes, particularly given the patient’s recent history of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia.

Interpreting complement levels in the context of active TMA requires careful consideration, as standard laboratory markers can be misleading. In this case, initial standard complement tests (C3 and C4) were within normal limits. A common pitfall is assuming normal C3/C4 levels exclude CM-HUS; however, complement levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course. Functional assays of the complement pathways were also initially reported as normal. Extended complement testing performed later showed a markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50), but this was likely a reflection of the treatment effects from plasma exchange and eculizumab rather than intrinsic complement depletion at the time of onset. Additionally, genetic testing did not identify pathogenic variants in complement regulatory genes. Despite the absence of a demonstrable genetic defect, the clinical presentation and response to therapy supported a diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS, illustrating that normal baseline complement levels do not preclude the diagnosis.

The role of renal biopsy in this case was pivotal for confirming the diagnosis and excluding other glomerulopathies. Due to the risk of bleeding associated with thrombocytopenia, the biopsy was delayed until the platelet count increased to 77×10⁹/L. Histological examination revealed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation, all consistent with TMA. Crucially, immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy demonstrated no evidence of primary glomerulopathy, which helped exclude immune-mediated vasculitis or glomerulonephritis. These findings, combined with the exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS, solidified the diagnosis of CM-HUS. The biopsy served as the definitive pathological anchor in a complex clinical scenario where laboratory markers alone were insufficient to distinguish between sepsis-related injury and primary microangiopathy.
