Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Sepsis Management

The clinical trajectory of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection often begins with a distinct exposure history, typically involving a dog bite, scratch, or close contact with saliva. In this case, a previously healthy woman in her late 40s presented four days after sustaining a bite to the upper lip. While *C. canimorsus* is a commensal organism in the oropharynx of dogs and cats, invasive human infection is rare but carries a high mortality rate, approaching 30%, particularly when diagnosis is delayed. The organism is a fastidious, gram-negative rod that can be difficult to culture, often leading to diagnostic delays. Crucially, this patient had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin for prophylaxis but failed to initiate treatment, leaving her unprotected against this pathogen. Clinicians managing dog bite wounds must recognize that while most infections are localized, fulminant sepsis can develop rapidly in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent hosts, necessitating a high index of suspicion when systemic symptoms arise shortly after exposure.

Upon admission, the patient exhibited signs of fulminant sepsis and early septic shock, characterized by a high fever exceeding 40 °C, malaise, and profound metabolic derangement. A notable and severe feature of her presentation was refractory hypoglycemia, with a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L, which persisted despite aggressive intravenous glucose and glucagon administration. This hypoglycemia, alongside a lactic acidosis (lactate 5.7 mmol/L) and metabolic acidosis (pH 7.27), reflected severe systemic inflammation and hepatic dysfunction. Physical examination revealed mottled and cool lower extremities with numbness and discolouration, suggestive of peripheral perfusion failure and microvascular thrombosis. Laboratory evaluation confirmed a systemic inflammatory response with pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and coagulopathy. These findings initially aligned with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia, a common complication of severe *Capnocytophaga* sepsis.

Differentiating sepsis-induced DIC from primary thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) presents a significant diagnostic challenge in the acute setting, as both conditions share features such as thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), and organ failure. In this case, the initial clinical picture was dominated by sepsis-related multiorgan involvement, which obscured the underlying TMA. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was initially considered unlikely due to the presence of leukopenia, significant inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocytes. To rule out other causes of pancytopenia and coagulopathy, such as acute leukaemia or haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, flow cytometry and a planned bone marrow biopsy were performed; however, normal results supported a sepsis-driven etiology. This diagnostic phase highlights the importance of recognizing that TMA can be masked during the early sepsis and DIC phase, often only becoming apparent once the initial infectious storm begins to subside.

Immediate management focused on stabilizing the patient and addressing the suspected bacterial source. Given the severity of the presentation and the specific exposure history, broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was initiated to cover dog-associated pathogens, including *Capnocytophaga* species, which are susceptible to beta-lactams. Although *C. canimorsus* was not confirmed until seven days later via blood cultures, the clinical suspicion justified this empiric coverage. Supportive care was equally critical; the patient received thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions to address coagulopathy and underwent aggressive fluid resuscitation. Due to the rapid progression to anuria and fluid overload, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was required to manage septic cardiomyopathy and metabolic derangements. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for close monitoring, where she remained hemodynamically stable without the need for vasopressors beyond stress-dose corticosteroids.

Despite initial stabilization, the clinical course provided warning signs of progression to multiorgan failure that exceeded typical sepsis recovery patterns. The patient developed complete anuria with worsening renal function that did not respond to loop diuretics, necessitating ongoing dialysis. Furthermore, diffuse skin bleeding, ecchymoses, and distal ischaemic changes in the toes and fingers suggested ongoing microthrombotic phenomena consistent with DIC. While coagulation parameters eventually normalized and inflammatory markers improved, the persistence of renal failure and the subsequent development of isolated haemolysis indicated that the initial diagnosis of sepsis-induced DIC was incomplete. This evolution underscores the necessity of continuous reassessment in septic patients, particularly when renal recovery lags behind hematological improvement, as this discrepancy may signal a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy requiring distinct therapeutic intervention.
