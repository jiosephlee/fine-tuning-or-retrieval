Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Strategies and Management of CM-HUS

The management of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) requires rapid differentiation between entities to guide therapy, as the treatment for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) differs significantly from complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). In this case, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated as a bridging therapy while awaiting confirmatory ADAMTS13 activity and complement studies. PEX remains the first-line treatment for TTP but offers only temporary benefit in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators without correcting the underlying dysregulation. As observed in this patient, PEX sessions led to a return of spontaneous diuresis, indicating transient stabilisation. However, sustained haematological remission and renal recovery were only achieved following the initiation of complement blockade, highlighting that PEX should be viewed as a temporising measure in CM-HUS rather than a definitive cure.

Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the terminal complement component C5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS and is critical for halting the endothelial damage driven by uncontrolled complement activation. The timing of initiation is a key determinant of prognosis, with delayed treatment associated with incomplete renal recovery. In this case, the decision to start eculizumab was balanced against the presence of active *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. Because terminal complement components are essential for host defence against encapsulated bacteria, initiating complement blockade during active infection carries a risk of uncontrolled sepsis. Consequently, eculizumab was withheld until blood cultures were negative, ensuring the infection was controlled before immunosuppression. Once cleared, an induction regimen of 900 mg intravenous weekly for four weeks was administered, leading to haematological remission and partial renal function recovery.

The use of complement inhibitors necessitates rigorous prophylactic measures to mitigate the risk of invasive meningococcal and other encapsulated bacterial infections. Vaccination against *Neisseria meningitidis* is mandatory prior to therapy. In this patient, both Menveo (serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B) were administered before the first dose of eculizumab. However, because the vaccines were not administered at least two weeks prior to starting complement blockade, the patient required antibiotic prophylaxis to cover the gap in immunity. Intravenous ampicillin was de-escalated to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) for this purpose, aligning with the pathogen’s susceptibility profile. This approach underscores the necessity of coordinating vaccination schedules and antimicrobial coverage to ensure patient safety during complement inhibition.

Transitioning from induction to maintenance therapy depends on clinical response, renal function stability, and the underlying aetiology. Following the four-week induction phase, this patient was transitioned to a maintenance dose of 1200 mg intravenous every two weeks. Although extended genetic testing did not identify pathogenic complement variants, the severity of the disease and the risk of relapse justified continuing therapy for at least four months post-induction. The decision to maintain treatment in the absence of a confirmed genetic predisposition reflects the clinical priority of preventing relapse in severe, infection-triggered CM-HUS. Ongoing monitoring in the nephrology outpatient clinic allows for reassessment of renal function, with the plan to discontinue therapy only if stability is maintained, illustrating the individualized nature of long-term management in CM-HUS.
