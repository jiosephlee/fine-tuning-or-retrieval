Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# The Dog Bite That Almost Killed: Recognizing Capnocytophaga Sepsis

We all know dog bites are common in the ED. Most are minor, requiring a quick wash, a tetanus shot, and maybe some oral antibiotics. But occasionally, a bite delivers a pathogen that turns a routine visit into a fight for life. A recent case report highlights a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented four days after a dog bite to her lip. She didn’t fill her penicillin prescription. By the time she hit our doors, she was in septic shock with severe hypoglycaemia, DIC, and acute kidney injury. She ended up on dialysis. This isn’t just a story about a bite; it’s a lesson on *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a rare but fulminant zoonosis that demands immediate recognition.

## The Pathogen: Capnocytophaga canimorsus

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod found in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. While human infections are rare, they are notorious for their severity. Transmission occurs via bites, scratches, or even close contact with saliva. The clinical spectrum is wide, ranging from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and multiorgan failure. Despite being susceptible to broad antibiotics, mortality can approach 30%.

Diagnosis is often delayed because the organism is fastidious and grows slowly in culture. In the case we’re discussing, *C. canimorsus* was suspected early due to the dog bite history, but confirmation took seven days via aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. This delay underscores the need for high clinical suspicion and the increasing utility of molecular methods like real-time nanopore sequencing when standard cultures lag.

## The Clinical Picture: Sepsis and the Red Flags

Our patient presented with a constellation of symptoms that should raise immediate alarm bells. Four days post-bite, she developed a high fever over 40°C, malaise, and numbness with discolouration of her feet. In the emergency department, she was septic with mottled, cool lower extremities.

One of the most striking features in this case was severe, refractory hypoglycaemia. Her glucose nadir was 0.6 mmol/L, which was resistant to oral glucose and glucagon. While hypoglycaemia can occur in severe sepsis, profound hypoglycaemia in the context of a dog bite is a specific red flag for *Capnocytophaga* infection. Her initial labs showed marked systemic derangement: severe inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy.

The initial interpretation was DIC secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis. Surgical causes like necrotising fasciitis were ruled out. Broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was started immediately to cover dog-associated pathogens. This is a critical teaching point: empiric coverage for bite wounds must include penicillin or piperacillin/tazobactam to target *Capnocytophaga*. In this case, the patient had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin initially but was unable to collect it, leaving her unprotected during the critical window.

## The Twist: When Sepsis Turns to HUS

Here is where the case gets complex. After transfer to the ICU, the patient became anuric and required continuous renal replacement therapy. She received supportive care and antibiotics, and initially, her inflammatory profile began to improve. However, on the nephrology ward, the clinical picture shifted again.

She developed a sudden, isolated drop in haemoglobin without bleeding, alongside persistent thrombocytopenia and rising LDH. A peripheral blood smear revealed schistocytes, consistent with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). This suggested a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The differential diagnosis for TMA in this setting is broad, including Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC-HUS).

TTP was ruled out as ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (TTP typically presents below 10%). STEC-HUS was excluded via negative stool PCR. The diagnosis landed on complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). This is a rare complication where the infection triggers dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, leading to endothelial damage. It is worth noting that her basic complement levels (C3/C4) were within normal limits. This is a crucial pearl: normal complement levels do not exclude CM-HUS, as they may remain in reference ranges during the disease course.

## Management and The Eculizumab Decision

Managing CM-HUS in the context of active or recent infection presents a therapeutic dilemma. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component 5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS and improves outcomes. However, terminal complement components are critical for defence against encapsulated bacteria, including *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*.

In this patient, eculizumab was initiated only after blood cultures became negative to minimize the risk of uncontrolled infection. Before starting the drug, she received meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero) and prophylactic antibiotics. Plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy, which provided transient stabilisation, but sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade.

The patient achieved haematological remission and partial renal recovery, though she remains on maintenance eculizumab. Extended genetic testing did not identify pathogenic variants, supporting a diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a hereditary complement defect.

## Take-Home Pearls

*   **Don't ignore the bite:** In any patient presenting with severe sepsis and a history of animal contact, especially dogs or cats, consider *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.
*   **Hypoglycaemia is a clue:** Profound, refractory hypoglycaemia in septic patients with animal exposure is a specific warning sign for this pathogen.
*   **Cover early:** Ensure empiric antibiotic coverage for bite wounds includes penicillin or piperacillin/tazobactam. Non-adherence to prophylaxis can be fatal.
*   **Watch for the second hit:** Even after sepsis appears controlled, monitor for delayed complications like TMA. New onset haemolysis or worsening renal function warrants investigation for HUS.
*   **Normal complement doesn't rule out HUS:** In suspected CM-HUS, do not be reassured by normal C3/C4 levels; they may not reflect the underlying dysregulation.
*   **Vaccinate before blocking:** If eculizumab is required, ensure meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are in place to prevent overwhelming infection.



# Sepsis, DIC, or TMA? Untangling the Coagulopathy Web

## The Dog Bite That Led to Dialysis

We all know the drill with dog bites: clean the wound, maybe give some penicillin, and send the patient home. But what happens when a simple bite on the upper lip spirals into septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and eventually dialysis dependence? This case report from Fredriksen et al. presents a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who turned out to be a textbook example of how infection can unmask a hidden hematologic catastrophe.

The pathogen was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a gram-negative rod lurking in the oropharynx of dogs. While *C. canimorsus* is known for causing fulminant sepsis, particularly in asplenic patients, this patient was immunocompetent. What makes this case a must-read for the emergency and critical care clinician is the diagnostic whiplash. She presented with a classic sepsis picture that looked like DIC, only to develop a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) once the initial storm seemed to pass. It forces us to ask: when does sepsis-induced coagulopathy stop being just sepsis and become something requiring complement blockade?

## The Clinical Timeline: When Sepsis Isn’t the Whole Story

The patient arrived with fever, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and coagulopathy. Her initial workup showed metabolic acidosis, hypoglycemia, and mottled extremities. The clinical team rightly suspected *C. canimorsus* early on and started broad-spectrum antibiotics. She was transferred to the ICU, managed for septic shock, and initially showed improvement. By day 8, she was stable enough to move to the nephrology ward, though she remained anuric and dialysis-dependent.

Here is where the case pivots. Despite the sepsis resolving and inflammatory markers improving, her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without bleeding. Her platelets stayed stubbornly low, and LDH skyrocketed. A peripheral smear revealed 15–20% schistocytes. This was the "red flag" that the initial diagnosis of sepsis/DIC was incomplete. The hemolysis was microangiopathic, suggesting a TMA process that had been obscured by the initial inflammatory storm.

This timeline is critical. In pure DIC, coagulation parameters usually normalize as the infection clears. In this patient, the coagulopathy resolved, but the hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persisted and worsened. This divergence suggests a second mechanism at play—in this case, complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) triggered by the infection.

## Differentiating DIC from TMA in the ICU

Distinguishing between sepsis-induced coagulopathy and TMA is one of the trickier challenges in the ICU. Both present with thrombocytopenia and renal failure. Both can show schistocytes on a smear, though they are often more abundant in TMA. The confusion is compounded by the fact that severe sepsis itself can trigger a TMA-like picture.

In this case, the team had to systematically rule out the other big players in the TMA differential. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was unlikely given an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5% (TTP is typically <10%). Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS) was ruled out via negative stool PCR. The diagnosis ultimately rested on the exclusion of these causes, the presence of MAHA, and a kidney biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and acute tubular necrosis consistent with TMA.

A major pitfall in diagnosing CM-HUS is relying too heavily on standard complement levels. In this patient, C3 and C4 were within normal limits. This is a crucial teaching point: normal complement levels do not exclude CM-HUS. The alternative pathway activity was actually reduced, but this was likely due to treatment effects rather than baseline depletion. The diagnosis of CM-HUS remains a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion, heavily reliant on recognizing the clinical phenotype when standard sepsis treatment fails to halt the hemolysis.

## The Therapeutic Dilemma: Complement Blockade in Infection

Once CM-HUS was suspected, the treatment decision became complex. The standard of care for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits the terminal complement component C5. However, giving a drug that blocks the complement system during an active bacterial infection is risky. Terminal complement is essential for killing encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria* and, ironically, *Capnocytophaga*.

The team navigated this by ensuring blood cultures were negative before starting eculizumab. They also administered meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero) and provided antibiotic prophylaxis. While plasma exchange (PEX) was used as a bridge, the patient’s sustained hematological and renal recovery only occurred after complement blockade was initiated. This highlights that while PEX is lifesaving in TTP, it offers only temporary benefit in CM-HUS. The key to renal recovery in CM-HUS appears to be the early initiation of eculizumab, even if it requires careful management of infection risks.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Watch the Timeline:** If a patient with sepsis and DIC shows persistent or worsening hemolysis and thrombocytopenia after the infection is controlled, suspect a superimposed TMA.
*   **Normal Complement Doesn’t Rule Out CM-HUS:** Standard C3 and C4 levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course; do not dismiss CM-HUS based on normal complement screening alone.
*   **Schistocytes Are a Clue, Not a Diagnosis:** While schistocytes can occur in DIC, a high percentage (e.g., >15–20%) combined with persistent thrombocytopenia and renal failure warrants a TMA workup.
*   **Rule Out the Big Two:** Always check ADAMTS13 activity to exclude TTP and stool PCR to exclude STEC-HUS before diagnosing CM-HUS.
*   **Infection Risk with Eculizumab:** When treating CM-HUS triggered by infection, ensure the infection is cleared and provide appropriate vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis before initiating complement blockade.
*   **Zoonotic Triggers:** Consider *Capnocytophaga* in any septic patient with a history of animal bites, even if they are immunocompetent, as it can trigger rare complications like CM-HUS.



# Normal Complement Levels Do Not Rule Out HUS: The CM-HUS Diagnostic Trap

Dog bites are a common presentation in the emergency department, often dismissed as minor soft tissue injuries requiring tetanus prophylaxis and perhaps a course of antibiotics. But what happens when a routine dog bite spirals into fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and dialysis-dependent kidney failure? Even more surprisingly, what happens when the patient stabilizes from sepsis, only to crash again with a new hematologic catastrophe? This case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection triggering complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) highlights a critical diagnostic pitfall that can leave clinicians staring at normal lab values while a patient’s kidneys shut down.

## The Clinical Course: From Sepsis to TMA

Our patient was a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented four days after sustaining a dog bite to her upper lip. She had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin but failed to fill the prescription. Her presentation was dramatic, featuring high fever, malaise, and numbness in her feet. On arrival, she was septic with mottled extremities, severe hypoglycemia, and metabolic acidosis. The initial workup revealed pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and coagulopathy consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

Blood cultures eventually confirmed *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod found in dog oropharyngeal flora. She was treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics and transferred to the ICU. Initially, the clinical picture seemed dominated by sepsis and DIC. However, the true complexity of her case emerged after the septic phase began to resolve. Despite improving inflammatory markers and recovering platelets, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. This was accompanied by undetectable haptoglobin and rising lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), signaling active hemolysis. A peripheral smear showed 15% to 20% schistocytes, raising the alarm for a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

## The Diagnostic Maze and the Complement Trap

When you see microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and AKI, your differential diagnosis narrows quickly to thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC-HUS), and complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). In this case, TTP was effectively ruled out with an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%, well above the <10% threshold required for diagnosis. STEC-HUS was excluded via negative stool PCR and the absence of prodromal enteric illness.

This left CM-HUS as the primary suspect, but here is where the diagnostic trap springs shut. Standard complement testing, specifically C3 and C4 levels, was within normal limits. In many clinical algorithms, normal complement levels are used to rule out atypical HUS, leading clinicians to stop the investigation prematurely. However, as this case demonstrates, normal C3/C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS. Complement levels can remain within reference ranges during the disease course, often because they are acute phase reactants that rise in response to inflammation, masking the consumption occurring at the endothelial level.

Further complicating the picture, the patient initially had a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), suggesting autoimmune hemolytic anemia. This was likely a false positive due to penicillin exposure or prior transfusions, as repeat testing was negative. The definitive diagnosis was secured through a renal biopsy, which revealed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation, consistent with TMA. Extended complement testing later showed reduced alternative pathway activity, but this was performed after treatment had begun, reinforcing that functional assays can be tricky to interpret in the acute setting.

## Management Challenges and Therapeutic Strategy

Treating CM-HUS in the context of active infection presents a unique therapeutic dilemma. The standard of care for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal complement component C5. While this halts the complement-mediated endothelial damage, it also impairs the host defense against encapsulated bacteria, including *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. In this patient, eculizumab was initiated only after blood cultures became negative to minimize the risk of uncontrolled infection.

Plasma exchange (PEX) was used as a bridging therapy. While PEX is life-saving in TTP, it offers only temporary benefit in CM-HUS by removing circulating inflammatory mediators without correcting the underlying complement dysfunction. The patient’s spontaneous diuresis returned after PEX, but sustained hematological and renal recovery occurred only after the initiation of complement blockade. Crucially, meningococcal vaccination (Menveo and Bexsero) and antibiotic prophylaxis were administered before starting eculizumab to mitigate the risk of opportunistic infections. The patient achieved hematological remission and partial renal recovery, though she remains on maintenance eculizumab with ongoing monitoring for relapse.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Normal C3/C4 Does Not Rule Out CM-HUS:** Complement levels can remain within reference ranges during the disease course due to their nature as acute phase reactants; do not rely on standard panels to exclude the diagnosis.
*   **Infection Can Trigger CM-HUS:** Even in immunocompetent adults without known genetic complement defects, severe infections like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* can act as the "second hit" required to trigger complement-mediated TMA.
*   **Biopsy Confirms TMA:** When laboratory markers are equivocal, a renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and glomerular ischemia is critical for confirming TMA and guiding therapy.
*   **Rule Out Mimics First:** Always exclude TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC-HUS (stool PCR) before diagnosing CM-HUS, as management differs significantly.
*   **Vaccinate Before Blocking:** If eculizumab is required in the setting of recent infection, ensure meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are in place to prevent life-threatening encapsulated bacterial infections.



# To Block or Not to Block: Eculizumab in the Setting of Active Bacteraemia

## The Case: From Dog Bite to Dialysis

We’ve all heard the adage that you should never ignore a dog bite, but few of us anticipate a trajectory from a lip wound to dialysis dependence. A recent case report highlights exactly this scenario, involving a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented with rapidly progressive sepsis four days after a dog bite to her upper lip. Despite receiving a tetanus vaccination, she failed to initiate her prescribed penicillin prophylaxis.

She arrived at the emergency department in septic shock with mottled extremities, severe hypoglycemia, and metabolic acidosis. Initial workup showed pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and coagulopathy consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Blood cultures eventually confirmed *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. While she initially improved with broad-spectrum antibiotics and supportive care, the clinical picture took a sinister turn. After eight days, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening renal function requiring continuous renal replacement therapy.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: Unmasking CM-HUS

The real diagnostic challenge emerged when the sepsis seemed to be resolving, yet the patient’s hemolysis and thrombocytopenia worsened. A peripheral smear revealed 15% to 20% schistocytes, indicating microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA). This constellation of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI raised the specter of Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA).

The team had to rapidly navigate the differential diagnosis. Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) was a primary concern, but ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively ruling it out as activity is typically below 10% in TTP. Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS) was excluded via negative stool PCR. The diagnosis ultimately settled on Complement-Mediated Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (CM-HUS), supported by a kidney biopsy showing thrombotic microangiopathy with fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli.

A critical teaching point from this case is the reliability of complement levels. Standard C3 and C4 levels were within normal limits. In CM-HUS, complement levels often remain normal during the disease course, so a normal result should not dissuade you from pursuing this diagnosis in the context of TMA.

## The Therapeutic Tightrope: Infection vs. Complement Blockade

Once CM-HUS was suspected, the team faced a significant therapeutic dilemma. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the terminal complement component C5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS and is crucial for preventing irreversible renal damage. However, terminal complement components are vital for host defense against encapsulated bacteria.

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is an encapsulated organism, and blocking C5 carries a risk of overwhelming infection, particularly with *Neisseria* species. The question became: do we block the complement to save the kidneys, or do we wait to avoid killing the patient with an uncontrolled infection?

In this case, the team chose a cautious approach. They initiated plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridging therapy to stabilize the patient. Eculizumab was withheld until blood cultures became negative, ensuring the active bacteraemia was cleared. Before starting the complement inhibitor, the patient received meningococcal vaccinations (Menveo and Bexsero) and was placed on antibiotic prophylaxis with penicillin. This strategy allowed for sustained haematological and renal recovery without triggering a superinfection.

The patient achieved haematological remission and regained spontaneous diuresis. While she remains on maintenance eculizumab with partial renal recovery, the decision to delay blockade until infection clearance appears to have been the key to her survival. This case underscores that while early eculizumab is generally preferred in CM-HUS, the presence of active invasive infection requires a nuanced risk-benefit analysis regarding timing.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Consider CM-HUS in TMA with Normal Complement Levels:** Do not rule out complement-mediated HUS based on normal C3 or C4 levels, as they can remain within reference ranges during active disease.
*   **Rule Out TTP and STEC-HUS:** ADAMTS13 activity above 10% and negative Shiga toxin PCR are essential to distinguish CM-HUS from TTP and STEC-HUS.
*   **Infection is a Trigger:** *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a rare but documented trigger for CM-HUS, even in immunocompetent adults without identified genetic complement defects.
*   **Timing of Eculizumab Matters:** In the setting of active bacteraemia with encapsulated organisms, consider delaying eculizumab until cultures clear to mitigate the risk of overwhelming infection, using plasma exchange as a bridge.
*   **Mandatory Prophylaxis:** Patients starting complement blockade require meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis to prevent encapsulated bacterial infections.



# Refractory Hypoglycemia in Sepsis: A Red Flag for Capnocytophaga

## The Bite That Went Wrong

Dog bites are a common complaint in the emergency department. Most are minor, requiring local wound care and perhaps a tetanus booster. But every now and then, a bite signals the start of a fulminant, life-threatening cascade. A recent case report highlights a healthy woman in her late 40s who presented four days after a dog bite to her upper lip. What started as a missed prescription for prophylactic penicillin evolved into septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and eventually a rare form of kidney failure.

What makes this case particularly instructive for the emergency clinician is a specific metabolic derangement that appeared early in her course: severe, refractory hypoglycemia. While sepsis-associated hypoglycemia is not unheard of, the severity and resistance to treatment in this patient served as a critical warning sign for a specific, fastidious pathogen lurking in the dog’s mouth.

## The Clinical Course

The patient arrived at the emergency department with a rapidly worsening condition. She had a high fever exceeding 40 °C, malaise, and reduced oral intake. Physical examination revealed mottled and cool lower extremities, suggesting poor perfusion. Her initial laboratory workup showed marked systemic derangement, including severe inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy.

The metabolic picture was alarming. Blood gas analysis revealed metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 and an elevated lactate of 5.7 mmol/L. More concerning was the blood glucose. She was found to have hypoglycemia that was refractory to oral glucose and glucagon, with a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L. This profound hypoglycemia, occurring in the context of sepsis, prompted urgent transfer to the hospital.

Given the constellation of fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and AKI, the initial working diagnosis was DIC secondary to bacteraemia. The medical team suspected *Capnocytophaga* species from the onset due to the history of the dog bite. Broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was started immediately to cover dog-associated pathogens. Seven days later, blood cultures confirmed *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia.

## The Pathogen: Capnocytophaga canimorsus

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod found in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. The clinical presentation is highly variable, ranging from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis and multiorgan failure.

Despite broad antibiotic susceptibility, mortality can approach 30%. Diagnosis is often delayed because the organism is fastidious and grows slowly in cultures. This delay can be critical, as seen in our patient, who did not initiate the prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin prophylaxis on the day of the bite.

In this case, the infection presented with septic shock, severe leukopaenia, and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. However, the clinical picture did not stabilize after antibiotic initiation. The patient became completely anuric with rapidly worsening renal function, requiring continuous renal replacement therapy.

## The Twist: Infection-Triggered HUS

The diagnostic complexity deepened after the initial sepsis phase. On day 3 in the ICU, the patient developed diffuse skin bleeding and distal cyanotic changes. After eight days of hospitalisation, she was transferred to the nephrology ward. While inflammatory markers improved and coagulation parameters normalized, she developed a sudden isolated drop in haemoglobin without evidence of bleeding.

Platelets remained persistently low, haptoglobin became undetectable, and LDH levels rose, indicating ongoing haemolysis. A peripheral blood smear revealed 15%–20% schistocytes, consistent with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). The differential diagnosis included thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*-associated HUS (STEC-HUS).

ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP, and stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative. A kidney biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis and ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The final diagnosis was complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS).

This highlights a crucial teaching point: CM-HUS may be obscured during the early sepsis and DIC phase. In this patient, haemolysis appeared only after sepsis began improving, suggesting infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a pre-existing disease. Notably, basic complement tests (C3/C4) were within normal limits, which does not exclude CM-HUS, as levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course.

## Management and Outcomes

Management of CM-HUS in the context of active infection presents unique challenges. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component 5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS. However, using complement blockade during active bacteraemia raises concerns, as terminal complement components are critical for defence against encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*.

In this case, eculizumab was initiated after blood cultures became negative to minimize the risk of uncontrolled infection. The patient received meningococcal vaccines and prophylactic antibiotics alongside the complement inhibitor. Plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy prior to the first dose of eculizumab.

Following the therapeutic intervention, the patient achieved haematological remission. Her kidney function partially recovered, though she remains dialysis-independent with reduced renal function (serum creatine 175 µmol/L). She continues maintenance eculizumab therapy with planned reassessment. Extended complement genetic testing did not identify any pathogenic variants, supporting the diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS without a demonstrable inherited complement predisposition.

## Clinical Pearls

This case underscores the importance of recognizing specific metabolic red flags in sepsis and maintaining a high index of suspicion for zoonotic pathogens. The refractory hypoglycemia was an early marker of severity that should have triggered immediate broad-spectrum coverage. Furthermore, clinicians must remain vigilant for superimposed thrombotic microangiopathies in patients recovering from severe sepsis, even if initial complement levels appear normal.

*   **Capnocytophaga canimorsus:** This gram-negative rod is found in dog and cat oropharyngeal flora. Infections are rare but carry a mortality rate of up to 30%. Diagnosis can be delayed due to slow growth in cultures.
*   **TMA after Sepsis:** Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be triggered by infection. It may be obscured during the early sepsis and DIC phase, presenting later with MAHA and thrombocytopenia.
*   **Normal Complement Levels:** Normal C3/C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS. Diagnosis is clinicopathological and requires exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC-HUS.
