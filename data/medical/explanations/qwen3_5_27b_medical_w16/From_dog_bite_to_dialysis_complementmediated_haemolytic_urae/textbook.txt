Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Sepsis Management

The clinical trajectory of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection often begins with a distinct exposure history, typically involving a dog bite, scratch, or close contact with saliva. In this case, a previously healthy woman in her late 40s presented four days after sustaining a bite to the upper lip. While *C. canimorsus* is a commensal organism in the oropharynx of dogs and cats, invasive human infection is rare but carries a high mortality rate, approaching 30%, particularly when diagnosis is delayed. The organism is a fastidious, gram-negative rod that can be difficult to culture, often leading to diagnostic delays. Crucially, this patient had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin for prophylaxis but failed to initiate treatment, leaving her unprotected against this pathogen. Clinicians managing dog bite wounds must recognize that while most infections are localized, fulminant sepsis can develop rapidly in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent hosts, necessitating a high index of suspicion when systemic symptoms arise shortly after exposure.

Upon admission, the patient exhibited signs of fulminant sepsis and early septic shock, characterized by a high fever exceeding 40 °C, malaise, and profound metabolic derangement. A notable and severe feature of her presentation was refractory hypoglycemia, with a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L, which persisted despite aggressive intravenous glucose and glucagon administration. This hypoglycemia, alongside a lactic acidosis (lactate 5.7 mmol/L) and metabolic acidosis (pH 7.27), reflected severe systemic inflammation and hepatic dysfunction. Physical examination revealed mottled and cool lower extremities with numbness and discolouration, suggestive of peripheral perfusion failure and microvascular thrombosis. Laboratory evaluation confirmed a systemic inflammatory response with pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and coagulopathy. These findings initially aligned with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia, a common complication of severe *Capnocytophaga* sepsis.

Differentiating sepsis-induced DIC from primary thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) presents a significant diagnostic challenge in the acute setting, as both conditions share features such as thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), and organ failure. In this case, the initial clinical picture was dominated by sepsis-related multiorgan involvement, which obscured the underlying TMA. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was initially considered unlikely due to the presence of leukopenia, significant inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocytes. To rule out other causes of pancytopenia and coagulopathy, such as acute leukaemia or haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, flow cytometry and a planned bone marrow biopsy were performed; however, normal results supported a sepsis-driven etiology. This diagnostic phase highlights the importance of recognizing that TMA can be masked during the early sepsis and DIC phase, often only becoming apparent once the initial infectious storm begins to subside.

Immediate management focused on stabilizing the patient and addressing the suspected bacterial source. Given the severity of the presentation and the specific exposure history, broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was initiated to cover dog-associated pathogens, including *Capnocytophaga* species, which are susceptible to beta-lactams. Although *C. canimorsus* was not confirmed until seven days later via blood cultures, the clinical suspicion justified this empiric coverage. Supportive care was equally critical; the patient received thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions to address coagulopathy and underwent aggressive fluid resuscitation. Due to the rapid progression to anuria and fluid overload, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was required to manage septic cardiomyopathy and metabolic derangements. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for close monitoring, where she remained hemodynamically stable without the need for vasopressors beyond stress-dose corticosteroids.

Despite initial stabilization, the clinical course provided warning signs of progression to multiorgan failure that exceeded typical sepsis recovery patterns. The patient developed complete anuria with worsening renal function that did not respond to loop diuretics, necessitating ongoing dialysis. Furthermore, diffuse skin bleeding, ecchymoses, and distal ischaemic changes in the toes and fingers suggested ongoing microthrombotic phenomena consistent with DIC. While coagulation parameters eventually normalized and inflammatory markers improved, the persistence of renal failure and the subsequent development of isolated haemolysis indicated that the initial diagnosis of sepsis-induced DIC was incomplete. This evolution underscores the necessity of continuous reassessment in septic patients, particularly when renal recovery lags behind hematological improvement, as this discrepancy may signal a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy requiring distinct therapeutic intervention.

## Pathophysiology of Infection-Triggered Thrombotic Microangiopathy

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), historically referred to as atypical HUS, is fundamentally driven by the dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway. While the complement system serves as a critical component of innate immunity, designed to opsonize pathogens and recruit inflammatory cells, uncontrolled activation can lead to significant host tissue injury. In the context of CM-HUS, regulatory proteins that normally constrain the alternative pathway—such as factor H, factor I, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—fail to function adequately. This failure results in a continuous cycle of complement activation on endothelial surfaces. In the case presented, although the patient presented with severe sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), the subsequent development of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and persistent thrombocytopenia indicated a shift toward a specific complement-mediated mechanism rather than sepsis-induced coagulopathy alone. Understanding this pathway is essential for distinguishing CM-HUS from other thrombotic microangiopathies (TMA) and initiating appropriate therapy.

The mechanism of tissue injury in CM-HUS involves direct damage to the vascular endothelium. Unregulated complement activation leads to the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on endothelial cells, triggering cell injury, detachment, and exposure of the subendothelial matrix. This injury promotes platelet adhesion and aggregation, resulting in the formation of microthrombi within the small vessels. Clinically, this manifests as the triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI). In this patient, the renal biopsy provided definitive pathological evidence of this process, revealing ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescent formation, consistent with TMA. The presence of schistocytes on the peripheral blood smear further confirmed mechanical haemolysis due to these microvascular thrombi. It is critical to recognize that these pathological findings can overlap with severe sepsis and DIC, as seen in the initial phase of this patient's illness, which often obscures the underlying TMA until the coagulopathy begins to resolve while haemolysis persists.

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* plays a unique role in this pathophysiology due to its specific interaction with the host immune system. As an encapsulated gram-negative rod found in canine oropharyngeal flora, *C. canimorsus* possesses virulence factors that allow it to evade host defenses, yet it also possesses the capacity to activate complement pathways. The case report highlights a clinical paradox: while complement activity is vital for clearing encapsulated bacteria like *C. canimorsus*, this same activation can trigger uncontrolled endothelial damage in susceptible individuals. The bacterium's variable serum susceptibility means that while some strains are complement-sensitive, capsule-expressing strains may show partial resistance, potentially leading to a prolonged inflammatory state. In this patient, the infection acted as the primary inflammatory stimulus, but the subsequent isolated haemolysis that developed after the initial sepsis improved suggests that the bacterial trigger initiated a self-perpetuating complement cascade that outlasted the active bacteraemia.

The concept of infection as a trigger is central to understanding the onset of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults. CM-HUS typically arises in individuals with an underlying genetic predisposition or autoantibodies affecting complement regulation, but disease penetrance is often incomplete. Consequently, an external "second hit" is usually required to precipitate clinical disease. Common triggers include infections, pregnancy, or medications. In this case, the patient was previously healthy with no significant medical history, and the onset of symptoms followed a dog bite and subsequent bacteraemia. The timing of the haemolytic crisis, which occurred after the initial septic phase began to improve, supports the hypothesis that the infection served as the necessary trigger to unmask a latent complement dysregulation. This underscores the importance of considering infectious triggers in patients presenting with new-onset TMA, even when the initial presentation mimics sepsis or DIC.

Finally, distinguishing between genetic predisposition and acquired complement activation is crucial for determining long-term management, though it remains a diagnostic challenge. Approximately 50% to 70% of CM-HUS patients harbour genetic variants or autoantibodies, but this patient’s extended complement genetic testing did not identify any pathogenic variants. This finding supports the diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS without a demonstrable inherited complement predisposition, a rare but documented presentation. Furthermore, standard complement levels (C3 and C4) were within normal limits during the disease course in this patient. This is a critical teaching point, as normal complement levels do not exclude the diagnosis of CM-HUS; levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course or be influenced by acute phase reactions. The reduction in alternative pathway activity (AH50) observed later was attributed to treatment effects from plasma exchange and eculizumab rather than intrinsic depletion. Thus, the diagnosis relies on a clinicopathological synthesis of exclusion, biopsy findings, and response to complement blockade, rather than relying solely on standard complement levels or genetic confirmation.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis of TMA

The diagnostic challenge in this case centered on distinguishing complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) from other causes of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) amidst a background of severe sepsis. The patient initially presented with septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), characterized by pancytopenia and coagulopathy. However, the clinical picture evolved after the initial septic phase, marked by a sudden isolated drop in haemoglobin, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI). This constellation of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and AKI constitutes the classic TMA triad. Recognizing this shift was critical, as the management of TMA differs fundamentally from supportive care for sepsis-induced organ dysfunction. In this patient, the emergence of schistocytes on peripheral blood smear and undetectable haptoglobin confirmed MAHA, necessitating a rigorous workup to identify the specific aetiology of the TMA to guide targeted therapy.

The first priority in the differential diagnosis of TMA in adults is excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), a condition requiring urgent plasma exchange. In this case, the clinical team utilized the PLASMIC score to assess the pretest probability of TTP while awaiting confirmatory testing. The patient’s score was 4/7, indicating a low probability of TTP. This was corroborated by ADAMTS13 activity levels, which returned at 43.5%. Since TTP is defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, typically presenting with activity below 10%, this result effectively ruled out TTP. This diagnostic distinction was vital because, while plasma exchange is the treatment of choice for TTP, it offers only temporary benefit in CM-HUS. The exclusion of TTP allowed the clinical team to pivot toward investigating other causes of TMA, specifically focusing on complement dysregulation.

Following the exclusion of TTP, the team evaluated for Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*-associated HUS (STEC-HUS), the most common cause of HUS in children but less frequent in adults. STEC-HUS is typically preceded by a prodromal diarrheal illness, which was absent in this patient’s history. Furthermore, stool polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis for Shiga toxin was negative. The combination of negative microbiological testing and the lack of enteric symptoms argued strongly against STEC-HUS. This step was essential to narrow the differential diagnosis toward non-infectious or infection-triggered complement-mediated processes, particularly given the patient’s recent history of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia.

Interpreting complement levels in the context of active TMA requires careful consideration, as standard laboratory markers can be misleading. In this case, initial standard complement tests (C3 and C4) were within normal limits. A common pitfall is assuming normal C3/C4 levels exclude CM-HUS; however, complement levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course. Functional assays of the complement pathways were also initially reported as normal. Extended complement testing performed later showed a markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50), but this was likely a reflection of the treatment effects from plasma exchange and eculizumab rather than intrinsic complement depletion at the time of onset. Additionally, genetic testing did not identify pathogenic variants in complement regulatory genes. Despite the absence of a demonstrable genetic defect, the clinical presentation and response to therapy supported a diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS, illustrating that normal baseline complement levels do not preclude the diagnosis.

The role of renal biopsy in this case was pivotal for confirming the diagnosis and excluding other glomerulopathies. Due to the risk of bleeding associated with thrombocytopenia, the biopsy was delayed until the platelet count increased to 77×10⁹/L. Histological examination revealed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation, all consistent with TMA. Crucially, immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy demonstrated no evidence of primary glomerulopathy, which helped exclude immune-mediated vasculitis or glomerulonephritis. These findings, combined with the exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS, solidified the diagnosis of CM-HUS. The biopsy served as the definitive pathological anchor in a complex clinical scenario where laboratory markers alone were insufficient to distinguish between sepsis-related injury and primary microangiopathy.

## Therapeutic Strategies and Management of CM-HUS

The management of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) requires rapid differentiation between entities to guide therapy, as the treatment for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) differs significantly from complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). In this case, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated as a bridging therapy while awaiting confirmatory ADAMTS13 activity and complement studies. PEX remains the first-line treatment for TTP but offers only temporary benefit in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators without correcting the underlying dysregulation. As observed in this patient, PEX sessions led to a return of spontaneous diuresis, indicating transient stabilisation. However, sustained haematological remission and renal recovery were only achieved following the initiation of complement blockade, highlighting that PEX should be viewed as a temporising measure in CM-HUS rather than a definitive cure.

Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the terminal complement component C5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS and is critical for halting the endothelial damage driven by uncontrolled complement activation. The timing of initiation is a key determinant of prognosis, with delayed treatment associated with incomplete renal recovery. In this case, the decision to start eculizumab was balanced against the presence of active *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. Because terminal complement components are essential for host defence against encapsulated bacteria, initiating complement blockade during active infection carries a risk of uncontrolled sepsis. Consequently, eculizumab was withheld until blood cultures were negative, ensuring the infection was controlled before immunosuppression. Once cleared, an induction regimen of 900 mg intravenous weekly for four weeks was administered, leading to haematological remission and partial renal function recovery.

The use of complement inhibitors necessitates rigorous prophylactic measures to mitigate the risk of invasive meningococcal and other encapsulated bacterial infections. Vaccination against *Neisseria meningitidis* is mandatory prior to therapy. In this patient, both Menveo (serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B) were administered before the first dose of eculizumab. However, because the vaccines were not administered at least two weeks prior to starting complement blockade, the patient required antibiotic prophylaxis to cover the gap in immunity. Intravenous ampicillin was de-escalated to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) for this purpose, aligning with the pathogen’s susceptibility profile. This approach underscores the necessity of coordinating vaccination schedules and antimicrobial coverage to ensure patient safety during complement inhibition.

Transitioning from induction to maintenance therapy depends on clinical response, renal function stability, and the underlying aetiology. Following the four-week induction phase, this patient was transitioned to a maintenance dose of 1200 mg intravenous every two weeks. Although extended genetic testing did not identify pathogenic complement variants, the severity of the disease and the risk of relapse justified continuing therapy for at least four months post-induction. The decision to maintain treatment in the absence of a confirmed genetic predisposition reflects the clinical priority of preventing relapse in severe, infection-triggered CM-HUS. Ongoing monitoring in the nephrology outpatient clinic allows for reassessment of renal function, with the plan to discontinue therapy only if stability is maintained, illustrating the individualized nature of long-term management in CM-HUS.

## Clinical Outcome, Prognosis, and Educational Implications

At the time of reporting, the patient had achieved haematological remission and returned to a good general condition with adequate urine output, although her renal function remained reduced with a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L. While she was no longer dialysis-dependent, the persistence of elevated creatinine indicates incomplete renal recovery, a common sequela of severe thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). This outcome underscores the risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) in complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), particularly when significant acute kidney injury (AKI) and anuria occur prior to treatment. The partial recovery observed here highlights that even with successful haematological control, renal parenchymal damage may be irreversible, necessitating long-term nephrology follow-up to monitor for chronic kidney disease progression.

Regarding the management of complement blockade, the patient was maintained on eculizumab (1200 mg intravenous every 2 weeks) for four months post-induction. The duration of therapy is typically guided by the presence of pathogenic complement variants and the risk of relapse. In this case, extended complement genetic testing did not identify pathogenic variants, and antifactor H antibodies were negative, suggesting an infection-triggered etiology rather than a hereditary form. Consequently, the clinical team planned reassessment for discontinuation if renal function stabilised, rather than committing to lifelong therapy. This approach illustrates the importance of monitoring for disease relapse when stopping complement inhibitors, as the absence of a genetic marker does not guarantee immunity against future episodes, especially if the initial trigger was an acquired inflammatory state.

Prognostic analysis in CM-HUS emphasizes that renal function at presentation and the time to initiation of eculizumab are the primary determinants of outcome. In this patient, the diagnosis of TMA was delayed until after the initial septic phase had improved, which likely contributed to the severity of the renal injury and the subsequent dialysis dependence. The case demonstrates that delayed initiation is associated with incomplete renal recovery. Therefore, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for TMA when patients with sepsis develop persistent thrombocytopenia or haemolysis that does not resolve with standard supportive care, as early intervention is critical to preserving renal function.

Beyond the specific management of CM-HUS, this case reinforces the necessity of considering rare infectious triggers in the workup of thrombotic microangiopathy. The history of a dog bite was the pivotal clue linking the patient to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia, a rare but potent trigger for complement dysregulation. While Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* is the most common infectious cause of HUS, zoonotic infections can present with overlapping features of sepsis and DIC before unmasking TMA. A thorough exposure history, including animal contact, is essential to identify such pathogens, particularly when standard HUS triggers are absent.

Finally, the management of sepsis-associated TMA requires navigating the therapeutic dilemma of immunosuppression during infection. The administration of eculizumab was deferred until blood cultures were negative to mitigate the risk of overwhelming infection from encapsulated bacteria, a known risk of terminal complement inhibition. Additionally, the patient received meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis prior to and during complement blockade. These steps are standard safety protocols that must be integrated into the treatment plan. The key takeaway for clinicians is to recognize the biphasic nature of such presentations, where TMA may emerge as sepsis resolves, and to balance the urgency of complement blockade with the safety requirements of infection control.
