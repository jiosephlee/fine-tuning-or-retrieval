Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenge

The clinical presentation of inflammatory myopathies can be highly variable, often leading to diagnostic delays when symptoms mimic more common conditions. In this case, a previously healthy male in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting the hip flexors, accompanied by mild myalgia. This constellation of symptoms created a high-stakes diagnostic environment, as neck swelling in the emergency setting necessitates the immediate exclusion of life-threatening airway compromises. The patient was afebrile and maintaining normal oxygen saturation on room air, yet the progression of swelling warranted urgent investigation. The initial clinical impression favored Ludwig’s angina, a severe cellulitis of the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces, due to the location of the swelling and the potential for rapid airway obstruction. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck supported this concern by revealing diffuse neck oedema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space. However, the absence of fever and the patient’s stable respiratory status prompted a broader differential diagnosis once deep neck space infection was clinically cleared.

Following the exclusion of an infectious etiology, the diagnostic focus shifted to idiopathic angioedema, particularly given the non-tender nature of the swelling and the lack of systemic signs of sepsis. The patient was transferred to a quaternary hospital with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgery services for further evaluation. The role of the ENT specialist was critical in ruling out structural causes; a flexible nasendoscopy demonstrated no significant anatomical abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space. While there were increased secretions and coughing, suggesting pharyngeal involvement, there was no evidence of a mass or obstruction that would explain the swelling structurally. Concurrently, the case was discussed with clinical immunology to evaluate for histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. The immunological review noted the absence of common precipitating factors, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and the patient had no personal or family history of angioedema. Standard investigations for hereditary or acquired angioedema, including C3, C4, and C1 esterase function, were within normal ranges, effectively ruling out these specific pathophysiological mechanisms.

Despite the negative workup for common causes of angioedema, the clinical team identified subtle physical examination findings that served as critical red flags for an underlying systemic autoimmune process. A thorough examination revealed generalised erythema of the face, specifically over the nasolabial folds, and an erythematous, swollen, and non-tender neck. More importantly, the patient exhibited subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema. Nailfold capillaroscopy was performed, revealing proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, which are hallmark microvascular abnormalities associated with connective tissue diseases such as dermatomyositis. Additionally, a rash was noted on the right lateral thigh, and motor examination demonstrated reduced hip flexor power of 4/5. Although classic dermatomyositis cutaneous features such as Gottron’s papules and a heliotrope eruption were absent at this stage, the combination of proximal muscle weakness, specific nailfold vascular changes, and atypical facial erythema provided a crucial clue that the pathology was not purely dermatological or allergic in nature.

The clinical reasoning that ultimately shifted the diagnosis away from angioedema relied on recognizing the neck swelling as a manifestation of non-histamine and non-bradykinin-mediated inflammation. The persistence of swelling without a structural or infective cause, coupled with the presence of proximal muscle weakness, prompted the request for a creatine kinase (CK) level. This decision was pivotal, as the significantly elevated CK of 16,184 U/L confirmed severe muscle injury consistent with myositis. The elevated transaminases and lactate dehydrogenase further supported muscle breakdown rather than primary hepatic pathology. This case illustrates that in patients presenting with unexplained neck oedema, particularly when accompanied by subtle cutaneous signs or unexplained muscle weakness, clinicians must consider inflammatory myopathies in the differential diagnosis. The swelling in this context arises from active muscle inflammation and associated subcutaneous oedema rather than vascular permeability changes seen in allergic reactions, highlighting the necessity of a high index of suspicion for dermatomyositis even in the absence of classic skin rashes.

## Pathophysiology of Dermatomyositis and the Anti-SAE1 Subtype

Dermatomyositis represents a distinct subset within the spectrum of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM), characterized by a specific combination of immune-mediated muscle inflammation and pathognomonic cutaneous manifestations. While IIMs share features such as proximal muscle weakness and elevated muscle enzymes, dermatomyositis is distinguished by its humoral immune mechanism targeting the microvasculature of the muscle and skin. In the general population, dermatomyositis exhibits a bimodal age distribution, peaking in children and adults aged 45 to 64 years, though cases in patients in their 40s, such as the male patient in this report, are consistent with the adult onset peak. The clinical presentation typically involves proximal muscle weakness, as seen in this patient’s hip flexor involvement, alongside characteristic skin findings like Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. However, the absence of classic cutaneous signs, as noted in this case where only subtle periungual changes and erythema were present initially, can obscure the diagnosis, leading to misclassification as other conditions like angioedema or deep neck infections. Understanding this spectrum is critical for clinicians to recognize that unexplained edema or weakness in the absence of fever or infection warrants investigation for autoimmune myopathy.

A crucial advancement in the classification and understanding of dermatomyositis involves the identification of myositis-specific antibodies, particularly the anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibody. SAE1 is an enzyme involved in the post-translational modification of proteins through sumoylation, a process structurally similar to ubiquitination. The presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies defines a specific clinical phenotype within dermatomyositis. In this case, the patient tested strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies with a signal intensity well above the normal range, which helped confirm the diagnosis when classic skin rashes were absent. Literature reviews indicate that anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis is associated with a distinct clinical profile compared to other myositis subtypes. Specifically, this antibody subtype carries a higher propensity for severe systemic involvement, including dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, as well as an increased risk of underlying malignancy. Recognizing this antibody profile allows clinicians to anticipate these specific complications and tailor surveillance strategies, such as the comprehensive malignancy screening and pulmonary monitoring undertaken for this patient.

The presentation of severe neck swelling in this patient highlights the phenomenon of pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis, a condition that must be differentiated from true histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. True angioedema, whether hereditary or acquired, typically involves the activation of the complement cascade or bradykinin pathways, leading to vascular permeability changes in the subcutaneous tissue. In contrast, the edema observed in dermatomyositis arises from active muscle inflammation and microvascular injury rather than mast cell degranulation or complement consumption. This distinction was clinically evident in this case, where complement levels (C3, C4) and C1 esterase function were normal, ruling out hereditary or acquired angioedema. The pathophysiology of dermatomyositis-associated swelling involves immune-mediated damage to the microvasculature, leading to leakage and inflammation within the muscle and subcutaneous tissues. Consequently, the neck swelling described as "pseudoangioedema" is a manifestation of the underlying myositis affecting the strap muscles and surrounding soft tissue, explaining why it did not respond to standard angioedema therapies and required immunosuppression.

The association between anti-SAE1 antibodies and specific organ involvement, particularly dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD), is central to the management and prognosis of these patients. Dysphagia in dermatomyositis results from inflammation of the pharyngeal and esophageal striated muscles, which can lead to severe aspiration risks, as demonstrated by this patient’s barium swallow study showing penetration to the vocal cords. This patient required nasogastric tube feeding for two months due to persistent dysphagia, underscoring the severity of muscle involvement in the SAE1 subtype. Similarly, interstitial lung disease is a major cause of morbidity in dermatomyositis, with anti-SAE1 positive patients showing a reported incidence of ILD around 43% in literature cohorts. In this case, pulmonary function tests revealed a restrictive pattern and CT imaging showed peribronchial consolidation, likely secondary to micro-aspiration from dysphagia rather than primary fibrosis, though the distinction requires careful monitoring. Understanding this pathophysiological link ensures that respiratory and swallowing function are prioritized during treatment, as airway compromise can be life-threatening.

Finally, the immunological basis of dermatomyositis is best understood through the characteristic histopathological findings of perifascicular atrophy and microangiopathy. The muscle biopsy in this case revealed necrotic and vacuolated myofibres in a perifascicular distribution, along with scattered perimysial inflammation. Crucially, immunohistochemistry demonstrated complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This finding supports the microangiopathy theory of dermatomyositis, where complement-mediated damage to the capillaries surrounding the muscle fascicles leads to ischemia and subsequent perifascicular necrosis. This mechanism differentiates dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies like polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, which typically lack this specific pattern of complement deposition and perifascicular involvement. Recognizing these pathological hallmarks confirms the diagnosis of dermatomyositis and validates the decision to use aggressive immunosuppression, such as the combination of corticosteroids, IVIG, and mycophenolate mofetil used in this case, to halt the microvascular injury and muscle destruction.

## Diagnostic Evaluation and Confirmatory Testing

The diagnostic trajectory in this case shifted from a primary concern for angioedema or deep neck infection to an autoimmune inflammatory myopathy once specific laboratory and imaging investigations were pursued. The initial suspicion of dermatomyositis was triggered by the atypical nature of the neck oedema and subtle cutaneous findings, necessitating a targeted workup to confirm muscle involvement, identify specific autoantibodies, and exclude malignancy.

**Interpretation of Creatine Kinase and Liver Transaminases**
The cornerstone of the biochemical diagnosis in this case was the measurement of serum creatine kinase (CK). In patients presenting with unexplained weakness or swelling where inflammatory myopathy is suspected, CK serves as a sensitive marker of muscle injury. This patient presented with a markedly elevated CK level of 16,184 U/L, indicating severe and active muscle breakdown. While the urine myoglobin screen was negative, suggesting the absence of frank rhabdomyolysis with renal compromise at that moment, the magnitude of the CK elevation was inconsistent with idiopathic angioedema. Concurrently, the patient exhibited elevated liver transaminases, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L. Clinicians must recognize that AST and ALT are not exclusively hepatic enzymes; they are also present in skeletal muscle. In the context of severe myositis, disproportionate elevation of AST relative to ALT often reflects muscle origin rather than primary liver pathology. Additionally, the elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 692 U/L further supported widespread tissue injury. These laboratory values collectively directed the clinical focus toward a systemic myopathic process rather than an isolated airway or dermatological disorder.

**Myositis Antibody Panel Analysis and Specificity of Anti-SAE1**
Following the biochemical evidence of myositis, serological testing was performed to classify the specific subtype of inflammatory myopathy. The patient tested positive for anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a high titre of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern, a non-specific finding common in autoimmune conditions. However, the diagnostic specificity was achieved through a myositis antibody panel. This panel identified strongly positive anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (normal range <35), while other common myositis antibodies such as anti-Jo-1 and anti-TIF1-γ were negative. Anti-SAE1 antibodies are a distinct subset associated with dermatomyositis and carry specific clinical implications. In the literature, anti-SAE1 positivity is frequently linked with a higher risk of interstitial lung disease and dysphagia, both of which were present in this patient. Identifying this specific autoantibody was crucial not only for confirming the diagnosis of dermatomyositis but also for risk stratification regarding malignancy and pulmonary complications.

**Muscle Biopsy Findings: Perifascicular Atrophy and Complement Deposition**
While serology provided strong evidence, the definitive confirmation of dermatomyositis required histological correlation. A muscle biopsy of the thigh revealed foci of necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerative myofibres arranged in a perifascicular distribution. Perifascicular atrophy is a hallmark histological feature of dermatomyositis, distinguishing it from polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy. Furthermore, immunohistochemistry demonstrated the deposition of the membrane attack complex, complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9), in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This finding indicates microangiopathy and complement-mediated damage to the capillary endothelium, which is pathognomonic for dermatomyositis. The presence of scattered perimysial inflammation consisting of dispersed histiocytes further supported the diagnosis. Together, the perifascicular necrosis and C5b-9 deposition confirmed the diagnosis of moderate to severe dermatomyositis, validating the clinical and serological suspicion.

**Utility of MRI and PET Scans in Assessing Muscle Inflammation and Malignancy Screening**
Advanced imaging played a dual role in this case: assessing the extent of muscle inflammation and screening for paraneoplastic malignancy. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs demonstrated changes consistent with active myositis in the obturator, adductor, and iliopsoas muscles, correlating with the patient’s proximal weakness. Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan provided a whole-body assessment, showing diffuse abnormal FDG accumulation within multiple muscle groups, most prominently in the head and neck. This imaging confirmed the systemic nature of the inflammation and explained the neck swelling as inflammatory myositis rather than angioedema. Crucially, the PET scan also served as a malignancy screen. Given that dermatomyositis is a paraneoplastic syndrome in a significant subset of patients, particularly those with anti-SAE1 antibodies, ruling out occult cancer is essential. The PET scan showed no metabolically active disease elsewhere to suggest malignancy, complementing the normal CT chest and abdomen findings.

**Evaluation of Dysphagia via Video Fluoroscopic Swallow Study**
Dysphagia is a critical complication of dermatomyositis that significantly impacts morbidity and mortality, primarily due to the risk of aspiration. In this patient, the presence of increased secretions and coughing during nasendoscopy prompted further functional assessment. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow study was performed and revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with significant pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. This objective evaluation confirmed that the dysphagia was due to pharyngeal muscle weakness rather than a structural obstruction. The findings necessitated immediate intervention, including nasogastric tube feeding to prevent aspiration pneumonia and ensure nutritional support. Recognizing the severity of the dysphagia was integral to the management plan, as it influenced the decision to avoid certain oral medications and guided the rehabilitation strategy during recovery.

## Management Strategies and Therapeutic Rationale

The management of dermatomyositis requires a multimodal approach aimed at rapidly suppressing inflammation to prevent irreversible muscle damage and multiorgan complications, particularly when airway involvement is present. In this case, the patient’s severe neck swelling, significant proximal weakness, and markedly elevated creatine kinase necessitated aggressive induction therapy. High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone was administered at 1000 mg daily for three days to achieve rapid immunosuppression. Following this pulse therapy, the patient was transitioned to an oral prednisone tapering regimen starting at 60 mg daily, weaned by 5 mg every two to three weeks. This initial strategy prioritizes the immediate control of systemic inflammation while preparing for a longer-term maintenance plan that minimizes cumulative steroid toxicity.

Given the severity of the presentation, including dysphagia and extensive muscle involvement, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added as an adjunctive therapy. A loading dose of 2 g/kg was administered over five days, followed by five subsequent cycles at four-week intervals. IVIG is particularly valuable in refractory cases or those with severe extramuscular manifestations, such as the pseudoangioedema and dysphagia observed here, where rapid functional recovery is critical. To facilitate steroid tapering and maintain remission, steroid-sparing agents were introduced. Mycophenolate mofetil was selected at a dose of 1 g twice daily due to its tolerability and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Notably, methotrexate was avoided despite being a common first-line agent because of its association with interstitial lung disease; this decision was reinforced by the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and radiological findings of peribronchial consolidation. Additionally, rituximab was utilized with two intravenous doses of 1000 mg administered two weeks apart. While evidence for optimal pharmacotherapy in anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains limited, the inclusion of rituximab aligns with heterogeneous treatment strategies observed in cohort studies for severe disease.

Supportive care and safety measures were integral to the management plan, particularly regarding airway protection and infection prophylaxis. The patient exhibited severe pharyngeal dysphagia with post-swallow penetration on video fluoroscopic study, necessitating nutritional support via a nasogastric tube at discharge. This tube was maintained for two months until swallowing function improved sufficiently to tolerate soft solids, highlighting the importance of monitoring bulbar involvement in dermatomyositis. Concurrently, the high-intensity immunosuppression required prophylaxis against *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia. The patient was initially started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim but developed haemolytic anaemia, prompting a switch to nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks. This adjustment underscores the necessity of monitoring for adverse drug reactions in immunocompromised patients and having alternative prophylactic strategies ready to ensure safety without compromising the primary treatment regimen.

## Clinical Outcomes, Surveillance, and Key Lessons

The clinical course of this patient illustrates the potential for significant recovery in dermatomyositis with aggressive immunosuppression, although the timeline for functional restoration can be protracted. Following a 21-day inpatient stay involving intravenous methylprednisolone, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and the initiation of mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab, the patient demonstrated moderate improvement in hip flexor weakness and marked reduction in cervical swelling, though dysphagia persisted. Three weeks after discharge, proximal muscle strength had fully recovered, and serum creatine kinase levels normalized to 37 U/L. Swallowing function improved sufficiently at the two-month mark to allow for the removal of the nasogastric tube, and by three months post-discharge, cervical soft tissue swelling had completely resolved. The steroid tapering regimen was successfully completed seven months after discharge, with full resolution of dysphagia achieved by that time. At the one-year follow-up, respiratory function remained stable with good exercise tolerance, and chest imaging showed near-complete resolution of the aspiration-related consolidation observed at presentation.

Given the diagnosis of dermatomyositis, long-term surveillance for malignancy is a critical component of management, as the disease is frequently paraneoplastic. According to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines for cancer screening, this patient was stratified as high risk due to the presence of dermatomyositis and dysphagia (high-risk factors) alongside anti-SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex (intermediate-risk factors). Consequently, a comprehensive screening protocol was initiated, including CT scans of the chest and abdomen, PET imaging, and tumor marker analysis, all of which were initially negative. To address the remaining risk, plans were established for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy to exclude occult malignancy. This case underscores the necessity of adhering to structured screening guidelines, as malignancy-associated dermatomyositis requires distinct oncological monitoring compared to non-paraneoplastic cases.

Pharmacologic management in this case required a multimodal approach to achieve remission while mitigating treatment-related toxicity. The patient was maintained on monthly IVIG and mycophenolate mofetil, with a plan to taper the latter over six months depending on clinical progress. A notable aspect of the management was the modification of *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia prophylaxis; the patient developed haemolytic anaemia on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim despite normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase levels, necessitating a switch to nebulised pentamidine. This highlights the importance of monitoring for adverse effects during intense immunosuppression, particularly in patients with significant inflammatory burden. The successful discontinuation of prednisone at seven months demonstrates that steroid-sparing agents can facilitate long-term disease control while minimizing glucocorticoid-associated morbidity.

From a diagnostic perspective, this case reinforces the utility of creatine kinase levels and detailed dermatological examination in evaluating unexplained neck swelling. The initial presentation of cervical oedema mimicked idiopathic angioedema, leading to a delay in correct identification. However, the presence of subtle periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages, combined with the atypical nature of the swelling, prompted the request for a creatine kinase level, which returned significantly elevated at 16,184 U/L. This finding shifted the diagnostic reasoning toward an inflammatory myopathy. Clinicians must recognize that neck swelling in the context of dermatomyositis arises from muscle inflammation and subcutaneous oedema rather than histamine or bradykinin-mediated pathways. Therefore, in patients presenting with angioedema-like symptoms that are refractory to standard therapy or lack a clear trigger, a muscle enzyme panel and thorough skin examination should be considered to rule out inflammatory myopathies.

Ultimately, the clinical significance of this case lies in expanding the differential diagnosis for acute neck swelling beyond common otolaryngological or allergic causes. Dermatomyositis, particularly the anti-SAE1 positive subset, can manifest with prominent cervical soft tissue swelling that masquerades as angioedema, potentially leading to airway anxiety or unnecessary surgical intervention if not recognized. The combination of elevated inflammatory markers, proximal weakness, and specific cutaneous signs serves as the diagnostic pivot point. Early recognition allows for the initiation of prompt immunosuppression, which is vital to prevent disease progression and multiorgan damage, including the aspiration pneumonitis seen in this patient. This case serves as a reminder that atypical presentations of autoimmune disease require a high index of suspicion and a systematic approach to exclude life-threatening mimics.
