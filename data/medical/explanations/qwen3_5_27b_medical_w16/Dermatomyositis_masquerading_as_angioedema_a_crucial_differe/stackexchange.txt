Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient presenting with acute neck swelling initially suspected to be angioedema or Ludwig's angina, what single laboratory test is critical to request to exclude inflammatory myopathy before initiating immunosuppression for angioedema?

The single critical laboratory test is the serum creatine kinase (CK) level.

In this case, a 40-year-old male presented with progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness initially suspected to be Ludwig’s angina or idiopathic angioedema. While the clinical picture suggested an otolaryngologic or immunologic etiology, the presence of subtle skin and nail changes prompted the request for a CK level, which returned significantly elevated at 16,184 U/L. This finding was pivotal in shifting the diagnosis from angioedema to dermatomyositis, confirmed subsequently by positive anti-SAE1 antibodies and muscle biopsy.

The pathophysiology here involves subcutaneous tissue oedema arising from underlying muscle inflammation (myositis) rather than histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated vascular permeability. As noted in the discussion, dermatomyositis can present with "pseudoangioedema," particularly in the neck, which may mimic deep neck space infections or idiopathic angioedema. Because the management of inflammatory myopathy requires intense immunosuppression distinct from standard angioedema protocols, and because myopathy can lead to severe complications such as dysphagia and respiratory failure, the authors emphasize that "examining for physical signs of dermatomyositis and requesting a creatine kinase level is critical" in any patient with unexplained neck swelling.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the pathophysiology of 'pseudoangioedema' in dermatomyositis differ from classic histamine-mediated or bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

The pathophysiology of "pseudoangioedema" in dermatomyositis is fundamentally distinct from classic angioedema because it arises from inflammatory myositis and complement-mediated microangiopathy rather than mediator-driven vascular permeability.

Classic angioedema involves rapid fluid extravasation due to mast cell degranulation (histamine) or C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency (bradykinin). In this case, these mechanisms were excluded by normal C3, C4, and C1 esterase function levels. Conversely, the patient’s neck swelling was driven by underlying muscle inflammation. The muscle biopsy demonstrated perifascicular necrosis and, crucially, deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) in endomysial capillaries. This complement-mediated microangiopathy causes local inflammation and edema that mimics angioedema clinically but originates from the myopathic process itself.

This distinction is critical for management. While classic angioedema requires antihistamines or bradykinin antagonists, pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis responds to immunosuppression. In this patient, the swelling resolved only after high-dose corticosteroids, IVIG, and mycophenolate mofetil addressed the underlying autoimmune myositis. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for dermatomyositis in patients with unexplained neck swelling, particularly when accompanied by elevated creatine kinase or subtle cutaneous signs like periungual erythema.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which myositis-specific autoantibody is strongly associated with the clinical phenotype of severe dysphagia and interstitial lung disease observed in this patient?

The myositis-specific autoantibody strongly associated with this clinical phenotype is the **anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibody**.

In this case, the patient’s myositis antibody panel was strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, while testing negative for other common myositis-specific antibodies such as anti-Mi-2α, anti-TIF1-γ, and anti-Jo-1. The clinical relevance of this finding is underscored in the discussion section of the report, which cites a large literature review indicating that anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is distinctively characterized by a high prevalence of severe dysphagia (46%) and interstitial lung disease (43%).

Although this patient’s initial pulmonary imaging suggested aspiration pneumonitis rather than frank fibrosis, the presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies necessitates a high index of suspicion for both dysphagia and ILD, as well as an increased risk of malignancy. In this patient, the antibody status aligned with the prominent clinical feature of severe pharyngeal dysphagia confirmed by barium swallow, which required nasogastric tube feeding for two months. Recognizing the anti-SAE1 phenotype is crucial for anticipating these complications and guiding appropriate immunosuppressive therapy and malignancy screening.

### [Clinical Pearls] Based on the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines, what specific risk factors in this case mandated a comprehensive malignancy screening protocol?

In accordance with the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines, this patient was classified as high risk for malignancy due to a specific constellation of clinical and serological features. As detailed in the discussion section of the report, the patient possessed two high-risk factors: the diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself and the presence of dysphagia. Additionally, he exhibited two intermediate-risk factors: positive anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme (SAE1) antibodies and male sex.

The aggregation of these factors mandated the comprehensive malignancy screening protocol he underwent, which included CT imaging of the chest and abdomen, tumor marker testing, a PET scan, and plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. This case underscores the necessity of applying IMACS stratification tools in all patients with idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, as the presence of specific features like dysphagia and certain autoantibodies significantly elevates the pre-test probability of an underlying occult malignancy, necessitating rigorous baseline and interval surveillance to exclude paraneoplastic associations.

### [Pathophysiology] What histological pattern on muscle biopsy is pathognomonic for dermatomyositis and distinguishes it from polymyositis?

The histological pattern that distinguishes dermatomyositis from polymyositis is **perifascicular atrophy and necrosis**. In this case, the diagnosis was confirmed by a thigh muscle biopsy demonstrating foci of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerate, myophagocytosed, and regenerative myofibers specifically in a **perifascicular distribution**. Furthermore, immunohistochemistry revealed complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in **perifascicular endomysial capillaries**, a finding the report identifies as typical of dermatomyositis. This perifascicular pattern reflects the underlying microangiopathy of the disease and serves to differentiate it from other inflammatory myopathies. In this patient, these specific histological findings were critical to establishing the diagnosis of dermatomyositis, which initially masqueraded as idiopathic angioedema.

### [Management] Why was methotrexate contraindicated in this patient's treatment plan despite being a first-line steroid-sparing agent for dermatomyositis?

Methotrexate was withheld in this patient primarily due to the risk of exacerbating pulmonary pathology, specifically interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary fibrosis. While methotrexate is a common first-line steroid-sparing agent for dermatomyositis, it carries a well-documented risk of pulmonary toxicity. In this case, the decision was grounded in the patient's abnormal baseline pulmonary function tests, which demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern.

As explicitly stated in the treatment section of the report, methotrexate was avoided "due to the association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, noting that the patient had abnormal lung function tests." Consequently, the team selected mycophenolate mofetil, which has a more favorable pulmonary safety profile in this context, combined with intravenous immunoglobulin and rituximab. This case underscores the necessity of tailoring immunosuppressive regimens to individual organ involvement, prioritizing agents that minimize the risk of worsening respiratory function in patients with inflammatory myopathies.

### [Management] What immediate intervention is required for a dermatomyositis patient with severe pharyngeal dysphagia to mitigate the risk of aspiration pneumonitis?

In patients with dermatomyositis presenting with severe pharyngeal dysphagia, the immediate intervention required to mitigate the risk of aspiration pneumonitis is the establishment of enteral access, typically via a nasogastric tube, to bypass oral intake for both nutrition and medication administration.

Pharyngeal dysphagia in dermatomyositis results from inflammatory myopathy affecting the striated muscles of the pharynx and upper esophagus. This weakness leads to residue retention and penetration of fluids into the airway, as demonstrated in this case by the video fluoroscopic swallow study, which revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick fluids to the vocal cords. The clinical consequence of this mechanism was evident in this patient, whose chest CT showed patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis.

While aggressive immunosuppression (such as high-dose corticosteroids, IVIG, and rituximab) is necessary to treat the underlying myositis and restore muscle function, the response is not immediate. Therefore, mechanical protection of the airway is critical during the treatment lag. In this case, the patient required nasogastric tube feeding for two months post-discharge until swallowing function improved sufficiently to allow tube removal and progression to soft solids. This approach prevents recurrent aspiration while the inflammatory process resolves, safeguarding pulmonary function during the critical early phase of disease management.

### [Morning Report] During the physical examination of a patient with suspected connective tissue disease, what specific nailfold capillaroscopy findings support a diagnosis of dermatomyositis when classic cutaneous signs are absent?

In the absence of classic cutaneous markers such as Gottron’s papules or a heliotrope eruption, nailfold capillaroscopy remains a critical diagnostic tool for suspected dermatomyositis. In this case, the patient presented with severe neck swelling mimicking angioedema and lacked characteristic rashes. However, nailfold capillaroscopy revealed **proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages, along with avascular areas**.

These specific microvascular abnormalities supported the provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis and justified the prompt request for creatine kinase levels and myositis antibody testing, which ultimately confirmed anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis. Clinicians should recognize that subtle nailfold changes, specifically microhaemorrhages and avascular zones, can unmask dermatomyositis even when the presentation is dominated by atypical features like pseudoangioedema or proximal weakness. In such scenarios, careful examination of the nailfold capillaries can provide the necessary diagnostic clue to initiate appropriate immunosuppressive therapy and prevent multiorgan damage.
