Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Angioedema Masquerade: Dermatomyositis as a Cause of Neck Swelling

Neck swelling in the emergency department is a high-stakes presentation. Your differential usually revolves around three scary buckets: airway obstruction from infection (like Ludwig’s angina), allergic reaction or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, or deep neck space abscesses. You secure the airway, you get a CT, and you start antibiotics or epinephrine. But what if the swelling isn't infectious, and it isn't histamine-driven? What if the edema is actually a sign of severe muscle inflammation?

This case report highlights a diagnostic trap where dermatomyositis presented as "pseudoangioedema," nearly leading to a misdiagnosis of idiopathic angioedema. It serves as a stark reminder that when the common causes don't fit the clinical picture, you need to dig deeper into the inflammatory myopathies.

## The Case Presentation

A previously healthy male in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors. He was afebrile and saturating well on room air. The initial impression was Ludwig’s angina given the severity of the swelling. A CT scan of the neck revealed diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. However, he lacked the classic fever or sepsis profile, and ENT surgeons cleared him of a deep neck space infection.

With no structural or infective cause found, the patient was referred to clinical immunology for suspected idiopathic angioedema. On review, he had generalized erythema of the face and a swollen, non-tender neck. Crucially, he denied taking ACE inhibitors or NSAIDs, and there was no history of hereditary angioedema. The clinical profile was atypical for acquired angioedema. A thorough physical exam revealed subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema and a rash on the right lateral thigh. Proximal nailfold capillaroscopy showed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas.

## The Diagnostic Pivot

The turning point came when a creatine kinase (CK) level was requested due to the atypical nature of the neck edema. The result was staggering: 16,184 U/L. His transaminases and lactate dehydrogenase were also significantly elevated. While the initial workup for angioedema showed normal complement levels and C1 esterase function, further autoantibody testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titre of 1:1280.

The patient tested strongly positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. A muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis, showing perifascicular necrosis and complement component 5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is typical of dermatomyositis. The neck swelling was not angioedema in the traditional sense but rather subcutaneous tissue edema arising from muscle inflammation in the setting of myositis.

## Clinical Implications and Management

This presentation is rare but dangerous. Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that typically affects the skin and muscles but can involve the respiratory system and gastrointestinal tract. In this case, the patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia confirmed by a barium swallow, leading to aspiration pneumonitis and the need for nasogastric tube feeding for two months. The anti-SAE1 antibody positivity is particularly notable, as it is associated with a higher risk of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease.

Because of the significant airway muscle involvement and dysphagia risk, the patient required aggressive immunosuppression. He was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by a prednisone taper, alongside intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. He was also screened extensively for malignancy, as dermatomyositis is paraneoplastic in a significant subset of patients. Fortunately, after three months, his cervical swelling resolved, CK normalized, and swallowing function recovered sufficiently to progress to soft solids.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Consider Inflammatory Myopathy in Unexplained Neck Swelling:** If a patient presents with significant neck edema that does not respond to standard angioedema management and lacks infectious signs, consider dermatomyositis as a differential diagnosis.
*   **Check the CK:** A creatine kinase level is a critical test in patients with unexplained edema, especially if there is any hint of muscle weakness or skin changes.
*   **Look for Subtle Skin Signs:** Classic rashes like Gottron’s papules or heliotrope eruptions may be absent. Look for periungual erythema, nailfold capillary changes, or subtle facial erythema.
*   **Assess for Dysphagia:** Dermatomyositis can cause severe pharyngeal muscle weakness leading to aspiration. A barium swallow may be necessary if dysphagia is suspected.
*   **Know the Antibodies:** Anti-SAE1 antibodies are associated with dermatomyositis and carry a higher risk of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease compared to other myositis antibodies.



# Look at the Nails: Cutaneous Clues to Missed Myositis

We all know the drill: a patient walks in with a swollen neck, and the differential immediately swings between Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space infection, and angioedema. Airway protection is priority number one. But what happens when the CT shows diffuse edema, the ENT clears infection, and the angioedema workup comes back negative?

In a recent case report published in *BMJ Case Reports*, a 40-year-old male presented with exactly this scenario. He had a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. The initial impression was Ludwig’s angina, followed by a suspicion of idiopathic angioedema. However, the diagnosis was actually dermatomyositis (DM) masquerading as angioedema. This case serves as a stark reminder that when the edema doesn't fit the classic histaminergic or bradykinin profile, you need to broaden your differential to include inflammatory myopathies.

The patient was afebrile and saturating well, but the CT neck revealed diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. Despite aggressive workup for infection and structural causes, the swelling persisted. The pivot point in this case wasn't a complex genetic test initially, but a simple physical exam finding and a lab value that didn't belong in an angioedema patient: the creatine kinase (CK) level.

### The Diagnostic Pivot

When the immunologist reviewed the patient, the clinical profile was atypical for acquired or hereditary angioedema. There was no history of ACE inhibitors, no family history, and complement levels (C3, C4, C1 esterase) were normal. However, a thorough physical exam revealed subtle clues that had been missed in the acute setting.

The patient had generalized erythema of the face, including the nasolabial folds, and an erythematous, swollen neck. More importantly, there were subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema and a rash on the right lateral thigh. Nailfold capillaroscopy showed proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular areas. These findings, combined with reduced hip flexor power (4/5), prompted a CK level, which returned a staggering 16,184 U/L.

This massive elevation in muscle enzymes, paired with the cutaneous findings, shifted the diagnosis from idiopathic angioedema to anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis. The muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis with perifascicular necrosis and complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries.

### Why the Nails Matter

In the emergency department, we often rush to secure the airway and order the CT, but we sometimes neglect the extremities. Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that typically involves skin and muscles, but it can affect the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million, but missing it can lead to multiorgan damage.

Pseudoangioedema is an uncommon feature of dermatomyositis, present in only about 6.6% of patients in one cohort study. When it does occur, it can masquerade as a primary otolaryngology disorder. In this case, the neck swelling was actually inflammatory edema secondary to myositis of the strap muscles, rather than vascular permeability driven by histamine or bradykinin.

The cutaneous clues are often the earliest warning signs. While classic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope eruptions are well-known, they were absent in this patient. Instead, the diagnosis relied on subtler findings:
*   **Periungual Erythema:** Redness around the nail folds.
*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy:** Dilated capillaries, microhemorrhages, and avascular areas are highly specific for connective tissue diseases like DM.
*   **The Holster Sign:** A rash on the lateral thigh, which was present in this patient.
*   **Facial Erythema:** Involvement of the nasolabial folds can be mistaken for rosacea or seborrheic dermatitis.

### Management and Malignancy Risk

Once diagnosed, the patient required prompt and intense immunosuppression to reduce disease progression. He received intravenous methylprednisolone pulses, followed by a tapering oral prednisone regimen. He also received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. This aggressive approach was necessary due to significant airway muscle involvement and severe dysphagia, which required nasogastric tube feeding for two months.

A critical component of managing dermatomyositis, particularly in adults, is malignancy screening. Anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis carries a risk of malignancy (reported around 18% in literature reviews). This patient had multiple high-risk factors, including dysphagia and the specific antibody profile. He underwent comprehensive screening including CT scans, PET scans, and plans for gastroscopy and colonoscopy. Fortunately, no malignancy was identified, but surveillance remains essential.

### Clinical Pearls

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for dermatomyositis in patients with unexplained edema, particularly when accompanied by muscle weakness. If the angioedema workup is negative, look at the skin and the muscles.

*   **Check the CK:** In any patient with unexplained swelling and weakness, a creatine kinase level is a low-cost, high-yield test. A level in the thousands should immediately trigger a workup for myositis.
*   **Inspect the Nails:** Don't skip the nailfold exam. Periungual erythema, telangiectasias, and capillaroscopy changes (avascular areas, microhemorrhages) are specific cutaneous markers for dermatomyositis.
*   **Recognize Pseudoangioedema:** Neck swelling in dermatomyositis is rare but can mimic angioedema. It is caused by muscle inflammation rather than mediator release and will not respond to antihistamines or epinephrine.
*   **Screen for Cancer:** Adult-onset dermatomyositis is paraneoplastic in a significant portion of cases. Patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies and dysphagia are at high risk and require thorough malignancy screening.
*   **Treat Aggressively:** Once diagnosed, dermatomyositis requires prompt immunosuppression to prevent respiratory failure and multiorgan damage. Early involvement of rheumatology or immunology is crucial.

In the end, this patient recovered well, with strength returning and swelling resolving over several months. But the initial presentation was a trap for the unwary. By looking past the neck and examining the nails, the team avoided a diagnostic dead end and initiated life-saving treatment. In the ED, the hands often tell the story the throat is too swollen to speak.



# SAE1 Positive: Recognizing a High-Risk Dermatomyositis Phenotype

You see a patient with significant neck swelling. The airway is stable, but the face is puffy, and the neck looks like it’s about to give out. Your differential immediately screams angioedema, Ludwig’s angina, or deep neck space infection. You call ENT. You order the CT. You clear the infection. You clear the structural causes. Now you’re staring at idiopathic angioedema. But what if the swelling isn’t histamine or bradykinin-mediated? What if it’s muscle inflammation masquerading as soft tissue edema?

This is the danger of dermatomyositis (DM) presenting as pseudoangioedema. It is a rare presentation, but missing it can be catastrophic. A recent case report highlights a 40-year-old male who presented with progressive neck swelling and proximal weakness. He was initially worked up for deep neck infection and then idiopathic angioedema. The turning point wasn’t the swelling itself, but the subtle nailfold changes and a creatine kinase (CK) level that skyrocketed to 16,184 U/L. He was eventually diagnosed with anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis.

This case isn’t just about a diagnostic pivot; it’s about recognizing a specific, high-risk subset of inflammatory myopathy. The presence of anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies signals a phenotype that demands aggressive monitoring and screening. When you see this antibody, you aren’t just treating muscle weakness; you are managing a patient at significant risk for severe dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and malignancy.

## The Masquerade of Pseudoangioedema

In the case described, the patient’s neck swelling was diffuse, involving the strap muscles and extending to the retropharyngeal space on CT. Because he was afebrile and lacked classic signs of infection, the team initially leaned toward angioedema. However, the clinical picture didn’t fit the typical profile. There was no history of ACE inhibitor use, no personal or family history of hereditary angioedema, and complement levels (C3, C4, C1 esterase) were normal.

The clue lay in the physical exam beyond the neck. While classic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rashes were absent, the patient had generalized facial erythema, periungual erythema, and nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy. These are the hallmarks of connective tissue disease. The swelling was actually subcutaneous tissue edema secondary to active myositis in the neck muscles, rather than a vascular permeability issue. This distinction is critical because treating this with epinephrine or antihistamines will do nothing, while delaying immunosuppression can lead to airway compromise from muscle weakness and aspiration.

## The SAE1 Phenotype: Why It Matters

Identifying the specific myositis antibody is not just academic; it changes your management strategy. This patient tested positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies with a high signal intensity. In the literature, SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is associated with a distinct clinical profile that is often more severe than classic DM.

Data from large cohort reviews indicate that patients with SAE1 antibodies have a 46% rate of dysphagia and a 43% rate of interstitial lung disease (ILD). In this specific case, the dysphagia was severe enough to require nasogastric tube feeding for two months post-discharge. The patient also had pulmonary nodules and consolidation attributed to aspiration pneumonitis, which resolved with treatment but highlighted the respiratory vulnerability of this phenotype.

Furthermore, the malignancy risk is substantial. According to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines, this patient was considered high risk for malignancy due to the combination of dermatomyositis diagnosis, dysphagia, male sex, and SAE1 positivity. Malignancy occurs in approximately 18% of SAE1-positive cases. Consequently, comprehensive screening including CT chest, abdominal imaging, and planned endoscopy is mandatory, even if the initial workup is negative.

## Clinical Management and Outcomes

The treatment for this phenotype requires a multi-modal, aggressive approach. In this case, the patient received a pulse dose of intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by a tapering oral prednisone regimen. Given the severity of the muscle involvement and dysphagia, steroid-sparing agents were added early. The regimen included mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG).

The response was significant but required time. Proximal muscle strength recovered within three weeks of discharge, and CK levels normalized. However, the dysphagia persisted longer, requiring continued NG tube support. By 12 months, the patient had stable respiratory function, and the cervical swelling had completely resolved. The key takeaway here is that while the acute swelling responds relatively quickly to immunosuppression, the functional recovery of the pharyngeal muscles can be slow, necessitating nutritional support and airway vigilance during the treatment phase.

## The Bottom Line

Dermatomyositis is a rare diagnosis, but it is a critical one to consider when "angioedema" is unexplained. The presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies identifies a high-risk patient population that requires specific vigilance.

*   **Consider DM in unexplained neck swelling:** If angioedema workup is negative and there are subtle cutaneous signs (nailfold changes, erythema), check a creatine kinase level immediately.
*   **Recognize the SAE1 risk profile:** Anti-SAE1 positive patients have a high association with severe dysphagia (46%), interstitial lung disease (43%), and malignancy (18%).
*   **Screen aggressively for cancer:** Follow IMACS guidelines for malignancy screening in these patients, as they are considered high risk based on antibody status and clinical features.
*   **Treat early and aggressively:** Severe myositis with airway involvement requires prompt immunosuppression with steroids, IVIG, and steroid-sparing agents to prevent permanent muscle damage and aspiration complications.
*   **Monitor for functional recovery:** Even with biochemical improvement, dysphagia may persist for months, requiring nutritional support and speech therapy during the recovery period.



# Airway Alert: Dysphagia and Aspiration Risk in Inflammatory Myopathy

## The Angioedema Mimic That Threatened the Airway

We often think of neck swelling in the emergency department as an airway emergency driven by infection or histamine. Ludwig’s angina, epiglottitis, or angioedema top the differential list. But what if the swelling isn’t an infection, and the airway threat isn’t just external compression? Consider a case where a previously healthy male in his 40s presented with progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. The initial impression was Ludwig’s angina, followed by a strong suspicion of idiopathic angioedema when infection was ruled out. It wasn’t until the clinical team looked closer at the skin and checked a creatine kinase level that the diagnosis shifted from a dermatological or immunological curiosity to a systemic inflammatory crisis. This patient had dermatomyositis, and the neck swelling was actually inflammatory myositis of the strap muscles masquerading as pseudoangioedema.

The real danger in this case, however, wasn’t just the swelling itself, but the bulbar involvement that followed. While the neck edema grabbed the initial attention, the patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia that put him at immediate risk for aspiration. A barium swallow study revealed severe pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. His chest CT already showed patchy peribronchial consolidation likely from aspiration pneumonitis before the diagnosis was even confirmed. This highlights a critical lesson: in inflammatory myopathies, the airway can be compromised not just by external compression, but by the failure of the swallowing mechanism itself.

## Recognizing the Subtle Signs of Bulbar Dysfunction

In this patient, the dysphagia was not the presenting complaint, but it was a defining feature of his morbidity. He presented with a 3-week history of neck swelling and hip flexor weakness, yet the swallowing dysfunction was severe enough to require a nasogastric tube for two months post-discharge. The physical exam offered clues that were initially missed amidst the workup for angioedema. There were increased secretions and coughing during flexible nasendoscopy, which are often dismissed as benign irritants but can signal bulbar weakness. Furthermore, the patient had subtle cutaneous signs, including nailfold microhemorrhages and periungual erythema, alongside a significantly elevated creatine kinase level of 16,184 U/L.

When you see unexplained neck swelling combined with muscle weakness, you must broaden your differential beyond infection and angioedema. The presence of dysphagia in the setting of inflammatory myopathy is a red flag for aspiration risk. In this case, the patient was anti-SAE1 antibody positive, a subtype associated with cutaneous features and a significant risk of interstitial lung disease and dysphagia. The inflammation wasn't just in the skeletal muscle; it was affecting the muscles of the head and neck, leading to the "pseudoangioedema" and the swallowing failure. Ignoring the swallowing assessment in a patient with suspected myositis can lead to recurrent aspiration, pneumonia, and prolonged hospitalization.

## Managing the Airway and Immunologic Storm

Once the diagnosis of dermatomyositis was confirmed via muscle biopsy and antibody panel, the treatment focused on rapid immunosuppression and airway protection. The patient received high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days, followed by a tapering oral regimen. Because of the severity of the disease and the airway involvement, he was also treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and mycophenolate mofetil, along with rituximab. However, pharmacotherapy alone was not enough to secure the airway. Due to persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge, oral medications were administered through a nasogastric tube.

Nutritional support and airway protection were paramount. The patient required NG tube feeding for an additional two months after discharge until swallowing function improved sufficiently. This underscores the necessity of early involvement of speech and language therapy and the willingness to place feeding tubes when dysphagia is identified. The aspiration pneumonitis seen on his initial chest CT eventually resolved with treatment, but the window for prevention was narrow. By 12 months, the patient had stable respiratory function and good exercise tolerance, demonstrating that aggressive management of both the immune system and the nutritional status leads to favorable outcomes.

## Take-Home Points

*   Dermatomyositis can present as non-histamine and non-bradykinin-mediated neck swelling (pseudoangioedema) due to inflammatory myositis of the strap muscles.
*   Dysphagia in inflammatory myopathy is a high-risk feature that can lead to aspiration pneumonitis and requires early assessment with swallow studies.
*   Unexplained neck swelling combined with proximal muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase should trigger a workup for autoimmune myopathy, including myositis antibody panels.
*   Severe bulbar involvement may necessitate nasogastric tube placement for nutritional support and medication administration until swallowing function recovers.
*   Anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis is associated with cutaneous features, dysphagia, and interstitial lung disease, requiring aggressive immunosuppression and malignancy screening.



# Paraneoplastic Dermatomyositis: When to Screen for Cancer

## The Angioedema Red Herring

Imagine a middle-aged male presenting to the emergency department with severe, progressive neck swelling. Your mind immediately races through the differentials: Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space infection, or perhaps idiopathic angioedema. In the case of a 40-year-old male described by Chan et al., this is exactly where the diagnostic journey began. He was reviewed by ENT surgeons, underwent CT imaging showing diffuse neck oedema involving the strap muscles, and was even transferred to a quaternary hospital for suspected angioedema. However, the clinical picture didn't quite fit. There was no response to standard angioedema management, and a closer look revealed subtle nailfold changes and proximal muscle weakness.

The creatine kinase (CK) level came back at a staggering 16,184 U/L. This patient didn't have angioedema; he had dermatomyositis (DM). While the dramatic presentation of neck swelling masquerading as angioedema is the headline of this case, there is a critical, life-or-death implication lurking beneath the surface that every clinician needs to recognize. When you diagnose dermatomyositis, you are not just treating muscle inflammation; you are potentially uncovering a paraneoplastic syndrome. This case highlights a crucial intersection where rheumatology, oncology, and emergency medicine collide: the malignancy screening protocol for new-onset dermatomyositis.

## The Malignancy Risk in Dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy with a strong association with underlying malignancy. In the literature review cited within this case, malignancy occurred in 18% of patients with anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis. This is not a rare coincidence; it is a frequent enough complication to warrant a structured, aggressive screening approach. In our patient, the diagnosis was confirmed via muscle biopsy showing perifascicular atrophy and complement deposition, alongside a positive anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibody.

The presence of specific clinical features and antibody profiles can stratify the risk of an occult cancer. The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) provides guidelines for this exact scenario. In this specific case, the patient was deemed high risk for malignancy based on a combination of factors. The guidelines identified two high-risk factors present in this patient: the diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself and the presence of dysphagia. Additionally, he carried two intermediate-risk factors: being male and testing positive for the SAE1 antibody. Recognizing these risk stratifiers is the first step in determining the intensity of your screening workup.

## Executing the Screening Protocol

Once the patient was identified as high risk, the team initiated a comprehensive malignancy screening protocol. This wasn't just a basic physical exam; it involved a multimodal approach designed to catch occult disease early. The patient underwent CT imaging of the chest and abdomen, which showed non-cavitating pulmonary nodules and peribronchial consolidation initially thought to be aspiration pneumonitis. To further evaluate for metabolically active disease, a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan was performed. This scan revealed diffuse abnormal FDG accumulation in multiple muscle groups but, crucially, no metabolically active disease elsewhere to suggest a malignancy at that time.

Laboratory screening included a panel of tumor markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), all of which were normal. Despite the negative initial imaging and labs, the protocol did not stop there. Given the high-risk stratification, the plan included a gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy. The patient remains under close surveillance with serial CT chest imaging and regular blood tests. This case underscores that a negative initial screen does not mean the workup is complete; ongoing monitoring is essential for patients with inflammatory myopathies.

## Clinical Management and Prognosis

While the focus of this blog is on screening, it is worth noting the aggressive immunosuppression required to manage the underlying disease, which can complicate the cancer picture. This patient received a 3-day course of intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily, followed by a prednisone taper. He also received loading dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. The treatment was successful, with proximal muscle strength recovering fully and CK normalizing to 37 U/L within three weeks of discharge. However, the dysphagia persisted for months, requiring nasogastric tube feeding. The resolution of the neck swelling and normalization of labs occurred, but the malignancy risk remained the primary concern for long-term follow-up. At 12 months, respiratory function was stable, and no malignancy had been identified, though surveillance continues.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   Dermatomyositis presenting with neck swelling can masquerade as angioedema; always check CK levels and look for subtle cutaneous signs like periungual erythema.
*   New-onset dermatomyositis carries a significant risk of underlying malignancy, requiring risk stratification using IMACS guidelines.
*   High-risk factors for malignancy include the diagnosis of dermatomyositis and dysphagia; intermediate factors include male sex and specific antibody positivity (e.g., SAE1).
*   A comprehensive screening workup for high-risk patients should include CT chest/abdomen, PET scan, tumor markers, and age-appropriate endoscopic evaluation (gastroscopy/colonoscopy).
*   Negative initial screening does not eliminate risk; patients require ongoing surveillance with serial imaging and clinical monitoring.



# Rescue Therapy: Managing Severe Dermatomyositis with IVIG and Rituximab

Imagine a 40-year-old male presenting to the emergency department with severe neck swelling and dysphagia. The differential diagnosis immediately floods with airway emergencies: Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space infection, or idiopathic angioedema. CT scans show diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. ENT clears infection, immunology suspects angioedema, but the clinical picture doesn't fit. There is no response to antihistamines, and the swelling is non-pitting and non-tender. This isn't just a throat issue; it is a systemic autoimmune storm masquerading as an airway emergency. This case highlights a critical diagnostic trap where dermatomyositis presents as pseudoangioedema, but more importantly, it serves as a blueprint for managing severe, life-threatening inflammatory myopathy when standard steroids aren't enough.

The patient in this case was a previously well male who ultimately tested positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) antibodies, confirming a diagnosis of dermatomyositis. The severity of his presentation was undeniable. His creatine kinase (CK) level skyrocketed to 16,184 U/L, and he suffered from significant proximal muscle weakness and severe pharyngeal dysphagia requiring nasogastric tube feeding. The presence of neck swelling threatened his airway, and the muscle inflammation was widespread, confirmed by MRI and PET scan. When you face dermatomyositis with this degree of systemic involvement, particularly with dysphagia and respiratory muscle threat, monotherapy with corticosteroids is often insufficient to halt progression quickly enough.

The treatment strategy employed in this case illustrates a robust, multi-agent immunosuppressive approach designed to induce rapid remission while minimizing long-term steroid toxicity. The patient received a pulse dose of intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days, followed by a tapering oral prednisone regimen. However, the cornerstone of his recovery involved the addition of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and rituximab. He received a loading dose of IVIG at 2 g/kg over five days, followed by five additional cycles four weeks apart. This aggressive dosing was chosen to rapidly modulate the immune response, particularly given the dysphagia and severe myositis. Concurrently, he was started on mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily as a steroid-sparing agent.

Rituximab was added to the regimen to target B-cell depletion, a strategy increasingly supported for refractory or severe cases of dermatomyositis. The patient received two doses of 1000 mg intravenously, spaced two weeks apart. This combination of IVIG, rituximab, and mycophenolate alongside high-dose steroids represents a "rescue" level of therapy for severe inflammatory myopathy. Notably, methotrexate was explicitly avoided in this patient. Despite being a common steroid-sparing agent, the team bypassed it due to the patient's abnormal lung function tests and the known association between methotrexate and interstitial lung disease or pulmonary fibrosis. This decision underscores the importance of tailoring immunosuppression to the patient's specific organ involvement.

Safety and prophylaxis are paramount when deploying this level of immunosuppression. The patient was started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis but developed hemolytic anemia, necessitating a switch to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks. This highlights that standard prophylaxis protocols must be monitored closely for adverse effects, even in the absence of known metabolic deficiencies like G6PD. Furthermore, the diagnostic workup included extensive malignancy screening. With anti-SAE1 positivity, dysphagia, and male sex, the patient was categorized as high risk for paraneoplastic disease. He underwent CT chest, PET scans, and plans for endoscopy, reinforcing that severe dermatomyositis often requires a cancer screen as part of the initial management bundle.

The outcome of this aggressive regimen was favorable but required patience. The patient was hospitalized for 21 days and required nasogastric feeding for an additional two months post-discharge due to persistent dysphagia. However, by three months post-discharge, his cervical swelling had completely resolved, and his proximal muscle strength was stable. By 12 months, his respiratory function remained stable, and he had successfully discontinued prednisone. This trajectory demonstrates that while the recovery from severe dermatomyositis can be protracted, early recognition and aggressive multi-modal immunosuppression can lead to full functional recovery.

*   **Recognize the Mimic:** Severe neck swelling and dysphagia in dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema or deep neck infection; a high index of suspicion is required when standard airway treatments fail.
*   **Treat Aggressively:** Severe cases with dysphagia, airway threat, or massive CK elevation require more than steroids; consider early combination therapy with IVIG and rituximab.
*   **Tailor Steroid-Sparing Agents:** Avoid methotrexate in patients with interstitial lung disease or abnormal pulmonary function tests; mycophenolate mofetil is a viable alternative.
*   **Monitor Prophylaxis:** PJP prophylaxis is essential with multi-agent immunosuppression, but be prepared to switch agents if adverse reactions like hemolytic anemia occur.
*   **Screen for Malignancy:** Patients with anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis and dysphagia are high risk for underlying malignancy and require comprehensive cancer screening.
