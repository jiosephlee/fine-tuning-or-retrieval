Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenge

The clinical presentation of inflammatory myopathies can be highly variable, often leading to diagnostic delays when symptoms mimic more common conditions. In this case, a previously healthy male in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting the hip flexors, accompanied by mild myalgia. This constellation of symptoms created a high-stakes diagnostic environment, as neck swelling in the emergency setting necessitates the immediate exclusion of life-threatening airway compromises. The patient was afebrile and maintaining normal oxygen saturation on room air, yet the progression of swelling warranted urgent investigation. The initial clinical impression favored Ludwig’s angina, a severe cellulitis of the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces, due to the location of the swelling and the potential for rapid airway obstruction. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck supported this concern by revealing diffuse neck oedema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space. However, the absence of fever and the patient’s stable respiratory status prompted a broader differential diagnosis once deep neck space infection was clinically cleared.

Following the exclusion of an infectious etiology, the diagnostic focus shifted to idiopathic angioedema, particularly given the non-tender nature of the swelling and the lack of systemic signs of sepsis. The patient was transferred to a quaternary hospital with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgery services for further evaluation. The role of the ENT specialist was critical in ruling out structural causes; a flexible nasendoscopy demonstrated no significant anatomical abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space. While there were increased secretions and coughing, suggesting pharyngeal involvement, there was no evidence of a mass or obstruction that would explain the swelling structurally. Concurrently, the case was discussed with clinical immunology to evaluate for histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. The immunological review noted the absence of common precipitating factors, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and the patient had no personal or family history of angioedema. Standard investigations for hereditary or acquired angioedema, including C3, C4, and C1 esterase function, were within normal ranges, effectively ruling out these specific pathophysiological mechanisms.

Despite the negative workup for common causes of angioedema, the clinical team identified subtle physical examination findings that served as critical red flags for an underlying systemic autoimmune process. A thorough examination revealed generalised erythema of the face, specifically over the nasolabial folds, and an erythematous, swollen, and non-tender neck. More importantly, the patient exhibited subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema. Nailfold capillaroscopy was performed, revealing proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, which are hallmark microvascular abnormalities associated with connective tissue diseases such as dermatomyositis. Additionally, a rash was noted on the right lateral thigh, and motor examination demonstrated reduced hip flexor power of 4/5. Although classic dermatomyositis cutaneous features such as Gottron’s papules and a heliotrope eruption were absent at this stage, the combination of proximal muscle weakness, specific nailfold vascular changes, and atypical facial erythema provided a crucial clue that the pathology was not purely dermatological or allergic in nature.

The clinical reasoning that ultimately shifted the diagnosis away from angioedema relied on recognizing the neck swelling as a manifestation of non-histamine and non-bradykinin-mediated inflammation. The persistence of swelling without a structural or infective cause, coupled with the presence of proximal muscle weakness, prompted the request for a creatine kinase (CK) level. This decision was pivotal, as the significantly elevated CK of 16,184 U/L confirmed severe muscle injury consistent with myositis. The elevated transaminases and lactate dehydrogenase further supported muscle breakdown rather than primary hepatic pathology. This case illustrates that in patients presenting with unexplained neck oedema, particularly when accompanied by subtle cutaneous signs or unexplained muscle weakness, clinicians must consider inflammatory myopathies in the differential diagnosis. The swelling in this context arises from active muscle inflammation and associated subcutaneous oedema rather than vascular permeability changes seen in allergic reactions, highlighting the necessity of a high index of suspicion for dermatomyositis even in the absence of classic skin rashes.
