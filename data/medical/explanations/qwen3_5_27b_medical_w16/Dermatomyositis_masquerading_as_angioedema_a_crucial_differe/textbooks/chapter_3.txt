Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Evaluation and Confirmatory Testing

The diagnostic trajectory in this case shifted from a primary concern for angioedema or deep neck infection to an autoimmune inflammatory myopathy once specific laboratory and imaging investigations were pursued. The initial suspicion of dermatomyositis was triggered by the atypical nature of the neck oedema and subtle cutaneous findings, necessitating a targeted workup to confirm muscle involvement, identify specific autoantibodies, and exclude malignancy.

**Interpretation of Creatine Kinase and Liver Transaminases**
The cornerstone of the biochemical diagnosis in this case was the measurement of serum creatine kinase (CK). In patients presenting with unexplained weakness or swelling where inflammatory myopathy is suspected, CK serves as a sensitive marker of muscle injury. This patient presented with a markedly elevated CK level of 16,184 U/L, indicating severe and active muscle breakdown. While the urine myoglobin screen was negative, suggesting the absence of frank rhabdomyolysis with renal compromise at that moment, the magnitude of the CK elevation was inconsistent with idiopathic angioedema. Concurrently, the patient exhibited elevated liver transaminases, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L. Clinicians must recognize that AST and ALT are not exclusively hepatic enzymes; they are also present in skeletal muscle. In the context of severe myositis, disproportionate elevation of AST relative to ALT often reflects muscle origin rather than primary liver pathology. Additionally, the elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 692 U/L further supported widespread tissue injury. These laboratory values collectively directed the clinical focus toward a systemic myopathic process rather than an isolated airway or dermatological disorder.

**Myositis Antibody Panel Analysis and Specificity of Anti-SAE1**
Following the biochemical evidence of myositis, serological testing was performed to classify the specific subtype of inflammatory myopathy. The patient tested positive for anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a high titre of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern, a non-specific finding common in autoimmune conditions. However, the diagnostic specificity was achieved through a myositis antibody panel. This panel identified strongly positive anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (normal range <35), while other common myositis antibodies such as anti-Jo-1 and anti-TIF1-γ were negative. Anti-SAE1 antibodies are a distinct subset associated with dermatomyositis and carry specific clinical implications. In the literature, anti-SAE1 positivity is frequently linked with a higher risk of interstitial lung disease and dysphagia, both of which were present in this patient. Identifying this specific autoantibody was crucial not only for confirming the diagnosis of dermatomyositis but also for risk stratification regarding malignancy and pulmonary complications.

**Muscle Biopsy Findings: Perifascicular Atrophy and Complement Deposition**
While serology provided strong evidence, the definitive confirmation of dermatomyositis required histological correlation. A muscle biopsy of the thigh revealed foci of necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerative myofibres arranged in a perifascicular distribution. Perifascicular atrophy is a hallmark histological feature of dermatomyositis, distinguishing it from polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy. Furthermore, immunohistochemistry demonstrated the deposition of the membrane attack complex, complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9), in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This finding indicates microangiopathy and complement-mediated damage to the capillary endothelium, which is pathognomonic for dermatomyositis. The presence of scattered perimysial inflammation consisting of dispersed histiocytes further supported the diagnosis. Together, the perifascicular necrosis and C5b-9 deposition confirmed the diagnosis of moderate to severe dermatomyositis, validating the clinical and serological suspicion.

**Utility of MRI and PET Scans in Assessing Muscle Inflammation and Malignancy Screening**
Advanced imaging played a dual role in this case: assessing the extent of muscle inflammation and screening for paraneoplastic malignancy. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs demonstrated changes consistent with active myositis in the obturator, adductor, and iliopsoas muscles, correlating with the patient’s proximal weakness. Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan provided a whole-body assessment, showing diffuse abnormal FDG accumulation within multiple muscle groups, most prominently in the head and neck. This imaging confirmed the systemic nature of the inflammation and explained the neck swelling as inflammatory myositis rather than angioedema. Crucially, the PET scan also served as a malignancy screen. Given that dermatomyositis is a paraneoplastic syndrome in a significant subset of patients, particularly those with anti-SAE1 antibodies, ruling out occult cancer is essential. The PET scan showed no metabolically active disease elsewhere to suggest malignancy, complementing the normal CT chest and abdomen findings.

**Evaluation of Dysphagia via Video Fluoroscopic Swallow Study**
Dysphagia is a critical complication of dermatomyositis that significantly impacts morbidity and mortality, primarily due to the risk of aspiration. In this patient, the presence of increased secretions and coughing during nasendoscopy prompted further functional assessment. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow study was performed and revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with significant pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. This objective evaluation confirmed that the dysphagia was due to pharyngeal muscle weakness rather than a structural obstruction. The findings necessitated immediate intervention, including nasogastric tube feeding to prevent aspiration pneumonia and ensure nutritional support. Recognizing the severity of the dysphagia was integral to the management plan, as it influenced the decision to avoid certain oral medications and guided the rehabilitation strategy during recovery.
