Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Angioedema Masquerade: Dermatomyositis as a Cause of Neck Swelling

Neck swelling in the emergency department is a high-stakes presentation. Your differential usually revolves around three scary buckets: airway obstruction from infection (like Ludwig’s angina), allergic reaction or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, or deep neck space abscesses. You secure the airway, you get a CT, and you start antibiotics or epinephrine. But what if the swelling isn't infectious, and it isn't histamine-driven? What if the edema is actually a sign of severe muscle inflammation?

This case report highlights a diagnostic trap where dermatomyositis presented as "pseudoangioedema," nearly leading to a misdiagnosis of idiopathic angioedema. It serves as a stark reminder that when the common causes don't fit the clinical picture, you need to dig deeper into the inflammatory myopathies.

## The Case Presentation

A previously healthy male in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors. He was afebrile and saturating well on room air. The initial impression was Ludwig’s angina given the severity of the swelling. A CT scan of the neck revealed diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. However, he lacked the classic fever or sepsis profile, and ENT surgeons cleared him of a deep neck space infection.

With no structural or infective cause found, the patient was referred to clinical immunology for suspected idiopathic angioedema. On review, he had generalized erythema of the face and a swollen, non-tender neck. Crucially, he denied taking ACE inhibitors or NSAIDs, and there was no history of hereditary angioedema. The clinical profile was atypical for acquired angioedema. A thorough physical exam revealed subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema and a rash on the right lateral thigh. Proximal nailfold capillaroscopy showed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas.

## The Diagnostic Pivot

The turning point came when a creatine kinase (CK) level was requested due to the atypical nature of the neck edema. The result was staggering: 16,184 U/L. His transaminases and lactate dehydrogenase were also significantly elevated. While the initial workup for angioedema showed normal complement levels and C1 esterase function, further autoantibody testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titre of 1:1280.

The patient tested strongly positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. A muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis, showing perifascicular necrosis and complement component 5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is typical of dermatomyositis. The neck swelling was not angioedema in the traditional sense but rather subcutaneous tissue edema arising from muscle inflammation in the setting of myositis.

## Clinical Implications and Management

This presentation is rare but dangerous. Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that typically affects the skin and muscles but can involve the respiratory system and gastrointestinal tract. In this case, the patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia confirmed by a barium swallow, leading to aspiration pneumonitis and the need for nasogastric tube feeding for two months. The anti-SAE1 antibody positivity is particularly notable, as it is associated with a higher risk of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease.

Because of the significant airway muscle involvement and dysphagia risk, the patient required aggressive immunosuppression. He was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by a prednisone taper, alongside intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. He was also screened extensively for malignancy, as dermatomyositis is paraneoplastic in a significant subset of patients. Fortunately, after three months, his cervical swelling resolved, CK normalized, and swallowing function recovered sufficiently to progress to soft solids.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Consider Inflammatory Myopathy in Unexplained Neck Swelling:** If a patient presents with significant neck edema that does not respond to standard angioedema management and lacks infectious signs, consider dermatomyositis as a differential diagnosis.
*   **Check the CK:** A creatine kinase level is a critical test in patients with unexplained edema, especially if there is any hint of muscle weakness or skin changes.
*   **Look for Subtle Skin Signs:** Classic rashes like Gottron’s papules or heliotrope eruptions may be absent. Look for periungual erythema, nailfold capillary changes, or subtle facial erythema.
*   **Assess for Dysphagia:** Dermatomyositis can cause severe pharyngeal muscle weakness leading to aspiration. A barium swallow may be necessary if dysphagia is suspected.
*   **Know the Antibodies:** Anti-SAE1 antibodies are associated with dermatomyositis and carry a higher risk of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease compared to other myositis antibodies.
