Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Rescue Therapy: Managing Severe Dermatomyositis with IVIG and Rituximab

Imagine a 40-year-old male presenting to the emergency department with severe neck swelling and dysphagia. The differential diagnosis immediately floods with airway emergencies: Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space infection, or idiopathic angioedema. CT scans show diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. ENT clears infection, immunology suspects angioedema, but the clinical picture doesn't fit. There is no response to antihistamines, and the swelling is non-pitting and non-tender. This isn't just a throat issue; it is a systemic autoimmune storm masquerading as an airway emergency. This case highlights a critical diagnostic trap where dermatomyositis presents as pseudoangioedema, but more importantly, it serves as a blueprint for managing severe, life-threatening inflammatory myopathy when standard steroids aren't enough.

The patient in this case was a previously well male who ultimately tested positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) antibodies, confirming a diagnosis of dermatomyositis. The severity of his presentation was undeniable. His creatine kinase (CK) level skyrocketed to 16,184 U/L, and he suffered from significant proximal muscle weakness and severe pharyngeal dysphagia requiring nasogastric tube feeding. The presence of neck swelling threatened his airway, and the muscle inflammation was widespread, confirmed by MRI and PET scan. When you face dermatomyositis with this degree of systemic involvement, particularly with dysphagia and respiratory muscle threat, monotherapy with corticosteroids is often insufficient to halt progression quickly enough.

The treatment strategy employed in this case illustrates a robust, multi-agent immunosuppressive approach designed to induce rapid remission while minimizing long-term steroid toxicity. The patient received a pulse dose of intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days, followed by a tapering oral prednisone regimen. However, the cornerstone of his recovery involved the addition of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and rituximab. He received a loading dose of IVIG at 2 g/kg over five days, followed by five additional cycles four weeks apart. This aggressive dosing was chosen to rapidly modulate the immune response, particularly given the dysphagia and severe myositis. Concurrently, he was started on mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily as a steroid-sparing agent.

Rituximab was added to the regimen to target B-cell depletion, a strategy increasingly supported for refractory or severe cases of dermatomyositis. The patient received two doses of 1000 mg intravenously, spaced two weeks apart. This combination of IVIG, rituximab, and mycophenolate alongside high-dose steroids represents a "rescue" level of therapy for severe inflammatory myopathy. Notably, methotrexate was explicitly avoided in this patient. Despite being a common steroid-sparing agent, the team bypassed it due to the patient's abnormal lung function tests and the known association between methotrexate and interstitial lung disease or pulmonary fibrosis. This decision underscores the importance of tailoring immunosuppression to the patient's specific organ involvement.

Safety and prophylaxis are paramount when deploying this level of immunosuppression. The patient was started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis but developed hemolytic anemia, necessitating a switch to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks. This highlights that standard prophylaxis protocols must be monitored closely for adverse effects, even in the absence of known metabolic deficiencies like G6PD. Furthermore, the diagnostic workup included extensive malignancy screening. With anti-SAE1 positivity, dysphagia, and male sex, the patient was categorized as high risk for paraneoplastic disease. He underwent CT chest, PET scans, and plans for endoscopy, reinforcing that severe dermatomyositis often requires a cancer screen as part of the initial management bundle.

The outcome of this aggressive regimen was favorable but required patience. The patient was hospitalized for 21 days and required nasogastric feeding for an additional two months post-discharge due to persistent dysphagia. However, by three months post-discharge, his cervical swelling had completely resolved, and his proximal muscle strength was stable. By 12 months, his respiratory function remained stable, and he had successfully discontinued prednisone. This trajectory demonstrates that while the recovery from severe dermatomyositis can be protracted, early recognition and aggressive multi-modal immunosuppression can lead to full functional recovery.

*   **Recognize the Mimic:** Severe neck swelling and dysphagia in dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema or deep neck infection; a high index of suspicion is required when standard airway treatments fail.
*   **Treat Aggressively:** Severe cases with dysphagia, airway threat, or massive CK elevation require more than steroids; consider early combination therapy with IVIG and rituximab.
*   **Tailor Steroid-Sparing Agents:** Avoid methotrexate in patients with interstitial lung disease or abnormal pulmonary function tests; mycophenolate mofetil is a viable alternative.
*   **Monitor Prophylaxis:** PJP prophylaxis is essential with multi-agent immunosuppression, but be prepared to switch agents if adverse reactions like hemolytic anemia occur.
*   **Screen for Malignancy:** Patients with anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis and dysphagia are high risk for underlying malignancy and require comprehensive cancer screening.
