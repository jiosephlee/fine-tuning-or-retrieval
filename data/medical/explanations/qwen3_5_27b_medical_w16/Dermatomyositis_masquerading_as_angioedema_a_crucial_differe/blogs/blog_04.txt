Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Airway Alert: Dysphagia and Aspiration Risk in Inflammatory Myopathy

## The Angioedema Mimic That Threatened the Airway

We often think of neck swelling in the emergency department as an airway emergency driven by infection or histamine. Ludwig’s angina, epiglottitis, or angioedema top the differential list. But what if the swelling isn’t an infection, and the airway threat isn’t just external compression? Consider a case where a previously healthy male in his 40s presented with progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. The initial impression was Ludwig’s angina, followed by a strong suspicion of idiopathic angioedema when infection was ruled out. It wasn’t until the clinical team looked closer at the skin and checked a creatine kinase level that the diagnosis shifted from a dermatological or immunological curiosity to a systemic inflammatory crisis. This patient had dermatomyositis, and the neck swelling was actually inflammatory myositis of the strap muscles masquerading as pseudoangioedema.

The real danger in this case, however, wasn’t just the swelling itself, but the bulbar involvement that followed. While the neck edema grabbed the initial attention, the patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia that put him at immediate risk for aspiration. A barium swallow study revealed severe pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. His chest CT already showed patchy peribronchial consolidation likely from aspiration pneumonitis before the diagnosis was even confirmed. This highlights a critical lesson: in inflammatory myopathies, the airway can be compromised not just by external compression, but by the failure of the swallowing mechanism itself.

## Recognizing the Subtle Signs of Bulbar Dysfunction

In this patient, the dysphagia was not the presenting complaint, but it was a defining feature of his morbidity. He presented with a 3-week history of neck swelling and hip flexor weakness, yet the swallowing dysfunction was severe enough to require a nasogastric tube for two months post-discharge. The physical exam offered clues that were initially missed amidst the workup for angioedema. There were increased secretions and coughing during flexible nasendoscopy, which are often dismissed as benign irritants but can signal bulbar weakness. Furthermore, the patient had subtle cutaneous signs, including nailfold microhemorrhages and periungual erythema, alongside a significantly elevated creatine kinase level of 16,184 U/L.

When you see unexplained neck swelling combined with muscle weakness, you must broaden your differential beyond infection and angioedema. The presence of dysphagia in the setting of inflammatory myopathy is a red flag for aspiration risk. In this case, the patient was anti-SAE1 antibody positive, a subtype associated with cutaneous features and a significant risk of interstitial lung disease and dysphagia. The inflammation wasn't just in the skeletal muscle; it was affecting the muscles of the head and neck, leading to the "pseudoangioedema" and the swallowing failure. Ignoring the swallowing assessment in a patient with suspected myositis can lead to recurrent aspiration, pneumonia, and prolonged hospitalization.

## Managing the Airway and Immunologic Storm

Once the diagnosis of dermatomyositis was confirmed via muscle biopsy and antibody panel, the treatment focused on rapid immunosuppression and airway protection. The patient received high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days, followed by a tapering oral regimen. Because of the severity of the disease and the airway involvement, he was also treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and mycophenolate mofetil, along with rituximab. However, pharmacotherapy alone was not enough to secure the airway. Due to persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge, oral medications were administered through a nasogastric tube.

Nutritional support and airway protection were paramount. The patient required NG tube feeding for an additional two months after discharge until swallowing function improved sufficiently. This underscores the necessity of early involvement of speech and language therapy and the willingness to place feeding tubes when dysphagia is identified. The aspiration pneumonitis seen on his initial chest CT eventually resolved with treatment, but the window for prevention was narrow. By 12 months, the patient had stable respiratory function and good exercise tolerance, demonstrating that aggressive management of both the immune system and the nutritional status leads to favorable outcomes.

## Take-Home Points

*   Dermatomyositis can present as non-histamine and non-bradykinin-mediated neck swelling (pseudoangioedema) due to inflammatory myositis of the strap muscles.
*   Dysphagia in inflammatory myopathy is a high-risk feature that can lead to aspiration pneumonitis and requires early assessment with swallow studies.
*   Unexplained neck swelling combined with proximal muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase should trigger a workup for autoimmune myopathy, including myositis antibody panels.
*   Severe bulbar involvement may necessitate nasogastric tube placement for nutritional support and medication administration until swallowing function recovers.
*   Anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis is associated with cutaneous features, dysphagia, and interstitial lung disease, requiring aggressive immunosuppression and malignancy screening.
