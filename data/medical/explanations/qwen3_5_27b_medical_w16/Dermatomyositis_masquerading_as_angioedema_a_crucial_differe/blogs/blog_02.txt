Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Look at the Nails: Cutaneous Clues to Missed Myositis

We all know the drill: a patient walks in with a swollen neck, and the differential immediately swings between Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space infection, and angioedema. Airway protection is priority number one. But what happens when the CT shows diffuse edema, the ENT clears infection, and the angioedema workup comes back negative?

In a recent case report published in *BMJ Case Reports*, a 40-year-old male presented with exactly this scenario. He had a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. The initial impression was Ludwig’s angina, followed by a suspicion of idiopathic angioedema. However, the diagnosis was actually dermatomyositis (DM) masquerading as angioedema. This case serves as a stark reminder that when the edema doesn't fit the classic histaminergic or bradykinin profile, you need to broaden your differential to include inflammatory myopathies.

The patient was afebrile and saturating well, but the CT neck revealed diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. Despite aggressive workup for infection and structural causes, the swelling persisted. The pivot point in this case wasn't a complex genetic test initially, but a simple physical exam finding and a lab value that didn't belong in an angioedema patient: the creatine kinase (CK) level.

### The Diagnostic Pivot

When the immunologist reviewed the patient, the clinical profile was atypical for acquired or hereditary angioedema. There was no history of ACE inhibitors, no family history, and complement levels (C3, C4, C1 esterase) were normal. However, a thorough physical exam revealed subtle clues that had been missed in the acute setting.

The patient had generalized erythema of the face, including the nasolabial folds, and an erythematous, swollen neck. More importantly, there were subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema and a rash on the right lateral thigh. Nailfold capillaroscopy showed proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular areas. These findings, combined with reduced hip flexor power (4/5), prompted a CK level, which returned a staggering 16,184 U/L.

This massive elevation in muscle enzymes, paired with the cutaneous findings, shifted the diagnosis from idiopathic angioedema to anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis. The muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis with perifascicular necrosis and complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries.

### Why the Nails Matter

In the emergency department, we often rush to secure the airway and order the CT, but we sometimes neglect the extremities. Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that typically involves skin and muscles, but it can affect the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million, but missing it can lead to multiorgan damage.

Pseudoangioedema is an uncommon feature of dermatomyositis, present in only about 6.6% of patients in one cohort study. When it does occur, it can masquerade as a primary otolaryngology disorder. In this case, the neck swelling was actually inflammatory edema secondary to myositis of the strap muscles, rather than vascular permeability driven by histamine or bradykinin.

The cutaneous clues are often the earliest warning signs. While classic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope eruptions are well-known, they were absent in this patient. Instead, the diagnosis relied on subtler findings:
*   **Periungual Erythema:** Redness around the nail folds.
*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy:** Dilated capillaries, microhemorrhages, and avascular areas are highly specific for connective tissue diseases like DM.
*   **The Holster Sign:** A rash on the lateral thigh, which was present in this patient.
*   **Facial Erythema:** Involvement of the nasolabial folds can be mistaken for rosacea or seborrheic dermatitis.

### Management and Malignancy Risk

Once diagnosed, the patient required prompt and intense immunosuppression to reduce disease progression. He received intravenous methylprednisolone pulses, followed by a tapering oral prednisone regimen. He also received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. This aggressive approach was necessary due to significant airway muscle involvement and severe dysphagia, which required nasogastric tube feeding for two months.

A critical component of managing dermatomyositis, particularly in adults, is malignancy screening. Anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis carries a risk of malignancy (reported around 18% in literature reviews). This patient had multiple high-risk factors, including dysphagia and the specific antibody profile. He underwent comprehensive screening including CT scans, PET scans, and plans for gastroscopy and colonoscopy. Fortunately, no malignancy was identified, but surveillance remains essential.

### Clinical Pearls

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for dermatomyositis in patients with unexplained edema, particularly when accompanied by muscle weakness. If the angioedema workup is negative, look at the skin and the muscles.

*   **Check the CK:** In any patient with unexplained swelling and weakness, a creatine kinase level is a low-cost, high-yield test. A level in the thousands should immediately trigger a workup for myositis.
*   **Inspect the Nails:** Don't skip the nailfold exam. Periungual erythema, telangiectasias, and capillaroscopy changes (avascular areas, microhemorrhages) are specific cutaneous markers for dermatomyositis.
*   **Recognize Pseudoangioedema:** Neck swelling in dermatomyositis is rare but can mimic angioedema. It is caused by muscle inflammation rather than mediator release and will not respond to antihistamines or epinephrine.
*   **Screen for Cancer:** Adult-onset dermatomyositis is paraneoplastic in a significant portion of cases. Patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies and dysphagia are at high risk and require thorough malignancy screening.
*   **Treat Aggressively:** Once diagnosed, dermatomyositis requires prompt immunosuppression to prevent respiratory failure and multiorgan damage. Early involvement of rheumatology or immunology is crucial.

In the end, this patient recovered well, with strength returning and swelling resolving over several months. But the initial presentation was a trap for the unwary. By looking past the neck and examining the nails, the team avoided a diagnostic dead end and initiated life-saving treatment. In the ED, the hands often tell the story the throat is too swollen to speak.
