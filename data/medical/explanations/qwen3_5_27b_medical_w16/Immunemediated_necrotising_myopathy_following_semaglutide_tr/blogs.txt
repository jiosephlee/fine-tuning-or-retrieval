Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Semaglutide Myopathy: The Next Safety Signal in the GLP-1 Boom?

## The GLP-1 Revolution and a New Safety Signal

We are living in the age of the GLP-1 receptor agonist. From semaglutide to tirzepatide, these drugs have transformed the landscape of obesity and type 2 diabetes management. We know the common side effects by heart: nausea, vomiting, gastroparesis, and the occasional gallbladder issue. We are also becoming increasingly aware of "sarcopenia" or muscle loss as a metabolic consequence of rapid weight loss. However, a recent case report challenges us to look beyond simple toxicity and consider a rare, aggressive autoimmune phenomenon: immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

This is the first reported case of IMNM probably associated with semaglutide use. It serves as a critical reminder that as we prescribe these medications more frequently, we must remain vigilant for novel adverse events that mimic common conditions but require vastly different management.

## The Case: A Slow Burn After a Single Dose

The patient was a female in her 60s with a history of obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). She had been evaluated for fatty liver for years and was finally initiated on semaglutide. Here is where the timeline gets interesting. Within hours of her *single* 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose, she developed severe nausea and vomiting, leading to a hospitalization for acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis. At that initial admission, her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at 337 U/L. She was treated with hydration, recovered, and was discharged.

However, the story didn't end there. Over the subsequent four months, she developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. She deteriorated to the point where she could not stand or sit upright without assistance. When she presented to a tertiary center, her CK had skyrocketed to 3992 U/L. There was no rash, no skin changes, and no history of statin use. She had not taken any other doses of semaglutide.

## Distinguishing IMNM from Rhabdomyolysis

The core clinical challenge in this case was differentiating between direct drug toxicity (rhabdomyolysis) and an immune-mediated process. We recently saw a case report of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis where the patient had a massive CK elevation that resolved within days of stopping the drug and hydration. That is the classic toxic myopathy picture.

This patient presented differently. Her CK elevation was progressive over months, not acute and self-limiting. More importantly, she required immunosuppression to recover. The muscle biopsy was the clincher. It revealed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with myophagocytosis (macrophages eating muscle fibers) and membrane attack complex (MAC) positivity. There was a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates, which is the hallmark of IMNM.

While she tested negative for the classic IMNM antibodies (anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP), she had a low-titre positive SS-B antibody. It is worth noting that roughly one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative, making the biopsy and clinical course paramount for diagnosis.

## Management and Outcome

The treatment approach mirrored that of idiopathic IMNM but with a crucial difference in the long-term outlook. She received pulse methylprednisolone (1000 mg IV daily for 5 days), followed by oral prednisone (1 mg/kg) and a course of IVIG (2 g/kg). The response was dramatic. Her CK levels normalized within two weeks, and her functional status improved steadily.

Because this was likely drug-induced, she did not require the prolonged, heavy immunosuppression often needed for idiopathic IMNM. She was successfully weaned off prednisone within two months and was off all immunosuppression for eight months by her last follow-up. She regained her baseline functional status, and her cardiac findings (which initially suggested possible myocarditis) also normalized.

## Clinical Reasoning and Pharmacovigilance

Why does this matter for the practicing clinician? As GLP-1 usage explodes, we are likely to see more rare adverse events. The temporal relationship here was strong: one dose, followed by a delayed but progressive decline. The causality was deemed "probable" based on WHO-Uppsala criteria due to the time relationship, the exclusion of other causes (like statins or connective tissue disease), and the response to withdrawal and treatment.

However, we must remain cautious. The patient had a pre-existing positive SS-B antibody and a history of liver disease, which complicates the picture. We cannot definitively rule out a smouldering autoimmune process that was unmasked by the drug. Nevertheless, the 12-fold rise in CK and the specific histology support a medication-related etiology. If you see a patient on a GLP-1 agonist presenting with progressive weakness and rising CK, do not dismiss it as simple rhabdomyolysis. Consider the autoimmune spectrum.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Differentiate Toxicity from Autoimmunity:** Direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically resolves with hydration and drug cessation. Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) progresses over weeks to months and requires immunosuppression.
*   **Know the Biopsy:** IMNM is characterized by necrotizing myofibers with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition, often with a paucity of lymphocytes.
*   **Serology Can Be Negative:** Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies; do not rule out the diagnosis based on negative serology alone.
*   **Monitor for Delayed Onset:** Adverse reactions to GLP-1 agonists may not be immediate. Progressive weakness appearing weeks to months after initiation warrants investigation.
*   **Drug-Induced IMNM May Be Treatable:** Unlike idiopathic IMNM, cases triggered by a medication may respond to a short course of immunosuppression and allow for rapid weaning once the offending agent is avoided.



# Rhabdo or Autoimmune? Differentiating Toxic vs. Immune Necrotizing Myopathy

The GLP-1 receptor agonist revolution is undeniable. With semaglutide and tirzepatide becoming household names for weight management and diabetes control, we are seeing these medications prescribed more frequently than ever before. While gastrointestinal side effects are common knowledge, and even muscle loss is a recognized phenomenon, severe myopathy is a rare but critical consideration. A recent case report challenges our understanding of drug-induced muscle injury, presenting a scenario where a single dose of semaglutide appeared to trigger a progressive, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.

This distinction matters because the management pathways diverge sharply. Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves with hydration and drug cessation, whereas IMNM requires aggressive immunosuppression. If we misclassify an autoimmune trigger as simple toxicity, we risk leaving a patient with permanent disability. Here is how to spot the difference when the CK is high and the history is vague.

## The Case: A Single Dose, A Long Decline

The patient was a female in her 60s with a history of obesity, NAFLD, and hypertension. She was started on semaglutide for weight management. Within hours of her first and only dose (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed severe nausea, vomiting, and acute renal failure requiring ICU admission for non-invasive ventilation. At that initial admission, her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at 337 U/L. She was discharged after five days once her renal function and GI symptoms stabilized.

However, the story did not end there. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced a progressive decline. She developed proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she presented to a tertiary center, she could not stand or sit upright without assistance. Her CK had skyrocketed to a peak of 3992 U/L. This was a 12-fold increase from her initial post-dose value, occurring months after the drug was stopped.

## Toxic vs. Immune: The Clinical Divergence

The critical teaching point in this case lies in the trajectory of the symptoms and the response to withdrawal. In classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, such as a case recently described with tirzepatide, the mechanism is direct toxicity. The CK spikes acutely while on the medication and normalizes within days of discontinuation and hydration. Those patients do not typically require immunosuppression.

In contrast, this patient’s course suggested an immune-mediated process. The CK elevation persisted and worsened over four months despite the drug being discontinued. The presence of progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and the specific histology on muscle biopsy pointed toward IMNM. The biopsy showed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with macrophage infiltration (CD68 positive) and membrane attack complex staining, consistent with an inflammatory response rather than simple necrosis. Notably, she was seronegative for common myositis antibodies like HMGCR and SRP, which occurs in about a third of IMNM cases, making the clinical picture and biopsy even more vital.

## Diagnostic Clues and Management

When you encounter a patient with unexplained weakness and elevated CK after starting a new medication, do not assume it is just "rhabdo" that will fix itself. Look for the red flags of IMNM. These include persistent or rising CK levels after the offending agent is stopped, bulbar symptoms like dysphagia, and failure to improve with supportive care alone. In this case, the patient required pulse dose methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a course of IVIG.

The response to treatment was rapid and robust. Her CK normalized within two weeks of immunosuppression initiation. She was able to wean off steroids over two months and had regained her baseline functional status ten months after evaluation, with no recurrence of symptoms or CK elevation. The authors note that while causality cannot be definitively proven without a rechallenge (which would be unethical), the temporal relationship and clinical response make semaglutide a probable trigger in this instance.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Time Course is Key:** Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves quickly after drug cessation, whereas immune-mediated necrotising myopathy often progresses over weeks to months despite stopping the medication.
*   **Watch the Bulbar Signs:** The presence of dysphagia and dysphonia alongside proximal weakness suggests a more severe myopathy like IMNM rather than simple drug toxicity.
*   **Biopsy Matters:** In seronegative cases with persistent weakness, muscle biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex staining supports a diagnosis of IMNM.
*   **Treat the Immune System:** Unlike toxic rhabdo, IMNM requires immunosuppression (corticosteroids and/or IVIG) to prevent permanent muscle damage.
*   **Pharmacovigilance:** As GLP-1 use expands, clinicians must remain aware that rare autoimmune triggers may exist beyond the common GI side effect profile.



# Don't Miss the Muscle: AST/ALT Elevation in the Setting of Proximal Weakness

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have taken the medical world by storm. From weight management to type 2 diabetes, drugs like semaglutide are becoming ubiquitous in outpatient clinics. While we know the gastrointestinal side effects well—nausea, vomiting, and the occasional acute renal failure from dehydration—there is a more insidious, less discussed complication that can mimic liver disease. This case highlights a rare but critical presentation where immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) masqueraded as worsening liver disease in a patient with known NAFLD.

## The Case: A Slow Burn After a Single Dose

Consider a female patient in her 60s with a history of obesity, hypertension, and biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). She had been followed for years with mild, persistent transaminase elevations consistent with her hepatic steatosis. Seeking weight management, she received a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide.

The immediate aftermath was dramatic but non-specific: nausea, vomiting, and acute renal failure requiring hospitalization. At that time, her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at 337 U/L. She recovered and was discharged. However, the story didn't end there. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced a progressive decline. She developed significant proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. She eventually presented unable to stand or sit upright without assistance.

By this second presentation, her CK had skyrocketed to a peak of 3992 U/L. Her liver enzymes were also elevated, with an AST of 191 U/L and ALT of 165 U/L. Given her history of NAFLD, the initial instinct might be to attribute these transaminase elevations to hepatic progression. However, the clinical picture of profound weakness demanded a broader differential.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Liver vs. Muscle

This case presents a classic diagnostic pitfall: the assumption that elevated AST and ALT always originate from the liver. Both enzymes are present in high concentrations in skeletal muscle. When muscle fibers break down, these enzymes leak into the serum, mimicking hepatocellular injury.

In this patient, distinguishing the source was crucial. She had a known history of NAFLD with prior biopsy confirmation, which made hepatic etiology a strong contender. However, several clues pointed toward a myopathic origin for the acute enzyme elevation:

1.  **Clinical Context:** The presence of significant proximal muscle weakness and bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysphonia) strongly suggests a neuromuscular pathology rather than a primary liver process.
2.  **CK Correlation:** The massive elevation in CK (nearly 4000 U/L) correlated temporally with the rise in transaminases. While the AST/ALT ratio can be helpful, the sheer magnitude of the CK elevation in the setting of weakness is the strongest discriminator.
3.  **Pattern of Injury:** During the myopathic presentation, her AST was higher than her ALT (191 vs 165). While not a hard rule, an AST > ALT ratio is often more suggestive of muscle injury than liver injury, especially in the context of known fatty liver where ALT is typically higher.
4.  **Response to Treatment:** Her liver enzymes normalized alongside her CK levels following immunosuppression, whereas her underlying NAFLD markers persisted without significant change.

## Confirming the Diagnosis

To confirm the suspicion of IMNM, the patient underwent a muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis. The histology was definitive for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy. The biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) and membrane attack complex staining. Notably, she was seronegative for the common autoantibodies associated with IMNM, such as anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. This is not uncommon, as approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative, making the clinical and histological correlation even more vital.

The causality with semaglutide is classified as probable. The patient had a single exposure, followed by a delayed onset of symptoms that progressed over months. Unlike typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, which resolves quickly upon discontinuation of the agent, this patient required immunosuppression (corticosteroids and IVIG) to halt the immune-mediated destruction. She responded well to a short course of treatment and avoidance of GLP-1 agonists, regaining baseline function within 10 months.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Think Muscle with Transaminases:** In any patient presenting with unexplained AST/ALT elevation, always check a CK level. If the CK is elevated, the transaminases may be of myopathic origin.
*   **Don't Rely on History Alone:** A history of NAFLD does not rule out concurrent myopathy. Chronic baseline elevations can mask acute muscle-derived spikes.
*   **Recognize IMNM:** Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy presents with aggressive proximal weakness and high CK. It is distinct from polymyositis and often requires immunosuppression rather than just drug withdrawal.
*   **Seronegative Cases Exist:** A negative myositis antibody panel does not exclude IMNM. Biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis in seronegative patients.
*   **Pharmacovigilance:** While rare, GLP-1 receptor agonists may be associated with immune-mediated myopathies. A single dose can trigger a delayed, progressive autoimmune response distinct from direct toxic rhabdomyolysis.



# Seronegative IMNM: When Antibody Panels Fail and Biopsy Saves the Day

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are the hottest ticket in metabolic medicine right now. We know the common side effects: nausea, vomiting, and yes, some loss of skeletal muscle mass. But what happens when the muscle loss isn't just atrophy, but an aggressive autoimmune destruction?

This case highlights a rare but critical pitfall in the era of weight loss drugs: Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) triggered by semaglutide. What makes this presentation particularly challenging for the clinician is that the patient was seronegative for the classic autoantibodies associated with the disease. In a setting where antibody panels are often the first line of defense for myositis, this case reminds us that when the labs say "no," the tissue might still be screaming "yes."

## The Clinical Picture: A Slow Burn After One Dose

Our patient was a woman in her 60s with obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). She received a single, low-dose subcutaneous injection of semaglutide (0.25 mg). The initial reaction was dramatic but expected for this class: severe nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis requiring ICU admission. Her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L at that time.

She recovered from the acute GI and renal insult, but the story didn't end there. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced a progressive decline. She developed significant proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she presented to a tertiary center, she could not stand or sit upright without assistance. Her CK had skyrocketed to a peak of 3992 U/L.

This trajectory is key. In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, you typically see a rapid rise and fall of CK once the offending agent is stopped. Here, the CK climbed over months despite the drug being discontinued weeks prior. This delayed, progressive necrosis is the hallmark of an immune-mediated process rather than direct toxicity.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: The Seronegative Trap

When you see a patient with proximal weakness and a CK in the thousands, the myositis panel is usually next. In this case, the panel was essentially a dead end. The patient was negative for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, Jo-1, and the full slate of classic myositis antibodies.

This is where many clinicians might stop looking for an autoimmune etiology. However, approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for the known markers. Relying solely on serology in this context can lead to a missed diagnosis or a delay in life-saving immunosuppression. The patient did have a low-titre positive SS-B antibody, but this was an unexpected finding in the absence of ANA or SS-A positivity and doesn't explain the necrotising pathology on its own.

## The Biopsy: The Definitive Answer

Because the serology was inconclusive, a muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis was performed. This is where the diagnosis was secured. The histology revealed the classic features of IMNM, distinguishing it from polymyositis or toxic myopathy.

The biopsy showed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres. Immunohistochemistry staining highlighted macrophage infiltration (CD68 positive) with myophagocytosis, indicating active cleanup of dead muscle tissue. Crucially, there was membrane attack complex (MAC) positivity on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres. This MAC deposition suggests complement-mediated injury, a specific feature of necrotising myopathy. There were only rare T-cells (CD3), which helps differentiate this from polymyositis, where endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates are prominent.

## Management and Outcome

Once the diagnosis of IMNM was established via biopsy, the treatment shifted from supportive care to aggressive immunosuppression. The patient received pulse intravenous methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone, along with a course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg.

The response was robust. Her CK levels normalized over two weeks, and she regained her baseline functional status. She was successfully weaned off prednisone over two months and remained off immunosuppression for eight months with no recurrence. Her cardiac findings, which initially suggested possible myocarditis, also normalized.

While we cannot ethically rechallenge the patient to prove causality, the temporal relationship, the exclusion of other causes, and the response to withdrawal and immunosuppression suggest a probable link between the single dose of semaglutide and the onset of IMNM.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Don't rule out IMNM based on serology alone:** Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative for HMGCR and SRP antibodies; a negative panel does not exclude the diagnosis in the right clinical context.
*   **Recognize the timeline:** Unlike toxic rhabdomyolysis which resolves quickly with drug cessation, IMNM presents with progressive weakness and rising CK over weeks to months.
*   **Biopsy is the gold standard in seronegative cases:** Look for necrotising myofibres, macrophage infiltration (CD68), and membrane attack complex (MAC) staining with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates.
*   **Consider GLP-1 agonists as a trigger:** While rare, there is emerging evidence linking GLP-1 receptor agonists to immune-mediated myopathies; maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with new-onset weakness after initiation.
*   **Treat aggressively:** IMNM requires immunosuppression (steroids and IVIG) rather than just hydration and observation, as the autoimmune process will not self-resolve.



# Drug-Induced IMNM: Why This Case Responded Faster Than Idiopathic Myositis

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are dominating the metabolic landscape, reshaping how we treat obesity and type 2 diabetes. We are well-versed in the GI side effects, and we’ve even seen reports of direct toxic rhabdomyolysis with this class. But a recent case report from Lam et al. throws a curveball that demands our attention: a single dose of semaglutide triggering immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) four months later.

Here is the kicker: unlike idiopathic IMNM, which often requires years of heavy immunosuppression, this patient achieved full remission with a short course of steroids and IVIG after simply stopping the drug. This distinction isn't just academic; it fundamentally changes how we approach management, prognosis, and steroid weaning in drug-induced myopathies.

## The Case: A Delayed Storm After One Dose

The patient was a female in her 60s with a history of obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). She was started on semaglutide for weight management. Within hours of her *first and only* dose, she developed severe nausea, vomiting, and acute renal failure requiring ICU admission. At that initial hospitalization, her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L. She recovered from the acute metabolic crisis and was discharged.

However, the story didn't end there. Over the next four months, while off the medication, she deteriorated. She developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. When she returned to the hospital, she could not stand or sit without assistance. Her CK had skyrocketed to a peak of 3992 U/L.

## Diagnosing the Seronegative Myopathy

The clinical picture was aggressive, but the serology was frustratingly quiet. The myositis panel was negative for the classic IMNM markers: anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) and anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR). She was also negative for ANA and most other connective tissue disease antibodies, aside from a low-titre positive SS-B.

This is where the muscle biopsy becomes non-negotiable. The biopsy of the vastus lateralis revealed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with myophagocytosis (macrophage staining positive for CD68). Crucially, there was a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates, which is the hallmark of IMNM compared to polymyositis. The membrane attack complex (MAC) stain was positive on necrotizing fibers, confirming complement-mediated injury.

Despite being seronegative, the histology and clinical course confirmed IMNM. The temporal relationship—a single dose followed by a 4-month lag and progressive weakness—strongly suggested the GLP-1 agonist was the trigger, even in the absence of a specific autoantibody.

## The Treatment Twist: Short Course vs. Long Haul

This is where the case offers a critical learning point for rheumatologists and emergency physicians alike. Idiopathic IMNM is notoriously difficult to treat. It is often steroid-resistant and requires prolonged, multi-agent immunosuppression (methotrexate, rituximab, etc.) for years to prevent relapse.

In this case, the patient received pulse methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 5 days), followed by oral prednisone (1 mg/kg) and a single course of IVIG (2 g/kg). She showed marked improvement within two weeks. Because the offending agent was identified and withdrawn, the autoimmune drive was effectively removed.

She was successfully weaned off prednisone over just two months. At 10-month follow-up, she had regained baseline functional status and had been off immunosuppression for 8 months. This rapid weaning would be dangerous in idiopathic IMNM but was appropriate here because the "fuel" for the fire—the semaglutide—was gone.

## Distinguishing Toxicity from Immune-Mediated Injury

It is vital to distinguish this presentation from direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. In cases of direct toxicity (like some tirzepatide reports), CK levels peak acutely and normalize within days of stopping the drug and hydration.

In this patient, the CK continued to rise over four months *after* the drug was stopped. This delayed progression indicates an immune-mediated process rather than direct toxicity. If we had treated this as simple rhabdomyolysis, she would have been discharged with hydration alone, and her condition would have worsened to a potentially fatal degree. Conversely, if we had treated this as idiopathic IMNM without considering the drug trigger, we might have committed her to unnecessary long-term immunosuppression.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Consider IMNM in the Differential:** If a patient on GLP-1 agonists presents with progressive proximal weakness and elevated CK, do not assume it is simple rhabdomyolysis, especially if symptoms persist or worsen after stopping the drug.
*   **Biopsy is Key:** Seronegative IMNM exists. A muscle biopsy showing necrotizing fibers with myophagocytosis and MAC positivity can confirm the diagnosis even when HMGCR and SRP antibodies are negative.
*   **Treatment Duration Depends on Etiology:** Drug-induced IMNM may respond to a short course of immunosuppression if the trigger is removed, unlike idiopathic IMNM which typically requires long-term maintenance therapy.
*   **Monitor the Timeline:** Direct toxic rhabdomyolysis improves within days of cessation. A progressive rise in CK over weeks to months suggests an immune-mediated mechanism requiring immunosuppression.
*   **Avoid Re-exposure:** Given the severity of the reaction, rechallenge is unethical. Patients should avoid GLP-1 receptor agonists permanently if this association is suspected.
