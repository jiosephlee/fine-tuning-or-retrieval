Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Drug-Induced IMNM: Why This Case Responded Faster Than Idiopathic Myositis

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are dominating the metabolic landscape, reshaping how we treat obesity and type 2 diabetes. We are well-versed in the GI side effects, and we’ve even seen reports of direct toxic rhabdomyolysis with this class. But a recent case report from Lam et al. throws a curveball that demands our attention: a single dose of semaglutide triggering immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) four months later.

Here is the kicker: unlike idiopathic IMNM, which often requires years of heavy immunosuppression, this patient achieved full remission with a short course of steroids and IVIG after simply stopping the drug. This distinction isn't just academic; it fundamentally changes how we approach management, prognosis, and steroid weaning in drug-induced myopathies.

## The Case: A Delayed Storm After One Dose

The patient was a female in her 60s with a history of obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). She was started on semaglutide for weight management. Within hours of her *first and only* dose, she developed severe nausea, vomiting, and acute renal failure requiring ICU admission. At that initial hospitalization, her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L. She recovered from the acute metabolic crisis and was discharged.

However, the story didn't end there. Over the next four months, while off the medication, she deteriorated. She developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. When she returned to the hospital, she could not stand or sit without assistance. Her CK had skyrocketed to a peak of 3992 U/L.

## Diagnosing the Seronegative Myopathy

The clinical picture was aggressive, but the serology was frustratingly quiet. The myositis panel was negative for the classic IMNM markers: anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) and anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR). She was also negative for ANA and most other connective tissue disease antibodies, aside from a low-titre positive SS-B.

This is where the muscle biopsy becomes non-negotiable. The biopsy of the vastus lateralis revealed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with myophagocytosis (macrophage staining positive for CD68). Crucially, there was a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates, which is the hallmark of IMNM compared to polymyositis. The membrane attack complex (MAC) stain was positive on necrotizing fibers, confirming complement-mediated injury.

Despite being seronegative, the histology and clinical course confirmed IMNM. The temporal relationship—a single dose followed by a 4-month lag and progressive weakness—strongly suggested the GLP-1 agonist was the trigger, even in the absence of a specific autoantibody.

## The Treatment Twist: Short Course vs. Long Haul

This is where the case offers a critical learning point for rheumatologists and emergency physicians alike. Idiopathic IMNM is notoriously difficult to treat. It is often steroid-resistant and requires prolonged, multi-agent immunosuppression (methotrexate, rituximab, etc.) for years to prevent relapse.

In this case, the patient received pulse methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 5 days), followed by oral prednisone (1 mg/kg) and a single course of IVIG (2 g/kg). She showed marked improvement within two weeks. Because the offending agent was identified and withdrawn, the autoimmune drive was effectively removed.

She was successfully weaned off prednisone over just two months. At 10-month follow-up, she had regained baseline functional status and had been off immunosuppression for 8 months. This rapid weaning would be dangerous in idiopathic IMNM but was appropriate here because the "fuel" for the fire—the semaglutide—was gone.

## Distinguishing Toxicity from Immune-Mediated Injury

It is vital to distinguish this presentation from direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. In cases of direct toxicity (like some tirzepatide reports), CK levels peak acutely and normalize within days of stopping the drug and hydration.

In this patient, the CK continued to rise over four months *after* the drug was stopped. This delayed progression indicates an immune-mediated process rather than direct toxicity. If we had treated this as simple rhabdomyolysis, she would have been discharged with hydration alone, and her condition would have worsened to a potentially fatal degree. Conversely, if we had treated this as idiopathic IMNM without considering the drug trigger, we might have committed her to unnecessary long-term immunosuppression.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Consider IMNM in the Differential:** If a patient on GLP-1 agonists presents with progressive proximal weakness and elevated CK, do not assume it is simple rhabdomyolysis, especially if symptoms persist or worsen after stopping the drug.
*   **Biopsy is Key:** Seronegative IMNM exists. A muscle biopsy showing necrotizing fibers with myophagocytosis and MAC positivity can confirm the diagnosis even when HMGCR and SRP antibodies are negative.
*   **Treatment Duration Depends on Etiology:** Drug-induced IMNM may respond to a short course of immunosuppression if the trigger is removed, unlike idiopathic IMNM which typically requires long-term maintenance therapy.
*   **Monitor the Timeline:** Direct toxic rhabdomyolysis improves within days of cessation. A progressive rise in CK over weeks to months suggests an immune-mediated mechanism requiring immunosuppression.
*   **Avoid Re-exposure:** Given the severity of the reaction, rechallenge is unethical. Patients should avoid GLP-1 receptor agonists permanently if this association is suspected.
