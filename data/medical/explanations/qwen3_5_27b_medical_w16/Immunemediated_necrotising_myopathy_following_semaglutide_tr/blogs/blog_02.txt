Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Rhabdo or Autoimmune? Differentiating Toxic vs. Immune Necrotizing Myopathy

The GLP-1 receptor agonist revolution is undeniable. With semaglutide and tirzepatide becoming household names for weight management and diabetes control, we are seeing these medications prescribed more frequently than ever before. While gastrointestinal side effects are common knowledge, and even muscle loss is a recognized phenomenon, severe myopathy is a rare but critical consideration. A recent case report challenges our understanding of drug-induced muscle injury, presenting a scenario where a single dose of semaglutide appeared to trigger a progressive, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.

This distinction matters because the management pathways diverge sharply. Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves with hydration and drug cessation, whereas IMNM requires aggressive immunosuppression. If we misclassify an autoimmune trigger as simple toxicity, we risk leaving a patient with permanent disability. Here is how to spot the difference when the CK is high and the history is vague.

## The Case: A Single Dose, A Long Decline

The patient was a female in her 60s with a history of obesity, NAFLD, and hypertension. She was started on semaglutide for weight management. Within hours of her first and only dose (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed severe nausea, vomiting, and acute renal failure requiring ICU admission for non-invasive ventilation. At that initial admission, her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at 337 U/L. She was discharged after five days once her renal function and GI symptoms stabilized.

However, the story did not end there. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced a progressive decline. She developed proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she presented to a tertiary center, she could not stand or sit upright without assistance. Her CK had skyrocketed to a peak of 3992 U/L. This was a 12-fold increase from her initial post-dose value, occurring months after the drug was stopped.

## Toxic vs. Immune: The Clinical Divergence

The critical teaching point in this case lies in the trajectory of the symptoms and the response to withdrawal. In classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, such as a case recently described with tirzepatide, the mechanism is direct toxicity. The CK spikes acutely while on the medication and normalizes within days of discontinuation and hydration. Those patients do not typically require immunosuppression.

In contrast, this patient’s course suggested an immune-mediated process. The CK elevation persisted and worsened over four months despite the drug being discontinued. The presence of progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and the specific histology on muscle biopsy pointed toward IMNM. The biopsy showed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with macrophage infiltration (CD68 positive) and membrane attack complex staining, consistent with an inflammatory response rather than simple necrosis. Notably, she was seronegative for common myositis antibodies like HMGCR and SRP, which occurs in about a third of IMNM cases, making the clinical picture and biopsy even more vital.

## Diagnostic Clues and Management

When you encounter a patient with unexplained weakness and elevated CK after starting a new medication, do not assume it is just "rhabdo" that will fix itself. Look for the red flags of IMNM. These include persistent or rising CK levels after the offending agent is stopped, bulbar symptoms like dysphagia, and failure to improve with supportive care alone. In this case, the patient required pulse dose methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a course of IVIG.

The response to treatment was rapid and robust. Her CK normalized within two weeks of immunosuppression initiation. She was able to wean off steroids over two months and had regained her baseline functional status ten months after evaluation, with no recurrence of symptoms or CK elevation. The authors note that while causality cannot be definitively proven without a rechallenge (which would be unethical), the temporal relationship and clinical response make semaglutide a probable trigger in this instance.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Time Course is Key:** Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves quickly after drug cessation, whereas immune-mediated necrotising myopathy often progresses over weeks to months despite stopping the medication.
*   **Watch the Bulbar Signs:** The presence of dysphagia and dysphonia alongside proximal weakness suggests a more severe myopathy like IMNM rather than simple drug toxicity.
*   **Biopsy Matters:** In seronegative cases with persistent weakness, muscle biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex staining supports a diagnosis of IMNM.
*   **Treat the Immune System:** Unlike toxic rhabdo, IMNM requires immunosuppression (corticosteroids and/or IVIG) to prevent permanent muscle damage.
*   **Pharmacovigilance:** As GLP-1 use expands, clinicians must remain aware that rare autoimmune triggers may exist beyond the common GI side effect profile.
