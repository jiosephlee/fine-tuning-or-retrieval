Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Don't Miss the Muscle: AST/ALT Elevation in the Setting of Proximal Weakness

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have taken the medical world by storm. From weight management to type 2 diabetes, drugs like semaglutide are becoming ubiquitous in outpatient clinics. While we know the gastrointestinal side effects well—nausea, vomiting, and the occasional acute renal failure from dehydration—there is a more insidious, less discussed complication that can mimic liver disease. This case highlights a rare but critical presentation where immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) masqueraded as worsening liver disease in a patient with known NAFLD.

## The Case: A Slow Burn After a Single Dose

Consider a female patient in her 60s with a history of obesity, hypertension, and biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). She had been followed for years with mild, persistent transaminase elevations consistent with her hepatic steatosis. Seeking weight management, she received a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide.

The immediate aftermath was dramatic but non-specific: nausea, vomiting, and acute renal failure requiring hospitalization. At that time, her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at 337 U/L. She recovered and was discharged. However, the story didn't end there. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced a progressive decline. She developed significant proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. She eventually presented unable to stand or sit upright without assistance.

By this second presentation, her CK had skyrocketed to a peak of 3992 U/L. Her liver enzymes were also elevated, with an AST of 191 U/L and ALT of 165 U/L. Given her history of NAFLD, the initial instinct might be to attribute these transaminase elevations to hepatic progression. However, the clinical picture of profound weakness demanded a broader differential.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Liver vs. Muscle

This case presents a classic diagnostic pitfall: the assumption that elevated AST and ALT always originate from the liver. Both enzymes are present in high concentrations in skeletal muscle. When muscle fibers break down, these enzymes leak into the serum, mimicking hepatocellular injury.

In this patient, distinguishing the source was crucial. She had a known history of NAFLD with prior biopsy confirmation, which made hepatic etiology a strong contender. However, several clues pointed toward a myopathic origin for the acute enzyme elevation:

1.  **Clinical Context:** The presence of significant proximal muscle weakness and bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysphonia) strongly suggests a neuromuscular pathology rather than a primary liver process.
2.  **CK Correlation:** The massive elevation in CK (nearly 4000 U/L) correlated temporally with the rise in transaminases. While the AST/ALT ratio can be helpful, the sheer magnitude of the CK elevation in the setting of weakness is the strongest discriminator.
3.  **Pattern of Injury:** During the myopathic presentation, her AST was higher than her ALT (191 vs 165). While not a hard rule, an AST > ALT ratio is often more suggestive of muscle injury than liver injury, especially in the context of known fatty liver where ALT is typically higher.
4.  **Response to Treatment:** Her liver enzymes normalized alongside her CK levels following immunosuppression, whereas her underlying NAFLD markers persisted without significant change.

## Confirming the Diagnosis

To confirm the suspicion of IMNM, the patient underwent a muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis. The histology was definitive for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy. The biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) and membrane attack complex staining. Notably, she was seronegative for the common autoantibodies associated with IMNM, such as anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. This is not uncommon, as approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative, making the clinical and histological correlation even more vital.

The causality with semaglutide is classified as probable. The patient had a single exposure, followed by a delayed onset of symptoms that progressed over months. Unlike typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, which resolves quickly upon discontinuation of the agent, this patient required immunosuppression (corticosteroids and IVIG) to halt the immune-mediated destruction. She responded well to a short course of treatment and avoidance of GLP-1 agonists, regaining baseline function within 10 months.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Think Muscle with Transaminases:** In any patient presenting with unexplained AST/ALT elevation, always check a CK level. If the CK is elevated, the transaminases may be of myopathic origin.
*   **Don't Rely on History Alone:** A history of NAFLD does not rule out concurrent myopathy. Chronic baseline elevations can mask acute muscle-derived spikes.
*   **Recognize IMNM:** Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy presents with aggressive proximal weakness and high CK. It is distinct from polymyositis and often requires immunosuppression rather than just drug withdrawal.
*   **Seronegative Cases Exist:** A negative myositis antibody panel does not exclude IMNM. Biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis in seronegative patients.
*   **Pharmacovigilance:** While rare, GLP-1 receptor agonists may be associated with immune-mediated myopathies. A single dose can trigger a delayed, progressive autoimmune response distinct from direct toxic rhabdomyolysis.
