Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Seronegative IMNM: When Antibody Panels Fail and Biopsy Saves the Day

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are the hottest ticket in metabolic medicine right now. We know the common side effects: nausea, vomiting, and yes, some loss of skeletal muscle mass. But what happens when the muscle loss isn't just atrophy, but an aggressive autoimmune destruction?

This case highlights a rare but critical pitfall in the era of weight loss drugs: Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) triggered by semaglutide. What makes this presentation particularly challenging for the clinician is that the patient was seronegative for the classic autoantibodies associated with the disease. In a setting where antibody panels are often the first line of defense for myositis, this case reminds us that when the labs say "no," the tissue might still be screaming "yes."

## The Clinical Picture: A Slow Burn After One Dose

Our patient was a woman in her 60s with obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). She received a single, low-dose subcutaneous injection of semaglutide (0.25 mg). The initial reaction was dramatic but expected for this class: severe nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis requiring ICU admission. Her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L at that time.

She recovered from the acute GI and renal insult, but the story didn't end there. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced a progressive decline. She developed significant proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she presented to a tertiary center, she could not stand or sit upright without assistance. Her CK had skyrocketed to a peak of 3992 U/L.

This trajectory is key. In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, you typically see a rapid rise and fall of CK once the offending agent is stopped. Here, the CK climbed over months despite the drug being discontinued weeks prior. This delayed, progressive necrosis is the hallmark of an immune-mediated process rather than direct toxicity.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: The Seronegative Trap

When you see a patient with proximal weakness and a CK in the thousands, the myositis panel is usually next. In this case, the panel was essentially a dead end. The patient was negative for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, Jo-1, and the full slate of classic myositis antibodies.

This is where many clinicians might stop looking for an autoimmune etiology. However, approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for the known markers. Relying solely on serology in this context can lead to a missed diagnosis or a delay in life-saving immunosuppression. The patient did have a low-titre positive SS-B antibody, but this was an unexpected finding in the absence of ANA or SS-A positivity and doesn't explain the necrotising pathology on its own.

## The Biopsy: The Definitive Answer

Because the serology was inconclusive, a muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis was performed. This is where the diagnosis was secured. The histology revealed the classic features of IMNM, distinguishing it from polymyositis or toxic myopathy.

The biopsy showed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres. Immunohistochemistry staining highlighted macrophage infiltration (CD68 positive) with myophagocytosis, indicating active cleanup of dead muscle tissue. Crucially, there was membrane attack complex (MAC) positivity on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres. This MAC deposition suggests complement-mediated injury, a specific feature of necrotising myopathy. There were only rare T-cells (CD3), which helps differentiate this from polymyositis, where endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates are prominent.

## Management and Outcome

Once the diagnosis of IMNM was established via biopsy, the treatment shifted from supportive care to aggressive immunosuppression. The patient received pulse intravenous methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone, along with a course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg.

The response was robust. Her CK levels normalized over two weeks, and she regained her baseline functional status. She was successfully weaned off prednisone over two months and remained off immunosuppression for eight months with no recurrence. Her cardiac findings, which initially suggested possible myocarditis, also normalized.

While we cannot ethically rechallenge the patient to prove causality, the temporal relationship, the exclusion of other causes, and the response to withdrawal and immunosuppression suggest a probable link between the single dose of semaglutide and the onset of IMNM.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Don't rule out IMNM based on serology alone:** Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative for HMGCR and SRP antibodies; a negative panel does not exclude the diagnosis in the right clinical context.
*   **Recognize the timeline:** Unlike toxic rhabdomyolysis which resolves quickly with drug cessation, IMNM presents with progressive weakness and rising CK over weeks to months.
*   **Biopsy is the gold standard in seronegative cases:** Look for necrotising myofibres, macrophage infiltration (CD68), and membrane attack complex (MAC) staining with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates.
*   **Consider GLP-1 agonists as a trigger:** While rare, there is emerging evidence linking GLP-1 receptor agonists to immune-mediated myopathies; maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with new-onset weakness after initiation.
*   **Treat aggressively:** IMNM requires immunosuppression (steroids and IVIG) rather than just hydration and observation, as the autoimmune process will not self-resolve.
