Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Pharmacology and Pathophysiology: GLP-1 Agonists and Muscle Toxicity

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, are incretin mimetics widely utilized in the management of type 2 diabetes and obesity. These agents bind to GLP-1 receptors located in the pancreas, central nervous system, and gastrointestinal tract to enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon release, slow gastric emptying, and promote satiety. In the context of this case, the patient was prescribed semaglutide for persistent obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) after failing lifestyle modifications. While the primary therapeutic goal is metabolic improvement, understanding the pharmacological profile is essential when evaluating adverse events. Although gastrointestinal disturbances are the most common side effects, the systemic nature of GLP-1 receptor distribution raises the theoretical possibility of extra-metabolic effects, including impacts on skeletal muscle, which must be distinguished from the expected physiological muscle loss associated with weight reduction.

The spectrum of musculoskeletal adverse effects associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists ranges from benign muscle mass reduction to severe myotoxicity. Weight loss induced by these agents often includes a reduction in lean muscle mass, which is generally not pathological. However, rare reports describe direct drug toxicity manifesting as rhabdomyolysis, characterized by acute muscle breakdown and significant creatine kinase (CK) elevation. A previously reported case involving tirzepatide, another GLP-1 agonist, demonstrated this toxic pattern: the patient presented with massive CK elevation that resolved rapidly within days of drug discontinuation and hydration, without the need for immunosuppression. This contrasts sharply with the current case, where the patient experienced a progressive decline over four months following a single dose, with CK levels rising 12-fold and persisting despite drug cessation. This temporal dissociation between drug exposure and symptom progression suggests a mechanism beyond direct chemical toxicity, pointing toward an immune-mediated etiology rather than simple rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct subset of inflammatory myopathy characterized clinically by aggressive proximal muscle weakness and laboratory evidence of muscle necrosis. Histologically, IMNM is defined by necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and a notable paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates, distinguishing it from polymyositis or dermatomyositis. In the case presented, the patient’s biopsy confirmed these features, including membrane attack complex deposition and macrophage staining, supporting the diagnosis of IMNM. Clinically, this condition often presents with severe proximal weakness and can involve bulbar muscles, as evidenced by the patient’s dysphagia and dysphonia. While many cases of IMNM are associated with specific autoantibodies, such as anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) or anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), a significant subset of patients, including the one described here, are seronegative. The absence of these antibodies complicates diagnosis but does not exclude the disease, particularly when histological and clinical features align.

The diagnostic reasoning in this case draws heavily on the historical precedent of statin-associated autoimmune myopathy (SAAM). Statins, commonly used in metabolic disorders, are known to trigger an autoimmune response against HMGCR, leading to IMNM that persists despite discontinuation of the offending agent. Unlike toxic myopathy, which resolves upon drug withdrawal, SAAM requires immunosuppressive therapy to halt the autoimmune process. The clinical course of the patient in this report mirrors this pattern: symptoms progressed over months after a single dose of semaglutide, and the condition only improved following treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). This parallel suggests that semaglutide may have acted as a trigger for an autoimmune response similar to statins, rather than causing direct muscle toxicity. The patient’s seronegativity for HMGCR and SRP antibodies further highlights the diagnostic challenge, as an estimated third of IMNM cases lack identifiable autoantibodies, implying potential undiscovered triggers or antibody targets.

The pathophysiology of drug-induced autoimmune reactions remains an area of active investigation, with potential mechanisms including molecular mimicry, hapten formation, or alteration of self-antigens. In this case, the causality is assessed as probable based on the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria, given the reasonable temporal relationship, the exclusion of other causes such as viral myositis or connective tissue disease, and the clinical response to withdrawal and immunosuppression. While a rechallenge would be unethical due to the severity of the reaction, the rapid resolution of symptoms upon cessation of semaglutide and avoidance of further GLP-1 receptor agonists supports the hypothesis of a drug-induced trigger. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for clinicians managing patients on metabolic therapies, as it underscores the need to monitor for progressive weakness and elevated CK levels that persist beyond the acute phase of drug exposure, distinguishing immune-mediated pathology from transient toxicity.

## Clinical Manifestations: Identifying Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy

The clinical presentation of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) can be insidious, often masked by concurrent medical issues or attributed to non-specific causes such as deconditioning. In this case, the patient’s presentation followed a distinct biphasic timeline that is critical for distinguishing an immune-mediated process from acute drug toxicity or simple physical deconditioning. Understanding the temporal relationship between medication exposure, initial adverse reactions, and the subsequent evolution of neuromuscular symptoms is essential for early recognition.

**Timeline Analysis: Acute Reaction versus Delayed Weakness**
The patient’s course began with an acute, immediate reaction to a single dose of semaglutide, characterized by nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis within hours of administration. At this initial hospitalization, her creatine kinase (CK) was only modestly elevated at 337 U/L, which is inconsistent with severe rhabdomyolysis or acute necrotising myopathy. Clinicians must recognize that direct drug toxicity often presents with immediate symptoms that resolve upon cessation of the agent. In contrast, this patient experienced a 4-month period of progressive decline after the drug was discontinued. Her CK levels eventually peaked at 3992 U/L, representing a 12-fold increase from her baseline. This dissociation between the acute gastrointestinal event and the delayed, progressive neuromuscular deterioration suggests an immune-mediated mechanism triggered by the medication, rather than a direct toxic effect. The persistence and worsening of symptoms despite drug withdrawal should prompt a search for an autoimmune etiology.

**Clinical Hallmarks of Proximal Myopathy and Bulbar Involvement**
The core clinical features of IMNM in this case were progressive proximal muscle weakness and significant bulbar involvement. The patient progressed from being fully independent to being unable to stand or sit upright without assistance over four months. This pattern of proximal weakness—difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, or lifting objects overhead—is a hallmark of inflammatory myopathies. However, the presence of dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and dysphonia (voice changes) served as critical red flags. Bulbar involvement indicates that the pathology extends beyond the limb girdles to the muscles of the pharynx and larynx, which significantly increases the risk of aspiration and respiratory compromise. In the context of new-onset proximal weakness, the emergence of dysphagia or dysphonia should trigger an urgent workup for myopathy, as these symptoms distinguish severe myopathic processes from milder conditions like polymyalgia rheumatica or simple fatigue.

**Differentiating Deconditioning from Pathological Weakness**
A common diagnostic pitfall in patients recovering from acute hospitalization is attributing ongoing weakness to deconditioning. This patient was discharged after her initial admission described as "deconditioned" following a period of non-invasive ventilation and poor oral intake. However, deconditioning typically stabilizes or improves with rest and rehabilitation; it does not manifest as progressive functional decline over months. The key differentiator in this case was the trajectory of the patient’s function. Her inability to sit upright without assistance worsened over time, and she developed new symptoms (dysphagia) that were not present during her initial recovery. Furthermore, the significant elevation in CK levels provided objective biochemical evidence of active muscle injury that cannot be explained by disuse atrophy alone. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for pathological weakness when a patient fails to improve with expected rehabilitation or demonstrates new neurological deficits after hospital discharge.

**Physical Examination Findings**
The physical examination in IMNM typically reveals symmetric proximal muscle weakness without sensory deficits or significant neurological signs outside the motor system. On evaluation at the tertiary hospital, this patient exhibited significant weakness most evident in the proximal muscle groups, while examination was otherwise negative for rashes, synovitis, or other neurological deficits. The absence of a rash helps distinguish IMNM from dermatomyositis, and the lack of sensory loss helps rule out neuropathic causes. The specific finding of proximal weakness, combined with the absence of distal weakness or sensory changes, localizes the pathology to the muscle itself. Additionally, the lack of muscle pain or tenderness on palpation is common in IMNM, further differentiating it from polymyositis or infectious myositis where myalgia may be prominent. Recognizing this specific pattern of motor deficit allows for targeted investigations, such as muscle biopsy and specific autoantibody panels, to confirm the diagnosis.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Workup and Histopathological Confirmation

Establishing the diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) in this patient required a systematic evaluation to distinguish between primary muscle disease, hepatic pathology, and potential drug toxicity. The diagnostic process began with the interpretation of laboratory values, specifically the pattern of creatine kinase (CK) elevation and transaminase levels. Upon initial presentation following the semaglutide dose, the patient’s CK was modestly elevated at 337 U/L, but over the subsequent four months, it rose dramatically to a peak of 3992 U/L. While the patient had a known history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with baseline transaminase elevations, the disproportionate rise in CK relative to the stable hepatic enzymes helped localize the primary pathology to skeletal muscle rather than the liver. In cases of significant muscle breakdown, AST and ALT can be elevated due to their presence in muscle tissue; however, in this patient, the persistence of borderline AST/ALT elevation after CK normalization later in the course suggested her transaminaemia was largely of hepatic origin, consistent with her biopsy-proven steatohepatitis. This distinction is critical to avoid misattributing muscle enzyme elevations solely to liver disease or vice versa.

Serological testing provided further diagnostic challenges and insights. A comprehensive myositis autoantibody panel was negative for the classic IMNM-associated antibodies, including anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) and anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), as well as antibodies associated with other inflammatory myopathies like Jo-1 and Mi-2. This seronegative status is a crucial teaching point, as approximately one-third of patients with IMNM lack detectable autoantibodies, which can delay diagnosis. The patient did present with a low-titre positive anti-SS-B antibody (2.4), an unexpected finding in the absence of anti-SSA or antinuclear antibodies (ANA). While this could suggest an underlying connective tissue disorder, there were no clinical features of Sjogren’s syndrome or systemic lupus erythematosus. In the context of IMNM, the absence of specific autoantibodies necessitates reliance on clinical presentation and histopathological findings rather than serology alone.

Imaging studies supported the diagnosis of an inflammatory myopathic process. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the bilateral femurs revealed mild, symmetric muscular oedema, most evident in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous oedema. This distribution of oedema in proximal muscle groups aligns with the patient’s clinical presentation of proximal weakness and helps differentiate myopathic processes from neurogenic causes, which typically present differently on MRI. Cardiac imaging, including echocardiogram and cardiac MRI, showed mild hypokinesis and myocardial oedema without late gadolinium enhancement. While these findings raised the possibility of myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy, they were not definitive for active myocardial inflammation, highlighting the need to correlate imaging with clinical symptoms and other diagnostic modalities.

The definitive confirmation of IMNM relied on the muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis. Histopathological examination demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, which is the hallmark of necrotising myopathy. Crucially, immunohistochemistry revealed acid phosphatase and CD68 staining, indicating macrophage infiltration and myophagocytosis, alongside a membrane attack complex (MAC) positive stain on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres. The presence of MAC deposition suggests complement-mediated injury, a feature characteristic of IMNM. Furthermore, the biopsy showed a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates, with only rare CD3-positive T-cells and perivascular inflammatory cells. This histological profile distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis, which typically features endomysial inflammation with CD8+ T-cells invading non-necrotic muscle fibres, and from toxic myopathies, which generally lack significant immune-mediated features like MAC deposition.

Integrating these findings allowed for the exclusion of alternative diagnoses and the confirmation of a drug-induced etiology. The differential diagnosis primarily considered toxic myopathy versus autoimmune myopathy. In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, such as cases previously reported with tirzepatide, CK levels typically normalize rapidly within days of drug discontinuation. In contrast, this patient experienced a progressive 12-fold increase in CK over four months despite the cessation of semaglutide, and the biopsy demonstrated immune-mediated necrosis rather than simple toxic degeneration. Additionally, the patient’s rapid and sustained response to immunosuppression with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) supports an autoimmune mechanism over a purely toxic one. The combination of progressive weakness, specific MRI oedema patterns, seronegative status, and biopsy-proven necrosis with MAC deposition confirmed the diagnosis of IMNM, likely precipitated by the single dose of semaglutide.

## Management Strategies: Immunosuppression and Monitoring

The decision to initiate immunosuppressive therapy in this case was driven by the specific diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) rather than simple drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. While direct toxicity from semaglutide was initially suspected due to the temporal relationship, the patient’s clinical trajectory—characterized by progressive proximal weakness and a 12-fold rise in creatine kinase (CK) over four months despite drug cessation—indicated an autoimmune process. This distinction is critical for management, as drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically resolves with hydration and withdrawal of the offending agent, whereas IMNM requires immunomodulation to halt muscle necrosis. In this case, the muscle biopsy confirmed necrotising myofibres with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition, providing the histological justification for aggressive immunosuppression. Consequently, the treatment protocol began with high-dose corticosteroids to rapidly suppress the inflammatory cascade. The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for five days, a pulse regimen designed to achieve immediate therapeutic concentrations. Following this acute phase, she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily to maintain immunosuppression while allowing for outpatient management.

Given the severity of the patient's presentation, including dysphagia, dysphonia, and an inability to stand or sit without assistance, adjunctive therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was incorporated into the management plan. IVIG is often reserved for severe or refractory cases of myopathy where rapid functional recovery is necessary or when corticosteroids alone may be insufficient. In this instance, IVIG was administered at a dose of 2 g/kg split over four days. The rationale for combining IVIG with corticosteroids in this specific case was to address the profound weakness and severe features that posed immediate risks to airway and mobility. Notably, unlike idiopathic IMNM which often requires prolonged and intensive immunosuppressive regimens, this patient did not receive additional courses of IVIG. This deviation from standard chronic IMNM protocols highlights a key management principle in drug-induced cases: once the offending agent is removed and the initial inflammatory surge is controlled, the disease course may be more self-limited, reducing the need for maintenance immunotherapy.

Monitoring therapeutic response relied heavily on serial laboratory values and functional assessments. The most objective marker of muscle injury in this case was the serum CK level, which peaked at 3992 U/L prior to treatment initiation. A marked improvement in CK was observed over the course of two weeks following the start of immunosuppression, serving as an early indicator of treatment efficacy. Beyond biochemical markers, clinical monitoring focused on the restoration of functional status, specifically the patient's ability to perform activities of daily living, swallow safely, and regain independence in ambulation. Additionally, because the patient presented with concurrent cardiac abnormalities, including elevated high-sensitivity troponin and pro-B-type natriureptide (proBNP) levels, cardiac monitoring was integrated into the follow-up plan. Subsequent echocardiograms and cardiac MRIs confirmed the normalization of these parameters, suggesting that the systemic inflammatory response had subsided. However, safety monitoring must also account for the risks of immunosuppression; twelve days after discharge, the patient was readmitted for septic shock from a urinary tract infection, underscoring the necessity of vigilance for opportunistic infections during treatment.

The strategy for weaning off immunosuppression in this case was more aggressive than typically seen in idiopathic IMNM, reflecting the likely drug-induced etiology. The patient was successfully weaned off prednisone rapidly over the course of two months, a timeline that contrasts with the prolonged tapering often required for autoimmune conditions that lack a clear trigger. By the ten-month follow-up, she had been off all immunosuppression for eight months and had regained her baseline functional status with persistently normal CK levels. This outcome reinforces the clinical lesson that in suspected drug-induced IMNM, a shorter course of immunosuppression may be sufficient if the trigger is strictly avoided. The management framework established here prioritizes rapid control of inflammation to prevent permanent muscle damage, followed by a cautious but potentially expedited taper once clinical and biochemical remission is achieved, all while balancing the risks of infection and the benefits of long-term functional recovery.

## Clinical Implications: Causality Assessment and Pharmacovigilance

Establishing a causal link between a medication and a rare adverse event requires structured assessment rather than reliance on temporal coincidence alone. In this case, the authors applied the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) causality categories to evaluate the relationship between semaglutide and the immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The assessment concluded a "probable" causality based on a reasonable time relationship between the single dose of semaglutide and symptom onset, the unlikelihood of IMNM being due to other diseases or drugs given the negative autoimmune panel and lack of statin use, and a clinically reasonable response upon withdrawal of the agent. This framework is essential for clinicians to systematically evaluate potential drug-induced adverse events, particularly when dealing with novel medication classes where long-term safety profiles are still evolving. By adhering to standardized criteria, clinicians can distinguish between likely drug effects and coincidental occurrences, facilitating more accurate reporting and future risk assessment.

Differentiating direct drug toxicity from drug-induced autoimmunity is critical for determining the appropriate management and prognosis. In this case, the clinical course distinguished IMNM from direct toxic rhabdomyolysis. While a recent case involving tirzepatide demonstrated typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis with acute creatine kinase (CK) elevation that resolved within days of discontinuation and hydration, this patient exhibited a progressive decline over four months with a 12-fold increase in CK despite being off the medication. Furthermore, the patient required immunosuppression with corticosteroids and IVIG for improvement, whereas direct toxicity typically resolves with supportive care alone. This distinction underscores that while GLP-1 receptor agonists may cause muscle mass loss or acute rhabdomyolysis, they may also trigger an autoimmune mechanism that persists and progresses without specific immunomodulatory treatment. Recognizing this pattern prevents the misclassification of immune-mediated pathology as simple deconditioning or transient toxicity.

The exponential increase in the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists for obesity and type 2 diabetes necessitates robust pharmacovigilance to identify rare but severe signals like IMNM. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for myopathy in patients initiating these therapies, particularly those presenting with progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, or dysphonia. While routine monitoring of CK levels is not universally mandated for all patients, any unexplained muscle symptoms or significant transaminase elevations should prompt investigation to rule out muscle breakdown versus hepatic pathology. Reporting such cases to pharmacovigilance databases is vital to aggregate data, as single case reports cannot definitively prove causality but contribute to the broader safety signal required to update prescribing guidelines. This case highlights the need for continued surveillance as the patient population exposed to these agents expands.

Finally, ethical considerations play a significant role in the evaluation of adverse drug reactions. In this case, a rechallenge with semaglutide was deemed unethical due to the severity of the adverse reaction, which included hospitalization, intensive care unit admission for respiratory support, and significant functional decline. Rechallenge is sometimes used to confirm causality but is contraindicated when the risk of severe morbidity or mortality outweighs the diagnostic benefit. The successful resolution of the patient's symptoms with immunosuppression and avoidance of the drug supports the withdrawal strategy without the need for re-exposure. This case highlights the importance of balancing the diagnostic certainty of rechallenge against patient safety when managing severe, potentially life-threatening adverse events, prioritizing the prevention of recurrence over definitive proof of causality.
