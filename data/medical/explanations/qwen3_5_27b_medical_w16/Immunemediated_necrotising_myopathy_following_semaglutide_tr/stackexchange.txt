Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Pathophysiology] What specific histological features on muscle biopsy distinguish immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from polymyositis, and how do these findings support the diagnosis in this case?

The critical histological distinction lies in the predominance of muscle fiber necrosis with macrophage-mediated clearance, rather than the endomysial T-cell invasion characteristic of polymyositis. As noted in the background of this report, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is defined by necrotising myofibres and a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates.

In this patient, the muscle biopsy confirmed this phenotype. The specimen demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres with prominent CD68 staining, indicating active myophagocytosis by macrophages. Crucially, immunohistochemistry revealed only rare CD3-positive T-cells, consistent with the required paucity of lymphocytes. Additionally, the presence of membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres further supported an immune-mediated mechanism.

These findings were essential for diagnosis in this case because the patient was seronegative for the classic IMNM autoantibodies (anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP). The biopsy phenotype of necrosis with macrophage dominance and minimal T-cell infiltration distinguished this from polymyositis, which typically presents with significant endomysial inflammation. This histological confirmation justified the initiation of immunosuppression with corticosteroids and IVIG, to which the patient responded, rather than treating it as a purely toxic myopathy or inflammatory myopathy with different histological drivers.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How does the clinical course of creatine kinase (CK) elevation and muscle weakness differentiate immune-mediated necrotising myopathy from direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis?

The critical distinction lies in the temporal trajectory of muscle injury relative to drug exposure and the response to withdrawal. In direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, muscle necrosis is driven by active toxicity; consequently, CK elevation and weakness typically peak during active treatment and resolve rapidly—often within days—upon discontinuation and supportive care. In contrast, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) involves a self-sustaining autoimmune process that persists and progresses even after the offending agent is removed.

In this case, the patient received only a single dose of semaglutide yet developed progressive proximal weakness and a 12-fold increase in CK over four months, rising from 337 U/L to a peak of 3992 U/L despite cessation of the drug. This delayed, progressive course contrasts sharply with the tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis described in the background, where CK levels normalized within days of stopping the medication without immunosuppression. Furthermore, our patient’s clinical improvement required corticosteroids and IVIG, whereas direct toxicity typically resolves with hydration alone. Thus, a progressive clinical course with rising CK levels after drug discontinuation, coupled with a response to immunosuppression, strongly favors IMNM over direct toxic rhabdomyolysis.

### [Morning Report] In a patient with significant muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase, how should elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels be interpreted regarding hepatic versus muscular origin?

In patients presenting with significant muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase (CK), elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels should initially raise suspicion for a muscular origin, as these enzymes are abundant in skeletal muscle and released during myocyte necrosis. However, distinguishing a hepatic from a muscular etiology requires careful longitudinal assessment and correlation with clinical history, rather than relying on transaminase levels alone.

In this case, the patient presented with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and markedly elevated CK, alongside elevated AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L). While transaminase elevation is a common feature of myopathy, this patient had a documented history of biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with transaminase elevation three years prior to her muscle symptoms. The critical diagnostic clue was the temporal dissociation between the muscle and liver markers: following immunosuppressive treatment, the patient’s CK levels normalized as her muscle strength improved, yet her borderline elevation in AST and ALT persisted.

This persistence of transaminase elevation after the resolution of the myopathy indicated that the AST and ALT elevation was primarily of hepatic origin, consistent with her underlying hepatosteatosis, rather than solely due to muscle breakdown. Therefore, when evaluating transaminase elevations in the context of myopathy, clinicians must consider underlying liver disease and monitor trends over time; if transaminases normalize concurrently with CK, a muscular origin is likely, whereas persistent elevation after CK normalization suggests concurrent or primary hepatic pathology.

### [Management] What clinical features in this case justified the addition of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) to corticosteroid therapy for the management of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy?

In this case, the decision to escalate therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) alongside corticosteroids was justified by the severity of the patient's clinical presentation and the persistence of symptoms despite initial high-dose steroid treatment.

Specifically, the patient exhibited profound functional impairment, characterized by the inability to stand or sit upright without assistance, alongside severe bulbar involvement manifesting as progressive dysphagia and dysphonia. These features represent high-risk indicators for significant morbidity in immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Furthermore, there was significant biochemical evidence of ongoing muscle injury, with creatine kinase levels peaking at 3992 U/L.

The case report explicitly states that IVIG was administered due to "persistent weakness and severe features." Given the aggressive nature of IMNM and the critical risk associated with bulbar dysfunction, early intensification of immunosuppression was warranted to prevent permanent disability. The patient’s subsequent marked improvement in CK levels and return to baseline functional status support the appropriateness of this therapeutic escalation in the setting of severe, steroid-refractory weakness.

### [Clinical Pearls] Beyond gastrointestinal effects and weight loss, what emerging musculoskeletal adverse effects should clinicians monitor for when prescribing glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists?

Beyond the well-established gastrointestinal side effects and weight-related muscle loss, clinicians must now consider immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) as a potential, albeit rare, adverse effect of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. While skeletal muscle mass reduction is a known consequence of rapid weight loss with these agents, and isolated cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis have been reported, this case highlights a distinct, progressive autoimmune pathology that requires specific recognition and management.

In the reported case, a patient in her 60s developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia over four months following a single dose of semaglutide. Unlike rhabdomyolysis, which typically presents with acute creatine kinase (CK) elevation that resolves rapidly upon drug cessation, this patient exhibited a 12-fold increase in CK levels over months despite stopping the medication. The diagnosis was confirmed via muscle biopsy, which demonstrated necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex positivity, consistent with IMNM. Notably, the patient was seronegative for common autoantibodies such as anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP, underscoring that IMNM can present without identifiable serologic markers.

Management of suspected GLP-1-associated IMNM involves immediate discontinuation of the agent and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. In this case, the patient responded to a course of intravenous methylprednisolone, oral prednisone, and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), regaining baseline functional status after eight months off immunosuppression. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for IMNM in patients presenting with unexplained progressive proximal weakness and elevated CK levels after initiating GLP-1 therapy, distinguishing this aggressive autoimmune process from simple deconditioning or transient rhabdomyolysis. Given the increasing prevalence of GLP-1 agonist use, pharmacovigilance for these emerging musculoskeletal complications is essential.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How does the absence of anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies impact the diagnostic classification and management approach for a patient with necrotising myopathy?

In the setting of necrotising myopathy, the absence of anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies classifies the patient as having seronegative immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). As noted in the background of this case, an estimated third of patients with IMNM are seronegative, meaning the lack of these specific autoantibodies does not exclude the diagnosis.

Diagnostic classification in this scenario relies heavily on clinical features and histopathology rather than serology. In our patient, despite a negative myositis panel—including HMGCR, SRP, and other common myositis antibodies—the diagnosis was confirmed by muscle biopsy. The biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres with myophagocytosis (macrophage/CD68 staining) and membrane attack complex positivity, while showing a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. These histological features are the hallmark of IMNM and remain diagnostic even in the absence of specific autoantibodies.

Regarding management, seronegativity itself does not mandate a different immunosuppressive regimen, but the underlying etiology significantly influences the treatment course. Idiopathic IMNM often requires prolonged and intensive immunosuppression. However, in this case, the probable association with semaglutide suggested a drug-induced etiology. Consequently, the management approach focused on withdrawal of the offending agent alongside a short course of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). The patient’s rapid improvement and successful weaning from immunosuppression within months align more closely with drug-induced IMNM than with the persistent course typical of idiopathic disease. Thus, while seronegativity presents a diagnostic challenge, identifying a potential trigger can refine the prognosis and allow for a more limited therapeutic trial.

### [Clinical Pearls] According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) criteria, what factors contributed to the assessment of 'probable' causality between semaglutide and the patient's myopathy?

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) criteria applied in this case, the assessment of 'probable' causality between semaglutide and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) relied on three specific factors. First, there was a reasonable temporal relationship; the patient’s progressive muscle weakness and creatine kinase elevation developed over four months following a single dose of semaglutide. Second, alternative causes were deemed unlikely. The patient had no history of statin use or other known triggers, and her extensive autoimmune workup was largely negative, with muscle biopsy confirming IMNM features consistent with drug induction rather than idiopathic disease. Third, there was a clinically reasonable response on withdrawal. The patient demonstrated marked improvement in weakness and normalization of creatine kinase levels after discontinuing semaglutide and receiving a short course of immunosuppression. The causality was classified as 'probable' rather than 'certain' because rechallenge was considered unethical given the severity of the reaction, preventing definitive confirmation of the association. This case underscores the importance of applying structured pharmacovigilance criteria when evaluating rare adverse events to new medication classes.

### [Management] What clinical and laboratory parameters indicate that a patient with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy is ready to begin weaning immunosuppressive therapy?

The decision to initiate weaning of immunosuppressive therapy in immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) hinges on two critical parameters: the sustained normalization of muscle enzymes and the objective restoration of functional capacity. In this case, the patient was cleared for a rapid prednisone taper once she demonstrated persistently normal creatine kinase (CK) levels and continued improvement in her functional status, ultimately regaining her baseline independence in activities of daily living.

Clinically, the patient progressed from severe proximal weakness and dysphagia to full functional recovery. Biochemically, her CK, which peaked at 3992 U/L, normalized and remained stable over follow-up. While idiopathic IMNM often necessitates prolonged immunosuppression, this patient presented with probable drug-induced IMNM following a single dose of semaglutide. The withdrawal of the offending agent facilitated a robust response to a short course of corticosteroids and IVIG. Consequently, the combination of stable, normal CK levels and the absence of recurrent weakness served as the safety signal to discontinue therapy. The patient remained off immunosuppression for 8 months with no recurrence, validating that sustained biochemical remission and clinical recovery are the definitive thresholds for tapering in this context.
