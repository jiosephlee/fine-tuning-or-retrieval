Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis

The diagnostic evaluation of abdominal masses during pregnancy requires a nuanced approach that balances the limitations of imaging and laboratory testing with the urgency of maternal symptoms. In this case, the initial ultrasound revealed two large, unilocular cystic masses, leading to a primary differential diagnosis between decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. Distinguishing between these entities is critical, as their management and obstetric implications differ significantly. While the sonographic features of the upper mass mimicked a decidualised endometrioma, the lower mass appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. To refine this diagnosis, the team utilized non-contrast MRI, which is the preferred modality in pregnancy due to the safety profile regarding gadolinium contrast. The MRI provided superior diagnostic accuracy by demonstrating the anatomical continuity of the caudal mass with the uterine tissue, whereas the upper mass lacked a clear connective stalk. This imaging continuity was a pivotal finding that oriented the diagnosis toward a uterine origin for the lower lesion, despite the initial suspicion of an adnexal process. Ultimately, surgical exploration confirmed that both masses were benign uterine leiomyomas, highlighting that imaging features alone can be misleading and that anatomical continuity remains a key differentiator between ovarian and uterine pathology.

Laboratory evaluation in this scenario presented further diagnostic complexity, particularly regarding the interpretation of serum tumour markers. The patient presented with an elevated CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeded the standard cut-off of 35 U/mL and showed an increasing trend from a previous value of 36 U/mL. In the context of pregnancy, CA-125 levels physiologically rise, typically peaking in the first and third trimesters, with median values in the second trimester often lower than the observed level in this patient. While the slight elevation and upward trend initially favoured a diagnosis of endometrioma or raised concern for malignancy, the other tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) remained within normal limits. This case illustrates that isolated CA-125 measurements are not diagnostic during pregnancy; rather, serial evaluations provide useful trends for differential diagnosis. The final pathological confirmation of benign leiomyomas with ischaemic necrosis underscores that even elevated markers in this context may reflect benign degenerative processes or physiological pregnancy changes rather than neoplasia.

Beyond the anatomical and laboratory diagnosis, the clinical reasoning hinged on assessing the risk of acute complications associated with the masses. The patient presented with severe abdominal pain, low-grade fever, and leukocytosis, which, in conjunction with the MRI findings of thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid, raised suspicion for torsion, rupture, or infection. Although the patient had a concurrent urinary tract infection with *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, the surgical findings revealed that the upper mass was a pedunculated, twisted, and leaking leiomyoma, confirming that the fever and pain were likely driven by mass-related complications such as torsion and necrosis. The presence of these acute symptoms, combined with the large size of the masses (18 cm and 9.5 cm) and the restrictive dyspnoea caused by diaphragmatic compression, shifted the management strategy from conservative observation to surgical intervention. This decision was driven by the necessity to prevent further deterioration, such as sepsis or fetal compromise due to mechanical compression, which could not be managed with analgesics and positioning alone.

The final management decision required a multidisciplinary risk assessment to balance obstetric risks against surgical necessity. Generally, surgical management of uterine masses during pregnancy is delayed until after birth to avoid risks of preterm labour, haemorrhage, or uterine rupture. However, in this case, the multidisciplinary team determined that the risk of complications from the masses outweighed the risks of surgery at 21 weeks of gestation. The team opted for a laparotomy to excise the symptomatic upper mass while leaving the caudal mass in situ. This selective approach was based on the assessment that the caudal mass, while large, was fixed and less likely to cause immediate acute complications compared to the twisted upper mass. Furthermore, the decision to leave the caudal mass avoided extensive dissection that could have jeopardised the pregnancy, while the removal of the upper mass alleviated the mechanical compression causing dyspnoea. This case demonstrates that while conservative treatment is preferred, myomectomy should be considered when there is evidence of torsion, persistent symptoms, or when the mass threatens the physiological enlargement of the uterus and fetal growth.
