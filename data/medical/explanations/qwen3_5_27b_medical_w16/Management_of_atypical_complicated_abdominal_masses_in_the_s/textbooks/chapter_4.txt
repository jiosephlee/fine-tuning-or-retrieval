Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 4: ## Surgical Management and Intraoperative Safety Strategies

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention during pregnancy requires a careful balance between maternal benefit and obstetric risk. While conservative management is generally preferred for uterine leiomyomas to avoid complications such as hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, or preterm labor, surgery becomes necessary when clinical conditions worsen or the risk of acute complications increases. In this case, the indication for intervention was driven by the patient’s severe abdominal pain, dyspnoea caused by mechanical compression from the massive abdominal lesions, and the specific risk of torsion or rupture associated with the pedunculated upper mass. The second trimester, particularly around 21 weeks, is often considered the optimal window for non-obstetric surgery during pregnancy, as the risk of miscarriage is lower than in the first trimester and the uterus has not yet reached the size that complicates access in the third trimester. The multidisciplinary team determined that the risks of delaying surgery, including potential hypoxia, necrosis, and infection from the twisted mass, outweighed the surgical risks to the pregnancy.

Regarding the surgical approach, the team opted for a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy rather than a laparoscopic procedure. While laparoscopy is increasingly utilized for myomectomy in early pregnancy or with smaller masses, the sheer size of the lesions (18 cm and 9 cm), the patient’s obesity, and the complex anatomical relationships necessitated an open approach. The laparotomy provided the necessary exposure to safely manipulate the large masses and the gravid uterus without the limitations of pneumoperitoneum, which can compromise venous return and fetal oxygenation. This approach is particularly indicated when masses are large, numerous, or located in critical areas where laparoscopic visualization and instrument maneuverability might be compromised. The extensive incision allowed for thorough exploration of the abdominal cavity and verification of fetal cardiac activity before proceeding with the resection.

Intraoperative fetal safety was maintained through continuous monitoring and pharmacological prophylaxis. It is mandatory to document fetal heart rate before, during, and after the surgery to ensure uterine quiescence and fetal well-being. In this case, fetal viability was confirmed upon entry into the abdominal cavity. To minimize the risk of preterm contractions triggered by surgical manipulation, the patient received progesterone prophylaxis. This began intraoperatively with 100 mg intramuscularly and continued postoperatively, transitioning to caproate hydroxyprogesterone every three days until delivery. This regimen supports the maintenance of pregnancy by promoting uterine relaxation. Additionally, antibiotic prophylaxis was tailored to the patient’s specific infection status; following the identification of *Klebsiella pneumoniae* in preoperative urine cultures, the regimen included cefazolin and clarithromycin to prevent postoperative sepsis and ensure wound healing.

The surgical strategy involved a selective myomectomy, guided by the distinct characteristics of the two masses. The cranial mass was identified as a pedunculated, highly vascularized leiomyoma that was already twisted and leaking, posing an immediate threat of necrosis and peritonitis. Consequently, it was excised using a double clamp technique to control the vascular pedicle, followed by careful closure of the exposed uterine root with a single absorbable suture to ensure hemostasis. In contrast, the caudal mass was left in situ. Although it was large, it appeared fixed with a smooth surface and was adherent to the uterine torus. Attempting to remove this intramural-subserosal mass would have carried a higher risk of significant hemorrhage and potential injury to the uterine wall, which could compromise the pregnancy further. This decision highlights the principle of minimizing obstetric risk by avoiding unnecessary dissection of fixed, non-acute masses when the primary threat (the twisted upper mass) has been addressed.

Postoperative management focused on addressing complications arising from the procedure and the patient’s baseline status. The patient experienced worsening anemia following surgery, requiring intravenous iron supplementation and blood transfusions to stabilize hemoglobin levels. This underscores the importance of anticipating blood loss in myomectomy during pregnancy, where vascularity is increased. The surgical wound was closed following standard protocols, and the patient was monitored for signs of infection or uterine irritability. Ultimately, the preservation of the caudal mass influenced the mode of delivery; because the residual pelvic myoma presented a potential obstacle to fetal head progression during labor, an elective caesarean section was planned at 37 weeks. This comprehensive approach, integrating surgical precision with obstetric safety protocols, resulted in an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome, demonstrating that timely, selective intervention can successfully manage complex abdominal masses during pregnancy.
