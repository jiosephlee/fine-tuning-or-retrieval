Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Fibroids or Ovaries? Navigating Abdominal Mass Imaging in Pregnancy

A 40-year-old woman presents in her second trimester with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On exam, the abdomen is more enlarged than expected for gestational age. Ultrasound reveals two massive lesions: one occupying the left subphrenic space and another filling the pouch of Douglas. The differential diagnosis swings wildly between decidualized endometriomas, ovarian malignancy, and degenerating leiomyomas.

This isn't just a diagnostic puzzle; it's a management tightrope walk. You have to balance the risk of surgical intervention against the threat of mass effect, torsion, or rupture, all while preserving a viable pregnancy. In this case, the imaging suggested ovarian pathology, but the operating room revealed something entirely different. Here is how we navigate the gray zone of abdominal masses in the gravid uterus.

## The Clinical Picture

The patient was a nulliparous woman in her 40s with a BMI of 33, presenting at 21 weeks of gestation. Her chief complaints were acute abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis (VAS 5/10) accompanied by mild dyspnea. While her vital signs were stable initially, the physical exam was striking. The abdomen was tense, with a taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm in the upper left quadrant. A bimanual exam revealed a fixed, hard 9 cm mass in the lesser pelvis bulging into the posterior fornix.

The dyspnea was mechanical, attributed to the "steric bulk" of the pregnant uterus combined with the enormous abdominal masses compressing the diaphragm. Initial management involved analgesia and semi-orthostatic positioning, which helped reduce pain but did not resolve the underlying issue. During her hospital stay, she developed a low-grade fever and a urinary tract infection (*Klebsiella pneumoniae*), which complicated the picture but was managed with antibiotics based on culture sensitivities.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: Imaging and Markers

The core challenge here was distinguishing uterine leiomyomas from adnexal masses. Ultrasound is the first-line modality, but in a gravid uterus, it can be misleading. In this case, US demonstrated two large, unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularization. The upper mass looked like a huge decidualized endometrioma, while the lower mass appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration.

Serum tumor markers added another layer of complexity. The patient had an elevated CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL. While the standard cutoff for ovarian cancer is often cited as 35 U/mL, CA-125 levels physiologically rise during the first and third trimesters. In the second trimester, levels are typically lower, but benign conditions like myomas and endometriosis can still elevate them. Relying on CA-125 alone would have likely steered the team toward an ovarian diagnosis.

To resolve the ambiguity, the team utilized non-contrast MRI. This is a critical safety point: gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally avoided in pregnancy due to potential risks, but non-contrast MRI is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester. The MRI provided superior anatomical definition, showing thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content. Crucially, it suggested the caudal mass was in continuity with the uterine tissue, favoring a myomatous origin over an ovarian one, despite the cystic appearance.

## Surgical Decision Making

Conservative management is usually preferred for uterine myomas during pregnancy due to risks of bleeding, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. However, this patient had worsening symptoms, restrictive lung disease due to mass effect, and a risk of acute complications like torsion or necrosis. The multidisciplinary team decided on a laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days.

Intraoperatively, the findings were surprising. The cranial mass was a huge, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma that was highly vascularized, twisted, and leaking—likely the source of the fever and pain. The team excised this mass carefully. However, the caudal mass, originating from the uterine torus, was left in situ. It was fixed and appeared to be a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma. Removing it would have posed a significant obstetric risk, so the team opted to minimize intervention.

The patient received progesterone prophylaxis intraoperatively and postoperatively to support the pregnancy. Post-surgery, she required blood transfusions and iron supplementation due to worsening anemia but was discharged after 10 days. The pregnancy continued without complication, resulting in an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks due to the residual pelvic mass obstructing the birth canal. The neonatal outcome was excellent.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Don't Trust the Cystic Appearance:** Leiomyomas can undergo cystic degeneration and mimic ovarian cysts or endometriomas on ultrasound, especially in the second trimester.
*   **MRI is Your Friend:** Non-contrast MRI is safe after the first trimester and offers superior diagnostic accuracy for determining the relationship between masses and the uterine cavity compared to ultrasound.
*   **CA-125 Context Matters:** Isolated CA-125 elevation is not diagnostic of malignancy in pregnancy. Levels fluctuate by trimester and are elevated in benign conditions like myomas and endometriosis.
*   **Surgery is for Complications:** Myomectomy during pregnancy should be reserved for cases with oncological suspicion, persistent severe symptoms, or acute complications like torsion or necrosis.
*   **Preserve the Pregnancy:** Surgical management should aim to minimize uterine manipulation. Leaving a fixed, non-obstructive mass in situ may be safer than aggressive resection if it avoids uterine trauma.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Imaging Hierarchy:** Use ultrasound first, but escalate to non-contrast MRI if the diagnosis is unclear or if surgical planning requires precise anatomical mapping.
*   **Timing:** If surgery is unavoidable, the second trimester is generally the safest window, balancing fetal organogenesis and preterm labor risks.
*   **Multidisciplinary Approach:** Management requires input from obstetrics, gynecology, and surgery to balance maternal morbidity with obstetric risks.
*   **Delivery Planning:** The mode of delivery depends on the size, number, and location of residual masses. A pelvic mass may necessitate an elective caesarean to avoid obstruction or scar rupture.
*   **Avoid Overtreatment:** Not all masses need removal. In the absence of malignancy or acute complications, expectant management with close monitoring is often the best course.



# The CA-125 Trap: Interpreting Tumor Markers in the Pregnant Patient

Imagine a 40-year-old woman in her second trimester presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. Ultrasound reveals two massive abdominal lesions, one occupying the entire left subphrenic space. You order tumor markers to rule out malignancy, and the CA-125 comes back elevated. In a non-pregnant patient, this might send you down a rabbit hole of oncology consults and staging CTs. But in pregnancy, the physiology is different, and the stakes are higher. This case highlights how easily benign uterine pathology can mimic ovarian malignancy when you don’t account for the unique hormonal landscape of gestation.

## The Clinical Presentation

Our patient was a nulliparous woman in her 40s with a BMI of 33, presenting at 21 weeks of gestation. She complained of severe acute abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis, alongside mild dyspnea. On physical exam, the abdomen was significantly enlarged for gestational age, with a tender, taut-elastic mass in the upper left quadrant measuring 18 cm. A bimanual exam revealed a separate, fixed 9 cm mass in the lesser pelvis.

Initial workup showed a low-grade fever and a urinary tract infection with *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, but the real concern was the masses. Ultrasound identified two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins. The upper mass extended from the subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. The differential diagnosis at this stage was wide, including decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degenerating leiomyomas.

## The CA-125 Conundrum

Here is where the trap lies. The patient’s serum CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL, with a normal reference range of less than 35 U/mL. Furthermore, there was an increasing trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. In a post-menopausal woman, this value combined with a complex mass would be highly suspicious for ovarian cancer. However, CA-125 is notoriously unreliable in pregnancy due to physiologic fluctuations.

The literature indicates that CA-125 levels typically reach two peaks: one in the first trimester and another in the third trimester. The second trimester usually sees a nadir, with a median value around 14.0 U/mL. Our patient’s value of 41.8 U/mL was significantly higher than the median for her gestational age, which initially raised oncological suspicion. Yet, CA-125 is non-specific and can be elevated in benign conditions such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and uterine leiomyomas. In this case, the slight elevation was likely driven by the benign myomatous disease and the urinary infection rather than malignancy. Relying on an isolated value without considering the trimester-specific baseline can lead to unnecessary anxiety and potentially harmful interventions.

## Imaging and Surgical Decision Making

To further characterize the masses without exposing the fetus to ionizing radiation or gadolinium, the team utilized non-contrast MRI. This modality is superior for determining the distance between a myoma and the uterine cavity and can identify solid components within cystic lesions. The MRI showed thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content but no adenopathy.

Despite the benign appearance on imaging, the decision to operate was driven by clinical necessity rather than oncological fear. The patient had worsening pain, restrictive lung disease due to the mechanical compression of the diaphragm, and a high risk of complications such as torsion, rupture, or infection. At 21 weeks and 6 days, she underwent a laparotomy.

Intraoperatively, the surprise continued. The upper mass was not an ovarian cyst but a huge, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma of the uterus that was twisted and leaking, explaining the fever. The surgical team excised this mass to relieve symptoms and prevent further necrosis. However, the lower mass, which originated from the uterine torus and was fixed, was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. This highlights a critical principle: surgical management in pregnancy should aim to preserve the pregnancy and avoid overtreatment. If a mass is not causing immediate life-threatening complications, leaving it alone is often safer than attempting a complex resection that could jeopardize uterine integrity.

## Obstetric Outcomes and Follow-Up

The surgical approach required careful fetal monitoring, with fetal heart rates documented before, during, and after the procedure. The patient received progesterone prophylaxis intraoperatively and continued daily until discharge to prevent preterm labor. Post-operatively, she required blood transfusions and iron supplementation due to worsening anemia but was discharged without complications ten days later.

The residual pelvic myoma would have obstructed vaginal delivery, so the patient underwent an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks using the same surgical scar. The outcome was excellent for both mother and neonate. Interestingly, the residual pelvic myoma regressed slightly postpartum, and the patient remained asymptomatic, choosing to defer further surgery. This case underscores that while myomas can grow rapidly during pregnancy—increasing by roughly 6.9% per week—they often regress significantly after delivery.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Know the CA-125 Physiology:** CA-125 levels physiologically rise in the first and third trimesters but normalize in the second trimester; an isolated elevation in the second trimester does not confirm malignancy.
*   **Trends Over Snapshots:** Serial evaluations of tumor markers can provide useful trends for differential diagnosis, but isolated measurements have low diagnostic value in pregnancy.
*   **Imaging Safety:** Use ultrasound and non-contrast MRI as safe imaging modalities to characterize abdominal masses; avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.
*   **Surgical Indications:** Reserve surgery for symptomatic masses with oncological suspicion or clinical complications like torsion, rupture, or obstruction; conservative management is preferred when possible.
*   **Delivery Planning:** The mode of delivery should depend on the size, number, and anatomical location of uterine masses, with caesarean section indicated if the mass obstructs the birth canal.



# When to Cut: Indications for Non-Obstetric Surgery in the Second Trimester

Expectant management is the gold standard for most pelvic masses discovered during pregnancy. We usually watch, wait, and deliver. But what happens when the "watch and wait" strategy threatens the mother's breathing or risks catastrophic torsion? A recent case report highlights a high-stakes scenario where a 21-week pregnant patient presented with massive abdominal lesions that forced a multidisciplinary team to make a critical call: operate now or risk complications later.

This case serves as a powerful reminder that while we strive to preserve pregnancy at all costs, maternal safety and the prevention of acute surgical emergencies sometimes necessitate intervention during the second trimester. The patient was a nulliparous woman in her 40s with a BMI of 33 who presented with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. Imaging revealed two distinct, large masses: an 18 cm lesion occupying the left subphrenic space extending to the uterine fundus, and a 9 cm mass filling the pouch of Douglas. The clinical picture was complicated by an elevated CA-125 and a fever, raising concerns for malignancy or infection.

The diagnostic journey was nuanced. Ultrasound showed unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularization, mimicking decidualised endometriomas. However, the lower mass appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. To refine the diagnosis without exposing the fetus to unnecessary risk, the team utilized non-contrast MRI. This is a crucial distinction in obstetric imaging; while gadolinium is generally avoided due to potential rheumatological and inflammatory risks, non-contrast MRI offers superior accuracy for determining the relationship between myomas and the uterine cavity. The MRI confirmed thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content but ruled out adenopathy.

The decision to proceed with laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days was driven by the worsening clinical condition. The patient had restrictive lung disease dyspnea due to the sheer steric bulk of the masses, alongside pain that was refractory to conservative management. There was also a tangible risk of torsion, rupture, or infection, particularly given the fever and the twisted, leaking nature of the upper mass found intraoperatively. The surgical team excised the upper pedunculated leiomyoma, which showed areas of ischemic necrosis, but wisely left the caudal mass in situ to minimize obstetric risk. This selective approach underscores the importance of limiting surgical time and trauma to preserve the pregnancy.

Postoperative management was aggressive and supportive. The patient required blood transfusions and iron supplementation for worsening anemia, alongside progesterone prophylaxis to prevent preterm labor. Fetal viability was monitored serially throughout the procedure. The outcome was excellent, with an elective caesarean section performed at 37 weeks. The pelvic mass, which would have obstructed vaginal delivery, was managed by choosing the appropriate mode of delivery rather than attempting a high-risk vaginal birth.

This case reinforces that while surgery in pregnancy carries risks of preterm delivery and bleeding, the second trimester remains the safest window for necessary intervention. The key is a multidisciplinary risk assessment that balances the oncological suspicion or clinical complications against the obstetric risks. We must remember that CA-125 levels physiologically rise during pregnancy, so isolated measurements can be misleading, and imaging should prioritize safety without sacrificing diagnostic accuracy.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Surgical Indications:** Surgery during pregnancy should be reserved for oncological suspicion, intractable pain, or complications like torsion, rupture, or infection that compromise maternal or fetal health.
*   **Imaging Safety:** Ultrasound is the first-line modality; non-contrast MRI is safe and superior for characterizing complex masses, but gadolinium should be avoided unless strictly necessary.
*   **Tumor Markers:** CA-125 levels can be elevated in normal pregnancy and benign conditions like endometriosis or myomas; rely on trends rather than single values for diagnosis.
*   **Timing:** The second trimester is generally the optimal window for non-obstetric surgery to minimize the risk of miscarriage and preterm labor compared to the first or third trimesters.
*   **Delivery Planning:** The mode of delivery should be dictated by the size, number, and anatomical location of uterine masses, with an elective caesarean section often required for large pelvic masses obstructing the birth canal.



# Protecting the Pregnancy: Perioperative Safety During Myomectomy

Imagine a patient in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On examination, her abdomen is significantly more enlarged than expected for gestational age. Imaging reveals not one, but two massive abdominal lesions: an 18-cm mass occupying the left subphrenic space and a 9-cm mass filling the pouch of Douglas. This is not a routine prenatal visit; this is a ticking clock where the differential diagnosis ranges from degenerating leiomyomas to malignant adnexal pathology. In this high-stakes scenario, the decision to operate is often the easy part. The real challenge lies in executing the surgery without compromising the viability of the pregnancy.

In this case report, the multidisciplinary team faced the classic dilemma of managing symptomatic, complicated abdominal masses during the second trimester. While conservative management is often preferred to avoid the risks of preterm labor or hemorrhage, the presence of severe pain, dyspnea due to diaphragmatic compression, and the risk of torsion or rupture necessitated intervention. The diagnostic workup highlighted the limitations and utilities of current imaging. Ultrasound identified the masses, but MRI without contrast was crucial for defining the relationship between the masses and the uterus, confirming the upper mass was a pedunculated leiomyoma and the lower mass was an intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. Notably, the CA-125 was slightly elevated at 41.8 U/mL, a finding that required careful interpretation given the physiological rise in CA-125 during pregnancy, particularly in the first and third trimesters.

Once the decision for laparotomy was made at 21 weeks and 6 days, the focus shifted entirely to perioperative safety protocols. The primary goal was to maintain uterine quiescence and ensure fetal well-being throughout the procedure. The team utilized serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds to monitor fetal viability and heart rate before, during, and after the surgery. This continuous surveillance allowed them to detect any signs of fetal distress immediately. To further support the pregnancy, prophylactic progesterone was administered starting intraoperatively. The patient received 100 mg intramuscularly during the procedure, followed by daily dosing until discharge, and subsequently switched to caproate hydroxyprogesterone every three days until delivery. This hormonal support aimed to prevent uterine contractions triggered by surgical manipulation.

Surgical technique also played a pivotal role in minimizing obstetric risk. Given the massive size of the lesions and the gestational age, a laparotomy was chosen over a laparoscopic approach to ensure adequate exposure and control. The procedure was kept as brief as possible, lasting 2 hours and 25 minutes, to reduce the duration of anesthesia and uterine manipulation. The upper mass, which was twisted and leaking, was excised gently using a double clamp technique, and the root was carefully closed with absorbable suture. Crucially, the lower mass was left in situ to minimize the risk of uterine trauma and bleeding, acknowledging that it could be addressed later. Postoperative management included iron supplementation for anemia and antibiotics tailored to a concurrent *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary infection. The strategy paid off, resulting in an excellent maternal outcome and an elective caesarean delivery at 37 weeks, necessitated by the remaining pelvic mass obstructing the birth canal.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Multidisciplinary Evaluation:** Management of large symptomatic masses in pregnancy requires a team approach balancing oncological suspicion, obstetric risk, and maternal symptoms.
*   **Imaging Safety:** Ultrasound with color Doppler and non-contrast MRI are the diagnostic modalities of choice; avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.
*   **Fetal Monitoring:** Continuous or serial monitoring of fetal heart rate via transvaginal ultrasound should be performed before, during, and after surgery to ensure fetal viability.
*   **Progesterone Prophylaxis:** Consider administering prophylactic progesterone intraoperatively and postoperatively to maintain uterine quiescence and reduce the risk of preterm labor.
*   **Surgical Timing and Technique:** The second trimester is often the safest window for surgery; keep operative time short, minimize uterine manipulation, and consider leaving non-critical masses in situ to preserve pregnancy.
*   **Delivery Planning:** The presence of residual pelvic masses may obstruct the birth canal, necessitating an elective caesarean section to prevent dystocia or uterine rupture.



# The Final Mile: How Pelvic Masses Dictate Mode of Delivery

When you encounter a large abdominal mass in a pregnant patient, your immediate instinct is often to worry about the diagnosis: Is it malignant? Is it torsed? Does it need to come out *now*? But once the acute crisis is managed, there is a critical question that often gets overshadowed: How does this mass impact the final mile of the pregnancy? Specifically, does the anatomy of the pelvis allow for a vaginal delivery, or has the mass effectively booked the operating room for term?

A recent case report highlights a nuanced scenario where surgical intervention during the second trimester was necessary, but the decision to leave a residual pelvic mass in situ fundamentally dictated the mode of delivery. This isn't just about managing the acute abdomen; it’s about strategic planning for the expulsive phase of labor.

## The Clinical Scenario

Consider a nulliparous woman in her 40s, presenting at 21 weeks gestation with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. Imaging revealed two massive lesions: an 18 cm upper mass occupying the left subphrenic space and a 9 cm lower mass filling the pouch of Douglas. The clinical picture was complicated by fever and anemia, raising concerns for infection or necrosis.

The multidisciplinary team faced a classic dilemma: intervene surgically and risk pregnancy loss, or wait and risk catastrophic complications like rupture or torsion. They opted for a laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days. During the procedure, the upper mass was identified as a pedunculated, twisted, and necrotic leiomyoma and was excised. However, the caudal mass, which appeared fixed and adherent to the posterior uterine wall, was left in situ. The rationale was to minimize obstetric risk during the pregnancy itself.

This decision to leave the lower mass behind was not an oversight; it was a calculated trade-off. By preserving the uterine integrity during the second trimester, they avoided the high bleeding risks associated with myomectomy on a gravid uterus. However, this choice carried a specific consequence for the delivery plan.

## Imaging and Diagnostic Pitfalls

Before reaching the delivery room, the diagnostic workup during pregnancy is fraught with pitfalls. In this case, ultrasound and non-contrast MRI were essential. The MRI was crucial in determining the relationship between the masses and the uterine cavity without exposing the fetus to gadolinium contrast.

One common pitfall in this population is interpreting tumor markers. The patient had a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which is above the standard cut-off of 35 U/mL. However, as noted in the case discussion, CA-125 levels physiologically rise during the first trimester and can fluctuate. In the context of pregnancy, isolated measurements are not diagnostic of malignancy. The slight increasing trend in this patient, combined with the imaging features of cystic degeneration, favored a benign etiology like decidualized endometriomas or degenerating leiomyomas rather than ovarian cancer.

## The Delivery Decision: Obstruction vs. Scar

The most critical learning point from this case lies in the delivery planning. The patient underwent an elective cesarean section at 37 weeks. While the prior laparotomy scar was a factor in minimizing the risk of uterine rupture, the primary driver for the cesarean was the residual pelvic mass.

The case report explicitly states that the caudal myoma would have represented a "huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour." This is a vital distinction for obstetricians and emergency physicians counseling patients with known fibroids. If a mass is located in the lower uterine segment or the pelvis, it can mechanically obstruct the birth canal regardless of the patient's desire for a vaginal trial of labor.

The team monitored the patient closely post-surgery. The residual pelvic myoma actually regressed slightly to 7.5 cm postpartum, but during the pregnancy, its fixed nature and location made vaginal delivery unsafe. The decision to perform an elective C-section on the same surgical scar at 37 weeks balanced the risks of spontaneous labor against the known anatomical obstruction.

## Strategic Management of Pregnancy Masses

This case reinforces that the management of abdominal masses in pregnancy is a continuum, not a single event. The initial surgical decision to leave the lower mass was made to preserve the pregnancy, but it required a commitment to a cesarean delivery.

When managing these cases, you must assess the size, number, and anatomical location of the masses relative to the birth canal. If a mass is intramural or subserosal in the lower uterine segment, it is less likely to be a candidate for myomectomy during pregnancy due to bleeding risks, but it is highly likely to necessitate a cesarean section at term. Conversely, masses in the fundus may be removed or left alone without impacting the mode of delivery as significantly.

The outcome in this case was excellent for both mother and neonate, validating the strategy of conservative surgical management during pregnancy followed by a planned delivery method that accounts for pelvic anatomy.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Surgical Timing:** Myomectomy during pregnancy should be reserved for complicated masses (torsion, necrosis, severe symptoms) and is safest in the second trimester.
*   **Diagnostic Caution:** Do not panic over isolated CA-125 elevations in pregnancy; use ultrasound and non-contrast MRI to characterize masses.
*   **Delivery Planning:** The location of residual pelvic masses is a primary determinant for mode of delivery. Lower segment masses often obstruct the expulsive phase, necessitating elective cesarean section.
*   **Obstetric Risk:** Leaving a mass in situ to minimize surgical bleeding risk during pregnancy often shifts the risk profile to the delivery, requiring careful planning for potential uterine rupture or obstruction.
*   **Multidisciplinary Care:** Management requires collaboration between obstetrics, gynecology, and surgery to balance fetal viability with maternal oncological and anatomical safety.
