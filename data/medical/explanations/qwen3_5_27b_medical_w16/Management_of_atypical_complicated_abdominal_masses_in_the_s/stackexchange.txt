Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a pregnant patient with an abdominal mass, how does the interpretation of CA-125 differ from the non-pregnant population, and what are the physiologic peaks during gestation?

In the pregnant population, the interpretation of CA-125 differs significantly from the non-pregnant standard due to physiologic fluctuations during gestation. While the standard oncological cutoff for ovarian cancer is generally ≥35 U/mL, CA-125 levels vary by trimester. As noted in the discussion of this case, serum CA-125 typically reaches two physiologic peaks: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL), with lower levels observed during the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL).

In our patient, the CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL at 21 weeks gestation. Although this exceeded both the standard cutoff and the second-trimester median, the final pathology revealed benign uterine leiomyomas. This underscores that CA-125 is not specific for malignancy in pregnancy, as elevations can occur with benign conditions such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, and myomas, as well as during menstruation and near-term pregnancy. Therefore, isolated measurements are not diagnostic. Clinical management should prioritize serial evaluations to establish trends and rely on safe imaging modalities, such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, rather than relying solely on tumor markers to avoid overtreatment.

### [Management] Why is gadolinium-enhanced MRI generally contraindicated during pregnancy, and what are the safety guidelines for non-contrast MRI in the second trimester?

Gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally contraindicated during pregnancy due to the risk of adverse fetal outcomes. As noted in the management of this patient, current recommendations advise against contrast administration unless strictly necessary because of the potential for rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin conditions in the neonate, as well as an increased risk of stillbirth or neonatal death.

For non-contrast MRI, which was utilized in this case to characterize the abdominal masses after ultrasound, safety guidelines are more permissive. The case highlights that non-contrast MRI is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester. Specifically, the multidisciplinary team avoided using magnetic field strengths greater than 1.5-T. In the second trimester, as seen with our 21-week patient, non-contrast MRI provides superior diagnostic accuracy for complex masses, such as large intramural or posterior leiomyomas, helping to determine the relationship between the mass and the uterine cavity without the teratogenic risks associated with gadolinium.

### [Management] What clinical criteria necessitate surgical intervention for uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy, as opposed to expectant management?

Expectant management is the standard of care for uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy, as surgical intervention carries significant risks of hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, and preterm labor. However, surgical intervention becomes necessary when specific clinical criteria indicate that the risks of the masses outweigh the risks of the procedure. Based on this case, the criteria necessitating surgery include persistent or worsening symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures, acute complications such as torsion or degeneration, and oncological suspicion.

In this patient, the decision for laparotomy at 21 weeks was driven by the worsening clinical condition, specifically severe abdominal pain and restrictive dyspnea caused by the mechanical compression of the large masses. Conservative measures, including analgesia and positioning, provided only temporary relief. Furthermore, the imaging and clinical picture suggested complicated masses at high risk for rupture, torsion, or infection; indeed, intraoperative findings confirmed the upper mass was a pedunculated leiomyoma that was twisted and leaking, consistent with ischemic necrosis.

Additionally, surgical management was indicated here to address diagnostic uncertainty. The masses presented with atypical features, including mixed solid and cystic components and a rising CA-125 trend, which raised concern for malignancy despite the likelihood of benign degeneration. The discussion emphasizes that myomectomy should be considered for complicated masses with torsion or persistent symptoms. In this instance, the surgical team prioritized removing the pedunculated, symptomatic upper mass while leaving the fixed lower mass in situ to minimize obstetric risk, illustrating that the goal is to preserve the pregnancy while mitigating immediate life-threatening complications.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why is the second trimester considered the optimal window for non-obstetric abdominal surgery during pregnancy?

The second trimester is considered the optimal window for non-obstetric abdominal surgery during pregnancy because it balances fetal safety with surgical feasibility, minimizing the risks associated with both earlier and later gestational ages. In this case, the decision to operate at 21 weeks was guided by the need to address acute symptoms while avoiding the specific hazards of the first and third trimesters.

First, the second trimester avoids the period of most rapid pathological growth. The discussion notes that uterine leiomyomas increase in size by 6.12% between the first and second trimesters, compared to only 3.10% between the second and third. By intervening in the second trimester, we avoid the accelerated expansion phase of the first trimester, which increases the risk of acute complications such as torsion, necrosis, or hemorrhage, as seen with the twisted pedunculated mass in this patient.

Second, diagnostic and surgical safety is optimized during this period. The case highlights that non-contrast MRI is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester, allowing for precise localization of masses without the risks associated with contrast agents. Additionally, the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus, which complicates surgical access and visualization, is less pronounced at 21 weeks than in the third trimester. This facilitates a shorter operative time, which is critical for preserving the pregnancy.

Finally, delaying surgery to the third trimester significantly increases obstetric morbidity. In this case, the residual pelvic mass would have obstructed vaginal delivery, necessitating an elective caesarean section. The discussion cites evidence that combining myomectomy with caesarean section increases hospitalization and surgery duration. Furthermore, the multidisciplinary team noted that elective surgery in the second trimester could be associated with a decreased risk of preterm delivery compared to managing complicated masses later in gestation. Therefore, when surgery is unavoidable, the second trimester offers the safest balance between maternal pathology control and fetal well-being.

### [Management] How does the location of a uterine leiomyoma (e.g., lower uterine segment vs. fundus) influence the mode of delivery?

The anatomical location of a uterine leiomyoma is a critical determinant in selecting the mode of delivery, primarily due to the risk of mechanical obstruction during labor. While fundal masses may not impede vaginal delivery, leiomyomas located in the lower uterine segment, isthmus, or pelvis can physically block the birth canal.

In this case, the decision for an elective caesarean delivery was driven specifically by the location of the caudal mass. Although the upper fundal mass was resected during the second trimester, the lower mass was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk during pregnancy. This caudal mass was located in the lesser pelvis and pouch of Douglas, adherent to the posterior intramural-subserosal myoma of the uterine torus and isthmic site. The clinical team determined that this mass would represent a "huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour." Consequently, despite the preservation of the pregnancy, an elective caesarean was performed at 37 weeks to avoid obstructed labor.

This case illustrates the management principle that the mode of delivery must depend on the size, number, and anatomical location of uterine masses. Specifically, large myomas adherent to the posterior wall of the isthmic site or occupying the pelvis often necessitate caesarean section, whereas masses in other locations may allow for vaginal birth if they do not obstruct the fetal descent.

### [Pathophysiology] What pathophysiologic mechanisms lead to red degeneration or torsion of pedunculated leiomyomas during pregnancy?

Based on the findings in this case, the pathophysiologic mechanisms leading to torsion and subsequent degenerative changes in uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy are driven primarily by anatomical predisposition and rapid hormonal growth.

In this patient, the upper abdominal mass was identified intraoperatively as a **huge pedunculated leiomyoma** with a connective stalk. This pedunculated anatomy is the critical structural factor predisposing the mass to **torsion**, which was confirmed by the finding that the mass was "initially twisted." The discussion notes that the presence of a pedicle presents a "significant risk for an acute, typical complication," specifically torsion.

The mechanism of **degeneration and necrosis** is closely linked to this vascular compromise and the physiological growth of the tumor during gestation. As detailed in the discussion, uterine leiomyomas follow a non-linear growth trend, increasing by approximately **6.90% every week**, with a significantly higher growth rate between the first and second trimesters. This rapid enlargement, combined with the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus, increases the mechanical leverage on the pedicle, facilitating torsion.

Once torsion occurs, the vascular supply is compromised. The pathological examination in this case revealed **"areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle,"** confirming that the twisting of the stalk led to hypoxia and tissue death. The discussion further supports this, noting that mass growth carries a "higher chance to go through hypoxia, necrosis and degeneration." While the lower mass showed cystic degeneration, the acute presentation of the upper mass was driven by the torsion-induced ischemia.

Therefore, the key mechanisms are the **pedunculated anatomy** allowing for rotation and the **pregnancy-induced rapid growth** that increases mass weight and vascular demand, ultimately precipitating torsion and ischemic necrosis.

### [Differential Diagnosis] On MRI, what specific features help differentiate a degenerating leiomyoma from a decidualized endometrioma in a pregnant patient?

In this case, the most critical MRI feature for differentiating a degenerating leiomyoma from a decidualized endometrioma is the **anatomical continuity or adherence of the mass to the uterine tissue**, specifically a known leiomyoma.

In this patient, the caudal mass was identified as a degenerating leiomyoma because the MRI demonstrated that the cleavage of the mass was not clear and it appeared **in continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma**. In contrast, decidualized endometriomas typically present as separate adnexal cysts without such uterine continuity.

Regarding signal characteristics, the degenerating leiomyomas in this case exhibited specific MRI findings including:
*   **Thick walls** (measuring 1.4 cm).
*   **Inhomogeneous fluid content** on both T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted images.
*   **Focal hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid components.**

However, imaging interpretation requires caution. The upper mass in this patient lacked an identifiable connective stalk or tumour-feeding vessel on MRI, which initially oriented the diagnosis toward an ovarian endometrioma. Surgical exploration revealed it was actually a **pedunculated leiomyoma**. Therefore, while thick walls and solid components are suggestive of degenerating fibroids in this context, the absence of a visible stalk does not rule out a myomatous origin. The anatomical relationship to the uterus remains the primary radiologic clue, though multidisciplinary assessment is essential when imaging is equivocal.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the rationale for administering progesterone prophylaxis following myomectomy during pregnancy?

**Clinical Pearl:** The rationale for administering progesterone prophylaxis following myomectomy during pregnancy is to maintain uterine quiescence and mitigate the risk of preterm labor induced by surgical manipulation of the gravid uterus.

In this case, a patient at 21 weeks gestation underwent laparotomy for a symptomatic, pedunculated leiomyoma. As noted in the discussion, surgical management of uterine masses during pregnancy is typically reserved for complicated cases due to inherent risks of pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. To counteract the uterine irritation associated with the myomectomy, the multidisciplinary team initiated intramuscular progesterone prophylaxis intraoperatively. This regimen continued daily until discharge and was maintained with hydroxyprogesterone caproate until the elective delivery at 37 weeks. The case illustrates that when surgical intervention is necessitated by complications such as torsion or severe symptoms, adjunctive progesterone therapy serves as a critical supportive measure to preserve fetal viability and extend gestation. The patient’s excellent maternal and neonatal outcome underscores the value of combining careful surgical technique with hormonal support to manage the obstetric risks of myomectomy.

### [Morning Report] What intraoperative monitoring is mandatory to ensure fetal viability during non-obstetric abdominal surgery in the second trimester?

To ensure fetal viability during non-obstetric abdominal surgery in the second trimester, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during, and after the procedure. In the presented case, a laparotomy was performed at 21 weeks’ gestation to address large, symptomatic uterine leiomyomas causing severe pain and dyspnea. To maintain fetal safety during this intervention, the surgical team utilized serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds to monitor fetal viability throughout the operation. 

This monitoring protocol aligns with multicentric study recommendations cited in the discussion, which emphasize that verifying fetal cardiac activity at all stages of surgery is essential. By combining this surveillance with intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis, the team successfully managed the maternal pathology while preserving the pregnancy, resulting in an excellent neonatal outcome.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the growth rate of uterine leiomyomas vary across the trimesters of pregnancy, and how does this impact surgical planning?

Leiomyoma growth during pregnancy follows a non-linear trajectory that significantly influences both the timing of intervention and the mode of delivery. According to the data cited in the discussion of this case report, uterine leiomyomas increase in size by approximately 6.90% per week, with the growth rate being significantly higher between the first and second trimesters (6.12%) compared to the period between the second and third trimesters (3.10%). Conversely, myoma size decreases significantly in the third trimester and postpartum, with a mean reduction of approximately 44% six weeks after delivery.

This growth pattern dictates surgical planning by balancing the risk of complications against obstetric safety. While conservative management is generally preferred to avoid hemorrhage or preterm labor, the accelerating growth in the early pregnancy and the absolute size of the masses necessitate intervention when symptoms become refractory or complications like torsion arise. In our patient, despite being in the second trimester where growth rates begin to decelerate, the presence of a pedunculated mass with signs of torsion and necrosis, combined with severe mechanical symptoms (dyspnea and pain), warranted laparotomy at 21 weeks to prevent further morbidity.

Furthermore, the anatomical location and potential for continued growth directly impact the decision regarding delivery. As illustrated by the caudal mass in this case, which was left in situ to minimize surgical risk, large pelvic leiomyomas can obstruct the birth canal. Consequently, the surgical team planned for an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks to bypass the obstruction posed by the residual pelvic myoma. Preoperative imaging is therefore critical to assess the size, number, and distance of myomas from the placenta and pelvic outlet to anticipate whether vaginal delivery is feasible or if a caesarean section is required to ensure maternal and fetal safety.
