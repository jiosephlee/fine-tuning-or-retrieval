Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Pathophysiology and Clinical Context of Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

The management of abdominal masses during gestation requires an understanding of the epidemiology and natural history of uterine pathology, as well as the physiological adaptations that alter standard diagnostic benchmarks. Uterine leiomyomas are the most common pelvic tumors in women, with an incidence during pregnancy ranging from 0.3% to 10.1%. These masses are hormonally sensitive, and their size typically follows a non-linear trend throughout gestation. In the first and second trimesters, leiomyomas may increase in size by approximately 6.90% per week due to high estrogen and progesterone levels, which can exacerbate mass effect and symptoms. However, following delivery, significant regression often occurs, as seen in this case where the residual pelvic myoma reduced to 7.5 cm postpartum. Recognizing this growth pattern is crucial for anticipating complications, such as the worsening pain and restrictive lung disease observed in the patient at 21 weeks, as the enlarging uterus and masses compete for intra-abdominal space.

Diagnostic interpretation must also account for physiological fluctuations in serum markers that differ from non-pregnant populations. Serum CA-125, often utilized as a marker for ovarian malignancy, exhibits a biphasic pattern during pregnancy, with median peaks in the first and third trimesters and a nadir in the second trimester. While the standard cut-off for malignancy is generally ≥35 U/mL, benign conditions such as endometriosis, myomas, and pregnancy itself can elevate levels. In this case, the patient presented with a CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL during the second trimester, where the median is typically 14.0 U/mL. Although this elevation raised suspicion for endometrioma or malignancy, it ultimately proved consistent with benign leiomyomatous disease. Therefore, isolated CA-125 measurements should not be considered diagnostic for cancer in pregnancy; rather, serial evaluations are necessary to assess trends, as the clinical team noted an increasing trend that contributed to the decision for further imaging and surgical evaluation.

Imaging modalities are the cornerstone of diagnosis, but their selection is governed by fetal safety profiles. Ultrasound with colour Doppler is the first-line investigation, providing initial characterization of vascularity and morphology, as demonstrated by the identification of the two large cystic masses in this patient. When ultrasound findings are indeterminate or require precise localization relative to the uterine cavity, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the preferred advanced modality. However, contrast agents must be used with extreme caution; gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally contraindicated due to potential risks of stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI, which offered superior accuracy in defining the thick walls, solid components, and continuity of the caudal mass with the uterine tissue. This imaging strategy allowed the team to differentiate between decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas without exposing the fetus to unnecessary contrast risks.

Finally, the clinical presentation of abdominal masses in pregnancy is driven by specific pathophysiological mechanisms, primarily mechanical compression and tissue degeneration. Large masses can create a significant steric bulk, displacing abdominal organs and elevating the diaphragm, which directly compromises respiratory mechanics. In this case, the patient’s dyspnoea was attributed to mechanical compression from the massive abdominal lesions rather than primary pulmonary or cardiac dysfunction. Additionally, rapid growth or torsion of a pedunculated mass can compromise blood supply, leading to ischaemic necrosis and red degeneration. This pathophysiology explains the patient’s severe abdominal pain and low-grade fever, which were later confirmed by pathology to result from the twisted, leaking pedunculated leiomyoma. Understanding these mechanisms helps clinicians distinguish between obstetric emergencies and mass-related complications, guiding the decision to proceed with surgical intervention to relieve symptoms and prevent further degeneration.

## Case Presentation and Multimodal Diagnostic Workup

The clinical presentation of abdominal masses during pregnancy requires a nuanced diagnostic approach that balances maternal safety with fetal well-being. In this case, a nulliparous woman in her 40s with a body mass index of 33 presented at 21 weeks of gestation with severe acute abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvis, alongside mild dyspnea. The pain was rated 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale at both sites. The dyspnea was attributed to mechanical compression from the abdominal masses rather than primary pulmonary or cardiac dysfunction, as evidenced by normal oxygen saturation and cardiac examination. This constellation of symptoms highlights the importance of recognizing mass effect in the gravid abdomen, where enlarging uterine or adnexal pathology can impinge upon the diaphragm and thoracic cavity, necessitating a thorough evaluation to distinguish between obstetric complications and non-obstetric surgical emergencies.

Physical examination revealed an abdomen more enlarged than expected for the gestational age, with a tender, taut-elastic mass occupying the entire upper left quadrant measuring approximately 18 cm. Bimanual vaginal examination identified a separate fixed mass in the lesser pelvis, measuring about 9 cm, which was rounded, hard, and partially bulging into the posterior fornix. These physical findings were critical in localizing the pathology, suggesting two distinct lesions rather than a single uterine enlargement. The presence of a hard, fixed pelvic mass raised suspicion for a uterine origin, while the upper abdominal mass required further characterization to determine if it was adnexal or uterine in nature. Initial conservative management with analgesics and a semi-orthostatic position provided symptomatic relief, but the development of a low-grade fever during hospitalization prompted a broader infectious and inflammatory workup.

Laboratory evaluation provided essential context for the patient’s systemic status and the nature of the masses. The patient exhibited iron deficiency anemia with a hemoglobin of 9.9 g/L and low serum ferritin, alongside a mild elevation in C-reactive protein and neutrophilia, which the team noted was consistent with physiological pregnancy-related changes. A nasopharyngeal swab for SARS-CoV-2 was negative, but urine cultures identified a *Klebsiella pneumoniae* infection, which was treated with antibiotics guided by antibiogram. While the fever was initially concerning for infection, subsequent surgical findings of a twisted, necrotic pedunculated leiomyoma suggested the mass itself may have contributed to the inflammatory response. Regarding oncological screening, serum tumor markers including CA 19-9, CA 15-3, CEA, and LDH were within normal limits. However, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL, showing an increasing trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. This finding required careful interpretation, as CA-125 levels physiologically rise in the first and third trimesters but typically normalize in the second; an elevation in the second trimester can indicate benign conditions like endometriosis or leiomyomas but warrants investigation for malignancy in the context of complex masses.

Imaging modalities were pivotal in characterizing the morphology and origin of the lesions. Ultrasonography with color Doppler served as the initial diagnostic tool, revealing two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. The upper mass measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm, extending from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass measured 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm and appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. To further define the relationship between the masses and the uterus, a non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan was performed. The use of non-contrast MRI is standard in pregnancy to avoid the potential risks associated with gadolinium contrast agents. The MRI demonstrated thick walls (1.4 cm) and inhomogeneous fluid content with focal hyperintense solid components on T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted images. Crucially, the imaging suggested continuity between the caudal mass and the posterior leiomyoma, although the cleavage plane was not clearly defined.

The integration of clinical, laboratory, and imaging data guided the differential diagnosis toward decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The slight elevation of CA-125 favored an endometrioma for the upper cyst, whereas the continuity with uterine tissue on MRI suggested a myomatous origin for the caudal mass. The diagnostic workup ultimately highlighted the complexity of managing large, symptomatic masses in the second trimester, where imaging must distinguish between benign degenerative changes and neoplastic processes. This multimodal approach confirmed the presence of complicated, vascularized masses with a high risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, providing the necessary justification for surgical intervention despite the inherent risks of operating during pregnancy.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis

The diagnostic evaluation of abdominal masses during pregnancy requires a nuanced approach that balances the limitations of imaging and laboratory testing with the urgency of maternal symptoms. In this case, the initial ultrasound revealed two large, unilocular cystic masses, leading to a primary differential diagnosis between decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. Distinguishing between these entities is critical, as their management and obstetric implications differ significantly. While the sonographic features of the upper mass mimicked a decidualised endometrioma, the lower mass appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. To refine this diagnosis, the team utilized non-contrast MRI, which is the preferred modality in pregnancy due to the safety profile regarding gadolinium contrast. The MRI provided superior diagnostic accuracy by demonstrating the anatomical continuity of the caudal mass with the uterine tissue, whereas the upper mass lacked a clear connective stalk. This imaging continuity was a pivotal finding that oriented the diagnosis toward a uterine origin for the lower lesion, despite the initial suspicion of an adnexal process. Ultimately, surgical exploration confirmed that both masses were benign uterine leiomyomas, highlighting that imaging features alone can be misleading and that anatomical continuity remains a key differentiator between ovarian and uterine pathology.

Laboratory evaluation in this scenario presented further diagnostic complexity, particularly regarding the interpretation of serum tumour markers. The patient presented with an elevated CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeded the standard cut-off of 35 U/mL and showed an increasing trend from a previous value of 36 U/mL. In the context of pregnancy, CA-125 levels physiologically rise, typically peaking in the first and third trimesters, with median values in the second trimester often lower than the observed level in this patient. While the slight elevation and upward trend initially favoured a diagnosis of endometrioma or raised concern for malignancy, the other tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) remained within normal limits. This case illustrates that isolated CA-125 measurements are not diagnostic during pregnancy; rather, serial evaluations provide useful trends for differential diagnosis. The final pathological confirmation of benign leiomyomas with ischaemic necrosis underscores that even elevated markers in this context may reflect benign degenerative processes or physiological pregnancy changes rather than neoplasia.

Beyond the anatomical and laboratory diagnosis, the clinical reasoning hinged on assessing the risk of acute complications associated with the masses. The patient presented with severe abdominal pain, low-grade fever, and leukocytosis, which, in conjunction with the MRI findings of thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid, raised suspicion for torsion, rupture, or infection. Although the patient had a concurrent urinary tract infection with *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, the surgical findings revealed that the upper mass was a pedunculated, twisted, and leaking leiomyoma, confirming that the fever and pain were likely driven by mass-related complications such as torsion and necrosis. The presence of these acute symptoms, combined with the large size of the masses (18 cm and 9.5 cm) and the restrictive dyspnoea caused by diaphragmatic compression, shifted the management strategy from conservative observation to surgical intervention. This decision was driven by the necessity to prevent further deterioration, such as sepsis or fetal compromise due to mechanical compression, which could not be managed with analgesics and positioning alone.

The final management decision required a multidisciplinary risk assessment to balance obstetric risks against surgical necessity. Generally, surgical management of uterine masses during pregnancy is delayed until after birth to avoid risks of preterm labour, haemorrhage, or uterine rupture. However, in this case, the multidisciplinary team determined that the risk of complications from the masses outweighed the risks of surgery at 21 weeks of gestation. The team opted for a laparotomy to excise the symptomatic upper mass while leaving the caudal mass in situ. This selective approach was based on the assessment that the caudal mass, while large, was fixed and less likely to cause immediate acute complications compared to the twisted upper mass. Furthermore, the decision to leave the caudal mass avoided extensive dissection that could have jeopardised the pregnancy, while the removal of the upper mass alleviated the mechanical compression causing dyspnoea. This case demonstrates that while conservative treatment is preferred, myomectomy should be considered when there is evidence of torsion, persistent symptoms, or when the mass threatens the physiological enlargement of the uterus and fetal growth.

## Surgical Management and Intraoperative Safety Strategies

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention during pregnancy requires a careful balance between maternal benefit and obstetric risk. While conservative management is generally preferred for uterine leiomyomas to avoid complications such as hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, or preterm labor, surgery becomes necessary when clinical conditions worsen or the risk of acute complications increases. In this case, the indication for intervention was driven by the patient’s severe abdominal pain, dyspnoea caused by mechanical compression from the massive abdominal lesions, and the specific risk of torsion or rupture associated with the pedunculated upper mass. The second trimester, particularly around 21 weeks, is often considered the optimal window for non-obstetric surgery during pregnancy, as the risk of miscarriage is lower than in the first trimester and the uterus has not yet reached the size that complicates access in the third trimester. The multidisciplinary team determined that the risks of delaying surgery, including potential hypoxia, necrosis, and infection from the twisted mass, outweighed the surgical risks to the pregnancy.

Regarding the surgical approach, the team opted for a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy rather than a laparoscopic procedure. While laparoscopy is increasingly utilized for myomectomy in early pregnancy or with smaller masses, the sheer size of the lesions (18 cm and 9 cm), the patient’s obesity, and the complex anatomical relationships necessitated an open approach. The laparotomy provided the necessary exposure to safely manipulate the large masses and the gravid uterus without the limitations of pneumoperitoneum, which can compromise venous return and fetal oxygenation. This approach is particularly indicated when masses are large, numerous, or located in critical areas where laparoscopic visualization and instrument maneuverability might be compromised. The extensive incision allowed for thorough exploration of the abdominal cavity and verification of fetal cardiac activity before proceeding with the resection.

Intraoperative fetal safety was maintained through continuous monitoring and pharmacological prophylaxis. It is mandatory to document fetal heart rate before, during, and after the surgery to ensure uterine quiescence and fetal well-being. In this case, fetal viability was confirmed upon entry into the abdominal cavity. To minimize the risk of preterm contractions triggered by surgical manipulation, the patient received progesterone prophylaxis. This began intraoperatively with 100 mg intramuscularly and continued postoperatively, transitioning to caproate hydroxyprogesterone every three days until delivery. This regimen supports the maintenance of pregnancy by promoting uterine relaxation. Additionally, antibiotic prophylaxis was tailored to the patient’s specific infection status; following the identification of *Klebsiella pneumoniae* in preoperative urine cultures, the regimen included cefazolin and clarithromycin to prevent postoperative sepsis and ensure wound healing.

The surgical strategy involved a selective myomectomy, guided by the distinct characteristics of the two masses. The cranial mass was identified as a pedunculated, highly vascularized leiomyoma that was already twisted and leaking, posing an immediate threat of necrosis and peritonitis. Consequently, it was excised using a double clamp technique to control the vascular pedicle, followed by careful closure of the exposed uterine root with a single absorbable suture to ensure hemostasis. In contrast, the caudal mass was left in situ. Although it was large, it appeared fixed with a smooth surface and was adherent to the uterine torus. Attempting to remove this intramural-subserosal mass would have carried a higher risk of significant hemorrhage and potential injury to the uterine wall, which could compromise the pregnancy further. This decision highlights the principle of minimizing obstetric risk by avoiding unnecessary dissection of fixed, non-acute masses when the primary threat (the twisted upper mass) has been addressed.

Postoperative management focused on addressing complications arising from the procedure and the patient’s baseline status. The patient experienced worsening anemia following surgery, requiring intravenous iron supplementation and blood transfusions to stabilize hemoglobin levels. This underscores the importance of anticipating blood loss in myomectomy during pregnancy, where vascularity is increased. The surgical wound was closed following standard protocols, and the patient was monitored for signs of infection or uterine irritability. Ultimately, the preservation of the caudal mass influenced the mode of delivery; because the residual pelvic myoma presented a potential obstacle to fetal head progression during labor, an elective caesarean section was planned at 37 weeks. This comprehensive approach, integrating surgical precision with obstetric safety protocols, resulted in an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome, demonstrating that timely, selective intervention can successfully manage complex abdominal masses during pregnancy.

## Obstetric Outcome, Delivery Planning, and Clinical Lessons

Following the laparotomy, the patient’s immediate postoperative course was complicated by worsening anemia, with hemoglobin levels dropping to 7.5 g/L and hematocrit at 24.3%. This decline, combined with pre-existing iron deficiency, necessitated aggressive correction to ensure maternal stability and fetal oxygenation. Management included intravenous iron supplementation with ferric carboxymaltose and two blood transfusions, which successfully restored hemoglobin to 10.0 g/L and hematocrit to 30.4%. Concurrently, antibiotic therapy was tailored to the identified urinary pathogen, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, utilizing cefazolin and clarithromycin based on sensitivity testing. The patient was discharged without complications ten days after surgery, highlighting the importance of managing postoperative hematologic and infectious risks in pregnant patients undergoing major abdominal procedures.

Obstetric follow-up was conducted strictly every three weeks to monitor fetal well-being and growth trajectory. Serial assessments confirmed appropriate fetal development, with growth parameters reaching the 86th percentile according to WHO fetal growth charts, and amniotic fluid index remained within normal limits. This close surveillance was critical given the previous surgical intervention and the presence of the residual pelvic mass. The successful monitoring of fetal growth and amniotic fluid volume demonstrated that, despite the significant maternal morbidity from the masses and surgery, the pregnancy could proceed to term with appropriate multidisciplinary oversight.

The mode of delivery was determined by the anatomical constraints imposed by the remaining pathology. Although the upper mass was excised, the caudal myoma, measuring 7.5 cm, remained in situ within the pelvis. Given its location and size, the mass was identified as a significant obstacle to fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labor. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was scheduled for 37 weeks of gestation. The procedure utilized the same longitudinal xiphos-pubic scar from the previous surgery to minimize additional uterine trauma. This decision resulted in an excellent neonatal outcome and underscores the principle that delivery planning must account for the size, number, and anatomical location of uterine masses to prevent obstructed labor.

Long-term maternal outcomes were favorable, with the residual pelvic leiomyoma showing partial postpartum regression to 7.5 cm. The patient remained asymptomatic regarding the myoma and did not require further surgical intervention at the time of reporting. She was able to breastfeed successfully and expressed a preference to defer additional surgery due to the absence of symptoms and the fulfillment of her reproductive goals. This outcome illustrates that while surgical intervention may be necessary during pregnancy for symptomatic relief, residual masses may regress postpartum, potentially avoiding the need for further morbid procedures.

Key clinical lessons from this case emphasize the necessity of a multidisciplinary approach when managing atypical abdominal masses in pregnancy. Diagnostic evaluation should rely on safe imaging modalities, specifically ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, to differentiate between adnexal and uterine origins without exposing the fetus to contrast agents. Surgical management should be reserved for cases with oncological suspicion or clinical complications, such as torsion, rupture, or intractable pain, rather than for asymptomatic masses. Furthermore, delivery planning must be individualized based on the specific anatomical impact of the masses, prioritizing maternal safety and the prevention of obstructed labor through timely elective caesarean section when indicated.
