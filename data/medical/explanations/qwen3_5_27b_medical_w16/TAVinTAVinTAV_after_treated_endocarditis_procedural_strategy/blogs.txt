# The Third Valve: Is TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Feasible?

## The Hook: When the Valve Gets a Valve Gets a Valve

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has revolutionized the management of aortic stenosis, but it has introduced a new clinical reality: structural valve deterioration (SVD). As patients live longer with these prostheses, we are increasingly facing the need for reintervention. Most of us are comfortable with a TAV-in-TAV, but what happens when the second valve fails? And what if that patient has a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)?

A recent case report from the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center presents a man in his late 70s who underwent a third transcatheter intervention—a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV—after surviving treated endocarditis. This isn't just a technical curiosity; it challenges the traditional dogma that PVE mandates surgical explantation and highlights the feasibility of lifetime transcatheter valve management in high-risk patients.

## The Case: A Timeline of Stents and Infections

The patient’s journey began in 2014 with a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis. By 2022, he presented with worsening dyspnea and a mean gradient of 83 mmHg. Given his comorbidities—including three-vessel CAD, COPD, and a pacemaker—he underwent a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. While his ejection fraction improved, the course took a dramatic turn three months later.

In September 2022, the patient was readmitted with fever and heart failure. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*, confirming prosthetic valve endocarditis. He completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy. By January 2024, however, he developed severe restenosis and regurgitation with a mean gradient of 49 mmHg. With a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and deep fixation of prior valves making surgery hazardous, the heart team opted for a third transcatheter intervention.

In June 2024, the patient underwent implantation of an Acurate neo2 valve (size S). The procedure utilized a strategy of high implantation and commissural alignment guided by multislice CT (MSCT). The immediate post-procedure echocardiogram was concerning, showing a mean gradient of 67 mmHg and severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). However, the clinical trajectory over the following year tells a different story.

## The Strategy: Imaging and Anatomy

The critical factor in this success was preprocedural planning. In a triple-stented anatomy, the risk of coronary artery obstruction is significant. The team used MSCT to assess the minimum lumen diameter (13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis) and evaluate the distance between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia.

The procedural strategy focused on two main goals: preserving coronary flow and maximizing the effective orifice area. The Acurate neo2 was selected for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and low frame height. During deployment, the device was aligned with the Evolut valve leaflets to fix them against the stent frame. This high implantation strategy prevented the inward folding of pre-existing leaflets, which would have further reduced the orifice area.

Coronary access was a major concern. By aligning commissures and using CT modeling, the team ensured that the cumulative mesh burden would not obstruct future coronary interventions. The procedure was performed under local anesthesia with radial and femoral access, and no coronary protection devices were required due to the favorable imaging assessment.

## The Outcome: Hemodynamics Over Time

The immediate hemodynamic profile following the third implantation was initially poor, consistent with severe PPM. The mean gradient was 67 mmHg, and the valve area was 0.9 cm². However, at one-month follow-up, the mean gradient dropped to 27 mmHg, and the valve area increased to 1.1 cm².

By the one-year mark in June 2025, the patient remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function. The mean gradient stabilized at 26 mmHg, the ejection fraction was 65%, and there was no evidence of recurrent endocarditis or valve-related complications. This improvement suggests that the high-pressure post-dilation likely contributed to radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame, enhancing leaflet motion and flow dynamics over time.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

This case underscores that TAVI is increasingly a lifelong management strategy rather than a one-time fix. While the history of endocarditis traditionally favors surgical replacement, individualized risk assessment and confirmed infection resolution can support a transcatheter approach in prohibitive surgical risk patients.

*   **Feasibility of Triple TAVI:** A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible with careful planning, though it requires meticulous attention to coronary access and effective orifice area.
*   **Imaging is Critical:** Preprocedural MSCT is essential to assess frame interaction, coronary ostial height, and the risk of neo-skirt obstruction in multi-prosthesis anatomy.
*   **Infection Status Matters:** In patients with prior PVE, proceeding with TAVI requires confirmed resolution of infection via blood cultures, TEE, and CT, with no active vegetations or suppressive antibiotic therapy required.
*   **Hemodynamic Evolution:** Immediate post-procedural gradients may be misleadingly high due to patient-prosthesis mismatch; functional improvement can occur over the first few months as frames expand and remodeling occurs.
*   **Valve Selection:** Choosing a valve with a low frame height and supra-annular leaflets (like the Acurate neo2) can optimize the residual effective orifice area in narrow, multi-layered anatomies.



# TAVI After Endocarditis: Navigating the Infection Dilemma

There are few scenarios in structural heart disease that make a heart team break a sweat quite like a patient with a "triple valve stack." Imagine three transcatheter aortic valves nested inside one another. Now, add a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) to the mix. This isn't just a technical puzzle; it’s a clinical minefield where the standard guidelines clash with prohibitive surgical risk.

We recently reviewed a fascinating case report detailing a patient in his late 70s who underwent a third TAVI procedure (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) following a resolved episode of infective endocarditis. The patient had a Lotus valve implanted in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut valve in 2022 due to severe degeneration. Just months later, in September 2022, he developed PVE caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. After completing a full course of antimicrobial therapy, the valve degenerated again, necessitating a third intervention in 2024. At one-year follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic with preserved valve function and no recurrent infection.

This case forces us to confront a uncomfortable reality: while surgical replacement remains the gold standard for PVE, not every patient is a candidate for the operating room.

## The Guidelines vs. The Reality

According to the 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines, surgical valve replacement is the recommended strategy for PVE, particularly when heart failure or uncontrolled infection is present. The logic is sound: surgery allows for debridement of infected tissue and complete removal of the prosthetic material. However, guidelines also emphasize individualized management based on surgical risk and anatomical feasibility.

In this patient, the surgical risk was not just high; it was prohibitive. The patient had a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, significant comorbidities including COPD and diabetes, and a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. Perhaps most critically, the two previously implanted transcatheter valves were extensively integrated into the aortic wall. Surgical explantation would likely have required a total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, a procedure with mortality risks that far outweighed the potential benefits.

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter intervention was not taken lightly. It was predicated on the fact that the infection was definitively resolved. The team confirmed this through multiple modalities: clinical assessment, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and multislice CT (MSCT). There was no evidence of active vegetations or paravalvular complications at the time of the third procedure. This distinction is vital. We are not talking about placing a valve in the setting of active bacteremia; we are talking about managing structural failure in a "sterilized" field.

## Technical Nuances of the Triple Stack

Even when the infection dilemma is resolved, the anatomical challenge of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is immense. The primary concerns are coronary artery obstruction and severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) due to the reduced effective orifice area.

In this case, preprocedural MSCT was the backbone of the strategy. The team analyzed coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and the interaction between the overlapping frames of the Lotus and Evolut valves. They opted for an Acurate neo2 prosthesis, selected for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height.

A key technical maneuver was the high implantation of the third valve. By aligning the device with the Evolut valve leaflets, the team fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame. This prevented further reduction of the effective orifice area caused by inward leaflet folding and minimized the formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia. Post-dilation was also utilized to optimize valve expansion and improve leaflet motion. While the initial post-procedural gradient was high (mean gradient 67 mmHg), it normalized significantly within one month, suggesting that the mechanical restriction of the prior frames was relieved by the new implantation strategy.

## The Critical Safety Net

The most critical takeaway from this case isn't the valve choice or the deployment technique; it's the infection clearance protocol. Placing a new prosthetic surface in a bed that was recently infected carries a theoretical risk of seeding. However, the risk of leaving a patient with severe aortic stenosis and heart failure is often higher.

The team ensured that antimicrobial treatment was completed (6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy) and that no long-term suppressive therapy was ongoing at the time of the procedure. They did not proceed until TEE and CT confirmed the absence of vegetations. This rigorous verification process is the safety net that makes such off-label, high-risk interventions defensible. It shifts the decision from "gambling with infection" to "managing structural failure in a high-risk host."

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Confirm Sterility:** Do not proceed with prosthetic valve intervention in the setting of PVE until active infection is ruled out via blood cultures, TEE, and CT imaging.
*   **Risk Stratification:** Surgical risk scores like EuroSCORE II are essential tools; a score >25% often shifts the balance toward transcatheter options even in complex PVE cases.
*   **Imaging is Mandatory:** In serial TAVI procedures, MSCT planning is non-negotiable to assess coronary clearance, frame interaction, and landing zones.
*   **Heart Team Approach:** Complex reinterventions require multidisciplinary consensus involving interventionalists, surgeons, imaging specialists, and infectious disease experts.
*   **Long-Term Planning:** Every TAVI should be viewed through a lifetime management lens; consider how current valve choices impact future coronary access and reintervention options.

As the longevity of TAVI patients improves, we will increasingly encounter scenarios where the "one-and-done" approach is no longer viable. This case illustrates that with meticulous planning and strict infection control, TAVI can remain a viable option even after the added complexity of treated endocarditis.



# CT Planning for the 'Russian Doll' Aorta: Avoiding Coronary Obstruction

## The Triple Valve Challenge
We have all seen the valve-in-valve procedure. It is a standard rescue strategy when a bioprosthetic TAVI fails. But what happens when you need to go deeper? What happens when you have a patient with three transcatheter valves stacked inside one another, complicated by a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis? This is the "Russian Doll" aorta, and it presents a nightmare of anatomical constraints and catastrophic risks, primarily coronary artery obstruction.

A recent case report details a man in his late 70s who navigated this exact scenario. His journey began in 2014 with a 27 mm Lotus valve. By 2022, degeneration set in, requiring a 23 mm Evolut valve implanted in the first. Just months later, he developed prosthetic valve endocarditis caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. After successfully treating the infection with a full course of antibiotics, he returned in 2024 with severe restenosis and regurgitation. With a prohibitive surgical risk and a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, the heart team opted for a third transcatheter intervention. The goal was to implant an Acurate neo2 valve in a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration without sacrificing coronary flow or causing immediate hemodynamic collapse.

## Why the CT Scan is Your Best Friend
In a standard TAVI, you look at the annulus. In a valve-in-valve, you look at the inner diameter of the existing frame. In a triple-valve scenario, the margin for error vanishes. The critical decision-making tool here was not the fluoroscopy room, but the multislice CT (MSCT) suite. The team used CT-guided planning to assess the feasibility of the third implantation before ever touching the patient.

The imaging team measured a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the second Evolut prosthesis, confirming severe hemodynamic compromise. More importantly, they analyzed the interaction between the frames of the previously implanted valves. They specifically evaluated the coronary ostial height, the dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, and the distance between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia. This data was essential to determine if the third valve could be implanted high enough to preserve coronary flow while still achieving adequate fixation. Without this precise measurement, the risk of burying the coronary ostia under the overlapping stent frames would have been unacceptably high.

## The Procedural Strategy and Outcome
Armed with the CT data, the team proceeded with a strategy-driven intervention under local anesthesia. They utilized both left radial and left femoral arterial access. A key technical decision was the implantation depth. They aimed for high implantation, aligning the new Acurate neo2 device with the leaflets of the Evolut valve. This approach fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing inward folding that could further reduce the effective orifice area. It also minimized the formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia.

The immediate post-procedural echocardiogram showed some initial challenges, with a mean gradient of 67 mmHg consistent with severe patient-prosthesis mismatch. However, this resolved over time. One month post-implantation, the mean gradient dropped to 27 mmHg, and the aortic valve area improved to 1.1 cm². At the 1-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function, a mean gradient of 26 mmHg, and no evidence of recurrent endocarditis. This outcome proves that even in the most complex, multi-layered anatomies, a third TAVI is feasible if the planning is meticulous.

## Clinical Pearls
*   **Imaging is Non-Negotiable:** In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios, multislice CT is mandatory to measure minimum lumen diameter, coronary ostial height, and valve-to-coronary distance before attempting the procedure.
*   **High Implantation Saves Coronaries:** Deliberate high implantation of the third valve can fix prior leaflets against the frame and prevent the neo-skirt from obstructing coronary ostia.
*   **PVE History is Not an Absolute Contraindication:** While surgery is often preferred for prosthetic valve endocarditis, a third TAVI can be considered if infection is fully resolved, surgical risk is prohibitive, and anatomical feasibility is confirmed.
*   **Expect Initial Mismatch:** Severe patient-prosthesis mismatch may be seen immediately post-procedure due to frame layering but can improve with remodeling and post-dilation over the first few months.
*   **Plan for the Future:** Each reintervention reduces future options; commissural alignment and frame interaction must be considered to preserve coronary access for potential future interventions.



# Commissural Alignment: Technical Pearls for Redo TAVI

Three valves in one aortic root. That’s the reality of this case. But it gets more complex: the patient had a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) sandwiched between the second and third interventions.

As TAVI longevity improves, we are entering the era of the "redo TAVI." We know TAV-in-TAV is feasible, but TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV? That’s a rare beast. This case report highlights a 1-year follow-up of a patient who underwent a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) after treated infective endocarditis.

The real story here isn’t just that they did it—it’s *how* they managed the hemodynamics and coronary access in a triple-stented anatomy. The team used specific technical strategies, including high implantation and commissural alignment, to turn a high-risk scenario into a successful long-term outcome.

## The Case: A Timeline of Complexity

The patient was a man in his late 70s with significant comorbidities, including COPD, diabetes, and three-vessel coronary artery disease. His valve journey began in 2014 with a 27 mm Lotus valve.

By 2022, the Lotus valve degenerated with a mean gradient of 83 mm Hg. He underwent a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. However, three months later, he developed prosthetic valve endocarditis caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. After a full course of antibiotics and confirmation of infection resolution, he returned in 2024 with severe restenosis (mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm²).

Surgical explantation was deemed prohibitive due to the deep fixation of prior valves and the patient’s EuroSCORE II of 27.36%. The heart team opted for a third TAVI using an Acurate neo2 prosthesis.

## The Technical Challenge: Stacked Frames and Coronaries

When you stack valves, you create a "tube within a tube within a tube." The primary risks are coronary obstruction, severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), and restricted leaflet motion due to frame layering.

In this case, the preprocedural multislice CT (MSCT) was the cornerstone of the strategy. They didn’t just look at annular sizing; they analyzed the interaction between the frames of the previously implanted valves, the sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and the valve-to-coronary distance.

The team had to ensure that the new valve frame wouldn’t push the existing leaflets inward, which would further reduce the effective orifice area (EOA). They also needed to guarantee that future coronary access wouldn’t be blocked by the cumulative mesh burden of three stents.

## The Strategy: High Implantation and Alignment

The procedural success relied on two specific technical maneuvers: high implantation and commissural alignment.

**High Implantation**
The team deliberately implanted the third valve (Acurate neo2) at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets. This wasn't just about anchoring; it was about mechanics. By fixing the pre-existing Evolut leaflets against the new stent frame, they prevented inward leaflet folding. This strategy minimized the formation of a "neo-skirt" below the coronary ostia, which is critical for preserving coronary flow.

**Commissural Alignment**
Using preprocedural CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks, the team aligned the commissures of the new valve with the existing ones. This was done to maximize the effective orifice area and reduce the risk of PPM. In a triple-stented configuration, misalignment can create a "maze" that obstructs flow and complicates future coronary interventions.

The procedure involved balloon pre-dilation, implantation of the self-expanding Acurate neo2, and post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon. The post-dilation was likely key to the final hemodynamics, as it may have contributed to further radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame, enhancing leaflet motion.

## The Hemodynamic Reality: Don't Panic at the Initial Gradient

Here is a crucial learning point for anyone considering redo TAVI: initial hemodynamics can be misleading.

Immediately post-procedure, the patient had a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg and an AVA of 0.9 cm², consistent with severe PPM. However, at 1 month, the mean gradient dropped to 27 mm Hg with an AVA of 1.1 cm². At the 1-year follow-up, the gradient was 26 mm Hg, and the patient was asymptomatic with an EF of 65%.

The disappearance of the severe gradient over time can be attributed to the optimization of commissural alignment, effective post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior frames. This suggests that while immediate post-procedural gradients may be high due to the "settling" of multiple frames, long-term function can be durable if the anatomy is preserved.

## Take-Home Points

*   **CT Planning is Non-Negotiable:** In multi-prosthesis scenarios, MSCT is essential to assess frame interaction, coronary clearance, and landing zones.
*   **Aim High:** High implantation of the redo valve can fix prior leaflets against the stent frame, preventing inward folding and preserving the effective orifice area.
*   **Align Commissures:** Aligning the new valve commissures with existing ones reduces patient-prosthesis mismatch and preserves future coronary access.
*   **Expect a Lag:** Initial post-procedural gradients in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV may be high due to frame settling; allow time for hemodynamic improvement before declaring failure.
*   **PVE History is Not an Absolute Contraindication:** With confirmed infection resolution and prohibitive surgical risk, TAVI remains a viable option for prosthetic valve dysfunction even after treated endocarditis.



# Lifetime Management: Planning for Future Valve Failure

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has revolutionized the management of severe aortic stenosis, but as our patients live longer, we are entering uncharted territory. We are seeing patients who need not one, but multiple valve interventions over a lifetime. While a TAV-in-TAV procedure is becoming more familiar in our interventional cardiology circles, a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is a rare beast. Even rarer is performing that third intervention in a patient with a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis. This case report challenges the conventional wisdom that surgery is the only answer for failed valves after infection and forces us to confront the concept of lifetime management in structural heart disease.

The patient in this report was a man in his late 70s with a medical history that reads like a checklist for high-risk cardiac intervention. He had three-vessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions, a pacemaker for third-degree heart block, type 2 diabetes, COPD, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. In 2014, he underwent his first TAVI with a 27 mm Lotus valve. Nearly a decade later, in 2022, the valve degenerated, leading to a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. The hemodynamics improved initially, but just three months later, he presented with fever and heart failure. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*, confirming prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). He completed a full course of antibiotics, and imaging confirmed the infection was resolved. However, by early 2024, the valve was failing again with severe stenosis and regurgitation. With a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and complex anatomy involving two overlapping stent frames, open surgical explantation was deemed prohibitive. The team proceeded with a third transcatheter intervention using an Acurate neo2 valve.

The technical complexity of this case cannot be overstated. In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenario, you are dealing with a "Russian doll" of stent frames. Each layer reduces the effective orifice area and increases the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch. Furthermore, the risk of coronary artery obstruction skyrockets when multiple frames are stacked in the aortic root. In this case, the team relied heavily on multislice CT (MSCT) for procedural planning. They analyzed the coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and the interaction between the frames of the previously implanted valves. A critical strategic decision was made to implant the third valve at a high level, aligning with the leaflets of the Evolut valve rather than pushing it lower. This approach helped fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing inward folding that would further reduce the orifice area. It also minimized the formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia, preserving coronary flow and future access.

This case highlights a crucial shift in how we must approach valve replacement, particularly in younger or high-risk patients. We can no longer view a valve implantation as a one-and-done fix. We must adopt a lifetime management strategy. When selecting the initial valve type and procedural strategy, we need to anticipate potential future reinterventions. For example, choosing a valve with a lower profile or specific frame design might facilitate a future valve-in-valve procedure. In this specific patient, the choice of the Acurate neo2 for the third intervention was driven by its supra-annular leaflet positioning and favorable hemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies. The team also prioritized commissural alignment during deployment to preserve future coronary access, acknowledging that the cumulative mesh burden of three valves could make future coronary interventions extremely difficult.

The outcome of this high-stakes procedure was impressive. Immediately post-procedure, there was significant transvalvular gradient, consistent with severe patient-prosthesis mismatch. However, by one month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mm Hg, and the aortic valve area increased to 1.1 cm². At the one-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with good exercise tolerance, a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, and an ejection fraction of 65%. There was no evidence of recurrent endocarditis or valve-related complications. This durability suggests that with meticulous planning, CT guidance, and a multidisciplinary heart team approach, even the most complex valve-in-valve-in-valve scenarios can be managed successfully outside the operating room.

The decision to proceed with a third TAVI after treated endocarditis is particularly noteworthy. Current guidelines generally recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, especially with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. However, this case demonstrates that in patients with prohibitive surgical risk and resolved infection, a transcatheter approach is a viable alternative. The key was ensuring the infection was completely cleared with negative blood cultures and imaging confirmation of no vegetations before touching the valve again. This underscores the importance of individualized risk assessment. We must weigh the anatomical feasibility and the severity of the hemodynamic compromise against the risks of open surgery, which in this case would have likely required total aortic root replacement.

As the population of TAVI recipients ages, we will see more of these complex scenarios. The lesson here is not just about the technical prowess required to implant a third valve, but about the foresight required at the first intervention. We need to think ahead. When we place that first TAVI, we should be considering what the anatomy will look like in 10 years. Will there be room for a second valve? Will coronary access be preserved? By integrating lifetime management into our initial decision-making, we can avoid running out of options for our patients when they inevitably face structural valve deterioration.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Lifetime Management is Critical:** When performing the initial TAVI, especially in younger or high-risk patients, consider the feasibility of future reinterventions to avoid running out of options.
*   **CT Planning is Non-Negotiable:** Detailed preprocedural CT imaging is essential for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures to assess coronary height, frame interaction, and effective orifice area.
*   **Infection Status Matters:** TAVI after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis may be feasible in prohibitive surgical risk patients if infection is fully resolved and confirmed by imaging and cultures.
*   **Strategic Implantation:** High implantation and commissural alignment can help preserve coronary access and optimize hemodynamics in multi-prosthesis anatomy.
*   **Multidisciplinary Approach:** Complex valve failures require a heart team approach involving interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, and imaging specialists to balance risks and benefits.



# Beyond the Score: Risk Stratification in Prohibitive Surgery Cases

We all know the standard algorithm for severe aortic stenosis: assess the risk, calculate the score, and send them to the OR or the cath lab. But what happens when the algorithm breaks down? What happens when the patient has already had two transcatheter valves, survived a bout of prosthetic valve endocarditis, and now presents with a mean gradient of 49 mmHg?

This isn't a theoretical board question. It’s a 70-something gentleman who walked into the cath lab for a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**.

This case report from the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center highlights a scenario that is becoming increasingly common as TAVI longevity improves: the need for serial reinterventions in patients with prohibitive surgical risk. It forces us to look beyond the risk calculators and into the complex anatomy and comorbidities that truly define "prohibitive."

## The Clinical Journey: A Triple Valve Stack

To understand the risk stratification challenge, you first have to appreciate the anatomy. This patient’s history is a roadmap of structural heart disease management over a decade.

In 2014, he received a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe stenosis. By 2022, structural valve deterioration set in with a mean gradient skyrocketing to 83 mmHg. He underwent a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. The procedure worked initially, but complications arose. Three months later, he presented with fever and heart failure. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*, confirming prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

He completed a full course of antibiotics. The infection resolved. But by early 2024, the valve failed again. Severe restenosis and regurgitation returned. The mean gradient was 49 mmHg, and the aortic valve area was a tiny 0.5 cm².

Here is where the rubber meets the road. The patient needed a valve replacement. But he was now a candidate for a third valve in the same root, following a history of endocarditis.

## EuroSCORE II vs. Reality

The prompt for this blog is "Beyond the Score," and this patient is the perfect example. His calculated EuroSCORE II was **27.36%**.

In the world of surgical risk stratification, a score above 20% is often considered prohibitive. However, a number on a screen doesn't capture the nuance of "technically hazardous." Why was open surgery off the table for this patient?

*   **Comorbidities:** He had COPD, Type 2 diabetes, and a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022.
*   **Coronary Disease:** Three-vessel CAD with multiple prior PCIs (2009–2014).
*   **Anatomical Nightmare:** Two overlapping bioprosthetic valves were already integrated into the aortic wall. Surgical explantation would likely have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement.
*   **Prior Complications:** His second TAVI was complicated by a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm requiring open vascular surgery.

The risk score quantified the mortality risk, but the Heart Team had to weigh the *technical feasibility* of surgery versus the *procedural risk* of a third TAVI. The decision wasn't just about survival probability; it was about whether the surgery could even be performed safely given the deep fixation of the prior valves.

## The Heart Team and the Endocarditis Question

One of the most contentious aspects of this case was the history of PVE. Current ESC guidelines generally recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, especially with heart failure. However, guidelines also emphasize individualized management.

The team made a crucial distinction: **Active infection vs. History of infection.**

At the time of the third intervention, the patient had completed antimicrobial therapy. Blood cultures were negative. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice CT (MSCT) confirmed no active vegetations or paravalvular complications. The infection was a historical footnote, not an active contraindication.

This decision required a multidisciplinary Heart Team meeting involving interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anaesthesiologists. They weighed the prohibitive surgical risk against the feasibility of a strategy-driven transcatheter intervention. The consensus was that TAVI was the only viable path forward, provided the anatomy allowed it.

## Imaging is the New Scalpel

When you are stacking a third valve inside a second valve inside a first valve, you cannot rely on fluoroscopy alone. This case underscores that **CT-guided procedural planning is non-negotiable** in complex reinterventions.

The team used MSCT to assess:
*   **Coronary Ostial Height:** Ensuring the new frame wouldn't obstruct flow.
*   **Valve-to-Coronary Distance:** Critical for preserving future coronary access.
*   **Frame Interaction:** Analyzing how the Acurate neo2 would sit within the Evolut frame.

They selected an Acurate neo2 (size S) for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and low frame height. The deployment strategy involved high implantation to fix the pre-existing Evolut leaflets against the stent frame, preventing inward folding and maximizing the effective orifice area.

The result? Immediate post-procedure gradients were high (67 mmHg) due to patient-prosthesis mismatch, but by one month, the gradient dropped to 27 mmHg. At one-year follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic, active, with a mean gradient of 26 mmHg and no evidence of recurrent endocarditis.

## Clinical Pearls for the Frontline

This case pushes the boundaries of what we consider standard of care, but the principles apply to every high-risk patient you encounter.

*   **Risk scores are guides, not verdicts.** A high EuroSCORE II signals danger, but you must evaluate the specific anatomical and comorbid barriers to surgery (e.g., prior vascular surgery, COPD, root fixation).
*   **Infection history doesn't always rule out TAVI.** If PVE is fully treated with no active signs on TEE or CT, and surgical risk is prohibitive, TAV-in-TAV remains a viable option.
*   **CT planning is mandatory for serial TAVI.** You need to measure the minimum lumen diameter, coronary clearance, and frame interaction before committing to a third valve.
*   **Commissural alignment matters.** In multi-valve anatomy, preserving coronary access for future interventions is a critical part of the procedural strategy.
*   **Expect hemodynamic evolution.** Post-procedure gradients may be high initially due to PPM but can improve as the valve expands and the LV remodels over the first few months.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is feasible** in highly selected patients with prohibitive surgical risk and treated PVE, provided infection is resolved.
*   **Multidisciplinary Heart Team decision-making** is essential when guidelines (like surgery for PVE) conflict with prohibitive surgical risk.
*   **Preprocedural CT imaging** is critical to assess coronary obstruction risk and guide valve selection in complex, multi-stented anatomy.
*   **Long-term surveillance** is key; as TAVI longevity improves, we must prepare for lifetime management strategies including serial reinterventions.
*   **Do not ignore the comorbidities.** Recurrent GI bleeding, COPD, and complex CAD often make open surgery technically hazardous regardless of the calculated mortality score.
