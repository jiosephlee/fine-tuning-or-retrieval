Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 1: ## Foundations of Transcatheter Valve Reintervention and Endocarditis

The landscape of aortic valve management has shifted significantly with the widespread adoption of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). Initially reserved for patients deemed inoperable, TAVI indications have expanded to include low-risk surgical candidates as procedural safety and durability have improved. Consequently, as the treated population ages and survival rates increase, clinicians are encountering a new cohort of patients requiring long-term management of structural valve disease. This case illustrates that evolution, documenting a patient who underwent initial TAVI in 2014 and required subsequent interventions a decade later. As patients live longer with transcatheter prostheses, structural valve deterioration (SVD) becomes an inevitable consideration, necessitating a transition from initial valve replacement to lifetime management strategies involving repeat interventions.

Structural valve deterioration in bioprosthetic valves typically manifests as progressive stenosis or regurgitation due to leaflet calcification, fibrosis, or tearing. In this patient, the initial Lotus valve implanted in 2014 functioned for approximately seven years before developing severe stenosis, evidenced by a mean gradient rising to 83 mm Hg by early 2022. This hemodynamic failure prompted the first reintervention, a transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure. Understanding SVD is critical because it dictates the timing of reintervention; however, the mechanism of failure can be multifactorial. In complex cases like this one, SVD may be accelerated by other insults, such as infection, which can cause leaflet damage and mechanical restriction distinct from standard degenerative calcification.

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) represents a distinct and high-risk complication in patients with prior valve interventions. PVE presents with systemic signs of infection, such as fever, alongside worsening heart failure symptoms and valvular dysfunction. In this case, the patient developed *Enterococcus faecalis* bacteremia three months after his second TAVI, accompanied by severe restenosis. The management of PVE fundamentally alters the reintervention strategy. Standard guidelines generally recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, particularly if there is uncontrolled infection or heart failure. However, in patients with prohibitive surgical risk, transcatheter strategies may be considered, provided the infection is fully resolved. This patient underwent a full six-week course of targeted intravenous and oral antimicrobial therapy. Crucially, the decision to proceed with the third TAVI was only made after clinical assessment, blood cultures, and imaging confirmed the absence of active infection, aligning with the principle that reintervention should not be performed during active bacteremia due to the risk of seeding the new prosthesis.

The decision to perform a valve-in-valve procedure rather than surgical explantation relies heavily on risk stratification. Indications for transcatheter valve-in-valve intervention include prosthetic valve dysfunction in patients with high or prohibitive surgical risk. This patient’s surgical risk was quantified by an EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, compounded by comorbidities such as COPD, diabetes, and prior gastrointestinal bleeding. Furthermore, the presence of two prior transcatheter frames fixed into the aortic root would have made surgical explantation technically hazardous, likely requiring total aortic root replacement. Therefore, the transcatheter approach was selected as the only viable option to restore valve function while avoiding the mortality risks associated with open surgery in a frail, high-risk host.

Serial reinterventions introduce specific anatomical risks that must be anticipated during planning. As multiple valve frames are stacked within the aortic root, the risk of coronary artery obstruction increases due to the potential for stent struts or displaced native leaflets to cover the coronary ostia. Additionally, each subsequent valve implantation reduces the effective orifice area, leading to patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). In this case, the immediate post-procedural gradient of 67 mm Hg and indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² reflected severe PPM, a known consequence of implanting a valve within a valve within a valve. Preprocedural multidetector computed tomography (MSCT) is essential to mitigate these risks by measuring the valve-to-coronary distance and planning implantation depth to preserve coronary flow. The successful outcome in this patient, characterized by improved gradients at one year, underscores the necessity of detailed imaging to optimize commissural alignment and frame expansion in complex, multi-prosthesis anatomies.
