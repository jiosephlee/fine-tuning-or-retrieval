Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Foundations of Transcatheter Valve Reintervention and Endocarditis

The landscape of aortic valve management has shifted significantly with the widespread adoption of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). Initially reserved for patients deemed inoperable, TAVI indications have expanded to include low-risk surgical candidates as procedural safety and durability have improved. Consequently, as the treated population ages and survival rates increase, clinicians are encountering a new cohort of patients requiring long-term management of structural valve disease. This case illustrates that evolution, documenting a patient who underwent initial TAVI in 2014 and required subsequent interventions a decade later. As patients live longer with transcatheter prostheses, structural valve deterioration (SVD) becomes an inevitable consideration, necessitating a transition from initial valve replacement to lifetime management strategies involving repeat interventions.

Structural valve deterioration in bioprosthetic valves typically manifests as progressive stenosis or regurgitation due to leaflet calcification, fibrosis, or tearing. In this patient, the initial Lotus valve implanted in 2014 functioned for approximately seven years before developing severe stenosis, evidenced by a mean gradient rising to 83 mm Hg by early 2022. This hemodynamic failure prompted the first reintervention, a transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure. Understanding SVD is critical because it dictates the timing of reintervention; however, the mechanism of failure can be multifactorial. In complex cases like this one, SVD may be accelerated by other insults, such as infection, which can cause leaflet damage and mechanical restriction distinct from standard degenerative calcification.

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) represents a distinct and high-risk complication in patients with prior valve interventions. PVE presents with systemic signs of infection, such as fever, alongside worsening heart failure symptoms and valvular dysfunction. In this case, the patient developed *Enterococcus faecalis* bacteremia three months after his second TAVI, accompanied by severe restenosis. The management of PVE fundamentally alters the reintervention strategy. Standard guidelines generally recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, particularly if there is uncontrolled infection or heart failure. However, in patients with prohibitive surgical risk, transcatheter strategies may be considered, provided the infection is fully resolved. This patient underwent a full six-week course of targeted intravenous and oral antimicrobial therapy. Crucially, the decision to proceed with the third TAVI was only made after clinical assessment, blood cultures, and imaging confirmed the absence of active infection, aligning with the principle that reintervention should not be performed during active bacteremia due to the risk of seeding the new prosthesis.

The decision to perform a valve-in-valve procedure rather than surgical explantation relies heavily on risk stratification. Indications for transcatheter valve-in-valve intervention include prosthetic valve dysfunction in patients with high or prohibitive surgical risk. This patient’s surgical risk was quantified by an EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, compounded by comorbidities such as COPD, diabetes, and prior gastrointestinal bleeding. Furthermore, the presence of two prior transcatheter frames fixed into the aortic root would have made surgical explantation technically hazardous, likely requiring total aortic root replacement. Therefore, the transcatheter approach was selected as the only viable option to restore valve function while avoiding the mortality risks associated with open surgery in a frail, high-risk host.

Serial reinterventions introduce specific anatomical risks that must be anticipated during planning. As multiple valve frames are stacked within the aortic root, the risk of coronary artery obstruction increases due to the potential for stent struts or displaced native leaflets to cover the coronary ostia. Additionally, each subsequent valve implantation reduces the effective orifice area, leading to patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). In this case, the immediate post-procedural gradient of 67 mm Hg and indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² reflected severe PPM, a known consequence of implanting a valve within a valve within a valve. Preprocedural multidetector computed tomography (MSCT) is essential to mitigate these risks by measuring the valve-to-coronary distance and planning implantation depth to preserve coronary flow. The successful outcome in this patient, characterized by improved gradients at one year, underscores the necessity of detailed imaging to optimize commissural alignment and frame expansion in complex, multi-prosthesis anatomies.

## Clinical Presentation and Comprehensive Diagnostic Workup

The clinical management of patients requiring serial transcatheter valve interventions requires a meticulous assessment of the patient's baseline risk profile and current physiological status. In this case, the patient was a man in his late 70s with a complex medical history that significantly influenced the decision-making process for the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). His background included arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. Cardiovascular history was extensive, featuring three-vessel coronary artery disease managed with multiple percutaneous coronary interventions between 2009 and 2014, and a third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating a pacemaker in 2014. Crucially, he had undergone two prior TAVI procedures: a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014 and a 23 mm Evolut valve in 2022. Given these comorbidities and the technical complexity of operating on a triple-stented aortic root, the patient was deemed to have a prohibitive surgical risk, with a calculated EuroSCORE II of 27.36%. This high-risk profile necessitated a percutaneous strategy, but only after confirming that the patient could tolerate the procedure and that the anatomical constraints allowed for a safe reintervention.

Symptom assessment provided the clinical indication for reintervention, reflecting the progression of prosthetic valve dysfunction. Upon admission in June 2024, the patient presented with dyspnea, fatigue, dizziness, and a spastic sensation in the left side of the chest during physical exertion. His functional capacity was notably limited, with walking distances restricted to 200–300 meters before symptom onset. These symptoms are consistent with severe aortic stenosis and heart failure, but in the context of a failed bioprosthesis, they signal structural valve deterioration (SVD) or prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The clinical team correlated these symptoms with objective hemodynamic data to confirm that the patient’s decline was valve-related rather than solely due to his underlying comorbidities. The presence of worsening heart failure symptoms despite guideline-directed medical therapy further supported the need for mechanical correction of the valve obstruction.

Echocardiographic evaluation served as the primary modality for assessing the hemodynamic severity of the valve dysfunction. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed severe prosthetic valve dysfunction characterized by a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg and a markedly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm². The AVA was calculated using the continuity equation, based on left ventricular outflow tract measurements and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals, which is the standard method for assessing stenosis severity in prosthetic valves. Additionally, TTE identified persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation and visualized two overlapping bioprosthetic valves. While TTE confirmed the hemodynamic burden, it also highlighted the limitations of ultrasound in complex multi-prosthesis anatomy, where acoustic shadowing from multiple stent frames can obscure detailed structural assessment. Consequently, echocardiography was used to track functional decline and rule out gross paravalvular leaks, but it was insufficient for procedural planning.

Multislice CT (MSCT) imaging was the cornerstone of the procedural planning strategy, providing critical anatomical details that echocardiography could not resolve. The MSCT revealed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the previously implanted Evolut prosthesis, confirming severe haemodynamic compromise and defining the landing zone for the new valve. Beyond lumen sizing, the CT assessment focused on the interaction between the frames of the previously implanted transcatheter valves and the coronary anatomy. Specific measurements included coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and the valve-to-coronary distance. These metrics were essential to evaluate the risk of coronary artery obstruction, a known complication in TAV-in-TAV procedures that is amplified in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios. The imaging also guided the strategy for implantation depth; the team planned a high implantation to preserve coronary flow and minimize the formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia. This detailed anatomical mapping allowed for the selection of an Acurate neo2 prosthesis and the determination of a deployment strategy that maximized the effective orifice area while maintaining coronary access.

Finally, confirming the resolution of prior infective endocarditis was a prerequisite for safe reintervention. The patient had a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis caused by *Enterococcus faecalis* in September 2022, which was treated with a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy. Before proceeding with the third TAVI in 2024, the clinical team had to establish that no active infection remained, as implanting a new prosthetic device into an infected field carries a high risk of recurrent endocarditis and treatment failure. Resolution was confirmed through a multimodal approach: clinical assessment showed no fever or signs of sepsis, repeated blood cultures were negative, and transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and MSCT demonstrated no evidence of vegetations or paravalvular complications. With the infection considered resolved and no long-term suppressive antibiotic therapy required, the management strategy aligned with that of patients without active endocarditis, allowing the team to prioritize anatomical feasibility and hemodynamic severity in their decision to proceed.

## Strategic Decision-Making and Risk Stratification

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) rather than surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) was driven by a rigorous risk stratification process conducted by the multidisciplinary Heart Team. This patient presented with a European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) of 27.36%, indicating prohibitive surgical risk due to significant comorbidities including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, type 2 diabetes, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. Anatomical considerations further complicated the surgical option; the two previously implanted transcatheter valves had extensively integrated into the aortic wall, meaning that open surgical explantation would likely have necessitated a total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. Consequently, the Heart Team consensus prioritized a transcatheter strategy to avoid the physiological stress of sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass, accepting the technical complexity of a triple-stented configuration in exchange for a significantly reduced procedural mortality risk.

A critical component of this decision-making process involved determining the appropriate timing for reintervention following the patient’s episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). While the 2025 European Society of Cardiology guidelines generally recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, particularly when complicated by heart failure or uncontrolled infection, management in this case required individualisation based on infection control status. The team deferred the third TAVI until after the patient had completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy for *Enterococcus faecalis*, which included six weeks of intravenous ampicillin followed by oral treatment. Preprocedural evaluation confirmed the resolution of infection through negative blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT, ensuring no active vegetations or paravalvular complications were present. This clearance was essential to prevent seeding the new prosthesis and to align the procedural risk profile with that of a patient without active infection, allowing the team to focus on anatomical feasibility rather than infectious control.

Once the decision for transcatheter intervention was made, the focus shifted to mitigating specific technical risks associated with a triple-stented anatomy, particularly coronary obstruction and patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). Multislice CT was utilised to assess the minimum lumen diameter of the innermost prosthesis, which measured 13.7 mm, and to evaluate the distance between the valve frames and the coronary ostia. To mitigate the risk of coronary obstruction, the procedural strategy emphasised high implantation of the third valve, positioning the device at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets to preserve coronary flow and prevent the formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia. Additionally, to address the inevitable reduction in effective orifice area caused by layering three prostheses, the team selected the Acurate neo2 valve. This self-expanding bioprosthesis was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height, features designed to maximise the residual effective orifice area in narrow anatomies. Commissural alignment was further employed during deployment to optimise haemodynamics and preserve future coronary access, acknowledging that while the initial post-procedural gradients were high, optimised expansion and alignment could improve long-term functional outcomes through partial mechanical expansion of the prior frames.

## Procedural Technique and Technical Nuances

The successful execution of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure requires meticulous technical planning and intra-procedural adaptability to navigate the complex anatomy created by multiple overlapping stent frames. In this case, the intervention was performed under local anaesthesia to minimize physiological stress on a patient with significant comorbidities, utilizing a hybrid vascular access strategy. Access was obtained via the left femoral and left radial arteries, providing the stability necessary for precise valve manipulation and the ability to manage potential complications from multiple entry points. A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the femoral access, establishing a stable rail for device delivery. This dual-access approach allows for simultaneous aortography and device deployment, which is critical for maintaining hemodynamic monitoring and ensuring real-time visualization of coronary perfusion throughout the procedure.

Before valve deployment, balloon pre-dilation was performed to facilitate the passage of the third prosthesis through the calcified and layered structures of the prior valves. A 22/40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute to reduce cardiac output and minimize the risk of embolization. Crucially, simultaneous aortography was performed during this phase to verify that coronary flow remained unobstructed. In sequential TAVI procedures, the risk of coronary artery obstruction is amplified by the cumulative height of the stent frames and the potential for leaflet prolapse. Confirming patent coronary flow prior to permanent valve deployment serves as a vital safety checkpoint, ensuring that the pre-dilation maneuver has not inadvertently compromised coronary ostia before the final prosthesis is committed to the position.

The selection and deployment of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis were guided by preprocedural CT modelling, with a specific emphasis on commissural alignment. In a triple-stented anatomy, the risk of future coronary access being blocked by the mesh of overlapping valves is significant. By aligning the commissures of the new valve with those of the existing Evolut prosthesis, the interventional team aimed to maximize the effective orifice area and reduce patient-prosthesis mismatch. More importantly, this alignment strategy was designed to preserve the pathways for future coronary interventions. The CT-guided planning allowed the operator to identify fluoroscopic landmarks that corresponded to the anatomical commissures, enabling precise rotational positioning of the self-expanding frame to maintain coronary accessibility.

A defining technical nuance in this case was the deliberate strategy of high implantation. The device was deployed at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets rather than lower in the annulus. This positioning was intended to fix the pre-existing leaflets of the Evolut valve against the stent frame of the new Acurate neo2 prosthesis. By anchoring the old leaflets outward, the team prevented inward leaflet folding, which would otherwise reduce the effective orifice area. Furthermore, high implantation minimized the formation of a "neo-skirt" below the coronary ostia. In complex redo-TAVI cases, the skirt of a new valve can extend below the coronary openings and obstruct flow or access; implanting the device higher within the aortic root mitigates this risk, ensuring that the coronary ostia remain clear of the prosthetic fabric.

Following valve deployment, post-dilation was performed using a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing to optimize frame expansion and leaflet mobility. This step is often necessary in TAV-in-TAV procedures to ensure the new valve fully expands within the constrained space of the prior prostheses. In this patient, the immediate post-procedural echocardiogram showed a high mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, which was attributed to the initial constraints of the triple-stented anatomy. However, the post-dilation maneuver, combined with the mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame, contributed to improved hemodynamics over time. Follow-up imaging demonstrated a reduction in the mean gradient to 27 mm Hg at one month and 26 mm Hg at one year, indicating that the procedural optimization of frame expansion and leaflet positioning successfully restored durable valve function despite the anatomical complexity.

## Clinical Outcomes, Follow-up, and Lifetime Management Lessons

The immediate procedural success of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was confirmed by the absence of paravalvular or transvalvular regurgitation and correct device positioning. However, the initial hemodynamic assessment revealed significant patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), a critical consideration in serial valve-in-valve procedures. Immediately following the implantation of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg and an aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²). The Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, consistent with severe PPM. This initial hemodynamic burden highlights a common challenge in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions, where the cumulative stent frames reduce the effective orifice area, potentially compromising flow dynamics despite the technical success of valve deployment.

Long-term follow-up data demonstrated a marked improvement in hemodynamic parameters, suggesting that initial gradients may not reflect the final functional state of the prosthesis. At one month post-implantation, the mean gradient decreased significantly to 27 mm Hg, and the AVA increased to 1.1 cm². By the one-year mark, the patient remained clinically stable and asymptomatic, with a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg and a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%. This resolution of severe PPM over time is attributed to the combination of optimized commissural alignment, effective post-dilatation, and the partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame during the procedure. The sustained improvement in valve function and the absence of recurrent endocarditis or regurgitation at one year confirm the durability of the intervention in this high-risk anatomical setting.

Surveillance strategies for patients with serial transcatheter prostheses must be rigorous, focusing on both hemodynamic performance and structural integrity. In this case, monitoring was conducted using transthoracic echocardiography at one month, 3.5 months, and one year post-procedure. Key metrics included mean gradients, AVA, and the presence of regurgitation or vegetations. Given the history of prosthetic valve endocarditis, the surveillance protocol also prioritized the detection of recurrent infection, confirmed through clinical assessment and repeat blood cultures. The stability of the prosthesis function over the first year suggests that, once infection is resolved, the management of prosthetic dysfunction aligns with standard structural valve deterioration protocols, provided anatomical feasibility is maintained.

Future considerations for lifetime management in patients with multiple prior TAVI procedures are complex and often limit options for subsequent failure. In this patient, the European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) was 27.36%, and the deep fixation of prior valves into the aortic wall would likely necessitate total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement for surgical explantation, rendering surgery prohibitively high-risk. Consequently, if the current prosthesis were to fail prematurely, emerging catheter-based solutions such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration techniques (e.g., BASILICA) might be considered. However, the feasibility and safety of these interventions in a triple-stented configuration remain uncertain. This case underscores the necessity of early, multidisciplinary planning regarding procedural trajectories, emphasizing that even at the time of the first intervention, clinicians must anticipate the potential need for serial reinterventions and the associated anatomical constraints.
