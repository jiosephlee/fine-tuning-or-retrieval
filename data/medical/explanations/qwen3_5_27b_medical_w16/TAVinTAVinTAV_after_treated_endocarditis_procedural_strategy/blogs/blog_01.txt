Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# The Third Valve: Is TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Feasible?

## The Hook: When the Valve Gets a Valve Gets a Valve

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has revolutionized the management of aortic stenosis, but it has introduced a new clinical reality: structural valve deterioration (SVD). As patients live longer with these prostheses, we are increasingly facing the need for reintervention. Most of us are comfortable with a TAV-in-TAV, but what happens when the second valve fails? And what if that patient has a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)?

A recent case report from the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center presents a man in his late 70s who underwent a third transcatheter intervention—a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV—after surviving treated endocarditis. This isn't just a technical curiosity; it challenges the traditional dogma that PVE mandates surgical explantation and highlights the feasibility of lifetime transcatheter valve management in high-risk patients.

## The Case: A Timeline of Stents and Infections

The patient’s journey began in 2014 with a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis. By 2022, he presented with worsening dyspnea and a mean gradient of 83 mmHg. Given his comorbidities—including three-vessel CAD, COPD, and a pacemaker—he underwent a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. While his ejection fraction improved, the course took a dramatic turn three months later.

In September 2022, the patient was readmitted with fever and heart failure. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*, confirming prosthetic valve endocarditis. He completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy. By January 2024, however, he developed severe restenosis and regurgitation with a mean gradient of 49 mmHg. With a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and deep fixation of prior valves making surgery hazardous, the heart team opted for a third transcatheter intervention.

In June 2024, the patient underwent implantation of an Acurate neo2 valve (size S). The procedure utilized a strategy of high implantation and commissural alignment guided by multislice CT (MSCT). The immediate post-procedure echocardiogram was concerning, showing a mean gradient of 67 mmHg and severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). However, the clinical trajectory over the following year tells a different story.

## The Strategy: Imaging and Anatomy

The critical factor in this success was preprocedural planning. In a triple-stented anatomy, the risk of coronary artery obstruction is significant. The team used MSCT to assess the minimum lumen diameter (13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis) and evaluate the distance between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia.

The procedural strategy focused on two main goals: preserving coronary flow and maximizing the effective orifice area. The Acurate neo2 was selected for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and low frame height. During deployment, the device was aligned with the Evolut valve leaflets to fix them against the stent frame. This high implantation strategy prevented the inward folding of pre-existing leaflets, which would have further reduced the orifice area.

Coronary access was a major concern. By aligning commissures and using CT modeling, the team ensured that the cumulative mesh burden would not obstruct future coronary interventions. The procedure was performed under local anesthesia with radial and femoral access, and no coronary protection devices were required due to the favorable imaging assessment.

## The Outcome: Hemodynamics Over Time

The immediate hemodynamic profile following the third implantation was initially poor, consistent with severe PPM. The mean gradient was 67 mmHg, and the valve area was 0.9 cm². However, at one-month follow-up, the mean gradient dropped to 27 mmHg, and the valve area increased to 1.1 cm².

By the one-year mark in June 2025, the patient remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function. The mean gradient stabilized at 26 mmHg, the ejection fraction was 65%, and there was no evidence of recurrent endocarditis or valve-related complications. This improvement suggests that the high-pressure post-dilation likely contributed to radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame, enhancing leaflet motion and flow dynamics over time.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

This case underscores that TAVI is increasingly a lifelong management strategy rather than a one-time fix. While the history of endocarditis traditionally favors surgical replacement, individualized risk assessment and confirmed infection resolution can support a transcatheter approach in prohibitive surgical risk patients.

*   **Feasibility of Triple TAVI:** A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible with careful planning, though it requires meticulous attention to coronary access and effective orifice area.
*   **Imaging is Critical:** Preprocedural MSCT is essential to assess frame interaction, coronary ostial height, and the risk of neo-skirt obstruction in multi-prosthesis anatomy.
*   **Infection Status Matters:** In patients with prior PVE, proceeding with TAVI requires confirmed resolution of infection via blood cultures, TEE, and CT, with no active vegetations or suppressive antibiotic therapy required.
*   **Hemodynamic Evolution:** Immediate post-procedural gradients may be misleadingly high due to patient-prosthesis mismatch; functional improvement can occur over the first few months as frames expand and remodeling occurs.
*   **Valve Selection:** Choosing a valve with a low frame height and supra-annular leaflets (like the Acurate neo2) can optimize the residual effective orifice area in narrow, multi-layered anatomies.
