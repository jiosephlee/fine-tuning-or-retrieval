## Pathophysiology and Classification of Complex Anaphylaxis

To understand the clinical course presented in this case, one must first grasp the immunopathogenesis of anaphylaxis. At its core, anaphylaxis is a systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by the rapid degranulation of mast cells and basophils, leading to the release of preformed and newly synthesized mediators such as histamine, tryptase, and leukotrienes. These mediators cause vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and bronchoconstriction, resulting in the hallmark symptoms of airway obstruction, respiratory distress, and circulatory collapse. In the presented case, the patient’s reaction to clarithromycin triggered this cascade, evidenced by hypotension, hypoxia, and cutaneous manifestations. Crucially, the measurement of serum tryptase serves as a biomarker for this mast cell activation. In this patient, elevated tryptase levels during the third anaphylactic episode and persistently elevated baseline levels provided key evidence linking the acute reaction to an underlying dysregulation of mast cell biology, distinguishing this case from typical IgE-mediated anaphylaxis where baseline tryptase is usually normal.

Beyond the acute mechanism, clinicians must accurately classify the temporal pattern of symptoms to guide management and observation. Standard anaphylaxis involves a single episode of symptoms that resolve with treatment. However, complex presentations include biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory courses. The Resuscitation Council UK defines biphasic anaphylaxis as the recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent resolution, without re-exposure to the allergen. Multiphasic anaphylaxis represents a more severe variant, involving multiple relapses. The patient in this report exemplifies multiphasic anaphylaxis, experiencing four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock within 24 hours, separated by asymptomatic periods. This differs from refractory anaphylaxis, which is defined as persistent symptoms with no or insufficient response to correctly administered epinephrine. In this case, the patient responded to epinephrine each time, ruling out refractory anaphylaxis and confirming a multiphasic course where symptom recurrence occurred despite initial therapeutic success.

Understanding the epidemiology of these recurrent episodes is vital for risk stratification. The incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis varies widely in literature, ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most data suggest a rate between 4% and 6%. While most recurrences occur within the first 24 hours, reports document symptom recurrence up to 78 hours after the initial exposure. This variability explains the clinical decision in this case to admit the patient to the intensive care unit after the first relapse. Despite the initial resolution of symptoms in the emergency department, the known risk of late recurrence necessitated prolonged monitoring. The multiphasic nature of this patient’s reaction, involving four separate episodes, falls outside the typical biphasic window and represents a rare clinical challenge with limited data regarding optimal observation duration.

Identifying patients at risk for these recurrent episodes remains a significant clinical challenge due to the lack of reliable predictors. Suggested risk factors for symptom recurrence include delayed administration of epinephrine, a severe initial presentation, the requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of prior biphasic reactions. In the presented case, the patient exhibited a severe initial presentation with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxia, and ultimately required four doses of epinephrine. Despite these risk factors, the text notes that reliable predictors allowing for targeted monitoring are not yet available. This uncertainty complicates the balance between patient safety and healthcare resource utilization, as clinicians must decide how long to observe patients who appear stable after initial treatment.

Finally, the pathophysiology of complex anaphylaxis may be influenced by underlying mast cell disorders, such as systemic mastocytosis. This condition involves an abnormal increase in mast cell burden and excessive mediator release, predisposing patients to severe and recurrent anaphylactic reactions. In this case, further analysis revealed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, supported by elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms, although the patient did not meet formal diagnostic criteria due to limited bone marrow material. This connection is critical for understanding why this patient experienced such a protracted and multiphasic course compared to typical anaphylaxis. The presence of an underlying mast cell disorder suggests that the threshold for degranulation may be lower or the mediator release more sustained, complicating standard management protocols and highlighting the importance of investigating atypical anaphylaxis courses for underlying etiologies.

## Acute Clinical Presentation and Initial Stabilization

The initial management of anaphylaxis relies on rapid recognition of the clinical syndrome and immediate intervention to prevent cardiovascular collapse. In this case, a woman in her 60s with a body weight of 104 kg presented to the emergency department following the ingestion of a single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin. Her medical history included type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, specifically a prior mild reaction to amoxicillin. While she had no previous history of anaphylaxis, the history of atopy and prior drug sensitivity is clinically relevant, as patients with a history of allergic reactions may be at increased risk for severe responses to new allergens. The temporal relationship between the oral intake of clarithromycin and the onset of symptoms—one hour post-ingestion—strongly supported a causal link, necessitating the immediate discontinuation of the suspected trigger.

Recognition of anaphylactic shock requires a systematic assessment of the airway, breathing, and circulation, particularly when cutaneous signs are present. This patient exhibited classic mucocutaneous symptoms, including pruritus, flushing, generalized erythema, urticaria, and facial swelling, alongside a subjective sensation of throat swelling. Crucially, the patient demonstrated signs of systemic involvement that defined the severity of the reaction. Her vital signs revealed significant hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxaemia (88% saturation on room air), indicating anaphylactic shock rather than a mild allergic reaction. Although cardiac and lung auscultation were initially normal and there was no observed tongue or throat swelling, the combination of hypotension and hypoxia with cutaneous manifestations met the clinical criteria for anaphylaxis, requiring urgent resuscitation.

The therapeutic rationale for initial stabilization prioritizes the restoration of perfusion and oxygenation while counteracting the systemic release of inflammatory mediators. In accordance with standard emergency protocols, the patient received intramuscular epinephrine as the first-line pharmacotherapy. Epinephrine acts on alpha-adrenergic receptors to induce vasoconstriction and improve blood pressure, while beta-adrenergic effects reduce mucosal edema and bronchospasm. Adjunctive therapies were administered to support these goals: supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) addressed the hypoxaemia, and a 500 mL bolus of normal saline was given to support intravascular volume in the setting of distributive shock. Additionally, intravenous clemastine was administered to manage the cutaneous symptoms. While antihistamines and corticosteroids do not reverse airway obstruction or hypotension, they are often used in conjunction with epinephrine to alleviate skin manifestations and potentially mitigate late-phase reactions, though their role in preventing recurrence remains debated.

A critical aspect of clinical decision-making in this case involved the selection of epinephrine dosage and administration route. Current guidelines recommend an intramuscular epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults. Given the patient’s weight of 104 kg, the calculated dose would approach the 0.5 mg maximum. However, the patient initially received 0.3 mg via an auto-injector. This decision reflects a practical compromise often encountered in emergency settings: while the 0.3 mg dose is technically lower than the weight-based recommendation for this patient, auto-injectors are often preferred in time-sensitive situations or by personnel less familiar with preparing individual doses. The primary objective is to minimize treatment delay, as hesitancy in administering vasoactive agents can lead to worse outcomes. This case highlights the tension between optimal dosing based on pharmacokinetics and the operational reality of rapid deployment in emergency medicine.

Following initial stabilization, the patient demonstrated a good clinical response, with blood pressure normalizing and cutaneous symptoms resolving. Despite this improvement, the decision was made to admit the patient to an acute internal ward for increased monitoring rather than discharge. This disposition decision is grounded in the risk of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, where symptoms can recur after an apparent resolution. Patients who present with anaphylactic shock, require multiple interventions, or have a delayed initial response to epinephrine are at higher risk for relapse. In this instance, the severity of the initial presentation (hypotension and hypoxia) warranted a period of observation to detect potential recurrence. The subsequent development of recurrent anaphylactic episodes within 12 hours validated the decision for admission, underscoring that initial clinical improvement does not guarantee safety and that observation protocols must account for the unpredictable course of severe allergic reactions.

## Management of Multiphasic and Refractory Episodes

The management of anaphylaxis becomes significantly more complex when the clinical course is multiphasic, characterized by recurrent episodes of symptoms despite initial resolution. In the presented case, a woman in her 60s experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes within the first 12 hours of admission following clarithromycin exposure. This clinical picture underscores a critical teaching point for clinicians: the initial response to epinephrine and supportive care does not guarantee safety, and vigilance for recurrence is required even after apparent stabilization. Biphasic or multiphasic reactions are defined by a recurrence of symptoms without re-exposure to the allergen, typically occurring within 24 hours, though reports exist of recurrences up to 78 hours later. In this case, the patient’s symptoms worsened progressively after the initial ED presentation, necessitating repeated interventions. Recognizing the pattern of recurrence is essential, as the intensity of symptoms in this patient lessened with each episode, ranging from deep hypoxaemia and hypotension initially to milder dyspnoea and rash in later episodes. This variability highlights the need for sustained monitoring rather than discharge based solely on the resolution of the first episode.

When recurrent anaphylactic shock occurs, the decision to escalate care to an intensive care unit (ICU) setting is often driven by the severity of the relapse and the need for continuous monitoring. In this case, the patient was transferred to the ICU following the first relapse of anaphylactic shock to allow for increased monitoring by specialised nursing staff. While the acute internal ward provided increased monitoring, it lacked telemetry control, which may be insufficient for patients demonstrating instability or refractory symptoms. The transition to the ICU allows for closer observation of vital signs and rapid response to further decompensation. However, optimal observation durations remain debated in the literature. Traditional observation periods range from 4 to 6 hours, yet guidelines from the World Allergy Organisation and the Dutch NHG note that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6 to 12 hours. The UK guideline 2021 proposes a risk-stratified approach, but consensus monitoring strategies are still needed to balance patient safety with healthcare resource allocation. In the absence of reliable predictors for biphasic reactions, such as delayed epinephrine administration or severe initial presentation, clinicians must exercise caution when determining the duration of observation for patients with atypical or severe courses.

Pharmacological escalation, particularly regarding epinephrine dosing and administration routes, presents another layer of decision-making in refractory cases. The patient in this report weighed 104 kg, yet the initial treatment involved a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. While auto-injectors are preferred in time-sensitive settings to minimise treatment delay and reduce hesitancy among personnel unfamiliar with vasoactive agents, this dosage may constitute underdosing for adults over 50 kg. Guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, with a maximum total dose of 0.5 mg, which is often simplified to 0.5 mg for most adults. In this case, the first two epinephrine doses were 0.3 mg, while the subsequent two doses were individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular injections. A 2025 retrospective study cited in the discussion indicates that patients initially receiving 0.3 mg required escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving 0.5 mg. Beyond intramuscular boluses, the department of allergology considered continuous intravenous epinephrine administration via pump as a preemptive measure. However, this approach was not ultimately utilized. The choice between intramuscular bolus therapy and intravenous infusion remains a consideration in refractory cases, weighing the risks of continuous vasoactive infusion against the need to prevent further relapse.

The role of adjunctive therapies, specifically corticosteroids, remains a subject of significant debate between traditional practice and emerging evidence. In this case, corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, consistent with the traditional role of these agents in preventing biphasic reactions. The patient received both 30 mg of prednisone intravenously daily for 5 days and 8 mg of dexamethasone intravenously once. However, the discussion section highlights that mounting evidence demonstrates no benefit to corticosteroids in reducing the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis, with a 2017 systematic review finding only one of 22 studies supporting their use. Furthermore, recent studies suggest potential harmful effects, including an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Consequently, most contemporary guidelines advise against routine corticosteroid administration, although their use in clinical practice persists. This case illustrates the tension between established hospital protocols and evolving evidence, suggesting that while corticosteroids may be administered in complex cases, their efficacy in preventing recurrence is not well-supported.

Ultimately, management decisions in multiphasic anaphylaxis often involve weighing preemptive treatment against expectant management, particularly when guidelines are lacking. For this patient, two alternative treatment courses were considered: continuous intravenous epinephrine to preemptively treat anaphylaxis or an expectant approach to see if symptoms would resolve spontaneously. Both options carried serious risks, underpinned by the lack of literature providing treatment guidance in multiphasic cases. Fortunately, neither approach was necessary as the patient did not show further symptom relapse after approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylaxis. This outcome supports an expectant strategy in cases where symptom intensity is decreasing, provided that adequate monitoring is in place. However, the underlying high probability of systemic mastocytosis in this patient complicates future management and underscores the need for long-term follow-up. The rarity of such complex courses makes evidence-based strategy development challenging, suggesting that expert-based consensus and case registries may be necessary to refine management protocols for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## Diagnostic Evaluation and Underlying Etiology

The atypical and prolonged clinical course of multiphasic anaphylaxis in this case necessitated a diagnostic investigation beyond the immediate management of the allergic reaction. While the initial presentation was consistent with drug-induced anaphylaxis, the recurrence of symptoms despite appropriate treatment and allergen avoidance raised suspicion for an underlying mast cell disorder. The investigative process focused on two primary areas: confirming the mediator release kinetics through serum tryptase evaluation and assessing for systemic mastocytosis (SM) as a predisposing factor. Understanding the limitations of the diagnostic workup in this specific case provides critical lessons on the timing of biomarker sampling and the interpretation of inconclusive pathological findings.

Serum tryptase is the primary biomarker used to confirm mast cell activation and to screen for underlying mast cell disorders. Guidelines recommend a specific sampling protocol to maximize diagnostic utility: an initial sample should be obtained as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second sample should be taken 1–2 hours after symptom onset when levels typically peak, and a third baseline sample should be collected at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution. In this case, the diagnostic accuracy was compromised by deviations from this protocol. Tryptase levels were not determined during the initial workup of the first anaphylactic episode, meaning the acute peak associated with the primary trigger was missed. Instead, the first sample was taken one hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and the second sample was obtained 27 hours after the last recurrence. While the elevated levels during the third episode and the persistently elevated baseline supported the diagnosis of mast cell activation, the lack of an initial peak measurement limited the ability to calculate the precise delta required to definitively distinguish acute anaphylaxis from baseline elevation in isolation.

Despite the sampling limitations, the clinical picture suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis. The World Health Organization (WHO) diagnostic criteria for SM require one major criterion or one major and one minor criterion, or three minor criteria. The major criterion involves the identification of multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in the bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs. Minor criteria include mast cells with atypical morphology, the presence of the KIT D816V mutation, expression of CD25 or CD2 on mast cells, and a serum tryptase level persistently greater than 20 ng/mL. In this patient, the bone marrow biopsy yielded limited material, rendering the major criterion unassessable. Consequently, she met only two of the minor criteria, which is insufficient for a formal diagnosis of SM. However, the clinical context, including hepatomegaly observed on abdominal sonography and neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression and memory problems, aligned with the systemic manifestations of mast cell disorders. This highlights the clinical challenge of interpreting inconclusive biopsy results; in the absence of definitive pathological proof, a high clinical probability may still guide long-term management and counseling.

Finally, the diagnostic team performed a risk-benefit analysis regarding the confirmation of the specific allergen trigger. While skin prick testing or oral challenge could theoretically confirm the sensitivity to clarithromycin, these procedures carry the risk of inducing a renewed, potentially severe anaphylactic reaction. Given the patient's history of multiphasic anaphylaxis and the suspected underlying mast cell instability, the risk of provocation was deemed disproportionate to the benefit. Furthermore, the ease of avoiding the offending agent in the future made confirmation unnecessary for immediate safety. The decision to forego allergy confirmation testing underscores a key principle in managing complex anaphylaxis: when the trigger is identified clinically and avoidance is feasible, the risks of diagnostic provocation may outweigh the diagnostic yield, particularly in patients with refractory or multiphasic courses.

## Clinical Guidelines, Controversies, and Practice Implications

The management of complex anaphylaxis, as illustrated by this case of multiphasic reaction, highlights significant gaps between standard emergency protocols and the reality of severe, recurring allergic presentations. Current clinical guidelines generally recommend an observation period of 4 to 6 hours following symptom resolution to monitor for biphasic reactions. However, this case demonstrates the limitations of fixed timeframes, as the patient experienced recurrent anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission despite initial stabilization. While organizations such as the Dutch NHG and the World Allergy Organisation note that half of biphasic reactions occur within 6 to 12 hours, consensus on optimal monitoring duration remains debated. For the clinician, this underscores the necessity of risk stratification; patients with severe initial presentations or those requiring multiple doses of epinephrine, like the patient in this report, may require extended observation beyond the traditional 4-hour window to ensure safety, balancing patient risk against healthcare resource utilization.

A critical controversy addressed in this case concerns the dosing of epinephrine, the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis. Guidelines recommend a weight-based dosage of 0.01 mg/kg, typically capped at 0.5 mg for adults. In this case, the patient weighed 104 kg, yet the initial treatment utilized a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. While auto-injectors are valuable for minimizing treatment delay in time-sensitive settings, the 0.3 mg dose represents a potential underdosing for adults over 50 kg. The case report notes that the patient required escalation to individually prepared 0.5 mg doses for subsequent episodes, and retrospective data cited in the discussion suggests that patients initially receiving 0.3 mg are more likely to require escalation of care. This discrepancy serves as a vital teaching point for residents: reliance on standard auto-injectors without considering patient weight may compromise efficacy in larger adults, necessitating a shift toward weight-based dosing standards where feasible.

The management of this patient also exposes broader barriers to evidence-based practice in rare, complex anaphylaxis. For instance, corticosteroids were administered in this case following the first recurrence, adhering to traditional protocols aimed at preventing biphasic reactions. However, the discussion highlights that contemporary evidence suggests no benefit for corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, with some studies indicating potential harm. The persistence of such practices, despite conflicting evidence, illustrates the difficulty in updating clinical behavior when robust randomized controlled trials are absent. The rarity of multiphasic anaphylaxis makes high-quality research difficult to conduct in emergency settings, leaving clinicians to rely on expert consensus or institutional protocols that may lag behind emerging data.

Despite the complexities of in-hospital management, the ultimate outcome relies heavily on effective patient education and discharge planning. Upon resolution, the patient was discharged with an epinephrine auto-injector and instructed on its use, alongside a new maintenance antihistamine regimen. This aligns with standard practice to ensure patient safety against future accidental exposures. Furthermore, avoidance strategies are paramount; in this case, clarithromycin was identified as the trigger, and further allergy confirmation testing was deemed unnecessary and potentially dangerous given the severity of the reaction. Clinicians must prioritize teaching patients to recognize early symptoms and carry rescue medication, as the risk of fatal anaphylaxis, while low, remains a significant concern for those with a history of severe reactions.

Finally, the resolution of this case underscores the need for future research strategies tailored to rare conditions. The authors propose that a registry of complex anaphylaxis cases could provide the backbone for data-driven expert recommendations, as traditional research methods are often hampered by the low incidence of multiphasic courses. For the medical student or resident, this implies that case reports are not merely anecdotal but serve as essential contributions to the medical literature when large-scale trials are impossible. Understanding the limitations of current evidence and the value of systematic data collection is crucial for developing more targeted, patient-centered management strategies for high-risk allergic presentations.
