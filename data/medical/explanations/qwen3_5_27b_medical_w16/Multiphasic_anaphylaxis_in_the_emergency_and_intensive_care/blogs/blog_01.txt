Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The 0.5mg Rule: Why Weight-Based Epinephrine Dosing Matters in Adult Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a high-stakes emergency where seconds count, but what happens after the first dose of epinephrine is often where the real danger lies. Most of us are trained to recognize the classic triad of airway, breathing, and circulation compromise, and we know the drill: epinephrine IM, fluids, and airway support. But a recent case report from Schneider et al. highlights a terrifying subset of patients who don't just get better and go home—they crash, recover, and crash again. This case of multiphasic anaphylaxis in a 104 kg woman serves as a stark reminder that our standard dosing protocols might not be cutting it for every adult, and that observation periods need to be flexible enough to catch the second, third, or fourth wave of symptoms.

The patient was a woman in her 60s weighing 104 kg who presented to the ED with anaphylaxis following a single dose of clarithromycin. Her initial presentation was severe, with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxia (88% on room air), and generalized urticaria. She was treated promptly with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids. Initially, she responded well, with blood pressure normalizing and swelling decreasing. However, the clinical course took a turn for the worse within the first 12 hours of admission. Despite adequate initial stabilization, she suffered recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock, requiring a total of four epinephrine doses across her hospital stay. She was eventually transferred to the ICU, and while the intensity of symptoms lessened with each episode, the recurrence pattern was unmistakable. She was discharged after three days with no further relapses, but the underlying cause was suspected to be systemic mastocytosis based on elevated tryptase levels and hepatomegaly, even though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria.

This case forces us to confront the "auto-injector standard" in the hospital setting. The patient received her first two doses of epinephrine via a 0.3 mg auto-injector, while the subsequent two doses were individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular injections. Current guidelines recommend an epinephrine dosage of 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, with a maximum total dose of 0.5 mg. For a 104 kg patient, the calculated weight-based dose would theoretically exceed 0.5 mg, but the cap applies. However, the use of a 0.3 mg auto-injector in an adult over 50 kg represents a significant underdosing compared to the 0.5 mg standard often recommended for adults. The discussion in the case report highlights a troubling trend: many auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg, even when intended for adults. A 2025 retrospective study cited in the text showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine needed escalation of care, such as additional injections, significantly more often than those who initially received 0.5 mg. While the auto-injector is invaluable for minimizing delay in preclinical settings or for staff unfamiliar with vasoactive agents, relying on it for a heavy adult in a medical setting may leave them undertreated during the critical first minutes of resuscitation.

Beyond the dosing debate, the multiphasic nature of this patient's reaction underscores the difficulty in defining observation protocols. Biphasic anaphylaxis is well-documented, but multiphasic courses with multiple relapses are rare and present a unique challenge. There are no clear risk factors to predict who will relapse, and definitions of biphasic reactions vary between guidelines. This patient required admission to the ICU after the first relapse and was monitored for approximately 24 hours without renewed symptoms before transfer to a standard ward. While traditional observation periods often range from 4 to 6 hours, this case suggests that for severe initial presentations or those requiring multiple doses of epinephrine, a longer observation window is prudent. Additionally, the workup for underlying causes is often missed. Tryptase levels were not drawn during the initial episodes in this case, violating the guideline recommendation for an initial sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a peak sample 1–2 hours after onset, and a baseline sample 24 hours after resolution. Missing these windows can obscure diagnoses like systemic mastocytosis, which predisposes patients to severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

Finally, we must address the role of adjunctive therapies like corticosteroids. In this case, corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence due to the traditional belief that they prevent biphasic reactions. However, the case report notes that mounting evidence demonstrates no benefit and even potential harmful effects, with recent systematic reviews showing no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Despite this, their use in clinical practice persists. While the patient in this report did not suffer further relapses after steroid administration, the evidence suggests this was likely due to the natural course of the reaction or the efficacy of repeated epinephrine rather than the steroids themselves. As we refine our management of complex anaphylaxis, we need to move away from reflexive steroid use and focus on adequate epinephrine dosing and appropriate observation.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Dose by Weight, Not Just Convenience:** For adults over 50 kg, consider using 0.5 mg of epinephrine rather than a 0.3 mg auto-injector to avoid potential underdosing, especially in severe presentations.
*   **Watch for Multiphasic Reactions:** Patients with severe initial anaphylaxis or those requiring multiple epinephrine doses are at higher risk for biphasic or multiphasic recurrence and may require observation longer than the standard 4–6 hours.
*   **Tryptase Timing is Critical:** To diagnose underlying mast cell disorders, obtain tryptase levels at three specific intervals: immediately upon presentation, 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and 24 hours after full resolution.
*   **Question Routine Steroids:** Current evidence does not support the routine use of corticosteroids to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis; focus on epinephrine and supportive care first.
*   **Consider Mastocytosis:** In cases of refractory or multiphasic anaphylaxis, investigate for systemic mastocytosis, particularly if baseline tryptase remains elevated.
