### [Management] In an adult patient weighing 104 kg presenting with anaphylactic shock, what is the recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose, and how does the standard 0.3 mg auto-injector compare to this guideline recommendation?

For an adult patient weighing 104 kg presenting with anaphylactic shock, the recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose is 0.5 mg. According to the guidelines cited in this case report, the standard dosing principle is 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, with a maximum total dose of 0.5 mg. While the patient’s weight-based calculation would theoretically exceed this cap, the guideline simplifies this to a flat 0.5 mg dose for adults weighing above 50 kg.

The standard 0.3 mg auto-injector represents a potentially critical underdose for adults in this weight category. In the case presented, the 104 kg patient initially received 0.3 mg via auto-injector in the emergency department. Despite an initial response, she experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis with four distinct episodes of shock. While the first two episodes were treated with the 0.3 mg auto-injector, the subsequent two episodes were managed with individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. The authors note that retrospective data suggests patients initially receiving 0.3 mg require escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving 0.5 mg.

However, clinical management must balance dosing accuracy with speed. As noted in the discussion, auto-injectors are preferred in time-sensitive settings or where personnel may hesitate with vasoactive agents, even if the dose is suboptimal. Ideally, for adults over 50 kg, particularly those with severe shock or higher body mass, clinicians should aim for the 0.5 mg dose via individually prepared syringe if available to avoid underdosing, while acknowledging that immediate administration of a 0.3 mg auto-injector remains preferable to treatment delay.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the recommended protocol for serum tryptase sampling to confirm anaphylaxis and assess for underlying mast cell disorders, including the specific timing relative to symptom onset and resolution?

To confirm anaphylaxis and assess for underlying mast cell disorders, current guidelines recommend obtaining a minimum of two, ideally three, serum tryptase samples at specific intervals. An initial sample should be drawn as soon as feasible during the acute reaction without delaying treatment. This is followed by a second sample 1 to 2 hours, but within 4 hours, of symptom onset, which corresponds to when tryptase levels usually peak. Finally, a third sample must be obtained at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish the patient's baseline level.

In the case presented, this recommended protocol was not adhered to during the initial workup. Tryptase levels were not determined during the first anaphylactic episodes; rather, the first sample was taken one hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and the second was drawn 27 hours after the last recurrence. Despite this suboptimal timing, the results revealed elevated tryptase levels during the episode and increased levels that remained elevated during follow-up. These findings supported a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although the patient did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. This case highlights that while delayed sampling can still yield diagnostic clues, strict adherence to the 1- to 2-hour peak window and the 24-hour post-resolution baseline is critical for accurately confirming anaphylaxis and distinguishing it from underlying mast cell pathology.

### [Management] Based on current systematic reviews and guideline updates, what is the evidence regarding the efficacy of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic reactions?

Based on the evidence summarized in this case report, current data do not support the efficacy of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. While our patient received intravenous corticosteroids following her first recurrence of anaphylaxis due to the traditional role of steroids in preventing biphasic courses, the discussion highlights a critical divergence between historical practice and contemporary evidence.

A 2017 systematic review cited in the report examined 22 studies, of which 21 showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Furthermore, recent studies emphasize potential harm; a 2019 study noted an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Consequently, most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration for anaphylaxis, despite the persistence of this practice in clinical settings as illustrated by our patient’s management. The focus of prevention should remain on prompt epinephrine administration and appropriate observation periods rather than adjunctive steroid therapy.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the clinical definition of multiphasic anaphylaxis differ from refractory anaphylaxis and sustained anaphylaxis due to ongoing allergen absorption?

**Clinical Pearl: Distinguishing Multiphasic, Refractory, and Sustained Anaphylaxis**

To distinguish these entities, you must focus on the temporal pattern of symptom resolution and the status of allergen exposure.

**Multiphasic anaphylaxis** is defined by an initial reaction followed by complete clinical resolution—an asymptomatic interval—and then a recurrence of symptoms without re-exposure to the trigger. In our case, the patient experienced four distinct episodes of anaphylaxis over approximately 12 hours. Crucially, between the initial emergency department presentation and the subsequent relapses, there were periods where blood pressure normalized and cutaneous symptoms decreased, indicating a true resolution before the next flare. This pattern, occurring after a single dose of clarithromycin which was immediately discontinued, fits the definition of multiphasic anaphylaxis rather than continuous exposure.

**Refractory anaphylaxis** differs fundamentally because the symptoms do not resolve. It refers to persistent anaphylaxis with no or insufficient response to correctly administered epinephrine. In our patient, epinephrine was effective in resolving each episode (e.g., blood pressure returned to normal, hypoxia resolved), distinguishing her course from refractory anaphylaxis where the shock state would have remained uncorrected despite treatment.

**Sustained anaphylaxis due to ongoing allergen absorption** mimics recurrence but lacks the true asymptomatic interval. This occurs when the allergen continues to enter the system, such as with residual food in the gastrointestinal tract or slow-release medications. While our patient took an oral antibiotic, the recurrence occurred after distinct asymptomatic gaps following a single 500-mg dose that was not repeated. The discussion notes that differentiating true biphasic reactions from ongoing absorption can be challenging, but the presence of a clear symptom-free window and the discontinuation of the trigger support a multiphasic diagnosis in this scenario.

**Clinical Takeaway:** Recognizing the asymptomatic interval is key. Because multiphasic reactions can occur up to 78 hours later and lack reliable predictors, this case underscores the necessity of extended observation for patients with severe initial presentations, especially those with underlying mast cell disorders like the suspected systemic mastocytosis in this patient.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with recurrent anaphylaxis and elevated baseline tryptase, what are the WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis, and which findings in this case suggested the diagnosis despite not meeting formal requirements?

Formal diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis requires meeting specific World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, which are categorized into major and minor findings. As noted in this case report, a formal diagnosis was not established because the major criteria were not assessable due to limited material obtained during the bone marrow biopsy, and the patient met only two of the WHO minor criteria.

Despite failing to meet the strict diagnostic threshold, several findings in this patient strongly suggested the diagnosis. Clinically, the patient presented with multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock requiring repeated epinephrine administration despite initial stabilization. Biochemically, tryptase levels were elevated during the third anaphylactic episode and remained elevated at baseline during follow-up. Additional supportive findings included hepatomegaly identified on abdominal sonography and possible neuropsychiatric symptoms, including depression and memory problems.

The underlying pathophysiology of systemic mastocytosis involves an abnormal increase in mast cells and excessive mediator release, which predisposes patients to severe and recurrent anaphylaxis. In this case, the combination of multiphasic anaphylaxis, elevated baseline tryptase, and organ involvement created a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, underscoring that clinical suspicion should remain high even when formal WHO criteria are not fully met due to sampling limitations.

### [Management] What is the generally recommended minimum observation period following an episode of anaphylaxis to monitor for biphasic reactions, and how should risk stratification influence this duration?

The traditional recommendation for observation following anaphylaxis is 4 to 6 hours after symptom resolution; however, this window may be insufficient for high-risk patients, as biphasic reactions can occur up to 78 hours later, with approximately half occurring within 6 to 12 hours. While the 2021 UK guideline proposes a three-tier risk-stratified approach to monitoring, the case report highlights that reliable predictors for targeted monitoring are not yet available.

In this case, the patient presented with severe initial anaphylactic shock requiring multiple epinephrine doses—a known risk factor for biphasic or multiphasic courses. Despite initial stabilization, she experienced recurrent episodes within the first 12 hours of admission, necessitating ICU transfer and extended monitoring. She was only transferred to a standard ward after 24 hours without renewed symptoms. This underscores that while standard observation periods range from 4 to 6 hours, patients with severe presentations, delayed epinephrine administration, or multiple epinephrine doses require prolonged observation, potentially up to 24 hours, to mitigate the risk of relapse. Current evidence does not support the use of corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions, making adequate observation the primary safety strategy. Ultimately, clinicians must balance healthcare resources with patient safety, recognizing that complex courses like multiphasic anaphylaxis defy standard protocols and demand individualized, extended monitoring.

### [Morning Report] During a morning report discussion, what clinical features are considered potential predictors for the development of a biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic course, and how reliable are these predictors currently?

There are currently no reliable clinical predictors for the development of a biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic course. While several features have been suggested in the literature—including delayed administration of epinephrine, severe initial presentation, the requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of prior biphasic reactions—these do not allow for targeted risk stratification or monitoring decisions.

As noted in the discussion of this case, the absence of reliable predictors complicates the management of anaphylaxis, particularly regarding observation duration. In the patient presented, several of these suggested risk factors were present: she had a severe initial presentation with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxaemia, and she required multiple doses of epinephrine (four total episodes). Despite this, the multiphasic nature of her reaction was not predictable based on these features alone.

Furthermore, while this patient’s workup revealed elevated tryptase levels and clinical features suggestive of systemic mastocytosis, the authors note that there is currently no research specifically addressing the role of mast cell disorders in biphasic anaphylaxis. Therefore, underlying mastocytosis cannot currently be used as a validated predictor.

Given the unreliability of clinical predictors, management must rely on adequate observation rather than risk stratification. Traditional observation periods of 4 to 6 hours may be insufficient, as biphasic reactions can occur up to 78 hours later. Additionally, clinicians should be aware that adjunctive therapies traditionally used to prevent relapse, such as corticosteroids, lack evidence of benefit for this purpose. In this case, despite the administration of prednisone and dexamethasone after the first recurrence, the patient continued to have episodes. Finally, attention to epinephrine dosing is critical; this patient, weighing 104 kg, initially received 0.3 mg auto-injectors, which may represent underdosing compared to the recommended 0.5 mg for adults.

In summary, because reliable predictors are unavailable, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for recurrence in any patient with severe anaphylaxis and ensure adequate observation, rather than relying on clinical features to rule out the risk of a biphasic course.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the pathophysiological mechanism underlying the recurrence of symptoms in biphasic anaphylaxis, and how might an underlying mast cell disorder like systemic mastocytosis predispose a patient to this course?

Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined clinically as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent symptom resolution in the absence of further allergen exposure. While the precise pathophysiological mechanism for recurrence in typical anaphylaxis remains debated, this case illustrates how an underlying mast cell disorder, such as systemic mastocytosis, may predispose a patient to a multiphasic course through an increased mast cell burden and excessive mediator release.

In the patient presented, a woman in her 60s experienced four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock following a single dose of clarithromycin. Despite initial stabilization with epinephrine and supportive care, she suffered recurrent relapses within the first 12 hours. Subsequent analysis revealed a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, characterized by elevated baseline tryptase levels that remained increased during follow-up, along with clinical findings such as hepatomegaly. As described in the discussion, systemic mastocytosis involves an abnormal increase in mast cells and excessive mediator release.

This pathophysiology offers a plausible explanation for the multiphasic recurrence observed in this patient. A higher baseline mast cell burden likely provided a larger reservoir of inflammatory mediators. Even after the initial allergen exposure was discontinued and the primary reaction treated, the underlying mast cell instability may have permitted delayed or repeated waves of degranulation. This aligns with the patient's elevated tryptase levels during the third episode and persistently elevated baseline levels, suggesting a chronic mast cell activation state rather than a simple acute allergic response.

Clinically, this underscores that patients presenting with severe, recurrent, or multiphasic anaphylaxis, particularly with elevated baseline tryptase, should be evaluated for underlying mast cell disorders. While current guidelines lack specific management strategies for multiphasic courses, recognizing the predisposition conferred by mast cell disorders is critical for risk stratification and determining the necessary duration of observation, as seen in this patient who required 24 hours of monitoring without relapse before transfer.
