Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Morning Report] During a morning report discussion, what clinical features are considered potential predictors for the development of a biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic course, and how reliable are these predictors currently?

There are currently no reliable clinical predictors for the development of a biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic course. While several features have been suggested in the literature—including delayed administration of epinephrine, severe initial presentation, the requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of prior biphasic reactions—these do not allow for targeted risk stratification or monitoring decisions.

As noted in the discussion of this case, the absence of reliable predictors complicates the management of anaphylaxis, particularly regarding observation duration. In the patient presented, several of these suggested risk factors were present: she had a severe initial presentation with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxaemia, and she required multiple doses of epinephrine (four total episodes). Despite this, the multiphasic nature of her reaction was not predictable based on these features alone.

Furthermore, while this patient’s workup revealed elevated tryptase levels and clinical features suggestive of systemic mastocytosis, the authors note that there is currently no research specifically addressing the role of mast cell disorders in biphasic anaphylaxis. Therefore, underlying mastocytosis cannot currently be used as a validated predictor.

Given the unreliability of clinical predictors, management must rely on adequate observation rather than risk stratification. Traditional observation periods of 4 to 6 hours may be insufficient, as biphasic reactions can occur up to 78 hours later. Additionally, clinicians should be aware that adjunctive therapies traditionally used to prevent relapse, such as corticosteroids, lack evidence of benefit for this purpose. In this case, despite the administration of prednisone and dexamethasone after the first recurrence, the patient continued to have episodes. Finally, attention to epinephrine dosing is critical; this patient, weighing 104 kg, initially received 0.3 mg auto-injectors, which may represent underdosing compared to the recommended 0.5 mg for adults.

In summary, because reliable predictors are unavailable, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for recurrence in any patient with severe anaphylaxis and ensure adequate observation, rather than relying on clinical features to rule out the risk of a biphasic course.
