Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] What is the pathophysiological mechanism underlying the recurrence of symptoms in biphasic anaphylaxis, and how might an underlying mast cell disorder like systemic mastocytosis predispose a patient to this course?

Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined clinically as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent symptom resolution in the absence of further allergen exposure. While the precise pathophysiological mechanism for recurrence in typical anaphylaxis remains debated, this case illustrates how an underlying mast cell disorder, such as systemic mastocytosis, may predispose a patient to a multiphasic course through an increased mast cell burden and excessive mediator release.

In the patient presented, a woman in her 60s experienced four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock following a single dose of clarithromycin. Despite initial stabilization with epinephrine and supportive care, she suffered recurrent relapses within the first 12 hours. Subsequent analysis revealed a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, characterized by elevated baseline tryptase levels that remained increased during follow-up, along with clinical findings such as hepatomegaly. As described in the discussion, systemic mastocytosis involves an abnormal increase in mast cells and excessive mediator release.

This pathophysiology offers a plausible explanation for the multiphasic recurrence observed in this patient. A higher baseline mast cell burden likely provided a larger reservoir of inflammatory mediators. Even after the initial allergen exposure was discontinued and the primary reaction treated, the underlying mast cell instability may have permitted delayed or repeated waves of degranulation. This aligns with the patient's elevated tryptase levels during the third episode and persistently elevated baseline levels, suggesting a chronic mast cell activation state rather than a simple acute allergic response.

Clinically, this underscores that patients presenting with severe, recurrent, or multiphasic anaphylaxis, particularly with elevated baseline tryptase, should be evaluated for underlying mast cell disorders. While current guidelines lack specific management strategies for multiphasic courses, recognizing the predisposition conferred by mast cell disorders is critical for risk stratification and determining the necessary duration of observation, as seen in this patient who required 24 hours of monitoring without relapse before transfer.
