# The 0.5mg Rule: Why Weight-Based Epinephrine Dosing Matters in Adult Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a high-stakes emergency where seconds count, but what happens after the first dose of epinephrine is often where the real danger lies. Most of us are trained to recognize the classic triad of airway, breathing, and circulation compromise, and we know the drill: epinephrine IM, fluids, and airway support. But a recent case report from Schneider et al. highlights a terrifying subset of patients who don't just get better and go home—they crash, recover, and crash again. This case of multiphasic anaphylaxis in a 104 kg woman serves as a stark reminder that our standard dosing protocols might not be cutting it for every adult, and that observation periods need to be flexible enough to catch the second, third, or fourth wave of symptoms.

The patient was a woman in her 60s weighing 104 kg who presented to the ED with anaphylaxis following a single dose of clarithromycin. Her initial presentation was severe, with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxia (88% on room air), and generalized urticaria. She was treated promptly with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids. Initially, she responded well, with blood pressure normalizing and swelling decreasing. However, the clinical course took a turn for the worse within the first 12 hours of admission. Despite adequate initial stabilization, she suffered recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock, requiring a total of four epinephrine doses across her hospital stay. She was eventually transferred to the ICU, and while the intensity of symptoms lessened with each episode, the recurrence pattern was unmistakable. She was discharged after three days with no further relapses, but the underlying cause was suspected to be systemic mastocytosis based on elevated tryptase levels and hepatomegaly, even though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria.

This case forces us to confront the "auto-injector standard" in the hospital setting. The patient received her first two doses of epinephrine via a 0.3 mg auto-injector, while the subsequent two doses were individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular injections. Current guidelines recommend an epinephrine dosage of 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, with a maximum total dose of 0.5 mg. For a 104 kg patient, the calculated weight-based dose would theoretically exceed 0.5 mg, but the cap applies. However, the use of a 0.3 mg auto-injector in an adult over 50 kg represents a significant underdosing compared to the 0.5 mg standard often recommended for adults. The discussion in the case report highlights a troubling trend: many auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg, even when intended for adults. A 2025 retrospective study cited in the text showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine needed escalation of care, such as additional injections, significantly more often than those who initially received 0.5 mg. While the auto-injector is invaluable for minimizing delay in preclinical settings or for staff unfamiliar with vasoactive agents, relying on it for a heavy adult in a medical setting may leave them undertreated during the critical first minutes of resuscitation.

Beyond the dosing debate, the multiphasic nature of this patient's reaction underscores the difficulty in defining observation protocols. Biphasic anaphylaxis is well-documented, but multiphasic courses with multiple relapses are rare and present a unique challenge. There are no clear risk factors to predict who will relapse, and definitions of biphasic reactions vary between guidelines. This patient required admission to the ICU after the first relapse and was monitored for approximately 24 hours without renewed symptoms before transfer to a standard ward. While traditional observation periods often range from 4 to 6 hours, this case suggests that for severe initial presentations or those requiring multiple doses of epinephrine, a longer observation window is prudent. Additionally, the workup for underlying causes is often missed. Tryptase levels were not drawn during the initial episodes in this case, violating the guideline recommendation for an initial sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a peak sample 1–2 hours after onset, and a baseline sample 24 hours after resolution. Missing these windows can obscure diagnoses like systemic mastocytosis, which predisposes patients to severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

Finally, we must address the role of adjunctive therapies like corticosteroids. In this case, corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence due to the traditional belief that they prevent biphasic reactions. However, the case report notes that mounting evidence demonstrates no benefit and even potential harmful effects, with recent systematic reviews showing no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Despite this, their use in clinical practice persists. While the patient in this report did not suffer further relapses after steroid administration, the evidence suggests this was likely due to the natural course of the reaction or the efficacy of repeated epinephrine rather than the steroids themselves. As we refine our management of complex anaphylaxis, we need to move away from reflexive steroid use and focus on adequate epinephrine dosing and appropriate observation.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Dose by Weight, Not Just Convenience:** For adults over 50 kg, consider using 0.5 mg of epinephrine rather than a 0.3 mg auto-injector to avoid potential underdosing, especially in severe presentations.
*   **Watch for Multiphasic Reactions:** Patients with severe initial anaphylaxis or those requiring multiple epinephrine doses are at higher risk for biphasic or multiphasic recurrence and may require observation longer than the standard 4–6 hours.
*   **Tryptase Timing is Critical:** To diagnose underlying mast cell disorders, obtain tryptase levels at three specific intervals: immediately upon presentation, 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and 24 hours after full resolution.
*   **Question Routine Steroids:** Current evidence does not support the routine use of corticosteroids to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis; focus on epinephrine and supportive care first.
*   **Consider Mastocytosis:** In cases of refractory or multiphasic anaphylaxis, investigate for systemic mastocytosis, particularly if baseline tryptase remains elevated.



# To Steroid or Not to Steroid? The Fading Role of Corticosteroids in Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a terrifying diagnosis for any clinician. It’s a race against time where epinephrine is the only true savior. But what happens when the patient stabilizes, only to crash again? And then again? We call this biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, and it presents a unique management nightmare.

A recent case report highlights exactly this struggle: a woman in her 60s who suffered through four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock following a single dose of clarithromycin. It’s a dramatic presentation that forces us to re-examine our standard protocols. Specifically, it forces us to ask a question that many of us answer on autopilot: Should we be giving corticosteroids to prevent these relapses?

In this case, the patient was admitted to the ICU after her first relapse. Following the first recurrence, the team administered corticosteroids—both prednisone and dexamethasone—based on the traditional belief that they prevent biphasic reactions. While the patient ultimately survived and was discharged after three days, the evidence suggests those steroids were likely doing nothing but adding potential side effects to her chart.

## The Case: When Anaphylaxis Keeps Coming Back

Our patient presented to the ED with hypotension (74/50 mmHg), hypoxia (88% on room air), and generalized urticaria one hour after taking her first dose of clarithromycin. She had a history of beta-lactam allergy but no prior anaphylaxis. She responded well to initial stabilization with 0.3 mg of intramuscular epinephrine, antihistamines, and fluids.

However, the drama didn't end there. Despite the initial resolution, she developed recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours. In total, she had four distinct anaphylactic episodes. Each required epinephrine to resolve. The intensity of symptoms actually lessened with each episode, but the recurrence was undeniable.

The clinical team escalated care to the ICU. After the first recurrence, they added corticosteroids to the regimen. She received 30 mg of IV prednisone daily for five days and a single dose of 8 mg IV dexamethasone. She also received additional antihistamines and fluids. Ultimately, she stabilized after 24 hours without further relapse.

While she recovered, the case revealed two critical teaching points: the potential underdosing of epinephrine in larger adults and the questionable utility of steroids in preventing relapse.

## The Steroid Dogma vs. The Evidence

For decades, the management of anaphylaxis included a "triple threat": epinephrine, antihistamines, and corticosteroids. The logic was sound in theory: if mast cells are degranulating, maybe steroids can dampen the inflammation and prevent a second wave.

However, the data simply does not support this practice. As noted in the case discussion, a 2017 systematic review examined 22 studies regarding the use of corticosteroids in anaphylaxis. The results were stark: only one study supported the use of corticosteroids, while 21 studies showed no evidence that they reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis.

Furthermore, we have to consider the risk profile. A 2019 study suggested an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. While the mechanism isn't fully clear, the signal is there. If a medication offers no proven benefit and carries a risk of increased resource utilization or side effects, we need to stop prescribing it routinely.

In our patient's case, the steroids were administered after the first relapse had already occurred. This timing highlights the reactive nature of the decision rather than a preventative strategy. Given that she had four episodes, it is impossible to say the steroids prevented a fifth, but the literature suggests they likely didn't stop the first three either. The true workhorse of anaphylaxis management remains epinephrine.

## The Epinephrine Dosing Trap

While we are on the topic of treatment optimization, this case also shines a light on a common pitfall: epinephrine dosing in adults.

Our patient weighed 104 kg. The recommended dose for anaphylaxis is 0.01 mg/kg, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For a 104 kg patient, the ideal dose is 1.04 mg, capped at 0.5 mg. However, her initial treatment utilized a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. While auto-injectors are fantastic for minimizing delay in time-sensitive settings, the 0.3 mg dose is a significant underdose for an adult of her size.

The case report notes that a 2025 retrospective study found patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine needed escalation of care significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg. In this patient, the team eventually switched to individually prepared 0.5 mg doses for the subsequent episodes.

This is a critical distinction for the ED and prehospital provider. If you have a large adult patient, do not settle for the 0.3 mg auto-injector if you can safely draw up 0.5 mg. Hesitancy to use vasoactive agents is common, but underdosing is a bigger risk.

## The Hidden Driver: Mastocytosis

Why did this patient have multiphasic anaphylaxis? The workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis. She had elevated baseline tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms consistent with the condition, though she did not meet all formal diagnostic criteria.

Systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe anaphylaxis due to an abnormal increase in mast cells and excessive mediator release. This underlying pathology likely explains the refractory and multiphasic nature of her reaction. It also highlights the importance of checking tryptase levels.

In this case, tryptase levels were not drawn during the initial workup, which is a missed opportunity. Guidelines recommend a sample as soon as feasible, a second sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset (when levels peak), and a third baseline sample 24 hours after resolution. Missing these windows makes diagnosing underlying mast cell disorders much harder.

## Bottom Line

This case reminds us that anaphylaxis management is evolving. We are moving away from "kitchen sink" therapy toward targeted, evidence-based interventions. While the patient in this report survived, her course underscores the need for better dosing, longer observation for high-risk patients, and a critical re-evaluation of our steroid habits.

*   **Epinephrine is the only priority:** Administer IM epinephrine immediately. Do not delay for antihistamines or steroids.
*   **Check the dose:** For adults over 50 kg, 0.3 mg auto-injectors may underdose. Aim for 0.5 mg (0.01 mg/kg) if available.
*   **Skip the steroids:** Current evidence shows no benefit for corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. Do not use them routinely.
*   **Watch the patient:** Biphasic reactions can occur up to 78 hours later, though most happen within 24 hours. Risk-stratify observation periods based on severity and epinephrine requirements.
*   **Think Mastocytosis:** In severe, refractory, or multiphasic cases, consider underlying mast cell disorders. Draw tryptase at onset, peak, and baseline (24 hours post-resolution).



# When to Discharge? Risk Stratification and Observation Times for Biphasic Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a high-acuity emergency that demands immediate action, but the story often doesn't end when the blood pressure stabilizes. We frequently discuss the initial management—epinephrine, airway, fluids—but the post-stabilization phase is where the real clinical ambiguity lies. How long do you watch them? When is it safe to send them home?

A recent case report from Schneider et al. highlights the nightmare scenario that keeps emergency physicians up at night: multiphasic anaphylaxis. This isn't just a biphasic reaction with one recurrence; this was a patient who crashed, stabilized, and then crashed three more times within the first 12 hours of admission. It forces us to reconsider our standard observation protocols and risk stratification models.

## The Case: A Rollercoaster of Anaphylactic Shock

The patient was a woman in her 60s weighing 104 kg with a history of diabetes, hypertension, and a known beta-lactam allergy. She presented to the ED one hour after taking her first dose of clarithromycin, prescribed for erysipelas after a rash developed on clindamycin. She was in anaphylactic shock: hypotensive at 74/50 mm Hg, hypoxic at 88% on room air, with generalized urticaria, flushing, and throat swelling.

The initial response was textbook. She received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector, antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids. Her vitals normalized, the swelling decreased, and she was admitted to an acute internal ward for monitoring.

Here is where the case becomes a cautionary tale. Despite the initial resolution, the patient developed recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours. She required a total of four epinephrine doses to manage the relapses. The first two were 0.3 mg via auto-injector, and the subsequent two were 0.5 mg prepared individually. The severity of the episodes varied, starting with deep hypoxia and cyanosis and progressing to dyspnea and rash recurrence, but each required intervention. She was transferred to the ICU after the first relapse.

Ultimately, she remained symptom-free for 24 hours before transferring back to a standard ward and was discharged on day three. Further workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, evidenced by elevated tryptase levels and hepatomegaly, though formal diagnostic criteria were not met.

## The Observation Time Debate

The core tension in this case revolves around observation duration. Traditional guidelines often suggest a 4 to 6-hour observation period following symptom resolution. However, the literature indicates that biphasic reactions can occur up to 78 hours later, with approximately half occurring within 6 to 12 hours.

In this patient’s case, a standard 4-hour observation window would have been catastrophic. She was stable initially, but the recurrence happened while she was already admitted, yet still within the first 12 hours. The UK Resuscitation Council 2021 guidelines attempt to address this with a three-tier risk-stratified approach, acknowledging that not all anaphylaxis patients carry the same risk of relapse.

Risk factors for biphasic reactions include severe initial presentation, delayed epinephrine administration, and the need for multiple doses of epinephrine. This patient hit almost every risk factor: she presented in shock, required multiple doses, and had an underlying mast cell disorder. This suggests that a "one-size-fits-all" observation period is insufficient. High-risk patients likely require extended monitoring, potentially overnight, rather than a rigid 6-hour rule.

## Dosing and Medication Nuances

This case also brings up critical discussions regarding epinephrine dosing and the role of adjunctive therapies. The patient weighed 104 kg. The recommended epinephrine dosage is 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults. However, the initial treatment utilized a 0.3 mg auto-injector. While auto-injectors are essential for speed and reducing hesitation, using a 0.3 mg device in an adult over 50 kg may result in underdosing. A 2025 retrospective study cited in the discussion notes that patients receiving 0.3 mg initially required escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving 0.5 mg.

Furthermore, the patient received corticosteroids (prednisone and dexamethasone) after the first recurrence, following traditional protocols intended to prevent biphasic reactions. However, current evidence is shifting away from this practice. A 2017 systematic review found no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis, and recent studies suggest they may even increase admission rates due to side effects. Despite this, their use persists in clinical practice, as seen in this case.

## The Mast Cell Connection

Finally, we must consider the underlying etiology. This patient had a multiphasic course, which is rarer than biphasic anaphylaxis. The investigation revealed elevated tryptase levels during and after the episodes, along with hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms, pointing toward systemic mastocytosis.

Systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe anaphylaxis and complex courses due to abnormal mast cell proliferation and mediator release. While there is no specific research linking mastocytosis directly to biphasic anaphylaxis yet, the presence of elevated baseline tryptase should prompt clinicians to consider this diagnosis in patients with refractory or multiphasic reactions. Unfortunately, tryptase levels were not drawn during the initial workup in this case, which highlights a gap in the acute management protocol. Guidelines recommend an initial sample, a peak sample 1–2 hours after onset, and a baseline sample 24 hours after resolution to properly evaluate this.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Observation times are not static:** While 4–6 hours is standard, patients with severe initial shock, multiple epinephrine requirements, or suspected mast cell disorders may require extended observation (12–24 hours) to rule out biphasic or multiphasic reactions.
*   **Watch the epinephrine dose:** For adults weighing over 50 kg, a 0.3 mg auto-injector may be an underdose. Consider 0.5 mg IM (0.01 mg/kg) to ensure adequate receptor stimulation, especially in severe shock.
*   **Re-evaluate steroid use:** Corticosteroids are traditionally given to prevent biphasic reactions, but evidence does not support this benefit and they carry risks. Reserve their use for specific indications rather than routine administration.
*   **Check the tryptase:** In complex or severe anaphylaxis, obtain tryptase levels at onset, 1–2 hours later, and 24 hours after resolution to screen for underlying mastocytosis.
*   **Risk stratify your discharges:** Do not discharge high-risk patients based solely on symptom resolution. Factors like shock severity and underlying comorbidities should dictate monitoring duration.



# The Silent Underlayer: Clinical Clues for Systemic Mastocytosis in Severe Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a high-stakes diagnosis that we train for relentlessly. The algorithm is usually straightforward: identify the trigger, recognize the systemic reaction, administer epinephrine, and support the airway and circulation. Most patients respond, stabilize, and go home with an auto-injector and a referral. However, a subset of cases refuses to follow the script. These are the patients who stabilize, only to crash again hours later, or those who require multiple doses of epinephrine despite aggressive management. When anaphylaxis becomes multiphasic or refractory, we must look beyond the immediate allergen. We need to ask if there is a silent underlayer driving the severity.

A recent case report highlights this exact challenge, presenting a woman in her 60s who experienced four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock following a single dose of clarithromycin. This wasn't just a biphasic reaction; it was multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by multiple relapses into shock despite adequate initial care. While the immediate trigger was identified, the clinical course suggested something more profound was at play. The analysis ultimately pointed toward systemic mastocytosis, a condition where abnormal mast cell proliferation predisposes patients to severe, recurrent mediator release. This case serves as a critical reminder that severe or recurrent anaphylaxis can be the presenting symptom of an underlying mast cell disorder, and we need to know what clues to look for.

## The Multiphasic Challenge

The patient presented to the emergency department with classic signs of anaphylaxis: hypotension, hypoxia, pruritus, flushing, and generalized urticaria. She had taken clarithromycin just one hour prior, a medication she had never used before, after switching from clindamycin due to a rash. She responded well initially, with blood pressure normalizing and skin symptoms resolving.

However, the stability was deceptive. Within the first 12 hours of admission, she suffered recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock. This required transfer to the intensive care unit and a total of four epinephrine administrations across the course of her admission. The intensity of symptoms waned with each episode, but the recurrence itself was alarming. This pattern—initial response followed by multiple relapses without re-exposure to the allergen—is the hallmark of multiphasic anaphylaxis. While biphasic reactions are discussed in guidelines, multiphasic courses present a unique management void. Clinicians are often left deciding between aggressive pre-emptive epinephrine infusion or an expectant approach, with little literature to guide the choice.

## Unmasking the Underlying Disorder

What drives such a volatile course? In this case, the clinical team suspected systemic mastocytosis. While the patient did not meet the formal diagnostic criteria—likely due to limited material obtained during bone marrow biopsy—the probability was high based on specific clinical and laboratory findings. This is where the "silent underlayer" becomes visible. We often focus entirely on the allergen, but specific non-allergic clues can point toward a mast cell mediator disorder.

In this patient, the workup revealed elevated tryptase levels during the third anaphylactic episode, and critically, her baseline tryptase levels remained increased during follow-up. Furthermore, abdominal sonography showed hepatomegaly, and the patient had a history of neuropsychiatric symptoms, including depression and memory problems. These findings align with the multiorgan inflammation and excessive mediator release seen in mastocytosis. Recognizing these associations is vital because patients with underlying mast cell disorders are at higher risk for severe, life-threatening anaphylaxis and may require different long-term management strategies than those with simple IgE-mediated allergies.

## The Tryptase Trap

One of the most frustrating aspects of this case was the timing of the diagnostic testing. Guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three, tryptase samples to accurately assess mast cell activation. The protocol suggests an initial sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second sample 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset when levels peak, and a third sample at least 24 hours after resolution to establish a baseline. In this case, the first tryptase sample was not taken until one hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode.

This delay is a common pitfall in emergency medicine. In the heat of resuscitation, obtaining a blood sample often takes a backseat to airway management and epinephrine administration. However, missing the acute tryptase peak and the baseline comparison can obscure the diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis. If the baseline is not established, we cannot determine if the elevation is due to the acute event or an underlying pathology. For patients with severe, recurrent, or unexplained anaphylaxis, adhering to this sampling schedule is a low-cost, high-yield intervention that can change the diagnostic trajectory.

## Management Nuances and Controversies

The management of this patient also highlights several ongoing debates in anaphylaxis care. First, consider the epinephrine dosing. The patient initially received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector for the first two episodes. While auto-injectors are essential for minimizing treatment delay, the patient weighed 104 kg. Guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults. The subsequent episodes required individually prepared doses of 0.5 mg. The case report notes that patients initially receiving 0.3 mg may need escalation of care more often than those receiving 0.5 mg, suggesting that reliance on standard auto-injector doses in larger adults could lead to underdosing.

Second, the role of corticosteroids remains contentious. This patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone after the first recurrence, adhering to traditional protocols aimed at preventing biphasic reactions. However, mounting evidence, including systematic reviews, shows no benefit in reducing the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis and highlights potential side effects. Despite this, many hospital protocols, including the one in this case, still include steroids. Clinicians must be aware that while steroids may be administered, they should not be relied upon to prevent relapse; epinephrine remains the cornerstone of therapy.

## Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for underlying mast cell disorders when anaphylaxis presents with an atypical or multiphasic course. The clues are often there if we look for them: unexplained hepatomegaly, neuropsychiatric history, and elevated baseline tryptase. Furthermore, optimizing our acute management—specifically regarding tryptase sampling timing and epinephrine dosing based on weight—can improve outcomes and diagnostic accuracy. Anaphylaxis is often a standalone emergency, but sometimes it is the tip of the iceberg.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect Mastocytosis in Severe Cases:** Consider underlying systemic mastocytosis in patients with severe, recurrent, or multiphasic anaphylaxis, especially if there are non-allergic clues like hepatomegaly or neuropsychiatric symptoms.
*   **Master Tryptase Timing:** Adhere to the three-sample tryptase protocol (acute, peak 1-2 hours, baseline 24 hours post-resolution) to differentiate acute activation from underlying mast cell disorders.

# The Tryptase Timing Trap: Diagnostic Pitfalls in Acute Anaphylaxis Workup

Anaphylaxis is one of those emergencies where the treatment is straightforward, but the aftermath is often messy. We recognize the hypotension, the urticaria, the airway compromise, and we push epinephrine. But what happens when the patient comes back? What happens when the symptoms resolve, only to return hours later? A recent case report from Schneider et al. highlights a woman in her 60s who suffered through four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock after a single dose of clarithromycin. While the clinical management of the multiphasic reaction was challenging, the real teaching moment lies in the diagnostic workup that followed. Specifically, this case illustrates how easily we can miss an underlying mast cell disorder simply by getting the lab timing wrong.

The patient presented with classic signs of anaphylaxis: hypotension, hypoxia, flushing, and generalized urticaria within an hour of taking the antibiotic. She responded well to initial treatment with intramuscular epinephrine and fluids, but she wasn't done. Over the next 12 hours, she relapsed into anaphylactic shock three more times, requiring a total of four epinephrine doses. This multiphasic course is rare, occurring in roughly 4% to 6% of cases, and it naturally raises the question of why this patient was so reactive. The team eventually suspected systemic mastocytosis due to elevated tryptase levels and hepatomegaly, but the diagnosis remained inconclusive. Why? Because the critical window for capturing the diagnostic data had closed before they even drew the blood.

Tryptase is the biomarker we rely on to confirm anaphylaxis and screen for mast cell disorders, but it is notoriously fleeting. It peaks quickly and then clears. In this case, the first tryptase sample was not drawn during the initial presentation. Instead, the first sample was taken one hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode. The second sample was drawn 27 hours after the last recurrence. By the time they got the labs, the acute spike had likely passed, and the baseline was difficult to contextualize against the peak. The guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three, tryptase samples taken at specific intervals: an initial sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset when levels peak, and a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish a baseline.

The consequence of this timing error in the case report was significant. The analysis showed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, yet the patient did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. This was partly because the major criteria could not be assessed due to limited material from the bone marrow biopsy, but the unreliable tryptase data compounded the uncertainty. Elevated tryptase levels during the third episode and elevated baseline levels were noted, but without a true peak measurement from the initial insult, the delta (the difference between peak and baseline) could not be calculated accurately. This delta is crucial for distinguishing acute anaphylaxis from underlying mast cell disease. Furthermore, the patient had other signs consistent with mastocytosis, including hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms like depression and memory problems, which might have been investigated more aggressively had the tryptase data been definitive earlier.

This case forces us to confront a common pitfall in the emergency department: the prioritization of stabilization over diagnostic sampling. We know that treatment should never be delayed for labs, but drawing a vial of blood takes seconds. In the heat of the moment, we often forget to draw the "acute" tryptase, or we draw it too late during a relapse phase. In this patient's case, the multiphasic nature of the reaction made the timing even more confusing, as the team had to decide which episode represented the "onset." The lesson is clear: if you suspect anaphylaxis, you need to lock in that baseline and peak data immediately. If you miss the window, you leave your patient with a diagnosis of exclusion rather than a confirmed underlying condition that could dictate long-term management, such as the need for mast cell stabilizers or avoidance of specific triggers beyond just the initial allergen.

We also have to consider the broader management context provided in this report. The patient received corticosteroids after the first recurrence, a practice that is becoming increasingly controversial. Recent evidence suggests corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic reactions and may increase the risk of ICU admission. Additionally, the epinephrine dosing is worth noting; the patient received 0.3 mg auto-injectors initially, despite weighing 104 kg. Current guidelines recommend 0.5 mg for adults, and studies suggest underdosing with 0.3 mg devices can lead to the need for escalation of care. While the focus here is on the tryptase timing, the combination of potential underdosing and missed diagnostic windows creates a perfect storm for suboptimal outcomes in complex anaphylaxis cases.

Ultimately, the patient was discharged after three days with an epinephrine auto-injector and antihistamines, having had no further episodes. However, the diagnostic uncertainty remains. A clearer understanding of complex anaphylaxis courses requires better data, and that data starts with a simple blood draw at the right time. We cannot change the past of this patient, but we can ensure the next one gets a definitive answer.

*   **Draw the acute tryptase immediately:** Obtain a sample as soon as feasible during the initial presentation without delaying epinephrine administration.
*   **Capture the peak:** Draw a second tryptase sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset to catch the peak levels.
*   **Establish the baseline:** A third sample must be drawn at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish baseline tryptase levels.
*   **Calculate the delta:** Compare the peak and baseline levels to assess for anaphylaxis severity and screen for underlying mast cell disorders like systemic mastocytosis.
*   **Watch for multiphasic courses:** Recognize that symptom recurrence can happen up to 78 hours later, requiring extended observation and careful documentation of symptom timing for lab correlation.
*   **Reconsider epinephrine dosing:** Ensure adults receive the recommended 0.5 mg dose rather than relying solely on 0.3 mg auto-injectors, especially in patients over 50 kg.



# Biphasic, Refractory, or Sustained? Defining the Course to Guide Management

Anaphylaxis is a high-stakes emergency where seconds count. We know the algorithm: airway, breathing, circulation, and epinephrine. But what happens when the patient stabilizes, only to crash again hours later? Or when they seem to respond to epinephrine but keep slipping back into shock?

A recent case report highlights a woman in her 60s who presented with anaphylaxis to clarithromycin, only to experience a total of four distinct anaphylactic episodes over the course of 12 hours. She required multiple doses of epinephrine, admission to the ICU, and a workup that suggested an underlying mast cell disorder. This isn't just a "bad allergy"; it's a complex clinical course that challenges our definitions and our management protocols.

In the ED, we often use terms like "biphasic" or "refractory" interchangeably. However, distinguishing between these courses is critical. It dictates how long you observe the patient, whether they need ICU admission, and how aggressively you escalate therapy. Let’s break down the terminology, learn from this case, and refine our approach to complex anaphylaxis.

## The Case: When One Dose Isn't Enough

The patient was a 60-year-old woman with a history of diabetes, hypertension, and a known beta-lactam allergy. She presented to the ED one hour after taking a single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin, prescribed for erysipelas. She was hypotensive (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxic (88% on room air), and had generalized urticaria, facial swelling, and agitation.

She received standard initial care: 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector, IV clemastine, oxygen, and 500 mL of normal saline. She responded well initially, with blood pressure normalizing and swelling decreasing. However, despite this apparent resolution, she suffered recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours.

In total, she had four anaphylactic episodes. The first two were treated with 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors, and the subsequent two required individually prepared 0.5 mg IM doses. She was eventually transferred to the ICU after the first relapse. Interestingly, the intensity of symptoms lessened with each episode, eventually resolving after approximately 24 hours without recurrence.

Workup revealed elevated tryptase levels during the third episode and at baseline, raising suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. This case underscores a crucial problem: multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, guidelines are lacking, and our terminology often obscures the clinical reality.

## Defining the Course: Why Semantics Matter

The discussion in the case report highlights a significant gap in consensus. We need to be precise about what we are seeing to manage it correctly.

**Biphasic Anaphylaxis** is defined by the Resuscitation Council UK as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent symptom resolution in the absence of further allergen exposure. In our case, the patient stabilized, then crashed again without taking more clarithromycin. This fits the biphasic (or in this case, multiphasic) definition.

**Refractory Anaphylaxis**, by contrast, refers to persistent anaphylaxis with no or insufficient response to correctly administered epinephrine. If a patient remains hypotensive and hypoxic despite adequate epinephrine dosing and fluids, they are refractory. This requires escalation to IV epinephrine infusion and aggressive resuscitation.

**Sustained Anaphylaxis** can mimic biphasic reactions but is actually due to ongoing allergen absorption. This might occur with slow-release medications or food allergens still in the GI tract. Differentiating this from true biphasic recurrence is challenging but vital.

In our patient, the symptoms resolved between episodes, pointing toward a multiphasic course rather than true refractory anaphylaxis. However, the need for repeated epinephrine doses blurred the line, necessitating ICU-level monitoring.

## Management Implications: Dosing, Steroids, and Observation

How does this classification change what we do at the bedside?

First, consider epinephrine dosing. Guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum total dose of 0.5 mg for adults. This is often simplified to 0.5 mg for most adults. However, our patient received 0.3 mg auto-injectors for the first two episodes. At 104 kg, she was significantly underdosed. A 2025 retrospective study cited in the report showed that patients initially receiving 0.3 mg needed escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving 0.5 mg. While auto-injectors reduce treatment delay, relying on them for adults over 50 kg in a hospital setting can lead to critical underdosing.

Second, the role of corticosteroids is increasingly controversial. Our patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone after the first recurrence, following traditional protocol to prevent biphasic reactions. However, mounting evidence suggests no benefit. A 2017 systematic review found only one study supporting steroids, with 21 showing no evidence they reduce biphasic risk. Recent data even suggests steroids might increase the likelihood of ICU admission. Most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine use, yet practice often lags behind evidence.

Third, observation periods are debated. Traditional advice is 4 to 6 hours. However, the Dutch NHG and World Allergy Organisation note that half of biphasic reactions occur within 6 to 12 hours. For high-risk patients—like those with severe initial presentation, delayed epinephrine, or multiple doses required—extended monitoring is prudent. In this case, the patient required ICU admission after the first relapse.

Finally, consider underlying pathology. Our patient had elevated tryptase levels and hepatomegaly, suggestive of systemic mastocytosis. This condition predisposes patients to severe anaphylaxis and may explain the multiphasic course. While not all anaphylaxis is mastocytosis, persistent or multiphasic reactions warrant a closer look at baseline tryptase and mast cell disorders.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Distinguish the Course:** Differentiate between biphasic (resolution then recurrence), refractory (persistent despite treatment), and sustained (ongoing absorption) anaphylaxis to guide monitoring and escalation.
*   **Dose Appropriately:** For adults over 50 kg, 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors may be underdosing. Consider 0.5 mg IM epinephrine in hospital settings to reduce the need for escalation.
*   **Re-evaluate Steroids:** Current evidence does not support routine corticosteroid use for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis; weigh the lack of benefit against potential side effects.
*   **Monitor Closely:** High-risk features (severe initial shock, multiple epinephrine doses, delayed treatment) warrant extended observation, potentially exceeding the standard 4–6 hours.
*   **Think Beyond the Allergen:** Recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis should prompt consideration of underlying mast cell disorders, supported by baseline and acute tryptase levels.
