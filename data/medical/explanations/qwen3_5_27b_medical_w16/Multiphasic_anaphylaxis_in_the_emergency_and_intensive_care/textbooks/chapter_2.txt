Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 2: ## Acute Clinical Presentation and Initial Stabilization

The initial management of anaphylaxis relies on rapid recognition of the clinical syndrome and immediate intervention to prevent cardiovascular collapse. In this case, a woman in her 60s with a body weight of 104 kg presented to the emergency department following the ingestion of a single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin. Her medical history included type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, specifically a prior mild reaction to amoxicillin. While she had no previous history of anaphylaxis, the history of atopy and prior drug sensitivity is clinically relevant, as patients with a history of allergic reactions may be at increased risk for severe responses to new allergens. The temporal relationship between the oral intake of clarithromycin and the onset of symptoms—one hour post-ingestion—strongly supported a causal link, necessitating the immediate discontinuation of the suspected trigger.

Recognition of anaphylactic shock requires a systematic assessment of the airway, breathing, and circulation, particularly when cutaneous signs are present. This patient exhibited classic mucocutaneous symptoms, including pruritus, flushing, generalized erythema, urticaria, and facial swelling, alongside a subjective sensation of throat swelling. Crucially, the patient demonstrated signs of systemic involvement that defined the severity of the reaction. Her vital signs revealed significant hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxaemia (88% saturation on room air), indicating anaphylactic shock rather than a mild allergic reaction. Although cardiac and lung auscultation were initially normal and there was no observed tongue or throat swelling, the combination of hypotension and hypoxia with cutaneous manifestations met the clinical criteria for anaphylaxis, requiring urgent resuscitation.

The therapeutic rationale for initial stabilization prioritizes the restoration of perfusion and oxygenation while counteracting the systemic release of inflammatory mediators. In accordance with standard emergency protocols, the patient received intramuscular epinephrine as the first-line pharmacotherapy. Epinephrine acts on alpha-adrenergic receptors to induce vasoconstriction and improve blood pressure, while beta-adrenergic effects reduce mucosal edema and bronchospasm. Adjunctive therapies were administered to support these goals: supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) addressed the hypoxaemia, and a 500 mL bolus of normal saline was given to support intravascular volume in the setting of distributive shock. Additionally, intravenous clemastine was administered to manage the cutaneous symptoms. While antihistamines and corticosteroids do not reverse airway obstruction or hypotension, they are often used in conjunction with epinephrine to alleviate skin manifestations and potentially mitigate late-phase reactions, though their role in preventing recurrence remains debated.

A critical aspect of clinical decision-making in this case involved the selection of epinephrine dosage and administration route. Current guidelines recommend an intramuscular epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults. Given the patient’s weight of 104 kg, the calculated dose would approach the 0.5 mg maximum. However, the patient initially received 0.3 mg via an auto-injector. This decision reflects a practical compromise often encountered in emergency settings: while the 0.3 mg dose is technically lower than the weight-based recommendation for this patient, auto-injectors are often preferred in time-sensitive situations or by personnel less familiar with preparing individual doses. The primary objective is to minimize treatment delay, as hesitancy in administering vasoactive agents can lead to worse outcomes. This case highlights the tension between optimal dosing based on pharmacokinetics and the operational reality of rapid deployment in emergency medicine.

Following initial stabilization, the patient demonstrated a good clinical response, with blood pressure normalizing and cutaneous symptoms resolving. Despite this improvement, the decision was made to admit the patient to an acute internal ward for increased monitoring rather than discharge. This disposition decision is grounded in the risk of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, where symptoms can recur after an apparent resolution. Patients who present with anaphylactic shock, require multiple interventions, or have a delayed initial response to epinephrine are at higher risk for relapse. In this instance, the severity of the initial presentation (hypotension and hypoxia) warranted a period of observation to detect potential recurrence. The subsequent development of recurrent anaphylactic episodes within 12 hours validated the decision for admission, underscoring that initial clinical improvement does not guarantee safety and that observation protocols must account for the unpredictable course of severe allergic reactions.
