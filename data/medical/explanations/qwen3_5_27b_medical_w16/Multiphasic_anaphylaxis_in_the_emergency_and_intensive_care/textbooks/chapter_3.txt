Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 3: ## Management of Multiphasic and Refractory Episodes

The management of anaphylaxis becomes significantly more complex when the clinical course is multiphasic, characterized by recurrent episodes of symptoms despite initial resolution. In the presented case, a woman in her 60s experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes within the first 12 hours of admission following clarithromycin exposure. This clinical picture underscores a critical teaching point for clinicians: the initial response to epinephrine and supportive care does not guarantee safety, and vigilance for recurrence is required even after apparent stabilization. Biphasic or multiphasic reactions are defined by a recurrence of symptoms without re-exposure to the allergen, typically occurring within 24 hours, though reports exist of recurrences up to 78 hours later. In this case, the patient’s symptoms worsened progressively after the initial ED presentation, necessitating repeated interventions. Recognizing the pattern of recurrence is essential, as the intensity of symptoms in this patient lessened with each episode, ranging from deep hypoxaemia and hypotension initially to milder dyspnoea and rash in later episodes. This variability highlights the need for sustained monitoring rather than discharge based solely on the resolution of the first episode.

When recurrent anaphylactic shock occurs, the decision to escalate care to an intensive care unit (ICU) setting is often driven by the severity of the relapse and the need for continuous monitoring. In this case, the patient was transferred to the ICU following the first relapse of anaphylactic shock to allow for increased monitoring by specialised nursing staff. While the acute internal ward provided increased monitoring, it lacked telemetry control, which may be insufficient for patients demonstrating instability or refractory symptoms. The transition to the ICU allows for closer observation of vital signs and rapid response to further decompensation. However, optimal observation durations remain debated in the literature. Traditional observation periods range from 4 to 6 hours, yet guidelines from the World Allergy Organisation and the Dutch NHG note that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6 to 12 hours. The UK guideline 2021 proposes a risk-stratified approach, but consensus monitoring strategies are still needed to balance patient safety with healthcare resource allocation. In the absence of reliable predictors for biphasic reactions, such as delayed epinephrine administration or severe initial presentation, clinicians must exercise caution when determining the duration of observation for patients with atypical or severe courses.

Pharmacological escalation, particularly regarding epinephrine dosing and administration routes, presents another layer of decision-making in refractory cases. The patient in this report weighed 104 kg, yet the initial treatment involved a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. While auto-injectors are preferred in time-sensitive settings to minimise treatment delay and reduce hesitancy among personnel unfamiliar with vasoactive agents, this dosage may constitute underdosing for adults over 50 kg. Guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, with a maximum total dose of 0.5 mg, which is often simplified to 0.5 mg for most adults. In this case, the first two epinephrine doses were 0.3 mg, while the subsequent two doses were individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular injections. A 2025 retrospective study cited in the discussion indicates that patients initially receiving 0.3 mg required escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving 0.5 mg. Beyond intramuscular boluses, the department of allergology considered continuous intravenous epinephrine administration via pump as a preemptive measure. However, this approach was not ultimately utilized. The choice between intramuscular bolus therapy and intravenous infusion remains a consideration in refractory cases, weighing the risks of continuous vasoactive infusion against the need to prevent further relapse.

The role of adjunctive therapies, specifically corticosteroids, remains a subject of significant debate between traditional practice and emerging evidence. In this case, corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, consistent with the traditional role of these agents in preventing biphasic reactions. The patient received both 30 mg of prednisone intravenously daily for 5 days and 8 mg of dexamethasone intravenously once. However, the discussion section highlights that mounting evidence demonstrates no benefit to corticosteroids in reducing the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis, with a 2017 systematic review finding only one of 22 studies supporting their use. Furthermore, recent studies suggest potential harmful effects, including an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Consequently, most contemporary guidelines advise against routine corticosteroid administration, although their use in clinical practice persists. This case illustrates the tension between established hospital protocols and evolving evidence, suggesting that while corticosteroids may be administered in complex cases, their efficacy in preventing recurrence is not well-supported.

Ultimately, management decisions in multiphasic anaphylaxis often involve weighing preemptive treatment against expectant management, particularly when guidelines are lacking. For this patient, two alternative treatment courses were considered: continuous intravenous epinephrine to preemptively treat anaphylaxis or an expectant approach to see if symptoms would resolve spontaneously. Both options carried serious risks, underpinned by the lack of literature providing treatment guidance in multiphasic cases. Fortunately, neither approach was necessary as the patient did not show further symptom relapse after approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylaxis. This outcome supports an expectant strategy in cases where symptom intensity is decreasing, provided that adequate monitoring is in place. However, the underlying high probability of systemic mastocytosis in this patient complicates future management and underscores the need for long-term follow-up. The rarity of such complex courses makes evidence-based strategy development challenging, suggesting that expert-based consensus and case registries may be necessary to refine management protocols for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis.
