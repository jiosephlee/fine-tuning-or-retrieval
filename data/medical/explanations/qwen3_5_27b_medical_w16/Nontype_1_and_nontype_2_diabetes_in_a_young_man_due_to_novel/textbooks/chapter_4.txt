Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 4: ## Genotype-Guided Management and Pharmacotherapy

The management of this patient illustrates the critical transition from empiric therapy to precision medicine once a monogenic etiology is confirmed. Upon initial presentation, the patient exhibited significant hyperglycemia with an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, and symptoms of catabolism including weight loss and polydipsia. In the absence of genetic data, the clinical team initiated a regimen consistent with type 2 diabetes or severe insulin deficiency, comprising basal insulin degludec, metformin, and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin. This initial empiric approach prioritized rapid glycemic control and addressed potential insulin resistance suggested by the patient's lipid profile, which indicated metabolic syndrome features. However, this strategy was provisional, intended to stabilize the patient while awaiting the results of molecular testing.

Once the novel heterozygous HNF1A mutation was identified, the treatment strategy shifted to align with the specific pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY. This condition is characterized by a primary defect in pancreatic β-cell function rather than insulin resistance or autoimmune destruction. Consequently, HNF1A-MODY is classically sensitive to secretagogues that stimulate residual insulin production. While low-dose sulfonylureas are traditionally considered first-line therapy due to their action on potassium-sensitive ATP channels, this case demonstrates the efficacy of alternative agents. Following genetic confirmation and an initial reduction in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin therapy was discontinued. It was replaced with the GLP-1 receptor agonist dulaglutide. Literature referenced in the case discussion supports this decision, noting that GLP-1 receptor agonists can be a safer and effective alternative to sulfonylureas, particularly for preserving pancreatic function and minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia. Ultimately, the patient’s regimen was simplified to metformin and saxagliptin for long-term maintenance, achieving an HbA1c of 6.0% without insulin.

The case report also highlights specific pharmacological contraindications relevant to HNF1A-MODY that must be considered during treatment planning. The HNF1A gene transcriptionally regulates the expression of the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal tubules. In patients with HNF1A mutations, SGLT2 expression is often reduced, leading to a lower renal threshold for glucose and resulting glycosuria. The discussion notes that adding SGLT2 inhibitors to the regimen in these patients may compound this effect, potentially increasing the risk of severe dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis. Therefore, despite the common use of SGLT2 inhibitors in type 2 diabetes, they are generally avoided in HNF1A-MODY to prevent these adverse outcomes.

The decision to discontinue insulin was predicated on the genetic diagnosis combined with biochemical evidence of preserved, albeit insufficient, endogenous insulin secretion. The patient’s stimulated C-peptide level was 0.83 nmol/L, indicating that β-cells were still functional enough to respond to non-insulin therapies. This contrasts with type 1 diabetes, where insulin replacement is mandatory due to autoimmune destruction. The successful withdrawal of insulin in this case underscores the importance of genetic testing in identifying patients who may be unnecessarily maintained on insulin. By confirming the diagnosis of MODY, clinicians can de-escalate therapy to oral agents or non-insulin injectables that target the specific defect in insulin secretion, thereby improving quality of life and reducing treatment burden.

Finally, pharmacotherapy was supported by standard lifestyle interventions. Upon diagnosis, the patient was advised to adhere to a structured dietary plan and engage in regular physical activity. While the primary driver of glycemic improvement in this case was the genotype-specific pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modifications remained an essential adjunctive component of the comprehensive management approach. The patient’s consistent maintenance of HbA1c below 6.5% over the follow-up period, without evidence of microalbuminuria or retinopathy, demonstrates that a tailored regimen combining precision pharmacotherapy with lifestyle measures can yield excellent long-term outcomes in young adults with monogenic diabetes.
