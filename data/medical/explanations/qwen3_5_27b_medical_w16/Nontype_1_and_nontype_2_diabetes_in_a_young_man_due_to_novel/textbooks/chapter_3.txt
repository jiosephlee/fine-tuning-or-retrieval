Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Genetic Variant Interpretation

The diagnostic journey for monogenic diabetes requires a systematic approach to differentiate Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) from type 1 and type 2 diabetes, particularly in young patients who do not fit classic phenotypic profiles. In this case, the initial clinical suspicion of MODY was driven by the patient’s young age at presentation, the absence of diabetic ketoacidosis, and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations. To rule out autoimmune type 1 diabetes, the standard of care involves testing for islet autoantibodies. This patient underwent testing for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A), both of which returned negative results. While the presence of autoantibodies confirms type 1 diabetes, their absence does not definitively exclude it; however, in the context of a strong family history and non-autoimmune clinical features, negative serology significantly increases the pre-test probability of a monogenic etiology. This step is crucial because misdiagnosing MODY as type 1 diabetes can lead to unnecessary lifelong insulin therapy, whereas misdiagnosing it as type 2 diabetes may delay appropriate genetic counseling and targeted treatment.

Following the exclusion of autoimmune markers, assessment of endogenous insulin secretion is necessary to evaluate β-cell function. In this case, a glucagon stimulation test was employed to measure stimulated C-peptide levels, providing a snapshot of residual β-cell capacity. The patient demonstrated a baseline C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L which rose to 0.83 nmol/L at six minutes post-glucagon injection. While a stimulated level greater than 0.60 nmol/L generally suggests preserved endogenous insulin secretion, the clinical interpretation in this instance described the function as moderately preserved yet insufficient to maintain normoglycemia. This finding helped distinguish the case from the absolute insulin deficiency typical of advanced type 1 diabetes, yet it also highlighted the progressive β-cell dysfunction characteristic of HNF1A-MODY. It is important to note that while the glucagon stimulation test is supportive, it is not definitive; therefore, biochemical evidence of residual insulin secretion combined with negative autoantibodies serves as a strong indicator for proceeding to genetic analysis.

Genetic confirmation was achieved using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel, which allows for the simultaneous analysis of multiple genes associated with monogenic diabetes. This technology is particularly valuable in cases where clinical suspicion is high but the specific genetic defect is unknown. In this patient, the NGS panel identified a novel heterozygous substitution in the HNF1A gene, specifically p.N270S (c.809A>G) in exon 4. The utility of NGS in this scenario lies in its ability to detect rare or previously unreported variants that might be missed by targeted single-gene testing. The identification of a variant in HNF1A was consistent with the clinical phenotype, as HNF1A-MODY is the most common form of MODY and typically presents with autosomal dominant inheritance and progressive β-cell failure. However, the novelty of the variant required rigorous interpretation to confirm its pathogenicity before finalizing the diagnosis.

Interpreting novel genetic variants requires adherence to standardized frameworks, such as the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). Because the p.N270S variant was absent from major databases like ClinVar and VarSome, its classification relied on multiple lines of evidence rather than population frequency data alone. The variant was situated in the DNA-binding domain of the HNF1A protein, a region known to harbor pathogenic mutations. Furthermore, in silico prediction tools, such as the AlphaMissense score, indicated a high probability of pathogenicity with a score of 0.995. These computational predictions were corroborated by clinical segregation analysis, which demonstrated that the variant co-segregated with diabetes within the patient’s family, including the mother and brother. This combination of computational data, structural location, and familial segregation allowed the clinical team to classify the variant as pathogenic according to ACMG criteria, confirming the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

The integration of biochemical testing and genetic variant interpretation underscores the necessity of precision medicine in diabetes management. In this case, the diagnostic workup moved beyond standard metabolic profiling to identify a specific molecular defect that explained the patient’s clinical course. The confirmation of a pathogenic HNF1A mutation validated the clinical suspicion raised by the negative autoantibodies and the specific pattern of β-cell dysfunction. This diagnostic clarity was not merely academic; it directly informed the subsequent management strategy, allowing for the discontinuation of insulin and the transition to agents more suitable for HNF1A-MODY, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists. Ultimately, this case illustrates that a structured diagnostic algorithm, incorporating autoantibody screening, functional testing, and advanced genetic interpretation, is essential for accurately identifying MODY and optimizing long-term patient outcomes.
