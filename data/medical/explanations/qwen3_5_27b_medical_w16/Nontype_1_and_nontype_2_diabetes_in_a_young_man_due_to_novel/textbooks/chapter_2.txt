Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY and Differential Diagnosis

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is defined as an autosomal dominant, monogenic form of diabetes characterized by a primary defect in pancreatic β-cell development and function, typically manifesting before the age of 25. In the clinical landscape of young-onset diabetes, MODY accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of cases, though prevalence varies significantly across ethnic and racial groups. Research has historically concentrated on White European populations, potentially leading to underestimation in other demographics, including Asian regions where this case report originates. The patient in this case presented with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at a young age without diabetic ketoacidosis, accompanied by a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations. This triad of early onset, familial clustering, and non-autoimmune presentation aligns with the clinical definition of MODY, distinguishing it from the more common polygenic forms of diabetes. Understanding these epidemiological nuances is critical, as genetic screening is often limited to individuals based on specific clinical characteristics, which can vary by population and lead to missed diagnoses.

The underlying molecular mechanism in this patient involves a novel heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene, specifically p.N270S (c.809A>G) in exon 4. HNF1A encodes a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas, playing a pivotal role in regulating insulin secretion and glucose metabolism. Mutations in this gene lead to a progressive decline in β-cell function through impaired expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes, as well as reduced pyruvate kinase activity. In this case, the mutation was located in the DNA-binding domain, a region where the majority of pathogenic mutations are identified. Although the specific variant was not found in public databases like ClinVar, its pathogenicity was supported by ACMG guidelines, including its location adjacent to known pathogenic residues and its segregation with diabetes in the family. This genetic defect explains the patient's insufficient insulin secretion observed on the glucagon stimulation test, where stimulated C-peptide levels indicated preserved yet declining β-cell function, a hallmark of the progressive nature of HNF1A-MODY.

A distinctive clinical feature observed in this patient was glycosuria in the absence of severe hyperglycaemia typical of uncontrolled diabetes. This phenomenon is directly linked to the pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY, as the HNF1A transcription factor regulates the expression of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal tubules. Mutations in HNF1A result in reduced SGLT2 expression, which lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, allowing glucose to spill into the urine at lower blood concentrations. This mechanism is clinically significant because it informs treatment decisions; for instance, the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY patients carries increased risks of dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to the already compromised residual SGLT-2 activity. Recognizing this specific pathophysiological link allows clinicians to avoid potentially harmful pharmacological classes and select safer alternatives, such as the GLP-1 receptor agonist used in this case.

Differentiating HNF1A-MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes requires a careful synthesis of clinical phenotype, biochemical markers, and genetic evidence, as features often overlap. Type 1 diabetes is typically autoimmune, characterized by positive islet autoantibodies (such as GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8) and absolute insulin deficiency. In this case, the patient tested negative for GADA and IA-2A antibodies, and the glucagon stimulation test revealed a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L, indicating preserved endogenous insulin secretion inconsistent with advanced type 1 diabetes. Conversely, type 2 diabetes is often associated with insulin resistance, obesity, and acanthosis nigricans. While the patient had a lipid profile suggesting metabolic syndrome, his BMI was 24.6 kg/m², and no acanthosis nigricans was observed, making typical type 2 diabetes less likely. The diagnostic reasoning in this case relied on the combination of negative autoantibodies, a strong family history of early-onset disease, and a MODY probability calculator score of 62.4%. Ultimately, molecular genetic testing confirmed the diagnosis, illustrating that while clinical algorithms can raise suspicion, genetic confirmation remains essential for precision medicine and appropriate management.
