Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Outcomes and Broader Implications for Practice

The clinical trajectory of this patient underscores the profound impact of precise genetic diagnosis on therapeutic management and long-term glycemic control. At presentation, the patient exhibited significant hyperglycemia with a glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) level of 10.2%, prompting an initial empiric treatment regimen of basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. This approach aimed to rapidly stabilize glucose levels while the diagnostic workup proceeded. However, following the genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, the treatment strategy was refined to align with the specific pathophysiology of the condition. Insulin therapy was discontinued in favor of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, reflecting the patient’s moderately preserved yet insufficient β-cell function. This targeted adjustment yielded a rapid and sustained improvement in glycemic metrics; within one month, HbA1c dropped to 9.1%, and by six months, it reached 6.0%. Subsequently, the regimen was simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor for long-term maintenance, with HbA1c consistently maintained below 6.5%. This outcome illustrates a core principle of precision medicine in endocrinology: accurate molecular diagnosis allows for the de-escalation of intensive therapies, such as insulin, to safer, more effective oral or injectable agents that specifically target the underlying defect in insulin secretion.

Beyond glycemic metrics, comprehensive monitoring for microvascular complications remains essential in MODY patients, even when glucose levels are well-controlled. In this case, baseline investigations revealed no microalbuminuria, and follow-up assessments confirmed the absence of diabetic retinopathy or nephropathy to date. However, the patient did exhibit severely reduced sudomotor function on electrochemical skin conductance testing, indicating significant autonomic dysfunction. This finding highlights that while HNF1A-MODY may spare some microvascular complications compared to type 1 or type 2 diabetes in the early stages, patients are not immune to diabetic sequelae. The absence of retinopathy and nephropathy at the time of follow-up is a favorable outcome, yet it necessitates ongoing surveillance. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for complications and ensure regular screening protocols are adhered to, as the long-term natural history of novel mutations may differ from established variants.

The autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of HNF1A-MODY carries significant implications for the patient’s family, making cascade genetic screening a critical component of care. The patient’s family history was notable for early-onset diabetes affecting the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother, consistent with the vertical transmission typical of MODY. Identifying the specific pathogenic variant in the proband enables targeted testing of at-risk relatives. Cascade screening is vital not only to confirm diagnoses in family members but also to facilitate early intervention and prevent the misdiagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes in relatives. For family members who test negative for the familial variant, the likelihood of MODY is excluded, sparing them from unnecessary monitoring and inappropriate treatment. Conversely, identifying affected relatives allows for the early initiation of sulfonylureas or other appropriate therapies, potentially delaying the onset of symptomatic hyperglycemia and preserving β-cell function.

Diagnostic algorithms for MODY often utilize clinical probability calculators, yet their utility must be interpreted with caution regarding population diversity. In this case, the patient’s clinical data entered into the Exeter MODY probability calculator yielded an estimated probability of 62.4%. While this score supported the clinical suspicion, the report notes that such tools were primarily derived from White European populations and may overestimate the probability of MODY in non-White groups. This limitation is particularly relevant given that MODY prevalence varies across ethnicities and may be underestimated in Asian and Australian regions due to limited screening. Relying solely on clinical calculators without genetic confirmation can lead to diagnostic errors. Therefore, while calculators serve as useful triage tools, molecular genetic testing remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis, especially in populations where the predictive values of clinical criteria may differ.

Finally, the identification of a novel heterozygous HNF1A mutation (p.N270S) not previously reported in databases like ClinVar highlights the evolving landscape of genetic diagnostics. The pathogenicity of this variant was established using American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines, supported by its location in the DNA-binding domain and segregation with diabetes in the family. This case reinforces the necessity of next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels to detect rare or novel variants that standard screening might miss. As the spectrum of known mutations expands, future directions must focus on developing more cost-effective NGS screening strategies to improve diagnostic rates. Enhancing access to genetic testing will ensure that patients with monogenic diabetes receive the specific therapeutic benefits they require, ultimately improving clinical outcomes and reducing the long-term burden of misdiagnosis.
