Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# One Diagnosis, Three Generations: The Imperative of Cascade Screening

You see a young patient in your clinic. They are not obese, they have no autoimmune antibodies, and they aren’t in ketoacidosis. Yet, their HbA1c is 10.2%. You treat them for type 2 diabetes, but something feels off. The family history is the loudest clue in the room: a maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, a mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother already diagnosed at age 20. This isn’t just a metabolic issue; it is a genetic legacy.

A recent case report highlights exactly this scenario in a young adult male with a novel HNF1A mutation causing Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). What makes this case particularly striking is not just the identification of a previously unreported pathogenic variant, but the profound shift in management once the diagnosis was secured. The patient was initially managed with basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor based on a presumed type 2 diabetes diagnosis. However, once genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY, insulin was discontinued, and the regimen was simplified to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin. Within six months, the HbA1c plummeted to 6.0%, and the patient remained stable on oral and non-insulin injectable therapy.

This case serves as a potent reminder that early-onset diabetes is frequently misdiagnosed. Approximately 1% to 6% of young-onset diabetes cases are due to MODY, a group of dominantly inherited disorders characterized by primary defects in pancreatic β-cell function. In this specific instance, the patient presented with hyperglycemia and glycosuria but no diabetic ketoacidosis. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, which is within the normal range, yet the lipid profile suggested metabolic syndrome features. Crucially, autoantibodies for type 1 diabetes, including GADA and IA-2A, were negative. The glucagon stimulation test showed insufficient insulin secretion, with a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, indicating preserved yet insufficient β-cell function.

The genetic workup revealed a heterozygous substitution, p.N270S in exon 4 of HNF1A. This variant was novel, absent from databases like ClinVar or VarSome, yet it met ACMG guidelines for a pathogenic classification due to its location in the DNA-binding domain, a high AlphaMissense score, and segregation with diabetes in the family. This underscores that negative database matches do not rule out pathogenicity when clinical and genetic evidence aligns. The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor essential for insulin secretion and renal glucose reabsorption. Mutations here lead to progressive β-cell decline and often present with glycosuria due to a reduced renal threshold for glucose.

The clinical implications of this diagnosis extend far beyond the index patient. MODY is autosomal dominant, meaning one positive diagnosis unlocks the medical history of an entire family. In this case, three generations were affected. If the patient’s mother, grandmother, and brother are all living with this mutation, they may be undergoing unnecessary treatments. Patients with HNF1A-MODY are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas, which act on potassium-sensitive ATP channels to enhance insulin secretion. However, as β-cell function declines, GLP-1 receptor agonists have emerged as a safer and effective alternative, offering lower risks of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas. Conversely, SGLT-2 inhibitors should be used with extreme caution or avoided. Because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression, patients already have reduced SGLT2 activity and glycosuria; inhibiting this further can precipitate severe dehydration, vascular collapse, or euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis.

Cascade screening is the logical next step after confirming a monogenic diabetes diagnosis. It is not merely an academic exercise; it is a public health imperative. Identifying at-risk relatives allows for the cessation of unnecessary insulin therapy, the avoidance of contraindicated medications like SGLT-2 inhibitors, and the initiation of precise, targeted therapies. It also facilitates family counseling regarding prognosis and the potential for complications. In this case, the patient had no microalbuminuria or retinopathy at follow-up, but long-term surveillance is critical. The diagnostic algorithm typically involves assessing clinical characteristics, ruling out autoimmunity, and utilizing genetic testing when suspicion is high. While tools like the MODY probability calculator exist, they were derived from White European populations and may underestimate or overestimate risk in other demographics, making clinical judgment and family history paramount.

Ultimately, this case illustrates the power of precision medicine in endocrinology. We often default to the binary of type 1 versus type 2 diabetes, but the spectrum is much wider. Recognizing the pattern of early-onset diabetes across multiple generations, combined with negative autoantibodies and preserved C-peptide, should trigger a genetic workup. By doing so, we do more than correct a lab value; we prevent decades of inappropriate treatment for the patient and their relatives.

*   **Suspect MODY in young-onset diabetes** (<25 years) with negative autoantibodies and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations.
*   **Genetic testing is definitive** for MODY diagnosis, even for novel variants not found in standard databases, provided they meet ACMG pathogenicity criteria.
*   **Treatment must be genotype-specific:** HNF1A-MODY patients respond well to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists but should generally avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors due to dehydration and DKA risks.
*   **Cascade screening is essential** because MODY is autosomal dominant; testing family members can prevent misdiagnosis and unnecessary insulin therapy in relatives.
*   **Monitor for complications** despite good control, as HNF1A-MODY is associated with progressive β-cell dysfunction and potential renal or retinal issues.
