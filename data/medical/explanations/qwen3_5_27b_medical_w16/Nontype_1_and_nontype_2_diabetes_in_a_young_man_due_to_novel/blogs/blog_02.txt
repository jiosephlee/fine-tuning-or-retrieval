Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# When the Database is Silent: Interpreting Novel Genetic Variants in Diabetes

You know the drill. A young patient walks in with hyperglycemia. They aren’t obese, so Type 2 diabetes seems unlikely. They aren’t in ketoacidosis, and their antibodies are negative, so Type 1 is less probable. You order a genetic panel for Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), and the report comes back with a variant you’ve never seen before. It’s not in ClinVar. It’s not in VarSome. The database is silent.

Do you ignore it? Do you treat it as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) and stick to standard diabetes protocols? Or do you dig deeper?

This week’s case highlights a young man with HNF1A-MODY caused by a novel mutation. The twist isn’t just the diagnosis—it’s how we validated a genetic finding that didn’t exist in the literature, and how that single discovery completely altered his treatment plan from insulin dependence to a simpler regimen.

## The Clinical Picture

The patient was a young adult male referred for evaluation of hyperglycemia and glycosuria discovered during an annual health exam. He presented with an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, and a 2-hour post-OGTT glucose of 19.8 mmol/L. There was no diabetic ketoacidosis.

Physically, he wasn't obese (BMI 24.6 kg/m²) and had no acanthosis nigricans. However, his family history was a screaming red flag for monogenic diabetes. His maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. This autosomal dominant pattern is classic for MODY, yet these patients are frequently misdiagnosed as having Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes due to overlapping clinical features.

Initial workup showed negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A). A glucagon stimulation test revealed a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, indicating insufficient but preserved endogenous insulin secretion. While this might suggest early Type 1, the negative antibodies and strong family history pushed the team toward a genetic etiology.

## The Genetic Dilemma

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified a heterozygous substitution in exon 4 of the *HNF1A* gene: p.N270S (c.809A>G). Here is where the case gets interesting. This specific mutation was not reported in the literature, nor was it found in the VarSome or ClinVar databases.

In the era of precision medicine, finding a "novel" variant can feel like hitting a dead end. How do you determine pathogenicity when the crowdsourced databases are empty? The team relied on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation, looking for independent lines of evidence beyond simple database matches.

They utilized an AlphaMissense score, which predicted a high likelihood of pathogenicity (0.995). Furthermore, the variant was located in the DNA-binding domain of the *HNF1A* protein, a region known to harbor pathogenic mutations. Crucially, the variant segregated with diabetes in the family. When clinical suspicion, biochemical evidence (glycosuria due to reduced renal threshold), and genetic evidence align, a novel variant can be classified as pathogenic even without prior publication.

## Changing the Course of Treatment

Why does this genetic label matter? Because HNF1A-MODY responds differently to medication than Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes.

Initially, before the genetic results were available, the patient was treated with basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor, reflecting a preliminary consideration of Type 2 diabetes. However, once the HNF1A-MODY diagnosis was confirmed, the team discontinued the insulin. They transitioned the patient to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) combined with metformin.

The results were striking. Within three months, his HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0%. Long-term maintenance was eventually simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor, with HbA1c consistently maintained below 6.5%.

This shift is critical because HNF1A-MODY patients are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas and GLP-1 receptor agonists due to the mechanism of the mutation, which affects insulin secretion. Conversely, the case discussion highlights a significant safety warning: SGLT-2 inhibitors should be used with extreme caution or avoided. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria is already present due to reduced SGLT2 expression. Adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor can lead to severe dehydration, vascular collapse, and even diabetic ketoacidosis due to high inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity.

## Why This Matters for the Frontline

MODY accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of young-onset diabetes cases, yet it remains frequently underestimated. The barrier to diagnosis is often a lack of clinical suspicion. Tools like the Exeter MODY probability calculator can help, but they were derived from White European populations and may overestimate probability in non-White groups.

In this case, the probability calculator estimated a 62.4% chance of MODY, but it was the genetic confirmation that allowed for de-escalation of therapy. Without the genetic diagnosis, this patient might have remained on unnecessary insulin, facing the risks of hypoglycemia and the burden of injections, despite having a condition that responds well to oral or injectable non-insulin therapies.

Furthermore, accurate diagnosis facilitates family counseling. Since MODY is autosomal dominant, first-degree relatives have a 50% chance of carrying the mutation. Identifying the proband allows for cascade screening of the family, potentially preventing misdiagnosis in siblings or parents.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect MODY in young-onset diabetes:** Look for non-obese patients with negative autoantibodies and a strong three-generation family history of early-onset diabetes.
*   **Don't ignore novel variants:** If a genetic variant isn't in ClinVar, use ACMG guidelines, AlphaMissense scores, and family segregation data to determine pathogenicity.
*   **Tailor therapy to the gene:** HNF1A-MODY patients respond well to sulfonylureas and GLP-1 receptor agonists; insulin is often unnecessary once control is established.
*   **Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY:** These patients already have reduced renal glucose reabsorption; adding SGLT-2 inhibitors increases the risk of dehydration and euglycemic DKA.
*   **Genetics change management:** Confirming monogenic diabetes can allow for de-escalation from insulin to simpler regimens, improving quality of life and reducing hypoglycemia risk.
