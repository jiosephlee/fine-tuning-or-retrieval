Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The SGLT2 Conundrum: Renal Thresholds and Drug Safety in MODY

We see diabetes all the time. In the ED or clinic, the differential usually narrows quickly to Type 1, Type 2, or maybe steroid-induced. But what about the young, non-obese patient with negative antibodies who doesn't quite fit the mold?

A recent case report by Liu et al. highlights a young man presenting with significant hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, initially managed as Type 2 diabetes before genetic testing revealed a novel mutation in the *HNF1A* gene. This diagnosis wasn't just a semantic change; it fundamentally altered his treatment plan and raised a critical safety question regarding a class of drugs we prescribe frequently: SGLT2 inhibitors.

## The Case: A Young Man Who Didn't Fit the Mold

The patient was a young adult referred for evaluation of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria during a routine health exam. His BMI was 24.6 kg/m², and he had no acanthosis nigricans. His HbA1c was a staggering 10.2%, with fasting glucose at 12.4 mmol/L. Crucially, there were no signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), and autoantibodies for Type 1 diabetes (GADA, IA-2A) were negative.

However, the family history was a red flag. His maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother all had early-onset diabetes. Initial treatment involved basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. But with the clinical suspicion of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), genetic testing was pursued.

The results confirmed a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in *HNF1A* (p.N270S), consistent with HNF1A-MODY. Once confirmed, the treatment strategy shifted. Insulin was discontinued, and he was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) alongside metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor. Within six months, his HbA1c dropped to 6.0%.

## The Pathophysiology: Why Does He Have Glycosuria?

Here is where the physiology gets interesting and clinically relevant. *HNF1A* encodes a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. In the pancreas, mutations lead to progressive β-cell dysfunction and impaired insulin secretion. But in the kidney, the story is different.

The case report notes that glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is attributed to reduced expression of SGLT2 (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2). SGLT2 is responsible for the majority of glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule. Because *HNF1A* regulates the transcription of SGLT2, a mutation leads to a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption.

This means patients with HNF1A-MODY excrete glucose in their urine at lower blood glucose levels than the general population. It explains the glycosuria seen in this patient despite the absence of DKA. It also sets the stage for a potential medication interaction that could be dangerous.

## The SGLT2 Inhibitor Risk

We love SGLT2 inhibitors (like empagliflozin or dapagliflozin) for Type 2 diabetes and heart failure. They lower glucose by blocking reabsorption in the kidney, forcing glucose out through the urine. But in a patient with HNF1A-MODY, the renal threshold is already compromised due to the genetic defect.

The discussion in the case report highlights a specific warning: prescribing SGLT2 inhibitors to HNF1A-MODY patients may be risky. Because the patient already has reduced SGLT2 activity due to the mutation, pharmacologically inhibiting the remaining activity could compound the effect.

The authors warn that this compounded inhibition can lead to severe dehydration and potentially diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), even in the absence of severe hyperglycaemia (euglycemic DKA). The risk of vascular collapse and genital infections may also be increased. While the patient in this case did not receive an SGLT2 inhibitor, the pathophysiology suggests that the marginal benefit of glucose lowering might be outweighed by the safety risks in this specific population.

## Treatment Pearls for MODY

This case reinforces that precision medicine isn't just a buzzword; it changes management. HNF1A-MODY patients are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin secretion via potassium-sensitive ATP channels. However, as β-cell function declines, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer alternative with a lower risk of hypoglycaemia compared to sulfonylureas.

In this patient, the transition from insulin to a GLP-1 agonist resulted in excellent glycaemic control (HbA1c <6.5%) without the burden of insulin therapy. Furthermore, avoiding SGLT2 inhibitors prevents the risk of exacerbating the inherent renal glucose handling defect.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect MODY in young, non-obese patients** with negative autoantibodies and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes.
*   **Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is physiological:** It results from reduced SGLT2 expression due to the transcriptional defect, not just hyperglycaemia.
*   **Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY:** The inherent reduction in renal glucose reabsorption capacity may predispose these patients to severe dehydration and euglycemic DKA when combined with pharmacological SGLT2 inhibition.
*   **Genetic testing guides therapy:** Confirming HNF1A-MODY allows for the discontinuation of insulin and the use of sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, which are more effective and safer for this phenotype.
*   **Don't rely on BMI alone:** While MODY patients are often lean, rising obesity rates can make BMI a less reliable discriminator between MODY and Type 2 diabetes.
