Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The Lean Diabetic Trap: When Young Hyperglycemia Isn't Type 1 or Type 2

## The Diagnostic Dilemma
In the emergency department or primary care clinic, we often fall into a binary trap when evaluating a young patient with hyperglycemia. If they are lean and symptomatic, we think Type 1 Diabetes. If they are overweight, we lean toward Type 2. But what happens when the patient is young, lean, has significant hyperglycemia, and fits neither mold perfectly? A recent case report highlights a scenario that challenges this binary thinking: a young adult male presenting with an HbA1c of 10.2% and glycosuria, yet negative for diabetic ketoacidosis and autoimmune markers.

This patient, with a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, presented with classic symptoms of diabetes including polydipsia, polyuria, and weight loss. The initial clinical picture was confusing. He lacked the obesity typically associated with Type 2, yet he didn't have the absolute insulin deficiency or autoantibodies expected in Type 1. The real story, however, lay in his pedigree. His family history was notable for early-onset diabetes across three generations: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. This pattern of autosomal dominant inheritance was the first major red flag for Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY).

## Unmasking the Mutation
MODY is an autosomal dominant monogenic form of diabetes, accounting for approximately 1% to 6% of young-onset diabetes cases. Despite its prevalence, it is frequently misdiagnosed because clinical features often overlap with Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes. In this case, the diagnostic workup included a glucagon stimulation test which showed a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L. While this indicated insufficient insulin secretion, it also suggested preserved endogenous insulin secretion, distinguishing it from the absolute deficiency seen in Type 1. Furthermore, tests for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A) were negative.

When the clinical suspicion for MODY was high, molecular genetic testing was performed. The results revealed a novel heterozygous pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S). This specific mutation had not been previously reported in databases like ClinVar, yet it met the American College of Medical Genetics criteria for pathogenicity based on its location in the DNA-binding domain and its segregation with diabetes in the family. Identifying this mutation was not just an academic exercise; it fundamentally altered the patient's management trajectory.

## Precision Medicine in Action
The treatment implications of a MODY diagnosis are profound. Initially, this patient was started on a regimen of basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin, reflecting a preliminary consideration of Type 2 diabetes to achieve rapid glycemic control. However, once the HNF1A-MODY diagnosis was confirmed, the treatment strategy shifted. Insulin therapy was discontinued because HNF1A-MODY patients often retain beta-cell function that can be supported without exogenous insulin, unlike Type 1.

The patient was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) and metformin. This switch proved highly effective, reducing his HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% within six months. Eventually, the regimen was simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor for long-term maintenance. This case underscores a critical point: patients with HNF1A-MODY are often sensitive to sulfonylureas, but GLP-1 receptor agonists serve as a safer and effective alternative, particularly as beta-cell function declines, with a lower risk of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas.

## Hidden Risks and Clinical Pearls
Beyond the benefits of targeted therapy, the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY also reveals specific risks to avoid. The discussion in the case report highlights a unique pathophysiology regarding glycosuria. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria is attributed to reduced expression of SGLT2, which is under the transcriptional control of HNF1A. Because these patients already have reduced renal threshold for glucose, the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors carries specific risks. There is a potential for severe dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and even diabetic ketoacidosis due to the high inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. Therefore, knowing the genetic etiology protects the patient from potentially harmful medication classes.

Additionally, clinicians should be aware that traditional diagnostic tools may have limitations. The MODY probability calculator developed by the Exeter group was used in this case, estimating a 62.4% probability of MODY. However, this tool was derived from White European populations and may overestimate probability in non-White groups. Furthermore, while obesity is often lower in MODY patients compared to Type 2, rising obesity rates in the general population can make BMI a less effective discriminator. The presence of metabolic syndrome features, such as the elevated triglycerides seen in this patient, should not rule out MODY.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect MODY in young patients:** Consider genetic testing for diabetes diagnosed before age 25, especially with a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations.
*   **Don't rely on BMI alone:** MODY patients are often lean, but rising obesity rates mean a normal BMI does not exclude Type 2, nor does it confirm MODY without genetic evidence.
*   **Check antibodies and C-peptide:** Negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) and preserved stimulated C-peptide levels are supportive of MODY rather than Type 1 diabetes.
*   **Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY:** Due to reduced SGLT2 expression, these patients are at higher risk for dehydration and DKA when using this class of medication.
*   **Tailor therapy to genetics:** HNF1A-MODY patients often respond well to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists and may not require lifelong insulin, improving quality of life and reducing hypoglycemia risk.
