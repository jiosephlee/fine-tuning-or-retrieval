Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Deprescribing Insulin: Precision Pharmacotherapy for HNF1A-MODY

Imagine a young adult walking into your clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2%. They have glycosuria, weight loss, and polydipsia. The immediate reflex for many of us is to start basal insulin, perhaps assuming Type 1 diabetes given the young age and hyperglycemia, or aggressive Type 2 management given the severity. But what if that insulin isn't just unnecessary—it's the wrong tool for the job?

This case highlights a scenario where a precise genetic diagnosis allowed for the deprescribing of insulin in favor of targeted oral and injectable agents, resulting in sustained remission of hyperglycemia without the burden of insulin therapy. It serves as a critical reminder that not all diabetes in the young is autoimmune, and not all non-autoimmune diabetes is Type 2.

## The Clinical Picture: Beyond the Binary

The patient in this report presented with significant hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) during a routine health exam. Crucially, there was no diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) at presentation. His BMI was 24.6 kg/m², placing him in a normal weight range, and he lacked physical signs of insulin resistance like acanthosis nigricans.

The family history was the first red flag. His maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. This three-generation history of early-onset diabetes strongly suggested a monogenic etiology rather than sporadic Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes.

Initial workup revealed negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), which argues against Type 1 diabetes. A glucagon stimulation test showed a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L. While this indicated insufficient insulin secretion, it also showed preserved endogenous function, distinguishing him from the absolute insulin deficiency seen in advanced Type 1. Despite these clues, the patient was initially started on basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor while awaiting genetic results—a common practice when the diagnosis is unclear and glycemic control is urgent.

## The Genetic Twist: HNF1A-MODY

Genetic testing confirmed the suspicion of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). Specifically, the patient had a novel heterozygous mutation in the *HNF1A* gene (p.N270S). HNF1A-MODY, also known as MODY 3, is the most common form of MODY, accounting for 30% to 65% of cases.

The pathophysiology here is distinct. The *HNF1A* gene encodes a transcription factor essential for pancreatic β-cell development and function. Mutations lead to a progressive decline in β-cell function, impaired insulin expression, and reduced pyruvate kinase activity. Interestingly, this mutation also affects the kidneys. HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression, and reduced SGLT2 activity lowers the renal threshold for glucose. This explains the patient’s glycosuria despite glucose levels that might not typically cause spilling in Type 2 diabetes.

## The Therapeutic Pivot: Why Stop the Insulin?

Once the genetic diagnosis was confirmed, the treatment plan underwent a significant shift. The patient’s insulin was discontinued, and he was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) and metformin. Within six months, his HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0%.

Why make this change? In HNF1A-MODY, the primary defect is insulin secretion, not insulin resistance. These patients remain exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release from the remaining functional β-cells. While sulfonylureas are often considered first-line, GLP-1 receptor agonists have emerged as a potent alternative. They enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion and preserve β-cell function without the high risk of hypoglycemia associated with sulfonylureas.

In this specific case, the patient had moderately preserved β-cell function. Continuing insulin would have been overtreatment, exposing the patient to unnecessary hypoglycemia and weight gain risks without addressing the underlying monogenic defect. The transition to a GLP-1 agonist provided robust glycemic control while simplifying the regimen.

## Medication Caveats: What to Avoid

Precision medicine in MODY isn't just about what to prescribe; it's also about what to avoid. The case report highlights a critical safety warning regarding SGLT-2 inhibitors.

Because HNF1A mutations already reduce SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules (leading to the glycosuria seen in the patient), adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor can be dangerous. The combination may lead to severe dehydration, vascular collapse, or even euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) due to the excessive inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. In a patient with HNF1A-MODY, the risks of these medications likely outweigh the benefits.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect MODY in young patients:** Early-onset diabetes (before age 25), strong family history across three generations, and negative autoantibodies should trigger consideration for genetic testing.
*   **Don't default to insulin:** In HNF1A-MODY, patients often respond dramatically to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists. Insulin is typically reserved for severe hyperglycemia or DKA at onset.
*   **Watch for glycosuria:** Unexplained glycosuria in the absence of extreme hyperglycemia can be a clue for HNF1A-MODY due to reduced renal glucose threshold.
*   **Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors:** Patients with HNF1A-MODY are at increased risk for dehydration and DKA when on SGLT-2 inhibitors due to pre-existing reduced SGLT2 expression.
*   **Genetic testing changes management:** Confirming the diagnosis allows for deprescribing of unnecessary medications (like insulin) and tailoring therapy to preserve β-cell function.
