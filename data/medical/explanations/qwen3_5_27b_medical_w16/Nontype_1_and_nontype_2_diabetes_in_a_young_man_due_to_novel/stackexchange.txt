Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult presenting with early-onset diabetes, which specific clinical and laboratory features should prompt a clinician to suspect Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) over type 1 or type 2 diabetes?

In a young adult presenting with early-onset diabetes, clinicians should suspect Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) when the clinical picture reveals an autosomal dominant family history of early-onset hyperglycemia, an absence of autoimmune markers, and a phenotype lacking significant insulin resistance. As illustrated by the case of the young man with a novel *HNF1A* mutation, the diagnostic suspicion is anchored in three key pillars: family history, serology, and specific metabolic features.

First, a strong family history of diabetes diagnosed before age 40 across three generations is a hallmark of MODY. In this case, the patient’s maternal grandmother, mother, and brother were all diagnosed in their 20s or 30s, consistent with the autosomal dominant transmission typical of *HNF1A*-MODY. Second, the absence of type 1 diabetes autoantibodies is critical for differentiation. This patient tested negative for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and presented without diabetic ketoacidosis, arguing against autoimmune type 1 diabetes. Third, the patient’s physical and metabolic profile did not align with typical type 2 diabetes; he had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and no acanthosis nigricans, suggesting a lack of severe insulin resistance.

Laboratory findings further support MODY suspicion. While the patient had significant hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%), he exhibited glycosuria disproportionate to plasma glucose, a feature of *HNF1A*-MODY caused by a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. Additionally, a glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L, indicating preserved but insufficient endogenous insulin secretion, which distinguishes MODY from the profound insulin deficiency of type 1 diabetes. When these clinical features converge—early onset, multigenerational history, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide—genetic testing is warranted to confirm the diagnosis and guide precision therapy, such as the transition from insulin to GLP-1 receptor agonists seen in this patient.

### [Pathophysiology] What specific renal transporter's expression is compromised in HNF1A-MODY, and how does this contribute to the clinical finding of glycosuria?

In HNF1A-MODY, the expression of the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) is compromised. As detailed in the discussion of this case, HNF1A functions as a transcription factor that regulates the expression of SGLT2 in the kidney. When the HNF1A gene carries a pathogenic mutation, such as the novel variant identified in this patient, SGLT2 expression is reduced. This reduction leads to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, causing glucose to spill into the urine (glycosuria) even at plasma glucose levels that might not typically cause glycosuria in other forms of diabetes.

This pathophysiology explains the specific clinical presentation of this patient, who exhibited hyperglycemia and glycosuria without signs of diabetic ketoacidosis. The background section of the report notes that glycosuria in MODY is attributed to this reduced renal threshold resulting from compromised glucose transport in the renal tubules. Understanding this mechanism is clinically significant for management; the case highlights that SGLT2 inhibitors may carry increased risks, such as dehydration or diabetic ketoacidosis, in these patients because they further inhibit the residual SGLT2 activity that is already diminished by the genetic defect.

### [Management] While sulfonylureas are traditionally first-line for HNF1A-MODY, why might a GLP-1 receptor agonist be preferred in certain patients, and what mechanism underlies the efficacy of sulfonylureas in this condition?

Colleagues, regarding the management of HNF1A-MODY, it is essential to understand both the mechanism of traditional therapy and the rationale for newer alternatives.

Traditionally, low-dose sulfonylureas are the first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY. As noted in the discussion of this case, sulfonylureas enhance insulin secretion by acting on potassium-sensitive ATP channels. This mechanism is particularly effective in HNF1A-MODY because the pathophysiology involves a progressive decline in β-cell function and decreased insulin secretion, yet residual β-cell function often remains sufficient to respond to secretagogues.

However, a GLP-1 receptor agonist may be preferred in certain clinical scenarios. According to the case report, GLP-1 receptor agonists serve as a safer and potentially more effective alternative to sulfonylureas as β-cell function declines, particularly in individuals prone to hypoglycaemia. The authors cite evidence that GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy presents a lower risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared with sulfonylureas like glimepiride. Furthermore, in this specific patient, the transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) following genetic confirmation was utilized to support glycaemic control and preserve pancreatic function, resulting in a reduction of HbA1c from 9.1% to 6.0% over six months. Thus, while sulfonylureas remain the classic choice, GLP-1 agonists offer a valuable alternative with a superior safety profile regarding hypoglycemia in appropriate patients.

### [Clinical Pearls] What specific safety concerns necessitate caution or avoidance of SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with confirmed HNF1A-MODY?

In patients with confirmed HNF1A-MODY, SGLT2 inhibitors should generally be avoided due to significant safety risks that outweigh their potential glycaemic benefits.

The clinical reasoning stems from the pathophysiology of the HNF1A mutation itself. As noted in the discussion of this case, the HNF1A gene transcriptionally controls the expression of SGLT2 in the renal tubules. Consequently, patients with HNF1A-MODY already exhibit reduced expression of SGLT2, leading to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. This explains why our patient presented with glycosuria at diagnosis despite not being on any renal glucose-lowering therapy.

Introducing an SGLT2 inhibitor in this setting inhibits the residual SGLT2 activity that is already compromised. According to the case report, this pharmacological inhibition can precipitate severe dehydration and potential vascular collapse. Furthermore, the discussion highlights specific risks including genital infections and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). These adverse events occur because the drug exacerbates the underlying defect in glucose transport and water retention.

Therefore, in managing HNF1A-MODY, we prioritize agents that do not target this pathway. In this case, the patient achieved excellent control (HbA1c 6.0%) using a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin, avoiding the risks associated with SGLT2 inhibition. Precision medicine in MODY requires recognizing these genetic-drug interactions to prevent iatrogenic harm.

### [Morning Report] How do stimulated C-peptide levels and islet autoantibody testing contribute to the diagnostic algorithm for distinguishing monogenic diabetes from autoimmune type 1 diabetes?

In the diagnostic algorithm for young-onset diabetes, stimulated C-peptide levels and islet autoantibody testing serve as critical initial screening tools to distinguish autoimmune type 1 diabetes (T1D) from monogenic forms like maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY). As illustrated in the case of HNF1A-MODY described by Liu et al., these biochemical markers guide the clinician toward genetic confirmation before initiating definitive therapy.

Islet autoantibody testing is the primary method for excluding autoimmune etiology. In this case, the patient tested negative for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A). The absence of these antibodies significantly lowers the probability of T1D, particularly when combined with a strong family history of early-onset diabetes, as seen in this patient’s maternal lineage. While the discussion notes that autoantibodies can rarely be present in MODY, their absence is a standard prerequisite for suspecting a monogenic disorder in current diagnostic algorithms.

Stimulated C-peptide levels assess residual β-cell function. In T1D, endogenous insulin secretion is typically absent or severely diminished. Conversely, MODY patients often retain some β-cell function. In this case, a glucagon stimulation test yielded a C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes. According to the diagnostic criteria referenced in the report, a stimulated level greater than 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion, supporting a diagnosis of non-type 1 diabetes. Although the report notes this level reflected "insufficient" secretion relative to normal physiology, it was sufficient to distinguish the patient from the absolute insulin deficiency characteristic of classic T1D.

However, clinicians must recognize that these tests are supportive rather than definitive. C-peptide levels may be normal in the early stages of T1D, and antibody status alone cannot confirm MODY. Therefore, as demonstrated in this case, these biochemical findings should trigger molecular genetic testing. The identification of a pathogenic HNF1A variant confirmed the diagnosis, allowing for the discontinuation of insulin and the transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin, which achieved superior glycemic control. Ultimately, while autoantibodies and C-peptide levels help stratify risk and identify candidates for genetic screening, DNA sequencing remains the gold standard for confirming monogenic diabetes and guiding precision medicine.

### [Clinical Pearls] When a genetic variant is novel and absent from databases like ClinVar, what lines of evidence are used to classify it as pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines?

When a genetic variant is novel and absent from databases like ClinVar, classification as pathogenic relies on integrating multiple independent lines of evidence rather than population frequency alone. In this case, the novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S) was not reported in ClinVar or VarSome, yet it was classified as pathogenic based on four key lines of evidence consistent with ACMG guidelines.

First, computational prediction provided strong support, with the variant exhibiting a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995. Second, the variant location was biologically significant; it affected an amino acid residue within the DNA-binding domain adjacent to known pathogenic residues. Third, familial segregation confirmed the association, as the variant tracked with early-onset diabetes in the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother, consistent with autosomal dominant transmission. Finally, the clinical phenotype was concordant with HNF1A-MODY, characterized by early onset, negative autoantibodies, glycosuria, and preserved C-peptide levels. By synthesizing these genetic, bioinformatic, and clinical data points, the team established a definitive diagnosis despite the variant's novelty. This allowed for precision medicine intervention—discontinuing insulin in favor of a GLP-1 receptor agonist—underscoring that variant interpretation depends on the totality of evidence when database records are lacking.

### [Management] Given the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of HNF1A-MODY, what are the immediate management implications for the patient's first-degree relatives?

Given the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of HNF1A-MODY, the immediate management implication for this patient’s first-degree relatives is the offer of cascade genetic screening. Because each child of an affected individual has a 50% probability of inheriting the pathogenic variant, identifying affected family members early is critical for preventing misdiagnosis and tailoring therapy.

In this case, the family history strongly supports this pattern, with the patient’s maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother all presenting with early-onset diabetes. Confirming the diagnosis in relatives allows for the avoidance of unnecessary insulin therapy and inappropriate medications. As highlighted in our patient’s course, genetic confirmation enabled the discontinuation of basal insulin and transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin, which achieved excellent glycemic control. Furthermore, the discussion notes that HNF1A-MODY patients may be at risk for severe dehydration or diabetic ketoacidosis if treated with SGLT-2 inhibitors due to reduced renal glucose reabsorption; knowing the genetic status protects relatives from these specific risks.

Therefore, beyond the index patient, we must counsel the family regarding the high likelihood of shared genetic etiology. We should arrange genetic testing for the mother, brother, and other first-degree relatives. Those who test positive should undergo regular glucose monitoring, while those who test negative can be reassured that their risk reverts to that of the general population. This precision medicine approach ensures that treatment is driven by pathophysiology rather than empirical classification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the limitations of using the MODY probability calculator developed by the Exeter group when evaluating patients from non-White European populations?

The primary limitation of the Exeter MODY probability calculator is its lack of generalizability across diverse ethnicities. As explicitly highlighted in the discussion of this case, the algorithm was derived from data specific to White European populations. When applied to patients from non-White groups, the tool may overestimate the probability of MODY.

In this specific case, the patient’s clinical data yielded a calculated probability of 62.4%; however, the authors caution that this score must be interpreted with care outside the original demographic. Consequently, reliance on the calculator alone is insufficient for diverse populations. Clinical judgment remains paramount. In patients presenting with early-onset diabetes, a strong family history of early-onset disease, and negative islet autoantibodies—as seen in this patient—genetic testing should be pursued regardless of the calculator’s output. This ensures accurate diagnosis and facilitates precision medicine, such as the appropriate transition from insulin to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy demonstrated in this case.
