## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The initial clinical encounter for this patient serves as a foundational example of how demographic factors and presenting symptoms guide the differential diagnosis in early-onset diabetes. The patient was a young adult male referred for evaluation following the incidental discovery of hyperglycemia and glycosuria during a routine annual health examination. While the biochemical abnormalities were found incidentally, the patient subsequently reported classic symptoms of hyperglycemia, including increased thirst, increased appetite, and recent weight loss. In a young patient, the presence of these symptoms alongside significant hyperglycemia typically raises immediate suspicion for type 1 diabetes; however, the absence of acute decompensation requires careful consideration of other etiologies. The incidental nature of the initial finding, combined with the symptomatic progression, underscores the importance of recognizing that monogenic forms of diabetes, such as maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), can present with significant glycemic dysregulation similar to polygenic forms, necessitating a thorough initial assessment to distinguish between them.

Physical examination findings provided critical clues that helped refine the diagnostic possibilities, particularly regarding the presence or absence of insulin resistance. The patient’s body mass index (BMI) was 24.6 kg/m², placing him in the normal to overweight range, which is atypical for the severe obesity often associated with classic type 2 diabetes, though it does not exclude it. Blood pressure was within normal limits at 112/70 mmHg. Notably, the physical examination revealed no evidence of acanthosis nigricans, a cutaneous marker frequently associated with significant insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome. The absence of this finding, in conjunction with a non-obese phenotype, initially argued against a diagnosis of typical type 2 diabetes, suggesting that the underlying pathophysiology might not be driven primarily by peripheral insulin resistance. However, clinicians must remain cautious, as phenotypic features can overlap significantly between diabetes subtypes, especially as obesity prevalence increases across populations.

A detailed family history analysis emerged as the most compelling indicator for a monogenic etiology in this case. The patient’s family history was notable for a strong pattern of early-onset diabetes affecting three consecutive generations: the maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at age 20. This vertical transmission of diabetes across multiple generations, specifically with onset prior to age 35, is characteristic of an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. In the context of a young patient with hyperglycemia, such a family history significantly increases the pre-test probability of MODY compared to type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The absence of early-onset cardiovascular disease among family members further supported a primary defect in glucose metabolism rather than a broader syndrome of severe metabolic dysfunction, reinforcing the need to investigate genetic causes when this specific pedigree pattern is identified.

The initial laboratory evaluation confirmed significant hyperglycemia but also highlighted features that complicated the immediate classification of the disease. The patient’s glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was markedly elevated at 10.2%, with a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L and a 2-hour postprandial glucose of 19.8 mmol/L following an oral glucose tolerance test. Crucially, there were no signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) at presentation, which helps differentiate this case from the absolute insulin deficiency often seen in new-onset type 1 diabetes. The lipid profile revealed elevated triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) and low high-density lipoprotein (1.01 mmol/L), suggesting features of metabolic syndrome. This finding initially supported a working diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, illustrating the diagnostic dilemma where metabolic syndrome features can obscure the presentation of MODY. Consequently, the initial assessment relied on the integration of the strong family history, the lack of acanthosis nigricans, and the absence of DKA to maintain a high index of suspicion for a monogenic cause, prompting further specialized testing beyond standard diabetes workup.

## Pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY and Differential Diagnosis

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is defined as an autosomal dominant, monogenic form of diabetes characterized by a primary defect in pancreatic β-cell development and function, typically manifesting before the age of 25. In the clinical landscape of young-onset diabetes, MODY accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of cases, though prevalence varies significantly across ethnic and racial groups. Research has historically concentrated on White European populations, potentially leading to underestimation in other demographics, including Asian regions where this case report originates. The patient in this case presented with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at a young age without diabetic ketoacidosis, accompanied by a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations. This triad of early onset, familial clustering, and non-autoimmune presentation aligns with the clinical definition of MODY, distinguishing it from the more common polygenic forms of diabetes. Understanding these epidemiological nuances is critical, as genetic screening is often limited to individuals based on specific clinical characteristics, which can vary by population and lead to missed diagnoses.

The underlying molecular mechanism in this patient involves a novel heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene, specifically p.N270S (c.809A>G) in exon 4. HNF1A encodes a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas, playing a pivotal role in regulating insulin secretion and glucose metabolism. Mutations in this gene lead to a progressive decline in β-cell function through impaired expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes, as well as reduced pyruvate kinase activity. In this case, the mutation was located in the DNA-binding domain, a region where the majority of pathogenic mutations are identified. Although the specific variant was not found in public databases like ClinVar, its pathogenicity was supported by ACMG guidelines, including its location adjacent to known pathogenic residues and its segregation with diabetes in the family. This genetic defect explains the patient's insufficient insulin secretion observed on the glucagon stimulation test, where stimulated C-peptide levels indicated preserved yet declining β-cell function, a hallmark of the progressive nature of HNF1A-MODY.

A distinctive clinical feature observed in this patient was glycosuria in the absence of severe hyperglycaemia typical of uncontrolled diabetes. This phenomenon is directly linked to the pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY, as the HNF1A transcription factor regulates the expression of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal tubules. Mutations in HNF1A result in reduced SGLT2 expression, which lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, allowing glucose to spill into the urine at lower blood concentrations. This mechanism is clinically significant because it informs treatment decisions; for instance, the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY patients carries increased risks of dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to the already compromised residual SGLT-2 activity. Recognizing this specific pathophysiological link allows clinicians to avoid potentially harmful pharmacological classes and select safer alternatives, such as the GLP-1 receptor agonist used in this case.

Differentiating HNF1A-MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes requires a careful synthesis of clinical phenotype, biochemical markers, and genetic evidence, as features often overlap. Type 1 diabetes is typically autoimmune, characterized by positive islet autoantibodies (such as GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8) and absolute insulin deficiency. In this case, the patient tested negative for GADA and IA-2A antibodies, and the glucagon stimulation test revealed a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L, indicating preserved endogenous insulin secretion inconsistent with advanced type 1 diabetes. Conversely, type 2 diabetes is often associated with insulin resistance, obesity, and acanthosis nigricans. While the patient had a lipid profile suggesting metabolic syndrome, his BMI was 24.6 kg/m², and no acanthosis nigricans was observed, making typical type 2 diabetes less likely. The diagnostic reasoning in this case relied on the combination of negative autoantibodies, a strong family history of early-onset disease, and a MODY probability calculator score of 62.4%. Ultimately, molecular genetic testing confirmed the diagnosis, illustrating that while clinical algorithms can raise suspicion, genetic confirmation remains essential for precision medicine and appropriate management.

## Diagnostic Workup and Genetic Variant Interpretation

The diagnostic journey for monogenic diabetes requires a systematic approach to differentiate Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) from type 1 and type 2 diabetes, particularly in young patients who do not fit classic phenotypic profiles. In this case, the initial clinical suspicion of MODY was driven by the patient’s young age at presentation, the absence of diabetic ketoacidosis, and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations. To rule out autoimmune type 1 diabetes, the standard of care involves testing for islet autoantibodies. This patient underwent testing for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A), both of which returned negative results. While the presence of autoantibodies confirms type 1 diabetes, their absence does not definitively exclude it; however, in the context of a strong family history and non-autoimmune clinical features, negative serology significantly increases the pre-test probability of a monogenic etiology. This step is crucial because misdiagnosing MODY as type 1 diabetes can lead to unnecessary lifelong insulin therapy, whereas misdiagnosing it as type 2 diabetes may delay appropriate genetic counseling and targeted treatment.

Following the exclusion of autoimmune markers, assessment of endogenous insulin secretion is necessary to evaluate β-cell function. In this case, a glucagon stimulation test was employed to measure stimulated C-peptide levels, providing a snapshot of residual β-cell capacity. The patient demonstrated a baseline C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L which rose to 0.83 nmol/L at six minutes post-glucagon injection. While a stimulated level greater than 0.60 nmol/L generally suggests preserved endogenous insulin secretion, the clinical interpretation in this instance described the function as moderately preserved yet insufficient to maintain normoglycemia. This finding helped distinguish the case from the absolute insulin deficiency typical of advanced type 1 diabetes, yet it also highlighted the progressive β-cell dysfunction characteristic of HNF1A-MODY. It is important to note that while the glucagon stimulation test is supportive, it is not definitive; therefore, biochemical evidence of residual insulin secretion combined with negative autoantibodies serves as a strong indicator for proceeding to genetic analysis.

Genetic confirmation was achieved using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel, which allows for the simultaneous analysis of multiple genes associated with monogenic diabetes. This technology is particularly valuable in cases where clinical suspicion is high but the specific genetic defect is unknown. In this patient, the NGS panel identified a novel heterozygous substitution in the HNF1A gene, specifically p.N270S (c.809A>G) in exon 4. The utility of NGS in this scenario lies in its ability to detect rare or previously unreported variants that might be missed by targeted single-gene testing. The identification of a variant in HNF1A was consistent with the clinical phenotype, as HNF1A-MODY is the most common form of MODY and typically presents with autosomal dominant inheritance and progressive β-cell failure. However, the novelty of the variant required rigorous interpretation to confirm its pathogenicity before finalizing the diagnosis.

Interpreting novel genetic variants requires adherence to standardized frameworks, such as the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). Because the p.N270S variant was absent from major databases like ClinVar and VarSome, its classification relied on multiple lines of evidence rather than population frequency data alone. The variant was situated in the DNA-binding domain of the HNF1A protein, a region known to harbor pathogenic mutations. Furthermore, in silico prediction tools, such as the AlphaMissense score, indicated a high probability of pathogenicity with a score of 0.995. These computational predictions were corroborated by clinical segregation analysis, which demonstrated that the variant co-segregated with diabetes within the patient’s family, including the mother and brother. This combination of computational data, structural location, and familial segregation allowed the clinical team to classify the variant as pathogenic according to ACMG criteria, confirming the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

The integration of biochemical testing and genetic variant interpretation underscores the necessity of precision medicine in diabetes management. In this case, the diagnostic workup moved beyond standard metabolic profiling to identify a specific molecular defect that explained the patient’s clinical course. The confirmation of a pathogenic HNF1A mutation validated the clinical suspicion raised by the negative autoantibodies and the specific pattern of β-cell dysfunction. This diagnostic clarity was not merely academic; it directly informed the subsequent management strategy, allowing for the discontinuation of insulin and the transition to agents more suitable for HNF1A-MODY, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists. Ultimately, this case illustrates that a structured diagnostic algorithm, incorporating autoantibody screening, functional testing, and advanced genetic interpretation, is essential for accurately identifying MODY and optimizing long-term patient outcomes.

## Genotype-Guided Management and Pharmacotherapy

The management of this patient illustrates the critical transition from empiric therapy to precision medicine once a monogenic etiology is confirmed. Upon initial presentation, the patient exhibited significant hyperglycemia with an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, and symptoms of catabolism including weight loss and polydipsia. In the absence of genetic data, the clinical team initiated a regimen consistent with type 2 diabetes or severe insulin deficiency, comprising basal insulin degludec, metformin, and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin. This initial empiric approach prioritized rapid glycemic control and addressed potential insulin resistance suggested by the patient's lipid profile, which indicated metabolic syndrome features. However, this strategy was provisional, intended to stabilize the patient while awaiting the results of molecular testing.

Once the novel heterozygous HNF1A mutation was identified, the treatment strategy shifted to align with the specific pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY. This condition is characterized by a primary defect in pancreatic β-cell function rather than insulin resistance or autoimmune destruction. Consequently, HNF1A-MODY is classically sensitive to secretagogues that stimulate residual insulin production. While low-dose sulfonylureas are traditionally considered first-line therapy due to their action on potassium-sensitive ATP channels, this case demonstrates the efficacy of alternative agents. Following genetic confirmation and an initial reduction in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin therapy was discontinued. It was replaced with the GLP-1 receptor agonist dulaglutide. Literature referenced in the case discussion supports this decision, noting that GLP-1 receptor agonists can be a safer and effective alternative to sulfonylureas, particularly for preserving pancreatic function and minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia. Ultimately, the patient’s regimen was simplified to metformin and saxagliptin for long-term maintenance, achieving an HbA1c of 6.0% without insulin.

The case report also highlights specific pharmacological contraindications relevant to HNF1A-MODY that must be considered during treatment planning. The HNF1A gene transcriptionally regulates the expression of the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal tubules. In patients with HNF1A mutations, SGLT2 expression is often reduced, leading to a lower renal threshold for glucose and resulting glycosuria. The discussion notes that adding SGLT2 inhibitors to the regimen in these patients may compound this effect, potentially increasing the risk of severe dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis. Therefore, despite the common use of SGLT2 inhibitors in type 2 diabetes, they are generally avoided in HNF1A-MODY to prevent these adverse outcomes.

The decision to discontinue insulin was predicated on the genetic diagnosis combined with biochemical evidence of preserved, albeit insufficient, endogenous insulin secretion. The patient’s stimulated C-peptide level was 0.83 nmol/L, indicating that β-cells were still functional enough to respond to non-insulin therapies. This contrasts with type 1 diabetes, where insulin replacement is mandatory due to autoimmune destruction. The successful withdrawal of insulin in this case underscores the importance of genetic testing in identifying patients who may be unnecessarily maintained on insulin. By confirming the diagnosis of MODY, clinicians can de-escalate therapy to oral agents or non-insulin injectables that target the specific defect in insulin secretion, thereby improving quality of life and reducing treatment burden.

Finally, pharmacotherapy was supported by standard lifestyle interventions. Upon diagnosis, the patient was advised to adhere to a structured dietary plan and engage in regular physical activity. While the primary driver of glycemic improvement in this case was the genotype-specific pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modifications remained an essential adjunctive component of the comprehensive management approach. The patient’s consistent maintenance of HbA1c below 6.5% over the follow-up period, without evidence of microalbuminuria or retinopathy, demonstrates that a tailored regimen combining precision pharmacotherapy with lifestyle measures can yield excellent long-term outcomes in young adults with monogenic diabetes.

## Clinical Outcomes and Broader Implications for Practice

The clinical trajectory of this patient underscores the profound impact of precise genetic diagnosis on therapeutic management and long-term glycemic control. At presentation, the patient exhibited significant hyperglycemia with a glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) level of 10.2%, prompting an initial empiric treatment regimen of basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. This approach aimed to rapidly stabilize glucose levels while the diagnostic workup proceeded. However, following the genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, the treatment strategy was refined to align with the specific pathophysiology of the condition. Insulin therapy was discontinued in favor of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, reflecting the patient’s moderately preserved yet insufficient β-cell function. This targeted adjustment yielded a rapid and sustained improvement in glycemic metrics; within one month, HbA1c dropped to 9.1%, and by six months, it reached 6.0%. Subsequently, the regimen was simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor for long-term maintenance, with HbA1c consistently maintained below 6.5%. This outcome illustrates a core principle of precision medicine in endocrinology: accurate molecular diagnosis allows for the de-escalation of intensive therapies, such as insulin, to safer, more effective oral or injectable agents that specifically target the underlying defect in insulin secretion.

Beyond glycemic metrics, comprehensive monitoring for microvascular complications remains essential in MODY patients, even when glucose levels are well-controlled. In this case, baseline investigations revealed no microalbuminuria, and follow-up assessments confirmed the absence of diabetic retinopathy or nephropathy to date. However, the patient did exhibit severely reduced sudomotor function on electrochemical skin conductance testing, indicating significant autonomic dysfunction. This finding highlights that while HNF1A-MODY may spare some microvascular complications compared to type 1 or type 2 diabetes in the early stages, patients are not immune to diabetic sequelae. The absence of retinopathy and nephropathy at the time of follow-up is a favorable outcome, yet it necessitates ongoing surveillance. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for complications and ensure regular screening protocols are adhered to, as the long-term natural history of novel mutations may differ from established variants.

The autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of HNF1A-MODY carries significant implications for the patient’s family, making cascade genetic screening a critical component of care. The patient’s family history was notable for early-onset diabetes affecting the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother, consistent with the vertical transmission typical of MODY. Identifying the specific pathogenic variant in the proband enables targeted testing of at-risk relatives. Cascade screening is vital not only to confirm diagnoses in family members but also to facilitate early intervention and prevent the misdiagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes in relatives. For family members who test negative for the familial variant, the likelihood of MODY is excluded, sparing them from unnecessary monitoring and inappropriate treatment. Conversely, identifying affected relatives allows for the early initiation of sulfonylureas or other appropriate therapies, potentially delaying the onset of symptomatic hyperglycemia and preserving β-cell function.

Diagnostic algorithms for MODY often utilize clinical probability calculators, yet their utility must be interpreted with caution regarding population diversity. In this case, the patient’s clinical data entered into the Exeter MODY probability calculator yielded an estimated probability of 62.4%. While this score supported the clinical suspicion, the report notes that such tools were primarily derived from White European populations and may overestimate the probability of MODY in non-White groups. This limitation is particularly relevant given that MODY prevalence varies across ethnicities and may be underestimated in Asian and Australian regions due to limited screening. Relying solely on clinical calculators without genetic confirmation can lead to diagnostic errors. Therefore, while calculators serve as useful triage tools, molecular genetic testing remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis, especially in populations where the predictive values of clinical criteria may differ.

Finally, the identification of a novel heterozygous HNF1A mutation (p.N270S) not previously reported in databases like ClinVar highlights the evolving landscape of genetic diagnostics. The pathogenicity of this variant was established using American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines, supported by its location in the DNA-binding domain and segregation with diabetes in the family. This case reinforces the necessity of next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels to detect rare or novel variants that standard screening might miss. As the spectrum of known mutations expands, future directions must focus on developing more cost-effective NGS screening strategies to improve diagnostic rates. Enhancing access to genetic testing will ensure that patients with monogenic diabetes receive the specific therapeutic benefits they require, ultimately improving clinical outcomes and reducing the long-term burden of misdiagnosis.
