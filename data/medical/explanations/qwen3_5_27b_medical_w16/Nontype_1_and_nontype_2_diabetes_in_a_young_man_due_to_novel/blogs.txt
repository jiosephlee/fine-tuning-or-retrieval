# The Lean Diabetic Trap: When Young Hyperglycemia Isn't Type 1 or Type 2

## The Diagnostic Dilemma
In the emergency department or primary care clinic, we often fall into a binary trap when evaluating a young patient with hyperglycemia. If they are lean and symptomatic, we think Type 1 Diabetes. If they are overweight, we lean toward Type 2. But what happens when the patient is young, lean, has significant hyperglycemia, and fits neither mold perfectly? A recent case report highlights a scenario that challenges this binary thinking: a young adult male presenting with an HbA1c of 10.2% and glycosuria, yet negative for diabetic ketoacidosis and autoimmune markers.

This patient, with a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, presented with classic symptoms of diabetes including polydipsia, polyuria, and weight loss. The initial clinical picture was confusing. He lacked the obesity typically associated with Type 2, yet he didn't have the absolute insulin deficiency or autoantibodies expected in Type 1. The real story, however, lay in his pedigree. His family history was notable for early-onset diabetes across three generations: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. This pattern of autosomal dominant inheritance was the first major red flag for Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY).

## Unmasking the Mutation
MODY is an autosomal dominant monogenic form of diabetes, accounting for approximately 1% to 6% of young-onset diabetes cases. Despite its prevalence, it is frequently misdiagnosed because clinical features often overlap with Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes. In this case, the diagnostic workup included a glucagon stimulation test which showed a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L. While this indicated insufficient insulin secretion, it also suggested preserved endogenous insulin secretion, distinguishing it from the absolute deficiency seen in Type 1. Furthermore, tests for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A) were negative.

When the clinical suspicion for MODY was high, molecular genetic testing was performed. The results revealed a novel heterozygous pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S). This specific mutation had not been previously reported in databases like ClinVar, yet it met the American College of Medical Genetics criteria for pathogenicity based on its location in the DNA-binding domain and its segregation with diabetes in the family. Identifying this mutation was not just an academic exercise; it fundamentally altered the patient's management trajectory.

## Precision Medicine in Action
The treatment implications of a MODY diagnosis are profound. Initially, this patient was started on a regimen of basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin, reflecting a preliminary consideration of Type 2 diabetes to achieve rapid glycemic control. However, once the HNF1A-MODY diagnosis was confirmed, the treatment strategy shifted. Insulin therapy was discontinued because HNF1A-MODY patients often retain beta-cell function that can be supported without exogenous insulin, unlike Type 1.

The patient was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) and metformin. This switch proved highly effective, reducing his HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% within six months. Eventually, the regimen was simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor for long-term maintenance. This case underscores a critical point: patients with HNF1A-MODY are often sensitive to sulfonylureas, but GLP-1 receptor agonists serve as a safer and effective alternative, particularly as beta-cell function declines, with a lower risk of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas.

## Hidden Risks and Clinical Pearls
Beyond the benefits of targeted therapy, the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY also reveals specific risks to avoid. The discussion in the case report highlights a unique pathophysiology regarding glycosuria. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria is attributed to reduced expression of SGLT2, which is under the transcriptional control of HNF1A. Because these patients already have reduced renal threshold for glucose, the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors carries specific risks. There is a potential for severe dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and even diabetic ketoacidosis due to the high inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. Therefore, knowing the genetic etiology protects the patient from potentially harmful medication classes.

Additionally, clinicians should be aware that traditional diagnostic tools may have limitations. The MODY probability calculator developed by the Exeter group was used in this case, estimating a 62.4% probability of MODY. However, this tool was derived from White European populations and may overestimate probability in non-White groups. Furthermore, while obesity is often lower in MODY patients compared to Type 2, rising obesity rates in the general population can make BMI a less effective discriminator. The presence of metabolic syndrome features, such as the elevated triglycerides seen in this patient, should not rule out MODY.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect MODY in young patients:** Consider genetic testing for diabetes diagnosed before age 25, especially with a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations.
*   **Don't rely on BMI alone:** MODY patients are often lean, but rising obesity rates mean a normal BMI does not exclude Type 2, nor does it confirm MODY without genetic evidence.
*   **Check antibodies and C-peptide:** Negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) and preserved stimulated C-peptide levels are supportive of MODY rather than Type 1 diabetes.
*   **Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY:** Due to reduced SGLT2 expression, these patients are at higher risk for dehydration and DKA when using this class of medication.
*   **Tailor therapy to genetics:** HNF1A-MODY patients often respond well to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists and may not require lifelong insulin, improving quality of life and reducing hypoglycemia risk.



# When the Database is Silent: Interpreting Novel Genetic Variants in Diabetes

You know the drill. A young patient walks in with hyperglycemia. They aren’t obese, so Type 2 diabetes seems unlikely. They aren’t in ketoacidosis, and their antibodies are negative, so Type 1 is less probable. You order a genetic panel for Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), and the report comes back with a variant you’ve never seen before. It’s not in ClinVar. It’s not in VarSome. The database is silent.

Do you ignore it? Do you treat it as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) and stick to standard diabetes protocols? Or do you dig deeper?

This week’s case highlights a young man with HNF1A-MODY caused by a novel mutation. The twist isn’t just the diagnosis—it’s how we validated a genetic finding that didn’t exist in the literature, and how that single discovery completely altered his treatment plan from insulin dependence to a simpler regimen.

## The Clinical Picture

The patient was a young adult male referred for evaluation of hyperglycemia and glycosuria discovered during an annual health exam. He presented with an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, and a 2-hour post-OGTT glucose of 19.8 mmol/L. There was no diabetic ketoacidosis.

Physically, he wasn't obese (BMI 24.6 kg/m²) and had no acanthosis nigricans. However, his family history was a screaming red flag for monogenic diabetes. His maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. This autosomal dominant pattern is classic for MODY, yet these patients are frequently misdiagnosed as having Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes due to overlapping clinical features.

Initial workup showed negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A). A glucagon stimulation test revealed a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, indicating insufficient but preserved endogenous insulin secretion. While this might suggest early Type 1, the negative antibodies and strong family history pushed the team toward a genetic etiology.

## The Genetic Dilemma

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified a heterozygous substitution in exon 4 of the *HNF1A* gene: p.N270S (c.809A>G). Here is where the case gets interesting. This specific mutation was not reported in the literature, nor was it found in the VarSome or ClinVar databases.

In the era of precision medicine, finding a "novel" variant can feel like hitting a dead end. How do you determine pathogenicity when the crowdsourced databases are empty? The team relied on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation, looking for independent lines of evidence beyond simple database matches.

They utilized an AlphaMissense score, which predicted a high likelihood of pathogenicity (0.995). Furthermore, the variant was located in the DNA-binding domain of the *HNF1A* protein, a region known to harbor pathogenic mutations. Crucially, the variant segregated with diabetes in the family. When clinical suspicion, biochemical evidence (glycosuria due to reduced renal threshold), and genetic evidence align, a novel variant can be classified as pathogenic even without prior publication.

## Changing the Course of Treatment

Why does this genetic label matter? Because HNF1A-MODY responds differently to medication than Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes.

Initially, before the genetic results were available, the patient was treated with basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor, reflecting a preliminary consideration of Type 2 diabetes. However, once the HNF1A-MODY diagnosis was confirmed, the team discontinued the insulin. They transitioned the patient to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) combined with metformin.

The results were striking. Within three months, his HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0%. Long-term maintenance was eventually simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor, with HbA1c consistently maintained below 6.5%.

This shift is critical because HNF1A-MODY patients are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas and GLP-1 receptor agonists due to the mechanism of the mutation, which affects insulin secretion. Conversely, the case discussion highlights a significant safety warning: SGLT-2 inhibitors should be used with extreme caution or avoided. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria is already present due to reduced SGLT2 expression. Adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor can lead to severe dehydration, vascular collapse, and even diabetic ketoacidosis due to high inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity.

## Why This Matters for the Frontline

MODY accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of young-onset diabetes cases, yet it remains frequently underestimated. The barrier to diagnosis is often a lack of clinical suspicion. Tools like the Exeter MODY probability calculator can help, but they were derived from White European populations and may overestimate probability in non-White groups.

In this case, the probability calculator estimated a 62.4% chance of MODY, but it was the genetic confirmation that allowed for de-escalation of therapy. Without the genetic diagnosis, this patient might have remained on unnecessary insulin, facing the risks of hypoglycemia and the burden of injections, despite having a condition that responds well to oral or injectable non-insulin therapies.

Furthermore, accurate diagnosis facilitates family counseling. Since MODY is autosomal dominant, first-degree relatives have a 50% chance of carrying the mutation. Identifying the proband allows for cascade screening of the family, potentially preventing misdiagnosis in siblings or parents.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect MODY in young-onset diabetes:** Look for non-obese patients with negative autoantibodies and a strong three-generation family history of early-onset diabetes.
*   **Don't ignore novel variants:** If a genetic variant isn't in ClinVar, use ACMG guidelines, AlphaMissense scores, and family segregation data to determine pathogenicity.
*   **Tailor therapy to the gene:** HNF1A-MODY patients respond well to sulfonylureas and GLP-1 receptor agonists; insulin is often unnecessary once control is established.
*   **Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY:** These patients already have reduced renal glucose reabsorption; adding SGLT-2 inhibitors increases the risk of dehydration and euglycemic DKA.
*   **Genetics change management:** Confirming monogenic diabetes can allow for de-escalation from insulin to simpler regimens, improving quality of life and reducing hypoglycemia risk.



# Deprescribing Insulin: Precision Pharmacotherapy for HNF1A-MODY

Imagine a young adult walking into your clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2%. They have glycosuria, weight loss, and polydipsia. The immediate reflex for many of us is to start basal insulin, perhaps assuming Type 1 diabetes given the young age and hyperglycemia, or aggressive Type 2 management given the severity. But what if that insulin isn't just unnecessary—it's the wrong tool for the job?

This case highlights a scenario where a precise genetic diagnosis allowed for the deprescribing of insulin in favor of targeted oral and injectable agents, resulting in sustained remission of hyperglycemia without the burden of insulin therapy. It serves as a critical reminder that not all diabetes in the young is autoimmune, and not all non-autoimmune diabetes is Type 2.

## The Clinical Picture: Beyond the Binary

The patient in this report presented with significant hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) during a routine health exam. Crucially, there was no diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) at presentation. His BMI was 24.6 kg/m², placing him in a normal weight range, and he lacked physical signs of insulin resistance like acanthosis nigricans.

The family history was the first red flag. His maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. This three-generation history of early-onset diabetes strongly suggested a monogenic etiology rather than sporadic Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes.

Initial workup revealed negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), which argues against Type 1 diabetes. A glucagon stimulation test showed a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L. While this indicated insufficient insulin secretion, it also showed preserved endogenous function, distinguishing him from the absolute insulin deficiency seen in advanced Type 1. Despite these clues, the patient was initially started on basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor while awaiting genetic results—a common practice when the diagnosis is unclear and glycemic control is urgent.

## The Genetic Twist: HNF1A-MODY

Genetic testing confirmed the suspicion of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). Specifically, the patient had a novel heterozygous mutation in the *HNF1A* gene (p.N270S). HNF1A-MODY, also known as MODY 3, is the most common form of MODY, accounting for 30% to 65% of cases.

The pathophysiology here is distinct. The *HNF1A* gene encodes a transcription factor essential for pancreatic β-cell development and function. Mutations lead to a progressive decline in β-cell function, impaired insulin expression, and reduced pyruvate kinase activity. Interestingly, this mutation also affects the kidneys. HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression, and reduced SGLT2 activity lowers the renal threshold for glucose. This explains the patient’s glycosuria despite glucose levels that might not typically cause spilling in Type 2 diabetes.

## The Therapeutic Pivot: Why Stop the Insulin?

Once the genetic diagnosis was confirmed, the treatment plan underwent a significant shift. The patient’s insulin was discontinued, and he was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) and metformin. Within six months, his HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0%.

Why make this change? In HNF1A-MODY, the primary defect is insulin secretion, not insulin resistance. These patients remain exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release from the remaining functional β-cells. While sulfonylureas are often considered first-line, GLP-1 receptor agonists have emerged as a potent alternative. They enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion and preserve β-cell function without the high risk of hypoglycemia associated with sulfonylureas.

In this specific case, the patient had moderately preserved β-cell function. Continuing insulin would have been overtreatment, exposing the patient to unnecessary hypoglycemia and weight gain risks without addressing the underlying monogenic defect. The transition to a GLP-1 agonist provided robust glycemic control while simplifying the regimen.

## Medication Caveats: What to Avoid

Precision medicine in MODY isn't just about what to prescribe; it's also about what to avoid. The case report highlights a critical safety warning regarding SGLT-2 inhibitors.

Because HNF1A mutations already reduce SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules (leading to the glycosuria seen in the patient), adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor can be dangerous. The combination may lead to severe dehydration, vascular collapse, or even euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) due to the excessive inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. In a patient with HNF1A-MODY, the risks of these medications likely outweigh the benefits.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect MODY in young patients:** Early-onset diabetes (before age 25), strong family history across three generations, and negative autoantibodies should trigger consideration for genetic testing.
*   **Don't default to insulin:** In HNF1A-MODY, patients often respond dramatically to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists. Insulin is typically reserved for severe hyperglycemia or DKA at onset.
*   **Watch for glycosuria:** Unexplained glycosuria in the absence of extreme hyperglycemia can be a clue for HNF1A-MODY due to reduced renal glucose threshold.
*   **Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors:** Patients with HNF1A-MODY are at increased risk for dehydration and DKA when on SGLT-2 inhibitors due to pre-existing reduced SGLT2 expression.
*   **Genetic testing changes management:** Confirming the diagnosis allows for deprescribing of unnecessary medications (like insulin) and tailoring therapy to preserve β-cell function.



# The SGLT2 Conundrum: Renal Thresholds and Drug Safety in MODY

We see diabetes all the time. In the ED or clinic, the differential usually narrows quickly to Type 1, Type 2, or maybe steroid-induced. But what about the young, non-obese patient with negative antibodies who doesn't quite fit the mold?

A recent case report by Liu et al. highlights a young man presenting with significant hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, initially managed as Type 2 diabetes before genetic testing revealed a novel mutation in the *HNF1A* gene. This diagnosis wasn't just a semantic change; it fundamentally altered his treatment plan and raised a critical safety question regarding a class of drugs we prescribe frequently: SGLT2 inhibitors.

## The Case: A Young Man Who Didn't Fit the Mold

The patient was a young adult referred for evaluation of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria during a routine health exam. His BMI was 24.6 kg/m², and he had no acanthosis nigricans. His HbA1c was a staggering 10.2%, with fasting glucose at 12.4 mmol/L. Crucially, there were no signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), and autoantibodies for Type 1 diabetes (GADA, IA-2A) were negative.

However, the family history was a red flag. His maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother all had early-onset diabetes. Initial treatment involved basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. But with the clinical suspicion of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), genetic testing was pursued.

The results confirmed a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in *HNF1A* (p.N270S), consistent with HNF1A-MODY. Once confirmed, the treatment strategy shifted. Insulin was discontinued, and he was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) alongside metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor. Within six months, his HbA1c dropped to 6.0%.

## The Pathophysiology: Why Does He Have Glycosuria?

Here is where the physiology gets interesting and clinically relevant. *HNF1A* encodes a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. In the pancreas, mutations lead to progressive β-cell dysfunction and impaired insulin secretion. But in the kidney, the story is different.

The case report notes that glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is attributed to reduced expression of SGLT2 (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2). SGLT2 is responsible for the majority of glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule. Because *HNF1A* regulates the transcription of SGLT2, a mutation leads to a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption.

This means patients with HNF1A-MODY excrete glucose in their urine at lower blood glucose levels than the general population. It explains the glycosuria seen in this patient despite the absence of DKA. It also sets the stage for a potential medication interaction that could be dangerous.

## The SGLT2 Inhibitor Risk

We love SGLT2 inhibitors (like empagliflozin or dapagliflozin) for Type 2 diabetes and heart failure. They lower glucose by blocking reabsorption in the kidney, forcing glucose out through the urine. But in a patient with HNF1A-MODY, the renal threshold is already compromised due to the genetic defect.

The discussion in the case report highlights a specific warning: prescribing SGLT2 inhibitors to HNF1A-MODY patients may be risky. Because the patient already has reduced SGLT2 activity due to the mutation, pharmacologically inhibiting the remaining activity could compound the effect.

The authors warn that this compounded inhibition can lead to severe dehydration and potentially diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), even in the absence of severe hyperglycaemia (euglycemic DKA). The risk of vascular collapse and genital infections may also be increased. While the patient in this case did not receive an SGLT2 inhibitor, the pathophysiology suggests that the marginal benefit of glucose lowering might be outweighed by the safety risks in this specific population.

## Treatment Pearls for MODY

This case reinforces that precision medicine isn't just a buzzword; it changes management. HNF1A-MODY patients are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin secretion via potassium-sensitive ATP channels. However, as β-cell function declines, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer alternative with a lower risk of hypoglycaemia compared to sulfonylureas.

In this patient, the transition from insulin to a GLP-1 agonist resulted in excellent glycaemic control (HbA1c <6.5%) without the burden of insulin therapy. Furthermore, avoiding SGLT2 inhibitors prevents the risk of exacerbating the inherent renal glucose handling defect.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect MODY in young, non-obese patients** with negative autoantibodies and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes.
*   **Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is physiological:** It results from reduced SGLT2 expression due to the transcriptional defect, not just hyperglycaemia.
*   **Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY:** The inherent reduction in renal glucose reabsorption capacity may predispose these patients to severe dehydration and euglycemic DKA when combined with pharmacological SGLT2 inhibition.
*   **Genetic testing guides therapy:** Confirming HNF1A-MODY allows for the discontinuation of insulin and the use of sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, which are more effective and safer for this phenotype.
*   **Don't rely on BMI alone:** While MODY patients are often lean, rising obesity rates can make BMI a less reliable discriminator between MODY and Type 2 diabetes.



# One Diagnosis, Three Generations: The Imperative of Cascade Screening

You see a young patient in your clinic. They are not obese, they have no autoimmune antibodies, and they aren’t in ketoacidosis. Yet, their HbA1c is 10.2%. You treat them for type 2 diabetes, but something feels off. The family history is the loudest clue in the room: a maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, a mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother already diagnosed at age 20. This isn’t just a metabolic issue; it is a genetic legacy.

A recent case report highlights exactly this scenario in a young adult male with a novel HNF1A mutation causing Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). What makes this case particularly striking is not just the identification of a previously unreported pathogenic variant, but the profound shift in management once the diagnosis was secured. The patient was initially managed with basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor based on a presumed type 2 diabetes diagnosis. However, once genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY, insulin was discontinued, and the regimen was simplified to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin. Within six months, the HbA1c plummeted to 6.0%, and the patient remained stable on oral and non-insulin injectable therapy.

This case serves as a potent reminder that early-onset diabetes is frequently misdiagnosed. Approximately 1% to 6% of young-onset diabetes cases are due to MODY, a group of dominantly inherited disorders characterized by primary defects in pancreatic β-cell function. In this specific instance, the patient presented with hyperglycemia and glycosuria but no diabetic ketoacidosis. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, which is within the normal range, yet the lipid profile suggested metabolic syndrome features. Crucially, autoantibodies for type 1 diabetes, including GADA and IA-2A, were negative. The glucagon stimulation test showed insufficient insulin secretion, with a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, indicating preserved yet insufficient β-cell function.

The genetic workup revealed a heterozygous substitution, p.N270S in exon 4 of HNF1A. This variant was novel, absent from databases like ClinVar or VarSome, yet it met ACMG guidelines for a pathogenic classification due to its location in the DNA-binding domain, a high AlphaMissense score, and segregation with diabetes in the family. This underscores that negative database matches do not rule out pathogenicity when clinical and genetic evidence aligns. The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor essential for insulin secretion and renal glucose reabsorption. Mutations here lead to progressive β-cell decline and often present with glycosuria due to a reduced renal threshold for glucose.

The clinical implications of this diagnosis extend far beyond the index patient. MODY is autosomal dominant, meaning one positive diagnosis unlocks the medical history of an entire family. In this case, three generations were affected. If the patient’s mother, grandmother, and brother are all living with this mutation, they may be undergoing unnecessary treatments. Patients with HNF1A-MODY are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas, which act on potassium-sensitive ATP channels to enhance insulin secretion. However, as β-cell function declines, GLP-1 receptor agonists have emerged as a safer and effective alternative, offering lower risks of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas. Conversely, SGLT-2 inhibitors should be used with extreme caution or avoided. Because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression, patients already have reduced SGLT2 activity and glycosuria; inhibiting this further can precipitate severe dehydration, vascular collapse, or euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis.

Cascade screening is the logical next step after confirming a monogenic diabetes diagnosis. It is not merely an academic exercise; it is a public health imperative. Identifying at-risk relatives allows for the cessation of unnecessary insulin therapy, the avoidance of contraindicated medications like SGLT-2 inhibitors, and the initiation of precise, targeted therapies. It also facilitates family counseling regarding prognosis and the potential for complications. In this case, the patient had no microalbuminuria or retinopathy at follow-up, but long-term surveillance is critical. The diagnostic algorithm typically involves assessing clinical characteristics, ruling out autoimmunity, and utilizing genetic testing when suspicion is high. While tools like the MODY probability calculator exist, they were derived from White European populations and may underestimate or overestimate risk in other demographics, making clinical judgment and family history paramount.

Ultimately, this case illustrates the power of precision medicine in endocrinology. We often default to the binary of type 1 versus type 2 diabetes, but the spectrum is much wider. Recognizing the pattern of early-onset diabetes across multiple generations, combined with negative autoantibodies and preserved C-peptide, should trigger a genetic workup. By doing so, we do more than correct a lab value; we prevent decades of inappropriate treatment for the patient and their relatives.

*   **Suspect MODY in young-onset diabetes** (<25 years) with negative autoantibodies and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations.
*   **Genetic testing is definitive** for MODY diagnosis, even for novel variants not found in standard databases, provided they meet ACMG pathogenicity criteria.
*   **Treatment must be genotype-specific:** HNF1A-MODY patients respond well to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists but should generally avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors due to dehydration and DKA risks.
*   **Cascade screening is essential** because MODY is autosomal dominant; testing family members can prevent misdiagnosis and unnecessary insulin therapy in relatives.
*   **Monitor for complications** despite good control, as HNF1A-MODY is associated with progressive β-cell dysfunction and potential renal or retinal issues.
