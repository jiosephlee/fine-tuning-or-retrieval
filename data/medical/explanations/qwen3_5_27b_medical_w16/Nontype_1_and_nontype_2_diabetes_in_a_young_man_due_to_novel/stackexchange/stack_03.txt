Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Management] While sulfonylureas are traditionally first-line for HNF1A-MODY, why might a GLP-1 receptor agonist be preferred in certain patients, and what mechanism underlies the efficacy of sulfonylureas in this condition?

Colleagues, regarding the management of HNF1A-MODY, it is essential to understand both the mechanism of traditional therapy and the rationale for newer alternatives.

Traditionally, low-dose sulfonylureas are the first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY. As noted in the discussion of this case, sulfonylureas enhance insulin secretion by acting on potassium-sensitive ATP channels. This mechanism is particularly effective in HNF1A-MODY because the pathophysiology involves a progressive decline in β-cell function and decreased insulin secretion, yet residual β-cell function often remains sufficient to respond to secretagogues.

However, a GLP-1 receptor agonist may be preferred in certain clinical scenarios. According to the case report, GLP-1 receptor agonists serve as a safer and potentially more effective alternative to sulfonylureas as β-cell function declines, particularly in individuals prone to hypoglycaemia. The authors cite evidence that GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy presents a lower risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared with sulfonylureas like glimepiride. Furthermore, in this specific patient, the transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) following genetic confirmation was utilized to support glycaemic control and preserve pancreatic function, resulting in a reduction of HbA1c from 9.1% to 6.0% over six months. Thus, while sulfonylureas remain the classic choice, GLP-1 agonists offer a valuable alternative with a superior safety profile regarding hypoglycemia in appropriate patients.
