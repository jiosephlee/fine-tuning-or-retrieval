Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Stabilization

The clinical presentation of this case highlights the diagnostic challenge posed by pancreatopleural fistula (PPF), a condition where pancreatic pathology manifests primarily as thoracic symptoms. The patient was a boy in late childhood presenting with a one-week history of progressive difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain. Initial demographic and social history revealed a body mass index of 35, indicating obesity, and a dietary pattern high in processed foods. Notably, the patient’s history included contact with pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch’s) in a grandparent, which initially influenced the diagnostic reasoning. However, a critical piece of the clinical puzzle, blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle sustained one month prior, was not elicited until the third hospital admission. This delayed disclosure is characteristic of pediatric cases where trauma history may be concealed or forgotten, leading clinicians to initially focus on pulmonary etiologies rather than abdominal causes.

On physical examination, the clinical picture was dominated by signs consistent with a significant right-sided pleural effusion. The patient exhibited mild tachypnea with a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation of 88%–89% on room air, indicating respiratory compromise. Chest auscultation and percussion revealed reduced chest movements on the right, stony dullness in the subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions, and significantly weaker vocal fremitus compared to the left side. Breath sounds were correspondingly decreased in these areas. These findings are classic for fluid accumulation within the pleural space, which compresses the underlying lung parenchyma and impedes gas exchange. The absence of abdominal tenderness or hepatosplenomegaly further obscured the abdominal origin of the pathology, reinforcing the "pulmonary mask" that often delays the diagnosis of PPF.

Immediate stabilization focused on correcting hypoxia and relieving respiratory distress. Due to the tachypnea and low oxygen saturation, the child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and placed on humidified oxygen via mask at 5 liters per minute. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated while blood cultures were pending. A chest X-ray confirmed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle, and bedside ultrasound estimated the fluid volume at approximately one liter. Therapeutic thoracocentesis was performed, yielding one liter of dark red, blood-stained pleural fluid. Following the aspiration, the patient’s tachypnea resolved, chest pain improved, and oxygen saturation normalized to 95%–98% on room air within 24 hours. This dramatic symptomatic relief allowed for discharge the following day, but the recurrence of symptoms underscored that the underlying fistulous tract remained patent.

The pattern of recurrent admissions was a pivotal feature of this case that ultimately guided the correct diagnosis. The child returned six days later with identical complaints of chest pain and dyspnea. Repeat imaging confirmed the re-accumulation of right-sided pleural effusion, and a second thoracocentesis again yielded one liter of dark red fluid. At this stage, the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) level was slightly elevated, and given the history of tuberculosis contact, the clinical team empirically initiated antitubercular therapy. However, the child was readmitted a third time just five days later with recurrent symptoms. It was only upon a thorough re-evaluation of the history during this third admission that the trauma from the bicycle handle was revealed. This sequence of recurrent, massive, hemorrhagic effusions despite drainage and anti-tubercular treatment should have raised suspicion for a non-infectious, non-pulmonary etiology. The recurrence pattern demonstrates that while thoracocentesis provides temporary symptomatic relief, it does not address the continuous leakage of pancreatic fluid, necessitating a broader diagnostic workup that includes abdominal imaging and pancreatic enzyme analysis.

## Pathophysiology and Etiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a rare and clinically deceptive complication where pancreatic secretions leak into the pleural space, creating a fistulous connection between the pancreas and the thoracic cavity. In the pediatric population, this entity is exceptionally uncommon, with fewer than 50 documented cases compared to over 300 in adults, making it a critical diagnostic consideration for recurrent pleural effusions. The underlying mechanism typically involves a disruption of the pancreatic duct, which acts as the conduit for enzyme-rich fluid. In the case presented, the etiology was blunt abdominal trauma sustained from a bicycle handle one month prior to presentation. This traumatic force can cause shearing injuries to the pancreatic parenchyma or ductal system, leading to a rupture. When the duct is compromised, pancreatic juice, under pressure, seeks the path of least resistance. If the disruption occurs in the posterior aspect of the pancreas, fluid is diverted away from the peritoneal cavity and into the retroperitoneal space, setting the stage for the development of a fistula that tracks superiorly toward the chest.

The anatomical pathway of this fluid leakage is dictated by natural fascial planes and diaphragmatic openings. In the reported case, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) visualized a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum. From there, the fluid tracked superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrower communication to the left. This trajectory is characteristic of PPF, where fluid ascends through the aortic or oesophageal hiatuses rather than eroding directly through the diaphragm. The result is an internal pancreatic fistula that functions like a leaky pipe, continuously diverting exocrine secretions into the thoracic cavity. This leakage is responsible for the massive, recurrent pleural effusions seen in the patient. The biochemical profile of the pleural fluid is diagnostic; in this case, amylase and lipase levels were significantly elevated (6118 U/L and 7211 U/L, respectively), confirming the pancreatic origin of the fluid. The dark red appearance of the aspirate further correlated with the history of trauma, indicating a hemorrhagic component to the effusion.

Understanding the etiology of PPF requires distinguishing between pediatric and adult presentations, as the risk factors differ substantially. In adults, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis is the predominant cause, accounting for nearly all cases in large reviews. Conversely, in children, the etiology is more heterogeneous. While genetic mutations such as those in the SPINK1 and PRSS1 genes are prominent drivers of hereditary and chronic pancreatitis in the pediatric population, blunt abdominal trauma is a significant non-hereditary trigger. In the cohort analyzed in the literature review accompanying this case, blunt injury comprised 32% of pediatric cases. Although this specific patient presented with trauma-induced PPF, the discussion of genetic factors is vital for pediatric residents; if pancreatitis recurs or appears chronic without a clear traumatic history, a thorough family history and genetic evaluation should be considered. The distinction is crucial because while adults often have a long history of alcohol use, a child presenting with pancreatic pathology may have a concealed history of trauma or an underlying genetic predisposition that remains undiagnosed until a complication like PPF arises.

The clinical presentation of PPF is often misleading, a phenomenon described as the "Pulmonary Mask." Because the pathology originates in the abdomen but manifests in the chest, the majority of patients present with thoracic symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, and massive pleural effusions, while abdominal symptoms like epigastric pain are less common or absent. In this case, the child presented with respiratory distress and recurrent right-sided effusions, leading the initial clinical team to suspect pulmonary tuberculosis or malignancy, particularly given the family history of tuberculosis and mildly elevated adenosine deaminase levels. It was only after repeated admissions and a detailed re-evaluation of the history that the abdominal trauma was disclosed. This diagnostic delay is common, as resources are initially directed toward finding thoracic pathology. The concept of the Pulmonary Mask underscores the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for abdominal sources in cases of unexplained, recurrent, or amylase-rich pleural effusions. Recognizing this pattern prevents unnecessary pulmonary interventions, such as prolonged antitubercular therapy, and ensures timely management of the underlying pancreatic injury.

## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Reasoning

The diagnostic journey in this case of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) illustrates the complexity of distinguishing thoracic pathology from abdominal origins when clinical presentations overlap. The patient presented with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions and respiratory distress, initially prompting a workup focused on pulmonary etiologies. The diagnostic process began with basic imaging and pleural fluid analysis, which eventually redirected attention to the abdomen. Chest X-ray initially revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle, while bedside lung ultrasound quantified the fluid at approximately one litre. However, the critical diagnostic pivot occurred during pleural fluid analysis. The aspirated fluid was consistently dark red, and biochemical analysis revealed significantly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) concentrations. In the context of pleural effusions, a pleural fluid amylase level greater than 1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. This finding is crucial because it indicates the leakage of pancreatic enzymes into the pleural space, distinguishing pancreatic pathology from common thoracic causes. The elevation of both amylase and lipase in the pleural fluid, mirroring serum levels, provided the first strong biochemical evidence that the pathology originated from the pancreas rather than the lung parenchyma or pleura.

Following the biochemical clues, advanced imaging was utilized to visualize the anatomical defect and confirm the fistulous tract. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest provided the initial structural insight, showing patchy air space consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated multiloculated hypodense collection. Crucially, the CT demonstrated this collection tracking from the right pleural space, through the right paraesophageal region, to the tail of the pancreas. While CT is reliable for identifying pancreatic calcifications or pseudocysts, it may not always depict the fistula itself. Therefore, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed to confirm the diagnosis. MRCP revealed a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and tracking superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. This imaging modality offered high sensitivity for visualizing the fistula and duct anatomy, confirming the communication between the pancreatic duct and the pleural space, which was essential for finalizing the diagnosis before treatment.

Concurrent with the search for pancreatic pathology, a rigorous differential diagnosis was required to rule out other causes of exudative, amylase-rich pleural effusions. Tuberculosis was a primary consideration due to the patient's history of contact with a grandfather treated for pulmonary Koch’s and a mildly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid. However, the negative GeneXpert for Mycobacterium tuberculosis and the absence of constitutional symptoms like fever or chronic cough allowed clinicians to rule out pulmonary Koch’s and discontinue antitubercular drugs. Malignancy, including lung cancer or metastatic disease, was also considered given the recurrent nature of the effusion and dark red fluid; however, normal blood counts, lack of weight loss, and negative pleural fluid cytology excluded this possibility. Additionally, Boerhaave’s syndrome (oesophageal perforation) can present with amylase-rich fluid but was ruled out based on the patient’s normal bowel function and lack of vomiting history. Hepatic hydrothorax and renal pathology were excluded through normal liver function tests and renal imaging. Understanding these exclusions is vital, as conditions like oesophageal perforation or malignancy can mimic PPF biochemically but require vastly different management strategies.

A nuanced aspect of the diagnostic reasoning involves the interpretation of amylase isoenzymes. While total amylase elevation is highly suggestive, the discussion highlights that pancreatic type amylase in the pleural fluid specifically distinguishes PPF from other conditions that may present with high salivary amylase levels. Although the case report focuses on total amylase and lipase levels to support the diagnosis, the principle remains that identifying the source of the enzyme helps prevent misdiagnosis. This biochemical differentiation is particularly important when ruling out malignancies or oesophageal pathology, which can sometimes yield elevated amylase levels but typically of salivary origin. In this case, the combination of markedly elevated lipase alongside amylase further reinforced the pancreatic origin, as lipase is more specific to pancreatic tissue than amylase alone.

Finally, the case underscores significant diagnostic pitfalls that can lead to delayed management. The most prominent pitfall was the initial focus on thoracic pathology due to the "pulmonary mask" of the disease, where respiratory symptoms dominate despite the abdominal source. This led to a delay in diagnosis as the patient was admitted three times for recurrent effusions before the abdominal etiology was suspected. A key factor in this delay was the history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior, which the child initially concealed due to school bullying. It was only upon repeated history-taking during the third admission that this trauma was revealed, linking the pancreatic injury to the pleural findings. This highlights the necessity of eliciting a detailed history of trauma, even when the presenting symptoms are respiratory, and recognizing that recurrent pleural effusions with high amylase should immediately trigger a search for pancreatic fistula rather than continuing an extensive pulmonary workup.

## Therapeutic Management and Decision Making

The management of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in the pediatric population presents a significant clinical challenge due to the rarity of the condition and the absence of standardized treatment guidelines. In this case, the clinical team adopted a stepwise therapeutic approach, prioritizing conservative medical management to promote spontaneous fistula closure before considering invasive interventions. The primary physiological goal of this strategy is to minimize pancreatic exocrine secretion, thereby reducing the volume of fluid leaking through the fistula tract and allowing the disrupted ductal system to heal. Upon confirming the diagnosis via MRCP and elevated pleural fluid amylase, the patient was immediately placed on nil per oral (NPO) status and initiated on total parenteral nutrition (TPN). This bowel rest is critical as it reduces the stimulation of pancreatic enzyme production, which is essential for decreasing the pressure within the pancreatic duct and facilitating tract closure.

Pharmacological intervention played a central role in suppressing pancreatic activity alongside nutritional support. The patient received a continuous infusion of Octreotide, a somatostatin analogue, at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day, which falls within the therapeutic range of 10–40 µg/kg/day described in pediatric case literature. Octreotide functions by inhibiting the release of gastrointestinal hormones and directly suppressing exocrine pancreatic secretion, effectively acting to reduce the flow of pancreatic fluid through the fistula. Additionally, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) was administered to modulate feedback mechanisms. Concurrently, the medical team made the decision to discontinue empiric antitubercular drugs once the diagnosis was confirmed, preventing unnecessary medication exposure and potential toxicity.

Control of the pleural effusion was managed through a progression of drainage techniques tailored to the patient's clinical course. Initially, bedside lung ultrasound and thoracocentesis were utilized during the first two admissions, providing temporary symptomatic relief by aspirating approximately one liter of dark red fluid. However, due to the recurrent nature of the effusion and the need for continuous monitoring, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted during the third admission. This intervention was vital not only for relieving respiratory distress and preventing lung compression but also for monitoring the therapeutic response. The ICD allowed the team to track the volume of fluid output daily, serving as a direct and quantitative marker of fistula activity.

Decision-making regarding the escalation of care is guided by the response to conservative measures and the anatomical findings of the pancreatic duct. While this patient responded well to medical management, the clinical framework dictates that if conservative therapy fails after two to three weeks, or if there is significant ductal stenosis or obstruction, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with trans-papillary stenting is the preferred next step. Stenting reduces intraductal pressure and diverts flow, with reported success rates exceeding 90% in mixed age cohorts. Surgical interventions, such as distal pancreatectomy or pancreatojejunostomy, are reserved for cases where endoscopic options are not feasible or have failed, typically involving higher morbidity and are considered only when less invasive measures are exhausted.

Monitoring therapeutic response in this case relied heavily on the reduction of fluid drainage from the ICD. Over a period of 10 days, the output gradually decreased and eventually ceased, indicating successful fistula closure. This clinical improvement allowed for the removal of the chest tube and the transition back to oral intake. The patient was discharged symptom-free and maintained stability during monthly follow-ups. This outcome underscores the importance of a dedicated conservative trial in pediatric PPF, provided there are no contraindications such as severe ductal anomalies, and highlights the necessity of close monitoring to determine the appropriate timing for invasive escalation.

## Clinical Outcome and Educational Significance

The clinical course of this patient demonstrated a favorable response to initial conservative management, highlighting the importance of a structured trial of non-invasive therapy in pediatric Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF). Following the confirmation of the diagnosis via MRCP and the insertion of an intercostal drain (ICD), the patient was managed with bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide infusion. Over a period of 10 days, the volume of fluid drainage from the ICD gradually decreased until it ceased completely, allowing for the safe removal of the chest tube and subsequent discharge. This timeline underscores that in the absence of complex ductal anomalies or severe sepsis, conservative measures can successfully promote fistula closure by reducing pancreatic exocrine secretion and allowing the pancreatic duct to heal.

Long-term follow-up confirmed the durability of this recovery, with the patient remaining symptom-free during monthly reviews. Notably, the clinical management extended beyond the acute fistula repair to address the patient's underlying metabolic risk factors, specifically his obesity with a body mass index of 35. The child was advised to modify his lifestyle, adopting a balanced diet and regular exercise, which facilitated his return to daily activities and school. He reported no difficulty with strenuous activities such as running or playing, indicating a full restoration of respiratory and functional capacity. This holistic approach ensures that the resolution of the fistula is maintained by mitigating factors that could exacerbate pancreatic stress.

A critical educational lesson from this case involves the necessity of persistently eliciting a history of trauma in pediatric patients presenting with unexplained thoracic pathology. Initially, the child did not volunteer the history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior, a detail that was only revealed after repeated history-taking sessions. The discussion notes that the child may have concealed this information due to bullying at school, illustrating the psychosocial barriers that can obscure the clinical picture in children. Clinicians must recognize that pediatric patients may withhold information regarding injuries, necessitating a thorough and repeated interrogation of the history, especially when the clinical presentation suggests a thoracic source but abdominal pathology is suspected.

Early diagnosis is paramount to preventing unnecessary pulmonary interventions and potential complications such as sepsis. In this instance, the recurrent pleural effusion and a history of tuberculosis contact initially led to a misdiagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis, resulting in the empirical administration of antitubercular drugs. This therapeutic detour was corrected only after pleural fluid analysis revealed significantly elevated amylase and lipase levels, and imaging confirmed the pancreatic origin. The case illustrates the concept of the "pulmonary mask," where pancreatic disease presents primarily with respiratory symptoms. Delaying the recognition of the abdominal source can lead to prolonged inappropriate treatment, unnecessary medication exposure, and increased risk of complications from recurrent effusions or invasive procedures.

In summary, several clinical pearls emerge for the recognition of PPF in children. First, the presence of recurrent, massive, blood-stained pleural effusion should trigger an investigation into pancreatic pathology, particularly if accompanied by a history of abdominal trauma. Second, pleural fluid amylase levels significantly exceeding 1000 IU/L are pathognomonic for PPF and should prompt immediate consideration of this diagnosis over pulmonary etiologies. Finally, while CT is useful for identifying collections, MRCP offers superior sensitivity for visualizing the fistulous tract and ductal anatomy. By integrating detailed trauma history, specific biochemical analysis of pleural fluid, and appropriate cross-sectional imaging, clinicians can avoid diagnostic delays and manage this rare entity effectively.
