Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# The Trauma Timebomb: Delayed Presentation of Blunt Abdominal Injury

## The Case: A Pulmonary Mask for an Abdominal Problem

Imagine a child presenting to your emergency department with respiratory distress and chest pain. You see a right-sided pleural effusion on the X-ray. You aspirate the fluid, and it is dark red. The child improves, goes home, and then comes back six days later with the exact same problem. You aspirate again. The fluid is still dark red. You think tuberculosis because there is a family history of pulmonary Koch’s, and the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) is slightly elevated. You start antitubercular drugs. But five days later, the child is back in the PICU with the same effusion.

This is the story of a boy in late childhood who had been admitted three times for what looked like a primary pulmonary pathology. The clinical picture was classic for recurrent pleural effusion, yet the root cause was hiding in plain sight. It wasn’t until the third admission, after repeated and persistent history taking, that the child admitted to blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. This case highlights the "pulmonary mask" of a Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF), a rare entity where pancreatic pathology presents as a thoracic emergency.

## The Diagnostic Pivot: Following the Enzymes

When a patient presents with recurrent, massive, blood-stained pleural effusion, the differential diagnosis is broad. In this case, the team had to rule out malignancy, empyema, and tuberculosis. While the family history of TB and the mildly elevated ADA initially pushed the team toward an infectious workup, the persistence of the effusion despite treatment raised red flags. The breakthrough came from the pleural fluid analysis itself.

The pleural fluid was not just bloody; it was enzymatically active. The analysis revealed significantly elevated amylase and lipase levels. Specifically, the pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase was 7211 U/L. A pleural fluid amylase level greater than 1000 IU/L is generally considered pathognomonic for a pancreatic origin. In the context of the delayed trauma history, these numbers pointed directly to a pancreatic duct disruption. To confirm the anatomy, a Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed. It visualized a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, confirming the diagnosis of a pancreatopleural fistula.

## Pathophysiology: How the Pancreas Talks to the Pleura

Understanding the mechanism is crucial for recognizing this diagnosis in the future. PPF is an extremely rare complication in the pediatric population, with only 47 documented cases reported in children compared to over 300 in adults. In adults, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis is the usual culprit, but in children, trauma and genetic mutations play a larger role.

The mechanism involves a disruption of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst rupture. If the rupture occurs in the posterior part of the pancreas, the pancreatic fluid leaks into the retroperitoneal space rather than the peritoneal cavity. Following the path of least resistance, the fluid tracks upward into the mediastinum through natural diaphragmatic hiatuses, most commonly the aortic or esophageal hiatus. From there, it breaks through the pleura to form a pleural effusion. This creates an internal pancreatic fistula that acts like a leaky pipe, diverting pancreatic secretions into the chest. Because the fluid accumulates in the thorax, the patient presents with respiratory symptoms like dyspnea and chest pain, while abdominal symptoms like epigastric pain are often absent or mild. This dissociation between the site of pathology (abdomen) and the site of symptoms (chest) is what delays diagnosis and leads to unnecessary pulmonary interventions.

## Management: Rest and Suppress

Once the diagnosis was confirmed, the antitubercular drugs were stopped, and the focus shifted to pancreatic rest. There are no standardized guidelines for PPF treatment in children due to its rarity, but conservative management is the first line of defense. The goal is to reduce pancreatic exocrine secretion to allow the fistulous tract to close spontaneously.

This patient was placed on nil per oral status and started on total parenteral nutrition (TPN) to ensure bowel rest. An intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted to manage the pleural fluid and prevent respiratory compromise. Crucially, the team initiated an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day. Octreotide is a somatostatin analogue that suppresses exocrine pancreatic function. Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy was also started. The response was favorable; over a period of 10 days, the drainage from the ICD gradually decreased and eventually stopped. The chest tube was removed, and the child was discharged. Follow-up showed the child remained symptom-free with no recurrent effusions. While endoscopic stenting or surgery are options for refractory cases, this patient was successfully managed with conservative medical therapy alone.

## Key Take-Home Pearls

*   **Think Pancreas with High Amylase Effusions:** A pleural fluid amylase level greater than 1000 IU/L is highly suggestive of a pancreatopleural fistula, even in the absence of classic abdominal symptoms.
*   **The Trauma History May Be Hidden:** In pediatric blunt abdominal trauma, the history may be concealed or forgotten. Repeated, gentle questioning is essential, especially when clinical findings do not match the initial history.
*   **Don't Get Fooled by the Pulmonary Mask:** Recurrent, blood-stained pleural effusions should trigger a search for pancreatic pathology, particularly if the patient has a history of abdominal trauma or pancreatitis.
*   **Conservative Management First:** Initial treatment for PPF involves bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, chest drainage, and somatostatin analogues like Octreotide before considering invasive endoscopic or surgical interventions.
*   **Rule Out the Mimics:** While TB, malignancy, and esophageal perforation can cause high amylase effusions, pleural fluid amylase typing (pancreatic vs. salivary) and imaging (MRCP/CT) are key to distinguishing PPF from these conditions.
