Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# The Pulmonary Mask: When Respiratory Distress Hides a Pancreatic Leak

You are seeing a child with progressive respiratory distress, chest pain, and a massive, blood-stained pleural effusion. The family history screams tuberculosis contact. The fluid is dark red. Your mind immediately goes to pulmonary pathology: TB empyema, malignancy, or perhaps a complicated parapneumonic effusion. You drain the fluid, the child improves, and you send them home. But three days later, they are back. And three days after that, they are back again.

This is the classic "Pulmonary Mask." It is a dangerous cognitive trap where thoracic symptoms dominate the clinical picture, leading clinicians to anchor on lung disease while the true pathology sits quietly in the abdomen. In this case, a Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF) mimicked primary lung disease, delaying diagnosis through three separate hospital admissions.

### The Case: A Mystery in Three Acts

Our patient was a boy in late childhood presenting with difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain. On initial examination, he was hypoxic on room air with stony dullness on the right. A chest X-ray confirmed a right-sided pleural effusion. After aspirating one liter of dark red fluid, his oxygen saturation improved, and he was discharged.

However, the recurrence was the first clue that something was wrong. He returned six days later with identical symptoms. Given the family history of pulmonary Koch’s (tuberculosis) and a mildly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level in the fluid, the clinical team empirically started antitubercular drugs. He was discharged again, only to return five days later for a third time.

It was during this third admission that the history finally cracked open. Upon repeated questioning, the child revealed blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior—a detail initially concealed, likely due to school bullying. A contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was finally ordered before inserting an intercostal drain. The imaging revealed an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region all the way to the tail of the pancreas.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) confirmed the diagnosis: bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, right greater than left. The pleural fluid analysis was the smoking gun, revealing significantly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L). The antitubercular drugs were stopped, and the child was managed conservatively with nil per oral status, total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide. Over ten days, drainage ceased, the chest tube was removed, and the child was discharged.

### Why We Miss It: The Pulmonary Mask

Pancreatopleural fistula is an extremely rare entity, particularly in the pediatric population, with only 47 cases reported in children compared to over 300 in adults. The rarity, combined with the presentation, makes it a diagnostic nightmare. Approximately 68% of PPF cases present with predominant respiratory symptoms like massive, recurrent pleural effusions and dyspnea. Less than 20% present with abdominal symptoms like epigastric pain.

Because the symptoms are thoracic, resources are often consumed finding a pulmonary cause. In this case, the dark red fluid and family history of TB created a strong anchor for tuberculosis. However, PPF fluid is often hemorrhagic or serosanguinous, and the amylase levels are disproportionately high. The pathophysiology involves a rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst. If the rupture occurs posteriorly, pancreatic fluid leaks into the retroperitoneum, follows the path of least resistance upward through the diaphragmatic hiatus (usually aortic or esophageal), and breaks through the pleura.

In adults, alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis is the dominant cause. In children, however, the etiology shifts. Genetic mutations (PRSS1, SPINK1) and blunt abdominal trauma are significant triggers. In this specific case, the trauma occurred a month prior, allowing time for the fistula to form and the fluid to track upward. The delay in diagnosis highlights the importance of digging deep into trauma history, even when the presenting complaint is respiratory.

### Diagnosis and Management

The diagnosis of PPF relies heavily on recognizing the biochemical signature of the pleural fluid. While tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and malignancy can cause elevated amylase, levels in PPF are typically massive. A pleural fluid amylase greater than 1000 IU/L is pathognomonic. Furthermore, distinguishing pancreatic-type amylase from salivary-type amylase can help rule out other causes.

Imaging plays a crucial role. While CT reliably shows pancreatic calcifications or pseudocysts, it often misses the actual fistula tract. MRCP offers 70–80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula and duct anatomy. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning if conservative measures fail.

Treatment is generally stepped. Since there are no standardized guidelines for pediatric PPF, conservative management is usually the first line. This involves bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogues like Octreotide to suppress exocrine function, and thoracic drainage. In this case, the patient responded well to medical management alone within 10 days. If conservative measures fail after 2–3 weeks, endoscopic interventions like ERCP with stenting are considered. Surgery is reserved for refractory cases.

### Clinical Pearls

*   **Suspect PPF in recurrent effusions:** If a patient presents with recurrent, massive, or hemorrhagic pleural effusions that do not respond to standard pulmonary management, look beyond the chest.
*   **Check the amylase:** Pleural fluid amylase levels ≥1000 IU/L are highly suggestive of a pancreatic origin. Do not stop at the diagnosis of TB or malignancy without ruling out PPF.
*   **Dig for trauma history:** In pediatric patients, blunt abdominal trauma can be concealed. A detailed, repeated history is essential, especially if respiratory symptoms follow an unexplained delay.
*   **Use the right imaging:** CT is good for anatomy, but MRCP is superior for visualizing the pancreatic duct and fistula tract.
*   **Treat conservatively first:** Most pediatric PPF cases can be managed initially with NPO, TPN, Octreotide, and drainage before resorting to endoscopic or surgical intervention.
