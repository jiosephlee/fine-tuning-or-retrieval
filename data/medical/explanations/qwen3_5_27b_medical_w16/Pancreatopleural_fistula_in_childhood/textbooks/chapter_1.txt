Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Stabilization

The clinical presentation of this case highlights the diagnostic challenge posed by pancreatopleural fistula (PPF), a condition where pancreatic pathology manifests primarily as thoracic symptoms. The patient was a boy in late childhood presenting with a one-week history of progressive difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain. Initial demographic and social history revealed a body mass index of 35, indicating obesity, and a dietary pattern high in processed foods. Notably, the patient’s history included contact with pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch’s) in a grandparent, which initially influenced the diagnostic reasoning. However, a critical piece of the clinical puzzle, blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle sustained one month prior, was not elicited until the third hospital admission. This delayed disclosure is characteristic of pediatric cases where trauma history may be concealed or forgotten, leading clinicians to initially focus on pulmonary etiologies rather than abdominal causes.

On physical examination, the clinical picture was dominated by signs consistent with a significant right-sided pleural effusion. The patient exhibited mild tachypnea with a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation of 88%–89% on room air, indicating respiratory compromise. Chest auscultation and percussion revealed reduced chest movements on the right, stony dullness in the subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions, and significantly weaker vocal fremitus compared to the left side. Breath sounds were correspondingly decreased in these areas. These findings are classic for fluid accumulation within the pleural space, which compresses the underlying lung parenchyma and impedes gas exchange. The absence of abdominal tenderness or hepatosplenomegaly further obscured the abdominal origin of the pathology, reinforcing the "pulmonary mask" that often delays the diagnosis of PPF.

Immediate stabilization focused on correcting hypoxia and relieving respiratory distress. Due to the tachypnea and low oxygen saturation, the child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and placed on humidified oxygen via mask at 5 liters per minute. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated while blood cultures were pending. A chest X-ray confirmed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle, and bedside ultrasound estimated the fluid volume at approximately one liter. Therapeutic thoracocentesis was performed, yielding one liter of dark red, blood-stained pleural fluid. Following the aspiration, the patient’s tachypnea resolved, chest pain improved, and oxygen saturation normalized to 95%–98% on room air within 24 hours. This dramatic symptomatic relief allowed for discharge the following day, but the recurrence of symptoms underscored that the underlying fistulous tract remained patent.

The pattern of recurrent admissions was a pivotal feature of this case that ultimately guided the correct diagnosis. The child returned six days later with identical complaints of chest pain and dyspnea. Repeat imaging confirmed the re-accumulation of right-sided pleural effusion, and a second thoracocentesis again yielded one liter of dark red fluid. At this stage, the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) level was slightly elevated, and given the history of tuberculosis contact, the clinical team empirically initiated antitubercular therapy. However, the child was readmitted a third time just five days later with recurrent symptoms. It was only upon a thorough re-evaluation of the history during this third admission that the trauma from the bicycle handle was revealed. This sequence of recurrent, massive, hemorrhagic effusions despite drainage and anti-tubercular treatment should have raised suspicion for a non-infectious, non-pulmonary etiology. The recurrence pattern demonstrates that while thoracocentesis provides temporary symptomatic relief, it does not address the continuous leakage of pancreatic fluid, necessitating a broader diagnostic workup that includes abdominal imaging and pancreatic enzyme analysis.
