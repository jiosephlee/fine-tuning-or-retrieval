Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology and Etiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a rare and clinically deceptive complication where pancreatic secretions leak into the pleural space, creating a fistulous connection between the pancreas and the thoracic cavity. In the pediatric population, this entity is exceptionally uncommon, with fewer than 50 documented cases compared to over 300 in adults, making it a critical diagnostic consideration for recurrent pleural effusions. The underlying mechanism typically involves a disruption of the pancreatic duct, which acts as the conduit for enzyme-rich fluid. In the case presented, the etiology was blunt abdominal trauma sustained from a bicycle handle one month prior to presentation. This traumatic force can cause shearing injuries to the pancreatic parenchyma or ductal system, leading to a rupture. When the duct is compromised, pancreatic juice, under pressure, seeks the path of least resistance. If the disruption occurs in the posterior aspect of the pancreas, fluid is diverted away from the peritoneal cavity and into the retroperitoneal space, setting the stage for the development of a fistula that tracks superiorly toward the chest.

The anatomical pathway of this fluid leakage is dictated by natural fascial planes and diaphragmatic openings. In the reported case, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) visualized a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum. From there, the fluid tracked superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrower communication to the left. This trajectory is characteristic of PPF, where fluid ascends through the aortic or oesophageal hiatuses rather than eroding directly through the diaphragm. The result is an internal pancreatic fistula that functions like a leaky pipe, continuously diverting exocrine secretions into the thoracic cavity. This leakage is responsible for the massive, recurrent pleural effusions seen in the patient. The biochemical profile of the pleural fluid is diagnostic; in this case, amylase and lipase levels were significantly elevated (6118 U/L and 7211 U/L, respectively), confirming the pancreatic origin of the fluid. The dark red appearance of the aspirate further correlated with the history of trauma, indicating a hemorrhagic component to the effusion.

Understanding the etiology of PPF requires distinguishing between pediatric and adult presentations, as the risk factors differ substantially. In adults, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis is the predominant cause, accounting for nearly all cases in large reviews. Conversely, in children, the etiology is more heterogeneous. While genetic mutations such as those in the SPINK1 and PRSS1 genes are prominent drivers of hereditary and chronic pancreatitis in the pediatric population, blunt abdominal trauma is a significant non-hereditary trigger. In the cohort analyzed in the literature review accompanying this case, blunt injury comprised 32% of pediatric cases. Although this specific patient presented with trauma-induced PPF, the discussion of genetic factors is vital for pediatric residents; if pancreatitis recurs or appears chronic without a clear traumatic history, a thorough family history and genetic evaluation should be considered. The distinction is crucial because while adults often have a long history of alcohol use, a child presenting with pancreatic pathology may have a concealed history of trauma or an underlying genetic predisposition that remains undiagnosed until a complication like PPF arises.

The clinical presentation of PPF is often misleading, a phenomenon described as the "Pulmonary Mask." Because the pathology originates in the abdomen but manifests in the chest, the majority of patients present with thoracic symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, and massive pleural effusions, while abdominal symptoms like epigastric pain are less common or absent. In this case, the child presented with respiratory distress and recurrent right-sided effusions, leading the initial clinical team to suspect pulmonary tuberculosis or malignancy, particularly given the family history of tuberculosis and mildly elevated adenosine deaminase levels. It was only after repeated admissions and a detailed re-evaluation of the history that the abdominal trauma was disclosed. This diagnostic delay is common, as resources are initially directed toward finding thoracic pathology. The concept of the Pulmonary Mask underscores the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for abdominal sources in cases of unexplained, recurrent, or amylase-rich pleural effusions. Recognizing this pattern prevents unnecessary pulmonary interventions, such as prolonged antitubercular therapy, and ensures timely management of the underlying pancreatic injury.
