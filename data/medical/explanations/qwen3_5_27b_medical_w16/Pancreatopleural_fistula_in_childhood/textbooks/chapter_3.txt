Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Reasoning

The diagnostic journey in this case of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) illustrates the complexity of distinguishing thoracic pathology from abdominal origins when clinical presentations overlap. The patient presented with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions and respiratory distress, initially prompting a workup focused on pulmonary etiologies. The diagnostic process began with basic imaging and pleural fluid analysis, which eventually redirected attention to the abdomen. Chest X-ray initially revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle, while bedside lung ultrasound quantified the fluid at approximately one litre. However, the critical diagnostic pivot occurred during pleural fluid analysis. The aspirated fluid was consistently dark red, and biochemical analysis revealed significantly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) concentrations. In the context of pleural effusions, a pleural fluid amylase level greater than 1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. This finding is crucial because it indicates the leakage of pancreatic enzymes into the pleural space, distinguishing pancreatic pathology from common thoracic causes. The elevation of both amylase and lipase in the pleural fluid, mirroring serum levels, provided the first strong biochemical evidence that the pathology originated from the pancreas rather than the lung parenchyma or pleura.

Following the biochemical clues, advanced imaging was utilized to visualize the anatomical defect and confirm the fistulous tract. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest provided the initial structural insight, showing patchy air space consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated multiloculated hypodense collection. Crucially, the CT demonstrated this collection tracking from the right pleural space, through the right paraesophageal region, to the tail of the pancreas. While CT is reliable for identifying pancreatic calcifications or pseudocysts, it may not always depict the fistula itself. Therefore, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed to confirm the diagnosis. MRCP revealed a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and tracking superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. This imaging modality offered high sensitivity for visualizing the fistula and duct anatomy, confirming the communication between the pancreatic duct and the pleural space, which was essential for finalizing the diagnosis before treatment.

Concurrent with the search for pancreatic pathology, a rigorous differential diagnosis was required to rule out other causes of exudative, amylase-rich pleural effusions. Tuberculosis was a primary consideration due to the patient's history of contact with a grandfather treated for pulmonary Koch’s and a mildly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid. However, the negative GeneXpert for Mycobacterium tuberculosis and the absence of constitutional symptoms like fever or chronic cough allowed clinicians to rule out pulmonary Koch’s and discontinue antitubercular drugs. Malignancy, including lung cancer or metastatic disease, was also considered given the recurrent nature of the effusion and dark red fluid; however, normal blood counts, lack of weight loss, and negative pleural fluid cytology excluded this possibility. Additionally, Boerhaave’s syndrome (oesophageal perforation) can present with amylase-rich fluid but was ruled out based on the patient’s normal bowel function and lack of vomiting history. Hepatic hydrothorax and renal pathology were excluded through normal liver function tests and renal imaging. Understanding these exclusions is vital, as conditions like oesophageal perforation or malignancy can mimic PPF biochemically but require vastly different management strategies.

A nuanced aspect of the diagnostic reasoning involves the interpretation of amylase isoenzymes. While total amylase elevation is highly suggestive, the discussion highlights that pancreatic type amylase in the pleural fluid specifically distinguishes PPF from other conditions that may present with high salivary amylase levels. Although the case report focuses on total amylase and lipase levels to support the diagnosis, the principle remains that identifying the source of the enzyme helps prevent misdiagnosis. This biochemical differentiation is particularly important when ruling out malignancies or oesophageal pathology, which can sometimes yield elevated amylase levels but typically of salivary origin. In this case, the combination of markedly elevated lipase alongside amylase further reinforced the pancreatic origin, as lipase is more specific to pancreatic tissue than amylase alone.

Finally, the case underscores significant diagnostic pitfalls that can lead to delayed management. The most prominent pitfall was the initial focus on thoracic pathology due to the "pulmonary mask" of the disease, where respiratory symptoms dominate despite the abdominal source. This led to a delay in diagnosis as the patient was admitted three times for recurrent effusions before the abdominal etiology was suspected. A key factor in this delay was the history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior, which the child initially concealed due to school bullying. It was only upon repeated history-taking during the third admission that this trauma was revealed, linking the pancreatic injury to the pleural findings. This highlights the necessity of eliciting a detailed history of trauma, even when the presenting symptoms are respiratory, and recognizing that recurrent pleural effusions with high amylase should immediately trigger a search for pancreatic fistula rather than continuing an extensive pulmonary workup.
