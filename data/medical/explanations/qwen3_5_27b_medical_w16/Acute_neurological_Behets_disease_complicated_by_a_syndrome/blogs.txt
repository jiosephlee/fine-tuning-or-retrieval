Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Normal MRI Trap: Diagnosing Neuro-Behçet's Without Parenchymal Lesions

We’ve all been there. A patient presents with fever, headache, and altered mental status. You rush them to CT, then MRI, and the radiologist comes back with a clean report. "Normal study," they say. But the patient is still sick. In the world of neuroinflammatory disorders, a normal MRI can be a dangerous false negative, especially in the early phases of disease. This case report challenges the dogma that parenchymal lesions are required for a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) and highlights a subtle, often overlooked complication: SIADH.

## The Case

A man in his 60s presented to the emergency department with a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, and persistent hiccups. He also reported arthralgia and oral aphthae. On examination, he was alert but fatigued with a fever of 38.1°C and tachycardia. Physical exam revealed oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on his face and trunk.

The initial labs were striking. He had marked hyponatraemia at 124 mmol/L, elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 180 mg/L), and elevated creatine kinase. Endocrine workup confirmed the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) with low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, and elevated ADH levels.

The neurological picture was puzzling. His headache and vertigo worsened on day three, raising suspicion for CNS inflammation. However, the brain MRI revealed no significant abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or other brain regions. There were no parenchymal lesions typically associated with Behçet’s disease. Despite the clean scan, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed aseptic meningitis with mildly elevated opening pressure, increased protein, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis. Infectious and malignant workups were negative.

On day five, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared. Combined with the oral aphthae and rash, this met the criteria for definite Behçet’s disease. The diagnosis became acute neuro-Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma

Neuro-Behçet’s disease typically develops years after the initial diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease, occurring in about 10% to 20% of patients. When it does present, we are trained to look for brainstem or parenchymal lesions on MRI. This case is rare because the systemic disease and neurological involvement were diagnosed concurrently.

The normal MRI created a significant diagnostic trap. Clinicians often rely on neuroimaging to confirm CNS involvement, but in acute neuro-BD, the disease may present as meningoencephalitis without parenchymal lesions, particularly in the early phase. A normal brain MRI does not exclude the diagnosis. In this patient, the absence of radiological findings initially complicated the process, forcing the team to rely heavily on clinical criteria and CSF analysis.

Furthermore, the hyponatraemia was not just a lab value; it was a clinical clue. The patient’s persistent hiccups are a known, though often underappreciated, symptom of severe hyponatraemia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. Additionally, the elevated creatine kinase suggested rhabdomyolysis, another complication of severe hyponatraemia that can be overlooked if electrolyte disturbances aren't suspected early.

## The Role of CSF

When the MRI is normal but clinical suspicion for CNS inflammation remains high, the CSF becomes the primary diagnostic tool. In this case, the CSF profile was the pivot point. It revealed aseptic meningitis characterized by increased protein and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis.

The team had to rigorously exclude other causes. Viral meningoencephalitis (HSV, VZV) was ruled out via negative PCR. Bacterial and tuberculous meningitis were excluded by negative cultures and staining. Primary CNS malignancy was unlikely given the normal MRI and negative cytology. Autoimmune conditions like neurosarcoidosis were considered but deemed unlikely based on normal ACE levels and lack of systemic features.

Once infectious and neoplastic causes were excluded, the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD was established based on the combination of CNS symptoms (headache, vertigo, aseptic meningitis profile) and the newly confirmed mucocutaneous findings of Behçet’s disease. This underscores that in the context of Behçet’s, neurological symptoms should prompt a lumbar puncture even if neuroimaging is unrevealing.

## Treatment and Outcome

Treatment for acute neuro-BD usually involves high-dose corticosteroids and immunosuppressants. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy. While systematic reviews often suggest colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-BD compared to steroids, this case demonstrated a rapid response.

The patient’s fever resolved the day after starting colchicine. Over the following two weeks, vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions improved. Crucially, the hyponatraemia resolved without the need for hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists; fluid restriction and treatment of the underlying inflammation were sufficient. By day 16, serum sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L. The patient was discharged after one month and remained stable on colchicine for four years without recurrence.

This outcome suggests that in certain presentations of acute neuro-BD, particularly those driven by meningoencephalitis without severe parenchymal destruction, colchicine may be a viable therapeutic option, especially when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable. It also highlights that treating the underlying inflammation can resolve the secondary SIADH.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Normal MRI does not rule out Neuro-Behçet’s:** Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, particularly in the early phase. Do not rely solely on neuroimaging.
*   **Trust the CSF:** In patients with Behçet’s disease presenting with headache or neurological symptoms, CSF analysis is critical. Look for lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein to support CNS inflammation.
*   **Watch for SIADH:** Hyponatraemia and symptoms like persistent hiccups may signal SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation in acute neuro-BD.
*   **Consider Colchicine:** While steroids are standard, colchicine monotherapy may be effective for acute neuro-BD with meningoencephalitis, potentially resolving both neurological and endocrine complications.
*   **Concurrent Diagnosis is Rare:** While neuro-BD usually follows systemic disease by years, concurrent presentation can occur. Maintain a high index of suspicion when mucocutaneous lesions and neurological symptoms appear together.



# Hiccups, Rhabdo, and Hyponatremia: Recognizing the Silent Killer in CNS Inflammation

Fever, headache, and persistent hiccups. On the surface, this triad might sound like a viral illness or a case of indigestion. But in a 60-year-old man presenting to the emergency department, these symptoms were the tip of an iceberg involving acute neurological Behçet’s disease (Neuro-BD) complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This case reminds us that seemingly benign symptoms like hiccups and subtle lab abnormalities like elevated creatine kinase can be vital clues to severe electrolyte disturbances driven by central nervous system inflammation.

## The Diagnostic Puzzle

The patient presented with a three-day history of fever, malaise, arthralgia, and those persistent hiccups. Physical exam revealed oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes, but the neurological exam was initially non-focal. The real red flag emerged from the labs: marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) and elevated inflammatory markers. When we look at hyponatremia in the context of CNS symptoms, SIADH is high on the differential. The workup confirmed this with low serum osmolality, elevated urine osmolality, and high urinary sodium.

What made this case particularly tricky was the neuroimaging. Brain MRI revealed no significant abnormalities, including in the hypothalamus. In the world of Neuro-BD, parenchymal lesions are common, but this patient presented with aseptic meningitis without visible brain lesions on MRI. This is a critical teaching point: a normal brain MRI in the early phase of acute Neuro-BD does not exclude the diagnosis. The cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, supporting CNS inflammation despite the clean scan.

## The Metabolic Clues

While the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease was eventually confirmed by the appearance of a genital ulcer on day five and a positive HLA-B51 test, the immediate clinical challenge was managing the metabolic derangement. The case highlights two often-overlooked associations with severe hyponatremia in the setting of CNS inflammation.

First, the patient’s persistent hiccups. While often dismissed as a nuisance, intractable hiccups can be a specific clinical clue to severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. Second, the patient had an elevated creatine kinase level of 1349 U/L. This indicated rhabdomyolysis, a recognized but frequently missed complication of severe hyponatremia. Clinicians often focus on the neurological symptoms of low sodium, such as confusion or seizures, but myogenic enzyme elevation can serve as a metabolic marker for the severity of the electrolyte disturbance. Recognizing these signs early can prompt faster correction and monitoring.

## Treatment and Outcome

Standard treatment for acute Neuro-BD typically involves high-dose corticosteroids and immunosuppressants. However, this patient was managed with colchicine monotherapy. This is unusual, as systematic reviews often suggest colchicine is ineffective for acute Neuro-BD compared to steroids. Yet, in this specific instance, colchicine led to the resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and the hyponatremia. The SIADH was managed conservatively with fluid restriction, and sodium normalized within two weeks without the need for hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

This outcome suggests a possible direct association between the inflammatory drive of Neuro-BD and the SIADH, potentially mediated by cytokines like IL-6. While this does not change the standard of care for all Neuro-BD patients, it underscores that in early or mild presentations, or where steroids are contraindicated, colchicine may have a role. The patient was discharged after one month with no recurrence over a four-year follow-up.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Hiccups as a Red Flag:** Intractable hiccups in a patient with fever and headache should raise suspicion for severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS inflammation.
*   **Rhabdomyolysis and Sodium:** Elevated creatine kinase can be a complication of severe hyponatremia; do not overlook myogenic enzyme levels in electrolyte workups.
*   **Normal MRI Doesn't Rule Out Neuro-BD:** Acute neurological Behçet’s disease can present as aseptic meningitis with a normal brain MRI, particularly in the early phase.
*   **SIADH in Neuro-BD:** Consider SIADH as a potential manifestation of acute Neuro-BD, especially when hyponatremia accompanies non-specific CNS symptoms.
*   **Treatment Variability:** While steroids are the mainstay for Neuro-BD, colchicine monotherapy may be effective in specific cases, particularly when concurrent mucocutaneous symptoms are prominent.



# Colchicine for the Brain? Challenging Steroid Dogma in Neuro-Behçet's

## The Dogma vs. The Reality

When you see a patient with acute neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease, the reflexive mental script is usually high-dose corticosteroids and heavy immunosuppression. Neuro-Behçet’s is a serious complication, often involving the brainstem and parenchyma, and the standard of care has long prioritized aggressive immunomodulation to prevent permanent disability. However, a recent case report challenges this narrative, presenting a scenario where colchicine monotherapy resolved not only the neurological and mucocutaneous symptoms but also a concurrent endocrine complication. This case forces us to reconsider whether aggressive steroid therapy is always the mandatory first line for every presentation of acute neuro-Behçet’s, particularly when the clinical picture is dominated by meningeal inflammation rather than parenchymal destruction.

## A Complex Presentation: Hiccups, Hyponatremia, and Ulcers

The patient was a man in his 60s who presented with a constellation of symptoms that initially pointed toward infection. He had a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, arthralgia, and persistent hiccups. On examination, he was febrile and tachycardic, with oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on his face and trunk. The laboratory workup revealed a striking hyponatremia of 124 mmol/L. Further endocrine evaluation confirmed the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), characterized by low serum osmolality, elevated urine osmolality, and increased urinary sodium.

The diagnostic journey became more complex when neurological symptoms worsened on day three, with increased headache and vertigo. A brain MRI was performed and showed no abnormalities, including in the hypothalamic region. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed aseptic meningitis with mildly elevated protein and lymphocytic pleocytosis, but cultures and viral panels were negative. It was not until day five, when a painful scrotal ulcer appeared, that the systemic picture became clear. Combined with the oral aphthae and skin findings, the patient met the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease. The concurrent neurological symptoms and CSF findings supported a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease.

## Diagnostic Pitfalls and the SIADH Connection

This case highlights two significant diagnostic challenges. First, the simultaneous diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s and acute neuro-Behçet’s is rare. Typically, neurological involvement occurs years after the initial diagnosis of the systemic disease. In this instance, the neurological symptoms appeared at the same time as the mucocutaneous features, making the diagnosis difficult to secure early on. Second, the brain MRI was normal. Clinicians often rely on radiological evidence of parenchymal lesions to diagnose neuro-Behçet’s, but this case demonstrates that acute neuro-Behçet’s can present as aseptic meningitis without visible MRI abnormalities, particularly in the early phase.

Furthermore, the presence of SIADH adds a layer of physiological complexity. While SIADH is a known complication of CNS inflammation, its association with acute neuro-Behçet’s in the absence of pharmacological triggers like cyclophosphamide is poorly understood. The authors suggest a potential mechanism involving interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine associated with increased ADH release, which is often elevated in neuroinflammatory disorders. The patient also exhibited rhabdomyolysis with an elevated creatine kinase, a recognized but often overlooked complication of severe hyponatremia. The resolution of the hyponatremia alongside the neurological symptoms suggests a direct link between the inflammatory drive of Behçet’s and the endocrine dysregulation.

## The Treatment Twist: Colchicine Monotherapy

The most provocative aspect of this report is the treatment strategy. On day eight, following the diagnosis of systemic and neurological Behçet’s, the patient was started on colchicine. Crucially, no systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were administered. The SIADH was managed conservatively with fluid restriction. The clinical response was rapid and robust. The fever resolved within a day of starting colchicine, and over the following two weeks, the vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually improved. By day 16, serum sodium had normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels dropped significantly.

This outcome is notable because systematic reviews generally indicate that colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-Behçet’s, with immunosuppressive agents typically required for disease control. Colchicine is usually reserved for mucocutaneous manifestations or used as an adjunct to steroids. However, this patient remained stable on colchicine monotherapy for four years with no recurrence of Behçet’s or neurological symptoms. While spontaneous resolution cannot be entirely ruled out, the temporal relationship suggests that colchicine may be a viable therapeutic option for the early phase of neuro-Behçet’s, particularly in cases presenting with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms without parenchymal lesions, or when corticosteroid therapy is contraindicated.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Consider Neuro-Behçet’s Early:** In patients with known or suspected Behçet’s disease presenting with fever, headache, and non-specific neurological symptoms, acute neuro-Behçet’s should be on the differential even if the MRI is normal.
*   **Watch for SIADH:** Hyponatremia and SIADH can occur as a complication of acute neuro-Behçet’s due to CNS inflammation; persistent hiccups may be a clinical clue to severe hyponatremia in this context.
*   **Re-evaluate Steroid Necessity:** While corticosteroids remain the mainstay for severe neuro-Behçet’s, colchicine monotherapy may be effective in cases presenting primarily with meningeal inflammation and mucocutaneous involvement, potentially sparing patients from steroid-related side effects.
*   **Don’t Ignore the Electrolytes:** Severe hyponatremia in neuroinflammatory conditions can lead to complications like rhabdomyolysis; monitor muscle enzymes and sodium levels carefully during the acute phase.



# The Concurrent Conundrum: Systemic and Neuro-Behçet's Diagnosed Simultaneously

Neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease is a high-stakes diagnosis. We typically teach that neurological manifestations—neuro-Behçet’s—occur years after the initial systemic diagnosis, often affecting the brainstem with parenchymal lesions. But what happens when the nervous system and the mucocutaneous system wake up at the same time?

A recent case report highlights a rare scenario where a patient presented with acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) concurrently with systemic Behçet’s disease, complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This case challenges the classic timeline we rely on in the emergency department and underscores a critical metabolic complication often overlooked in neuroinflammatory conditions.

## The Clinical Presentation

The patient was a man in his 60s who arrived at the ED with a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, arthralgia, and persistent hiccups. One of the biggest pitfalls in this case was the initial reliance on neuroimaging to rule out CNS pathology. In typical neuro-BD, we look for parenchymal lesions, often in the brainstem. However, acute neuro-BD can present as meningoencephalitis without visible lesions on MRI, especially in the early phase.

In this patient, the normal brain MRI initially made the diagnosis of neuro-BD difficult. It is a crucial reminder that a normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD, particularly when clinical features like aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis are present alongside mucocutaneous signs. The diagnosis of definite Behçet’s was eventually secured using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, scoring points for oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, and skin lesions.

The presence of SIADH added a layer of complexity to this case. While SIADH is a known complication of CNS inflammation, its direct association with acute neuro-BD in the absence of pharmacological triggers is not well-documented. The inflammatory cytokines associated with neuro-BD, such as IL-6, may contribute to dysregulated ADH secretion.

Two specific clinical clues in this case pointed toward the severity of the hyponatremia. First, the patient had persistent hiccups, which have been reported as a clinical clue to severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS inflammation. The SIADH resolved as the underlying inflammation was controlled, with sodium normalizing by day 16.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Concurrent Diagnosis is Possible:** Do not assume neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease only occurs years after the initial diagnosis; systemic and neuro-BD can present simultaneously.
*   **Normal MRI Does Not Rule Out Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-BD may present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions on brain MRI.
*   **Watch for SIADH:** Consider SIADH as a potential complication of acute neuro-BD in patients presenting with non-specific CNS symptoms and hyponatremia.
*   **Recognize Hyponatremia Clues:** Persistent hiccups and unexplained rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK) can be important indicators of severe hyponatremia in the context of CNS inflammation.
*   **Treatment Flexibility:** While steroids are the mainstay, colchicine monotherapy may be effective for certain presentations of acute neuro-BD, especially when sparing steroids is a priority.



# The Day 5 Ulcer: Why Serial Physical Exams Matter in Cryptic Autoimmune Disease

We’ve all been there. A patient walks in with fever, headache, and a confusing electrolyte abnormality. The workup for infection is negative, the MRI is clean, and the differential diagnosis stretches from occult malignancy to vasculitis. It is the classic "fever of unknown origin" scenario that keeps us up at night. In a recent case report from Japan, a 60-year-old man presented with exactly this picture: fever, headache, persistent hiccups, and severe hyponatraemia. The twist? The definitive diagnosis wasn’t found in the lab or the imaging suite. It was found on a repeat physical examination five days later, when a genital ulcer finally appeared.

This case of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) highlights a critical lesson in emergency and hospital medicine: the physical exam is not a one-time event. In patients with non-specific febrile illness and potential autoimmune disease, serial examinations can reveal the mucocutaneous findings that confirm a diagnosis initially missed.

The patient arrived with a constellation of symptoms that initially pointed toward a central nervous system infection. He had oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and a temperature of 38.1°C. Laboratory investigations revealed marked hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) and elevated inflammatory markers. The workup for SIADH was positive, showing low serum osmolality and high urine osmolality, consistent with impaired water excretion. Brain MRI was unremarkable, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed aseptic meningitis with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis but no pathogens. At this stage, the diagnosis was a moving target. Viral meningoencephalitis was suspected but ruled out. Autoimmune serologies were largely negative. The clinical picture was frustratingly incomplete.

The diagnostic breakthrough occurred on day 5 of admission. A painful scrotal ulcer developed. This single finding transformed the clinical picture. Combined with the oral aphthae and skin lesions, the patient met the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) with a score of five points. The genital aphthosis alone contributed two points, which is a highly specific finding for Behçet’s. With the systemic diagnosis secured, the neurological symptoms were reclassified as acute neuro-BD. The concurrent SIADH was likely a complication of the CNS inflammation, a rare but documented association where neuroinflammatory cytokines dysregulate antidiuretic hormone secretion.

What makes this case particularly instructive is the timing and the treatment response. Typically, neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease occurs years after the initial diagnosis, and acute neuro-BD is usually treated with high-dose corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. In this instance, the patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy. Remarkably, his fever, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia all resolved without the need for steroids. This suggests that in select cases of acute neuro-BD, particularly those presenting with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, colchicine may be sufficient, though this remains an outlier compared to standard practice.

Beyond the treatment nuance, the diagnostic journey underscores the limitations of relying on a single admission exam. On day 1, the patient lacked the genital ulcers required to definitively score the ICBD criteria. If the team had discharged him or stopped looking for a unifying diagnosis after the negative infectious workup, he might have been labeled with a vague "viral syndrome" or "psychogenic headache." The persistence of symptoms and the evolution of the rash forced a re-evaluation. Additionally, the case reminds us to look for subtle signs of severe hyponatraemia. This patient presented with persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase (rhabdomyolysis), both of which are underappreciated complications of severe SIADH that can occur in the setting of CNS inflammation.

Ultimately, this report serves as a reminder to maintain a high index of suspicion for systemic autoimmune disorders in patients with cryptic CNS symptoms and electrolyte disturbances. When the initial workup is negative, the answer may not be in a new test, but in a new look. Serial physical examinations allow clinicians to capture the evolution of mucocutaneous lesions that define diseases like Behçet’s. By documenting the timeline of lesion appearance, we can connect the dots between seemingly unrelated symptoms like headache, hyponatraemia, and skin ulcers, ensuring patients receive the specific therapy they need.

*   **Serial physical examinations are critical in cryptic febrile illnesses, as diagnostic mucocutaneous lesions (like genital ulcers in Behçet’s) may appear days after admission.**
*   **Acute neuro-Behçet’s disease can present concurrently with systemic disease, often mimicking viral meningoencephalitis with a normal brain MRI.**

# Fever, Headache, and Hyponatremia: Navigating the Diagnostic Maze in the ED

The triad of fever, headache, and hyponatremia is a classic alarm bell in the emergency department. Your immediate differential likely swings hard toward infectious etiologies—meningitis, encephalitis, or sepsis. You order the lumbar puncture, the MRI, and the broad-spectrum antibiotics. But what happens when the cultures come back negative, the MRI is clean, and the patient still looks sick?

A recent case report highlights a rare but critical mimic that often gets lost in the noise of the infectious workup: acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This case challenges the assumption that neurological involvement in Behçet’s always presents years after diagnosis and reminds us that a normal brain MRI does not rule out CNS inflammation.

## The Case: A Diagnostic Puzzle

An Asian man in his 60s presented to the ED with a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, and persistent hiccups. He also complained of arthralgia and oral aphthae. On examination, he was febrile (38.1°C) and tachycardic. Physical inspection revealed oral ulcers and folliculitis-like rashes on his face and trunk.

The labs were the first major red flag. He had marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), a significantly elevated CRP (180 mg/L), and elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L). Initial workup for common infections (HSV, VZV, bacterial meningitis) and malignancy was negative. A brain MRI showed no abnormalities, and CSF analysis revealed aseptic meningitis with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis.

The diagnosis hung in the balance until day 5, when a painful genital ulcer appeared. Combined with the oral aphthae and skin lesions, the patient met the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease. The concurrent neurological symptoms and hyponatremia pointed toward acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.

## The Normal MRI Trap

One of the most frustrating aspects of this case was the normal brain MRI. In neuro-Behçet’s, we typically expect to see parenchymal brain lesions, often in the brainstem or basal ganglia. However, this patient presented with aseptic meningitis without parenchymal involvement.

This is a crucial teaching point: acute neuro-BD can present as meningoencephalitis with a normal MRI, particularly in the early phase. Relying solely on neuroimaging to exclude CNS involvement in a patient with systemic inflammatory signs and unexplained neurological symptoms can lead to a missed diagnosis. The CSF findings of lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein were the true indicators of CNS inflammation here, not the imaging.

## The SIADH and Hiccups Connection

The patient’s hyponatremia was severe and consistent with SIADH. While SIADH is well-known in the context of CNS infections or malignancies, its association with Behçet’s is less appreciated. In this case, the CNS inflammation likely drove dysregulated ADH secretion, potentially mediated by proinflammatory cytokines like IL-6.

A subtle but significant clinical clue in this presentation was the persistent hiccups. While often dismissed as benign or gastroesophageal, in the context of CNS inflammation and hyponatremia, hiccups can be a marker of severe electrolyte disturbance or brainstem irritation. Furthermore, the elevated creatine kinase indicated rhabdomyolysis, a known but often overlooked complication of severe hyponatremia. Recognizing these "unrelated" symptoms as part of the electrolyte/CNS picture can accelerate the diagnosis.

## Treatment: When Colchicine Surprises

Standard management for acute neuro-BD usually involves high-dose corticosteroids and immunosuppressants like cyclophosphamide. Colchicine is typically reserved for mucocutaneous symptoms. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy, alongside fluid restriction for the SIADH.

Remarkably, the patient’s fever, neurological symptoms, and hyponatremia resolved without the need for steroids. By day 16, his sodium normalized, and ADH levels dropped. While systematic reviews suggest colchicine is generally ineffective for acute neuro-BD, this case suggests it may be a viable option in specific scenarios, particularly when corticosteroid therapy is undesirable or contraindicated. The resolution of the SIADH alongside the neurological symptoms supports the theory that controlling the underlying inflammation was the key to correcting the electrolyte abnormality.

## Avoiding the Over-Test Spiral

The diagnostic journey also serves as a reminder to avoid indiscriminate testing. The team evaluated for sarcoidosis (ACE, lysozyme) and lymphoma (sIL-2R) to rule out mimics. While the sIL-2R was mildly elevated, it was non-specific. The authors emphasize that these tests should be pursued only when supported by clinical features. In this case, the diagnosis was confirmed by the clinical criteria (oral and genital ulcers) rather than a specific serological marker like HLA-B51, which was positive but not diagnostic on its own.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Consider Neuro-BD in the Triad:** In patients presenting with fever, headache, and hyponatremia, consider acute neurological Behçet’s disease, even if the neurological symptoms appear at the time of initial systemic diagnosis.
*   **Normal MRI Does Not Rule Out CNS Inflammation:** Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions on MRI; rely on CSF analysis and clinical context.
*   **Watch for SIADH:** CNS inflammation in Behçet’s can trigger SIADH. Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis may be subtle clues to underlying severe hyponatremia.
*   **Clinical Criteria Over Serology:** Diagnosis relies on clinical features (oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions) rather than HLA-B51 status or non-specific inflammatory markers like sIL-2R.
*   **Colchicine May Have a Role:** While steroids are standard, colchicine monotherapy resulted in resolution of neurological and endocrine symptoms in this case, offering a potential alternative when steroids are contraindicated.
