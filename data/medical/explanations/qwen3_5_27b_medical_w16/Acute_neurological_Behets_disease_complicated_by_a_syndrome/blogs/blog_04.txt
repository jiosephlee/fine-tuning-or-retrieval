Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Concurrent Conundrum: Systemic and Neuro-Behçet's Diagnosed Simultaneously

Neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease is a high-stakes diagnosis. We typically teach that neurological manifestations—neuro-Behçet’s—occur years after the initial systemic diagnosis, often affecting the brainstem with parenchymal lesions. But what happens when the nervous system and the mucocutaneous system wake up at the same time?

A recent case report highlights a rare scenario where a patient presented with acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) concurrently with systemic Behçet’s disease, complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This case challenges the classic timeline we rely on in the emergency department and underscores a critical metabolic complication often overlooked in neuroinflammatory conditions.

## The Clinical Presentation

The patient was a man in his 60s who arrived at the ED with a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, arthralgia, and persistent hiccups. On initial exam, he was alert but fatigued with a fever of 38.1°C. Physical inspection revealed oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk.

The labs were where the story got interesting. He presented with marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 180 mg/L), and elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L). His volume status was clinically euvolemic. Endocrine workup ruled out thyroid and adrenal causes, but serum osmolality was low while urine osmolality was inappropriately high with elevated urinary sodium. This profile confirmed SIADH.

Neurologically, he worsened by day 3 with vertigo. A brain MRI was unremarkable, showing no abnormalities in the hypothalamus or other typical lesion sites. However, a lumbar puncture revealed aseptic meningitis with elevated protein and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis. Infectious causes, including HSV, VZV, and bacterial pathogens, were ruled out. By day 5, a painful genital ulcer appeared, solidifying the clinical picture of Behçet’s disease alongside the neurological findings.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Normal Imaging

One of the biggest pitfalls in this case was the initial reliance on neuroimaging to rule out CNS pathology. In typical neuro-BD, we look for parenchymal lesions, often in the brainstem. However, acute neuro-BD can present as meningoencephalitis without visible lesions on MRI, especially in the early phase.

In this patient, the normal brain MRI initially made the diagnosis of neuro-BD difficult. It is a crucial reminder that a normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD, particularly when clinical features like aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis are present alongside mucocutaneous signs. The diagnosis of definite Behçet’s was eventually secured using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, scoring points for oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, and skin lesions.

## The Metabolic Complication: SIADH

The presence of SIADH added a layer of complexity to this case. While SIADH is a known complication of CNS inflammation, its direct association with acute neuro-BD in the absence of pharmacological triggers is not well-documented. The inflammatory cytokines associated with neuro-BD, such as IL-6, may contribute to dysregulated ADH secretion.

Two specific clinical clues in this case pointed toward the severity of the hyponatremia. First, the patient had persistent hiccups, which have been reported as a clinical clue to severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS inflammation. Second, the elevated creatine kinase indicated rhabdomyolysis, a recognized but often overlooked complication of severe hyponatremia. Recognizing these non-specific symptoms as potential electrolyte disturbances is vital in patients with neuroinflammatory presentations.

## Treatment Nuances

Standard treatment for acute neuro-BD typically involves high-dose corticosteroids and immunosuppressants. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy, along with conservative fluid restriction for the SIADH. Despite systematic reviews suggesting colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-BD, this patient experienced a resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia without the need for steroids.

While this does not change the standard of care for severe neuro-BD, it suggests that colchicine may be a practical therapeutic option in the early phase of neuro-BD, particularly when corticosteroid therapy is undesirable or contraindicated. The SIADH resolved as the underlying inflammation was controlled, with sodium normalizing by day 16.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Concurrent Diagnosis is Possible:** Do not assume neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease only occurs years after the initial diagnosis; systemic and neuro-BD can present simultaneously.
*   **Normal MRI Does Not Rule Out Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-BD may present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions on brain MRI.
*   **Watch for SIADH:** Consider SIADH as a potential complication of acute neuro-BD in patients presenting with non-specific CNS symptoms and hyponatremia.
*   **Recognize Hyponatremia Clues:** Persistent hiccups and unexplained rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK) can be important indicators of severe hyponatremia in the context of CNS inflammation.
*   **Treatment Flexibility:** While steroids are the mainstay, colchicine monotherapy may be effective for certain presentations of acute neuro-BD, especially when sparing steroids is a priority.
