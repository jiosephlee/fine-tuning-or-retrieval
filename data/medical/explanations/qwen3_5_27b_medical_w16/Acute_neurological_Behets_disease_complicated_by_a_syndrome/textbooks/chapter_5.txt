Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Outcomes, Follow-up, and Educational Takeaways

The patient's clinical course demonstrated a favorable response to targeted therapy, with careful monitoring of both neurological and metabolic parameters. Following the initiation of colchicine on day 8, the patient’s fever resolved within 24 hours, and systemic symptoms such as malaise, vertigo, and hiccups improved gradually over the subsequent two weeks. Crucially, the management of SIADH involved conservative fluid restriction and daily monitoring of serum sodium to prevent rapid correction, which can lead to osmotic demyelination syndrome. By day 16, serum sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L, and antidiuretic hormone levels decreased significantly to 1.8 pg/mL, indicating the resolution of the inappropriate secretion. Concurrently, myogenic enzyme levels, which had initially suggested rhabdomyolysis, returned to normal, confirming that the metabolic derangement was reversible with the treatment of the underlying inflammatory drive and electrolyte correction.

Long-term follow-up provided valuable insight into the prognosis of this rare concurrent presentation. The patient was discharged after one month of hospitalization, during which rehabilitation addressed the physical decline associated with the acute illness. He remained on colchicine monotherapy for approximately four years, during which time there was no recurrence of Behçet’s disease symptoms or neurological manifestations. This sustained remission is notable because neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease often follows a relapsing-remitting course and typically requires more aggressive immunosuppression. In this instance, the stability achieved with colchicine alone suggests that in select cases of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, particularly those presenting primarily with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms without severe parenchymal damage, less intensive immunosuppression may be sufficient to maintain long-term disease control.

Specific clinical features in this case offer important diagnostic pearls for recognizing severe metabolic complications. The patient presented with persistent hiccups, a symptom often overlooked but recognized as a potential indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from CNS irritation or inflammation. Additionally, the initial laboratory evaluation revealed elevated creatine kinase levels, indicating rhabdomyolysis, a complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely investigated in the context of fever and malaise. These findings underscore the necessity of considering electrolyte disturbances when evaluating patients with non-specific neurological symptoms like hiccups or unexplained muscle pain. Clinicians should recognize that such seemingly unrelated symptoms may be direct manifestations of profound hyponatraemia secondary to conditions like SIADH, rather than independent neurological or muscular pathologies.

The diagnostic process highlights the challenges and importance of early recognition of neuro-Behçet’s disease in patients with newly diagnosed systemic Behçet’s disease. Typically, neurological involvement occurs years after the initial diagnosis, making the concurrent presentation seen here unusual. A key lesson from this case is that a normal brain MRI in the early phase of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease does not exclude the diagnosis, as inflammatory changes may be confined to the meninges or not yet visible on imaging. The diagnosis was established through the combination of characteristic mucocutaneous lesions, CSF findings of aseptic meningitis, and the exclusion of infectious and neoplastic causes. This reinforces the need to maintain a high suspicion for neuro-Behçet’s disease when patients with mucocutaneous Behçet’s disease present with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, even in the absence of radiological abnormalities.

Finally, the management of this case provides therapeutic lessons regarding treatment selection and comorbidity management. While corticosteroids are the standard mainstay for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, this patient achieved resolution with colchicine monotherapy, avoiding the metabolic side effects of steroids, which is particularly relevant given his history of poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus. Following the stabilization of the Behçet’s disease, biguanide therapy was initiated, and good glycaemic control was subsequently maintained. This sequence illustrates that in patients with systemic inflammatory disorders, managing the acute inflammatory phase is a prerequisite for effective long-term control of metabolic comorbidities. Ultimately, this case underscores the importance of recognizing SIADH as a potential complication of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease and considering colchicine as a viable therapeutic option when corticosteroids are contraindicated or when the disease presentation is primarily meningeal.
