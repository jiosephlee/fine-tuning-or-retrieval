Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 1: ## Pathophysiology and Background of Neuro-Behçet's and SIADH

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory vasculitis characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin lesions. Understanding the systemic nature of BD is essential for recognizing atypical presentations, such as the concurrent neurological and endocrine involvement seen in this case. Diagnosis typically relies on clinical criteria, such as the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD), which assigns points for specific features. In this case, the patient met the threshold for definite BD with a score of five, derived from oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, and folliculitis-like rashes. While uveitis is a common major manifestation, it was absent in this patient, highlighting that the absence of ocular involvement does not exclude the diagnosis when mucocutaneous criteria are met. The presence of systemic symptoms, including fever and malaise, alongside the specific mucocutaneous lesions, provided the foundational clinical evidence for the systemic diagnosis before neurological and endocrine complications were fully elucidated.

Neurological involvement, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), occurs in approximately 10% to 20% of patients but typically manifests years after the initial systemic diagnosis. This case represents a rare scenario where systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently. Neuro-BD generally presents in two phenotypes: parenchymal involvement, which affects the brainstem and basal ganglia, and non-parenchymal involvement, such as aseptic meningitis or intracranial hypertension. In this patient, the clinical picture suggested acute meningoencephalitis with headache, vertigo, and hiccups, supported by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showing lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. Notably, brain MRI revealed no parenchymal abnormalities. This underscores a critical diagnostic principle: a normal MRI, particularly in the early phase of the disease, does not exclude neuro-BD, especially when the pathology is primarily meningeal or inflammatory without frank tissue destruction.

The patient’s presentation of marked hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) was attributed to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), a known complication of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation. SIADH is characterized by euvolemic hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, inappropriately elevated urine osmolality, and elevated urine sodium, all of which were present in this patient. The pathophysiology involves the disinhibition of ADH release or direct stimulation of the posterior pituitary due to CNS pathology. In the context of this case, the low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) combined with high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L) confirmed the diagnosis after excluding other causes such as adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism. Clinicians must recognize that in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders, unexplained hyponatraemia should prompt an evaluation for SIADH, as it can exacerbate neurological symptoms and complicate management.

A potential mechanistic link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH involves inflammatory cytokines, specifically interleukin-6 (IL-6). IL-6 is a proinflammatory cytokine frequently elevated in neuroinflammatory disorders and is known to stimulate ADH release. Although IL-6 levels were not directly measured in this patient, the markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration suggests a significant underlying inflammatory response likely mediated by IL-6. This cytokine-mediated pathway provides a plausible biological explanation for the concurrent SIADH in the absence of pharmacological triggers, such as cyclophosphamide, which are more commonly associated with SIADH in BD patients. The resolution of hyponatraemia following anti-inflammatory treatment with colchicine further supports the hypothesis that controlling the underlying CNS inflammation is key to resolving the endocrine complication.

Finally, the demographic and epidemiological context of this case highlights the diagnostic challenges of concurrent systemic and neurological BD. While neurological symptoms at the time of initial BD presentation are uncommon, occurring in fewer than 5% of patients, this case demonstrates that they must be considered when non-specific CNS symptoms accompany mucocutaneous lesions. The patient’s age (in his 60s) is also notable, as BD onset is typically younger, yet the clinical features remained consistent with the disease. Additionally, while the patient tested positive for HLA-B51, this genetic marker is not required for diagnosis and was not the primary driver of the diagnostic decision in this instance. Recognizing the rarity of concurrent diagnosis and the potential for normal imaging in early neuro-BD is vital for preventing diagnostic delays in patients presenting with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia.
