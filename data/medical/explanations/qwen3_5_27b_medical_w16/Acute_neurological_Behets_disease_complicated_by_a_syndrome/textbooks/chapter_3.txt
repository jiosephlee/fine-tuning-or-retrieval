Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis Strategy

The diagnostic approach in this case required a systematic evaluation of a patient presenting with a complex triad of fever, neurological symptoms, and significant hyponatraemia. The clinical logic prioritized the exclusion of life-threatening and common causes of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation before confirming a rare multisystem disorder. Initially, the combination of fever, headache, and malaise raised a high suspicion for CNS infection or systemic inflammatory disease. Consequently, the diagnostic strategy followed a hierarchical process: first ruling out infectious and neoplastic mimics, then applying specific criteria for Behçet’s disease (BD), and finally confirming the concurrent endocrine complication of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This stepwise exclusion is critical because the definitive diagnosis of acute neuro-BD is often one of exclusion, relying on the absence of alternative explanations for meningoencephalitis in a patient with characteristic mucocutaneous findings.

The initial phase of differential diagnosis focused on distinguishing infectious from non-infectious causes of acute CNS inflammation. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly caused by herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella zoster virus (VZV), was a primary concern given the fever and headache. However, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis without neutrophilic predominance, which made bacterial meningitis less likely. Crucially, CSF cultures and polymerase chain reaction tests for bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal pathogens were all negative. These negative results effectively excluded viral and bacterial etiologies, allowing the clinicians to shift focus toward autoimmune or inflammatory causes. This exclusion of infection is a prerequisite for diagnosing neuro-BD, as infectious meningoencephalitis is statistically more common and requires immediate antimicrobial intervention.

Concurrently, the diagnostic team evaluated for neoplastic and granulomatous diseases that can mimic neuro-BD, such as primary CNS lymphoma and neurosarcoidosis. Primary CNS malignancy was considered due to the neurological symptoms, but brain MRI showed no abnormal findings, and CSF cytology revealed no malignant cells. Although soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels were mildly elevated, the report notes that this finding alone was insufficient to support a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma, as mild elevations can be non-specific and reflect general inflammatory activity. Similarly, neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential because MRI findings may be normal in early stages; however, normal serum lysozyme and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels, combined with the absence of other systemic features, rendered this diagnosis unlikely. This case illustrates the utility of ACE and sIL-2R as screening tools, while emphasizing that their limitations require careful interpretation within the broader clinical context rather than relying on them as standalone diagnostic markers.

Once infectious and neoplastic causes were excluded, the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease was established using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) scoring system. The patient accumulated a total score of five points, meeting the threshold for definite disease (≥4 points). The scoring was derived from oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and folliculitis-like skin lesions (one point). Notably, the genital ulcers appeared on day 5 of admission, which solidified the diagnosis after the initial presentation only showed oral lesions. This highlights the importance of repeated physical examinations in suspected BD cases, as the full spectrum of mucocutaneous manifestations may not be present at the initial visit. Once definite BD was confirmed, the presence of aseptic meningitis and neurological symptoms in the absence of other causes allowed for the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD.

In parallel with the neurological workup, the patient’s hyponatraemia required specific diagnostic criteria to confirm SIADH. The laboratory profile demonstrated hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia with a serum osmolality of 267 mOsm/kg, paired with inappropriately elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg) and high urinary sodium concentration (88 mmol/L). These values met the standard thresholds for SIADH, indicating impaired water excretion despite low serum osmolality. Additionally, the patient was clinically euvolemic, and thyroid and adrenal function were normal, further supporting the diagnosis. The elevated antidiuretic hormone (ADH) level of 22.4 pg/mL provided direct biochemical evidence of the syndrome. Recognizing SIADH in this context was vital not only for managing the electrolyte disturbance but also for understanding the pathophysiology linking CNS inflammation to endocrine dysregulation.

Finally, the role of genetic testing in the diagnostic process warrants clarification, specifically regarding HLA-B51. This patient tested positive for HLA-B51, which is an allele associated with BD susceptibility. However, the report explicitly states that this result did not contribute to establishing the diagnosis, as international criteria do not require HLA-B51 positivity. This underscores a key clinical lesson: while HLA-B51 may be supportive in ambiguous cases, it lacks the specificity to diagnose BD on its own and should not delay treatment or override clinical criteria. Furthermore, the normal brain MRI in this case did not exclude acute neuro-BD. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, particularly in the early phase. Therefore, clinicians must maintain a high suspicion for neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous findings and non-specific CNS symptoms, even when neuroimaging appears unremarkable.
