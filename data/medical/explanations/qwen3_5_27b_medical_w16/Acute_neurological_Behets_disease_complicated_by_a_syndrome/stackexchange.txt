Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] Based on the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), which specific clinical features contributed to this patient's score of five points, and what is the threshold for a definite diagnosis?

The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) utilize a weighted scoring system to standardize diagnosis, where a total score of 4 points or greater indicates definite disease. In this case, the patient achieved a score of 5 points based on three specific clinical features: oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), and a folliculitis-like rash (1 point).

As highlighted in the case report, genital aphthosis is considered a particularly specific finding for Behçet’s disease, which strongly supported the diagnosis in this patient. It is important to note that while the patient tested positive for HLA-B51, this genetic marker did not contribute to the ICBD score, as the criteria rely primarily on clinical manifestations. The accumulation of these points allowed for a definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease, which was crucial for identifying the concurrent acute neuro-Behçet’s disease and the associated syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In the initial evaluation of fever, headache, and aseptic meningitis, what specific cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and imaging findings helped exclude viral meningoencephalitis and bacterial meningitis in favor of neuro-Behçet's disease?

In this case, the exclusion of viral meningoencephalitis and bacterial meningitis relied primarily on specific cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) profiles and negative microbiological testing, supported by unremarkable neuroimaging.

The CSF analysis revealed a pattern of **aseptic meningitis**: mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein (0.97 g/L), and a **mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL)**. This cellular profile was critical in ruling out bacterial meningitis, which typically presents with neutrophilic predominance and positive cultures. Furthermore, specific CSF testing for **herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and bacterial/mycobacterial pathogens was negative**, effectively excluding viral meningoencephalitis and tuberculous meningitis.

Regarding imaging, the **brain MRI showed no significant abnormalities**, including in the hypothalamic region. While parenchymal lesions are common in neuro-Behçet’s disease, the absence of structural abnormalities helped exclude primary CNS malignancy and focal infectious processes such as abscesses or typical HSV encephalitis changes. Ultimately, the diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was established by exclusion of these infectious and neoplastic causes, in conjunction with the emergence of pathognomonic mucocutaneous findings (oral aphthae and genital ulcers) that satisfied the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed pathophysiological link between acute neuro-Behçet's disease and the development of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) in this patient?

The proposed pathophysiological link is cytokine-mediated dysregulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion driven by central nervous system (CNS) inflammation. In this patient, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease presented as aseptic meningitis without hypothalamic structural abnormalities on MRI, suggesting a functional rather than structural etiology for the SIADH.

The authors posit that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine known to stimulate ADH release and frequently elevated in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD, triggered the inappropriate ADH secretion. Although IL-6 levels were not directly measured in this case, the markedly elevated serum C-reactive protein (180 mg/L) serves as a surrogate marker for an underlying IL-6–mediated inflammatory response. Furthermore, the concurrent resolution of neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia following colchicine monotherapy supports this mechanism, as colchicine inhibits cytokine production, including IL-6. Thus, the SIADH is attributed to neuroinflammatory cytokine activity rather than direct hypothalamic damage or pharmacological triggers.

### [Clinical Pearls] Aside from neurological deficits, what two specific clinical manifestations in this case served as critical clues to the presence of severe hyponatremia and underlying CNS inflammation?

The two specific clinical manifestations that served as critical clues were **persistent hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis**, evidenced by the markedly elevated creatine kinase.

In this case, the patient presented with fever and headache, which are non-specific markers of CNS inflammation. However, the discussion highlights that persistent hiccups are a distinct clinical clue to severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, such as the aseptic meningitis observed here. Furthermore, the initial laboratory finding of elevated creatine kinase revealed rhabdomyolysis, a recognized but often overlooked complication of severe hyponatremia.

Clinically, this underscores the importance of recognizing "unrelated" symptoms in the context of neuroinflammatory conditions. When evaluating a patient with suspected CNS pathology, intractable hiccups or unexplained muscle enzyme elevation should raise immediate suspicion for profound electrolyte disturbances like SIADH. Identifying these clues is essential, as they signal the severity of the hyponatremia and the underlying inflammatory process, guiding appropriate fluid management and immunomodulatory therapy.

### [Management] Although corticosteroids are typically the mainstay of treatment for acute neuro-Behçet's disease, what therapeutic approach was successful in this case, and what does this suggest about treatment options when corticosteroids are contraindicated?

In this case, colchicine monotherapy was the successful therapeutic approach, leading to the resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia without the administration of systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. Although corticosteroids are typically the mainstay of treatment for acute neuro-Behçet's disease, this patient had poorly controlled type 2 diabetes, rendering systemic steroid therapy undesirable. Despite systematic reviews generally indicating that colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-Behçet's, this patient demonstrated rapid clinical improvement and normalization of serum sodium levels following the initiation of colchicine.

This outcome suggests that colchicine may serve as a viable therapeutic option for acute neuro-Behçet's disease when corticosteroid therapy is contraindicated or undesirable. The mechanism likely involves colchicine's anti-inflammatory effects, specifically the inhibition of neutrophil migration and suppression of cytokine production such as IL-1β and IL-6, which may address the underlying CNS inflammation driving both the neurological symptoms and the concurrent syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). While not the standard of care, clinicians should consider colchicine monotherapy in specific contexts where high-dose steroids pose significant risks, particularly in patients with concurrent systemic Behçet's disease and endocrine complications like SIADH.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the finding of a normal brain MRI in the early phase of acute neuro-Behçet's disease impact the diagnostic process, and what specific type of CNS involvement can present without parenchymal lesions?

A normal brain MRI in the early phase of acute neuro-Behçet's disease can complicate the diagnostic process by failing to reveal typical parenchymal lesions, potentially leading to diagnostic delay. In this case, despite the patient presenting with headache, vertigo, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evidence of inflammation, brain MRI showed no significant abnormalities in the hypothalamus or other brain regions. This finding illustrates that acute neuro-Behçet's disease can present specifically as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions. Therefore, the diagnosis should not be excluded based on neuroimaging alone; instead, clinicians must integrate systemic mucocutaneous criteria with CSF analysis to confirm CNS involvement when radiological findings are absent and infectious or neoplastic causes are excluded.

### [Management] What are the key laboratory parameters required to diagnose SIADH, and how did this patient's serum and urine osmolality and sodium levels satisfy these diagnostic criteria?

In diagnosing the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), we rely on a constellation of laboratory findings that demonstrate euvolemic hyponatremia with impaired free water excretion. The essential diagnostic parameters include hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, inappropriately elevated urine osmolality relative to serum osmolality, elevated urine sodium concentration, and clinical euvolemia, alongside the exclusion of other causes such as adrenal or thyroid insufficiency.

In this patient, the laboratory profile satisfied these criteria precisely. First, he presented with marked hyponatremia, with a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L, accompanied by low serum osmolality of 267 mOsm/kg. Despite this systemic hypotonicity, which should normally suppress antidiuretic hormone (ADH) and lead to dilute urine, his urine osmolality was inappropriately elevated at 900 mOsm/kg. Furthermore, his urinary sodium concentration was increased at 88 mmol/L, indicating continued renal sodium excretion despite the hyponatremic state. Clinically, he was assessed as euvolemic with no peripheral edema, and endocrine evaluations confirmed normal thyroid-stimulating hormone, free T4, and serum cortisol levels, ruling out hypothyroidism or adrenal insufficiency as alternative etiologies.

These findings, supported by an elevated ADH level of 22.4 pg/mL, confirmed the diagnosis of SIADH. In the context of this case, the SIADH was likely driven by the underlying CNS inflammation associated with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, illustrating how neurological pathology can disrupt the normal osmotic regulation of water balance.

### [Morning Report] Given that neurological involvement in Behçet's disease typically occurs years after the initial diagnosis, what is the clinical significance of the concurrent diagnosis of systemic and neurological manifestations in this patient?

The concurrent diagnosis of systemic and neurological manifestations in this patient is clinically significant because it challenges the typical temporal sequence of Behçet’s disease and highlights a specific, treatable cause of hyponatremia in the setting of CNS inflammation.

Typically, neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) develops several years after the initial diagnosis, occurring in fewer than 5% of patients at presentation. However, this case demonstrates that acute neuro-BD can present simultaneously with mucocutaneous lesions. The significance lies in the diagnostic recognition: when a patient presents with non-specific CNS symptoms such as fever, headache, and persistent hiccups alongside mucocutaneous findings, clinicians must maintain a high suspicion for neuro-BD even if the brain MRI is normal. In this case, the patient had aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis despite a lack of parenchymal lesions on MRI, illustrating that radiological normality does not exclude acute neuro-BD.

Furthermore, the concurrent diagnosis underscores a critical pathophysiological link between CNS inflammation and endocrine dysfunction. The patient’s marked hyponatremia was due to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), likely driven by the inflammatory cytokine milieu of the acute neuro-BD. This association is rare but clinically actionable; recognizing SIADH as a complication of neuro-BD prevents the misattribution of hyponatremia to other causes and guides management toward treating the underlying inflammation. As evidenced by this patient’s response to colchicine monotherapy, resolving the systemic and CNS inflammation can normalize sodium levels and neurological symptoms without the immediate need for high-dose corticosteroids. Therefore, the concurrent presentation serves as a reminder to evaluate for SIADH in patients with new-onset Behçet’s disease presenting with neurological complaints and electrolyte abnormalities.
