Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Pathophysiology and Background of Neuro-Behçet's and SIADH

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory vasculitis characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin lesions. Understanding the systemic nature of BD is essential for recognizing atypical presentations, such as the concurrent neurological and endocrine involvement seen in this case. Diagnosis typically relies on clinical criteria, such as the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD), which assigns points for specific features. In this case, the patient met the threshold for definite BD with a score of five, derived from oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, and folliculitis-like rashes. While uveitis is a common major manifestation, it was absent in this patient, highlighting that the absence of ocular involvement does not exclude the diagnosis when mucocutaneous criteria are met. The presence of systemic symptoms, including fever and malaise, alongside the specific mucocutaneous lesions, provided the foundational clinical evidence for the systemic diagnosis before neurological and endocrine complications were fully elucidated.

Neurological involvement, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), occurs in approximately 10% to 20% of patients but typically manifests years after the initial systemic diagnosis. This case represents a rare scenario where systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently. Neuro-BD generally presents in two phenotypes: parenchymal involvement, which affects the brainstem and basal ganglia, and non-parenchymal involvement, such as aseptic meningitis or intracranial hypertension. In this patient, the clinical picture suggested acute meningoencephalitis with headache, vertigo, and hiccups, supported by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showing lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. Notably, brain MRI revealed no parenchymal abnormalities. This underscores a critical diagnostic principle: a normal MRI, particularly in the early phase of the disease, does not exclude neuro-BD, especially when the pathology is primarily meningeal or inflammatory without frank tissue destruction.

The patient’s presentation of marked hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) was attributed to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), a known complication of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation. SIADH is characterized by euvolemic hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, inappropriately elevated urine osmolality, and elevated urine sodium, all of which were present in this patient. The pathophysiology involves the disinhibition of ADH release or direct stimulation of the posterior pituitary due to CNS pathology. In the context of this case, the low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) combined with high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L) confirmed the diagnosis after excluding other causes such as adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism. Clinicians must recognize that in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders, unexplained hyponatraemia should prompt an evaluation for SIADH, as it can exacerbate neurological symptoms and complicate management.

A potential mechanistic link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH involves inflammatory cytokines, specifically interleukin-6 (IL-6). IL-6 is a proinflammatory cytokine frequently elevated in neuroinflammatory disorders and is known to stimulate ADH release. Although IL-6 levels were not directly measured in this patient, the markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration suggests a significant underlying inflammatory response likely mediated by IL-6. This cytokine-mediated pathway provides a plausible biological explanation for the concurrent SIADH in the absence of pharmacological triggers, such as cyclophosphamide, which are more commonly associated with SIADH in BD patients. The resolution of hyponatraemia following anti-inflammatory treatment with colchicine further supports the hypothesis that controlling the underlying CNS inflammation is key to resolving the endocrine complication.

Finally, the demographic and epidemiological context of this case highlights the diagnostic challenges of concurrent systemic and neurological BD. While neurological symptoms at the time of initial BD presentation are uncommon, occurring in fewer than 5% of patients, this case demonstrates that they must be considered when non-specific CNS symptoms accompany mucocutaneous lesions. The patient’s age (in his 60s) is also notable, as BD onset is typically younger, yet the clinical features remained consistent with the disease. Additionally, while the patient tested positive for HLA-B51, this genetic marker is not required for diagnosis and was not the primary driver of the diagnostic decision in this instance. Recognizing the rarity of concurrent diagnosis and the potential for normal imaging in early neuro-BD is vital for preventing diagnostic delays in patients presenting with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia.

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Workup

The clinical picture of Behçet’s disease (BD) often begins with non-specific systemic symptoms that can mimic infectious or rheumatologic processes, requiring careful differentiation during the initial workup. In this case, a man in his 60s presented with a three-day history of fever, general malaise, loss of appetite, and arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. These constitutional symptoms, combined with a temperature of 38.1°C and tachycardia, initially raised concern for an infectious etiology or systemic inflammatory disorder. However, the presence of neurological complaints alongside these systemic features required a broader differential diagnosis. Notably, the patient reported persistent hiccups, a symptom that, while often benign, served as a critical neurological red flag in this context. Persistent hiccups can be a manifestation of severe hyponatraemia or meningeal irritation, prompting the evaluation of electrolyte disturbances and central nervous system (CNS) pathology. The combination of headache, vertigo, and hiccups in the setting of fever suggested CNS inflammation, necessitating a workup that extended beyond simple infectious meningitis to include autoimmune and metabolic causes.

A crucial step in characterizing this patient's condition involved the recognition of mucocutaneous hallmarks, which are central to the diagnostic criteria for BD. On initial examination, the patient exhibited oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. While oral ulcers are common, the concurrent presence of specific skin lesions helped narrow the differential diagnosis. The diagnostic picture became more definitive on day five when a painful scrotum ulcer appeared. The combination of oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes satisfied the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, yielding a score of five points. It is important to note that genital ulcers are considered a specific finding for BD and may not be present at the very onset of systemic symptoms. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for BD when mucocutaneous lesions evolve alongside systemic inflammation, as the absence of genital ulcers at presentation can delay diagnosis, as seen in this patient where the ulcer appeared after admission.

Initial laboratory investigations revealed a complex metabolic and inflammatory profile that guided the diagnostic reasoning. The most striking abnormality was marked hyponatraemia with a serum sodium level of 124 mmol/L. Further endocrine evaluation confirmed the diagnosis of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), characterized by low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), and increased urinary sodium concentration (88 mmol/L) in a euvolemic patient. Concurrently, inflammatory markers were significantly elevated, with a C-reactive protein concentration of 180 mg/L and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour, indicating a robust systemic inflammatory response. Additionally, the creatine kinase level was markedly elevated at 1349 U/L. This finding highlighted a potential complication of severe hyponatraemia, specifically rhabdomyolysis, which is often overlooked in the acute workup. Interpreting these labs required distinguishing between primary endocrine disorders, which were excluded by normal thyroid and cortisol levels, and secondary causes driven by CNS inflammation.

Neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis were pivotal in confirming the neurological involvement while ruling out alternative diagnoses. Brain MRI revealed no significant abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or other brain regions, a finding that can be misleading in early acute neuro-BD. This case underscores that a normal brain MRI does not exclude the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD, particularly when the presentation is meningoencephalitis without parenchymal brain lesions. To further evaluate CNS inflammation, a lumbar puncture was performed. CSF analysis revealed mildly elevated opening pressure, increased protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). The absence of neutrophilic predominance and negative cultures for bacterial, mycobacterial, and viral pathogens supported a diagnosis of aseptic meningitis. By synthesizing the normal neuroimaging with the CSF findings of aseptic meningitis and the clinical context of mucocutaneous lesions, the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD was established after excluding infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune causes.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis Strategy

The diagnostic approach in this case required a systematic evaluation of a patient presenting with a complex triad of fever, neurological symptoms, and significant hyponatraemia. The clinical logic prioritized the exclusion of life-threatening and common causes of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation before confirming a rare multisystem disorder. Initially, the combination of fever, headache, and malaise raised a high suspicion for CNS infection or systemic inflammatory disease. Consequently, the diagnostic strategy followed a hierarchical process: first ruling out infectious and neoplastic mimics, then applying specific criteria for Behçet’s disease (BD), and finally confirming the concurrent endocrine complication of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This stepwise exclusion is critical because the definitive diagnosis of acute neuro-BD is often one of exclusion, relying on the absence of alternative explanations for meningoencephalitis in a patient with characteristic mucocutaneous findings.

The initial phase of differential diagnosis focused on distinguishing infectious from non-infectious causes of acute CNS inflammation. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly caused by herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella zoster virus (VZV), was a primary concern given the fever and headache. However, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis without neutrophilic predominance, which made bacterial meningitis less likely. Crucially, CSF cultures and polymerase chain reaction tests for bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal pathogens were all negative. These negative results effectively excluded viral and bacterial etiologies, allowing the clinicians to shift focus toward autoimmune or inflammatory causes. This exclusion of infection is a prerequisite for diagnosing neuro-BD, as infectious meningoencephalitis is statistically more common and requires immediate antimicrobial intervention.

Concurrently, the diagnostic team evaluated for neoplastic and granulomatous diseases that can mimic neuro-BD, such as primary CNS lymphoma and neurosarcoidosis. Primary CNS malignancy was considered due to the neurological symptoms, but brain MRI showed no abnormal findings, and CSF cytology revealed no malignant cells. Although soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels were mildly elevated, the report notes that this finding alone was insufficient to support a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma, as mild elevations can be non-specific and reflect general inflammatory activity. Similarly, neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential because MRI findings may be normal in early stages; however, normal serum lysozyme and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels, combined with the absence of other systemic features, rendered this diagnosis unlikely. This case illustrates the utility of ACE and sIL-2R as screening tools, while emphasizing that their limitations require careful interpretation within the broader clinical context rather than relying on them as standalone diagnostic markers.

Once infectious and neoplastic causes were excluded, the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease was established using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) scoring system. The patient accumulated a total score of five points, meeting the threshold for definite disease (≥4 points). The scoring was derived from oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and folliculitis-like skin lesions (one point). Notably, the genital ulcers appeared on day 5 of admission, which solidified the diagnosis after the initial presentation only showed oral lesions. This highlights the importance of repeated physical examinations in suspected BD cases, as the full spectrum of mucocutaneous manifestations may not be present at the initial visit. Once definite BD was confirmed, the presence of aseptic meningitis and neurological symptoms in the absence of other causes allowed for the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD.

In parallel with the neurological workup, the patient’s hyponatraemia required specific diagnostic criteria to confirm SIADH. The laboratory profile demonstrated hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia with a serum osmolality of 267 mOsm/kg, paired with inappropriately elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg) and high urinary sodium concentration (88 mmol/L). These values met the standard thresholds for SIADH, indicating impaired water excretion despite low serum osmolality. Additionally, the patient was clinically euvolemic, and thyroid and adrenal function were normal, further supporting the diagnosis. The elevated antidiuretic hormone (ADH) level of 22.4 pg/mL provided direct biochemical evidence of the syndrome. Recognizing SIADH in this context was vital not only for managing the electrolyte disturbance but also for understanding the pathophysiology linking CNS inflammation to endocrine dysregulation.

Finally, the role of genetic testing in the diagnostic process warrants clarification, specifically regarding HLA-B51. This patient tested positive for HLA-B51, which is an allele associated with BD susceptibility. However, the report explicitly states that this result did not contribute to establishing the diagnosis, as international criteria do not require HLA-B51 positivity. This underscores a key clinical lesson: while HLA-B51 may be supportive in ambiguous cases, it lacks the specificity to diagnose BD on its own and should not delay treatment or override clinical criteria. Furthermore, the normal brain MRI in this case did not exclude acute neuro-BD. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, particularly in the early phase. Therefore, clinicians must maintain a high suspicion for neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous findings and non-specific CNS symptoms, even when neuroimaging appears unremarkable.

## Therapeutic Management and Treatment Rationale

The management of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) typically prioritizes aggressive immunosuppression to prevent permanent neurological damage. Standard of care generally involves high-dose systemic corticosteroids, often combined with other immunosuppressants such as cyclophosphamide or azathioprine, particularly when parenchymal brain lesions are present. In this case, however, the treatment strategy deviated from this conventional approach. The patient was managed successfully with colchicine monotherapy without the administration of systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. This decision was influenced by the patient’s comorbidities, specifically uncontrolled type 2 diabetes mellitus with an HbA1c of 8.2%, which posed a significant risk for hyperglycemic complications if high-dose steroids were introduced. While systematic reviews often suggest colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-BD compared to corticosteroids, this case illustrates that in selected patients where steroid therapy is undesirable, colchicine may serve as a viable alternative, particularly when the neurological presentation is limited to meningoencephalitis without extensive parenchymal destruction.

The physiological rationale for selecting colchicine lies in its specific anti-inflammatory mechanisms, which address the underlying pathophysiology of Behçet’s disease. Colchicine functions primarily by inhibiting neutrophil migration and activation, thereby reducing the inflammatory cascade. Furthermore, it suppresses the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). In the context of this case, the discussion highlights a potential link between IL-6-mediated inflammation and the development of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Given that elevated IL-6 levels have been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with acute neuro-BD, the use of colchicine aimed to dampen this cytokine drive. By targeting the systemic and central inflammation responsible for both the mucocutaneous lesions and the dysregulated antidiuretic hormone secretion, colchicine monotherapy addressed the root cause of the patient’s multisystem presentation.

Concurrent with the immunomodulatory treatment, the patient’s SIADH required specific metabolic management to correct the marked hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) and prevent neurological sequelae. The primary intervention was conservative fluid restriction, limited to less than 800 mL per day, combined with careful daily monitoring of serum sodium levels. This approach aims to create a negative water balance, allowing serum sodium to rise gradually as the underlying inflammation resolves. In this case, hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not required, as the patient responded to fluid restriction and the treatment of the underlying neuro-BD. This highlights that in SIADH secondary to inflammatory conditions, treating the primary disease process is often as critical as managing the electrolyte imbalance itself.

A critical safety principle in managing SIADH-induced hyponatraemia is the avoidance of rapid sodium correction. Rapid elevation of serum sodium can lead to osmotic demyelination syndrome, a severe and potentially fatal complication involving damage to the myelin sheath in the brainstem. Consequently, the medical team monitored sodium levels closely to ensure a slow, controlled normalization. In this patient, serum sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16, indicating a safe rate of correction. This gradual improvement coincided with the decrease in antidiuretic hormone levels from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL, confirming that the resolution of SIADH was tied to the resolution of the CNS inflammation rather than aggressive electrolyte manipulation.

Finally, adjunctive local therapies were employed to manage the specific mucocutaneous manifestations of Behçet’s disease, providing symptomatic relief while systemic treatment took effect. Oral aphthous lesions were treated with topical corticosteroid ointment, which reduces local inflammation and pain without contributing significantly to systemic steroid load. Similarly, genital ulcers and cutaneous lesions were managed with silver sulfadiazine cream to prevent secondary infection and promote healing. These local measures complemented the systemic colchicine therapy, ensuring comprehensive care for the patient’s dermatological symptoms. The successful resolution of all symptoms, including the neurological and endocrine manifestations, supports the efficacy of this multimodal approach in the specific context of acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.

## Clinical Outcomes, Follow-up, and Educational Takeaways

The patient's clinical course demonstrated a favorable response to targeted therapy, with careful monitoring of both neurological and metabolic parameters. Following the initiation of colchicine on day 8, the patient’s fever resolved within 24 hours, and systemic symptoms such as malaise, vertigo, and hiccups improved gradually over the subsequent two weeks. Crucially, the management of SIADH involved conservative fluid restriction and daily monitoring of serum sodium to prevent rapid correction, which can lead to osmotic demyelination syndrome. By day 16, serum sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L, and antidiuretic hormone levels decreased significantly to 1.8 pg/mL, indicating the resolution of the inappropriate secretion. Concurrently, myogenic enzyme levels, which had initially suggested rhabdomyolysis, returned to normal, confirming that the metabolic derangement was reversible with the treatment of the underlying inflammatory drive and electrolyte correction.

Long-term follow-up provided valuable insight into the prognosis of this rare concurrent presentation. The patient was discharged after one month of hospitalization, during which rehabilitation addressed the physical decline associated with the acute illness. He remained on colchicine monotherapy for approximately four years, during which time there was no recurrence of Behçet’s disease symptoms or neurological manifestations. This sustained remission is notable because neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease often follows a relapsing-remitting course and typically requires more aggressive immunosuppression. In this instance, the stability achieved with colchicine alone suggests that in select cases of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, particularly those presenting primarily with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms without severe parenchymal damage, less intensive immunosuppression may be sufficient to maintain long-term disease control.

Specific clinical features in this case offer important diagnostic pearls for recognizing severe metabolic complications. The patient presented with persistent hiccups, a symptom often overlooked but recognized as a potential indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from CNS irritation or inflammation. Additionally, the initial laboratory evaluation revealed elevated creatine kinase levels, indicating rhabdomyolysis, a complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely investigated in the context of fever and malaise. These findings underscore the necessity of considering electrolyte disturbances when evaluating patients with non-specific neurological symptoms like hiccups or unexplained muscle pain. Clinicians should recognize that such seemingly unrelated symptoms may be direct manifestations of profound hyponatraemia secondary to conditions like SIADH, rather than independent neurological or muscular pathologies.

The diagnostic process highlights the challenges and importance of early recognition of neuro-Behçet’s disease in patients with newly diagnosed systemic Behçet’s disease. Typically, neurological involvement occurs years after the initial diagnosis, making the concurrent presentation seen here unusual. A key lesson from this case is that a normal brain MRI in the early phase of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease does not exclude the diagnosis, as inflammatory changes may be confined to the meninges or not yet visible on imaging. The diagnosis was established through the combination of characteristic mucocutaneous lesions, CSF findings of aseptic meningitis, and the exclusion of infectious and neoplastic causes. This reinforces the need to maintain a high suspicion for neuro-Behçet’s disease when patients with mucocutaneous Behçet’s disease present with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, even in the absence of radiological abnormalities.

Finally, the management of this case provides therapeutic lessons regarding treatment selection and comorbidity management. While corticosteroids are the standard mainstay for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, this patient achieved resolution with colchicine monotherapy, avoiding the metabolic side effects of steroids, which is particularly relevant given his history of poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus. Following the stabilization of the Behçet’s disease, biguanide therapy was initiated, and good glycaemic control was subsequently maintained. This sequence illustrates that in patients with systemic inflammatory disorders, managing the acute inflammatory phase is a prerequisite for effective long-term control of metabolic comorbidities. Ultimately, this case underscores the importance of recognizing SIADH as a potential complication of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease and considering colchicine as a viable therapeutic option when corticosteroids are contraindicated or when the disease presentation is primarily meningeal.
