Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Dilemma

The clinical presentation of this patient underscores the diagnostic complexity of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) when it manifests primarily through respiratory and bulbar dysfunction rather than classic neurological signs. The patient, a woman in her early 40s, presented with a distinct symptom triad consisting of recurrent choking episodes, chronic occipital headaches, and asymptomatic hypoxia. The choking episodes were particularly notable for affecting both solids and liquids equally, a pattern that typically suggests a neurogenic etiology rather than a mechanical obstruction. These events were unpredictable, occurring during meals and sleep, and were characterized by violent coughing and gasping without resulting in complete airway obstruction. Concurrently, the patient suffered from chronic headaches localized to the occipital and vertex regions, which had persisted for 14 years. The most alarming feature of her presentation was the documented episodes of severe oxygen desaturation to 70%–80% without any subjective sensation of respiratory distress or dyspnea. This dissociation between physiological hypoxia and the conscious sensation of breathlessness is a hallmark of central hypoventilation, suggesting a disruption in the brainstem's automatic respiratory control centers while cortical voluntary breathing pathways remained intact.

The chronology of symptoms provides critical context for the etiology and progression of the disease. The patient’s symptom onset followed a minor motor vehicle accident 14 years prior, which involved a brief loss of consciousness. Shortly after the trauma, she developed persistent occipital headaches, followed by the progressive development of bulbar and respiratory symptoms over the subsequent decade. This temporal relationship raises the diagnostic question of whether the trauma induced the tonsillar herniation or merely unmasked a pre-existing congenital anatomical variant. A pivotal element in resolving this chronology was the retrospective review of a non-contrast CT scan of the head performed immediately after the accident. While originally reported as normal, a detailed re-evaluation revealed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. This finding suggests that the anatomical abnormality was present at the time of the trauma, yet the clinical significance was overlooked for 14 years, highlighting the potential for delayed diagnosis when imaging is not interpreted with specific attention to the craniocervical junction in the context of relevant symptoms.

The diagnostic dilemma was further compounded by an extensive initial workup that failed to identify the underlying neurological pathology. Because the choking episodes were the most distressing symptom, the patient underwent multiple evaluations by gastroenterology and otolaryngology services, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies. All these investigations were reported as normal, effectively ruling out structural lesions, tumors, or inflammatory causes of dysphagia. This negative workup is a crucial teaching point: when a patient presents with dysphagia affecting all consistencies and normal endoscopic findings, a neurological cause must be prioritized over gastrointestinal or laryngeal pathology. Similarly, pulmonary evaluations, including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, were normal, excluding intrinsic lung disease. The persistence of symptoms despite negative organ-specific investigations should have prompted a broader neurological assessment earlier in the clinical course, as the symptoms were ultimately driven by brainstem compression rather than primary dysfunction of the airway or lungs.

Beyond the physiological symptoms, the psychosocial impact of this undiagnosed condition was profound, significantly impairing the patient's quality of life. The unpredictability of the choking episodes led to severe anxiety and social withdrawal; the patient avoided restaurants and gatherings due to the fear of public choking incidents. Additionally, the management of her chronic headaches involved a culturally sensitive compromise. She reported that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline vertex pain from 6/10 to 3/10, likely due to reduced tactile stimulation of an allodynic scalp area associated with occipital neuralgia. However, this decision conflicted with her cultural expectations, contributing to further emotional distress. This aspect of the case illustrates that the burden of neurological disease extends beyond physical morbidity, affecting social functioning and cultural identity. The combination of chronic pain, respiratory insecurity, and social isolation emphasizes the necessity of a timely and accurate diagnosis to prevent long-term disability and psychological harm.

## Pathophysiology of Chiari Malformation Type I and Respiratory Dysfunction

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is anatomically defined by the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). While contemporary imaging studies suggest CMI is present in approximately 1% of the adult population, symptomatic presentations occur in only about 0.1% of the general population. In the context of this case, the patient exhibited a significant 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent, well above the diagnostic threshold. This anatomical anomaly is typically rooted in a developmentally small posterior fossa, creating a volumetric mismatch between the neural structures and the available intracranial space. This crowding results in the obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow at the craniocervical junction, a finding corroborated in this patient by the re-review of a historical CT scan which revealed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of CSF space. Understanding this structural constraint is essential, as the resulting pressure dynamics directly precipitate the neurological and respiratory sequelae observed in symptomatic individuals.

The clinical manifestations of CMI arise primarily from the compression of the brainstem and lower cranial nerves by the herniated tonsils. In this patient, the compression of the medulla and adjacent structures explains the constellation of symptoms, including chronic occipital headaches and oropharyngeal dysfunction. Lower cranial nerve involvement, reported in 15% to 26% of patients, typically presents as bulbar dysfunction such as palatal weakness or cricopharyngeal achalasia, which aligns with this patient’s recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. Furthermore, the obstruction of CSF flow can lead to intracranial pressure fluctuations, contributing to the paroxysmal headaches often precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres. However, the most critical pathophysiological consequence in this case involves the direct compression of the respiratory control centers within the medulla, leading to a disruption of the neural circuits responsible for maintaining automatic ventilation.

This brainstem compression results in a respiratory pathology that mimics acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, often referred to as Ondine’s curse, distinct from the congenital form associated with PHOX2B mutations. In the patient described, this manifested as severe oxygen desaturation episodes ranging from 70% to 80% without associated respiratory distress or awareness. The mechanism involves the impairment of the automatic respiratory drive while sparing the voluntary pathways. This distinction is clinically pivotal: voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways, which remain intact in CMI, allowing the patient to breathe normally when consciously focused on respiration. In contrast, automatic breathing, which is controlled by brainstem centers and essential during sleep or distraction, becomes compromised due to the mechanical compression of medullary structures. The patient’s experience at the airport illustrates this selective failure; a momentary distraction precipitated profound desaturation without dyspnoea, vividly demonstrating the loss of automatic control while voluntary capacity remained preserved.

The absence of dyspnoea during these hypoxic episodes is a particularly dangerous and instructive feature of this pathophysiology. The sensation of breathlessness, or air hunger, is a form of interoception that depends on afferent neural signals ascending from the brainstem to the cortex, specifically involving regions such as the anterior insular cortex. In this case, the primary brainstem dysfunction likely interrupts these afferent pathways, preventing the cortex from receiving the necessary input to generate the conscious sensation of distress. Consequently, the patient remained unaware of significant physiological compromise until oxygen saturation was measured objectively. This phenomenon underscores the importance of recognizing that structural brainstem pathology can present with unexplained hypoxia and central apnoea, even in the absence of subjective respiratory symptoms. The sleep study findings, which showed a predominance of central events (72%) despite a mild overall apnoea–hypopnoea index, further support the diagnosis of central hypoventilation driven by this anatomical compression rather than primary pulmonary or obstructive pathology.

## Diagnostic Evaluation and Reasoning

The diagnostic journey in this case underscores the critical importance of synthesizing disparate clinical symptoms and re-evaluating historical data when standard workups fail to yield answers. The patient presented with a complex triad of chronic occipital headaches, recurrent choking episodes, and unexplained hypoxia, a combination that initially prompted investigations into gastroenterological, pulmonary, and psychiatric etiologies. A pivotal turning point occurred during the retrospective review of a non-contrast CT scan performed 14 years prior following a motor vehicle accident. Although originally reported as normal, a detailed re-examination revealed a crowded foramen magnum with loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. This finding illustrates a vital clinical lesson: historical imaging should be re-interpreted when a new neurological hypothesis arises, as initial reports may overlook subtle structural anomalies that become relevant in the context of evolving symptoms.

To confirm the suspected anatomical abnormality, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head and neck was obtained, serving as the gold standard for diagnosing Chiari malformation type I (CMI). The MRI demonstrated cerebellar tonsillar descent measuring 11 mm below the foramen magnum, exceeding the diagnostic threshold of 5 mm typically required for CMI. This imaging confirmation was essential not only for establishing the structural diagnosis but also for assessing the degree of brainstem compression. The presence of significant tonsillar descent provided the anatomical substrate necessary to explain the patient’s neurological dysfunction, shifting the diagnostic focus from peripheral or functional causes to a central structural etiology.

Respiratory evaluation was complicated by the limitations of the available diagnostic tools. The patient underwent a Type III sleep study, which monitors respiratory effort, airflow, oxygen saturation, and heart rate but lacks electroencephalogram (EEG) monitoring. The study reported an apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) of 6.0, which technically falls within the mild range, yet 72% of the events were central in nature. This high proportion of central events is a critical finding in the context of brainstem pathology. However, the diagnostic reasoning must account for the limitations of Type III monitoring; without EEG data, total sleep time cannot be accurately determined, and the AHI is calculated based on total recording time, which includes wakefulness. Consequently, the reported AHI likely underestimates the true severity of the sleep-disordered breathing. Despite this limitation, the predominance of central events, combined with documented severe oxygen desaturations to 70%–80% without patient awareness, strongly supported the hypothesis of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome secondary to brainstem compression.

Before attributing the symptoms to CMI, a systematic exclusion of other potential causes was necessary to ensure diagnostic accuracy. Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease was considered due to the choking episodes, but normal pH studies and endoscopies ruled this out. Myasthenia gravis was evaluated given the bulbar symptoms, yet negative antibody testing and the absence of fatigability or diurnal variation made this unlikely. Similarly, primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following the hypoxic episodes; normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease. This process of elimination is a fundamental principle in clinical reasoning, ensuring that rare diagnoses are not pursued until common and treatable conditions have been rigorously excluded.

Finally, the diagnostic conclusion relied on correlating the anatomical findings with the specific clinical phenotype. The 11 mm tonsillar descent compresses the medulla, disrupting the respiratory control centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei. This anatomical disruption explains the patient’s choking episodes, which affect both solids and liquids equally due to neurogenic oropharyngeal dysfunction, and the central hypoventilation, where automatic breathing control is impaired while voluntary breathing remains intact. Furthermore, the lack of dyspnoea during severe desaturation episodes suggests brainstem pathology interrupting the afferent neural pathways required for the conscious sensation of air hunger. By integrating the re-interpreted CT findings, confirmatory MRI measurements, sleep study data, and the systematic exclusion of other differentials, the clinical team was able to unify the patient’s 14-year history of headaches, choking, and hypoxia under the single diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I.

## Management Strategies and Prognosis

The management of symptomatic Chiari malformation type I (CMI) requires a nuanced approach that balances the risks of surgical intervention against the potential for neurological deterioration. In the presented case, the patient’s presentation of central hypoventilation, severe choking episodes, and chronic occipital headaches necessitated a multidisciplinary consultation to determine the optimal therapeutic path. The primary intervention for symptomatic CMI is posterior fossa decompression surgery, which aims to relieve compression of the brainstem and restore normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow dynamics. In this specific case, surgical intervention was recommended due to the presence of significant brainstem compression evidenced by an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent and the life-threatening nature of the respiratory symptoms, including documented oxygen desaturations to the 70%–80% range. The rationale for surgery is grounded in the understanding that mechanical decompression can potentially reverse respiratory symptoms by alleviating the pressure on the medullary respiratory control centres, thereby addressing the acquired central hypoventilation syndrome.

Despite the recommendation for surgery, the patient initially opted for conservative management while contemplating the risks and benefits of the procedure. This decision highlights the importance of shared decision-making in neurological care, particularly when the diagnosis involves structural abnormalities with variable natural histories. Conservative management in this context was not merely a delay but involved active coping strategies tailored to the patient’s specific symptom profile. For instance, the patient utilized dietary modifications to manage choking risks and continued regular hair cutting to alleviate the marked allodynia at the vertex, which had previously reduced her baseline pain. While conservative management is generally reserved for asymptomatic anatomical variants or patients with mild symptoms, it may be considered temporarily in symptomatic patients who require time for decision-making or further diagnostic clarification, provided there is a robust plan for monitoring clinical stability.

Given the patient’s choice to defer surgery, a structured monitoring protocol was implemented to detect any signs of clinical deterioration that would necessitate urgent intervention. The surveillance plan established in this case included monthly clinical assessments to evaluate neurological status and symptom progression, quarterly overnight oximetry to monitor for worsening hypoxia, and annual MRI surveillance to track anatomical changes at the craniocervical junction. This rigorous schedule is critical because respiratory dysfunction in CMI can progress unpredictably, and the absence of dyspnoea during hypoxic episodes, as seen in this patient, removes a natural warning signal. Furthermore, the management plan included arranging for a Type I polysomnography with full EEG monitoring to overcome the limitations of the initial Type III study. This step is essential for accurately characterising the severity of sleep-disordered breathing and establishing a baseline before committing to surgical decompression.

The expected outcome of surgical intervention in cases of CMI with respiratory involvement is the restoration of automatic respiratory drive and the resolution of bulbar symptoms. Literature and pathophysiological principles suggest that posterior fossa decompression can reverse the acquired central hypoventilation by relieving brainstem compression, distinguishing this from congenital forms where outcomes may differ. However, prognosis remains individualised; in this case, the patient remained under close observation for eight months with the understanding that any clinical decline would trigger surgical intervention. The long-term prognosis is contingent upon the timeliness of decompression, as prolonged brainstem compression may lead to irreversible neurological deficits. The case underscores that while surgery offers a potential cure for the respiratory and bulbar manifestations, the decision must be weighed against the patient’s quality of life and willingness to undergo invasive procedures.

Ultimately, the management of this patient illustrates the necessity of a patient-centred approach in complex neurological diagnoses. The diagnostic delay of 14 years emphasises that management strategies must also include education on the potential neurological origins of seemingly unrelated symptoms, such as dysphagia and unexplained hypoxia. By integrating neurological, respiratory, and surgical perspectives, the care team ensured that the patient was not merely managed symptomatically but was positioned for a definitive resolution of the underlying structural pathology. The case serves as a clinical lesson that when conservative management is chosen, it must be accompanied by vigilant monitoring to prevent catastrophic respiratory events, ensuring that the window for effective surgical intervention is not lost.

## Clinical Lessons and Educational Implications

This case underscores the critical importance of recognizing neurological etiologies in patients presenting with unexplained dysphagia and choking. While gastrointestinal and otolaryngological causes are common, the patient’s inability to swallow both solids and liquids equally without structural obstruction on multiple endoscopies pointed toward a neurogenic origin. In Chiari malformation type I, compression of the lower cranial nerve nuclei can result in oropharyngeal dysfunction, including palatal weakness and cricopharyngeal achalasia. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for brainstem pathology when patients report recurrent choking episodes that do not correlate with mechanical obstruction or respond to acid suppression therapy. As highlighted in the discussion of this case, persistent dysphagia without a clear structural cause on endoscopy should prompt consideration of a neurological workup, particularly when accompanied by other neurological symptoms such as occipital headaches or cranial nerve deficits.

The diagnostic journey in this case also illustrates the value of re-evaluating historical imaging in chronic, undiagnosed presentations. The pivotal finding of a crowded foramen magnum was identified only upon re-review of a non-contrast CT scan performed 14 years prior to the current presentation. Initially reported as normal, the scan revealed cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction, findings suggestive of Chiari malformation type I that were overlooked at the time. This emphasizes that in patients with long-standing, progressive neurological symptoms, retrieving and re-interpreting prior imaging can provide crucial baseline data or reveal previously missed anatomical abnormalities. It is essential to correlate historical imaging with current clinical findings, especially when the initial diagnosis was made in the context of acute trauma or unrelated complaints.

Identifying red flags for central hypoventilation is another vital lesson derived from this patient’s presentation. The hallmark feature was severe oxygen desaturation to the 70%–80% range without associated respiratory distress or dyspnoea, observed during both sleep and waking states such as the airport incident. This dissociation between physiological distress and conscious sensation occurs because brainstem compression disrupts automatic respiratory control while sparing voluntary cortical pathways. Consequently, patients may lack the interoceptive drive to feel air hunger despite significant hypoxia. Clinicians should consider central hypoventilation in neurological patients who exhibit unexplained hypoxia, particularly if sleep studies show a predominance of central apnoeic events. Although this patient’s type III sleep study likely underestimated the severity of sleep-disordered breathing due to the lack of EEG monitoring, the high percentage of central events and documented desaturation provided sufficient evidence to pursue a neurological diagnosis.

Finally, this case highlights the necessity of an interdisciplinary approach and the danger of diagnostic anchoring on initial negative specialist workups. The patient underwent extensive evaluations by gastroenterologists and otolaryngologists, with multiple normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies, leading to a period of diagnostic uncertainty and attribution of symptoms to anxiety. This anchoring bias delayed the correct diagnosis for 14 years. Effective management of complex neuro-respiratory presentations requires synthesizing data across specialties, including neurology, pulmonology, sleep medicine, and otolaryngology. When a unifying diagnosis is not apparent after negative workups in one system, clinicians must remain open to alternative pathophysiological mechanisms. In this instance, the integration of headache history, swallowing dysfunction, and unexplained hypoxia by the primary care team facilitated the pursuit of a neurological diagnosis that explained the entire clinical picture.
