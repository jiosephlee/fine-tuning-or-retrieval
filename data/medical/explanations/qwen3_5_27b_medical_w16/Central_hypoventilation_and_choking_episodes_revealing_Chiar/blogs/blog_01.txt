Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Forget to Breathe: Central Hypoventilation Mimicking Ondine’s Curse in Chiari Malformation

Imagine a patient sitting in the waiting room. They look calm. They aren't gasping. They aren't using accessory muscles. But the pulse oximeter is screaming 78%.

Usually, severe hypoxia triggers a primal panic response—air hunger, tachypnea, distress. But in this case, the patient was asymptomatic until the number was read. This phenomenon, "silent hypoxia" in the context of brainstem dysfunction, is a terrifying but teachable presentation of Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI).

This case highlights a rare but critical presentation where structural brainstem compression mimics acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse). It serves as a stark reminder that when respiratory symptoms lack a pulmonary or airway cause, you need to look up at the brainstem.

## The Case: A 14-Year Diagnostic Odyssey

A woman in her early 40s presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. These episodes were unpredictable, occurring during meals and 3–4 times weekly during sleep, often waking her with violent coughing. Despite the severity, she never experienced complete airway obstruction.

The workup was exhaustive and frustratingly negative. She underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all reported as normal. Gastroenterology and ENT cleared her. She was told it might be anxiety.

The story took a darker turn during the COVID-19 pandemic. Home monitoring revealed repeated episodes of oxygen desaturation dropping to the 70%–80% range. Crucially, these were asymptomatic; she didn't feel short of breath. The climax of this diagnostic journey occurred at an international airport. She felt dizzy and nauseous, and on-site medical personnel documented an oxygen saturation of 78%. She was denied boarding. Supplemental oxygen resolved the symptoms within minutes, but pulmonary function tests and chest imaging were normal.

The breakthrough came from looking backward. A review of a CT scan from 14 years prior—performed after a minor motor vehicle accident—revealed a previously overlooked crowded foramen magnum. A current MRI confirmed Chiari Malformation Type I with an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent. A sleep study showed an Apnoea–Hypopnoea Index (AHI) of 6.0, but 72% of events were central.

## The Pathophysiology: Voluntary vs. Automatic Breathing

The core of this case lies in the dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing control.

Voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways. In this patient, she could breathe normally when asked or when awake and focused. Automatic breathing, however, is controlled by brainstem centers in the medulla and pons. These centers are responsible for the rhythmic drive to breathe during sleep and distraction.

In CMI, the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils compresses the brainstem. This compression disrupts the respiratory control centers, leading to an acquired form of central hypoventilation. The patient literally "forgets to breathe" when the automatic drive fails, such as during sleep or moments of distraction (like waiting at the airport).

Why didn't she feel the air hunger?

The sensation of dyspnea is a form of interoception. It relies on afferent neural pathways ascending from the brainstem to the cortex (specifically the anterior insular cortex). The brainstem compression in this case likely interrupted these afferent signals. The physiological distress was real (70% sats), but the cortical perception of "air hunger" was blocked. This explains the profound discrepancy between the severity of hypoxia and the lack of respiratory distress.

## The Headache and the Hair

While the respiratory symptoms were the most dangerous, the patient's headache history provided a subtle clue. She suffered from chronic occipital and vertex headaches for 14 years, starting after her accident. The pain was localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex with marked allodynia.

The patient noted that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain significantly. This phenomenon points to occipital neuralgia or a "burning vertex syndrome" associated with the CMI. The weight of the hair triggered discomfort due to cutaneous hypersensitivity at the scalp. While often dismissed as tension headaches, this specific pattern of vertex allodynia can be a neurological red flag for posterior fossa pathology.

## Clinical Limitations and Nuance

We must acknowledge the limitations in this diagnostic workup. The sleep study performed was a Type III study, which lacks EEG monitoring. Without EEG, total sleep time cannot be accurately calculated, meaning the AHI of 6.0 likely underestimates the true severity of sleep-disordered breathing. The reported AHI is calculated against total recording time (including wakefulness) rather than true sleep time.

Despite this, the predominance of central events (72%) combined with documented severe desaturation and anatomical compression supports the clinical hypothesis of central hypoventilation. A Type I polysomnography with full EEG and end-tidal capnography is recommended for definitive characterization.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Consider Neuro for Choking:** Persistent dysphagia or choking affecting solids and liquids equally, with normal endoscopic evaluation, should prompt a neurological workup. CMI is a known cause of neurogenic dysphagia.
*   **Silent Hypoxia is Real:** Severe oxygen desaturation without dyspnea suggests a disruption in interoceptive pathways, often pointing to brainstem pathology rather than primary lung disease.
*   **Re-Review Old Imaging:** In cases of chronic, unexplained neurological or respiratory symptoms, re-evaluating historical imaging (CT/MRI) can reveal structural abnormalities missed at the time of initial trauma or investigation.
*   **Know Your Sleep Study Limits:** Type III sleep studies lack EEG and may underestimate the severity of central sleep apnea. In patients with suspected brainstem compression, a full Type I polysomnography is preferred.
*   **Surgical Reversibility:** Posterior fossa decompression can restore CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms, making early recognition of CMI critical for patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction.
