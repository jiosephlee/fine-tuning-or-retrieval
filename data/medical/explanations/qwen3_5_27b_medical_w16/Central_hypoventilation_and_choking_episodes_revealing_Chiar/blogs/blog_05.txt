Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# The Chiari Conundrum: Incidental Anatomical Variant or Pathological Malformation?

Imagine waiting for an international flight when suddenly you feel dizzy and nauseous. You aren't panicking. You aren't gasping for air. You just feel unwell. Medical staff check your pulse oximeter, and it reads 78%. You are denied boarding. This isn't a panic attack. This is a woman whose brainstem literally forgot to tell her lungs to breathe.

This is the reality of acquired central hypoventilation, and in the case of a 40-year-old woman, it turned out to be the tip of the iceberg for a Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) that had been hiding in plain sight for 14 years. Her journey from recurrent choking to a diagnosis of brainstem compression highlights a critical tension in modern neurology: when does an anatomical variant become a pathological malformation requiring intervention?

## The 14-Year Diagnostic Odyssey

Our patient presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. These weren't just awkward moments; they were violent, distressing events occurring unpredictably during meals and awakening her from sleep three to four times a week. Naturally, the workup focused on the airway and esophagus. She underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies. All were normal.

While the choking was the most alarming symptom, the history revealed a deeper timeline. Fourteen years prior, she had been in a minor motor vehicle accident with a brief loss of consciousness. Immediately after, she developed persistent occipital and vertex headaches. She was diagnosed with nummular headache and treated with scalp injections with little success. A peculiar detail emerged: she experienced marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her hair triggered pain. Cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain, but she struggled with the cultural implications of this decision.

The pivot point occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic. Self-monitoring with a home pulse oximeter revealed asymptomatic episodes of oxygen desaturation dropping to the 70%–80% range. These episodes were unrecognised until she checked the device. It was the combination of chronic headaches, progressive choking, and unexplained hypoxia that finally prompted a neurological review.

## The Smoking Gun in the Archives

The breakthrough came not from a new scan, but from an old one. A non-contrast CT scan of the head performed 14 years earlier, following her accident, had been reported as normal at the time. However, a detailed re-review revealed a crowded foramen magnum and a loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. These findings were suggestive of CMI and had been missed initially.

Current MRI of the head and neck confirmed the suspicion: Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent. A sleep study demonstrated mild sleep apnoea with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, but critically, 72% of the events were central. This constellation of findings explained the choking, the headaches, and the terrifying oxygen drops.

## Physiology of "Forgetting to Breathe"

Why did this patient desaturate to 78% without feeling breathless? This is the hallmark of central hypoventilation, often referred to as acquired Ondine’s curse. In CMI, the pathophysiology centres on brainstem compression and disruption of respiratory control centres.

Voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways and typically remains intact in CMI patients. However, automatic breathing, controlled by brainstem centres and essential during sleep or distraction, becomes compromised due to tonsillar compression of medullary structures. Our patient preserved voluntary ventilation yet lost automatic respiratory drive. The airport episode illustrates this selective failure perfectly: a momentary distraction precipitated profound desaturation without dyspnoea.

We hypothesise that a primary brainstem dysfunction caused the alarming absence of dyspnoea. The experience of breathlessness is a form of interoception that depends on afferent neural pathways ascending to the cortex. By disrupting these signals during their transit through the brainstem, the patient’s cortex did not receive the necessary input to generate the feeling of air hunger. She literally "forgot to breathe" without knowing it.

## Incidental Variant or Pathological Malformation?

This case forces us to confront the Chiari Conundrum. Contemporary imaging studies reveal CMI in 1% of the adult population, though symptomatic presentations occur in approximately 0.1% of the general population. The condition demonstrates a modest female predominance and typically presents during the third decade of life.

The background literature challenges the traditional conceptualisation of CMI as universally pathological. Instead, it proposes a spectrum from normal anatomical variants to clinically significant malformations. Recent volumetric studies demonstrate substantial demographic variability in posterior fossa measurements without consistent symptom correlation. This change in thinking necessitates individualised, symptom-based clinical decision-making.

In our patient, the 11 mm tonsillar descent is significant, especially given the clinical picture. Chiari syndrome represents the symptomatic clinical manifestation of CMI, characterised by a distinctive constellation of neurological findings. The cardinal symptom is paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres, present in over 80% of cases. Lower cranial nerve involvement is reported in 15%–26% of patients, typically presenting with oropharyngeal dysfunction such as palatal weakness or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory manifestations represent an increasingly recognised complication of CMI. In a prospective study of 90 adults with CMI, 50% were found to have sleep-related breathing disorders. While pure central sleep apnoea occurred in only one case in that series, our patient's presentation underscores that respiratory pathology can extend beyond sleep disturbance to acute respiratory failure and life-threatening hypoxaemia.

## Management and The Road Ahead

Following multidisciplinary consultation, treatment options included conservative management versus surgical intervention (posterior fossa decompression). Surgical intervention was recommended given the severity of the respiratory symptoms and the anatomical evidence of compression. Crucially, posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms, underscoring the importance of recognising CMI in unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

However, the patient opted for conservative management while contemplating surgical options. She implemented coping strategies, including regular hair cutting for headache relief and dietary modifications. A structured monitoring plan was implemented, including monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance. The patient has remained under close observation with the understanding that any clinical deterioration may necessitate surgical intervention.

This case highlights the significant diagnostic challenge posed by CMI when it presents with atypical or misleading symptoms such as central hypoventilation and choking, leading to a 14-year delay in diagnosis. The prevalence of CMI in a retrospective study of children with persistent dysphagia was 3.3%, which is considerably higher than the general paediatric population. This suggests that persistent dysphagia can be a key indicator of underlying CMI.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Think Neuro with Choking:** Dysphagia or choking affecting both solids and liquids without a structural cause on endoscopy should prompt consideration of a neurological origin, specifically brainstem pathology.
*   **Silent Hypoxia:** Patients with CMI may experience severe oxygen desaturation without dyspnoea due to disruption of brainstem afferent pathways (acquired Ondine's curse).
*   **Re-Read Old Imaging:** Historical imaging should be reviewed when symptoms suggest a chronic structural etiology; a "normal" CT from years prior may hide a crowded foramen magnum.
*   **Symptom-Based Decision Making:** Distinguish asymptomatic anatomical variants from pathological Chiari syndrome based on clinical presentation rather than imaging measurements alone.
*   **Sleep Study Nuance:** Type III sleep studies may underestimate the severity of sleep-disordered breathing in CMI patients due to the lack of EEG monitoring for accurate sleep time calculation.
*   **Surgical Potential:** Posterior fossa decompression can potentially reverse respiratory symptoms and restore normal CSF flow dynamics in symptomatic CMI.
