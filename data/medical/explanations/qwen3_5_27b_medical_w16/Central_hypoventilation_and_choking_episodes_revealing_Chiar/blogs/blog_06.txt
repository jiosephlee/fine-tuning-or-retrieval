Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Anxiety vs. Anatomy: Avoiding the Psychiatric Label in Unexplained Hypoxia

Imagine waiting at an international airport, feeling dizzy and nauseous, when medical staff document your oxygen saturation at 78%. You are denied boarding. Yet, you are not gasping for air. You have no respiratory distress. You simply feel like you are about to pass out. This is the reality for a patient described in a recent case report, where a 14-year diagnostic odyssey finally revealed the culprit: a structural brain abnormality masquerading as anxiety and gastrointestinal dysfunction.

This case highlights a dangerous diagnostic pitfall in emergency and primary care medicine: the tendency to attribute unexplained hypoxia and choking episodes to psychiatric causes like panic attacks or anxiety disorders. When the lungs look normal on X-ray, and the swallowing scope is clear, it is tempting to label the symptoms as functional. However, when a patient presents with profound desaturation without the expected sensation of breathlessness, we must look beyond the chest and consider the brainstem.

## The Diagnostic Odyssey

The patient was a woman in her early 40s presenting with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes that occurred unpredictably during meals and sleep. These episodes affected both solids and liquids equally, a critical detail that often points away from mechanical obstruction and toward neurogenic dysfunction. Nocturnal choking occurred three to four times per week, often waking her with violent coughing and gasping. Despite the severity of the symptoms, which caused significant social withdrawal and anxiety, three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies were all reported as normal.

The clinical picture was further complicated by chronic occipital and vertex headaches spanning 14 years, which began after a minor motor vehicle accident. These headaches were so sensitive that the weight of her hair triggered discomfort, leading her to cut her hair short for relief. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home pulse oximetry revealed asymptomatic episodes of oxygen desaturation dropping to the 70%–80% range. Multiple emergency evaluations attributed these hypoxic episodes to anxiety, a diagnosis that persisted until a unifying neurological etiology was finally pursued.

## The Anatomy Behind the Anxiety

The turning point came from a review of old records. A non-contrast CT scan of the head performed 14 years earlier, following her accident, had been reported as normal at the time. However, a detailed re-review revealed a crowded foramen magnum with loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Current MRI confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent.

The pathophysiology here is elegant and dangerous. The patient’s symptoms represented an acquired form of central hypoventilation syndrome, often termed Ondine’s curse. In this condition, voluntary breathing, mediated by cortical pathways, remains intact. However, automatic breathing, controlled by brainstem centres, becomes compromised due to tonsillar compression of medullary structures. This explains why the patient could breathe voluntarily but would "forget to breathe" during sleep or distraction, leading to severe desaturation without the conscious sensation of air hunger. The brainstem pathology likely interrupted the afferent neural pathways required to produce the conscious sensation of dyspnea, creating a dangerous dissociation between physiological distress and patient awareness.

## Recognizing the Red Flags

This case serves as a stark reminder that dysphagia or choking without a clear structural cause on endoscopy should prompt consideration of a neurological origin. The patient’s choking affected solids and liquids equally, which distinguishes neurogenic from mechanical dysphagia. Furthermore, the combination of chronic occipital headaches, progressive choking episodes, and unexplained hypoxia provided a unifying neurological diagnosis that had been missed for over a decade.

The delay in diagnosis was significant, with the patient undergoing extensive gastrointestinal and pulmonary workups that were unrevealing. Pulmonary function tests were normal, and chest imaging showed no intrinsic lung disease. Myasthenia gravis and gastro-oesophageal reflux were also ruled out. The key to unlocking this mystery was recognizing that the respiratory symptoms were not primary lung pathology but rather a consequence of brainstem compression. This connection is supported by literature suggesting that posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms.

## Clinical Pearls for the Trenches

When evaluating a patient with unexplained desaturation, choking, or headaches, keep the following in mind. Do not let a normal chest X-ray or a normal swallowing study close the door on neurological evaluation. The absence of dyspnea during hypoxia is a specific red flag for central control failure rather than peripheral lung disease. Additionally, be aware that Chiari malformation type I can present with respiratory symptoms that closely mimic acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, distinct from the congenital form.

*   **Suspect brainstem pathology in unexplained hypoxia:** If a patient has documented oxygen desaturation without respiratory distress or dyspnea, consider central hypoventilation due to structural brain abnormalities like Chiari malformation.
*   **Differentiate neurogenic dysphagia:** Choking episodes affecting solids and liquids equally, especially with normal endoscopic findings, suggest a neurological cause rather than mechanical obstruction.
*   **Re-evaluate old imaging:** In patients with chronic neurological symptoms, historical imaging may reveal overlooked structural anomalies such as a crowded foramen magnum.
*   **Avoid the psychiatric trap:** Unexplained physiological events like hypoxia should not be attributed to anxiety until organic causes, particularly brainstem compression, are ruled out.
*   **Consider sleep studies:** Patients with Chiari malformation and suspected sleep-disordered breathing should undergo polysomnography, as central apnoea is a recognized complication of brainstem compression.
