Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Haircut for Headache: Vertex Allodynia as a Neurologic Red Flag

Imagine a patient presenting with a 14-year history of chronic headaches that only improve when they cut their hair short. In the clinic, we often hear patients describe the intensity of their pain, but we rarely hear that the weight of their hair is the primary trigger. This sounds like a dermatological or primary headache complaint, but in the case of a 40-year-old woman with recurrent choking and unexplained hypoxia, this peculiar symptom was a crucial neurological red flag pointing toward a structural brainstem abnormality.

This case report highlights a woman who spent over a decade seeking answers for recurrent choking episodes and chronic occipital headaches. While the respiratory symptoms eventually led to the diagnosis of Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI), it was the unique nature of her headache phenotype that underscores the importance of detailed history-taking. Her headaches were constant, pressure-like, and localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex. The most distinctive feature was marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. She eventually discovered that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10. While this decision conflicted with her cultural expectations and caused emotional distress, it provided a critical clue to the underlying pathology.

## The Chiari Connection

Chiari Malformation Type I is defined by the caudal displacement of cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum. In this patient, MRI confirmed an 11 mm descent with a crowded foramen magnum. While CMI is often associated with the classic Valsalva-induced occipital headache, the presentation can be far more variable. The pathophysiology centers on a developmentally small posterior fossa creating a volumetric mismatch, leading to brainstem compression.

In this specific case, the headache symptoms began shortly after a minor motor vehicle accident 14 years prior. While we cannot definitively say the trauma caused the malformation without pre-trauma imaging, the immediate onset of occipital headaches suggests the trauma may have exacerbated or unmasked a previously asymptomatic congenital variant. The compression at the craniocervical junction can affect lower cranial nerves and sensory pathways. Lower cranial nerve involvement is reported in 15% to 26% of patients, typically presenting with oropharyngeal dysfunction. However, the sensory disturbances can extend to the scalp.

## Occipital Neuralgia and Burning Vertex Syndrome

The patient’s vertex pain and cutaneous allodynia raise the question of whether this represents occipital neuralgia or a distinct entity like burning vertex syndrome. Occipital neuralgia associated with CMI can cause scalp hypersensitivity. Diagnostic criteria for occipital neuralgia require dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair. This patient met that description precisely; the mere weight of her hair was sufficient to trigger pain.

Alternatively, the symptoms align with the recently described burning vertex syndrome, which presents with scalp pain and cutaneous allodynia that does not fit neatly into established primary headache classifications like migraine or tension-type headache. The hypothesized mechanism involves small fibre pathways originating in the scalp. In our patient, the vertex symptoms likely represented occipital neuralgia related to CMI, though burning vertex syndrome occurring coincidentally remains a consideration. The key takeaway is that scalp allodynia in the context of posterior fossa pathology is a real, albeit understudied, phenomenon. Systematic documentation of cutaneous allodynia patterns in CMI populations is currently lacking, which means clinicians may overlook this sign without a high index of suspicion.

## The Unifying Diagnosis

While the headache was the chronic complaint, the acute danger lay in the respiratory dysfunction. The patient presented with recurrent choking affecting solids and liquids equally, along with nocturnal choking episodes. Home monitoring revealed severe oxygen desaturation to the 70%–80% range without respiratory distress. This dissociation between physiological hypoxia and the conscious sensation of breathlessness is a hallmark of brainstem compression.

The mechanism involves the disruption of respiratory control centres and the afferent neural pathways required for interoception. Voluntary breathing, mediated by cortical pathways, typically remains intact in CMI patients. In contrast, automatic breathing, controlled by brainstem centres, becomes compromised. The patient preserved voluntary ventilation yet lost automatic respiratory drive, creating a phenotype reminiscent of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome. The airport episode, where distraction precipitated profound desaturation without dyspnoea, vividly illustrates this selective failure of automatic control. The brainstem pathology interrupts the signals that must ascend to the cortex to produce the conscious sensation of air hunger.

## Clinical Implications

This case serves as a reminder that dysphagia or choking without a clear structural cause on endoscopy should prompt consideration of a neurological origin. The patient underwent multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies that were reported as normal, delaying the diagnosis for years. Furthermore, the historical CT scan performed after the accident had shown a crowded foramen magnum but was missed initially. A detailed re-review of the scan was pivotal in connecting the 14-year timeline of symptoms to the anatomical finding.

When evaluating patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction, particularly central hypoventilation patterns, we must consider structural brain abnormalities. Posterior fossa decompression can restore normal cerebrospinal fluid flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms. In this case, the patient opted for conservative management initially, but the understanding that her symptoms were potentially reversible was crucial for her care plan. The discrepancy between her severe symptoms and the mild findings on the type III sleep study (AHI 6.0) further emphasizes the need for comprehensive evaluation, as standard monitoring may underestimate the severity of central events without EEG data.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Scalp Allodynia is a Red Flag:** Vertex pain relieved by cutting hair or scalp allodynia may indicate occipital neuralgia secondary to posterior fossa crowding or Chiari malformation.
*   **Choking Can Be Neurologic:** Recurrent choking affecting solids and liquids equally with normal endoscopic findings warrants investigation for brainstem pathology rather than just gastroenterological causes.
*   **Silent Hypoxia:** Significant oxygen desaturation without dyspnoea suggests central hypoventilation and potential brainstem compression, distinct from primary pulmonary disease.
*   **Re-Review Old Imaging:** Historical imaging, particularly CT scans from trauma workups, should be re-evaluated for craniocervical junction abnormalities when neurological symptoms evolve over time.
*   **Consider Chiari in Respiratory Failure:** Acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse) can be a manifestation of Chiari Malformation Type I due to brainstem compression.
