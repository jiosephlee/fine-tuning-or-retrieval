# Forget to Breathe: Central Hypoventilation Mimicking Ondine’s Curse in Chiari Malformation

Imagine a patient sitting in the waiting room. They look calm. They aren't gasping. They aren't using accessory muscles. But the pulse oximeter is screaming 78%.

Usually, severe hypoxia triggers a primal panic response—air hunger, tachypnea, distress. But in this case, the patient was asymptomatic until the number was read. This phenomenon, "silent hypoxia" in the context of brainstem dysfunction, is a terrifying but teachable presentation of Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI).

This case highlights a rare but critical presentation where structural brainstem compression mimics acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse). It serves as a stark reminder that when respiratory symptoms lack a pulmonary or airway cause, you need to look up at the brainstem.

## The Case: A 14-Year Diagnostic Odyssey

A woman in her early 40s presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. These episodes were unpredictable, occurring during meals and 3–4 times weekly during sleep, often waking her with violent coughing. Despite the severity, she never experienced complete airway obstruction.

The workup was exhaustive and frustratingly negative. She underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all reported as normal. Gastroenterology and ENT cleared her. She was told it might be anxiety.

The story took a darker turn during the COVID-19 pandemic. Home monitoring revealed repeated episodes of oxygen desaturation dropping to the 70%–80% range. Crucially, these were asymptomatic; she didn't feel short of breath. The climax of this diagnostic journey occurred at an international airport. She felt dizzy and nauseous, and on-site medical personnel documented an oxygen saturation of 78%. She was denied boarding. Supplemental oxygen resolved the symptoms within minutes, but pulmonary function tests and chest imaging were normal.

The breakthrough came from looking backward. A review of a CT scan from 14 years prior—performed after a minor motor vehicle accident—revealed a previously overlooked crowded foramen magnum. A current MRI confirmed Chiari Malformation Type I with an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent. A sleep study showed an Apnoea–Hypopnoea Index (AHI) of 6.0, but 72% of events were central.

## The Pathophysiology: Voluntary vs. Automatic Breathing

The core of this case lies in the dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing control.

Voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways. In this patient, she could breathe normally when asked or when awake and focused. Automatic breathing, however, is controlled by brainstem centers in the medulla and pons. These centers are responsible for the rhythmic drive to breathe during sleep and distraction.

In CMI, the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils compresses the brainstem. This compression disrupts the respiratory control centers, leading to an acquired form of central hypoventilation. The patient literally "forgets to breathe" when the automatic drive fails, such as during sleep or moments of distraction (like waiting at the airport).

Why didn't she feel the air hunger?

The sensation of dyspnea is a form of interoception. It relies on afferent neural pathways ascending from the brainstem to the cortex (specifically the anterior insular cortex). The brainstem compression in this case likely interrupted these afferent signals. The physiological distress was real (70% sats), but the cortical perception of "air hunger" was blocked. This explains the profound discrepancy between the severity of hypoxia and the lack of respiratory distress.

## The Headache and the Hair

While the respiratory symptoms were the most dangerous, the patient's headache history provided a subtle clue. She suffered from chronic occipital and vertex headaches for 14 years, starting after her accident. The pain was localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex with marked allodynia.

The patient noted that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain significantly. This phenomenon points to occipital neuralgia or a "burning vertex syndrome" associated with the CMI. The weight of the hair triggered discomfort due to cutaneous hypersensitivity at the scalp. While often dismissed as tension headaches, this specific pattern of vertex allodynia can be a neurological red flag for posterior fossa pathology.

## Clinical Limitations and Nuance

We must acknowledge the limitations in this diagnostic workup. The sleep study performed was a Type III study, which lacks EEG monitoring. Without EEG, total sleep time cannot be accurately calculated, meaning the AHI of 6.0 likely underestimates the true severity of sleep-disordered breathing. The reported AHI is calculated against total recording time (including wakefulness) rather than true sleep time.

Despite this, the predominance of central events (72%) combined with documented severe desaturation and anatomical compression supports the clinical hypothesis of central hypoventilation. A Type I polysomnography with full EEG and end-tidal capnography is recommended for definitive characterization.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Consider Neuro for Choking:** Persistent dysphagia or choking affecting solids and liquids equally, with normal endoscopic evaluation, should prompt a neurological workup. CMI is a known cause of neurogenic dysphagia.
*   **Silent Hypoxia is Real:** Severe oxygen desaturation without dyspnea suggests a disruption in interoceptive pathways, often pointing to brainstem pathology rather than primary lung disease.
*   **Re-Review Old Imaging:** In cases of chronic, unexplained neurological or respiratory symptoms, re-evaluating historical imaging (CT/MRI) can reveal structural abnormalities missed at the time of initial trauma or investigation.
*   **Know Your Sleep Study Limits:** Type III sleep studies lack EEG and may underestimate the severity of central sleep apnea. In patients with suspected brainstem compression, a full Type I polysomnography is preferred.
*   **Surgical Reversibility:** Posterior fossa decompression can restore CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms, making early recognition of CMI critical for patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction.



# The Normal Scope Trap: Recognizing Neurogenic Dysphagia in Posterior Fossa Pathology

We’ve all seen the patient who swallows like they’re choking on air, yet the endoscopy comes back clean. In emergency medicine and gastroenterology, a normal scope often signals the end of the workup for dysphagia. But what if the obstruction isn’t in the esophagus? What if the problem is sitting right above the cricopharyngeus, where the brainstem meets the spinal cord?

This case highlights a diagnostic trap that can delay treatment for over a decade. A woman in her early 40s presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. She experienced nocturnal choking 3–4 times weekly, often waking herself with violent coughing. Despite three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, every report was normal. The social cost was high; she avoided restaurants and gatherings due to the fear of public choking.

The real alarm bell, however, wasn’t the choking. It was the oxygen. During the pandemic, she began home monitoring and documented asymptomatic oxygen desaturations dropping to 70%–80%. She had no respiratory distress. In fact, during an incident at an airport where her saturation hit 78%, she felt dizzy and nauseous but not short of breath. She was denied boarding, yet supplemental oxygen resolved the symptoms within minutes. Pulmonary function tests were normal. Chest imaging was normal.

When mechanical obstruction and primary lung disease are ruled out, you have to look up. A re-review of a CT scan from 14 years prior—performed after a minor motor vehicle accident—revealed a previously overlooked crowded foramen magnum. An MRI confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent.

## The Brainstem Connection

Chiari malformation type I is defined by the caudal displacement of cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum. While often associated with headaches, this case illustrates a more insidious presentation: acquired central hypoventilation and neurogenic dysphagia.

The pathophysiology here is a volumetric mismatch. A developmentally small posterior fossa creates pressure on the brainstem. This compression disrupts two critical systems. First, it affects the lower cranial nerve nuclei, leading to oropharyngeal dysfunction. This explains the choking on both solids and liquids—a hallmark of neurogenic rather than mechanical dysphagia. Mechanical obstruction usually presents with solids first, progressing to liquids. Neurogenic dysphagia hits all consistencies equally because the coordination of the swallow reflex itself is compromised.

Second, the compression impacts the respiratory control centers in the medulla. This creates a phenotype reminiscent of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, or "Ondine’s curse." The patient retains voluntary breathing control but loses automatic respiratory drive during sleep or distraction. This explains the profound hypoxia without dyspnea; the brainstem pathways responsible for the sensation of "air hunger" are interrupted. The patient literally forgets to breathe, and her cortex never receives the signal to panic.

## The Headache Clue

The respiratory and swallowing symptoms were the tip of the iceberg. The patient had a 14-year history of chronic occipital and vertex headaches that began immediately after her motor vehicle accident. These were not typical migraines. She described marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. She found relief only by cutting her hair short.

While occipital neuralgia is known to occur with CMI, the specific presentation of vertex allodynia and pain relief with hair reduction is an under-recognized clinical pearl. In the context of unexplained dysphagia and respiratory anomalies, chronic occipital pain should raise suspicion for craniocervical junction pathology.

## Diagnostic Pitfalls and Sleep Studies

This case underscores the limitations of standard workups. The patient underwent extensive pulmonary and GI evaluation that yielded no answers. Furthermore, the sleep study performed was a Type III monitor, which lacks EEG monitoring. Consequently, the Apnoea–Hypopnoea Index (AHI) was calculated as 6.0, suggesting mild sleep apnea. However, 72% of the events were central.

Without EEG to distinguish sleep from wakefulness, the AHI is likely underestimated. The true severity of the central hypoventilation was better reflected in the clinical history of desaturations to 70%–80% without distress. In patients with suspected brainstem compression, a full Type I polysomnography with capnography is often required to capture the true extent of respiratory failure.

## Clinical Pearls

The delay in this patient’s diagnosis was 14 years. It serves as a reminder that when the scope is normal, the problem may be neurological. The combination of dysphagia, unexplained hypoxia, and occipital headaches is a specific constellation that should trigger a neuroimaging workup focused on the posterior fossa.

Posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms. However, as seen in this case, management decisions are complex. The patient opted for conservative management initially, with close monitoring including quarterly overnight oximetry and annual MRI surveillance.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Solids and Liquids:** Dysphagia affecting both solids and liquids equally with normal endoscopic findings suggests a neurogenic cause rather than mechanical obstruction.
*   **The Silent Hypoxia:** Unexplained oxygen desaturations without respiratory distress (dyspnea) should raise suspicion for central hypoventilation and brainstem pathology.
*   **Occipital Pain:** Chronic occipital or vertex headaches, especially with scalp allodynia, are cardinal symptoms of Chiari malformation type I and should prompt neuroimaging.
*   **Re-Review Old Imaging:** Historical CT scans may reveal crowded foramen magnum or tonsillar descent that was missed during initial interpretation.
*   **Sleep Study Limitations:** Type III sleep studies may underestimate central sleep apnea in CMI patients; consider full polysomnography if clinical suspicion is high.
*   **Unifying Diagnosis:** When multiple systems (swallowing, breathing, pain) are involved without local structural cause, look for a unifying brainstem etiology.



# 14 Years in the Archives: The Critical Value of Re-Reading Historical Imaging

We’ve all seen the diagnostic odyssey. The patient bounces from specialty to specialty, accumulates a stack of normal reports, and leaves the clinic still suffering. But sometimes, the answer isn’t in the new test ordered today; it’s sitting in the radiology archive from a decade ago. A recent case report highlights a woman in her early 40s who spent 14 years chasing a diagnosis for chronic headaches and choking episodes. The culprit? A Chiari malformation type I (CMI) that was hiding in plain sight on a CT scan performed during a minor motor vehicle accident more than a decade prior.

This case is a stark reminder that "normal" radiology reports are not gospel, especially when the clinical picture doesn't fit. The patient presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. These weren't just awkward moments at dinner; she experienced nocturnal choking 3–4 times weekly. The real red flag, however, emerged during the pandemic when she began self-monitoring her oxygen saturation. She documented episodes of severe desaturation dropping to 70%–80%, yet she had no respiratory distress. She didn't feel short of breath. She literally forgot to breathe.

The workup was exhaustive and frustrating. Three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies were all normal. Pulmonary function tests and chest imaging were unremarkable. Anxiety was the working diagnosis for a long time, even after an airport incident where she was denied boarding due to an oxygen saturation of 78% and acute dizziness. It wasn't until the team stopped looking at her lungs and throat and started looking at her brainstem that the puzzle pieces fit.

The pivotal moment came when the team retrieved a non-contrast CT scan of the head from 14 years earlier, performed after her minor trauma. The original report had been read as normal. However, a detailed re-review of the images revealed a crowded foramen magnum and a loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. A subsequent MRI confirmed the diagnosis: Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent. This structural abnormality explained the chronic occipital headaches, the bulbar dysfunction causing the choking, and the acquired central hypoventilation syndrome.

The pathophysiology here is fascinating and clinically dangerous. In CMI, the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils compresses the brainstem. This compression disrupts the automatic respiratory control centres while leaving voluntary breathing intact. This creates a phenotype that mimics congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, or "Ondine’s curse." The patient can breathe if she is focused on it, but during sleep or distraction—like waiting at an airport—the automatic drive fails, leading to profound hypoxia without the sensation of air hunger. The brainstem compression also disrupts the afferent neural pathways required to generate the conscious sensation of dyspnoea, which explains why she remained asymptomatic during significant desaturation events.

Beyond the respiratory implications, the case underscores the diagnostic weight of neurological dysphagia. The patient’s choking affected solids and liquids equally, which is a hallmark of neurogenic rather than mechanical obstruction. When endoscopy is normal but dysphagia persists, the differential must expand beyond the GI tract to include brainstem pathology. In this specific case, the delay in diagnosis was compounded by the fact that the initial imaging was dismissed as normal. It serves as a powerful lesson in clinical humility: if the patient is sick and the workup is negative, look at the old images again.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Messages

*   **Re-evaluate historical imaging:** In patients with chronic, undiagnosed neurological symptoms, retrieving and personally reviewing historical imaging can reveal structural abnormalities missed on initial interpretation.
*   **Silent hypoxia is a brainstem sign:** Unexplained oxygen desaturation without respiratory distress or dyspnoea suggests central hypoventilation and warrants investigation of brainstem pathology.
*   **Neurogenic dysphagia:** Choking on both solids and liquids with normal endoscopic findings should prompt a neurological workup, including consideration of Chiari malformation.
*   **Chiari symptoms are variable:** While occipital headaches are common, CMI can present primarily with respiratory dysfunction or bulbar symptoms, particularly in adults.
*   **Trauma can unmask anatomy:** Head trauma may exacerbate or reveal a previously asymptomatic Chiari malformation, linking the onset of symptoms to the injury even years later.



# Haircut for Headache: Vertex Allodynia as a Neurologic Red Flag

Imagine a patient presenting with a 14-year history of chronic headaches that only improve when they cut their hair short. In the clinic, we often hear patients describe the intensity of their pain, but we rarely hear that the weight of their hair is the primary trigger. This sounds like a dermatological or primary headache complaint, but in the case of a 40-year-old woman with recurrent choking and unexplained hypoxia, this peculiar symptom was a crucial neurological red flag pointing toward a structural brainstem abnormality.

This case report highlights a woman who spent over a decade seeking answers for recurrent choking episodes and chronic occipital headaches. While the respiratory symptoms eventually led to the diagnosis of Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI), it was the unique nature of her headache phenotype that underscores the importance of detailed history-taking. Her headaches were constant, pressure-like, and localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex. The most distinctive feature was marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. She eventually discovered that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10. While this decision conflicted with her cultural expectations and caused emotional distress, it provided a critical clue to the underlying pathology.

## The Chiari Connection

Chiari Malformation Type I is defined by the caudal displacement of cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum. In this patient, MRI confirmed an 11 mm descent with a crowded foramen magnum. While CMI is often associated with the classic Valsalva-induced occipital headache, the presentation can be far more variable. The pathophysiology centers on a developmentally small posterior fossa creating a volumetric mismatch, leading to brainstem compression.

In this specific case, the headache symptoms began shortly after a minor motor vehicle accident 14 years prior. While we cannot definitively say the trauma caused the malformation without pre-trauma imaging, the immediate onset of occipital headaches suggests the trauma may have exacerbated or unmasked a previously asymptomatic congenital variant. The compression at the craniocervical junction can affect lower cranial nerves and sensory pathways. Lower cranial nerve involvement is reported in 15% to 26% of patients, typically presenting with oropharyngeal dysfunction. However, the sensory disturbances can extend to the scalp.

## Occipital Neuralgia and Burning Vertex Syndrome

The patient’s vertex pain and cutaneous allodynia raise the question of whether this represents occipital neuralgia or a distinct entity like burning vertex syndrome. Occipital neuralgia associated with CMI can cause scalp hypersensitivity. Diagnostic criteria for occipital neuralgia require dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair. This patient met that description precisely; the mere weight of her hair was sufficient to trigger pain.

Alternatively, the symptoms align with the recently described burning vertex syndrome, which presents with scalp pain and cutaneous allodynia that does not fit neatly into established primary headache classifications like migraine or tension-type headache. The hypothesized mechanism involves small fibre pathways originating in the scalp. In our patient, the vertex symptoms likely represented occipital neuralgia related to CMI, though burning vertex syndrome occurring coincidentally remains a consideration. The key takeaway is that scalp allodynia in the context of posterior fossa pathology is a real, albeit understudied, phenomenon. Systematic documentation of cutaneous allodynia patterns in CMI populations is currently lacking, which means clinicians may overlook this sign without a high index of suspicion.

## The Unifying Diagnosis

While the headache was the chronic complaint, the acute danger lay in the respiratory dysfunction. The patient presented with recurrent choking affecting solids and liquids equally, along with nocturnal choking episodes. Home monitoring revealed severe oxygen desaturation to the 70%–80% range without respiratory distress. This dissociation between physiological hypoxia and the conscious sensation of breathlessness is a hallmark of brainstem compression.

The mechanism involves the disruption of respiratory control centres and the afferent neural pathways required for interoception. Voluntary breathing, mediated by cortical pathways, typically remains intact in CMI patients. In contrast, automatic breathing, controlled by brainstem centres, becomes compromised. The patient preserved voluntary ventilation yet lost automatic respiratory drive, creating a phenotype reminiscent of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome. The airport episode, where distraction precipitated profound desaturation without dyspnoea, vividly illustrates this selective failure of automatic control. The brainstem pathology interrupts the signals that must ascend to the cortex to produce the conscious sensation of air hunger.

## Clinical Implications

This case serves as a reminder that dysphagia or choking without a clear structural cause on endoscopy should prompt consideration of a neurological origin. The patient underwent multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies that were reported as normal, delaying the diagnosis for years. Furthermore, the historical CT scan performed after the accident had shown a crowded foramen magnum but was missed initially. A detailed re-review of the scan was pivotal in connecting the 14-year timeline of symptoms to the anatomical finding.

When evaluating patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction, particularly central hypoventilation patterns, we must consider structural brain abnormalities. Posterior fossa decompression can restore normal cerebrospinal fluid flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms. In this case, the patient opted for conservative management initially, but the understanding that her symptoms were potentially reversible was crucial for her care plan. The discrepancy between her severe symptoms and the mild findings on the type III sleep study (AHI 6.0) further emphasizes the need for comprehensive evaluation, as standard monitoring may underestimate the severity of central events without EEG data.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Scalp Allodynia is a Red Flag:** Vertex pain relieved by cutting hair or scalp allodynia may indicate occipital neuralgia secondary to posterior fossa crowding or Chiari malformation.
*   **Choking Can Be Neurologic:** Recurrent choking affecting solids and liquids equally with normal endoscopic findings warrants investigation for brainstem pathology rather than just gastroenterological causes.
*   **Silent Hypoxia:** Significant oxygen desaturation without dyspnoea suggests central hypoventilation and potential brainstem compression, distinct from primary pulmonary disease.
*   **Re-Review Old Imaging:** Historical imaging, particularly CT scans from trauma workups, should be re-evaluated for craniocervical junction abnormalities when neurological symptoms evolve over time.
*   **Consider Chiari in Respiratory Failure:** Acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse) can be a manifestation of Chiari Malformation Type I due to brainstem compression.



# The Chiari Conundrum: Incidental Anatomical Variant or Pathological Malformation?

Imagine waiting for an international flight when suddenly you feel dizzy and nauseous. You aren't panicking. You aren't gasping for air. You just feel unwell. Medical staff check your pulse oximeter, and it reads 78%. You are denied boarding. This isn't a panic attack. This is a woman whose brainstem literally forgot to tell her lungs to breathe.

This is the reality of acquired central hypoventilation, and in the case of a 40-year-old woman, it turned out to be the tip of the iceberg for a Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) that had been hiding in plain sight for 14 years. Her journey from recurrent choking to a diagnosis of brainstem compression highlights a critical tension in modern neurology: when does an anatomical variant become a pathological malformation requiring intervention?

## The 14-Year Diagnostic Odyssey

Our patient presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. These weren't just awkward moments; they were violent, distressing events occurring unpredictably during meals and awakening her from sleep three to four times a week. Naturally, the workup focused on the airway and esophagus. She underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies. All were normal.

While the choking was the most alarming symptom, the history revealed a deeper timeline. Fourteen years prior, she had been in a minor motor vehicle accident with a brief loss of consciousness. Immediately after, she developed persistent occipital and vertex headaches. She was diagnosed with nummular headache and treated with scalp injections with little success. A peculiar detail emerged: she experienced marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her hair triggered pain. Cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain, but she struggled with the cultural implications of this decision.

The pivot point occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic. Self-monitoring with a home pulse oximeter revealed asymptomatic episodes of oxygen desaturation dropping to the 70%–80% range. These episodes were unrecognised until she checked the device. It was the combination of chronic headaches, progressive choking, and unexplained hypoxia that finally prompted a neurological review.

## The Smoking Gun in the Archives

The breakthrough came not from a new scan, but from an old one. A non-contrast CT scan of the head performed 14 years earlier, following her accident, had been reported as normal at the time. However, a detailed re-review revealed a crowded foramen magnum and a loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. These findings were suggestive of CMI and had been missed initially.

Current MRI of the head and neck confirmed the suspicion: Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent. A sleep study demonstrated mild sleep apnoea with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, but critically, 72% of the events were central. This constellation of findings explained the choking, the headaches, and the terrifying oxygen drops.

## Physiology of "Forgetting to Breathe"

Why did this patient desaturate to 78% without feeling breathless? This is the hallmark of central hypoventilation, often referred to as acquired Ondine’s curse. In CMI, the pathophysiology centres on brainstem compression and disruption of respiratory control centres.

Voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways and typically remains intact in CMI patients. However, automatic breathing, controlled by brainstem centres and essential during sleep or distraction, becomes compromised due to tonsillar compression of medullary structures. Our patient preserved voluntary ventilation yet lost automatic respiratory drive. The airport episode illustrates this selective failure perfectly: a momentary distraction precipitated profound desaturation without dyspnoea.

We hypothesise that a primary brainstem dysfunction caused the alarming absence of dyspnoea. The experience of breathlessness is a form of interoception that depends on afferent neural pathways ascending to the cortex. By disrupting these signals during their transit through the brainstem, the patient’s cortex did not receive the necessary input to generate the feeling of air hunger. She literally "forgot to breathe" without knowing it.

## Incidental Variant or Pathological Malformation?

This case forces us to confront the Chiari Conundrum. Contemporary imaging studies reveal CMI in 1% of the adult population, though symptomatic presentations occur in approximately 0.1% of the general population. The condition demonstrates a modest female predominance and typically presents during the third decade of life.

The background literature challenges the traditional conceptualisation of CMI as universally pathological. Instead, it proposes a spectrum from normal anatomical variants to clinically significant malformations. Recent volumetric studies demonstrate substantial demographic variability in posterior fossa measurements without consistent symptom correlation. This change in thinking necessitates individualised, symptom-based clinical decision-making.

In our patient, the 11 mm tonsillar descent is significant, especially given the clinical picture. Chiari syndrome represents the symptomatic clinical manifestation of CMI, characterised by a distinctive constellation of neurological findings. The cardinal symptom is paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres, present in over 80% of cases. Lower cranial nerve involvement is reported in 15%–26% of patients, typically presenting with oropharyngeal dysfunction such as palatal weakness or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory manifestations represent an increasingly recognised complication of CMI. In a prospective study of 90 adults with CMI, 50% were found to have sleep-related breathing disorders. While pure central sleep apnoea occurred in only one case in that series, our patient's presentation underscores that respiratory pathology can extend beyond sleep disturbance to acute respiratory failure and life-threatening hypoxaemia.

## Management and The Road Ahead

Following multidisciplinary consultation, treatment options included conservative management versus surgical intervention (posterior fossa decompression). Surgical intervention was recommended given the severity of the respiratory symptoms and the anatomical evidence of compression. Crucially, posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms, underscoring the importance of recognising CMI in unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

However, the patient opted for conservative management while contemplating surgical options. She implemented coping strategies, including regular hair cutting for headache relief and dietary modifications. A structured monitoring plan was implemented, including monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance. The patient has remained under close observation with the understanding that any clinical deterioration may necessitate surgical intervention.

This case highlights the significant diagnostic challenge posed by CMI when it presents with atypical or misleading symptoms such as central hypoventilation and choking, leading to a 14-year delay in diagnosis. The prevalence of CMI in a retrospective study of children with persistent dysphagia was 3.3%, which is considerably higher than the general paediatric population. This suggests that persistent dysphagia can be a key indicator of underlying CMI.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Think Neuro with Choking:** Dysphagia or choking affecting both solids and liquids without a structural cause on endoscopy should prompt consideration of a neurological origin, specifically brainstem pathology.
*   **Silent Hypoxia:** Patients with CMI may experience severe oxygen desaturation without dyspnoea due to disruption of brainstem afferent pathways (acquired Ondine's curse).
*   **Re-Read Old Imaging:** Historical imaging should be reviewed when symptoms suggest a chronic structural etiology; a "normal" CT from years prior may hide a crowded foramen magnum.
*   **Symptom-Based Decision Making:** Distinguish asymptomatic anatomical variants from pathological Chiari syndrome based on clinical presentation rather than imaging measurements alone.
*   **Sleep Study Nuance:** Type III sleep studies may underestimate the severity of sleep-disordered breathing in CMI patients due to the lack of EEG monitoring for accurate sleep time calculation.
*   **Surgical Potential:** Posterior fossa decompression can potentially reverse respiratory symptoms and restore normal CSF flow dynamics in symptomatic CMI.

The absence of dyspnea during hypoxia is a specific red flag for central control failure rather than peripheral lung disease. Additionally, be aware that Chiari malformation type I can present with respiratory symptoms that closely mimic acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, distinct from the congenital form.

*   **Suspect brainstem pathology in unexplained hypoxia:** If a patient has documented oxygen desaturation without respiratory distress or dyspnea, consider central hypoventilation due to structural brain abnormalities like Chiari malformation.
*   **Differentiate neurogenic dysphagia:** Choking episodes affecting solids and liquids equally, especially with normal endoscopic findings, suggest a neurological cause rather than mechanical obstruction.
*   **Re-evaluate old imaging:** In patients with chronic neurological symptoms, historical imaging may reveal overlooked structural anomalies such as a crowded foramen magnum.
*   **Avoid the psychiatric trap:** Unexplained physiological events like hypoxia should not be attributed to anxiety until organic causes, particularly brainstem compression, are ruled out.
*   **Consider sleep studies:** Patients with Chiari malformation and suspected sleep-disordered breathing should undergo polysomnography, as central apnoea is a recognized complication of brainstem compression.
