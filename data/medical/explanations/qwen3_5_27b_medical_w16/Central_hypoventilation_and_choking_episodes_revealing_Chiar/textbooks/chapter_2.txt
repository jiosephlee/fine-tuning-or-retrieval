Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of Chiari Malformation Type I and Respiratory Dysfunction

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is anatomically defined by the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). While contemporary imaging studies suggest CMI is present in approximately 1% of the adult population, symptomatic presentations occur in only about 0.1% of the general population. In the context of this case, the patient exhibited a significant 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent, well above the diagnostic threshold. This anatomical anomaly is typically rooted in a developmentally small posterior fossa, creating a volumetric mismatch between the neural structures and the available intracranial space. This crowding results in the obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow at the craniocervical junction, a finding corroborated in this patient by the re-review of a historical CT scan which revealed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of CSF space. Understanding this structural constraint is essential, as the resulting pressure dynamics directly precipitate the neurological and respiratory sequelae observed in symptomatic individuals.

The clinical manifestations of CMI arise primarily from the compression of the brainstem and lower cranial nerves by the herniated tonsils. In this patient, the compression of the medulla and adjacent structures explains the constellation of symptoms, including chronic occipital headaches and oropharyngeal dysfunction. Lower cranial nerve involvement, reported in 15% to 26% of patients, typically presents as bulbar dysfunction such as palatal weakness or cricopharyngeal achalasia, which aligns with this patient’s recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. Furthermore, the obstruction of CSF flow can lead to intracranial pressure fluctuations, contributing to the paroxysmal headaches often precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres. However, the most critical pathophysiological consequence in this case involves the direct compression of the respiratory control centers within the medulla, leading to a disruption of the neural circuits responsible for maintaining automatic ventilation.

This brainstem compression results in a respiratory pathology that mimics acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, often referred to as Ondine’s curse, distinct from the congenital form associated with PHOX2B mutations. In the patient described, this manifested as severe oxygen desaturation episodes ranging from 70% to 80% without associated respiratory distress or awareness. The mechanism involves the impairment of the automatic respiratory drive while sparing the voluntary pathways. This distinction is clinically pivotal: voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways, which remain intact in CMI, allowing the patient to breathe normally when consciously focused on respiration. In contrast, automatic breathing, which is controlled by brainstem centers and essential during sleep or distraction, becomes compromised due to the mechanical compression of medullary structures. The patient’s experience at the airport illustrates this selective failure; a momentary distraction precipitated profound desaturation without dyspnoea, vividly demonstrating the loss of automatic control while voluntary capacity remained preserved.

The absence of dyspnoea during these hypoxic episodes is a particularly dangerous and instructive feature of this pathophysiology. The sensation of breathlessness, or air hunger, is a form of interoception that depends on afferent neural signals ascending from the brainstem to the cortex, specifically involving regions such as the anterior insular cortex. In this case, the primary brainstem dysfunction likely interrupts these afferent pathways, preventing the cortex from receiving the necessary input to generate the conscious sensation of distress. Consequently, the patient remained unaware of significant physiological compromise until oxygen saturation was measured objectively. This phenomenon underscores the importance of recognizing that structural brainstem pathology can present with unexplained hypoxia and central apnoea, even in the absence of subjective respiratory symptoms. The sleep study findings, which showed a predominance of central events (72%) despite a mild overall apnoea–hypopnoea index, further support the diagnosis of central hypoventilation driven by this anatomical compression rather than primary pulmonary or obstructive pathology.
