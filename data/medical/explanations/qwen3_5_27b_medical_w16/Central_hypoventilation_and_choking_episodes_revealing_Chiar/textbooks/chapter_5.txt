Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Lessons and Educational Implications

This case underscores the critical importance of recognizing neurological etiologies in patients presenting with unexplained dysphagia and choking. While gastrointestinal and otolaryngological causes are common, the patient’s inability to swallow both solids and liquids equally without structural obstruction on multiple endoscopies pointed toward a neurogenic origin. In Chiari malformation type I, compression of the lower cranial nerve nuclei can result in oropharyngeal dysfunction, including palatal weakness and cricopharyngeal achalasia. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for brainstem pathology when patients report recurrent choking episodes that do not correlate with mechanical obstruction or respond to acid suppression therapy. As highlighted in the discussion of this case, persistent dysphagia without a clear structural cause on endoscopy should prompt consideration of a neurological workup, particularly when accompanied by other neurological symptoms such as occipital headaches or cranial nerve deficits.

The diagnostic journey in this case also illustrates the value of re-evaluating historical imaging in chronic, undiagnosed presentations. The pivotal finding of a crowded foramen magnum was identified only upon re-review of a non-contrast CT scan performed 14 years prior to the current presentation. Initially reported as normal, the scan revealed cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction, findings suggestive of Chiari malformation type I that were overlooked at the time. This emphasizes that in patients with long-standing, progressive neurological symptoms, retrieving and re-interpreting prior imaging can provide crucial baseline data or reveal previously missed anatomical abnormalities. It is essential to correlate historical imaging with current clinical findings, especially when the initial diagnosis was made in the context of acute trauma or unrelated complaints.

Identifying red flags for central hypoventilation is another vital lesson derived from this patient’s presentation. The hallmark feature was severe oxygen desaturation to the 70%–80% range without associated respiratory distress or dyspnoea, observed during both sleep and waking states such as the airport incident. This dissociation between physiological distress and conscious sensation occurs because brainstem compression disrupts automatic respiratory control while sparing voluntary cortical pathways. Consequently, patients may lack the interoceptive drive to feel air hunger despite significant hypoxia. Clinicians should consider central hypoventilation in neurological patients who exhibit unexplained hypoxia, particularly if sleep studies show a predominance of central apnoeic events. Although this patient’s type III sleep study likely underestimated the severity of sleep-disordered breathing due to the lack of EEG monitoring, the high percentage of central events and documented desaturation provided sufficient evidence to pursue a neurological diagnosis.

Finally, this case highlights the necessity of an interdisciplinary approach and the danger of diagnostic anchoring on initial negative specialist workups. The patient underwent extensive evaluations by gastroenterologists and otolaryngologists, with multiple normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies, leading to a period of diagnostic uncertainty and attribution of symptoms to anxiety. This anchoring bias delayed the correct diagnosis for 14 years. Effective management of complex neuro-respiratory presentations requires synthesizing data across specialties, including neurology, pulmonology, sleep medicine, and otolaryngology. When a unifying diagnosis is not apparent after negative workups in one system, clinicians must remain open to alternative pathophysiological mechanisms. In this instance, the integration of headache history, swallowing dysfunction, and unexplained hypoxia by the primary care team facilitated the pursuit of a neurological diagnosis that explained the entire clinical picture.
