Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Management] Beyond radiological findings, what clinical criteria distinguish asymptomatic anatomical variants of Chiari malformation type I from pathological entities requiring surgical intervention?

The distinction between an asymptomatic anatomical variant and a pathological Chiari malformation type I (CMI) requiring intervention relies not on radiological measurements alone, but on the presence of specific clinical symptoms attributable to brainstem compression. As noted in the background of this case, while CMI is found in 1% of the adult population on imaging, symptomatic presentations occur in only 0.1%. Therefore, the diagnosis of "Chiari syndrome"—the pathological entity—is defined by the clinical manifestation rather than the tonsillar descent itself.

The clinical criteria distinguishing pathology from variant include a constellation of neurological and respiratory findings. The cardinal symptom is typically a paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres, present in over 80% of cases. However, as illustrated by our patient, the spectrum of pathology extends to lower cranial nerve involvement and respiratory dysfunction. In this case, the patient’s recurrent choking episodes affecting solids and liquids equally, chronic occipital and vertex headaches, and unexplained oxygen desaturation without respiratory distress constituted the pathological criteria. These symptoms reflect brainstem compression and disruption of respiratory control centres, distinguishing her from an asymptomatic variant.

Management principles dictate individualised, symptom-based clinical decision-making. While asymptomatic anatomical variants require no intervention, the presence of unexplained neurological or respiratory dysfunction—such as the central hypoventilation and bulbar symptoms seen here—warrants consideration of surgical decompression to restore CSF flow dynamics. Our patient’s case underscores that even in the absence of classic Valsalva-exacerbated headaches, the combination of bulbar dysfunction and central respiratory compromise confirms a pathological entity necessitating surgical evaluation, rather than observation of an incidental finding.
