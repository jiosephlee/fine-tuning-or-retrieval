Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the presence of cutaneous allodynia or vertex headaches relate to Chiari malformation type I, and why is this often overlooked in clinical assessments?

In this case, vertex headaches and cutaneous allodynia represent an atypical neurological manifestation of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) that significantly contributed to a 14-year diagnostic delay. While the cardinal symptom of CMI is typically a paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva maneuvers, this patient presented with constant, pressure-like vertex headaches and marked allodynia, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. The case suggests these symptoms may relate to CMI through mechanisms such as occipital neuralgia or 'burning vertex syndrome' involving small fiber pathways at the craniocervical junction, though the authors note there is no conclusive data establishing a definitive causative link.

This presentation is often overlooked for three primary reasons. First, the symptoms deviate from the classic CMI phenotype; the patient’s headaches were not exacerbated by coughing or straining, leading physicians to diagnose nummular headache and treat with scalp injections rather than investigating structural pathology. Second, there is a lack of systematic documentation regarding cutaneous allodynia in CMI populations, meaning clinicians lack validated tools to recognize scalp hypersensitivity as a potential sign of posterior fossa compression. Third, initial imaging may be insufficient; the patient’s non-contrast CT scan from 14 years prior revealed a crowded foramen magnum that was unrecognised at the time, allowing the headache symptoms to be managed in isolation from the underlying malformation. Clinicians should therefore consider structural brainstem abnormalities in patients with chronic vertex headaches or scalp allodynia, particularly when accompanied by bulbar or respiratory dysfunction.
