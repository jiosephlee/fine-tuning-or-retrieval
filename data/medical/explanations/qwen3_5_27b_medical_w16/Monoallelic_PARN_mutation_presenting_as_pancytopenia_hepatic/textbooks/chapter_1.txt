Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 1: ## Telomere Biology Disorders: Pathophysiology and Clinical Spectrum

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences located at the ends of chromosomes that protect genomic integrity from degradation during cell division. Under physiological conditions, telomeres shorten with each replication cycle until they reach a critical length, triggering cellular senescence or apoptosis. To counteract this attrition, the enzyme telomerase elongates telomeres; this ribonucleoprotein complex includes the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC). In the context of the presented case, the *PARN* gene plays a critical regulatory role in this system. *PARN* encodes an exoribonuclease required for the removal of oligo(A) tails from nuclear RNAs, a process essential for the 3'-end maturation of TERC. Consequently, pathogenic variants in *PARN* impair telomerase function, leading to premature telomere attrition. Understanding this molecular mechanism is fundamental to recognizing why defects in telomere maintenance result in the multisystem failure observed in the patient.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are a group of rare, premature-ageing syndromes caused by Mendelian defects in telomere-related genes. These disorders are characterized by abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres, which disproportionately affect tissues with high cellular turnover. The clinical spectrum is broad, ranging from severe pediatric presentations to adult-onset conditions. Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is the prototype TBD and is classically defined by a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. In many cases, DC is also associated with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). However, as illustrated by the case report, patients may present without the classical triad. The patient in this report exhibited premature greying and hepatosplenomegaly but lacked the hallmark mucocutaneous features, highlighting that TBDs can manifest subtly in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease.

Beyond classic DC, the TBD spectrum includes severe childhood-onset phenotypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, which typically involve more profound developmental and neurological deficits. In contrast, adult presentations often mimic acquired conditions, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or cryptic cirrhosis. The case patient presented with pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis in her late 20s, a demographic where these findings are atypical for common acquired etiologies. This underscores the importance of considering a genetic etiology when fibrosis and marrow failure co-occur in young patients, particularly when standard workups for autoimmune or infectious causes are negative.

The pathophysiology of organ involvement in TBDs is driven by the susceptibility of rapidly dividing cells to telomere shortening. Hematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, and lung epithelial cells require frequent replication and are therefore most vulnerable to replicative senescence caused by defective telomere maintenance. In the presented case, this vulnerability explains the constellation of pancytopenia due to bone marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis resulting from hepatocyte dysfunction, and pulmonary fibrosis. The patient’s bone marrow examination showed trilineage haematopoiesis with ineffective erythropoiesis, and imaging confirmed diffuse fibrotic changes in the lungs and liver. Recognizing this pattern of multiorgan involvement involving high-turnover tissues is a key diagnostic clue for TBDs.

Genetic variability and environmental factors contribute to the complex clinical picture of TBDs, including variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance. While biallelic variants in *PARN* are associated with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), monoallelic variants are more frequently linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). The patient in this case harbored a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Despite the uncertainty of the specific variant’s pathogenicity, the clinical correlation supported a diagnosis of TBD. This highlights that monoallelic mutations can lead to significant disease phenotypes through mechanisms such as haploinsufficiency, even without a family history or classical genetic markers. Clinicians must therefore maintain a high index of suspicion for TBDs in young adults with unexplained fibrosis and cytopenia, as early recognition guides genetic counselling, surveillance, and potential consideration of bone marrow transplantation.
