Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 4: ## Molecular Mechanisms: The Role of PARN in Telomere Maintenance

The identification of a monoallelic *PARN* mutation in this patient provides the molecular basis for her multisystem presentation, linking her clinical features to a telomere biology disorder (TBD). To understand this diagnosis, one must first appreciate the function of the *PARN* gene within the context of telomere maintenance. Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences that cap chromosome ends, protecting genomic integrity during cell division. Under physiological conditions, the telomerase complex, comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (*TERT*) and telomerase RNA component (*TERC*), maintains telomere length. The *PARN* gene encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease, an exoribonuclease that regulates gene expression by shortening mRNA poly(A) tails through deadenylation. Crucially, *PARN* is required for the removal of post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails from the *TERC* RNA 3-prime terminal. This process is essential for the 3-prime-end maturation of *TERC* and the subsequent assembly of a functional telomerase complex. When *PARN* function is impaired, telomerase activity is compromised, leading to premature telomere attrition. This molecular defect results in accelerated cellular senescence or apoptosis, particularly in actively dividing cell populations such as bone marrow, hepatocytes, and lung epithelium, which explains the patient’s pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis.

The clinical phenotype associated with *PARN* mutations is influenced significantly by the zygosity of the variant. Germline pathogenic variants in *PARN* are recognized causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), typically resulting from biallelic variants that cause severe childhood-onset phenotypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. In contrast, monoallelic germline pathogenic variants are more frequently implicated in telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). While monoallelic *PARN* mutations are commonly associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the clinical spectrum is expanding. This patient presented with a monoallelic variant yet exhibited a broader constellation of symptoms including pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and premature ageing features. This case illustrates that monoallelic *PARN* mutations can manifest with multisystem involvement beyond isolated pulmonary fibrosis, potentially rendering the bone marrow more susceptible to haematopoietic stress, such as the severe vitamin B12 deficiency observed in this patient.

The specific genetic finding in this case was a monoallelic missense variant in *PARN* (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This variant alters a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain, a critical functional region of the protein. Although classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) because the role of this specific variant in disease could not be definitively ascertained, multiple lines of evidence support its pathogenic potential. In silico missense prediction tools, including LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT, predicted a deleterious functional effect. Furthermore, the variant meets several ACMG/AMP supportive criteria for pathogenicity: it occurs within a critical functional domain with no known benign variation (PM1), is absent or extremely rare in population databases (PM2), is predicted to be damaging by computational tools (PP3), and occurs in a gene with a low rate of benign missense variation (PP2). These findings are consistent with other reported *PARN* missense variants, such as p.Lys59Arg and p.Ala383Val, which are classified as likely pathogenic in clinical databases.

Ultimately, the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder in this patient relied on a strong clinical–genetic correlation rather than a definitively pathogenic genetic classification alone. The gold standard for diagnosing suspected TBDs includes genetic testing via next-generation sequencing and flow cytometry-based assays to measure telomere length. In this case, the clinical exome sequencing identified the *PARN* variant, which, when viewed alongside the patient’s unexplained multisystem fibrosis, cytopenia, and premature ageing, supported the diagnosis despite the VUS classification. This underscores a critical principle for medical trainers: genetic variants must be interpreted within the context of the patient’s phenotype. The presence of the *PARN* variant provided a unifying explanation for the patient’s condition, guiding genetic counselling and surveillance for progressive organ involvement. It highlights that early recognition of TBDs is crucial, even in young adults lacking classical mucocutaneous features, to facilitate appropriate monitoring and consideration of interventions such as bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.
