Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Presentation: Multisystem Manifestations in a Young Adult

The clinical picture of this patient was defined by a chronic, progressive decline in functional capacity that began in her mid-twenties and worsened over five years. The history of present illness revealed generalized weakness and early fatigability that eventually impaired her ability to perform activities of daily living, necessitating assistance for self-care. Concurrently, she developed exertional dyspnea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia. In a young adult, such a prolonged history of progressive multisystem failure is atypical for common acquired conditions and warrants a broad differential diagnosis that includes hematologic, autoimmune, infectious, and genetic etiologies. The trajectory of her illness, characterized by a slow onset followed by an acceleration of symptoms in the final three months, suggested an underlying pathophysiology affecting high-turnover tissues rather than an acute inflammatory or infectious process.

Physical examination provided critical clues pointing toward a syndromic etiology. While the patient exhibited signs consistent with anemia and liver dysfunction, such as pallor and icterus, she also displayed features suggestive of premature aging. These included premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with atrophy of the lingual papillae. These mucocutaneous findings are often associated with telomere biology disorders, even in the absence of the classic triad of dyskeratosis congenita. Abdominal examination revealed significant hepatomegaly, extending 5 cm below the right costal margin, while respiratory examination noted reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scapular regions, hinting at underlying parenchymal lung disease. Musculoskeletal assessment identified thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, further indicating systemic involvement beyond a single organ system.

Hematological evaluation was central to the diagnostic workup, revealing pancytopenia with a markedly low reticulocyte count of 0.39%. Peripheral blood smear analysis demonstrated dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, findings typically suggestive of ineffective erythropoiesis. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed, which initially provided a plausible explanation for the anemia and neuropathy. However, the bone marrow examination showed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, without evidence of malignancy or dysplasia. Although the direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG, the low reticulocyte response and marrow findings were more consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis than peripheral immune-mediated destruction. This hematological profile indicated a marrow that was attempting to compensate for peripheral loss or destruction but was failing to produce adequate functional cells, a pattern often seen in marrow failure syndromes.

Organ involvement extended beyond the hematopoietic system, presenting with fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs. Abdominal ultrasound confirmed hepatosplenomegaly, and transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis and cirrhosis with a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Autoimmune and infectious workups for hepatic dysfunction were largely negative, leaving the etiology of the fibrosis unexplained by common causes. Similarly, positron emission tomography–CT imaging of the chest revealed diffuse ground-glass haziness with multiple fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes in both lung fields. In a patient in her late twenties, the presence of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic cirrhosis is highly unusual and strongly suggests a genetic predisposition affecting cellular replicative capacity.

Additional systemic findings included secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte disturbances, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis confirmed on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which explained her recurrent bone pain and fractures. Electromyography and nerve conduction studies further revealed asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes. The clinical reasoning in this case hinged on the discrepancy between the severity of the systemic symptoms and the initial nutritional findings. While the severe vitamin B12 deficiency accounted for the macrocytic anemia and neuropathy, the constellation of pancytopenia, premature greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic involvement of the liver and lungs was disproportionate to a nutritional deficiency alone. This mismatch, particularly the presence of fibrosis and premature aging signs in a young adult, shifted the clinical suspicion toward a cryptic inherited disorder, specifically a telomere biology disorder, necessitating genetic sequencing for confirmation.
