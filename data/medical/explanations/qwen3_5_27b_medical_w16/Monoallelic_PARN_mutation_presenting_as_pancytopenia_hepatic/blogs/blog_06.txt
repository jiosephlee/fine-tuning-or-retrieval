Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# The Diagnostic Algorithm for Undifferentiated Pancytopenia and Organ Fibrosis

You are seeing a woman in her late 20s with a five-year history of progressive fatigue. She looks tired, yes, but the physical exam throws you a curveball. She has premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue. The labs show pancytopenia, but the peripheral smear is messy with dimorphic erythrocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils. You find severe vitamin B12 deficiency and treat it, but the story doesn't end there. Imaging reveals cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis.

In a patient this young, this constellation of findings is a massive red flag. When you see a young adult with unexplained bone marrow failure, liver fibrosis, and lung fibrosis, you have to stop thinking about common autoimmune or nutritional causes and start thinking about Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs). This case highlights a diagnostic pivot point where recognizing a genetic underpinning changes the surveillance, counseling, and management trajectory entirely.

The patient in this report presented with a clinical picture that initially screamed nutritional deficiency and perhaps autoimmune disease. The severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency explained the pancytopenia and the ineffective erythropoiesis seen on the bone marrow. However, the clinical dysfunction remained disproportionate even after nutritional correction. The presence of hepatic fibrosis confirmed by elastography and pulmonary fibrosis seen on CT, alongside osteoporosis and neuropathy, suggested a multisystem process. The physical exam findings of premature greying and mucocutaneous changes were subtle but critical clues pointing toward a premature ageing syndrome.

The diagnostic workup required ruling out the usual suspects first. Autoimmune serologies were negative, and infectious causes for the hepatitis were excluded. The bone marrow showed trilineage haematopoiesis without malignancy, which ruled out primary leukemia or myelodysplasia as the primary driver. The clinchers were the fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs combined with the cytopenia in a young patient. When you have pancytopenia plus organ fibrosis without a clear environmental or autoimmune cause, the differential must include TBDs, even if the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is absent.

Genetic testing is the cornerstone of confirming this diagnosis. In this case, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene. While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the variant affected a highly conserved residue and aligned with the clinical phenotype of a telomere biology disorder. The discussion in the literature suggests that while biallelic *PARN* variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita, monoallelic variants are increasingly associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. The patient’s presentation fits this expanding spectrum, where hepatic fibrosis is also becoming a recognized feature that was previously under-recognized.

Developing a practical algorithm for these patients is essential because TBDs are often misdiagnosed as acquired conditions. If you encounter a patient under 40 with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or unexplained cirrhosis, especially if accompanied by cytopenia, you should prioritize genetic evaluation. The gold standard involves measuring telomere length via flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation, but next-generation sequencing panels or whole-exome sequencing are increasingly used to identify specific gene mutations like *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC*. Early recognition is vital not just for diagnosis, but for guiding family genetic counseling and considering bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

Management remains largely supportive, as there is no cure for the underlying genetic defect. This patient received cyanocobalamin for her deficiency, transfusions for symptomatic anemia, and supplementation for osteoporosis. She was referred for genetic counseling and placed on regular surveillance for hepatic and pulmonary function. The key takeaway is that correcting the nutritional deficiencies improved her symptoms, but the underlying genetic predisposition requires long-term monitoring for marrow failure or malignancy.

Here are the critical take-home points for your clinical practice:

*   Consider Telomere Biology Disorders in young adults presenting with unexplained pancytopenia combined with hepatic or pulmonary fibrosis.
*   Do not rely solely on the absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad to rule out TBDs; phenotypes can be variable and subtle.
*   Severe nutritional deficiencies like B12 deficiency can coexist with TBDs and may mask or exacerbate the bone marrow failure phenotype.
*   Genetic testing via telomere length measurement or exome sequencing is indicated when common autoimmune and infectious causes are excluded.
*   Early diagnosis facilitates appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance for malignancy, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.
