Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis in a 20-Year-Old: When 'Idiopathic' Means Genetic

Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) is a disease of the elderly. It is a diagnosis typically reserved for patients over 60, often with a history of smoking or environmental exposure. When you encounter restrictive lung disease and fibrotic changes in a woman in her late 20s, the label "idiopathic" should immediately trigger alarm bells. In the context of young adults, "idiopathic" is often a placeholder for "we haven't looked hard enough for a genetic cause yet." A recent case report highlights a young woman presenting with fatigue, pancytopenia, and multisystem fibrosis, ultimately revealing a hidden genetic etiology that changes management and prognosis.

The patient was a woman in her late 20s who presented with a five-year history of progressive fatigue and weakness that had worsened to the point of requiring assistance with self-care. She also reported exertional dyspnea and weight loss. On examination, she looked older than her years, exhibiting premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue. Physical exam also revealed hepatosplenomegaly and reduced breath sounds in the lung bases. Initial labs showed pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. While the B12 deficiency was treated aggressively, her systemic dysfunction persisted, and further imaging revealed cirrhosis with a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa and diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic changes in the lungs.

The clinical picture was a puzzle. The severe B12 deficiency explained the anemia and neuropathy, but it did not account for the pulmonary fibrosis, the hepatic fibrosis, or the premature aging features like early greying and osteoporosis. Autoimmune and infectious workups were largely negative. The constellation of cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, and pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult shifted the suspicion toward a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD). Clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene. While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the variant affected a highly conserved residue and was clinically consistent with TBD, providing a unifying diagnosis for her multisystem disease.

Telomere biology disorders are rare premature-aging syndromes caused by defects in telomere maintenance. Telomeres protect chromosome ends, and when they become too short, cells undergo senescence or apoptosis. This disproportionately affects tissues with high cell turnover, such as the bone marrow, liver, and lung epithelium. The prototype is Dyskeratosis Congenita, classically defined by a mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. However, as this case illustrates, patients can present with isolated organ fibrosis or bone marrow failure without the classic triad. *PARN* variants are increasingly recognized in this spectrum, with monoallelic mutations often associated with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, while biallelic mutations cause more severe recessive forms.

This case underscores a critical gap in our diagnostic approach to young patients with fibrosis. Hepatic fibrosis in TBD is often under-recognized, with recent data suggesting a high prevalence of liver stiffness abnormalities in patients with telomerase mutations. Furthermore, the presence of a nutritional deficiency like B12 can act as a red herring, masking the underlying genetic susceptibility. In this patient, the B12 deficiency likely exacerbated the bone marrow failure, but the genetic defect was the root cause of the multisystem involvement. Recognizing TBD early is vital for genetic counseling, surveillance for malignancy, and considering bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

### Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

*   **Young Age is the Red Flag:** Do not accept a diagnosis of "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis or cirrhosis in a patient under 40 without a thorough genetic workup.
*   **Look Beyond the Lungs:** Telomere biology disorders are multisystem diseases. Screen for bone marrow failure (pancytopenia), liver fibrosis, and premature aging signs (early greying, nail changes, osteoporosis).
*   **Nutritional Deficiencies Can Mask Genetics:** Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can mimic or exacerbate bone marrow failure in TBD patients, but correction of the deficiency will not resolve the underlying fibrotic or genetic pathology.
*   **The *PARN* Connection:** While *TERT* and *TERC* are the classic culprits, *PARN* mutations are an emerging cause of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, particularly in sporadic cases.
*   **Genetic Counseling is Key:** Early diagnosis allows for family screening and appropriate surveillance for malignancies and organ failure, even when the genetic variant is classified as VUS but clinically concordant.
