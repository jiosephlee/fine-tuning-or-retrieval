Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Dyskeratosis Congenita Without the Triad: Recognizing Atypical Telomere Biology Disorders

You’re working up a young woman in her late 20s with progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, and signs of premature aging. You find severe vitamin B12 deficiency, so you treat it. But the picture doesn’t quite fit. There’s hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. Autoimmune serologies are negative. Viral workup is negative. The reticulocyte count is low, pointing to ineffective erythropoiesis, but the bone marrow isn’t showing malignancy or overt dysplasia.

This is the clinical trap of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs). We often associate these syndromes with the classic triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC): nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin pigmentation. But when those dermatological hallmarks are absent, TBDs can masquerade as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, or unexplained bone marrow failure in young adults.

We recently reviewed a case that highlights exactly this diagnostic challenge. It serves as a reminder that when multisystem failure hits a young person without a clear acquired cause, you need to look at the genome, not just the organ systems.

## The Case: A Puzzle of Fibrosis and Failure

Our patient was a woman in her late 20s presenting with a five-year history of worsening weakness and fatigue. Her functional capacity had declined to the point where she required assistance with self-care. She reported exertional dyspnea, chest discomfort, and weight loss.

On examination, the clues were scattered but significant. She had pallor, icterus, and premature greying of the hair. There was knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae. Abdominal exam revealed hepatosplenomegaly, and respiratory exam showed reduced breath sounds in the infra-scapular regions.

The labs were a storm of abnormalities. She had pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count. The peripheral smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and hypersegmented neutrophils. Serum studies confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. Iron studies showed elevated ferritin, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis.

Here is where the workup got tricky. A direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG, initially raising flags for autoimmune hemolytic anemia. However, the low reticulocyte response and marrow findings argued against peripheral destruction and pointed more toward production failure. An upper endoscopy was macroscopically normal, though a duodenal biopsy showed reduced parietal cells, explaining the B12 malabsorption.

Despite correcting the nutritional deficiencies, the systemic dysfunction remained disproportionate. Imaging revealed the real extent of the disease: transient elastography showed cirrhosis, and CT imaging demonstrated diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic changes in both lung fields. She also had osteoporosis and sensorimotor polyneuropathy.

## The Genetic Connection

Given the constellation of cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, and fibrosis in a young adult, the team suspected a syndromic genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg).

While this variant was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the clinical-genetic correlation supported a diagnosis of a Telomere Biology Disorder. The *PARN* gene is involved in the maturation of telomerase RNA. Germline pathogenic variants in *PARN* are known to cause Dyskeratosis Congenita (autosomal recessive form 6) and telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure.

In this case, the monoallelic variant likely contributed to the phenotype. While monoallelic *PARN* mutations are often associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, this patient presented with a broader spectrum including hepatic fibrosis and marrow failure. The presence of severe B12 deficiency may have acted as a stressor, unmasking the underlying telomere insufficiency in the bone marrow.

## Why This Matters for the Clinician

Telomeres are the protective caps at the ends of chromosomes that shorten with every cell division. In TBDs, defects in telomere maintenance genes lead to abnormally short telomeres, causing cellular senescence or apoptosis. This disproportionately affects actively dividing cells: bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair, and hepatocytes.

The classic teaching is that DC presents with the mucocutaneous triad. However, as this case demonstrates, TBDs can present with isolated organ-specific involvement or a combination of marrow failure and fibrosis without the skin findings. This is particularly true for monoallelic variants in genes like *TERT*, *TERC*, *RTEL1*, and *PARN*.

Missing this diagnosis has significant implications. Patients with TBDs have a high risk of malignancy and may respond poorly to immunosuppressive therapy typically used for aplastic anemia. Furthermore, recognizing the diagnosis allows for appropriate genetic counseling and surveillance. In progressive cases, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be curative for the marrow failure component, but it requires careful screening to avoid transplant-related complications in patients with underlying telomere defects.

Treatment for our patient was supportive. She received intravenous cyanocobalamin, transfusions, and supplementation for osteoporosis. She underwent genetic counseling and continues to be monitored for hepatic and pulmonary progression. While curative options were not feasible in this specific instance, early recognition prevented unnecessary interventions and clarified the prognosis for her family.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Think TBD in Young Adults with Multisystem Fibrosis:** If you see a patient under 50 with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and bone marrow failure, consider a Telomere Biology Disorder even without the classic Dyskeratosis Congenita triad.
*   **Don’t Stop at Nutritional Deficiencies:** Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can coexist with TBDs and may unmask marrow failure, but the severity of systemic symptoms (like fibrosis) often exceeds what is expected from nutrition alone.
*   **Genetic Testing is Key:** Next-generation sequencing or telomere length measurement (flow-FISH) should be considered in cases of unexplained pancytopenia with premature aging features.
*   **Avoid Unnecessary Immunosuppression:** Patients with TBDs may be misdiagnosed with autoimmune conditions; avoid immunosuppressive therapy unless the diagnosis is confirmed, as it may not be effective and carries risks.
*   **Family Counseling is Crucial:** TBDs are hereditary. Once diagnosed, family members should be offered genetic counseling and surveillance for similar organ involvement.



# Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis in a 20-Year-Old: When 'Idiopathic' Means Genetic

Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) is a disease of the elderly. It is a diagnosis typically reserved for patients over 60, often with a history of smoking or environmental exposure. When you encounter restrictive lung disease and fibrotic changes in a woman in her late 20s, the label "idiopathic" should immediately trigger alarm bells. In the context of young adults, "idiopathic" is often a placeholder for "we haven't looked hard enough for a genetic cause yet." A recent case report highlights a young woman presenting with fatigue, pancytopenia, and multisystem fibrosis, ultimately revealing a hidden genetic etiology that changes management and prognosis.

The patient was a woman in her late 20s who presented with a five-year history of progressive fatigue and weakness that had worsened to the point of requiring assistance with self-care. She also reported exertional dyspnea and weight loss. On examination, she looked older than her years, exhibiting premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue. Physical exam also revealed hepatosplenomegaly and reduced breath sounds in the lung bases. Initial labs showed pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. While the B12 deficiency was treated aggressively, her systemic dysfunction persisted, and further imaging revealed cirrhosis with a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa and diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic changes in the lungs.

The clinical picture was a puzzle. The severe B12 deficiency explained the anemia and neuropathy, but it did not account for the pulmonary fibrosis, the hepatic fibrosis, or the premature aging features like early greying and osteoporosis. Autoimmune and infectious workups were largely negative. The constellation of cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, and pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult shifted the suspicion toward a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD). Clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene. While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the variant affected a highly conserved residue and was clinically consistent with TBD, providing a unifying diagnosis for her multisystem disease.

Telomere biology disorders are rare premature-aging syndromes caused by defects in telomere maintenance. Telomeres protect chromosome ends, and when they become too short, cells undergo senescence or apoptosis. This disproportionately affects tissues with high cell turnover, such as the bone marrow, liver, and lung epithelium. The prototype is Dyskeratosis Congenita, classically defined by a mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. However, as this case illustrates, patients can present with isolated organ fibrosis or bone marrow failure without the classic triad. *PARN* variants are increasingly recognized in this spectrum, with monoallelic mutations often associated with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, while biallelic mutations cause more severe recessive forms.

This case underscores a critical gap in our diagnostic approach to young patients with fibrosis. Hepatic fibrosis in TBD is often under-recognized, with recent data suggesting a high prevalence of liver stiffness abnormalities in patients with telomerase mutations. Furthermore, the presence of a nutritional deficiency like B12 can act as a red herring, masking the underlying genetic susceptibility. In this patient, the B12 deficiency likely exacerbated the bone marrow failure, but the genetic defect was the root cause of the multisystem involvement. Recognizing TBD early is vital for genetic counseling, surveillance for malignancy, and considering bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

### Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

*   **Young Age is the Red Flag:** Do not accept a diagnosis of "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis or cirrhosis in a patient under 40 without a thorough genetic workup.
*   **Look Beyond the Lungs:** Telomere biology disorders are multisystem diseases. Screen for bone marrow failure (pancytopenia), liver fibrosis, and premature aging signs (early greying, nail changes, osteoporosis).
*   **Nutritional Deficiencies Can Mask Genetics:** Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can mimic or exacerbate bone marrow failure in TBD patients, but correction of the deficiency will not resolve the underlying fibrotic or genetic pathology.
*   **The *PARN* Connection:** While *TERT* and *TERC* are the classic culprits, *PARN* mutations are an emerging cause of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, particularly in sporadic cases.
*   **Genetic Counseling is Key:** Early diagnosis allows for family screening and appropriate surveillance for malignancies and organ failure, even when the genetic variant is classified as VUS but clinically concordant.



# The B12 Red Herring: Distinguishing Nutritional Marrow Failure from Telomere Disease

We’ve all been there. You’re working up a young patient with fatigue and pancytopenia. The labs come back showing a severe vitamin B12 deficiency. You treat the deficiency, the numbers improve, and you close the chart. But what if the B12 deficiency was just the straw that broke the camel’s back? What if the underlying camel was already broken by a genetic defect in telomere maintenance?

This is the diagnostic tension presented in a recent case of a woman in her late 20s who arrived with a five-year history of progressive weakness and dyspnea. On the surface, her presentation screamed nutritional deficiency. She had pancytopenia, a low reticulocyte count, and a peripheral smear showing dimorphic anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed, and her bone marrow showed ineffective erythropoiesis without malignancy. It seemed like a straightforward case of megaloblastic anemia.

However, the clinical picture refused to stay contained within the boundaries of a nutritional disorder. This patient exhibited signs of premature ageing that had no place in a standard B12 deficiency workup. She had premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, osteoporosis with fractures, and neuropathy. More alarmingly, imaging revealed hepatic fibrosis with cirrhosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. While correcting the B12 deficiency provided symptomatic relief and stabilized her blood counts temporarily, the constellation of multisystem fibrosis and premature ageing features pointed toward a deeper, unifying diagnosis.

This case highlights the critical importance of recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease. TBDs are rare premature-ageing syndromes caused by Mendelian defects in telomere-related genes. These defects lead to abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres, which disproportionately affect actively dividing cells like bone marrow, skin, mucosa, and hepatocytes. The classic prototype is Dyskeratosis Congenita, often defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. Yet, as this case demonstrates, the classical triad may be absent. Instead, patients may present with cryptic phenotypes like isolated bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or hepatic dysfunction.

In this specific patient, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, the variant alters a highly conserved residue and is predicted to be damaging. Germline pathogenic variants in *PARN* are associated with Dyskeratosis Congenita type 6 and telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. The presence of this variant, combined with the clinical phenotype of cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and premature greying, supported the diagnosis of a TBD. It is crucial to note that while the B12 deficiency was real and contributed to the cytopenia, the underlying telomere shortening likely rendered the bone marrow more susceptible to haematopoietic stress.

The management implications of this distinction are profound. If this patient had been treated solely for nutritional deficiency, the progressive nature of the telomere disorder might have been missed until irreversible organ damage occurred. Patients with TBD require specific surveillance for malignancy, monitoring of pulmonary and hepatic function, and genetic counselling for family members. Furthermore, the consideration of bone marrow transplantation changes based on this diagnosis, as outcomes can differ significantly in patients with underlying telomere defects compared to acquired aplastic anaemia. While treatment remains largely supportive, as seen in this case with vitamin supplementation and transfusions, early recognition guides long-term prognosis and family planning.

This case serves as a reminder to look beyond the obvious. When you encounter pancytopenia with features of premature ageing or unexplained organ fibrosis in a young adult, do not stop at the nutritional workup. The B12 deficiency may be the precipitating event, but the telomere biology disorder is the underlying substrate.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Look for the Syndromic Clues:** In young adults with pancytopenia, investigate for features of premature ageing such as premature greying, osteoporosis, or unexplained organ fibrosis (lung or liver) which may suggest a Telomere Biology Disorder.
*   **Nutritional Deficiencies Can Mask Genetic Disease:** Severe nutritional deficiencies like B12 can exacerbate marrow failure in patients with underlying telomere defects; correcting the deficiency may not resolve the underlying susceptibility.
*   **The Classical Triad is Not Required:** Dyskeratosis Congenita and related TBDs can present without the classic mucocutaneous triad, manifesting instead as isolated bone marrow failure or pulmonary fibrosis.
*   **Genetic Testing is Crucial:** Clinical exome sequencing should be considered in cases of unexplained multisystem disease to identify variants in genes like *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC*, even if classified as Variants of Uncertain Significance.
*   **Management Implications:** Early diagnosis of TBD is vital for genetic counselling, surveillance for malignancy, and appropriate consideration of haematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive cases.



# Decoding the VUS: Clinical Correlation in Genetic Testing for Rare Syndromes

We’ve all been there. You order a genetic panel for a patient with a complex, multisystem presentation, and the result comes back as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). The natural instinct is to file it away. It’s not pathogenic, it’s not benign, so it’s likely noise. But in the world of rare genetic syndromes, a VUS might be the smoking gun if you know where to look.

This case highlights a woman in her late 20s who walked into the clinic with fatigue, pancytopenia, and fibrosis in both her liver and lungs. It’s a constellation of findings that screams "premature aging," yet she lacked the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita. When genetic testing returned a monoallelic *PARN* mutation classified as a VUS, the team didn’t discard it. Instead, they used the clinical phenotype to validate the genetic finding, diagnosing a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD).

## The Clinical Picture: Young, Tired, and Fibrotic

Our patient presented with a five-year history of progressive fatigue and weakness that eventually impaired her ability to care for herself. She had exertional dyspnea, weight loss, and anorexia. On exam, the clues were scattered but telling: pallor, icterus, hepatosplenomegaly, and reduced breath sounds.

What really stood out were the signs of accelerated aging. She had premature greying of her hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae, and osteoporosis confirmed by DEXA scan. She also had thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy.

Initial workup was a rabbit hole. She had pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. The peripheral smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Correcting the B12 deficiency helped somewhat, but her systemic dysfunction remained disproportionate to a simple nutritional cause.

Imaging revealed the extent of the damage. Transient elastography showed cirrhosis, and CT scans demonstrated diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic changes in the lungs. There was no evidence of malignancy or granulomatous disease. Autoimmune and infectious workups were largely negative. We were left with a young woman with bone marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis.

## The Genetic Puzzle: When a VUS Isn’t Just Noise

Given the multisystem involvement, clinical exome sequencing was performed. It identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). Under standard ACMG/AMP guidelines, this was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance.

Usually, a VUS is a diagnostic dead end. However, the *PARN* gene is a known player in telomere biology. Germline pathogenic variants in *PARN* are associated with Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6) when biallelic, and telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4) when monoallelic.

The variant in this patient altered a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain and was predicted to be damaging by multiple in silico tools. While the pathogenicity of this specific variant remained uncertain, the clinical-genetic correlation supported the diagnosis of a TBD. The key takeaway here is that the phenotype drove the interpretation of the genotype. The patient’s presentation fit the spectrum of TBD perfectly—cytopenia, fibrosis, and premature aging features—even without the classic nail dystrophy or leukoplakia.

## Connecting the Dots: Telomere Biology Disorders

Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) are rare premature-aging syndromes caused by defects in telomere-related genes. They lead to abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres, affecting actively dividing cells like bone marrow, skin, mucosa, and hepatocytes.

Dyskeratosis Congenita is the prototype, classically defined by the triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. However, TBDs are notorious for variable penetrance. They can present in adulthood without the mucocutaneous triad, manifesting instead as isolated organ fibrosis or marrow failure.

In this case, the *PARN* mutation likely compromised the replicative potential of hematopoietic stem cells and hepatocytes. While the severe B12 deficiency contributed to the cytopenia, the underlying genetic predisposition likely rendered the bone marrow more susceptible to stress. Furthermore, hepatic fibrosis in TBD is often under-recognized; recent data suggests it may be more prevalent than previously appreciated in patients with telomere mutations.

## Management and Prognosis

Treatment for TBD is currently supportive. This patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin for her deficiency, transfusions for symptomatic anemia, and supplementation for osteoporosis and electrolyte imbalances. She also underwent physiotherapy.

Crucially, the family received genetic counseling. While curative options like bone marrow transplantation are considered in progressive cases, the patient and family accepted that curative options were not immediately feasible. She was discharged with a plan for regular surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function. At her two-week follow-up, her blood counts were stable, and her fatigue had improved.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Think TBD in young adults with unexplained fibrosis:** If you see a patient under 50 with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or hepatic fibrosis, especially with cytopenias, consider a Telomere Biology Disorder.
*   **Don’t dismiss the VUS:** A Variant of Uncertain Significance should not be ignored if the clinical phenotype strongly aligns with the gene’s known disease spectrum. Clinical correlation can upgrade a VUS to a working diagnosis.
*   **Look for subtle signs of aging:** Premature greying, osteoporosis, and knuckle hyperpigmentation in a young patient are red flags for genetic premature aging syndromes, even without the classic Dyskeratosis Congenita triad.
*   **Nutritional deficiencies can mask the underlying defect:** Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can mimic marrow failure. If the clinical picture remains disproportionate after correction, dig deeper for a genetic cause.
*   **Surveillance is key:** Patients with TBD require monitoring for malignancy, progression of fibrosis, and bone marrow failure, alongside genetic counseling for family members.



# Therapeutic Pitfalls in Telomere Biology Disorders: HSCT, Androgens, and Avoiding Toxins

You’re looking at a woman in her late 20s. She’s tired, pale, and has a 5-year history of worsening weakness. She has pancytopenia, liver fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. You correct her severe B12 deficiency, but she doesn’t get better. You think autoimmune, you think malignancy, but the workup keeps coming back negative or inconclusive.

This isn’t just "anemia of chronic disease" or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in a young adult. This is a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD).

The case of a young woman presenting with a monoallelic *PARN* mutation highlights a diagnostic and therapeutic minefield. While the classic triad of dyskeratosis congenita (nail dystrophy, leukoplakia, reticular pigmentation) is often the textbook trigger for suspicion, this patient lacked those mucocutaneous features. Instead, she presented with the "silent killers" of TBD: marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, and lung fibrosis.

The diagnosis was confirmed via clinical exome sequencing, identifying a *PARN* missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the clinical-genetic correlation strongly supported a TBD diagnosis. This case forces us to confront the therapeutic realities of TBDs: they are not just about finding the gene; they are about managing the fragility of the patient’s cells without breaking them further.

## The Pathophysiology of Fragility
To understand the treatment pitfalls, you have to understand the cell biology. Telomeres cap the ends of chromosomes, preserving genomic integrity. With every cell division, they shorten. In TBDs, genetic defects (like in *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC*) impair the telomerase complex, leading to premature attrition.

The organs hit hardest are those with high cell turnover. In this case, the bone marrow, liver, and lungs were the primary victims. The patient’s bone marrow showed ineffective erythropoiesis and trilineage haematopoiesis without dysplasia, but the stem cells were fundamentally compromised. The discussion in the case report notes that telomere shortening compromises the replicative potential of haematopoietic stem cells, leading to accelerated senescence. This biological reality dictates that standard therapies for marrow failure or fibrosis can backfire spectacularly if the underlying telomere defect isn’t recognized.

## Therapeutic Considerations and Pitfalls
The management of this patient was primarily supportive: correction of B12 and folate, transfusions for symptomatic anemia, and vitamin D/calcium for osteoporosis. However, the discussion section of the report outlines critical considerations for long-term management that extend beyond simple supplementation.

**Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT)**
For patients with progressive hematologic manifestations, HSCT is the only potentially curative option. In typical aplastic anemia, we might reach for immunosuppression. In TBD, immunosuppression alone often fails because the stem cells are intrinsically defective, not just suppressed. The case report notes that HSCT can be curative in patients with hematologic manifestations. However, the conditioning regimen for HSCT is cytotoxic. In a patient with short telomeres, the standard chemotherapy doses used to prepare for transplant can be fatal or lead to catastrophic graft failure. This requires specialized centers with experience in TBD conditioning protocols.

**The Role of Androgens**
What about medical therapy to stimulate the marrow? The case report discusses the use of androgens like danazol and nandrolone. Preclinical and small clinical studies suggest these agents might induce telomere elongation or stimulate erythropoiesis. However, the long-term effects remain unknown. In a patient with underlying malignancy risk (TBDs carry an increased risk of hematological and solid malignancies), the long-term safety profile of androgens is a significant concern. It is a tool to consider, but one that requires careful risk-benefit analysis.

**Avoiding Cytotoxic Stress**
Perhaps the most critical "negative" pearl is knowing what *not* to do. Because telomere shortening compromises the replicative potential of stem cells, these patients are exquisitely sensitive to cytotoxic stress. Chemotherapy or radiation intended to treat other conditions can precipitate irreversible marrow failure. The case emphasizes that the presence of a *PARN* variant underscores the importance of monitoring for evolving marrow failure, implying that any additional stressor could tip the balance. While the patient in this report did not require chemotherapy, the therapeutic landscape for TBD demands that we avoid unnecessary cytotoxic therapies that could accelerate the inevitable cellular senescence.

## Hepatic and Pulmonary Fibrosis
One of the most surprising aspects of this case was the hepatic involvement. Cirrhosis and hepatic fibrosis in TBD are often under-recognized. The report cites a registry analysis where nearly 90% of adult patients with pathogenic *TERC* or *TERT* mutations had moderate hepatic fibrosis. This patient had a monoallelic *PARN* mutation yet developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. This expands the phenotype beyond just lung and marrow. Clinicians must screen for liver stiffness and pulmonary function in any young patient with unexplained cytopenias, even without the classic skin findings.

## Take-Home Points
*   **Think Genetic in Young Fibrosis:** Consider Telomere Biology Disorders in young adults with unexplained pancytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, or hepatic fibrosis, even if the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is absent.
*   **HSCT is Curative but Risky:** Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is the only curative option for hematologic manifestations, but conditioning regimens must be tailored to avoid lethal toxicity in telomere-deficient stem cells.
*   **Androgens are Unproven:** Danazol or nandrolone may be used to stimulate marrow, but long-term safety and efficacy data are lacking, and malignancy risk must be weighed.
*   **Avoid Cytotoxicity:** Be extremely cautious with chemotherapy or radiation in TBD patients, as their compromised replicative capacity makes them highly susceptible to accelerated marrow failure.
*   **Screen the Liver:** Hepatic fibrosis is common in TBD and often under-recognized; transient elastography or biopsy should be considered in the workup of multisystem TBD.

*   Consider Telomere Biology Disorders in young adults presenting with unexplained pancytopenia combined with hepatic or pulmonary fibrosis.
*   Do not rely solely on the absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad to rule out TBDs; phenotypes can be variable and subtle.
