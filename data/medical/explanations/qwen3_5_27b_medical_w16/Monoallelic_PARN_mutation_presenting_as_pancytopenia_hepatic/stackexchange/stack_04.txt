Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Morning Report] This patient presented with severe vitamin B12 deficiency and pancytopenia. Why was nutritional correction alone insufficient to resolve her clinical picture, and what does this imply about the interaction between acquired stressors and genetic predisposition in this disorder?

Colleagues, the key to understanding this case lies in distinguishing between the acute metabolic trigger and the underlying genetic substrate. Nutritional correction was insufficient to resolve the patient’s full clinical picture because while vitamin B12 deficiency accounted for the ineffective erythropoiesis and pancytopenia, it did not address the multisystem manifestations of the underlying Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD).

As noted in the case report, the patient presented with severe B12 deficiency, which caused megaloblastic changes and cytopenia. However, the persistence of hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis despite nutritional repletion signaled a broader pathophysiology. The identified monoallelic *PARN* mutation impairs telomere maintenance, leading to abnormally short telomeres and reduced replicative potential in actively dividing tissues, including bone marrow, hepatocytes, and pneumocytes.

This case illustrates a critical interaction between acquired stressors and genetic predisposition. The genetic defect created a baseline vulnerability—shortened telomeres and compromised stem cell reserves—that lowered the threshold for organ failure. The acquired stressor, in this instance severe vitamin B12 deficiency, acted as a precipitating event that unmasked the underlying TBD. As the discussion highlights, the presence of a *PARN* variant may render the bone marrow more susceptible to hematopoietic stress.

Therefore, while correcting the B12 deficiency stabilized her acute counts, it could not reverse the telomere attrition or the established fibrotic changes. This implies that in patients with unexplained multisystem disease, particularly involving fibrosis and cytopenia, one must consider that acquired deficiencies may be "unmasking" a genetic disorder. Management requires not only correcting the nutritional deficit but also long-term surveillance for progressive bone marrow failure and organ fibrosis, as the genetic predisposition remains.
