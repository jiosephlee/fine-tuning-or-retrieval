Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Telomere Biology Disorders: Pathophysiology and Clinical Spectrum

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences located at the ends of chromosomes that protect genomic integrity from degradation during cell division. Under physiological conditions, telomeres shorten with each replication cycle until they reach a critical length, triggering cellular senescence or apoptosis. To counteract this attrition, the enzyme telomerase elongates telomeres; this ribonucleoprotein complex includes the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC). In the context of the presented case, the *PARN* gene plays a critical regulatory role in this system. *PARN* encodes an exoribonuclease required for the removal of oligo(A) tails from nuclear RNAs, a process essential for the 3'-end maturation of TERC. Consequently, pathogenic variants in *PARN* impair telomerase function, leading to premature telomere attrition. Understanding this molecular mechanism is fundamental to recognizing why defects in telomere maintenance result in the multisystem failure observed in the patient.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are a group of rare, premature-ageing syndromes caused by Mendelian defects in telomere-related genes. These disorders are characterized by abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres, which disproportionately affect tissues with high cellular turnover. The clinical spectrum is broad, ranging from severe pediatric presentations to adult-onset conditions. Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is the prototype TBD and is classically defined by a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. In many cases, DC is also associated with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). However, as illustrated by the case report, patients may present without the classical triad. The patient in this report exhibited premature greying and hepatosplenomegaly but lacked the hallmark mucocutaneous features, highlighting that TBDs can manifest subtly in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease.

Beyond classic DC, the TBD spectrum includes severe childhood-onset phenotypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, which typically involve more profound developmental and neurological deficits. In contrast, adult presentations often mimic acquired conditions, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or cryptic cirrhosis. The case patient presented with pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis in her late 20s, a demographic where these findings are atypical for common acquired etiologies. This underscores the importance of considering a genetic etiology when fibrosis and marrow failure co-occur in young patients, particularly when standard workups for autoimmune or infectious causes are negative.

The pathophysiology of organ involvement in TBDs is driven by the susceptibility of rapidly dividing cells to telomere shortening. Hematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, and lung epithelial cells require frequent replication and are therefore most vulnerable to replicative senescence caused by defective telomere maintenance. In the presented case, this vulnerability explains the constellation of pancytopenia due to bone marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis resulting from hepatocyte dysfunction, and pulmonary fibrosis. The patient’s bone marrow examination showed trilineage haematopoiesis with ineffective erythropoiesis, and imaging confirmed diffuse fibrotic changes in the lungs and liver. Recognizing this pattern of multiorgan involvement involving high-turnover tissues is a key diagnostic clue for TBDs.

Genetic variability and environmental factors contribute to the complex clinical picture of TBDs, including variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance. While biallelic variants in *PARN* are associated with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), monoallelic variants are more frequently linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). The patient in this case harbored a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Despite the uncertainty of the specific variant’s pathogenicity, the clinical correlation supported a diagnosis of TBD. This highlights that monoallelic mutations can lead to significant disease phenotypes through mechanisms such as haploinsufficiency, even without a family history or classical genetic markers. Clinicians must therefore maintain a high index of suspicion for TBDs in young adults with unexplained fibrosis and cytopenia, as early recognition guides genetic counselling, surveillance, and potential consideration of bone marrow transplantation.

## Clinical Presentation: Multisystem Manifestations in a Young Adult

The clinical picture of this patient was defined by a chronic, progressive decline in functional capacity that began in her mid-twenties and worsened over five years. The history of present illness revealed generalized weakness and early fatigability that eventually impaired her ability to perform activities of daily living, necessitating assistance for self-care. Concurrently, she developed exertional dyspnea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia. In a young adult, such a prolonged history of progressive multisystem failure is atypical for common acquired conditions and warrants a broad differential diagnosis that includes hematologic, autoimmune, infectious, and genetic etiologies. The trajectory of her illness, characterized by a slow onset followed by an acceleration of symptoms in the final three months, suggested an underlying pathophysiology affecting high-turnover tissues rather than an acute inflammatory or infectious process.

Physical examination provided critical clues pointing toward a syndromic etiology. While the patient exhibited signs consistent with anemia and liver dysfunction, such as pallor and icterus, she also displayed features suggestive of premature aging. These included premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with atrophy of the lingual papillae. These mucocutaneous findings are often associated with telomere biology disorders, even in the absence of the classic triad of dyskeratosis congenita. Abdominal examination revealed significant hepatomegaly, extending 5 cm below the right costal margin, while respiratory examination noted reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scapular regions, hinting at underlying parenchymal lung disease. Musculoskeletal assessment identified thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, further indicating systemic involvement beyond a single organ system.

Hematological evaluation was central to the diagnostic workup, revealing pancytopenia with a markedly low reticulocyte count of 0.39%. Peripheral blood smear analysis demonstrated dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, findings typically suggestive of ineffective erythropoiesis. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed, which initially provided a plausible explanation for the anemia and neuropathy. However, the bone marrow examination showed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, without evidence of malignancy or dysplasia. Although the direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG, the low reticulocyte response and marrow findings were more consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis than peripheral immune-mediated destruction. This hematological profile indicated a marrow that was attempting to compensate for peripheral loss or destruction but was failing to produce adequate functional cells, a pattern often seen in marrow failure syndromes.

Organ involvement extended beyond the hematopoietic system, presenting with fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs. Abdominal ultrasound confirmed hepatosplenomegaly, and transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis and cirrhosis with a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Autoimmune and infectious workups for hepatic dysfunction were largely negative, leaving the etiology of the fibrosis unexplained by common causes. Similarly, positron emission tomography–CT imaging of the chest revealed diffuse ground-glass haziness with multiple fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes in both lung fields. In a patient in her late twenties, the presence of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic cirrhosis is highly unusual and strongly suggests a genetic predisposition affecting cellular replicative capacity.

Additional systemic findings included secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte disturbances, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis confirmed on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which explained her recurrent bone pain and fractures. Electromyography and nerve conduction studies further revealed asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes. The clinical reasoning in this case hinged on the discrepancy between the severity of the systemic symptoms and the initial nutritional findings. While the severe vitamin B12 deficiency accounted for the macrocytic anemia and neuropathy, the constellation of pancytopenia, premature greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic involvement of the liver and lungs was disproportionate to a nutritional deficiency alone. This mismatch, particularly the presence of fibrosis and premature aging signs in a young adult, shifted the clinical suspicion toward a cryptic inherited disorder, specifically a telomere biology disorder, necessitating genetic sequencing for confirmation.

## Diagnostic Odyssey: From Nutritional Deficiency to Genetic Etiology

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the critical importance of systematic exclusion and the recognition of syndromic patterns when common etiologies fail to explain the full clinical picture. The patient presented with a complex constellation of pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, premature greying, and organ fibrosis. In a young adult, the initial differential diagnosis prioritized acquired conditions, including nutritional deficiencies, autoimmune disorders, infectious etiologies, and malignancies. While severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency was confirmed early in the workup, explaining the dimorphic anemia and neuropathy, the persistence of systemic dysfunction despite nutritional correction signaled an underlying pathology beyond simple malabsorption. This discrepancy between the severity of the clinical phenotype and the identified nutritional deficit was the first pivot point toward a genetic etiology.

To further refine the differential, the clinical team systematically evaluated for autoimmune and infiltrative diseases. Autoimmune cytopenias and connective tissue diseases were considered given the pancytopenia and inflammatory markers; however, serologies for antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis were negative. Although the direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG, the low reticulocyte count and bone marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral destruction argued against primary autoimmune hemolysis. Similarly, the possibility of occult malignancy or granulomatous disease was investigated using positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT). While the scan revealed inhomogeneous bone marrow uptake and pulmonary fibrosis, it explicitly excluded overt malignancy or granulomatous pathology. The exclusion of these common acquired causes, combined with the presence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis in a patient in her late 20s, strongly suggested a cryptic inherited disorder.

The bone marrow biopsy played a pivotal role in narrowing the diagnostic scope by characterizing the hematopoietic failure. The examination revealed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, but critically, it showed no evidence of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative disease. Cytogenetic analysis was also normal. These findings effectively ruled out primary bone marrow failure syndromes such as myelodysplastic syndrome or leukemia, which typically present with dysplastic features or clonal abnormalities. Instead, the marrow morphology supported a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis, consistent with the identified vitamin deficiencies, but the lack of response to treatment and the multisystem involvement indicated a broader defect in cellular replication or maintenance.

Given the unexplained multisystem disease involving the bone marrow, liver, lungs, and integumentary system, clinical exome sequencing was indicated. In clinical practice, genetic testing is warranted when a patient presents with a constellation of findings that do not fit a single acquired disease model, particularly when premature ageing features like osteoporosis and premature greying accompany organ fibrosis. In this case, the sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). The decision to proceed with exome sequencing was driven by the failure of standard management to resolve the systemic decline and the recognition of a phenotype overlapping with telomere biology disorders (TBDs), which can mimic acquired conditions but lack the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita.

Interpreting genetic results requires careful clinical correlation, particularly when a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) is identified. In this case, the *PARN* variant was classified as a VUS because its specific pathogenicity could not be definitively ascertained. However, the clinical team utilized ACMG/AMP standards to evaluate the variant's potential relevance. The substitution occurred within a critical functional domain (PM1), was absent from population databases (PM2), and was predicted to be damaging by multiple computational tools (PP3). Furthermore, *PARN* is a gene with a low rate of benign missense variation (PP2). Despite the VUS classification, the strong clinical–genetic correlation—where the patient’s features of bone marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis align with the known spectrum of *PARN*-associated TBDs—supported the diagnosis. This case underscores that a VUS should not be dismissed if the clinical phenotype is highly suggestive, and it highlights the necessity of integrating genetic data with comprehensive phenotypic assessment to guide management and genetic counselling.

## Molecular Mechanisms: The Role of PARN in Telomere Maintenance

The identification of a monoallelic *PARN* mutation in this patient provides the molecular basis for her multisystem presentation, linking her clinical features to a telomere biology disorder (TBD). To understand this diagnosis, one must first appreciate the function of the *PARN* gene within the context of telomere maintenance. Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences that cap chromosome ends, protecting genomic integrity during cell division. Under physiological conditions, the telomerase complex, comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (*TERT*) and telomerase RNA component (*TERC*), maintains telomere length. The *PARN* gene encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease, an exoribonuclease that regulates gene expression by shortening mRNA poly(A) tails through deadenylation. Crucially, *PARN* is required for the removal of post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails from the *TERC* RNA 3-prime terminal. This process is essential for the 3-prime-end maturation of *TERC* and the subsequent assembly of a functional telomerase complex. When *PARN* function is impaired, telomerase activity is compromised, leading to premature telomere attrition. This molecular defect results in accelerated cellular senescence or apoptosis, particularly in actively dividing cell populations such as bone marrow, hepatocytes, and lung epithelium, which explains the patient’s pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis.

The clinical phenotype associated with *PARN* mutations is influenced significantly by the zygosity of the variant. Germline pathogenic variants in *PARN* are recognized causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), typically resulting from biallelic variants that cause severe childhood-onset phenotypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. In contrast, monoallelic germline pathogenic variants are more frequently implicated in telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). While monoallelic *PARN* mutations are commonly associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the clinical spectrum is expanding. This patient presented with a monoallelic variant yet exhibited a broader constellation of symptoms including pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and premature ageing features. This case illustrates that monoallelic *PARN* mutations can manifest with multisystem involvement beyond isolated pulmonary fibrosis, potentially rendering the bone marrow more susceptible to haematopoietic stress, such as the severe vitamin B12 deficiency observed in this patient.

The specific genetic finding in this case was a monoallelic missense variant in *PARN* (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This variant alters a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain, a critical functional region of the protein. Although classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) because the role of this specific variant in disease could not be definitively ascertained, multiple lines of evidence support its pathogenic potential. In silico missense prediction tools, including LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT, predicted a deleterious functional effect. Furthermore, the variant meets several ACMG/AMP supportive criteria for pathogenicity: it occurs within a critical functional domain with no known benign variation (PM1), is absent or extremely rare in population databases (PM2), is predicted to be damaging by computational tools (PP3), and occurs in a gene with a low rate of benign missense variation (PP2). These findings are consistent with other reported *PARN* missense variants, such as p.Lys59Arg and p.Ala383Val, which are classified as likely pathogenic in clinical databases.

Ultimately, the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder in this patient relied on a strong clinical–genetic correlation rather than a definitively pathogenic genetic classification alone. The gold standard for diagnosing suspected TBDs includes genetic testing via next-generation sequencing and flow cytometry-based assays to measure telomere length. In this case, the clinical exome sequencing identified the *PARN* variant, which, when viewed alongside the patient’s unexplained multisystem fibrosis, cytopenia, and premature ageing, supported the diagnosis despite the VUS classification. This underscores a critical principle for medical trainers: genetic variants must be interpreted within the context of the patient’s phenotype. The presence of the *PARN* variant provided a unifying explanation for the patient’s condition, guiding genetic counselling and surveillance for progressive organ involvement. It highlights that early recognition of TBDs is crucial, even in young adults lacking classical mucocutaneous features, to facilitate appropriate monitoring and consideration of interventions such as bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

## Clinical Management, Prognosis, and Lessons for Practice

The management of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is primarily supportive, focusing on treating specific organ involvement and mitigating complications while monitoring for disease progression. In this case, immediate therapeutic priorities addressed reversible contributors to the patient’s cytopenia and systemic symptoms. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency were aggressively corrected with intravenous cyanocobalamin and oral folic acid, acknowledging that nutritional deficits can exacerbate marrow failure in genetically predisposed individuals. Symptomatic anaemia was managed with whole blood transfusions, while osteoporosis and electrolyte disturbances were treated with vitamin D, calcium, and standard supplementation. This approach underscores a critical principle in TBD management: distinguishing between reversible metabolic stressors and the underlying genetic defect is essential, as correcting deficiencies may improve symptoms without altering the natural history of the telomere attrition itself.

Long-term prognosis and safety depend on rigorous surveillance protocols tailored to the affected organ systems. Given the patient’s presentation with hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and pancytopenia, ongoing monitoring must include regular assessment of liver stiffness via elastography, pulmonary function tests, and complete blood counts to detect evolving bone marrow failure. The case highlights that hepatic fibrosis in TBD is often under-recognized; therefore, liver function surveillance is as critical as pulmonary monitoring in adults with these mutations. While the patient initially responded well to supportive care with stable blood counts and improved fatigue at two weeks, the progressive nature of telomere shortening necessitates lifelong follow-up. In cases where bone marrow failure becomes severe or refractory, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only potentially curative option, though it carries significant risks and requires careful patient selection.

Genetic counseling is a cornerstone of care for patients with confirmed or suspected TBDs, particularly when variants of uncertain significance (VUS) are identified in the context of a compatible clinical phenotype. In this case, the identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant prompted counseling for the patient and her family regarding inheritance risks and reproductive options. Although the specific variant was classified as a VUS, the clinical correlation supported a diagnosis of TBD, emphasizing that genetic counseling should not be withheld while variant pathogenicity is being resolved. Families must be educated on the variable penetrance and inheritance patterns, which can range from autosomal recessive to dominant with reduced penetrance, to inform future screening of at-risk relatives.

This case provides vital clinical lessons regarding the recognition of TBDs in non-classical presentations. Medical trainees should maintain a high index of suspicion for TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem disease, particularly the combination of cytopenia and organ fibrosis, even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. Premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy, as seen in this patient, are subtle but important red flags that suggest a systemic premature-ageing syndrome rather than isolated organ pathology. Relying solely on the presence of nail dystrophy or reticular skin pigmentation may lead to delayed diagnosis and missed opportunities for early intervention.

Finally, the complexity of TBDs necessitates a multidisciplinary care model involving hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, and genetics. The patient’s management required coordination between specialists to address pancytopenia, liver cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and bone health simultaneously. This collaborative approach ensures that treatment for one system does not adversely affect another and facilitates comprehensive surveillance. By integrating genetic diagnosis with organ-specific management, clinicians can optimize outcomes for patients with these rare disorders, ensuring that supportive care is timely and that curative interventions are considered at the appropriate stage of disease progression.
