Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult presenting with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, which genetic syndrome should be suspected even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation?

In a young adult presenting with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, you should suspect a **Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD)**, specifically within the **Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC)** spectrum. 

As demonstrated in this case, a woman in her late 20s presented with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. While DC is classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, this patient lacked the complete triad, instead presenting with premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. Pathophysiologically, defects in telomere maintenance genes—such as the monoallelic *PARN* missense variant identified in this patient—lead to premature telomere attrition. This disproportionately affects rapidly dividing tissues, resulting in the failure of high-turnover organs like the bone marrow and lungs. 

The diagnostic standard involves genetic testing, such as clinical exome sequencing or flow cytometry-based telomere length measurement, particularly when common acquired causes are excluded. This case underscores that TBDs can present with isolated organ fibrosis or marrow failure in adulthood without the hallmark skin findings. Consequently, a high index of suspicion for TBD is warranted in young patients with unexplained multisystem fibrosis or cytopenias to guide appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the fundamental cellular mechanism linking pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes to the premature ageing and organ failure observed in telomere biology disorders?

The fundamental mechanism is **premature telomere attrition leading to replicative senescence in high-turnover tissues.**

Telomeres preserve genomic integrity by shielding chromosome ends from degradation. Under physiological conditions, the enzyme telomerase maintains telomere length through elongation. However, pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes impair this function. In this case, the *PARN* gene encodes an exoribonuclease required for the 3-prime-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). The patient’s monoallelic *PARN* variant disrupts this maturation, compromising telomerase activity and leading to abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres.

As cells divide, telomeres shorten until they reach a critical length that triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis. This loss of replicative capacity predominantly affects actively dividing cell groups, such as bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair, and hepatocytes. Consequently, the patient presented with pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, and premature greying. The multisystem organ failure observed in telomere biology disorders, therefore, results from the inability of high-turnover tissues to sustain replication due to genetically driven telomere shortening.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the clinical phenotype and inheritance pattern of monoallelic PARN variants typically differ from biallelic PARN variants in the spectrum of telomere biology disorders?

In the spectrum of telomere biology disorders (TBDs), the clinical phenotype and inheritance pattern associated with *PARN* variants are critically dependent on zygosity. As outlined in our case report, biallelic *PARN* variants typically follow an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern and are associated with Dyskeratosis Congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), or severe childhood-onset syndromes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome. In contrast, monoallelic *PARN* variants are more often implicated in telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4), frequently presenting in adults with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematological manifestations.

Pathophysiologically, *PARN* encodes an exoribonuclease required for the 3-prime-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC) by removing post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails. Loss of function leads to premature telomere attrition, affecting actively dividing cells such as those in the bone marrow, liver, and lungs. While the typical monoallelic phenotype is dominated by pulmonary fibrosis, our patient illustrates the expanding spectrum of *PARN*-associated TBDs. Despite harboring only a monoallelic missense variant, she presented with a multisystem phenotype including pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. This underscores that while zygosity generally dictates the clinical syndrome, variable penetrance and environmental factors can result in overlapping features, necessitating a high index of suspicion for TBDs in young adults with unexplained multisystem fibrosis and cytopenia.

### [Morning Report] This patient presented with severe vitamin B12 deficiency and pancytopenia. Why was nutritional correction alone insufficient to resolve her clinical picture, and what does this imply about the interaction between acquired stressors and genetic predisposition in this disorder?

Colleagues, the key to understanding this case lies in distinguishing between the acute metabolic trigger and the underlying genetic substrate. Nutritional correction was insufficient to resolve the patient’s full clinical picture because while vitamin B12 deficiency accounted for the ineffective erythropoiesis and pancytopenia, it did not address the multisystem manifestations of the underlying Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD).

As noted in the case report, the patient presented with severe B12 deficiency, which caused megaloblastic changes and cytopenia. However, the persistence of hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis despite nutritional repletion signaled a broader pathophysiology. The identified monoallelic *PARN* mutation impairs telomere maintenance, leading to abnormally short telomeres and reduced replicative potential in actively dividing tissues, including bone marrow, hepatocytes, and pneumocytes.

This case illustrates a critical interaction between acquired stressors and genetic predisposition. The genetic defect created a baseline vulnerability—shortened telomeres and compromised stem cell reserves—that lowered the threshold for organ failure. The acquired stressor, in this instance severe vitamin B12 deficiency, acted as a precipitating event that unmasked the underlying TBD. As the discussion highlights, the presence of a *PARN* variant may render the bone marrow more susceptible to hematopoietic stress.

Therefore, while correcting the B12 deficiency stabilized her acute counts, it could not reverse the telomere attrition or the established fibrotic changes. This implies that in patients with unexplained multisystem disease, particularly involving fibrosis and cytopenia, one must consider that acquired deficiencies may be "unmasking" a genetic disorder. Management requires not only correcting the nutritional deficit but also long-term surveillance for progressive bone marrow failure and organ fibrosis, as the genetic predisposition remains.

### [Clinical Pearls] Hepatic fibrosis is an emerging feature of telomere biology disorders. According to registry data cited in the discussion, what percentage of adult patients with pathogenic TERT or TERC mutations demonstrated at least moderate hepatic fibrosis?

According to the discussion, a retrospective analysis from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry found that **88.8%** of adult patients with pathogenic TERT or TERC mutations demonstrated at least moderate hepatic fibrosis.

This data underscores that hepatic fibrosis is a prevalent, yet frequently under-recognized, manifestation of telomere biology disorders. While our patient presented with a monoallelic PARN mutation, the high prevalence of fibrosis in TERT and TERC carriers supports the broader clinical principle that hepatic involvement should be actively evaluated in adults with suspected telomere biology disorders, particularly when presenting with multisystem disease such as cytopenias or pulmonary fibrosis, even in the absence of classical mucocutaneous features.

### [Management] What is the primary approach to treatment for telomere biology disorders, and under what specific clinical circumstances is haematopoietic stem cell transplantation considered?

The primary approach to treatment for telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is supportive care, tailored to symptom severity and specific organ involvement. In this case, management focused on correcting severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies, providing transfusion support for symptomatic anaemia, and implementing surveillance for hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis.

Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is considered a potentially curative intervention, but it is specifically indicated for patients with progressive haematologic manifestations, such as bone marrow failure. Although our patient presented with pancytopenia, her condition was managed supportively because the cytopenia was attributed to nutritional deficiency rather than primary marrow failure at that stage. However, the discussion highlights that HSCT should be evaluated in progressive cases with haematologic involvement, as telomere shortening compromises the replicative potential of haematopoietic stem cells. Thus, while supportive care remains the mainstay, early recognition of TBD is crucial to guide genetic counselling and timely consideration of transplantation in those with evolving marrow failure.

### [Management] Given the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder, which specific malignancies and organ systems require lifelong surveillance due to increased risk?

The diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) necessitates lifelong surveillance for haematological and solid malignancies, as well as close monitoring of the bone marrow, liver, and pulmonary systems.

This management strategy is grounded in the pathophysiology of TBDs, which are caused by Mendelian defects leading to abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres. As described in the background of this case, actively dividing cells are predominantly affected, including bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair, and hepatocytes. Premature telomere attrition in these tissues triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis, predisposing patients to organ failure and neoplastic transformation.

In our case, the patient presented with a constellation of findings reflecting this multisystem vulnerability: pancytopenia indicating bone marrow involvement, hepatic fibrosis progressing to cirrhosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. The discussion section explicitly notes that recognised manifestations of TBD include an increased risk of haematological and solid malignancies. Consequently, the patient’s follow-up plan was tailored to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement, alongside regular surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function.

For the clinician, the takeaway is that early recognition of TBD—particularly in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease—is crucial not only for genetic counselling but for instituting this specific, lifelong surveillance protocol to detect malignancy and organ failure early.

### [Clinical Pearls] Beyond next-generation sequencing, what is the gold standard functional assay used to quantify telomere length and support the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder?

The gold standard functional assay used to quantify telomere length, complementing next-generation sequencing, is flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation (Flow-FISH).

As detailed in the discussion of this case, telomere biology disorders (TBDs) arise from Mendelian defects in telomere-related genes that lead to abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres. Under physiological conditions, telomerase maintains telomere length; however, pathogenic variants impair this function, causing premature telomere attrition. This shortening triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis, predominantly affecting actively dividing cells such as those in the bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair, and hepatocytes.

In this patient, the clinical presentation of pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult raised suspicion for a TBD despite the absence of the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. While clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic *PARN* variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the discussion explicitly notes that the gold standard for diagnosing suspected TBD includes both genetic testing and Flow-FISH assays to measure telomere length. This functional assay is critical for quantifying telomere attrition and supporting the diagnosis, particularly when genetic findings are ambiguous or when the phenotype involves multisystem fibrosis and marrow failure consistent with the spectrum of TBD.
