Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] In the context of cardiac surgery, what specific exposure history explains the development of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) symptoms prior to the typical postoperative day 5–10 window?

In the context of cardiac surgery, the development of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) symptoms prior to the typical postoperative day 5–10 window is explained by **recent preoperative heparin exposure**.

While classic HIT typically manifests between postoperative days 5 and 10, approximately 10% of patients with post-cardiac surgery HIT present with a platelet count fall before day 5. As illustrated in this case, this early onset is linked to prior sensitization from heparin administered during a preoperative procedure. Specifically, this patient underwent diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to his surgery, during which he received 2,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). This initial exposure primed the immune system to form antibodies against the heparin–platelet factor 4 complex. Consequently, upon re-exposure to heparin during cardiopulmonary bypass or subsequent postoperative thromboprophylaxis, the patient mounted a rapid anamnestic antibody response, precipitating thrombocytopenia and thrombosis earlier than the conventional timeline.

Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for HIT even in the early postoperative period if there is a history of recent heparin exposure, such as a catheterization or prior surgery within the preceding month. In this case, recognizing the preoperative catheterization as the sensitizing event was critical to diagnosing autoimmune HIT (aHIT) and initiating appropriate non-heparin anticoagulation.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the serotonin-release assay (SRA) profile of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) differ from classic HIT regarding platelet activation in the absence of added heparin?

The critical distinction lies in the requirement of heparin to trigger platelet activation. In classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), the serotonin-release assay (SRA) demonstrates platelet activation only when heparin is present to facilitate the formation of immune complexes between PF4, heparin, and IgG antibodies. Conversely, in autoimmune HIT (aHIT), the antibodies possess heparin-independent platelet-activating properties.

In this case, the patient’s SRA profile revealed strong serotonin release (96%) in the complete absence of unfractionated heparin (0 U/mL), which is significantly above the reference range of less than 20%. The report explicitly notes that this finding is a characteristic feature of aHIT but is not observed in classic heparin-dependent HIT. While both conditions typically show a lack of activation at supratherapeutic heparin concentrations (100 U/mL) due to the inability to form immune complexes, the presence of significant serotonin release without added heparin confirms the diagnosis of aHIT. This heparin-independent activation explains the patient’s persistent thrombocytopenia and thrombotic risk despite the cessation of heparin exposure.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which initial laboratory findings in this case helped exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) as the primary cause of thrombocytopenia despite the later appearance of schistocytes?

The key initial laboratory findings that helped exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) were the elevated haptoglobin level and the absence of schistocytes on the initial peripheral blood film. TTP is a thrombotic microangiopathy characterized by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia; therefore, one would typically expect low haptoglobin due to hemoglobin binding and the presence of circulating red cell fragments early in the clinical course.

In this case, the patient’s haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), consistent with the absence of overt hemolysis, and the review of the postoperative day 7 peripheral blood film confirmed thrombocytopenia without red cell fragments. Although schistocytes did appear later (postoperative days 9 to 16), the case report attributes this to severe, progressive HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy rather than primary TTP. Recognizing the initial lack of hemolytic markers was essential to prioritize heparin-induced thrombocytopenia over TTP in the differential diagnosis.

### [Management] Why was the anticoagulant switched from argatroban to fondaparinux in this patient despite initial use of a direct thrombin inhibitor?

The switch from argatroban to fondaparinux in this patient was primarily driven by laboratory evidence of persistent, uncontrolled hypercoagulability despite therapeutic anticoagulation with the direct thrombin inhibitor.

Although argatroban was initially selected for its short half-life in the setting of a recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia, the patient demonstrated signs of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy during therapy. Specifically, you will note a progressive decrease in fibrinogen levels, rising d-dimer levels, and the new appearance of red cell fragments (schistocytes) on peripheral blood film between postoperative days 9 and 16. Furthermore, the platelet count showed only a modest increase following high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, which is atypical for autoimmune HIT (aHIT) and suggested insufficient control of the underlying immune-mediated thrombosis.

Consequently, the team transitioned to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. While ease of administration (subcutaneous once daily versus intravenous PTT-adjusted infusion) was a secondary consideration, the clinical imperative was to achieve better control of the HIT-associated hypercoagulability. Importantly, because fondaparinux is an indirect antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, the team supplemented antithrombin concentrate prior to initiation, as the patient’s baseline antithrombin level was mildly reduced. This adjustment was critical to ensuring the efficacy of the new agent. The switch ultimately resulted in platelet recovery and resolution of the neurological deficits.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the clinical significance of a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA paired with an 'indeterminate' or atypical serotonin-release assay result in a patient with suspected HIT?

This laboratory profile is highly suggestive of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT), a severe variant of HIT that requires aggressive management. In this case, the "indeterminate" serotonin-release assay (SRA) result did not rule out HIT; rather, it revealed the specific pathophysiology of aHIT.

While the strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA indicated the presence of antibodies, the SRA demonstrated heparin-independent platelet activation. Specifically, the patient’s serum caused strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL) and showed partial resistance to inhibition by Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibodies. This atypical serological profile distinguishes aHIT from classic heparin-dependent HIT and correlates with a more severe clinical course, as evidenced by this patient’s profound thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10⁹/L) and thrombotic stroke.

Therefore, when a strongly positive ELISA is paired with an indeterminate SRA in a patient with clinical features of HIT, do not dismiss the diagnosis. Instead, consider aHIT, which may present with heparin-independent activation and requires prompt treatment with non-heparin anticoagulants, such as argatroban or fondaparinux, as demonstrated in this patient's successful recovery.

### [Pathophysiology] What unique serological feature regarding Fc receptor blockade was observed in the serotonin-release assay of this patient, and what does it suggest about the reactivity of their HIT antibodies?

In this case, the unique serological feature observed in the serotonin-release assay (SRA) was the **partial resistance of platelet activation to Fc receptor blockade**.

Typically, in classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), the addition of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) completely inhibits antibody-mediated platelet activation, reducing serotonin release to less than 20%. However, in this patient, the addition of IV.3 failed to fully inhibit the reaction; **76% serotonin release persisted** at 0.1 U/mL UFH in the presence of the blocking antibody.

This atypical profile suggests that the patient’s HIT antibodies were **unusually reactive** and possessed heparin-independent platelet-activating properties, consistent with the diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). While classic HIT relies on immune complex formation requiring heparin and subsequent Fc receptor clustering, this patient’s antibodies activated platelets even in the absence of heparin (96% release at 0 U/mL) and demonstrated a serological phenotype where the Fc receptor pathway could not be fully blocked by standard monoclonal intervention. Recognizing this partial resistance is critical, as it distinguishes this severe form of aHIT from classic HIT and may explain the profound thrombocytopenia and resistance to standard therapies observed in this patient.

### [Management] What is the role of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in the management of autoimmune HIT, and how did this patient's platelet response compare to the typical expectation?

High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is utilized in autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) as an adjunct to anticoagulation to neutralize pathogenic antibodies and accelerate platelet recovery. In typical cases of aHIT, we expect a brisk correction of thrombocytopenia following IVIG administration. In this patient, however, the response was markedly attenuated. Despite receiving 100 g of IVIG on three consecutive days, his platelet count increased only modestly from a nadir of 13×10⁹/L to 54×10⁹/L over seven days, a rise significantly lower than the usual expectation for aHIT.

This blunted response aligned with his unique serological profile, which demonstrated partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade in vitro, indicating an unusually severe form of the disease. Consequently, despite IVIG therapy, persisting hypercoagulability necessitated a switch from argatroban to fondaparinux. This case illustrates that while IVIG is standard in aHIT, clinical response varies, and severe phenotypes may require intensified anticoagulant management despite immunomodulatory therapy.

### [Morning Report] How does the serological profile of HIT antibodies typically evolve during the recovery phase of autoimmune HIT, as evidenced by the patient's follow-up testing?

In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), the serological profile typically evolves from heparin-independent platelet activation toward a heparin-dependent pattern as the disease resolves. In this patient, the initial serotonin-release assay (SRA) demonstrated strong platelet activation in the absence of heparin (96% release at 0 U/mL), confirming the autoimmune nature of the antibodies. However, follow-up testing at postoperative day 124, after platelet count recovery, revealed a distinct shift: serotonin release was minimal without heparin (15%) but robust with heparin (100% at 0.3 U/mL). 

This transition reflects the waning of the specific heparin-independent aHIT antibodies, leaving a residual profile characteristic of classic heparin-dependent HIT. Therefore, persistent antibody positivity months after recovery does not necessarily indicate active disease; rather, the evolution from heparin-independent to heparin-dependent reactivity signifies the resolution of the autoimmune phenotype. Clinicians must interpret follow-up serology in the context of this potential phenotypic shift to avoid misdiagnosing residual antibodies as active thrombocytopenia.
