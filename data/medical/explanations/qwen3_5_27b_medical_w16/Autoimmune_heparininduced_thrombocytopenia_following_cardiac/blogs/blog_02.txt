Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# When the SRA Doesn't Make Sense: Decoding Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

We all know the HIT algorithm. You see a platelet drop, you calculate the 4Ts, you order the PF4 ELISA, and if that’s positive, you send off for the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) to confirm. Usually, the SRA gives a clear answer: either the patient’s serum activates platelets in the presence of heparin, or it doesn’t. But what happens when the confirmatory test comes back as "indeterminate," or worse, looks like it’s contradicting itself?

A recent case report from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory highlights a scenario where the serological profile defied the classic textbook description of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). This patient presented with a stroke and severe thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, yet the SRA showed strong platelet activation even in the absence of heparin. Furthermore, the antibodies showed partial resistance to standard blocking mechanisms. This isn’t just a lab curiosity; it’s a window into autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a more severe and complex variant that can be missed if you aren’t looking for the nuances in the assay results.

## The Case: Early Onset and a Confusing Lab Picture

The patient was a man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation who underwent complex cardiac surgery, including aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta replacement. His immediate postoperative course was initially stable, but by postoperative day 5 (POD5), his platelet count had plummeted from 210×10⁹/L to 50×10⁹/L. By POD6, the count dropped further to 26×10⁹/L, and a CT scan revealed an acute parietal lobe stroke.

The timeline was the first red flag. Classic HIT typically presents between POD5 and POD10. However, this patient’s platelet decline began before POD5. The key to this early onset was his history: he had undergone a diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to surgery where he received a low dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH). This preoperative exposure sensitized his immune system, allowing the thrombocytopenia to manifest sooner than the typical postoperative window.

When the PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive, the team moved to the SRA. This is where things got tricky. The assay showed strong serotonin release at low heparin concentrations, which is consistent with HIT. However, it also showed strong serotonin release at *zero* heparin concentration. Additionally, when the lab added an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) to the mix—which usually inhibits platelet activation in classic HIT—the inhibition was only partial. The initial report labeled this "indeterminate," but closer review confirmed a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT.

## Understanding the Serological Anomaly

To understand why this SRA profile was so unusual, you have to look at the pathophysiology. Classic HIT involves IgG antibodies that recognize platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to heparin. These immune complexes cluster platelet Fc receptors, causing activation and thrombosis. In the SRA, this activation requires heparin to be present; without heparin, the antibody shouldn’t bind PF4 and shouldn’t activate the platelets.

In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), the antibodies have heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. They can bind PF4 and activate platelets even when no heparin is present. This explains the 96% serotonin release seen at 0 U/mL heparin in this patient. This finding is the hallmark of aHIT and distinguishes it from classic HIT.

The second anomaly was the response to the IV.3 antibody. In standard HIT, blocking the Fc receptor prevents the immune complex from signaling the platelet, essentially shutting down the serotonin release. In this case, the patient’s antibodies were so reactive that the blocking antibody failed to completely inhibit the activation. There was still 76% serotonin release in the presence of the blocker. This partial resistance suggests a uniquely severe serological phenotype where the antibody-platelet interaction is robust enough to bypass standard inhibition mechanisms. Recognizing this profile prevents the misdiagnosis of a false-negative SRA and ensures the patient gets treated for the hypercoagulable state.

## Management of the Hypercoagulable State

Treating aHIT requires navigating a minefield of severe thrombocytopenia and active thrombosis. This patient was started on argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, alongside high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). While IVIG is often effective in aHIT, this patient showed only a modest platelet recovery initially. More concerning was the evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation.

During the argatroban phase, the patient’s fibrinogen levels dropped, D-dimer levels peaked, and schistocytes appeared on the peripheral smear. These signs indicated that the hypercoagulability was not fully controlled. The team made the decision to switch from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. Fondaparinux was chosen for ease of administration and its efficacy in HIT, though the patient required supplemental antithrombin concentrate due to mild deficiency. This switch, combined with continued supportive care, led to a gradual recovery of platelets and complete neurological resolution.

## Clinical Pearls

This case underscores that HIT is not a monolithic diagnosis. The presence of heparin-independent activation and atypical SRA profiles signals a more aggressive disease course that requires vigilance.

*   **Early onset HIT is possible:** If a patient has recent preoperative heparin exposure (such as during cardiac catheterization), HIT-related thrombocytopenia can occur before the typical postoperative day 5 window.
*   **Zero-heparin activation defines aHIT:** If the SRA shows strong platelet activation in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL), this is characteristic of autoimmune HIT rather than classic heparin-dependent HIT.
*   **Atypical SRA inhibition:** Some aHIT antibodies may show partial resistance to Fc receptor-blocking antibodies in the SRA, leading to indeterminate results that should not rule out the diagnosis.
*   **Monitor for consumptive coagulopathy:** In severe aHIT, look for falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytes even while on therapeutic anticoagulation, as these may signal the need to adjust the anticoagulant strategy.
*   **Outcome is generally favorable:** Despite the severity, patients with aHIT can achieve full neurological and hematologic recovery with appropriate non-heparin anticoagulation and time.
