Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# HIT and the Brain: Recognizing Thrombotic Stroke as a Presentation of HIT

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is often taught as a lab value problem, but clinically, it is a clotting problem. The "T" in HIT stands for Thrombocytopenia, but the "H" stands for Heparin, and the real danger lies in the hypercoagulable state that drives catastrophic thrombosis. When a patient on heparin develops new neurological deficits and dropping platelets, HIT must be at the top of your differential, even if the timing feels slightly off. A recent case report from St Bernard et al. illustrates exactly why missing this diagnosis can lead to devastating outcomes like ischemic stroke, and how atypical presentations—specifically autoimmune HIT (aHIT)—can complicate the picture.

### The Case: A Stroke After Heart Surgery

Consider an elderly man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation and newly diagnosed aortic stenosis. He underwent diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to his scheduled cardiac surgery. During the cath, he received a low dose of unfractionated heparin (2,000 U). A week later, he underwent aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta replacement using cardiopulmonary bypass, which required 45,000 U of unfractionated heparin. The surgery itself was uneventful with good hemodynamics.

The trouble started in the postoperative period. His preoperative platelet count was 210×10⁹/L. By postoperative day 1 (POD1), it was 155×10⁹/L, which looked like a standard post-bypass dip. However, the count continued to fall progressively. By POD5, platelets had dropped to 50×10⁹/L. The patient was developing postoperative delirium, which worsened. By POD6, platelets plummeted to 26×10⁹/L.

A CT brain was ordered for the worsening mental status and revealed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe. This was not a hemorrhagic stroke; it was thrombotic. The clinical team suspected HIT, but the timing was tricky. Classic HIT typically presents between POD5 and POD10. This patient’s thrombocytopenia began falling before POD5. However, the key was the preoperative catheterization. The prior exposure to heparin during the cath one week earlier likely primed the immune system, leading to an earlier onset than the textbook window suggests.

### The Pathophysiology: Why HIT Causes Stroke

The mechanism behind HIT-related thrombosis is immune-mediated platelet activation. Antibodies, typically IgG, form complexes with platelet factor 4 (PF4) and heparin. These complexes bind to Fc receptors on platelets, causing massive activation and thrombin generation. This creates a pro-thrombotic storm that can occlude cerebral vessels.

In this specific case, the patient had a rare and severe variant known as autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Unlike classic HIT, which requires heparin to trigger antibody binding, aHIT antibodies can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. The laboratory results were striking. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive. The confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) showed strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL of heparin, confirming heparin-independent platelet activation. Furthermore, the antibodies showed partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade, suggesting an unusually aggressive serological profile. This hypercoagulability was severe enough to cause a parietal stroke despite the patient being on prophylactic anticoagulation.

### Diagnostic Pitfalls and Timing

The biggest pitfall in diagnosing HIT is the "4 Ts" score, specifically the timing. Clinicians often rule out HIT if the platelet drop happens too early, assuming it is due to the surgery itself. In cardiac surgery, a mild drop in platelets is common on POD1 due to dilution and consumption. However, a progressive decline beyond POD3 or POD4 should raise alarm bells.

In this case, the onset was early (before POD5), which is atypical for primary post-cardiac surgery HIT. However, the patient had significant preoperative heparin exposure during the catheterization. About 10% of cardiac surgery patients with HIT present before POD5, usually linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure. If you see thrombocytopenia plus a new thrombus (like a stroke) in a patient with recent heparin exposure, you must test for HIT antibodies regardless of whether the count is "technically" within the classic window.

### Management: Stopping the Storm

Once HIT is suspected, the immediate action is to stop all heparin sources, including flushes and catheter locks. The patient in this case was switched from low-molecular-weight heparin (dalteparin) to fondaparinux initially, then to therapeutic argatroban once the stroke was confirmed. Argatroban was chosen for its short half-life given the recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia. High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was also administered, which is often used in aHIT to neutralize the antibodies.

Despite this, the patient’s hypercoagulability persisted. Fibrinogen levels fell, D-dimer rose, and schistocytes appeared on the blood film, indicating ongoing consumptive coagulopathy. The platelet count response to IVIG was modest. The team eventually switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux with supplemental antithrombin concentrate. This strategy finally worked. The platelet count recovered gradually, reaching normal levels by POD62, and the patient made a complete neurological recovery.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Stroke is a common complication of HIT:** If a patient on heparin develops new neurological deficits and thrombocytopenia, assume HIT until proven otherwise.
*   **Timing can be deceptive:** Early onset thrombocytopenia (before POD5) does not rule out HIT if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure, such as during catheterization.
*   **aHIT is severe:** Autoimmune HIT can present with heparin-independent platelet activation and may require aggressive management with non-heparin anticoagulants and IVIG.
*   **Stop all heparin:** Immediate cessation of all heparin products is critical, followed by initiation of a non-heparin anticoagulant like argatroban or fondaparinux.
*   **Monitor for recovery:** Platelet recovery can be slow in severe cases, and serological testing may remain positive for months even after clinical resolution.
