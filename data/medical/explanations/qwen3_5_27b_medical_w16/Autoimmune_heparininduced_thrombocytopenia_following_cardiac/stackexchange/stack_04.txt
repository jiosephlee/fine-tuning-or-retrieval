Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Management] Why was the anticoagulant switched from argatroban to fondaparinux in this patient despite initial use of a direct thrombin inhibitor?

The switch from argatroban to fondaparinux in this patient was primarily driven by laboratory evidence of persistent, uncontrolled hypercoagulability despite therapeutic anticoagulation with the direct thrombin inhibitor.

Although argatroban was initially selected for its short half-life in the setting of a recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia, the patient demonstrated signs of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy during therapy. Specifically, you will note a progressive decrease in fibrinogen levels, rising d-dimer levels, and the new appearance of red cell fragments (schistocytes) on peripheral blood film between postoperative days 9 and 16. Furthermore, the platelet count showed only a modest increase following high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, which is atypical for autoimmune HIT (aHIT) and suggested insufficient control of the underlying immune-mediated thrombosis.

Consequently, the team transitioned to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. While ease of administration (subcutaneous once daily versus intravenous PTT-adjusted infusion) was a secondary consideration, the clinical imperative was to achieve better control of the HIT-associated hypercoagulability. Importantly, because fondaparinux is an indirect antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, the team supplemented antithrombin concentrate prior to initiation, as the patient’s baseline antithrombin level was mildly reduced. This adjustment was critical to ensuring the efficacy of the new agent. The switch ultimately resulted in platelet recovery and resolution of the neurological deficits.
