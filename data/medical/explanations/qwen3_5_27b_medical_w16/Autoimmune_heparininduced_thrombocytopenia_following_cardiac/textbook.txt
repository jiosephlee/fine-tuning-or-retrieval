Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The clinical presentation of this case centers on an elderly male patient with significant cardiovascular comorbidities undergoing complex cardiac surgery. The patient, in his 70s, presented with a two-year history of exertional dyspnea and longstanding atrial fibrillation managed with apixaban. Evaluation for a newly detected systolic murmur led to a diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to surgery. Notably, because the patient had not held his apixaban, the procedure was performed via a transradial approach with a reduced dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH) anticoagulation. Angiography revealed moderate aortic stenosis, a dilated ascending aorta, and coronary artery disease, necessitating aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta replacement, and coronary artery bypass grafting. The surgery utilized cardiopulmonary bypass with standard high-dose UFH anticoagulation (45,000 U). Understanding the patient's surgical context is vital for risk stratification, as cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass is a high-risk setting for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) due to extensive heparin exposure and the inflammatory response associated with the procedure.

A critical component of the initial assessment involves constructing a precise timeline of platelet counts relative to heparin exposure. The patient’s preoperative platelet count was normal at 210×10⁹/L. On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the count decreased to 155×10⁹/L, a finding consistent with the expected minor decline seen after cardiac surgery. However, from POD1 through POD5, the platelet count progressively fell, reaching 50×10⁹/L by POD5 and dropping further to 26×10⁹/L by POD6. Classically, HIT typically manifests between POD5 and POD10; therefore, a significant decline beginning on POD1 is atypical. This early onset initially complicates the diagnosis but is explained in this case by the preoperative exposure to UFH during the heart catheterization one week prior. For the clinician, recognizing that recent heparin exposure within the preceding week can precipitate early-onset HIT is essential to avoid missing the diagnosis in the immediate postoperative period.

The clinical picture was further complicated by neurological symptoms that warranted urgent investigation. While the patient had no focal neurological deficits initially, he experienced worsening postoperative delirium concurrent with the rapid platelet decline. This combination of thrombocytopenia and neurological change prompted brain imaging, which revealed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe. In the context of HIT, thrombosis is a defining and dangerous complication, occurring in a significant proportion of patients. The identification of a thrombotic stroke in a patient with unexplained postoperative thrombocytopenia strongly supports a prothrombotic etiology like HIT over other causes of low platelets, such as dilution or consumption without thrombosis. Consequently, the neurological assessment serves not only to evaluate the patient's status but also to provide evidence of the hypercoagulable state characteristic of the disease.

Initial laboratory evaluation was pivotal in narrowing the differential diagnosis for severe thrombocytopenia. A complete blood count on POD5 showed anemia with a hemoglobin of 97 g/L, which was stable compared to previous days, indicating expected surgical blood loss without ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count and differential were normal, and reticulocyte counts were unremarkable. Crucially, the haptoglobin level was elevated, which argues against active intravascular hemolysis. Coagulation studies demonstrated a normal prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time, while fibrinogen was elevated at 6.59 g/L, consistent with the acute phase response of surgery rather than consumption. A peripheral blood film reviewed on POD7 confirmed thrombocytopenia but initially showed no platelet clumps or red cell fragments (schistocytes). These findings helped exclude alternative diagnoses such as disseminated intravascular coagulation, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, and sepsis-associated coagulopathy, thereby focusing the diagnostic reasoning toward an immune-mediated etiology.

## Pathophysiology of Classic and Autoimmune HIT

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is fundamentally an immune-mediated disorder driven by the formation of antibodies against platelet factor 4 (PF4) complexes. In classic HIT, unfractionated heparin (UFH) binds to the cationic protein PF4, which is released from platelet alpha-granules, creating a neoantigen. This PF4-heparin complex stimulates the production of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies. When these antibodies bind to the complex, they cross-link Fc gamma receptors (FcγRIIa) on the surface of platelets. This clustering of Fc receptors triggers profound platelet activation, leading to thrombin generation, platelet consumption, and a high risk of thrombosis. In the context of the presented case, the patient’s cardiac surgery required significant UFH exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass, providing the necessary antigenic stimulus for this immune response. The resulting thrombotic stroke and severe thrombocytopenia are direct clinical consequences of this pathophysiological cascade, where activated platelets drive both clot formation and platelet clearance.

However, the patient’s laboratory profile revealed a distinct phenotype known as autoimmune HIT (aHIT), which diverges from the classic heparin-dependent mechanism. While classic HIT antibodies require the presence of heparin to form the PF4 complex and activate platelets, aHIT antibodies possess heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. This distinction was evident in the patient’s serotonin-release assay (SRA) results, which demonstrated strong serotonin release (96%) even in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL). In classic HIT, platelet activation is negligible without the heparin cofactor; therefore, the ability of the patient’s serum to activate platelets without heparin confirmed the autoimmune nature of the disorder. Furthermore, the case highlighted a unique serological feature where the platelet activation was only partially inhibited by an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. This partial resistance suggests an unusually severe serological phenotype where the antibodies may activate platelets through mechanisms that are less dependent on standard Fc receptor clustering, contributing to the profound thrombocytopenia and persisting hypercoagulability observed despite anticoagulation.

The timing of thrombocytopenia onset provides critical diagnostic clues regarding the patient’s prior immune sensitization. Typically, classic HIT presents between postoperative days 5 and 10, allowing time for the immune system to generate antibodies after initial heparin exposure. In this case, the platelet count decline began on postoperative day 1 and progressed steadily, which is atypical for a first-time heparin exposure. This early onset was explained by the patient’s preoperative heart catheterization one week prior to surgery, during which he received a lower dose of UFH. This prior exposure likely sensitized the immune system, leading to an anamnestic response where pre-existing memory B cells rapidly produced antibodies upon re-exposure to heparin during cardiac surgery. Recognizing this link between prior sensitization and early onset is essential for clinicians, as it prevents the dismissal of HIT in patients presenting with thrombocytopenia before the traditional five-day window. Ultimately, understanding these pathophysiological nuances—specifically the heparin-independent activation and the impact of prior sensitization—allows for the correct identification of aHIT and guides the selection of appropriate non-heparin anticoagulation strategies.

## Diagnostic Workup and Serological Interpretation

The diagnostic reasoning in this case required distinguishing autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) from other common causes of severe postoperative thrombocytopenia. In the immediate postoperative period following cardiac surgery, platelet counts often decline due to hemodilution, consumption on the cardiopulmonary bypass circuit, or sequestration. However, a progressive fall to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L, accompanied by a thrombotic stroke, necessitated a rigorous exclusion of alternative etiologies. Common explanations such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness were unlikely given the patient’s stable hemodynamics. Infectious causes were ruled out by negative blood and urine cultures. Furthermore, rare entities like post-transfusion purpura were excluded due to the absence of blood transfusions, and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was deemed unlikely initially due to normal haptoglobin levels and the absence of red cell fragments (schistocytes) on the peripheral blood film. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was also considered improbable because the timing of the platelet count decline occurred too early to be attributed to postoperative medications such as vancomycin or cefazolin. Consequently, despite the unusually early onset of thrombocytopenia before postoperative day 5, the clinical picture of thrombosis and thrombocytopenia, combined with prior heparin exposure during preoperative catheterization, strongly favored a diagnosis of HIT.

Initial laboratory evaluation relied on the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) as a screening tool for HIT antibodies. In this case, the screening immunoassay yielded a strong-positive result with an optical density (OD) of 2.2 units, well above the reference range of less than 0.4 OD units. While the PF4/polyanion ELISA is highly sensitive for detecting antibodies against the platelet factor 4–heparin complex, it lacks specificity and can yield false-positive results. Therefore, a positive screening test requires confirmation with a functional platelet activation assay, typically the serotonin-release assay (SRA), to distinguish clinically significant HIT from non-specific antibody binding. The strong positivity of the ELISA in this patient provided the initial impetus for further investigation but could not alone confirm the diagnosis or characterize the specific subtype of HIT.

The confirmatory SRA results provided the critical serological evidence for aHIT, revealing a profile distinct from classic heparin-dependent HIT. In classic HIT, platelet activation and serotonin release are dependent on the presence of heparin to form immune complexes. In this patient, however, the serum caused strong serotonin release (96%) in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL), which is a hallmark feature of aHIT. The patient’s serum also demonstrated strong release at low heparin concentrations (0.1 and 0.3 U/mL) and, consistent with HIT pathophysiology, a lack of release at supratherapeutic heparin concentrations (100 U/mL), where high heparin levels prevent immune complex formation. This combination of heparin-independent activation at zero concentration alongside heparin-dependent suppression at high concentrations confirmed the presence of antibodies capable of activating platelets without heparin, defining the autoimmune phenotype.

A particularly nuanced aspect of this case involved the interpretation of the SRA regarding Fc receptor blockade, which initially led to an "indeterminate" laboratory report. Standard HIT antibodies typically activate platelets via Fc receptor clustering, a process that can be inhibited by adding a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3). In classic HIT, this blockade reduces serotonin release to less than 20%. In this patient, the addition of IV.3 failed to completely inhibit activation, resulting in 76% serotonin release at 0.1 U/mL heparin. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade indicated that the patient’s antibodies possessed unusually reactive, heparin-independent platelet-activating properties that were not fully suppressed by the blocking antibody. Recognizing this atypical SRA profile was essential, as it prevented the dismissal of the diagnosis despite the indeterminate label. Ultimately, the convergence of the strong ELISA, the heparin-independent SRA release, and the partial Fc blockade resistance confirmed the diagnosis of severe aHIT, explaining the patient’s profound thrombocytopenia and thrombotic complications.

## Therapeutic Management and Decision Making

Once the diagnosis of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) was strongly suspected, immediate management required the cessation of all heparin products and the initiation of alternative anticoagulation to prevent further thrombotic complications, particularly given the confirmed cerebral infarct. In this case, the treating team selected argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, as the initial therapeutic anticoagulant. The rationale for choosing argatroban over fondaparinux at this stage was driven by safety considerations specific to the patient's clinical status. Although fondaparinux is an effective off-label option for HIT, the patient had recently suffered a stroke and presented with profound thrombocytopenia (platelet nadir 13×10⁹/L), creating a significant risk of intracranial or systemic hemorrhage. Argatroban was preferred due to its shorter half-life, which allows for more rapid reversibility should bleeding occur, compared to the longer half-life of fondaparinux. The initial dosing was set at 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a target partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 55–85 seconds, aiming to balance thrombosis prevention against bleeding risk.

In addition to anticoagulation, the patient received high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), an adjunctive therapy specifically indicated for aHIT to block platelet Fc receptors and reduce immune-mediated platelet activation. The treatment regimen involved 100 g of IVIG administered on two consecutive days, followed by a third 100 g dose. This additional dose was administered because the patient failed to demonstrate an initial platelet count increase following the first two days of therapy. While high-dose IVIG typically results in a brisk correction of thrombocytopenia in aHIT patients, this case highlighted a unique severity where the antibody activity was unusually resistant to standard immunomodulation. The modest platelet recovery, rising from 13×10⁹/L to only 54×10⁹/L despite aggressive IVIG, underscored the atypical and severe nature of the patient’s serological profile, which included partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Monitoring therapeutic efficacy in HIT requires looking beyond platelet counts to assess the control of the underlying hypercoagulable state. In this patient, serial monitoring of fibrinogen and d-dimer levels provided critical insight into the adequacy of anticoagulation. Despite argatroban therapy, the patient exhibited a progressive decrease in fibrinogen levels from postoperative day 6 to day 17, with a nadir of 2.49 g/L, alongside peak d-dimer levels around day 12 and the appearance of schistocytes on peripheral blood film. These laboratory trends were consistent with severe, progressive consumptive coagulopathy, indicating that the thrombotic burden was not adequately controlled by argatroban alone. This monitoring strategy is essential because platelet recovery can lag behind the control of thrombosis; therefore, worsening coagulation markers despite therapeutic anticoagulation suggest the need for a change in management strategy.

Based on the evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and the practical challenges of managing intravenous argatroban, the anticoagulant was switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on postoperative day 14. The decision to switch was multifactorial: it addressed the concern that argatroban was insufficient to control the HIT-associated hypercoagulability, as evidenced by the falling fibrinogen and rising d-dimer levels, and offered the practical advantage of once-daily subcutaneous administration compared to PTT-adjusted intravenous infusion. Crucially, because fondaparinux is an indirect antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, the team assessed antithrombin levels prior to initiation. The patient’s pre-infusion antithrombin level was mildly reduced at 0.70 U/mL; consequently, supplemental antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) was administered to ensure the efficacy of the fondaparinux. This switch, combined with continued immune modulation, resulted in gradual platelet count recovery and resolution of neurological abnormalities, demonstrating the importance of tailoring anticoagulant selection to both the pathophysiology of the HIT variant and the patient’s individual response profile.

## Clinical Outcome and Educational Significance

The clinical trajectory of this patient underscores the potential for full recovery in severe autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) when appropriate anticoagulation and supportive care are instituted, despite an initially ominous presentation. Following the initiation of therapeutic anticoagulation with argatroban and subsequent transition to fondaparinux, the patient’s platelet count gradually recovered, reaching 155×10⁹/L by postoperative day (POD) 62 and 202×10⁹/L by POD 87. Although the patient suffered an acute parietal stroke confirmed by CT imaging on POD 6, neurological abnormalities resolved completely during the follow-up period. This outcome is notable given the profound thrombocytopenia, with a nadir platelet count of 13×10⁹/L on POD 7, and the presence of consumptive coagulopathy markers such as falling fibrinogen and rising d-dimer levels. For the learner, this case reinforces that even in cases of severe aHIT complicated by thrombosis, platelet recovery and neurological restoration are achievable, though the timeline may be prolonged compared to classic HIT. The response to high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was also atypical; while IVIG is often used in aHIT to accelerate platelet recovery, this patient exhibited only a modest increase from 13×10⁹/L to 54×10⁹/L over a week, suggesting that severe variants of aHIT may exhibit resistance to standard immunomodulatory therapies.

Serological monitoring provided critical confirmation of the diagnosis and illustrated the dynamic evolution of HIT antibodies over time. Initially, the serotonin-release assay (SRA) demonstrated a profile characteristic of aHIT, with strong platelet activation in the absence of heparin (heparin-independent) and partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. Serial SRA testing remained strongly positive for several months, but the profile evolved as the patient recovered. By POD 124, the SRA profile shifted to a predominantly heparin-dependent reaction, showing minimal release without heparin but 100% release with heparin. This evolution is a key educational point: it indicates that the heparin-independent autoimmune antibodies waned over time, leaving behind classic heparin-dependent antibodies that eventually cleared as well. Understanding this serological progression helps clinicians interpret persistent positive immunoassays during recovery and confirms that the initial atypical profile was indeed pathogenic rather than a false positive or unrelated finding.

This case offers vital clinical pearls regarding the timing of HIT diagnosis in the cardiac surgery setting. Classically, HIT is taught to present between POD 5 and 10 following surgery. However, this patient’s platelet decline began on POD 1 and reached diagnostic thresholds by POD 5. The precipitating factor was identified as unfractionated heparin exposure during a preoperative heart catheterization performed one week prior to surgery. This highlights a common diagnostic pitfall: "rapid-onset" HIT can occur if the patient has had heparin exposure within the previous 100 days. In the context of cardiac surgery, learners must maintain a high index of suspicion for HIT even in the early postoperative period if there is a history of recent preoperative heparin exposure, such as during catheterization or prior bypass procedures. Ruling out HIT solely based on early onset can lead to dangerous delays in initiating non-heparin anticoagulation.

Finally, the management of long-term anticoagulation in this case demonstrates a pragmatic approach to transitioning from acute to chronic therapy. After achieving platelet recovery and stabilizing the hypercoagulable state with intravenous argatroban and subcutaneous fondaparinux, the patient was transitioned to oral apixaban on POD 65. Fondaparinux was chosen for the intermediate phase due to its efficacy in HIT and ease of subcutaneous administration compared to intravenous argatroban, though supplemental antithrombin was administered to ensure efficacy. The successful switch to a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) for long-term management illustrates that once platelets have recovered and the patient is stable, DOACs can be utilized for secondary prevention, provided the thrombotic risk warrants continued anticoagulation. This case reinforces the importance of monitoring coagulation parameters, such as fibrinogen and d-dimer, during the acute phase to ensure the chosen anticoagulant is effectively controlling the HIT-associated hypercoagulability before transitioning to oral regimens.
