Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# The 5-Day Rule is a Myth: Early Onset HIT After Cardiac Surgery

## The Hook: When Heuristics Fail

We all know the classic teaching: Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) typically presents between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10. If the platelets drop on day 2 or 3 after cardiac surgery, we usually blame the pump, the trauma, or sepsis. We tell ourselves, "It’s too early for HIT."

But what if the clock started ticking before the patient even stepped into the OR?

A recent case report highlights a terrifying reality for cardiac surgery patients: the "5-day rule" is not absolute. In patients with recent preoperative heparin exposure, HIT can manifest immediately post-op. Even more concerning, this case describes a rare, severe variant known as Autoimmune HIT (aHIT), characterized by heparin-independent platelet activation and resistance to standard antibody blockade mechanisms. This patient suffered a parietal stroke and a platelet nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L, proving that missing this diagnosis early can be catastrophic.

## Case Walkthrough

Consider a man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation on apixaban who presented with dyspnea and aortic stenosis. One week prior to his scheduled valve replacement, he underwent a diagnostic heart catheterization. Due to his apixaban, the cardiologist used a transradial approach with a lower-than-usual dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH)—only 2,000 U.

He then underwent cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), receiving the standard 45,000 U of UFH. The surgery was uneventful. However, the postoperative course took a dark turn.

His platelet count started dropping immediately. It fell from 210 × 10⁹/L pre-op to 155 × 10⁹/L on POD1. By POD5, it had plummeted to 50 × 10⁹/L. On POD6, the count hit 26 × 10⁹/L, and the patient developed worsening delirium. A CT brain scan revealed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe.

The team suspected HIT, but the timing was "too early" for classic post-cardiac surgery HIT. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (2.2 OD units). However, the confirmatory Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) was initially labeled "indeterminate." Why? Because the patient’s serum activated platelets even without heparin (0 U/mL), and the activation was only partially blocked by an Fc receptor-blocking antibody. This is the hallmark of Autoimmune HIT (aHIT).

## The Pathophysiology: Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

Classic HIT relies on heparin-dependent antibodies. The antibody needs heparin to bind to Platelet Factor 4 (PF4) to form an immune complex that activates platelets via Fc receptors. In aHIT, the antibodies are "autoimmune" in nature—they can activate platelets *without* heparin present.

This patient’s serological profile was unique. His SRA showed 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin. Furthermore, the standard inhibition test using the monoclonal antibody IV.3 failed to suppress activation completely (76% release remained). This indicates a highly reactive antibody phenotype that is partially resistant to Fc receptor blockade.

The critical teaching point here is the timeline. Classic HIT requires sensitization, usually taking 5-10 days after initial heparin exposure. However, this patient had been sensitized during his cardiac catheterization one week prior. When he received UFH again during surgery, his immune system was primed. The platelet drop began on POD1, well before the traditional "safe window."

## Management in the ICU

Treating severe HIT, especially aHIT, is a balancing act between preventing thrombosis and managing bleeding risks. This patient’s thrombocytopenia was profound (nadir 13 × 10⁹/L) and complicated by a stroke.

The team initially switched to argatroban and administered high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). The response was poor; platelets only rose modestly to 54 × 10⁹/L, and markers of consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, schistocytes) persisted.

Recognizing that argatroban might not be controlling the hypercoagulability sufficiently, they switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. Because fondaparinux is antithrombin-dependent, they supplemented antithrombin concentrate before initiation. This strategy worked. Platelets gradually recovered to normal by POD62, and the patient made a complete neurological recovery.

This case underscores that in severe aHIT, IVIG alone may not suffice, and switching anticoagulants based on clinical response (D-dimer, fibrinogen trends) is vital.

## Take-Home Pearls

*   **Don’t trust the 5-day rule blindly:** If a patient had heparin exposure in the week prior to surgery (e.g., cardiac cath), HIT can present immediately post-op (POD 1-5).
*   **Suspect aHIT in severe cases:** Look for heparin-independent platelet activation. If the SRA shows serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin, consider autoimmune HIT.
*   **ELISA is a screen, not a diagnosis:** A strong-positive ELISA combined with an "indeterminate" SRA does not rule out HIT; in aHIT, the SRA profile can be atypical.
*   **Monitor consumptive coagulopathy:** In severe HIT/aHIT, watch fibrinogen and D-dimer trends. Falling fibrinogen and rising D-dimer despite anticoagulation suggest uncontrolled hypercoagulability.
*   **IVIG response varies:** While high-dose IVIG is a mainstay for aHIT, some severe cases show a blunted platelet recovery and may require switching anticoagulants (e.g., from argatroban to fondaparinux).



# When the SRA Doesn't Make Sense: Decoding Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

We all know the HIT algorithm. You see a platelet drop, you calculate the 4Ts, you order the PF4 ELISA, and if that’s positive, you send off for the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) to confirm. Usually, the SRA gives a clear answer: either the patient’s serum activates platelets in the presence of heparin, or it doesn’t. But what happens when the confirmatory test comes back as "indeterminate," or worse, looks like it’s contradicting itself?

A recent case report from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory highlights a scenario where the serological profile defied the classic textbook description of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). This patient presented with a stroke and severe thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, yet the SRA showed strong platelet activation even in the absence of heparin. Furthermore, the antibodies showed partial resistance to standard blocking mechanisms. This isn’t just a lab curiosity; it’s a window into autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a more severe and complex variant that can be missed if you aren’t looking for the nuances in the assay results.

## The Case: Early Onset and a Confusing Lab Picture

The patient was a man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation who underwent complex cardiac surgery, including aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta replacement. His immediate postoperative course was initially stable, but by postoperative day 5 (POD5), his platelet count had plummeted from 210×10⁹/L to 50×10⁹/L. By POD6, the count dropped further to 26×10⁹/L, and a CT scan revealed an acute parietal lobe stroke.

The timeline was the first red flag. Classic HIT typically presents between POD5 and POD10. However, this patient’s platelet decline began before POD5. The key to this early onset was his history: he had undergone a diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to surgery where he received a low dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH). This preoperative exposure sensitized his immune system, allowing the thrombocytopenia to manifest sooner than the typical postoperative window.

When the PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive, the team moved to the SRA. This is where things got tricky. The assay showed strong serotonin release at low heparin concentrations, which is consistent with HIT. However, it also showed strong serotonin release at *zero* heparin concentration. Additionally, when the lab added an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) to the mix—which usually inhibits platelet activation in classic HIT—the inhibition was only partial. The initial report labeled this "indeterminate," but closer review confirmed a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT.

## Understanding the Serological Anomaly

To understand why this SRA profile was so unusual, you have to look at the pathophysiology. Classic HIT involves IgG antibodies that recognize platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to heparin. These immune complexes cluster platelet Fc receptors, causing activation and thrombosis. In the SRA, this activation requires heparin to be present; without heparin, the antibody shouldn’t bind PF4 and shouldn’t activate the platelets.

In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), the antibodies have heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. They can bind PF4 and activate platelets even when no heparin is present. This explains the 96% serotonin release seen at 0 U/mL heparin in this patient. This finding is the hallmark of aHIT and distinguishes it from classic HIT.

The second anomaly was the response to the IV.3 antibody. In standard HIT, blocking the Fc receptor prevents the immune complex from signaling the platelet, essentially shutting down the serotonin release. In this case, the patient’s antibodies were so reactive that the blocking antibody failed to completely inhibit the activation. There was still 76% serotonin release in the presence of the blocker. This partial resistance suggests a uniquely severe serological phenotype where the antibody-platelet interaction is robust enough to bypass standard inhibition mechanisms. Recognizing this profile prevents the misdiagnosis of a false-negative SRA and ensures the patient gets treated for the hypercoagulable state.

## Management of the Hypercoagulable State

Treating aHIT requires navigating a minefield of severe thrombocytopenia and active thrombosis. This patient was started on argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, alongside high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). While IVIG is often effective in aHIT, this patient showed only a modest platelet recovery initially. More concerning was the evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation.

During the argatroban phase, the patient’s fibrinogen levels dropped, D-dimer levels peaked, and schistocytes appeared on the peripheral smear. These signs indicated that the hypercoagulability was not fully controlled. The team made the decision to switch from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. Fondaparinux was chosen for ease of administration and its efficacy in HIT, though the patient required supplemental antithrombin concentrate due to mild deficiency. This switch, combined with continued supportive care, led to a gradual recovery of platelets and complete neurological resolution.

## Clinical Pearls

This case underscores that HIT is not a monolithic diagnosis. The presence of heparin-independent activation and atypical SRA profiles signals a more aggressive disease course that requires vigilance.

*   **Early onset HIT is possible:** If a patient has recent preoperative heparin exposure (such as during cardiac catheterization), HIT-related thrombocytopenia can occur before the typical postoperative day 5 window.
*   **Zero-heparin activation defines aHIT:** If the SRA shows strong platelet activation in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL), this is characteristic of autoimmune HIT rather than classic heparin-dependent HIT.
*   **Atypical SRA inhibition:** Some aHIT antibodies may show partial resistance to Fc receptor-blocking antibodies in the SRA, leading to indeterminate results that should not rule out the diagnosis.
*   **Monitor for consumptive coagulopathy:** In severe aHIT, look for falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytes even while on therapeutic anticoagulation, as these may signal the need to adjust the anticoagulant strategy.
*   **Outcome is generally favorable:** Despite the severity, patients with aHIT can achieve full neurological and hematologic recovery with appropriate non-heparin anticoagulation and time.



# Post-Bypass Platelet Drop: HIT vs. The Usual Suspects

## The Case That Broke the Rules

You know the drill after cardiac surgery. The patient comes off the pump, and the platelets drop. It’s usually the cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) machine, dilution, or consumption. You watch them, maybe hold the LMWH, and they bounce back. But what happens when the drop doesn’t stop? What happens when the patient has a stroke on postoperative day 6, and the platelets are hovering around 20,000?

We recently reviewed a case from St Bernard et al. that challenges the standard algorithm for post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia. An elderly man with atrial fibrillation underwent aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta replacement. He had a preoperative heart catheterization one week prior, where he received a low dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH). Post-op, his platelets declined steadily from 155×10⁹/L on day 1 to 50×10⁹/L on day 5. By day 6, they hit 26×10⁹/L, and he suffered an acute parietal stroke.

This wasn’t just a slow recovery. This was a crash. The diagnosis wasn’t classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), nor was it dilutional thrombocytopenia. It was autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a severe variant that defied the usual timing and serological expectations.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: Timing and Context

In the immediate post-cardiac surgery period, thrombocytopenia is common. The differential diagnosis is a crowded room. You have to rule out the usual suspects: dilution from CPB, consumption from the circuit, sepsis, or drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP).

In this case, the clinical team had to look past the standard timeline. Classic HIT typically presents between postoperative day 5 and day 10. This patient’s platelets started falling on day 1. Usually, that screams "CPB effect" or "dilution." However, the hemoglobin remained stable (97 g/L), arguing against significant ongoing blood loss or hemolysis. Haptoglobin was elevated, and there were no schistocytes initially, making TTP or DIC less likely at that stage.

The key to unlocking the diagnosis was the history of heparin exposure. The patient had received UFH during a catheterization one week before the surgery. While the intraoperative dose was massive (45,000 U), the sensitization likely occurred during that preoperative procedure. This explains the unusually early onset of thrombocytopenia. About 10% of cardiac surgery patients with HIT present before day 5, almost always linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure.

## The Twist: Autoimmune HIT and Serology

The screening PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive at 2.2 OD units. But the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) gave an "indeterminate" result that required a deep dive.

Classic HIT antibodies need heparin to activate platelets. They form immune complexes with PF4 and heparin, clustering Fc receptors. This patient’s antibodies were different. The SRA showed strong serotonin release (96%) even in the *absence* of heparin (0 U/mL). This heparin-independent activation is the hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Furthermore, the antibodies showed partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade, a unique serological profile not previously reported in the literature.

This distinction matters because aHIT is associated with more severe thrombocytopenia and persistent hypercoagulability. This patient’s platelet nadir was 13×10⁹/L. Despite starting therapeutic argatroban and high-dose IVIG, he developed signs of consumptive coagulopathy—falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and eventually schistocytes.

## Management: When Argatroban Isn't Enough

The treatment course highlights the difficulty of managing severe aHIT. The team initially switched to argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, given the recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia. They also added high-dose IVIG, which is often effective in aHIT to block Fc receptors.

However, the platelet count only modestly improved to 54×10⁹/L by day 14. The consumptive coagulopathy persisted. The team switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg daily) after administering antithrombin concentrate. Fondaparinux is an indirect factor Xa inhibitor that doesn’t cross-react with HIT antibodies. This switch, combined with the antithrombin support, finally broke the cycle. The platelets gradually recovered to 155×10⁹/L by day 62, and the patient had a complete neurological recovery.

## Clinical Pearls

This case reinforces that while CPB-related thrombocytopenia is common, you must maintain a high index of suspicion for HIT when the drop is profound or associated with thrombosis. The presence of preoperative heparin exposure can shift the timeline earlier than you expect.

*   **Check the Heparin History:** If a patient has thrombocytopenia before postoperative day 5, look for preoperative heparin exposure (e.g., catheterization, bridging). This can trigger early-onset HIT.
*   **Don’t Ignore the SRA Nuance:** A positive ELISA is common post-cardiac surgery. The SRA is the gold standard. Be aware of aHIT profiles where serotonin release occurs at 0 U/mL heparin, indicating heparin-independent activation.
*   **Monitor for Consumption:** Severe aHIT can cause consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer) even on anticoagulation. If argatroban fails to stabilize coagulation parameters, consider switching to fondaparinux with antithrombin support.
*   **IVIG Response Varies:** While IVIG is a mainstay for aHIT, response can be variable. In severe cases with partial Fc receptor resistance, platelet recovery may be delayed despite treatment.

**Take-Home Points**

*   **Early Onset HIT:** Thrombocytopenia starting before POD5 after cardiac surgery is rare but possible if there was preoperative heparin exposure.
*   **aHIT Serology:** Look for heparin-independent platelet activation (positive SRA at 0 U/mL heparin) to distinguish autoimmune HIT from classic HIT.
*   **Treatment Escalation:** Persistent hypercoagulability on argatroban may necessitate a switch to fondaparinux and antithrombin supplementation.
*   **Stroke Risk:** HIT in the post-cardiac surgery setting carries a high risk of thrombosis, including cerebral infarction, even without focal neurological signs initially.



# Argatroban vs. Fondaparinux: Navigating Anticoagulation in Severe HIT

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a well-known nightmare for anyone managing post-cardiac surgery patients. We know the drill: platelet drop between days 5 and 10, thrombosis risk, and the scramble to stop heparin and start an alternative anticoagulant. But what happens when the platelets crash *before* day 5? What if the antibodies don't just need heparin to activate platelets, but do it anyway? And what if your first-line alternative anticoagulant isn't enough to stop the clotting cascade?

This case report from St Bernard et al. walks us through a terrifying presentation of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) following cardiac surgery. It challenges our assumptions about timing, laboratory confirmation, and the hierarchy of alternative anticoagulants when standard therapy fails to control a hypercoagulable state.

## The Case: Early Onset and a Stroke

Our patient was a man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation and aortic stenosis. He underwent a diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to surgery, receiving 2,000 U of unfractionated heparin (UFH) via a transradial approach. He then proceeded to cardiac surgery (aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta graft, and CABG) with standard cardiopulmonary bypass anticoagulation using 45,000 U of UFH.

The immediate postoperative course seemed standard at first. Platelets were 210 × 10⁹/L pre-op and dropped to 155 × 10⁹/L on postoperative day 1 (POD1), which is expected after bypass. However, the decline accelerated unexpectedly. By POD5, platelets had plummeted to 50 × 10⁹/L. The patient developed worsening delirium, prompting a CT brain scan that revealed an acute parietal lobe infarct. By POD6, platelets were down to 24 × 10⁹/L.

The timing was the first red flag. Classic HIT typically presents between POD5 and POD10. A drop this early is rare unless there is recent heparin exposure. In this case, the preoperative catheterization provided that priming exposure, triggering the antibody formation before the surgery even happened.

## The Diagnosis: Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

The laboratory workup confirmed the suspicion but revealed a twist. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive. However, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) yielded an "indeterminate" result that required deep diving.

The SRA showed strong serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin, which is consistent with HIT. However, it also showed strong release (96%) at **0 U/mL heparin**. This is the hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), where antibodies activate platelets independently of heparin. Furthermore, the platelet activation was only partially inhibited by an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. In classic HIT, this antibody usually blocks activation completely. The partial resistance suggested a uniquely severe and reactive antibody profile.

## The Treatment Battle: Argatroban to Fondaparinux

Once the diagnosis was suspected, the team stopped all heparin products. They initiated therapeutic-dose argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), the latter being a specific consideration for aHIT to neutralize the antibodies.

Here is where the case gets critical for management. Despite starting argatroban and administering 300 g of IVIG over three days, the patient's hypercoagulability persisted. Fibrinogen levels dropped, d-dimer levels peaked, and schistocytes appeared on the blood smear. The platelet count only modestly improved from a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L to 54 × 10⁹/L.

The team recognized that argatroban, while a direct thrombin inhibitor with a short half-life (useful in bleeding risk), was not sufficiently controlling the consumptive coagulopathy. They switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg daily SC). Given fondaparinux is antithrombin-dependent, they also administered supplemental antithrombin concentrate. This switch correlated with a gradual recovery of platelets and resolution of the hypercoagulable markers.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

This case highlights that HIT is not a monolithic diagnosis. The presence of thrombosis, the severity of thrombocytopenia, and the laboratory profile can dictate a different management approach than the standard textbook algorithm.

*   **Watch the Timing:** Early postoperative thrombocytopenia (before POD5) should raise suspicion for HIT if there was recent heparin exposure, such as a preoperative catheterization.
*   **Know the aHIT Profile:** Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) features heparin-independent platelet activation. SRA results showing serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin confirm this, even if the result is initially labeled "indeterminate."
*   **IVIG Response Varies:** While high-dose IVIG is often used for aHIT, response can be blunted in severe cases. Do not assume platelet recovery will be brisk; monitor for persistent hypercoagulability.
*   **Anticoagulant Flexibility:** If a patient remains hypercoagulable (falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, schistocytes) despite argatroban, consider switching to fondaparinux. Ensure antithrombin levels are adequate before switching, as fondaparinux is antithrombin-dependent.
*   **Severe aHIT is Real:** A nadir platelet count of 13 × 10⁹/L with stroke indicates a severe phenotype. These patients may require aggressive monitoring and potentially multiple lines of therapy to achieve hemostatic control.



# Platelets Aren't Enough: Monitoring HIT Treatment with Fibrinogen and D-Dimer

## The Platelet Trap

We are trained to watch the platelet count like a hawk when treating heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). The logic is sound: if the immune-mediated destruction stops and the marrow catches up, the platelets should rise. But in severe cases, particularly autoimmune HIT (aHIT), the platelet count can be a lagging indicator that misleads you into thinking the thrombotic storm is over when it is still raging.

A recent case report from Warkentin et al. highlights a dangerous discordance between platelet recovery and ongoing consumptive coagulopathy. An elderly man underwent cardiac surgery and developed aHIT, complicated by a parietal stroke. Once diagnosed, he was started on argatroban and high-dose IVIG. On the surface, things looked okay. His platelet count crept up from a nadir of 13×10⁹/L to 54×10⁹/L. By conventional metrics, he was responding to therapy. However, a closer look at his coagulation profile told a very different story.

## The Hidden Consumption

While the platelets were ticking upward, the patient’s fibrinogen levels were crashing. His fibrinogen started at a robust 6.59 g/L on postoperative day 5, consistent with the acute phase reaction of surgery. By postoperative day 17, during treatment with argatroban, it had plummeted to 2.49 g/L. Simultaneously, his D-dimer levels peaked around postoperative day 12, and a peripheral blood smear finally revealed red cell fragments (schistocytes) between days 9 and 16.

These findings were not consistent with a patient who was simply recovering from HIT. They were hallmarks of severe, progressive HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. The platelet count had stabilized slightly, but the underlying thrombotic drive was consuming clotting factors and shearing red blood cells. The treating team recognized that the anticoagulation was insufficient to control the hypercoagulability, despite the "improving" platelet count. They switched from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux with supplemental antithrombin concentrate. Only then did the patient achieve a full recovery, with platelets normalizing by postoperative day 62 and neurological deficits resolving.

## Why the Discrepancy?

This case underscores a critical nuance in managing severe HIT and aHIT. In classic HIT, platelet activation is heparin-dependent. In aHIT, antibodies can activate platelets independently of heparin, often leading to a more severe clinical course with profound thrombocytopenia. When you treat these patients, you are trying to stop a massive thrombotic cascade. Platelet recovery is necessary, but it is not sufficient to declare victory.

If you only monitor the platelet count, you might miss the ongoing microvascular thrombosis that is depleting fibrinogen and elevating D-dimer. In this specific case, the persistence of schistocytes and the falling fibrinogen were the true signals that the hypercoagulable state was uncontrolled. The platelet count was essentially a distraction; the coagulation factors were screaming for help. This is particularly relevant in post-cardiac surgery patients where baseline fibrinogen is high, making a drop more clinically significant.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Do not rely solely on platelet counts:** In severe HIT or aHIT, a rising platelet count does not guarantee that the thrombotic process has halted.
*   **Track fibrinogen trends:** A falling fibrinogen level during HIT treatment suggests ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and inadequate anticoagulation control.
*   **Watch the D-dimer:** Persistently elevated or rising D-dimer levels indicate active thrombosis despite therapeutic anticoagulation.
*   **Check the smear:** The appearance of schistocytes during treatment is a red flag for microangiopathic hemolysis secondary to ongoing HIT-associated thrombosis.
*   **Consider aHIT:** If the clinical course is unusually severe or the laboratory profile is atypical (e.g., heparin-independent platelet activation), suspect autoimmune HIT and monitor closely for consumptive features.



# HIT and the Brain: Recognizing Thrombotic Stroke as a Presentation of HIT

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is often taught as a lab value problem, but clinically, it is a clotting problem. The "T" in HIT stands for Thrombocytopenia, but the "H" stands for Heparin, and the real danger lies in the hypercoagulable state that drives catastrophic thrombosis. When a patient on heparin develops new neurological deficits and dropping platelets, HIT must be at the top of your differential, even if the timing feels slightly off. A recent case report from St Bernard et al. illustrates exactly why missing this diagnosis can lead to devastating outcomes like ischemic stroke, and how atypical presentations—specifically autoimmune HIT (aHIT)—can complicate the picture.

### The Case: A Stroke After Heart Surgery

Consider an elderly man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation and newly diagnosed aortic stenosis. He underwent diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to his scheduled cardiac surgery. During the cath, he received a low dose of unfractionated heparin (2,000 U). A week later, he underwent aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta replacement using cardiopulmonary bypass, which required 45,000 U of unfractionated heparin. The surgery itself was uneventful with good hemodynamics.

The trouble started in the postoperative period. His preoperative platelet count was 210×10⁹/L. By postoperative day 1 (POD1), it was 155×10⁹/L, which looked like a standard post-bypass dip. However, the count continued to fall progressively. By POD5, platelets had dropped to 50×10⁹/L. The patient was developing postoperative delirium, which worsened. By POD6, platelets plummeted to 26×10⁹/L.

A CT brain was ordered for the worsening mental status and revealed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe. This was not a hemorrhagic stroke; it was thrombotic. The clinical team suspected HIT, but the timing was tricky. Classic HIT typically presents between POD5 and POD10. This patient’s thrombocytopenia began falling before POD5. However, the key was the preoperative catheterization. The prior exposure to heparin during the cath one week earlier likely primed the immune system, leading to an earlier onset than the textbook window suggests.

### The Pathophysiology: Why HIT Causes Stroke

The mechanism behind HIT-related thrombosis is immune-mediated platelet activation. Antibodies, typically IgG, form complexes with platelet factor 4 (PF4) and heparin. These complexes bind to Fc receptors on platelets, causing massive activation and thrombin generation. This creates a pro-thrombotic storm that can occlude cerebral vessels.

In this specific case, the patient had a rare and severe variant known as autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Unlike classic HIT, which requires heparin to trigger antibody binding, aHIT antibodies can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. The laboratory results were striking. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive. The confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) showed strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL of heparin, confirming heparin-independent platelet activation. Furthermore, the antibodies showed partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade, suggesting an unusually aggressive serological profile. This hypercoagulability was severe enough to cause a parietal stroke despite the patient being on prophylactic anticoagulation.

### Diagnostic Pitfalls and Timing

The biggest pitfall in diagnosing HIT is the "4 Ts" score, specifically the timing. Clinicians often rule out HIT if the platelet drop happens too early, assuming it is due to the surgery itself. In cardiac surgery, a mild drop in platelets is common on POD1 due to dilution and consumption. However, a progressive decline beyond POD3 or POD4 should raise alarm bells.

In this case, the onset was early (before POD5), which is atypical for primary post-cardiac surgery HIT. However, the patient had significant preoperative heparin exposure during the catheterization. About 10% of cardiac surgery patients with HIT present before POD5, usually linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure. If you see thrombocytopenia plus a new thrombus (like a stroke) in a patient with recent heparin exposure, you must test for HIT antibodies regardless of whether the count is "technically" within the classic window.

### Management: Stopping the Storm

Once HIT is suspected, the immediate action is to stop all heparin sources, including flushes and catheter locks. The patient in this case was switched from low-molecular-weight heparin (dalteparin) to fondaparinux initially, then to therapeutic argatroban once the stroke was confirmed. Argatroban was chosen for its short half-life given the recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia. High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was also administered, which is often used in aHIT to neutralize the antibodies.

Despite this, the patient’s hypercoagulability persisted. Fibrinogen levels fell, D-dimer rose, and schistocytes appeared on the blood film, indicating ongoing consumptive coagulopathy. The platelet count response to IVIG was modest. The team eventually switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux with supplemental antithrombin concentrate. This strategy finally worked. The platelet count recovered gradually, reaching normal levels by POD62, and the patient made a complete neurological recovery.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Stroke is a common complication of HIT:** If a patient on heparin develops new neurological deficits and thrombocytopenia, assume HIT until proven otherwise.
*   **Timing can be deceptive:** Early onset thrombocytopenia (before POD5) does not rule out HIT if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure, such as during catheterization.
*   **aHIT is severe:** Autoimmune HIT can present with heparin-independent platelet activation and may require aggressive management with non-heparin anticoagulants and IVIG.
*   **Stop all heparin:** Immediate cessation of all heparin products is critical, followed by initiation of a non-heparin anticoagulant like argatroban or fondaparinux.
*   **Monitor for recovery:** Platelet recovery can be slow in severe cases, and serological testing may remain positive for months even after clinical resolution.
