Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning in Recurrent Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion: Differential Diagnosis, Fluid Analysis, and Imaging

A child with recurrent, unilateral blood-stained pleural effusion should immediately prompt a broad differential diagnosis, because pleural fluid is a manifestation rather than a diagnosis. In this case, the effusion recurred after temporary relief from aspiration, which argued against a simple self-limited parapneumonic process and suggested either ongoing pleural inflammation, persistent leakage from an extrathoracic source, or an occult structural lesion. The key diagnostic task was therefore to explain why a young child had repeated large-volume, dark red pleural collections and to avoid anchoring on the chest alone.

Tuberculous pleural effusion was considered early because of the family contact history and a mildly raised pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA). However, ADA is only supportive, not diagnostic, and can be elevated in several inflammatory effusions. The absence of fever, chronic cough, weight loss, and systemic illness made tuberculosis less convincing, and GeneXpert for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was negative. In a child, these negative features matter because a positive exposure history can easily bias clinicians toward tuberculosis even when the overall pattern does not fit. The case illustrates that ADA should be interpreted in clinical context rather than used as a stand-alone test.

Empyema or a complicated parapneumonic effusion was also less likely. These usually present with febrile illness, toxicity, inflammatory blood counts, and pleural fluid that is frankly purulent or at least neutrophil-predominant. Although the chest radiograph and CT showed right lung changes, the repeated aspiration of dark red fluid, the lack of infectious symptoms, and the eventual discovery of a pancreatic tract argued against a primary thoracic infection. Likewise, malignancy was considered because some tumors can produce amylase-rich pleural effusions, but the child had no weight loss or constitutional decline, the blood picture and peripheral smear were normal, and pleural fluid cytology did not show malignant cells.

Amylase-rich pleural effusion narrows the differential considerably. The classic causes are pancreatopleural fistula, esophageal perforation, and certain malignancies. Boerhaave syndrome was unlikely here because there was no history of forceful vomiting, acute severe post-emetic chest pain, or mediastinal catastrophe. Other nonpancreatic explanations were also excluded by the case workup: acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not seen on imaging, hepatic hydrothorax was inconsistent with normal liver function and imaging, and renal pathology was not supported by renal tests or ultrasound. This stepwise exclusion is important, because a dramatic pleural effusion can tempt clinicians into a thoracic diagnosis when the source is actually subdiaphragmatic.

Pleural fluid analysis was the most important biochemical clue. In pancreatopleural fistula, pleural fluid is typically large volume, recurrent, and exudative, often appearing dark, brownish, or blood-stained. The decisive feature is markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase, usually accompanied by elevated lipase. In this patient, pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase 7211 U/L, which is far above the high-yield threshold of 1000 IU/L used to strongly suggest pancreatic origin. Serum amylase and lipase can be elevated as supportive evidence, but they are less specific than pleural fluid values because the leak may be localized and serum levels may not always track the severity of the fistula. When available, amylase isoenzyme testing can further refine the diagnosis: pancreatic-type amylase points toward a pancreatic source, whereas salivary-type amylase favors esophageal rupture or some malignancies.

Imaging then provided anatomic confirmation. Chest X-ray and bedside ultrasound established the presence and size of the effusion but did not explain its cause. Contrast-enhanced CT added crucial information by showing right hydropneumothorax, patchy right lung consolidation, and an elongated multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural region through the paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. This pattern suggested a retroperitoneal-to-thoracic track rather than isolated pleural disease. CT is useful because it is fast and widely available, and it can demonstrate collections, pseudocysts, ductal dilation, or indirect fistulous tracts, but it may not always show the actual communication.

MRCP then established the diagnosis more definitively. It demonstrated pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreas tracking posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow communication to the left pleural cavity and narrowing of the pancreatic duct proximal to the communication. This is the key advantage of MRCP in suspected pancreatopleural fistula: it noninvasively maps ductal anatomy and the fistulous tract without instrumentation. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning because it can directly define the duct and allow stent placement, but it is invasive and is usually reserved for cases in which intervention is being considered or when MRCP is inconclusive.

Taken together, the diagnosis emerged from convergence of history, fluid chemistry, and imaging rather than from any single test. The delayed disclosure of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier explained the likely pancreatic injury, and the recurrent dark red pleural fluid with marked amylase/lipase elevation supported pancreatic leakage into the pleural space. CT and MRCP then localized the tract and confirmed pancreatopleural fistula, including bilateral extension. Once this was established, empiric antitubercular therapy was no longer justified and was stopped, preventing further delay in appropriate management.
