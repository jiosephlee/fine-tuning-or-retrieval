Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Pancreatopleural Fistula in Children: Core Concepts and Pathophysiology

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is an internal pancreatic fistula in which pancreatic secretions escape from the pancreatic ductal system and track into the pleural cavity. It is called an “internal” fistula because the leak drains into another body compartment rather than to the skin or an external opening. In practical terms, the pancreas is the source of the problem, but the patient often presents with what appears to be a primarily thoracic illness. This is the key concept behind the “pulmonary mask” described in the case: repeated pleural effusions and respiratory distress may dominate the presentation even though the initiating pathology lies in the abdomen.

PPF is exceptionally rare in children, with only scattered case reports in the pediatric literature, whereas it is better recognized in adults. That rarity contributes to delayed diagnosis. Clinicians usually investigate common causes of pleural effusion first, such as infection, tuberculosis, malignancy, or primary lung disease. In a child, the diagnosis is even easier to miss because pancreatic disease is not initially high on the differential when the main complaints are chest pain, dyspnea, and recurrent effusion. Repeated thoracentesis may temporarily relieve symptoms, but the pleural fluid typically reaccumulates until the pancreatic leak is identified and controlled.

The causes of PPF in children differ somewhat from those in adults. In adults, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis is the classic setting. In children, the more relevant etiologies are acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, blunt abdominal trauma, rupture of a pancreatic pseudocyst, and hereditary pancreatitis. This distinction matters because a child with recurrent pancreatic inflammation should raise concern for an underlying genetic predisposition, especially if episodes recur without a clear environmental trigger. Variants in PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC are important pediatric risk factors because they can predispose to recurrent pancreatitis, duct injury, and pseudocyst formation.

The anatomy explains how a pancreatic injury can present as pleural disease. The pancreas is retroperitoneal, and its ductal system runs close to spaces that can transmit fluid posteriorly. When the pancreatic duct is disrupted, or when a pseudocyst leaks or ruptures, enzyme-rich fluid can escape into the retroperitoneum. If the disruption is posterior, the fluid tends to move upward along the path of least resistance. It may enter the mediastinum through natural diaphragmatic openings, most often the esophageal hiatus and less commonly the aortic hiatus, and from there extend into the pleural cavity. Less commonly, direct trans-diaphragmatic erosion may occur. This is why imaging may show a retroperitoneal collection tracking into the chest, as in this child.

The direction of leakage determines the clinical pattern. An anterior duct disruption usually leads to pancreatic ascites because the fluid spills into the peritoneal cavity. A posterior disruption, however, favors retroperitoneal spread and thoracic extension, producing mediastinal involvement and pleural effusion. Pleural effusions from PPF may be unilateral, bilateral, massive, and recurrent. Although left-sided effusions are often described because of the usual anatomic course from the body and tail of the pancreas, right-sided or bilateral effusions certainly occur, depending on the route of tracking and the side of diaphragmatic passage. In this case, the effusion was predominantly right-sided, with imaging later showing a tract into the right pleural cavity and a smaller communication with the left side.

The pleural fluid in PPF is characteristically rich in pancreatic enzymes, especially amylase and often lipase. This reflects direct contamination of the pleural space by pancreatic juice rather than a primary inflammatory pleural process. The fluid may also appear blood-stained or dark red, likely because pancreatic enzymes produce intense local inflammation and tissue injury, which can increase capillary permeability and cause hemorrhagic admixture. A markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase is one of the most important clues to the diagnosis, especially when it is far higher than would be expected in other causes of exudative effusion. In the child described here, the very high pleural fluid amylase and lipase strongly supported a pancreatic source.

The reason respiratory symptoms dominate is that the pleural effusion itself becomes the immediate cause of morbidity. A large effusion compresses the lung, causing tachypnea, hypoxemia, chest pain, and reduced breath sounds. Because the abdomen may remain soft and relatively silent, clinicians can be misled into pursuing only thoracic explanations. This “pulmonary mask” is especially important in children, where a history of trauma may be missed unless it is specifically elicited. In this case, the bicycle-handle injury became apparent only after repeated history-taking, and that detail was crucial for connecting the recurrent pleural effusions to pancreatic trauma.

When pediatric PPF is suspected, it is important to think about underlying pancreatitis risk factors, especially if the course is recurrent or unexplained. Genetic causes are particularly relevant in children, and recurrent episodes should prompt consideration of hereditary pancreatitis syndromes linked to PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC. Recognizing these associations helps explain why a child may develop repeated ductal injury or pseudocyst formation after what seems like a limited inciting event. Understanding this pathophysiology is essential to interpreting the case: a seemingly primary pleural disease was actually the downstream manifestation of a disrupted pancreatic duct with retroperitoneal and pleural extension.

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Stabilization: Recognizing the Pulmonary Mask

This child’s illness illustrates how a pancreatopleural fistula can first appear to be a primary thoracic problem. He presented repeatedly over a short period with right-sided chest pain, progressive dyspnea, and respiratory distress. Each admission was driven by the same pattern: a large right pleural effusion causing symptoms, temporary relief after drainage, and then rapid recurrence within days. This recurring course is a key clue that the pleural effusion is not simply a one-time inflammatory or infectious event, but may reflect an ongoing source of fluid entry into the pleural space.

At the bedside, the initial assessment was dominated by respiratory findings. The child had mild tachypnea, hypoxemia with oxygen saturation in the high 80s on room air, and tachycardia, all of which signaled physiologic stress from impaired ventilation. Examination of the right hemithorax showed reduced chest movement, stony dullness to percussion, decreased vocal fremitus, and diminished breath sounds over the dependent lung fields. These are classic findings of a large pleural effusion and help distinguish pleural fluid from parenchymal consolidation alone. Because the effusion was large and the child was symptomatic, he required Pediatric Intensive Care Unit monitoring and supplemental oxygen while the diagnostic workup proceeded.

The first tests appropriately focused on confirming the pleural process and quantifying its extent. A chest radiograph showed a right pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle, and bedside lung ultrasound demonstrated a large effusion of about one liter. In a child with respiratory distress, ultrasound is especially useful because it can rapidly confirm pleural fluid at the bedside and guide drainage. Therapeutic thoracocentesis was therefore performed early, which immediately improved the dyspnea and oxygen saturation. This transient improvement is clinically important: it confirms that the symptoms are being driven by pleural compression, but it does not explain why the effusion keeps returning.

The repeated aspiration of dark red pleural fluid should prompt the clinician to widen the differential diagnosis beyond a routine parapneumonic effusion. Blood-stained effusions raise concern for tuberculosis, malignancy, trauma, pulmonary embolic phenomena in older patients, and less commonly pancreatic disease. In this case, the recurring hemorrhagic appearance was an important warning sign that the pleural space was receiving an abnormal fluid with a different origin. The persistent recurrence after each tap made a simple infectious effusion less likely and suggested an ongoing communication or leak.

History taking was therefore crucial, and the eventual diagnosis depended on revisiting it carefully. The child had a history of contact with a relative treated for tuberculosis, and the pleural fluid ADA was mildly elevated, so antitubercular therapy was started empirically. This is understandable in a setting where tuberculosis is common, but it also illustrates how anchoring on a plausible pulmonary diagnosis can delay recognition of a non-thoracic cause. Only after repeated admissions and repeated questioning did the child disclose blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier. That seemingly minor detail became the pivotal clue, because it pointed toward pancreatic injury as the source of the recurrent pleural effusion.

The immediate priority in such a presentation is stabilization of airway, breathing, and oxygenation while the cause is being investigated. The child received oxygen, monitoring, and prompt drainage of the large effusion to relieve respiratory compromise. This supportive approach is essential because even before the definitive diagnosis is known, the pleural space itself can be the main driver of clinical instability. At the same time, the pattern of recurrent large, dark red effusions after abdominal trauma should keep pancreatopleural fistula in the differential, especially when pulmonary investigations do not fully explain the course.

## Diagnostic Reasoning in Recurrent Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion: Differential Diagnosis, Fluid Analysis, and Imaging

A child with recurrent, unilateral blood-stained pleural effusion should immediately prompt a broad differential diagnosis, because pleural fluid is a manifestation rather than a diagnosis. In this case, the effusion recurred after temporary relief from aspiration, which argued against a simple self-limited parapneumonic process and suggested either ongoing pleural inflammation, persistent leakage from an extrathoracic source, or an occult structural lesion. The key diagnostic task was therefore to explain why a young child had repeated large-volume, dark red pleural collections and to avoid anchoring on the chest alone.

Tuberculous pleural effusion was considered early because of the family contact history and a mildly raised pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA). However, ADA is only supportive, not diagnostic, and can be elevated in several inflammatory effusions. The absence of fever, chronic cough, weight loss, and systemic illness made tuberculosis less convincing, and GeneXpert for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was negative. In a child, these negative features matter because a positive exposure history can easily bias clinicians toward tuberculosis even when the overall pattern does not fit. The case illustrates that ADA should be interpreted in clinical context rather than used as a stand-alone test.

Empyema or a complicated parapneumonic effusion was also less likely. These usually present with febrile illness, toxicity, inflammatory blood counts, and pleural fluid that is frankly purulent or at least neutrophil-predominant. Although the chest radiograph and CT showed right lung changes, the repeated aspiration of dark red fluid, the lack of infectious symptoms, and the eventual discovery of a pancreatic tract argued against a primary thoracic infection. Likewise, malignancy was considered because some tumors can produce amylase-rich pleural effusions, but the child had no weight loss or constitutional decline, the blood picture and peripheral smear were normal, and pleural fluid cytology did not show malignant cells.

Amylase-rich pleural effusion narrows the differential considerably. The classic causes are pancreatopleural fistula, esophageal perforation, and certain malignancies. Boerhaave syndrome was unlikely here because there was no history of forceful vomiting, acute severe post-emetic chest pain, or mediastinal catastrophe. Other nonpancreatic explanations were also excluded by the case workup: acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not seen on imaging, hepatic hydrothorax was inconsistent with normal liver function and imaging, and renal pathology was not supported by renal tests or ultrasound. This stepwise exclusion is important, because a dramatic pleural effusion can tempt clinicians into a thoracic diagnosis when the source is actually subdiaphragmatic.

Pleural fluid analysis was the most important biochemical clue. In pancreatopleural fistula, pleural fluid is typically large volume, recurrent, and exudative, often appearing dark, brownish, or blood-stained. The decisive feature is markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase, usually accompanied by elevated lipase. In this patient, pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase 7211 U/L, which is far above the high-yield threshold of 1000 IU/L used to strongly suggest pancreatic origin. Serum amylase and lipase can be elevated as supportive evidence, but they are less specific than pleural fluid values because the leak may be localized and serum levels may not always track the severity of the fistula. When available, amylase isoenzyme testing can further refine the diagnosis: pancreatic-type amylase points toward a pancreatic source, whereas salivary-type amylase favors esophageal rupture or some malignancies.

Imaging then provided anatomic confirmation. Chest X-ray and bedside ultrasound established the presence and size of the effusion but did not explain its cause. Contrast-enhanced CT added crucial information by showing right hydropneumothorax, patchy right lung consolidation, and an elongated multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural region through the paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. This pattern suggested a retroperitoneal-to-thoracic track rather than isolated pleural disease. CT is useful because it is fast and widely available, and it can demonstrate collections, pseudocysts, ductal dilation, or indirect fistulous tracts, but it may not always show the actual communication.

MRCP then established the diagnosis more definitively. It demonstrated pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreas tracking posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow communication to the left pleural cavity and narrowing of the pancreatic duct proximal to the communication. This is the key advantage of MRCP in suspected pancreatopleural fistula: it noninvasively maps ductal anatomy and the fistulous tract without instrumentation. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning because it can directly define the duct and allow stent placement, but it is invasive and is usually reserved for cases in which intervention is being considered or when MRCP is inconclusive.

Taken together, the diagnosis emerged from convergence of history, fluid chemistry, and imaging rather than from any single test. The delayed disclosure of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier explained the likely pancreatic injury, and the recurrent dark red pleural fluid with marked amylase/lipase elevation supported pancreatic leakage into the pleural space. CT and MRCP then localized the tract and confirmed pancreatopleural fistula, including bilateral extension. Once this was established, empiric antitubercular therapy was no longer justified and was stopped, preventing further delay in appropriate management.

## Management of Pediatric Pancreatopleural Fistula: Conservative Therapy First, Escalation When Needed

The immediate priorities in pancreatopleural fistula are to stabilize respiration and to stop the pancreatic leak that is feeding the pleural space. The pleural effusion itself causes dyspnea, chest pain, and hypoxemia, so drainage is needed first for symptom relief. However, repeated pleural taps alone do not treat the source of the problem; if pancreatic secretions continue to escape through the fistula, the effusion will simply reaccumulate. In this case, the child initially improved after thoracocentesis, but the rapid recurrence of a large right pleural effusion showed that supportive drainage without pancreatic rest was not enough.

For acute pleural decompression, repeated thoracocentesis is useful when the effusion is large and causing respiratory distress, especially as a first temporizing measure and as a diagnostic step. When effusions recur quickly, when a very large volume must be removed repeatedly, or when hydropneumothorax is present, an intercostal drainage tube becomes more practical because it provides continuous evacuation and better symptom control. That is why the child ultimately received a right intercostal drain after multiple recurrences. The drain also allowed the team to follow output over time, which becomes one of the most useful bedside indicators of whether the fistula is closing.

Once pancreatopleural fistula was recognized, management shifted to conservative pancreatic rest. The child was kept nil per oral and given total parenteral nutrition to bypass the gastrointestinal tract and minimize pancreatic stimulation. Octreotide was started as a somatostatin analogue to suppress exocrine pancreatic secretion, reduce splanchnic blood flow, and lower the volume of fluid traversing the fistula. In practice, octreotide is commonly used in weight-based dosing, and this child received a continuous infusion at 30 µg/kg/day, which falls within the range reported in pediatric cases. During treatment, the main things to monitor are drain output, respiratory status, and clinical tolerance; glucose disturbances and gastrointestinal side effects are also important to watch for, particularly when treatment is prolonged.

Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy was added as an adjunct. Its role in fistula closure is less direct than octreotide or bowel rest, but it is often used during recovery to support nutrition and may reduce endogenous pancreatic stimulation through feedback mechanisms. In this child, the combined approach of bowel rest, TPN, octreotide, PERT, and pleural drainage produced a clear response: the intercostal drain output steadily fell over about 10 days, respiratory symptoms improved, and the tube could be removed before discharge. That rapid decrease in drainage is exactly the kind of bedside sign that suggests the fistulous tract is closing.

This case also shows why conservative treatment was reasonable even though MRCP demonstrated ductal narrowing proximal to the communication. Ductal abnormality raises concern for persistent leakage, and it is one reason some patients eventually need intervention, but conservative therapy is still the usual first step in children if they are clinically stable and responding. Pediatric guidelines are not standardized because the condition is so rare, and published success rates for conservative therapy are modest. For that reason, close observation is essential, and the treatment plan must be ready to escalate if the leak does not settle.

Escalation to ERCP is considered when there is persistent high-output drainage, failure of improvement after roughly 2 to 4 weeks of medical therapy, or when ductal decompression is needed because of stenosis or obstruction. ERCP can allow transpapillary stenting, sphincterotomy, or balloon dilatation in selected strictures; the key principle is to lower intraductal pressure so pancreatic juice preferentially flows into the duodenum rather than through the fistula. Surgery is reserved for severe ductal obstruction, a distal fistula that cannot be treated endoscopically, or failure of medical and endoscopic therapy. Depending on anatomy, options include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy, or cystojejunostomy. In this child, those steps were not needed because conservative therapy succeeded promptly, and the absence of further pleural collections after discharge confirmed that the nonoperative approach had worked.

## Outcome, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons for Practice

In this child, conservative management was successful. After the diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula was confirmed, antitubercular therapy was stopped, the child was kept nil per oral, total parenteral nutrition was started, octreotide was given, and pleural drainage was maintained with an intercostal drain. Over the next 10 days, the drain output steadily decreased and then stopped, allowing the chest tube to be removed and the child to be discharged. This short-term response is important because it shows that some pediatric pancreatopleural fistulas, particularly those recognized early and without a fixed structural obstruction requiring immediate intervention, can close with bowel rest and suppression of pancreatic secretion.

Follow-up after discharge should focus on two parallel goals: confirming that the fistula has not recurred and ensuring that the child is recovering functionally. In practice, this means monitoring for new chest pain, dyspnea, cough, or rapid reaccumulation of pleural fluid, while also assessing nutrition, weight gain, and overall activity tolerance. In this case, the child remained symptom-free at follow-up, had no further pleural effusions, returned to school, and was able to run and play without difficulty. Those later findings are reassuring because they suggest that the acute pancreatic leak resolved and that respiratory reserve normalized.

Lifestyle counseling was also part of the child’s recovery. He was advised to adopt a more balanced diet and regular exercise, which is sensible given his marked obesity and likely metabolic risk. However, it is important to separate this counseling from the cause of the fistula itself: the fistula followed blunt abdominal trauma and pancreatic duct disruption, not diet-related disease. Counseling in this context supports long-term health, but it does not replace the need to think about traumatic or pancreatic pathology when recurrent pleural effusions are present.

After recovery, further pancreatic evaluation is warranted if the child develops recurrent abdominal pain, recurrent pleural effusions, or recurrent pancreatitis, or if there is a strong family history suggesting hereditary pancreatitis. In children, repeated pancreatic events should prompt consideration of genetic causes such as PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, or CTRC-related disease, because an apparently isolated episode may be the first clue to an underlying predisposition. This matters because recurrence risk and long-term management differ if the child has chronic or hereditary pancreatic disease rather than a single traumatic event.

This case also illustrates the cost of delayed diagnosis. The child underwent repeated thoracocenteses and was briefly exposed to unnecessary antitubercular treatment before the pancreatic source was recognized. In other cases, such delays can lead to prolonged hospitalization, repeated invasive procedures, persistent respiratory distress, and infection of the pleural or retroperitoneal collections, with a risk of sepsis. The key problem is not that pleural drainage was wrong, but that drainage alone treats the consequence, not the source.

For learners, the bedside clues that should raise suspicion for pancreatopleural fistula are fairly consistent: a massive or recurrent unilateral pleural effusion, rapid reaccumulation after aspiration, dark red or hemorrhagic pleural fluid, and a lack of convincing pulmonary infectious features. A history of recent or remote abdominal trauma is particularly important, even if it is discovered only after repeated questioning. Amylase-rich pleural fluid is a major diagnostic clue, and when the pleural fluid amylase is markedly elevated, the diagnosis should shift away from common thoracic causes.

The main cognitive lesson from this case is to avoid a purely thoracic frame when the clinical course does not fit. Recurrent pleural effusion that improves transiently after aspiration should trigger a broader search for an extrapulmonary source, especially when the fluid is bloody and routine pulmonary workup is unrevealing. Re-taking the history is often decisive, because the inciting abdominal event may not be volunteered at first. In this case, the diagnosis emerged only after repeated admissions, revisiting the trauma history, and correlating it with imaging and pleural fluid biochemistry.

A practical approach to future cases is to start with standard pleural fluid assessment, but to add amylase and lipase early when the effusion is recurrent, unexplained, or hemorrhagic. If these are elevated, pancreatic imaging should follow, with CT to look for collections and MRCP to define the duct and any fistulous tract. ERCP may then be considered when therapeutic planning is needed, especially if ductal intervention is being contemplated. Working across pediatrics, radiology, pulmonology, gastroenterology, surgery, and intensive care improves diagnosis and avoids isolated decision-making.

The central message of this report is straightforward: in a child with recurrent unexplained pleural effusion, especially when the aspirate is dark red and the effusion reaccumulates quickly, pancreatopleural fistula must be considered. Early recognition redirects care toward the true source of the problem, prevents repeated unnecessary procedures, and gives the child the best chance of recovery with conservative therapy.
