Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the respective roles and main limitations of contrast-enhanced CT, MRCP, and ERCP in the diagnosis and treatment planning of pancreatopleural fistula in children?

In a child with recurrent, amylase-rich pleural effusion, the three studies play different roles.

**Contrast-enhanced CT is usually the first problem-solving study.** Its value is to show the *consequences* of pancreatic disruption and to raise suspicion for a pancreatopleural fistula: in this case, CT demonstrated right hydropneumothorax and a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas, which strongly suggested a pancreatic source. CT is also useful to look for pancreatitis, pseudocyst, ductal dilatation, and other abdominal or thoracic pathology. **Its main limitation** is that it often does **not directly show the fistulous tract**; the literature in the report notes fistula visualization in only about **33%–47%** of cases.

**MRCP is the best noninvasive test to define the pancreatic ductal anatomy and confirm the fistula.** In this case, MRCP clearly showed the collection in the distal pancreas tracking through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural cavity, with communication to the pancreatic duct and even a contralateral pleural connection. This is why MRCP is so useful once PPF is suspected: it maps the ductal disruption without instrumentation. **Its main limitation** is that it is diagnostic rather than therapeutic; it tells you where the leak is, but it does not treat it or allow stent placement.

**ERCP is the gold standard for treatment planning, not just diagnosis.** It best defines ductal anatomy when endoscopic therapy is being considered and allows **transpapillary stenting or balloon dilation**, which lowers intraductal pressure and promotes fistula closure. The report notes that ERCP visualizes the fistula in **38%–78%** of children and is the key study when endoscopic intervention is likely. **Its limitations** are that it is invasive, requires technical expertise, and may not be feasible if there is severe ductal obstruction or a distal fistula; it also carries the usual procedure-related risks.  

So, in practice: **CT suggests the diagnosis, MRCP confirms and maps it, and ERCP plans and delivers definitive endoscopic therapy when needed.**
