Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Morning Report] In a child repeatedly admitted with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions that improve after thoracentesis but recur within days, what elements of the history and imaging should prompt you to shift the diagnostic focus from a primary thoracic process to an abdominal source?

In a child whose pleural effusion is **recurrent, large, and rapidly reaccumulates after thoracentesis**, the key shift in thinking comes when the picture no longer fits a primary pleural or pulmonary disease.

**History clues that should push you toward an abdominal source** are:
- **Recent blunt abdominal trauma** — in this case, a bicycle-handle injury was elicited only after repeated history-taking.
- A **recurrent, blood-stained or dark red effusion**, which is unusual for simple parapneumonic disease.
- **Minimal thoracic infectious symptoms** and no convincing systemic picture for tuberculosis, empyema, or malignancy.
- Any history suggestive of **pancreatic injury or pancreatitis**, even if abdominal symptoms are subtle or absent.

**Imaging clues are even more decisive**:
- A pleural effusion that is accompanied by **hydropneumothorax** or an atypical, loculated collection.
- CT findings of a **retroperitoneal or paraesophageal hypodense collection tracking upward through the esophageal hiatus** toward the pleural cavity.
- Evidence of **communication with the pancreatic tail or pancreatic duct**, or associated pancreatitis/pseudocyst.

In other words, when a child has a “pulmonary” effusion that keeps returning, especially if it is **hemorrhagic and right-sided**, you should think beyond the chest and look for a **pancreatic leak or pancreatopleural fistula**. In this case, the combination of **recent abdominal trauma** and **CT/MRCP showing a tract from the pancreas to the pleura** correctly redirected the diagnosis away from a primary thoracic process.
