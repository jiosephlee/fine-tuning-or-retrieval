Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Pathophysiology] How can blunt pancreatic trauma lead to a pleural effusion rather than pancreatic ascites, and why does posterior pancreatic duct disruption preferentially produce thoracic manifestations?

Blunt pancreatic trauma can cause a **ductal leak or pancreatic pseudocyst rupture**. Whether the patient develops **ascites** or a **pleural effusion** depends on the **direction of the leak**.

If the disruption is **anterior**, pancreatic juice escapes into the **peritoneal cavity**, producing **pancreatic ascites**. If the disruption is **posterior**, the pancreas is already retroperitoneal, so leaked enzyme-rich fluid enters the **retroperitoneum** rather than the peritoneum. From there, it tends to track **upward along the path of least resistance**, often through the **esophageal or aortic hiatus** into the **mediastinum**, and then through the pleura into the thoracic cavity, forming a **pancreatopleural fistula** and a large, recurrent pleural effusion.

That is why **posterior duct disruption preferentially produces thoracic manifestations**: the retroperitoneum provides a conduit to the chest, and the pleural space functions as a low-pressure reservoir. In the case reported, the child’s blunt bicycle-handle trauma likely injured the distal duct/body-tail region; MRCP showed a tract from the pancreatic tail **posteriorly into the right pleural cavity**, and the pleural fluid was markedly **amylase-rich**, confirming the thoracic route of pancreatic leakage.

So the key teaching point is: **anterior leak = ascites; posterior leak = retroperitoneal tracking to the mediastinum and pleura, causing recurrent pleural effusion.**
