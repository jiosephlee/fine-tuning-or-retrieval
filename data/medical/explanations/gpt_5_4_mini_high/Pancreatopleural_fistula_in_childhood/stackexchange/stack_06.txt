Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Why can a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA be misleading in this setting, and what findings in this case argued against empiric treatment for tuberculosis despite a history of TB contact?

A mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA can be misleading because ADA is **not specific for tuberculosis**; it is a marker of **cellular immune activation**, and modest elevations can occur in other exudative pleural processes, including **inflammatory pancreatic pleural effusions**. In this case, the ADA result was a distraction because the overall pleural fluid profile and imaging strongly pointed to a **pancreatopleural fistula**, not TB.

Several findings argued against empiric antitubercular therapy despite the history of TB contact:

- The child had **no constitutional symptoms** suggestive of tuberculosis — no fever, chronic cough, or weight loss.
- The pleural fluid was **recurrently dark red/blood-stained**, which is not typical of tuberculous pleuritis.
- Most importantly, the fluid was **markedly amylase- and lipase-rich**; in this report, pleural fluid amylase was **6118 U/L**, which is highly supportive of pancreatopleural fistula.
- **GeneXpert for MTB was negative**, further weakening the TB diagnosis.
- CT and MRCP demonstrated a **tract from the pancreatic region to the pleural cavity**, confirming a pancreatic source.
- The history of **blunt abdominal trauma** provided a clear alternative explanation.

So, the teaching point is that a **slightly elevated ADA should never override the whole clinical picture**. In a child with **recurrent hemorrhagic, amylase-rich pleural effusion**, the priority is to search for a pancreatic source rather than assume tuberculosis based on exposure history alone.
