Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# TB Contact, Mildly Elevated ADA, and the Risk of Anchoring

## Why this case sticks with you

A child with recurrent pleural effusions, a TB contact, and a mildly elevated pleural ADA is exactly the kind of case that can pull a team toward tuberculosis. In this report, that pull was strong enough that the child was empirically started on antitubercular therapy.

And yet the real diagnosis lived somewhere else entirely: a pancreatopleural fistula after blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle.

That’s the teaching point. This wasn’t just a rare pancreatic complication wearing a thoracic disguise. It was also a case of diagnostic anchoring, premature closure, and diagnostic momentum in action.

## The setup: a thoracic problem that wasn’t thoracic

The child presented with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory distress. Imaging showed a large right pleural effusion, and bedside ultrasound confirmed a substantial collection. Thoracocentesis temporarily improved his symptoms, but the effusion came right back.

On the second admission, the pleural fluid ADA was mildly elevated, and there was a history of TB contact. That combination is familiar, reassuring, and dangerous. Familiar because TB is common in the differential of pleural effusion. Dangerous because familiarity can become a shortcut.

So the team did what many of us might do: they anchored on TB.

But a few details didn’t fit neatly:
- The effusion was recurrent and large-volume
- The pleural fluid was dark red
- The child had no fever, chronic cough, or weight loss
- GeneXpert for MTB was negative
- The problem kept recurring despite drainage

When a diagnosis keeps failing to explain the whole picture, the job is not to defend the diagnosis harder. It’s to reopen the case.

## The cognitive error: how anchoring happens

Anchoring is when an early piece of information grabs the diagnostic steering wheel and doesn’t let go.

In this case, the anchor was:
- TB contact history
- Mildly elevated ADA

From there, a second error often follows: premature closure. Once TB is “good enough,” we stop digging.

Then diagnostic momentum kicks in: once a label is applied, each subsequent clinician starts from that label rather than from the raw clinical problem. Now the patient doesn’t have “recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion of unclear cause”; he has “probable TB.”

That’s the trap.

Pleural ADA can be helpful, but it is not a diagnosis. It is a clue that must be interpreted in context. And in this child, the context was not adding up.

## The history that changed everything

The real diagnostic rescue came from going back to the bedside and asking again.

On the third admission, the child revealed a blunt abdominal injury from a bicycle handle about a month earlier.

That’s the kind of history that can be missed the first time, especially in pediatrics. Kids may minimize symptoms, forget details, or not volunteer something they don’t think is “important.” Repeated history-taking is not redundancy. It’s diagnostic strategy.

Once trauma entered the story, the next steps made sense:
- CT showed a multiloculated collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas
- MRCP confirmed a fistulous communication into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow connection to the left side as well
- Pleural fluid amylase and lipase were markedly elevated
- The diagnosis became pancreatopleural fistula

## Why pancreatopleural fistula is such a good mimic

PPF is rare in children, and that rarity is exactly why it gets missed. Most of us think “pleural effusion” and go to the chest. But PPF is a pancreatic leak with thoracic consequences.

Pancreatic fluid can track posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then ascend through the diaphragmatic hiatus into the mediastinum and pleura. The chest is where the symptoms land, even though the leak begins in the abdomen.

That is why the presentation can be so misleading:
- recurrent large pleural effusions
- chest pain
- dyspnea
- sometimes blood-stained pleural fluid
- minimal or absent abdominal symptoms

In this child, the respiratory complaints were the headline. The pancreas was the plot twist.

## What should have slowed the TB reflex?

A few features should always make us pause before labeling a pleural effusion as TB:

- Recurrent massive effusions despite drainage
- Dark red or frankly hemorrhagic pleural fluid
- Minimal systemic symptoms
- Negative MTB testing
- A history of trauma, pancreatitis, or abdominal pain
- Very high pleural fluid amylase

In this case, the pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L. That is not a subtle clue. When amylase in pleural fluid is markedly elevated, pancreatic or esophageal sources move up the list fast.

## Practical bedside lesson: don’t let ADA work alone

Pleural ADA can support TB in the right clinical setting, but it is not specific. If the rest of the story is off, don’t let a mildly elevated ADA carry the whole case.

A useful mental move is this: when ADA is elevated, ask yourself, “What else causes recurrent exudative or hemorrhagic pleural effusion with high amylase?”

Think about:
- pancreatopleural fistula
- esophageal perforation
- malignancy
- less commonly other inflammatory causes

In children, if the effusion is recurrent and amylase-rich, pancreatic pathology should be on the radar early.

## How the case was managed

Once the diagnosis was made, treatment shifted away from TB and toward pancreatic rest:
- the child was kept nil per oral
- total parenteral nutrition was started
- octreotide infusion was given
- pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy was started
- chest drainage was maintained with an intercostal drain

Over 10 days, the drainage decreased and stopped, and the tube was removed. He was discharged symptom-free and remained well on follow-up.

That outcome matters because it reinforces the value of stepping back before committing to a diagnosis that doesn’t fully explain the case.

## What this case teaches about pleural effusion workups

The big lesson is not “don’t ever think TB.” It’s “don’t stop thinking TB too early.”

When you see recurrent pleural effusion, especially if it is large-volume or hemorrhagic, your job is to build a differential that stays elastic. If the first explanation doesn’t fit all the data, resist the urge to force it.

Repeated history-taking matters. So does listening for the single detail that didn’t seem relevant the first time. In this case, that detail was bicycle-handle trauma.

## Take-home points

- TB contact plus a mildly elevated pleural ADA can anchor the team too early.
- Recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion should trigger a broader differential, especially if it keeps coming back after drainage.
- High pleural fluid amylase should immediately raise concern for a pancreatic source, including pancreatopleural fistula.
- In children, repeated history-taking can uncover the key clue that was missed initially.
- If the diagnosis doesn’t explain the whole story, reopen the case instead of defending the label.

## Final thought

This case is a good reminder that the diagnosis is sometimes hiding in plain sight, but behind a cognitive screen. The chest findings were real, the TB exposure was real, and the ADA was real. But none of them were the whole story.

The real skill is not just recognizing the plausible diagnosis. It’s recognizing when plausibility has turned into anchoring.
