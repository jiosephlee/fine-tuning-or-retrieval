Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# A Pancreatic Disease Wearing a Pulmonary Mask

## The surprise in this case

A child with recurrent **right-sided pleural effusions**, chest pain, and respiratory distress does not immediately make you think of the pancreas. That’s exactly what makes this case so important. The pleural effusion kept coming back, the fluid was **dark red**, and the child improved briefly after drainage—classic setup for anchoring on a thoracic problem.

But the real pathology was **below the diaphragm**: a **post-traumatic pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)** tracking from the pancreatic tail into the pleural space, confirmed on MRCP.

In other words: this was a pancreatic injury wearing a pulmonary mask.

## Why PPF gets missed

PPF is rare in children, but when it happens, it can be deeply misleading. These patients often present like they have a primary chest problem:

- dyspnea
- chest pain
- recurrent pleural effusion
- respiratory distress

What they often **don’t** have is dramatic abdominal pain, vomiting, or obvious pancreatitis. That absence is part of why the diagnosis slips by.

In this case, the child first underwent repeated thoracocenteses and even got started on antitubercular therapy because of a contact history and mildly elevated ADA. That’s a very human diagnostic trap: recurrent hemorrhagic effusion plus a TB contact can pull the team in one direction, while the true source is somewhere else entirely.

## The clues that should make you pause

Several features should make a pancreatic source rise on the differential:

A pleural effusion that is **recurrent and massive**, especially if it re-accumulates quickly after aspiration.

A **blood-stained or dark red pleural fluid**. That is not specific for PPF, but in the right clinical setting it should make you think beyond infection and malignancy.

A history of **blunt abdominal trauma**, even if it comes out late. In this case, the child eventually revealed bicycle handle trauma one month earlier.

An effusion with **very high pleural fluid amylase and lipase**. Here, pleural fluid amylase was **6118 U/L** and lipase **7211 U/L**. That is the key biochemical clue.

And finally, imaging that shows a collection or tract moving from the retroperitoneum toward the thorax, rather than a primary lung process.

## Anatomy matters here

The pathophysiology is actually elegant, if painful. Pancreatic duct disruption or a pancreatic collection can leak posteriorly into the retroperitoneum. From there, pancreatic fluid can follow the path of least resistance upward through the **oesophageal or aortic hiatus**, enter the mediastinum, and then break into the pleural space.

That’s why a pancreatic leak can look like a chest disease.

This child’s CT showed a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail, with right-sided hydropneumothorax. MRCP then nailed it: pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail, tracking into the **right pleural cavity**, with a narrow communication to the **left pleural cavity** as well.

## Imaging and labs: what helps most

CT is useful, but it may not fully define the fistula. In this case, CT suggested the abnormal track, but **MRCP** confirmed the ductal anatomy and the fistulous communication.

That distinction matters because PPF is fundamentally a **duct problem**. Once you see the connection to the pancreatic duct, the diagnosis becomes much more secure.

A practical point: when you tap a recurrent pleural effusion and the fluid is hemorrhagic or dark brown-red, send it for **amylase and lipase** early. Don’t wait for the third recurrence.

Also, elevated pleural fluid ADA does not automatically mean tuberculosis. TB remains important in many settings, but hemorrhagic recurrent effusions with pancreatic enzyme elevation should redirect the workup.

## Management: drain the chest, rest the pancreas

Initial management is usually conservative, especially in children and when there isn’t a clear ductal obstruction requiring immediate intervention.

This child was treated with:

- oxygen support
- repeat thoracocentesis, then intercostal drainage
- **nil per oral**
- **total parenteral nutrition**
- **octreotide infusion**
- **pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy**

The logic is straightforward: reduce pancreatic secretion, give the duct a chance to seal, and control the pleural burden while the leak closes.

In this case, the drainage gradually fell over about **10 days**, the chest tube was removed, and the child was discharged symptom-free.

If conservative management fails, the next step in many cases is **ERCP with trans-papillary stenting**. Surgery is reserved for refractory disease, ductal anatomy that won’t cooperate, or persistent leakage.

## What this case should change in your thinking

When a child has a recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusion, especially if it is blood-stained and the story doesn’t quite fit infection or malignancy, stop and ask:

“Could this be pancreatic?”

That one question can save days of repeated drainage, unnecessary antibiotics, and misdirected workups.

## Take-home points

- **Recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion is not always a lung problem.**
- **High pleural fluid amylase/lipase should trigger a pancreatic workup.**
- **Ask again about abdominal trauma, even if the history is delayed or subtle.**
- **MRCP is particularly useful for defining the fistula and ductal anatomy.**
- **Conservative therapy can work, but only if you make the diagnosis first.**

The chest may be where the child lands, but sometimes the source of the problem is quietly leaking from the pancreas.
