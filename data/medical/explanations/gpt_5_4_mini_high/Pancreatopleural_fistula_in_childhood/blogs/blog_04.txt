Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# CT, MRCP, or ERCP? Imaging Strategy for Suspected Pancreatopleural Fistula

## The case that should make you pause before calling it “just another pleural effusion”

A late-childhood boy kept coming back with a recurrent right-sided pleural effusion, chest pain, and respiratory distress. The pleural fluid was dark red each time. He briefly improved after thoracentesis, only to re-present days later. Tuberculosis was considered. Then, on the third admission, a more careful history revealed blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier.

That’s the trap with pancreatopleural fistula (PPF): the chest gets your attention, but the problem is in the pancreas.

In this case, the imaging sequence mattered. CT first suggested the diagnosis by showing a retroperitoneal collection tracking from the paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail. MRCP then mapped the ductal disruption and fistulous tract. ERCP was not needed diagnostically here, but in many cases it becomes the therapeutic tool.

So when do you choose CT, MRCP, or ERCP in suspected PPF? And how do you avoid over-scanning a child while still getting the answer?

## First, understand the anatomy of the leak

PPF is basically a pancreatic duct rupture that finds a way into the chest.

The usual route is:

- pancreatic duct disruption or pseudocyst
- leakage into the retroperitoneum
- tracking upward along the path of least resistance
- passage through the oesophageal hiatus or aortic hiatus
- entry into the mediastinum and pleural cavity

In this case, MRCP showed a collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum, then superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow communication to the left pleural cavity as well. That’s the anatomy you’re looking for when the chest keeps refilling and the pancreas is the real source.

## CT: the fast, broad, useful first look

CT is usually the first cross-sectional study that gets ordered, and for good reason. It is fast, widely available, and helps you see a lot more than the chest.

What CT can do well:

- show pleural effusion or hydropneumothorax
- identify pancreatic trauma, pancreatitis, pseudocyst, or ductal dilation
- show retroperitoneal collections and their direction of spread
- suggest the fistulous pathway, especially when it tracks through the mediastinum or diaphragmatic hiatus
- exclude other thoracic or abdominal pathology

What CT may miss:

- the actual fistulous tract
- detailed pancreatic duct anatomy
- subtle ductal disruption, especially if the scan is limited to the chest

That last point is important. If you only look at the chest, you may diagnose the consequence but miss the source. In this case, the CT chest was still helpful because it captured enough upper abdomen to show an elongated multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space down to the pancreatic tail.

**Clinical pearl:** In a child with recurrent hemorrhagic or blood-stained pleural effusion, a CT that includes the upper abdomen can be the scan that shifts your thinking from “thorax” to “pancreas.”

## MRCP: the noninvasive map of the duct

If CT raises suspicion, MRCP is often the best next study in a child.

Why MRCP is so attractive:

- noninvasive
- no ionizing radiation
- excellent for ductal anatomy
- can show the pancreatic duct, the site of disruption, and the fistulous communication
- better suited to pediatric patients when available and feasible

In this case, MRCP confirmed pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending into the retroperitoneum and then through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow left-sided pleural communication.

That’s exactly what MRCP is for: proving the connection.

What MRCP may not do as well:

- direct intervention
- immediate drainage or stenting
- precise therapeutic planning when anatomy is complex

Still, for a child, MRCP often gives the best balance of diagnostic yield and safety.

**Clinical pearl:** If CT suggests a pancreatic source but you want to avoid a more invasive test, MRCP is usually the next best move.

## ERCP: the invasive test that becomes the treatment

ERCP is different. It is not just a diagnostic study; it is a therapeutic platform.

What ERCP can do:

- directly visualize ductal disruption or obstruction
- define duct anatomy in detail
- allow trans-papillary stenting
- reduce intraductal pressure and help the fistula close

Why not just go straight to ERCP?

Because it is invasive, requires procedural resources, and carries risk. In children especially, you want to make sure the benefit is worth it.

When ERCP becomes most useful:

- when noninvasive imaging is inconclusive but suspicion remains high
- when there is persistent leak despite conservative therapy
- when ductal stenting is likely to help close the fistula
- when you need therapeutic intervention rather than just a diagnosis

The case report notes that ERCP is the gold standard for therapeutic planning, but it’s not always the first diagnostic step. That distinction matters.

**Clinical pearl:** ERCP is where diagnosis and therapy overlap. If you already know the patient has a duct leak and conservative management is failing, ERCP can be the bridge to definitive treatment.

## A practical imaging pathway in suspected pediatric PPF

If a child presents with recurrent pleural effusion, especially if it is blood-stained and relapsing after drainage, think beyond the chest.

A reasonable sequence looks like this:

1. **Start with chest imaging**  
   Chest X-ray and bedside ultrasound confirm the effusion and its size.

2. **Send pleural fluid studies early**  
   In this case, pleural fluid amylase and lipase were markedly elevated, which is a huge clue. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L strongly supports PPF in the right clinical context.

3. **Use CT to look for the source and anatomy**  
   CT can reveal pancreatitis, pseudocyst, retroperitoneal extension, or a tract crossing the diaphragm.

4. **Follow with MRCP if PPF is suspected and the child is stable**  
   MRCP gives you the ductal map without radiation or procedural risk.

5. **Reserve ERCP for therapeutic planning or unresolved cases**  
   If the leak persists or stenting is needed, ERCP becomes the next step.

That pathway balances yield and invasiveness, which is especially important in pediatrics.

## What made this case tricky

This child had a few features that pulled attention in the wrong direction:

- recurrent unilateral pleural effusion
- dark red pleural fluid
- mildly elevated ADA
- Koch’s contact history
- initial improvement after thoracentesis
- no obvious abdominal symptoms at presentation

That combination can make you chase infection or malignancy. The diagnosis was delayed because the “mask” was pulmonary. Only after repeated recurrence and a deeper trauma history did the pancreatic source become obvious.

The imaging turned the corner:

- **CT** suggested a retroperitoneal collection extending from the pleural space to the pancreatic tail
- **MRCP** confirmed the fistulous tract and ductal communication
- **Pleural fluid amylase/lipase** sealed the diagnosis biologically

## Take-home points

- Recurrent blood-stained pleural effusion should trigger consideration of PPF, especially if thoracic workup is unrevealing.
- CT is the fastest way to look for pancreatic trauma, pseudocyst, or retroperitoneal tracking, but it may miss the actual fistula.
- MRCP is the best noninvasive test to map ductal disruption and fistulous anatomy in children.
- ERCP is primarily a therapeutic tool when stenting or ductal intervention is needed.
- If pleural fluid amylase is very high, stop thinking only about the chest and image the pancreas and retroperitoneum.
- In pediatrics, choose the least invasive test that can answer the question, but don’t stop short if the anatomy still isn’t explained.

## Bottom line

If a child keeps re-accumulating a hemorrhagic pleural effusion, especially after blunt abdominal trauma, don’t let the chest fool you. CT can raise suspicion, MRCP can map the tract, and ERCP can fix it. The trick is knowing which one answers the question you actually need answered.
