Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Pleural Fluid Amylase: The Forgotten Test in Recurrent Hemorrhagic Effusion

The chest was the decoy. The pancreas was the offender.

A boy in late childhood came in with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain, and respiratory distress. Each time the fluid was drained, he improved—briefly. Each time, it came back. The pleural fluid was dark red, which pushed everyone toward the usual chest-based differential: infection, tuberculosis, malignancy, maybe trauma. But the real diagnosis was hiding in plain sight, and the turning point was a test that often doesn’t get ordered early enough: pleural fluid amylase, paired with lipase.

This case is a great reminder that not every “thoracic” effusion is thoracic.

## Why this case was so easy to miss

The presentation was respiratory: dyspnea, chest pain, hypoxemia, a large right pleural effusion. The first tap brought off about a liter of dark red fluid and the child got better. That pattern is seductive, because it makes the problem feel solved.

It wasn’t.

He re-presented twice with the same picture. A mildly elevated pleural ADA and a history of Koch’s contact pushed the team toward empiric antitubercular treatment. That’s an understandable move in the right epidemiologic setting, but this case is a good warning against anchoring. TB can be a tempting explanation for a recurrent exudative effusion, but it doesn’t explain everything—especially not a repeatedly hemorrhagic, rapidly recurrent effusion in a child.

The key history only came out later: blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle about a month earlier. That mattered. CT then showed a multiloculated collection tracking from the pancreatic region upward, and MRCP confirmed pancreatitis with fistulous communication into the pleural cavity.

The chest wasn’t the source. It was the exit route.

## When should you send pleural amylase and lipase?

If a pleural effusion is unexplained, recurrent, exudative, or blood-stained, pleural amylase should be on your radar early—not after the third thoracentesis.

The clue is especially strong when you have any of the following:

- recurrent large unilateral effusion
- hemorrhagic or dark-red pleural fluid
- hydropneumothorax
- history of abdominal trauma
- pancreatitis, pseudocyst, or abdominal pain
- effusion that keeps returning after drainage

In this child, pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase was 7211 U/L. That kind of elevation should make you think pancreatic leak until proven otherwise.

## Amylase-rich pleural fluid: the differential

Pleural fluid amylase does not equal pancreatopleural fistula automatically. It narrows the field, but you still need to think.

The big differentials are:

### 1) Pancreatopleural fistula
This is the classic diagnosis to not miss. Pancreatic fluid tracks through the retroperitoneum, often via the esophageal or aortic hiatus, and drains into the pleural space. In children, trauma and pancreatitis are important triggers; in adults, chronic pancreatitis dominates.

### 2) Esophageal perforation
Boerhaave’s syndrome can also produce amylase-rich pleural fluid, usually from salivary-type amylase. The clinical context is usually much more toxic: vomiting, chest pain, systemic illness, mediastinal contamination.

### 3) Malignancy
Lung cancer, pancreatic malignancy, lymphoma, and leukemia can all be associated with elevated pleural amylase. Again, the subtype matters.

### 4) Tuberculosis mimics
TB is less a classic cause of amylase-rich pleural fluid and more a clinical trap. In a high-prevalence setting, a mildly elevated ADA and a contact history can distract you from a different diagnosis entirely.

## Pancreatic vs salivary amylase: why the subtype matters

If your lab can distinguish the amylase type, it can be very helpful.

- **Pancreatic-type amylase** supports a pancreatic source: pancreatitis, pseudocyst, or fistula.
- **Salivary-type amylase** is more often seen with esophageal rupture or some malignancies.

In other words, “amylase-rich” is not the finish line. It’s the doorway.

Lipase is a useful companion test. In a pancreatic leak, pleural lipase strengthens the case for a pancreatic source rather than a nonspecific inflammatory process.

## Imaging: CT can suggest it, MRCP can map it

CT is useful, but it may not show the fistula itself. In this case, CT demonstrated a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the pleural space down toward the pancreatic tail. That was the clue.

MRCP then did the heavy lifting: it showed a collection in the distal body/tail of the pancreas tracking posteriorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow communication to the left side as well.

That’s the kind of anatomy you want before you commit to definitive therapy.

ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning, but MRCP is an excellent noninvasive way to define the duct and fistula.

## Treatment: don’t just drain the chest, stop the leak

The management here was conservative and worked.

The child was kept nil per oral, started on total parenteral nutrition, octreotide infusion, and pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy. An intercostal drain was placed for pleural drainage. Over about 10 days, output fell and then stopped, allowing tube removal and discharge.

That’s the real lesson: pleural drainage helps the symptom, but pancreatic rest helps the disease.

If conservative treatment fails, endoscopic stenting via ERCP is often the next move; surgery is reserved for refractory cases or unfavorable ductal anatomy.

## Why this matters in the ED, ward, and PICU

A child with a recurrent bloody pleural effusion can send you straight down the wrong path if you only think “chest.” This case shows how a pancreatic injury can masquerade as respiratory disease, and how repeated drainage without a diagnosis just buys time for the fistula to keep leaking.

Pleural amylase and lipase are cheap, fast, and high-yield in the right context. The test is old-school, but it still saves diagnoses.

## Take-home points

- Recurrent hemorrhagic or exudative pleural effusion should trigger pleural **amylase and lipase** early.
- A very high pleural amylase should make **pancreatic fistula/leak** a leading diagnosis.
- **TB, malignancy, and esophageal perforation** can all confuse the picture; pleural fluid context and amylase subtype matter.
- **Pancreatic-type amylase** points toward pancreatic pathology; **salivary-type** raises concern for esophageal rupture or some malignancies.
- In suspected pancreatopleural fistula, **MRCP helps map the tract**, while **ERCP is for therapeutic planning**.
- Don’t anchor on a mildly elevated ADA if the effusion is **recurrent, bloody, and unexplained**.
