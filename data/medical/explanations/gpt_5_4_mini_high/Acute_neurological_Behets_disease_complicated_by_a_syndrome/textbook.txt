Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Behçet’s Disease, Neuro-Behçet’s Disease, and SIADH: Core Concepts Needed for This Case

Behçet’s disease is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disorder that behaves like a variable vasculitis and can affect many organ systems. The classic clues are recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and skin lesions such as papulopustular or folliculitis-like eruptions; ocular disease, particularly uveitis, is also common and clinically important because of the risk of visual loss. Less obvious but well-recognized involvement includes vascular, gastrointestinal, and neurological disease. For this case, the key point is that Behçet’s disease is usually diagnosed clinically, not by a single confirmatory test, and it may present first with mucocutaneous findings before other organs become involved.

The epidemiology helps frame suspicion. Behçet’s disease is more common in countries along the historical “Silk Road,” including parts of the Middle East and East Asia, but it can occur anywhere. HLA-B51 is associated with increased risk and supports the biologic plausibility of the diagnosis, yet it is not diagnostic: many carriers never develop the disease, and some patients with definite Behçet’s disease are HLA-B51 negative. In practice, the diagnosis is based on pattern recognition and exclusion of mimics. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) uses a weighted clinical score; recurrent oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, and skin lesions contribute to the score, and a total of 4 points or more supports definite disease. That is why the combination of oral ulcers, a genital ulcer, and folliculitis-like lesions in this patient was enough to establish systemic Behçet’s disease even before neurological disease was fully proven.

Neurological involvement, or neuro-Behçet’s disease, usually develops years after the systemic diagnosis, so simultaneous presentation is unusual and diagnostically challenging. Acute neuro-Behçet’s disease is the inflammatory form and commonly presents as meningoencephalitis or a brainstem/diencephalic syndrome; non-parenchymal disease includes vascular complications such as cerebral venous thrombosis. Typical symptoms are headache, fever, vertigo, behavioral change, and focal deficits, sometimes with confusion or reduced alertness. Cerebrospinal fluid often shows inflammatory changes, especially lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. Importantly, a normal brain MRI does not exclude early acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Imaging can be unrevealing at the start of illness, particularly when inflammation is mild, meningeal, or below the threshold of radiographic detection, so CSF findings and the broader clinical context become especially important.

The hyponatraemia in this case is best understood through the physiology of SIADH. Normally, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) is suppressed when plasma osmolality falls, allowing the kidney to excrete free water. In SIADH, ADH secretion or action remains inappropriately active despite hypo-osmolality, so the patient retains water, serum sodium falls, and the urine remains inappropriately concentrated. The bedside diagnostic pattern is euvolemic hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality, urine osmolality that is not suppressed, and urine sodium that is relatively high, after excluding hypovolemia, adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, renal failure, and medication causes. CNS inflammation is a classic trigger for SIADH, and in neuroinflammatory disorders cytokines such as interleukin-6 are thought to promote non-osmotic ADH release. In this case, the markedly elevated inflammatory markers and concurrent meningitic picture make that mechanism biologically plausible, although not proven.

Recognising SIADH matters because severe hyponatraemia can produce symptoms that may be mistaken for part of the neurologic disease itself. Headache, nausea, fatigue, and altered mental status are common, but persistent hiccups can also be a clue to significant hyponatraemia in the setting of CNS inflammation. Rhabdomyolysis is another underappreciated complication of severe hyponatraemia and may explain an elevated creatine kinase, as in this patient. For a case like this, understanding these links is essential: Behçet’s disease provided the systemic diagnosis, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease explained the meningitic symptoms, and SIADH accounted for the euvolemic hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia.

## From First Symptoms to a Focused Clinical Problem Representation

This patient’s illness began as a short, acute febrile syndrome: 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia involving the shoulders and ankles, with oral aphthae already present at presentation. For the learner, the important first step is to avoid treating these as unrelated complaints. Taken together, they suggest a single inflammatory process rather than isolated symptoms. Fever, headache, and malaise raise concern for infection or inflammatory disease; arthralgia adds systemic involvement; and oral aphthae immediately introduce a mucocutaneous clue that should prompt consideration of Behçet’s disease even before the diagnosis becomes classifiable.

The early physical examination sharpened that suspicion. He had oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk, but no focal neurological deficits, no muscle weakness, and no peripheral edema. That combination matters. Oral and cutaneous lesions are common in Behçet’s disease, whereas the absence of focal deficits does not exclude central nervous system involvement, especially in acute meningoencephalitic presentations that may begin with nonspecific symptoms such as headache or vertigo. His clinically euvolemic status is also essential because it frames the hyponatremia as a water-balance problem rather than a depletion state from vomiting, diuretics, or overt volume loss.

The initial laboratory profile reinforced the impression of active systemic inflammation. He had marked hyponatremia, very high CRP and ESR, mild transaminitis, elevated creatine kinase, and poor glycemic control with hyperglycemia. In an acutely ill patient, hyponatremia should never be dismissed as incidental. Mild hyperglycemia can lower the measured sodium, but it does not explain the entire picture here, especially when the broader clinical context suggests inflammation and the patient appears euvolemic. The elevated creatine kinase broadened the differential further, because it suggested muscle injury or rhabdomyolysis, a possible complication of severe hyponatremia or systemic inflammatory illness. Mild AST and ALT elevation are nonspecific but fit with a generalized inflammatory syndrome.

Persistent hiccups deserve special attention because they are often overlooked. In an acutely ill patient, hiccups can be a clue to diaphragmatic irritation, metabolic derangement, or central nervous system pathology. In this case, they fit with the possibility of CNS inflammation and, in retrospect, were compatible with the hyponatremic state that later proved to be due to SIADH. When hiccups occur together with headache, fever, and hyponatremia, they should prompt clinicians to think beyond gastrointestinal causes and consider neurologic or electrolyte-related explanations.

A useful initial problem representation at this stage would be: an older man with an acute febrile inflammatory illness, mucocutaneous lesions, headache/vertigo, persistent hiccups, arthralgia, and euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia. Before the genital ulcer appeared, the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease was not yet definite, so the early differential reasonably included CNS infection, inflammatory meningitis or encephalitis, autoimmune disease, malignancy, and evolving Behçet’s disease. The oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash were the key early clues that the illness was systemic and inflammatory, while the hyponatremia and headache signaled that CNS involvement had to remain high on the list.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning: Establishing Definite Behçet’s Disease, Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease, and SIADH

The diagnostic challenge in this case began with a patient who had fever, headache, malaise, and marked hyponatraemia. The first step was to determine the mechanism of the low sodium. The serum osmolality was low, confirming true hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia rather than pseudohyponatraemia or hypertonic hyponatraemia. At the same time, the urine was inappropriately concentrated and the urine sodium was elevated, while the patient was clinically euvolemic and had normal renal function. This pattern is classic for impaired free-water excretion and strongly suggests syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, or SIADH, rather than hypovolaemic or hypervolaemic hyponatraemia.

Before diagnosing SIADH, clinicians must exclude endocrine causes that can mimic it, especially hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency. In this case, thyroid-stimulating hormone, free T4, and serum cortisol were all normal, making those alternative explanations unlikely. The measured antidiuretic hormone level was elevated despite hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, which is physiologically inappropriate because ADH secretion should be suppressed when serum osmolality is low. Although ADH levels are not always required in routine practice, in this patient they supported the diagnosis by showing that water retention was being driven by persistent antidiuretic activity rather than by an appropriate renal response to volume depletion.

Because the patient also had fever and worsening headache, the clinicians had to evaluate for central nervous system infection or another inflammatory process. Normal head CT and brain MRI were helpful in excluding large structural lesions, hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, or an obvious parenchymal brainstem lesion, but normal imaging does not rule out early inflammatory meningoencephalitis or acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. The lumbar puncture was therefore crucial. Cerebrospinal fluid showed mildly increased opening pressure, elevated protein, and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, a pattern consistent with aseptic meningitis rather than pyogenic bacterial infection. This CSF profile fits inflammatory meningitis and is compatible with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, even when MRI is unrevealing.

The next task was to exclude common infectious mimics. Bacterial meningitis was unlikely because the CSF did not show neutrophilic predominance and cultures were negative. HSV and VZV encephalitis were considered early because of fever and headache, but CSF testing for viral pathogens was negative. Tuberculous meningitis was also excluded by negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing, and cryptococcal infection was ruled out by negative cryptococcal antigen testing. In parallel, the team considered malignant and inflammatory disorders that can present with chronic or subacute meningoencephalitis. CSF cytology did not show malignant cells, arguing against leptomeningeal malignancy or lymphoma, and MRI did not show a mass lesion. Mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor and normal ACE were not persuasive for lymphoma or sarcoidosis; both tests are non-specific screening markers and must be interpreted in the clinical context. Likewise, there was no constellation of findings to support a connective tissue disease such as lupus or vasculitis.

A key turning point came when a painful genital ulcer appeared on day 5. In a patient who already had oral aphthae and a folliculitis-like rash, this new lesion shifted the diagnostic focus toward Behçet’s disease. Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, oral aphthosis contributes 2 points, genital aphthosis 2 points, and skin lesions 1 point. This patient therefore reached 5 points, exceeding the threshold of 4 points for definite Behçet’s disease. The genital ulcer was particularly important because genital aphthosis is a relatively specific clinical clue and is much more convincing than the earlier nonspecific febrile inflammatory picture.

Once Behçet’s disease was established, the neurological syndrome was reinterpreted as acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. This diagnosis does not require a characteristic MRI lesion if the clinical context and CSF findings are supportive. Acute neuro-Behçet’s disease often manifests as meningoencephalitis-like illness, with headache, fever, and CSF pleocytosis, and MRI can be normal early in the course or when there is no focal parenchymal involvement. Thus, the combination of definite systemic Behçet’s disease, acute meningeal inflammation on CSF analysis, and compatible neurological symptoms provided a coherent diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease despite the absence of MRI abnormalities.

HLA-B51 positivity in this patient supported the diagnosis but was not essential. HLA-B51 is associated with Behçet’s disease risk, yet it is not part of the criteria required to establish the diagnosis. Its main value here was confirmatory rather than determinative; the diagnosis rested on clinical features and exclusion of mimics.

Overall, the most parsimonious synthesis was concurrent systemic Behçet’s disease, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, and SIADH. The hyponatraemia was best explained by inappropriate ADH-mediated water retention in the setting of CNS inflammation, while the mucocutaneous lesions and CSF findings anchored the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease with neurological involvement. This case illustrates how careful stepwise reasoning, rather than any single test, is often required to diagnose neuro-Behçet’s disease when MRI is nondiagnostic and the presentation initially resembles infection or another systemic inflammatory disorder.

## Management Decisions in an Uncertain Acute Presentation

At presentation, the patient had fever, headache, and hyponatraemia with evolving central nervous system symptoms, so the initial priority was to treat potentially dangerous infectious causes while the diagnostic workup was still incomplete. Empiric cefepime was started after blood cultures were obtained because bacterial infection could not yet be excluded and early antimicrobial treatment is appropriate when meningitis or systemic infection remains in the differential. When the headache and vertigo worsened and viral meningoencephalitis was still a concern, acyclovir was added to cover herpes simplex virus and varicella zoster virus, both of which require prompt treatment if suspected. Once cerebrospinal fluid studies, cultures, and viral testing were negative, these empiric agents were stopped, illustrating an important principle in acute care: empiric therapy should be reassessed and narrowed or discontinued as soon as objective data make a dangerous infection unlikely.

The hyponatraemia was managed as syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion rather than as hypovolaemic or salt-wasting hyponatraemia. The patient appeared euvolaemic, had low serum osmolality with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urinary sodium, and had normal thyroid and adrenal function, which supported SIADH. Because he was clinically stable and his sodium level was moderately reduced rather than critically low, treatment was conservative: fluid restriction and close monitoring of serum sodium were used, with attention to avoiding overly rapid correction. This cautious approach matters because overly rapid sodium correction can cause osmotic demyelination, especially when hyponatraemia is not clearly acute. Hypertonic saline is generally reserved for severe or symptomatic hyponatraemia, such as seizures, coma, or marked neurological compromise, and vasopressin receptor antagonists are usually considered in selected resistant cases; neither was needed here because the sodium abnormality improved with restriction alone.

Once Behçet’s disease became clinically evident from oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, and painful genital ulceration, treatment shifted toward supportive care for mucocutaneous disease and anti-inflammatory therapy. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used for oral ulcers, while local wound care was provided for the genital and skin lesions. Colchicine was then started as the systemic agent for Behçet’s disease. Mechanistically, colchicine suppresses neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion, and activation and can dampen inflammatory cytokine signaling, which is why it is commonly used for mucocutaneous Behçet’s manifestations. Its established role is strongest for oral and genital ulcers, erythema nodosum-like lesions, and some arthritic symptoms; it is not usually relied upon as definitive therapy for active parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease.

That point makes the therapeutic course in this case unusual. Standard treatment for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, particularly parenchymal disease or meningoencephalitis with significant inflammation, usually includes systemic corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppressive therapy such as azathioprine or other agents when disease is severe, recurrent, or steroid-dependent. Here, however, the patient improved without corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. Several features may have influenced this restrained approach: the neurological presentation was relatively mild, there were no focal deficits or cranial nerve findings, MRI was normal, and the CSF profile was compatible with aseptic meningitis rather than destructive parenchymal disease. In addition, some cases of acute neuro-Behçet’s with meningeal symptoms can improve spontaneously, so clinicians may reasonably individualize therapy when the diagnosis is evolving and the patient is stable. The report does not prove colchicine monotherapy is standard for neuro-Behçet’s; rather, it shows that in this specific mild presentation, improvement occurred without escalation to systemic steroids.

During hospitalization, management depended not only on treatment selection but also on serial reassessment. Neurological symptoms were followed closely for progression to focal deficits, worsening encephalopathy, or signs of meningeal irritation. Serum sodium was monitored daily to ensure gradual correction, while inflammatory markers and creatine kinase were trended to gauge systemic inflammation and to confirm resolution of the rhabdomyolysis-like muscle injury associated with the acute illness. Functional status was also observed, and rehabilitation was provided after the acute phase because prolonged inflammatory illness and electrolyte disturbance can temporarily reduce physical performance. The overall course reinforced a practical lesson: in a patient with newly recognized Behçet’s disease, non-specific neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia should prompt consideration of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease with SIADH, while management must balance empiric coverage for infection, careful electrolyte correction, and targeted anti-inflammatory therapy.

## Outcome, Pathophysiologic Interpretation, and Practical Clinical Lessons

The patient improved rapidly after colchicine was started. Fever resolved the next day, appetite returned, and over the subsequent 2 weeks his vertigo, hiccups, malaise, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually abated. In parallel, serum sodium normalized and plasma ADH fell to the normal range, which supports the interpretation that the hyponatraemia was due to resolving SIADH rather than to hypovolaemia or another sodium-wasting state. Creatine kinase and transaminases also returned to normal, likely reflecting improvement in the overall inflammatory state and correction of the hyponatraemia-associated muscle injury that had probably contributed to the rhabdomyolysis-like biochemical pattern.

Long-term follow-up is clinically important in a case like this because the acute hospitalization only captures the inflammatory peak. In this patient, continued colchicine therapy was associated with no recurrence of Behçet-related or neurological symptoms over approximately 4 years, and diabetes management was later optimized once the acute illness had stabilized. That favorable course is reassuring, but it should not be overinterpreted as proof that colchicine alone was definitively disease-modifying for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Some acute neuro-Behçet presentations, especially those resembling aseptic meningitis, can improve spontaneously. Therefore, the clinical response in this case is best viewed as consistent with treatment benefit, but not as definitive evidence that colchicine was solely responsible for recovery.

A useful way to interpret the SIADH is through a neuroinflammatory model. Acute neuro-Behçet’s disease can generate central nervous system inflammation, and inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 may stimulate non-osmotic ADH release. Excess ADH then impairs free water excretion, producing euvolemic hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium, as seen here. Although IL-6 was not measured directly in this patient, the markedly elevated CRP and the temporal association between neurological improvement and correction of hyponatraemia make this mechanism plausible. The case therefore suggests, but does not prove, a link between acute neuroinflammation in Behçet’s disease and SIADH.

This report also reinforces an important diagnostic principle: a normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. In early or meningeal-predominant disease, MRI can be unrevealing even when CSF shows aseptic inflammation. In this patient, the combination of headache, fever, mildly inflammatory CSF, and subsequent mucocutaneous evolution was more informative than imaging. In practice, clinicians should not dismiss neuro-Behçet’s disease solely because the MRI is normal when the clinical picture suggests CNS inflammation.

The evolving mucocutaneous findings were pivotal. Oral aphthae were present at presentation, but the diagnosis became much more secure only when the painful genital ulcer appeared several days later. This illustrates why serial examinations matter in suspected Behçet’s disease: key diagnostic lesions may emerge after admission, and genital aphthosis is particularly helpful because it is relatively specific. When unexplained neurological symptoms occur together with recurrent oral ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, or genital lesions, Behçet’s disease should move high on the differential, even before all criteria are fully apparent.

The case also highlights underappreciated consequences of hyponatraemia. Persistent hiccups can be a clue to significant electrolyte disturbance, especially in the setting of CNS inflammation, and elevated creatine kinase may reflect rhabdomyolysis triggered or worsened by severe hyponatraemia. These findings are easy to miss if the clinician focuses only on the headache or fever. In patients with suspected neuroinflammatory disease, it is worthwhile to consider whether “nonspecific” symptoms are actually metabolic complications of SIADH.

Finally, the diagnostic workup shows the value of broad exclusion studies used judiciously. Infection, malignancy, and sarcoidosis were reasonably considered because fever, headache, and hyponatraemia are nonspecific, but mildly abnormal tests such as a modestly elevated soluble IL-2 receptor should not drive the diagnosis in isolation. Such results are best interpreted in the context of the clinical syndrome, CSF profile, imaging, and longitudinal examination. The practical approach for future patients is straightforward: in a patient with Behçet features, fever or headache, and euvolemic hyponatraemia, consider acute neuro-Behçet’s disease with SIADH early; confirm hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine; evaluate CSF even if MRI is normal; search repeatedly for mucocutaneous lesions; and manage hyponatraemia carefully while treating the underlying inflammatory disorder.
