Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Missing Genital Ulcer: Serial Exams and the Bedside Diagnosis of Behçet’s

## Why this case should make you slow down and re-examine the skin

The surprising part of this case wasn’t the hyponatremia, the headache, or even the normal brain MRI. It was the diagnosis becoming clearer only after repeated bedside exams.

A man in his 60s came in with fever, headache, malaise, hiccups, arthralgia, oral aphthae, and a folliculitis-like rash. That’s a messy inflammatory picture, but not yet a clean diagnosis. Then, on hospital day 5, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared. At that point, the story sharpened: this was Behçet’s disease, and not just mucocutaneous Behçet’s — it was acute neuro-Behçet’s complicated by SIADH.

This is the kind of case that reminds us why Behçet’s is often a bedside diagnosis before it is anything else.

## Behçet’s doesn’t always arrive in textbook order

Behçet’s disease is classically taught as recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, eye disease, and skin lesions. In real life, those features don’t always show up together, and they definitely don’t all announce themselves on the first exam.

In this patient, the early clues were there:

- oral aphthae
- folliculitis-like skin lesions
- fever and systemic inflammation
- later, a scrotal ulcer

But early on, there was also enough noise to keep the differential broad: CNS infection, viral meningoencephalitis, bacterial meningitis, TB, malignancy, connective tissue disease, neurosarcoidosis. The initial workup made sense. Empiric cefepime and then acyclovir were reasonable while the team sorted out the signal from the noise.

The key move was not a fancy test. It was repeated examination.

## The genital ulcer changed everything

Behçet’s can be hard to recognize before the genital lesions appear, because oral ulcers and folliculitis-like eruptions are common, nonspecific findings. The scrotal ulcer in this case was the bedside clue that turned suspicion into diagnosis.

Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, this patient scored:

- oral aphthae: 2 points
- genital aphthosis: 2 points
- folliculitis-like rash: 1 point

That’s 5 points, which meets the threshold for definite disease.

That matters because Behçet’s is a clinical diagnosis. You do not need a diagnostic MRI pattern. You do not need a positive serologic marker. HLA-B51 can support the story, but it does not make the diagnosis for you. In this case, HLA-B51 was positive, but the diagnosis came from the phenotype.

That’s the lesson: if the first exam doesn’t give you the answer, re-examine. Mucosal disease evolves. Skin lesions evolve. A “missing” genital ulcer may simply be one that hasn’t been looked for again.

## A normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s

Once the neurologic symptoms worsened — headache and vertigo, with no meningismus or cranial nerve findings — the team pursued CNS inflammation. MRI was unrevealing, but CSF showed an aseptic meningitis pattern: mildly elevated opening pressure, elevated protein, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis. Infectious studies were negative.

That’s very compatible with acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially when the mucocutaneous picture is simultaneously becoming more obvious.

A practical pearl here: neuro-Behçet’s can present without the classic parenchymal brainstem lesion people expect. A normal MRI, especially early, does not rule it out. If the patient has inflammatory mucocutaneous disease plus CNS symptoms, keep neuro-Behçet’s on the table even when imaging is bland.

## The hyponatremia was not a side issue

The other twist in this case was SIADH. The patient had hypo-osmolar hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium, in the setting of euvolemia and elevated ADH. Endocrine causes were excluded. That made SIADH the right diagnosis.

This likely mattered clinically more than it first appeared. Persistent hiccups and elevated CK were clues to the physiologic impact of the hyponatremia, and both resolved as the inflammatory syndrome improved.

Whether SIADH was directly driven by CNS inflammation from acute neuro-Behçet’s or reflected a broader inflammatory state, the bedside message is the same: in a patient with suspected CNS inflammation and hyponatremia, don’t treat the sodium in isolation. Look for the driver.

## Treatment was simpler than expected

What makes this case especially interesting is that the patient improved on colchicine monotherapy, without corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants.

That’s not the usual playbook for acute neuro-Behçet’s, where steroids are often considered the mainstay. But in this case, fever, mucocutaneous lesions, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatremia all improved after colchicine was started, and sodium normalized with fluid restriction and careful monitoring.

This doesn’t mean colchicine is a standard monotherapy for neuro-Behçet’s. It does mean that early recognition of the disease phenotype can sometimes prevent escalation before the diagnosis is secure, and that bedside improvement can be striking once the inflammatory process is controlled.

## What to take back to the ED and wards

Behçet’s is one of those diagnoses that rewards repetition. A single exam may show oral ulcers and a rash. The next exam may reveal the piece that makes the diagnosis obvious.

In a patient with fever, headache, inflammatory markers, mucosal lesions, and hyponatremia, think beyond infection alone. Re-examine the skin. Inspect the genitalia. Ask again about ulcers. And if the neuro picture is present, don’t let a normal MRI falsely reassure you.

### Take-home points

- Behçet’s is primarily a clinical diagnosis; serial skin and mucosal exams matter.
- Oral aphthae and folliculitis-like lesions are suggestive, but a genital ulcer can be the missing bedside clue that clinches the diagnosis.
- The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease can help structure the diagnosis, but they don’t replace clinical judgment.
- A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially early in the course.
- Inflammatory CNS disease can be complicated by SIADH; treat the sodium, but keep looking for the underlying driver.



# Normal MRI, Inflamed CNS: How to Recognize Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Anyway

## The MRI was clean. The CNS clearly wasn’t.

Here’s the diagnostic trap: a man in his 60s shows up with fever, headache, vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and hyponatremia. His brain MRI is unrevealing. If you stop there, you may miss the real story.

This case is a reminder that **acute neuro-Behçet’s disease can present like aseptic meningitis with a normal MRI**, especially early on or when the process is non-parenchymal. In other words: a normal scan does not exclude inflammatory CNS disease when the clinical phenotype is doing the talking.

## The case in brief

This patient presented with a 3-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia. He also had oral aphthae. On exam, he had oral ulcers and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. A few days later, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared.

His initial workup showed:

- **Hyponatremia**: 124 mmol/L
- **Hypo-osmolar serum**
- **Concentrated urine** with high urine sodium
- **Elevated ADH**
- Marked systemic inflammation: CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70 mm/h
- Normal renal, thyroid, and cortisol testing

That pattern fit **SIADH**.

When his headache and vertigo worsened, CSF showed:

- Mildly elevated opening pressure
- **Protein 0.97 g/L**
- **Lymphocytic pleocytosis** (11 cells/µL)
- Negative infectious studies, including bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal testing

MRI of the brain was normal.

The key turning point was the evolving mucocutaneous picture: oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, and folliculitis-like skin lesions. With HLA-B51 positivity, he met criteria for **definite Behçet’s disease**. Putting the systemic findings together with the aseptic meningitis picture, the team diagnosed **acute neuro-Behçet’s disease**, complicated by **SIADH**.

He improved with **colchicine monotherapy** plus fluid restriction for the hyponatremia.

## Why this presentation is so slippery

Behçet’s disease is a multisystem inflammatory disorder, but neuro involvement doesn’t usually announce itself on day one. In many patients, neurologic disease comes years after the initial Behçet diagnosis. This case is unusual because **the systemic disease and the neuro-Behçet’s were recognized concurrently**.

That matters, because acute neuro-Behçet’s often doesn’t arrive as a classic focal deficit or a dramatic MRI lesion. It can look like:

- Headache
- Fever
- Meningitis-like symptoms
- Vertigo
- Confusion or behavioral change
- Nonspecific neurologic complaints

And sometimes the MRI is normal.

That doesn’t mean the CNS is fine. It means the inflammation may be **too early, too subtle, or too non-parenchymal** to show up radiographically.

## Why a normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s

This is the big take-home.

Classic neuro-Behçet’s often involves the brainstem, basal ganglia, or thalamus, but acute disease can also present as **aseptic meningoencephalitis without obvious parenchymal lesions**. In early disease, MRI may be unrevealing even when symptoms are real and CSF is inflammatory.

So if you have:

- Compatible systemic Behçet features
- CNS symptoms
- CSF inflammation
- Negative infectious and malignant workup

…a normal MRI should not close the case.

Think of MRI as one piece of evidence, not a veto.

## The CSF is doing the heavy lifting

In this case, the CSF pattern mattered:

- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- Elevated protein
- No organisms
- No malignant cells

That’s not specific for neuro-Behçet’s, but it is highly supportive of an **aseptic inflammatory CNS process** once infection and malignancy are reasonably excluded.

The workup here appropriately considered viral encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, lymphoma, sarcoidosis, and autoimmune disease. That’s the right approach. But once the infectious studies were negative and the Behçet phenotype emerged, the diagnosis became much more coherent.

## Behçet’s is a clinical diagnosis first

This case also reminds us not to get hypnotized by labs that are only indirectly helpful.

The diagnosis of Behçet’s here came from the bedside pattern:

- Oral aphthae
- Genital aphthosis
- Folliculitis-like skin lesions

That gave him a score consistent with **definite Behçet’s disease** by the International Criteria. HLA-B51 was positive, but that was supportive rather than decisive.

A practical pearl: when you’re trying to unify fever, headache, CSF pleocytosis, and hyponatremia, **look carefully for mucocutaneous clues**. Genital ulcers and recurrent oral ulcers are easy to miss unless you ask directly.

## SIADH may be the clue that something inflammatory is happening in the CNS

The hyponatremia here wasn’t incidental. It fit SIADH:

- Low serum osmolality
- High urine osmolality
- High urine sodium
- Elevated ADH
- Clinically euvolemic

CNS inflammation is a well-known trigger for SIADH, and acute neuroinflammatory syndromes can do this. In this case, the authors suggest a possible inflammatory link between neuro-Behçet’s and SIADH, potentially mediated by cytokines such as IL-6, though that mechanism was not directly measured.

Two clinical pearls here:

1. **Don’t dismiss persistent hiccups** in an acutely ill patient with hyponatremia. They can be a clue to CNS irritation and/or electrolyte disturbance.
2. **Follow sodium carefully**. The team treated conservatively with fluid restriction and avoided rapid correction, hypertonic saline, and vaptans.

Also worth noting: the elevated CK likely reflected rhabdomyolysis, which can accompany severe hyponatremia and is easy to overlook if you don’t check it.

## Treatment: when the diagnosis drives the therapy

Empiric cefepime and acyclovir were reasonable while infection was still in play. Once the workup pointed away from infection and toward Behçet’s, colchicine was started.

What happened next is interesting:

- Fever improved the day after colchicine
- Appetite returned
- Vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and skin/mucosal lesions improved over 2 weeks
- Sodium normalized by day 16
- ADH fell to normal
- No corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were used

That is not the usual textbook course for acute neuro-Behçet’s, where corticosteroids are generally considered mainstay therapy. So this should not be overgeneralized. But it is a reminder that **case-based decisions sometimes happen in the gray zone**, and that some patients may improve without steroids, especially when the presentation is mild, self-limited, or caught early.

## What to do at the bedside

When you see headache + fever + hyponatremia + normal MRI, don’t reflexively back away from the neurologic differential. Instead, ask:

- Is there **CSF inflammation**?
- Are there **mucocutaneous signs of Behçet’s**?
- Is the hyponatremia consistent with **SIADH**?
- Have infection and malignancy been reasonably excluded?

If the answer to those questions points toward inflammatory neuro-Behçet’s, the diagnosis can be made even without MRI abnormalities.

## Take-home points

- **Acute neuro-Behçet’s can present with meningitis-like symptoms and a normal MRI.**
- **Aseptic CSF pleocytosis/protein elevation plus Behçet mucocutaneous findings can justify the diagnosis** after infection and malignancy are excluded.
- **Normal MRI does not rule out early or non-parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s.**
- **Hyponatremia should trigger a search for SIADH and CNS inflammation**, not just volume status.
- **Look for oral aphthae and genital ulcers early**—they may be the clue that unifies the whole case.

If you remember one thing: **the scan can be normal while the CNS is very much inflamed**. In the right clinical setting, Behçet’s is a bedside diagnosis supported by CSF, not excluded by MRI.



# When Sodium Tells the Story: SIADH as a Clue to Neuroinflammation

## A case that starts as “just hyponatremia” and ends in Behçet’s

Hyponatremia is common. Neuroinflammatory disease is not. What makes this case interesting is the way the sodium abnormality was not just a lab nuisance, but a clue that the central nervous system was inflamed.

A man in his 60s came in with fever, headache, malaise, hiccups, arthralgia, and oral aphthae. He was hyponatremic, euvolemic, and had a urine profile that screamed SIADH: low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, and high urine sodium. A few days later, his headache worsened, CSF showed aseptic meningitis, and mucocutaneous findings declared themselves more clearly with genital ulceration and folliculitis-like rash. The final diagnosis was systemic Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s, complicated by SIADH.

That sequence matters. Sometimes sodium is not the primary problem. Sometimes it is the breadcrumb.

## First: what makes this SIADH?

Before we start linking hyponatremia to neuroinflammation, it’s worth grounding ourselves in the classic SIADH framework. The diagnosis is fundamentally clinical and biochemical.

This case had the key pieces:

- Hypotonic hyponatremia
- Euvolemic exam
- Low serum osmolality
- Inappropriately high urine osmolality
- Elevated urine sodium
- Normal renal function
- Normal thyroid function
- Normal cortisol

That’s the standard pattern. The ADH level was elevated here, which supports the physiology, but in real-world practice you don’t need an ADH assay to diagnose SIADH. The diagnosis is made by pattern recognition, not by chasing a hormone level that is often hard to interpret.

The important bedside question is not “Is this sodium low?” It’s “Why is the kidney holding onto water when it shouldn’t?”

## SIADH is a diagnosis — but also a signal

SIADH is often treated like a destination diagnosis. In practice, it should trigger a search for the upstream driver. Common causes include:

- CNS disease or inflammation
- Pulmonary disease
- Malignancy
- Medications

In this case, the eventual answer was CNS inflammation related to acute neuro-Behçet’s.

That’s the teaching point: SIADH can be the biochemical footprint of a neuroinflammatory process, even when MRI is unrevealing early on. A normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially when the CSF shows aseptic meningitis and the clinical picture is evolving.

## Why CNS inflammation can raise ADH

The physiology is plausible, even if the exact mechanism isn’t fully proven in every case. Inflammatory signaling within the CNS can dysregulate hypothalamic ADH release. Cytokines are likely part of that story, and interleukin-6 has been repeatedly implicated in neuroinflammatory hyponatremia.

That matters here because Behçet’s disease is an inflammatory vasculitis with immune activation, and acute neuro-Behçet’s can produce meningitis-like or parenchymal inflammatory syndromes. The authors specifically raise IL-6 as a possible bridge between the CNS inflammation and SIADH.

To be careful: IL-6 was not measured in this patient, so this remains biologically plausible rather than proven. But the model fits the case: inflammation in the CNS, inappropriate ADH effect, hyponatremia, then improvement as the inflammatory syndrome settled.

## Neuro-Behçet’s can be there before you see the full Behçet’s picture

This was also a diagnostic trap. Neuro-Behçet’s usually appears years after Behçet’s is diagnosed, not at the same time. Concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD is uncommon.

Here, the diagnosis emerged from the mucocutaneous findings:

- Oral aphthae
- Genital ulcer
- Folliculitis-like rash
- HLA-B51 positivity

The neurological piece was less dramatic than classic focal deficits, but the story fit: fever, headache, vertigo, and CSF pleocytosis with elevated protein. Acute neuro-Behçet’s can present as aseptic meningitis without obvious brain MRI lesions, especially early. If you wait for the MRI to “light up,” you may miss the diagnosis.

## Don’t let the sodium distract you from the syndrome

This case also highlights a practical ED/inpatient pitfall: anchoring on one abnormality and missing the broader inflammatory syndrome.

The patient had hiccups and elevated CK, which turned out to be associated with the hyponatremic state. Those are easy to dismiss as incidental symptoms. In context, they can be clues that the electrolyte abnormality is clinically significant and that the underlying process is not benign.

Likewise, the team appropriately ruled out the big dangerous alternatives first:

- Viral meningoencephalitis
- Bacterial meningitis
- Tuberculous meningitis
- Malignancy
- Sarcoidosis
- Other autoimmune disease

That broad approach was reasonable, especially before genital ulcers appeared and Behçet’s became obvious. But once the mucocutaneous pattern declared itself, the diagnostic center of gravity shifted fast.

## Treatment: the unusual part is what worked

Most clinicians would think of corticosteroids as the backbone of treatment for acute neuro-Behçet’s. That’s fair. But in this case, colchicine monotherapy was followed by improvement in fever, mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and sodium.

That does **not** mean colchicine is a universal treatment for neuro-Behçet’s. The literature still generally supports immunosuppression, and spontaneous improvement can’t be excluded here. But it is a reminder that the response to treatment may be informative, and that some early inflammatory presentations can improve with less aggressive therapy than expected.

For the SIADH itself, conservative management was used:

- Fluid restriction
- Daily sodium monitoring
- Avoiding rapid correction

No hypertonic saline or vaptan was needed.

## Practical bedside approach

When you see hyponatremia in a patient with fever, headache, or other nonspecific neurologic symptoms, don’t stop at the sodium value. Ask:

1. Is this hypotonic hyponatremia?
2. Is the patient euvolemic?
3. Are the thyroid and adrenal axes intact?
4. Is the urine inappropriately concentrated?
5. Is there evidence of CNS inflammation, infection, malignancy, or drug exposure?

If the answer points toward SIADH, then the sodium abnormality may be giving you an early warning about the brain.

In a patient with oral/genital ulcers or skin lesions, Behçet’s should move up the list quickly. In a patient with headache and aseptic CSF findings, acute neuro-Behçet’s should be on the radar even if MRI is normal.

## Take-home points

- SIADH is not just an electrolyte diagnosis; it can be a clue to CNS inflammation.
- Classic SIADH physiology is hypotonic hyponatremia with euvolemia, concentrated urine, and elevated urine sodium after thyroid/adrenal/renal causes are excluded.
- Acute neuro-Behçet’s may present with headache, fever, and aseptic meningitis even when MRI is normal early.
- Inflammatory cytokine signaling, including a possible IL-6 link, is a plausible bridge between neuroinflammation and ADH dysregulation.
- In patients with hyponatremia plus mucocutaneous lesions, don’t miss Behçet’s.
- Treat the sodium carefully, but keep looking for the underlying inflammatory syndrome.

## Bottom line

Sometimes sodium is the first abnormality that tells the whole story. In this case, SIADH was not an isolated lab issue — it was a clue pointing toward acute neuro-Behçet’s. When hyponatremia shows up alongside fever, headache, and systemic inflammatory features, think beyond replacement fluids and ask what the brain is trying to tell you.



# Hiccups, Rhabdo, and Hyponatremia: The Strange Bedfellows of SIADH

## The case that should make you stop and think

A man in his 60s came to the ED with fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia. He also had oral aphthae. His exam added more fuel to the fire: folliculitis-like skin lesions, hyponatremia, and a very inflammatory-looking lab profile. A little later, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared, and the puzzle started to click into place: Behçet’s disease, with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease on top, complicated by SIADH.

What makes this case interesting isn’t just the rarity. It’s the way the “extra” symptoms mattered. The hiccups and CK elevation could easily be dismissed as unrelated noise. In reality, they were clues to the severity and systemic impact of the hyponatremia.

## Hiccups are annoying. They’re also not always benign.

Persistent hiccups are one of those symptoms that can get minimized fast. Most of the time they are transient and benign. But in a patient with fever, headache, and CNS symptoms, hiccups should make you widen the lens.

In this case, the patient’s hiccups sat alongside a worsening neurologic picture. He had headache and vertigo, and CSF later showed a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, consistent with aseptic meningitis. Brain MRI was unrevealing, which is an important reminder: normal imaging does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially early.

The practical point is simple: persistent hiccups in a sick patient are not a diagnosis. They’re a symptom. And when they show up with hyponatremia, think about CNS pathology and SIADH.

## The CK elevation is easy to ignore — don’t

This patient’s creatine kinase was 1349 U/L. That’s not a casual lab abnormality. The report highlights an important teaching point: severe hyponatremia can be associated with muscle injury and rhabdomyolysis, and this is often underrecognized unless you actually look for it.

That matters because CK elevation can feel disconnected from the rest of the presentation. Fever? Sure. Headache? Sure. Sodium 124? Okay, maybe SIADH. But CK? That’s where people can drift into tunnel vision. In reality, the muscle injury may just be another organ system telling you the hyponatremia is doing real physiologic damage.

This patient didn’t have focal weakness or obvious muscle findings, which is exactly why the CK is worth remembering. If you don’t check it, you won’t find it. And if you don’t find it, you may underestimate the severity of the illness.

## Recognizing SIADH at the bedside

The sodium story in this case was classic SIADH once the data were assembled:

- Hypotonic hyponatremia
- Euvolemic exam
- Low serum osmolality
- Inappropriately high urine osmolality
- Urine sodium not low
- Normal thyroid and cortisol testing
- Normal renal function
- Elevated ADH

That’s the pattern. Not every hyponatremic patient with a high urine sodium has SIADH, but this was a strong fit.

A good habit here is to think in layers:

1. Confirm true hypotonic hyponatremia.
2. Decide if the patient is hypovolemic, euvolemic, or hypervolemic.
3. Check whether the kidneys are acting appropriately.
4. Exclude common endocrine mimics.

Once those boxes are checked, SIADH becomes much more than a vague label — it becomes the mechanism you can work on.

## Why neuro-Behçet matters here

Behçet’s disease is a multisystem inflammatory disorder, and neurologic involvement can be delayed for years. This case is unusual because the systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently.

That’s clinically important. Acute neuro-BD often presents as meningoencephalitis or aseptic meningitis, and MRI can be normal early. So if the story is inflammatory and the CSF supports it, don’t let a clean scan talk you out of the diagnosis.

The authors also raise a plausible inflammatory link between neuro-BD and SIADH. CNS inflammation can drive inappropriate ADH release, and cytokine-mediated pathways — especially IL-6 — are a biologically plausible bridge. That said, this is still association, not proof. The key bedside lesson is not mechanistic certainty; it’s recognition that CNS inflammatory disease can be an SIADH trigger.

## Treatment: don’t forget the source

This patient improved with fluid restriction and colchicine. No hypertonic saline. No vasopressin antagonist. No corticosteroids. His fever settled, neurologic symptoms improved, sodium normalized, and ADH fell.

That’s a notable outcome, but don’t overread it. Corticosteroids are generally the mainstay for acute neuro-Behçet’s, and colchicine monotherapy is not standard treatment for neuroinflammatory disease. Still, the case reinforces a core principle: when SIADH is secondary to inflammation, you have to address the inflammatory driver, not just the sodium number.

And whatever you do, correct sodium carefully. Rapid overcorrection is a separate hazard, and it doesn’t get less dangerous because the cause is inflammatory.

## Take-home points

- Persistent hiccups in a sick patient are not “just hiccups” — think CNS disease and check electrolytes.
- CK elevation can be an overlooked clue to the systemic impact of severe hyponatremia, including rhabdomyolysis.
- SIADH is a pattern diagnosis: hypotonic hyponatremia, inappropriately concentrated urine, euvolemia, and exclusion of thyroid/adrenal/renal causes.
- Acute neuro-Behçet’s can present with aseptic meningitis and a normal MRI early on.
- In inflammatory SIADH, treat the underlying disease and correct sodium cautiously.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that hyponatremia can have odd-looking bedside companions. Hiccups and an elevated CK may seem unrelated at first glance, but in a patient with CNS inflammation they can be clues that SIADH is doing more than just lowering the sodium — it’s producing real systemic illness.



# Steroids Optional? What to Make of Colchicine-Only Treatment in Acute Neuro-Behçet’s

## The part of this case that should make you pause

Behçet’s disease is already a slippery diagnosis. Add acute neuro-Behçet’s, hyponatremia from SIADH, a normal brain MRI, and then a clinical recovery without corticosteroids, and suddenly you’ve got a case that challenges your reflexes.

That’s exactly what happened here: a man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, hiccups, malaise, oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, and later a painful genital ulcer. CSF showed an aseptic meningitis pattern, brain MRI was unrevealing, and the labs met SIADH criteria. He improved after colchicine was started — without steroids, without cyclophosphamide, without biologics.

That is unusual. It is also not the same as proof that colchicine is a standard neuro-Behçet’s treatment.

## What makes acute neuro-Behçet’s tricky

Acute neuro-Behçet’s often presents as a meningoencephalitic syndrome, sometimes with focal deficits and often with parenchymal brain lesions, classically involving the brainstem. But not always. A normal MRI, especially early, does not rule it out. In this case, the CSF told the story: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, negative infectious studies, and a clinical picture that fit inflammatory CNS disease in the setting of new definite Behçet’s.

The sodium abnormality matters too. SIADH can be a consequence of CNS inflammation, and this patient had the full biochemical pattern: low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, high urine sodium, and elevated ADH. The hiccups were a nice clue that something central and metabolic was going on — not just “a bad viral illness.”

## What is the usual treatment for acute neuro-Behçet’s?

In most cases, corticosteroids are the backbone of acute neuro-Behçet’s treatment. The goal is to suppress CNS inflammation quickly and prevent neurological injury. Depending on severity, phenotype, and relapse risk, clinicians often add steroid-sparing immunosuppressants.

That’s why this case stands out: the patient got colchicine monotherapy after the diagnosis was made, and he got better. But colchicine is classically a drug for mucocutaneous Behçet’s, not a proven rescue therapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s.

So the real question is not, “Can colchicine ever work?” It’s, “Did colchicine treat the CNS disease, or did this patient have a milder, self-limited inflammatory phenotype that was already on the way down?”

## Colchicine-only: true effect or natural history?

There are a few reasons to be cautious about overcalling efficacy here.

First, acute neuro-Behçet’s can be self-limited in some meningoencephalitic presentations. The report itself acknowledges that spontaneous improvement cannot be excluded.

Second, the patient was not treated in a vacuum. He also had fluid restriction for SIADH, careful sodium monitoring, and supportive care. Sodium correction and symptom improvement happened over time, not overnight.

Third, colchicine was started on hospital day 8, after the illness had already been evolving for several days. The temporal association is suggestive, but it is still only temporal.

That said, the case is not empty of signal. Fever improved the day after colchicine started, and over the next two weeks his vertigo, malaise, hiccups, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatremia all resolved. Four years of follow-up without recurrence is also reassuring. So while this does not prove colchicine is a neuro-Behçet’s standard, it does suggest that some patients with a limited inflammatory phenotype may do better than expected without steroids.

## When might a steroid-sparing approach be reasonable?

This is where clinical judgment matters.

A steroid-sparing strategy may be reasonable only in very selected situations, such as a patient with:

- mild, nonfocal neuro symptoms
- no parenchymal lesion on MRI
- CSF supportive of aseptic inflammation but no red flags for infection or malignancy
- clear mucocutaneous Behçet’s features establishing the diagnosis
- close inpatient or very close outpatient follow-up
- a low threshold to escalate if neurologic status worsens

In other words: if the patient is stable, the diagnosis is secure, and the course is already improving, you might be able to watch carefully rather than reach immediately for high-dose steroids.

That is very different from saying colchicine is an evidence-based substitute for corticosteroids in acute neuro-Behçet’s. It isn’t.

## When should you still reach for steroids?

Do not let this case tempt you into under-treating the usual neuro-Behçet’s patient.

Steroids are still the safer default when you have:

- focal deficits
- encephalopathy
- brainstem involvement
- clear parenchymal MRI lesions
- progressive symptoms
- severe headache or meningoencephalitis that is not improving
- uncertainty about infection that has not yet been reasonably excluded

This case had no focal deficits, no MRI lesion, and a CSF picture more consistent with aseptic meningitis than destructive parenchymal disease. That likely lowered the threshold for conservative management. It also means you should be careful about generalizing the result to more severe neuro-Behçet’s phenotypes.

## The practical bedside pearl

If a Behçet’s patient shows up with headache and hyponatremia, don’t stop at “SIADH.” Ask why the ADH is high. In this case, the answer may have been CNS inflammation from acute neuro-Behçet’s.

And if you’re considering a steroid-sparing strategy, don’t ask whether colchicine is “good enough” in the abstract. Ask whether the patient’s disease is mild, clearly inflammatory, already improving, and safe to observe closely.

## Take-home points

- Acute neuro-Behçet’s is usually treated with corticosteroids; colchicine is not established standard therapy for CNS disease.
- This case is interesting because the patient improved with colchicine monotherapy, but spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded.
- A normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially early.
- Hyponatremia from SIADH can be a clue to CNS inflammation in Behçet’s, not just an incidental lab abnormality.
- A steroid-sparing approach may be reasonable only in carefully selected, stable, improving patients with close monitoring and a low threshold to escalate.



# Broad Differential, Narrow Thinking: Diagnostic Stewardship in Fever, Headache, and Aseptic Meningitis

## Why this case is worth your attention

This is the kind of patient that forces everyone to widen their lens: fever, headache, tachycardia, marked inflammation, hyponatremia, and a CSF profile that ultimately fit aseptic meningitis. Early on, he looked infectious. He also looked inflammatory. And because CNS disease was in play, malignancy stayed on the table too.

The surprise is not just the final diagnosis — concurrent new systemic Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s, complicated by SIADH — but how easy it would have been to get lost in the noise. The case is a great reminder that a broad differential is good medicine. A broad, unfocused workup is not.

## What the first few hours got right

On arrival, the team did what you want done in a sick febrile patient with possible CNS involvement: they treated the dangerous stuff while the diagnosis was still being assembled. Blood cultures were obtained and empiric cefepime was started because bacterial infection had not yet been excluded. That’s reasonable bridge therapy when the patient has fever, headache, high inflammatory markers, and no clear source.

When the headache and vertigo worsened, empiric acyclovir was added. Also reasonable. HSV and VZV encephalitis can present before the picture is obvious, and early MRI can be unrevealing. In other words: you do not need a perfect diagnosis to justify early antiviral coverage when encephalitis is on the table.

The key is what happens next. Empiric therapy is not a commitment to diagnostic inertia. Once CSF testing, cultures, and viral studies fail to support those diagnoses, de-escalation matters.

## A broad differential is not the same thing as a broad panel

This case also shows how quickly “I’ve ordered a lot of tests” can masquerade as good diagnostic strategy.

The team appropriately considered other inflammatory and malignant causes of CNS disease, including sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancy. But the supporting evidence was thin: brain CT and MRI were unrevealing, CSF cytology was negative, and there were no imaging or clinical features that really made neurosarcoid or lymphoma jump out.

That’s where low-pretest-probability testing gets dangerous. Serum ACE and soluble IL-2 receptor were checked, but neither meaningfully advanced the diagnosis. That’s not a knock on the tests themselves; it’s a reminder of where they belong.

ACE has limited sensitivity for sarcoidosis. sIL-2R can be elevated in lymphoma, but mild elevations are nonspecific and can reflect inflammation. In a patient with CNS symptoms and systemic inflammation, a borderline abnormal result can create more heat than light. The test result starts driving the narrative instead of the phenotype.

A good rule: if the clinical syndrome doesn’t already point toward sarcoid or lymphoma, a weak biomarker is rarely the thing that should pull you there.

## The clue was in the skin and mucosa

The turning point here was not a clever lab panel. It was pattern recognition.

He already had oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash on presentation. Then a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on hospital day 5. Put those together and Behçet’s rises quickly on the list. That’s the kind of clue you don’t get from a single blood test; you get it from repeated, careful examination and from refusing to let one organ system own the case.

Behçet’s is still a clinical diagnosis. HLA-B51 was positive here, but that supports the picture; it does not make the diagnosis by itself. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease were met once the mucocutaneous findings were assembled.

The teaching point is simple: if a patient with fever and headache has oral ulcers, a skin eruption, and especially genital aphthosis, stop thinking about “mysterious fever” in the abstract and start thinking syndromically.

## Aseptic meningitis, normal MRI, and SIADH

By the time CSF was obtained, the picture had shifted toward aseptic meningitis: mildly elevated opening pressure, elevated protein, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, with negative bacterial, mycobacterial, fungal, and viral studies. Brain MRI was normal. That normal MRI matters, because it can falsely reassure clinicians into backing away from neuro-Behçet’s too early.

Acute neuro-Behçet’s can present as meningoencephalitis without a striking MRI lesion, especially early. So a clean scan does not exclude the diagnosis if the clinical syndrome fits.

The hyponatremia was not a side quest. Serum osmolality was low, urine osmolality was high, urine sodium was elevated, thyroid and cortisol were normal, and ADH was inappropriately elevated — all consistent with SIADH. Inflammatory CNS disease is a classic trigger. The exact mechanism in this case remains unproven, but the temporal association is hard to ignore.

This matters because symptoms like hiccups or elevated CK/rhabdomyolysis can be easy to dismiss as incidental. Sometimes they are the hyponatremia talking.

## Take-home points

- A broad differential is appropriate in fever, headache, and possible meningitis — but testing should still be hypothesis-driven.
- Empiric antibiotics and acyclovir are reasonable early bridges when bacterial meningitis or HSV/VZV encephalitis is plausible.
- Normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially when CSF suggests aseptic meningitis.
- ACE and sIL-2R are easy to send and easy to overvalue; in low-pretest-probability settings, mild abnormalities are often just noise.
- Oral aphthae, skin lesions, and genital ulcers are the syndromic clue to Behçet’s — don’t wait for the “classic” textbook presentation.
- Hyponatremia with a SIADH pattern in a CNS inflammatory syndrome should prompt you to look for an inflammatory neurologic driver, not just a sodium problem.
