Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Differential Diagnosis] For a patient with fever, headache, hyponatremia, and aseptic meningitis, what alternative diagnoses should be prioritized before labeling the illness acute neuro-Behçet disease, and which specific cerebrospinal fluid, imaging, and microbiologic findings argue against HSV or VZV encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, CNS lymphoma, and neurosarcoidosis?

Before labeling this as acute neuro-Behçet disease, the first priority is to exclude **CNS infection**—especially **HSV/VZV encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, and tuberculous meningitis**—and then consider **neoplastic and inflammatory mimics** such as **CNS lymphoma** and **neurosarcoidosis**. In this case, that exclusion-based approach was appropriate because acute neuro-Behçet can present as **aseptic meningitis with a normal early MRI**, so the diagnosis rests on the clinical context and on ruling out more common causes.

The findings that argued against the major alternatives were these: **HSV/VZV encephalitis** was unlikely because CSF testing for **HSV and VZV antigen and antibodies was negative**, and MRI showed **no focal parenchymal lesions**. **Bacterial meningitis** was unlikely because the CSF showed only **mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11/µL)** rather than a neutrophilic predominance, with **negative CSF cultures**. **Tuberculous meningitis** was argued against by **negative acid-fast staining and negative tuberculosis DNA testing**. **CNS lymphoma** was made less likely by a **normal brain MRI** and **negative CSF cytology for malignant cells**; the mildly elevated **sIL-2R** was nonspecific. **Neurosarcoidosis** was also unlikely because there were **no supportive clinical, radiologic, or CSF findings**, the MRI was normal, and the screening markers were **normal ACE and lysozyme**; again, a mild sIL-2R elevation alone does not establish the diagnosis.

Only after those mimics were excluded, and once the patient declared himself with **oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and folliculitis-like skin lesions**, did the diagnosis of **definite Behçet disease with acute neuro-Behçet involvement** become the best unifying explanation.
