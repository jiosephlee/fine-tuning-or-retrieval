Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Management] How should SIADH due to suspected CNS inflammation be managed during the acute hospitalization, including fluid restriction, indications for hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists, and safeguards against overly rapid correction of sodium?

In this case, the hyponatremia was consistent with **euvolemic SIADH from CNS inflammation**, so the acute inpatient approach is **supportive water restriction plus close sodium surveillance while treating the neurologic/inflammatory trigger**. In the report, the team used **fluid restriction to <800 mL/day**, followed the **serum sodium daily**, and the sodium normalized as the neuroinflammatory process improved; **hypertonic saline and vasopressin antagonists were not needed**.

The first-line treatment for SIADH is therefore **fluid restriction**, usually enough when the patient is **awake, hemodynamically stable, and only mildly symptomatic or asymptomatic**. If the underlying CNS inflammation begins to resolve, endogenous water excretion can improve quickly, so the sodium may rise faster than expected; that is why **frequent reassessment is essential**.

**Hypertonic saline (3%) is reserved for severe or symptomatic hyponatremia**—for example, **seizure, coma, marked confusion, or other serious neurologic symptoms** attributable to hyponatremia, or when the sodium is profoundly low and the clinical situation is unstable. In that setting, the goal is not normalization, but a **small prompt increase** in sodium to reverse cerebral edema and neurologic symptoms, followed by slow controlled correction.

**Vasopressin antagonists (“vaptans”) are not first-line in acute hospitalized SIADH**, especially when the cause is a potentially reversible CNS inflammatory process. They can produce **unpredictable aquaresis and overcorrection**, so they are generally reserved for **selected refractory cases** when fluid restriction fails and the patient is stable enough for very close monitoring. They are **not appropriate as the initial therapy for severe symptomatic hyponatremia**, where hypertonic saline is preferred.

The major safeguard is to **avoid overly rapid correction**, because of the risk of osmotic demyelination. Practically, that means:
- **Check serum sodium frequently** during active correction, often every 4–6 hours if the sodium is changing or if any therapy is escalating.
- Aim for only a **modest initial rise** if symptomatic, then slow the pace.
- Do **not exceed about 8 mmol/L in 24 hours** in most patients; use even more caution in those at high risk for osmotic demyelination.
- Watch for a sudden water diuresis as the inflammatory trigger improves.
- If sodium is rising too quickly, be prepared to **stop the offending therapy, give desmopressin, and/or replace free water with D5W** to relower or “clamp” the correction.

So, for a patient like the one in the report, the key inpatient management is **treat the CNS inflammatory disorder, restrict free water, monitor sodium closely, and reserve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms or unstable profound hyponatremia**—all while ensuring that correction remains slow and controlled.
