Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a euvolemic patient with serum sodium 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, and urine sodium 88 mmol/L, what criteria establish SIADH, and which endocrine, renal, or medication-related mimics must be excluded first?

This is the classic laboratory pattern of **SIADH**: **true hypotonic hyponatremia** (Na 124 mmol/L with serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg), **clinical euvolemia**, **inappropriately concentrated urine** despite hyponatremia (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), and **urine sodium that is not low** (88 mmol/L), reflecting continued renal sodium excretion rather than sodium retention. In the case report, the diagnosis was further supported by **normal renal function**, **normal TSH/free T4**, and **normal cortisol**. An elevated ADH level is compatible with SIADH, but it is **not required** for diagnosis; SIADH is a **diagnosis of exclusion**.

Before making that diagnosis, you must first exclude the common mimics:

- **Endocrine causes:**  
  - **Adrenal insufficiency**  
  - **Hypothyroidism**

- **Renal/volume-related causes:**  
  - **Renal failure or markedly impaired free-water excretion**  
  - **Hypovolemic hyponatremia** and salt-wasting states

- **Medication-related causes of SIADH or hyponatremia:**  
  - **Thiazide diuretics**  
  - **SSRIs/SNRIs**  
  - **Carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine**  
  - **Antipsychotics**  
  - **Cyclophosphamide**  
  - **Vincristine/ifosfamide**  
  - **Opioids** and sometimes **NSAIDs**

In practice, the report’s approach was appropriate: once thyroid, adrenal, and renal disease were excluded, and no medication trigger was evident, the pattern of hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia with concentrated urine established **SIADH**, in this case likely related to acute neuro-Behçet inflammation.
