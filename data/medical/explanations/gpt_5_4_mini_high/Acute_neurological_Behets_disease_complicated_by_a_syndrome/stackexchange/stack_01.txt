Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] A man in his 60s develops oral aphthae, a painful genital ulcer, and folliculitis-like skin lesions. Using the International Criteria for Behçet Disease, which features in this presentation are sufficient to classify him as having definite Behçet disease, and why is HLA-B51 supportive but not diagnostic?

Using the **International Criteria for Behçet Disease (ICBD)**, this man would be classified as having **definite Behçet disease** on clinical grounds alone.

The features in his presentation that count are:

- **Oral aphthae** = **2 points**
- **Genital aphthosis/ulcer** = **2 points**
- **Skin lesion** consistent with Behçet disease, such as the **folliculitis-like rash** = **1 point**

That gives him a **total of 5 points**, which exceeds the **≥4-point threshold** for classification as Behçet disease. In the case report, this is exactly how he was classified.

The reason **HLA-B51 is supportive but not diagnostic** is that it is a **genetic susceptibility marker**, not a disease-defining test. It increases the likelihood of Behçet disease in the right clinical context, but:

- it is **not present in all patients with Behçet disease**
- it can also be found in **people without Behçet disease**
- therefore, it lacks sufficient **sensitivity and specificity** to establish the diagnosis by itself

So, **Behçet disease is a clinical diagnosis/classification based on mucocutaneous and organ features**, and HLA-B51 only adds support to an already compatible presentation. In this case, the diagnosis was secured by the **oral ulcers, genital ulcer, and skin lesions**, not by HLA-B51.
