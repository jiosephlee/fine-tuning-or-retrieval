Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] A man in his 60s develops oral aphthae, a painful genital ulcer, and folliculitis-like skin lesions. Using the International Criteria for Behçet Disease, which features in this presentation are sufficient to classify him as having definite Behçet disease, and why is HLA-B51 supportive but not diagnostic?

Using the **International Criteria for Behçet Disease (ICBD)**, this man would be classified as having **definite Behçet disease** on clinical grounds alone.

The features in his presentation that count are:

- **Oral aphthae** = **2 points**
- **Genital aphthosis/ulcer** = **2 points**
- **Skin lesion** consistent with Behçet disease, such as the **folliculitis-like rash** = **1 point**

That gives him a **total of 5 points**, which exceeds the **≥4-point threshold** for classification as Behçet disease. In the case report, this is exactly how he was classified.

The reason **HLA-B51 is supportive but not diagnostic** is that it is a **genetic susceptibility marker**, not a disease-defining test. It increases the likelihood of Behçet disease in the right clinical context, but:

- it is **not present in all patients with Behçet disease**
- it can also be found in **people without Behçet disease**
- therefore, it lacks sufficient **sensitivity and specificity** to establish the diagnosis by itself

So, **Behçet disease is a clinical diagnosis/classification based on mucocutaneous and organ features**, and HLA-B51 only adds support to an already compatible presentation. In this case, the diagnosis was secured by the **oral ulcers, genital ulcer, and skin lesions**, not by HLA-B51.

### [Morning Report] In a patient with newly recognized Behçet disease who has fever, headache, vertigo, and cerebrospinal fluid showing mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, how would you diagnose acute neuro-Behçet disease when brain MRI is normal, and what neurologic phenotype does this presentation represent?

Acute neuro-Behçet disease is a **clinical diagnosis** in this setting. In a patient who already meets criteria for Behçet disease, the combination of **new fever, headache, vertigo, and inflammatory CSF**—here, **mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein with negative bacterial, mycobacterial, and viral studies**—supports acute neuro-Behçet **even if brain MRI is normal**. A normal MRI does **not** exclude the diagnosis, particularly early in the illness; the key is to recognize the neurologic syndrome in the context of definite Behçet disease and to **exclude mimics** such as infection, malignancy, and other inflammatory disorders.

This presentation represents the **acute meningeal phenotype of neuro-Behçet—essentially aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis-like disease without parenchymal MRI lesions**. In other words, it is a **nonfocal inflammatory CNS presentation**, rather than the classic brainstem parenchymal lesion pattern often seen in neuro-Behçet.

### [Differential Diagnosis] For a patient with fever, headache, hyponatremia, and aseptic meningitis, what alternative diagnoses should be prioritized before labeling the illness acute neuro-Behçet disease, and which specific cerebrospinal fluid, imaging, and microbiologic findings argue against HSV or VZV encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, CNS lymphoma, and neurosarcoidosis?

Before labeling this as acute neuro-Behçet disease, the first priority is to exclude **CNS infection**—especially **HSV/VZV encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, and tuberculous meningitis**—and then consider **neoplastic and inflammatory mimics** such as **CNS lymphoma** and **neurosarcoidosis**. In this case, that exclusion-based approach was appropriate because acute neuro-Behçet can present as **aseptic meningitis with a normal early MRI**, so the diagnosis rests on the clinical context and on ruling out more common causes.

The findings that argued against the major alternatives were these: **HSV/VZV encephalitis** was unlikely because CSF testing for **HSV and VZV antigen and antibodies was negative**, and MRI showed **no focal parenchymal lesions**. **Bacterial meningitis** was unlikely because the CSF showed only **mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11/µL)** rather than a neutrophilic predominance, with **negative CSF cultures**. **Tuberculous meningitis** was argued against by **negative acid-fast staining and negative tuberculosis DNA testing**. **CNS lymphoma** was made less likely by a **normal brain MRI** and **negative CSF cytology for malignant cells**; the mildly elevated **sIL-2R** was nonspecific. **Neurosarcoidosis** was also unlikely because there were **no supportive clinical, radiologic, or CSF findings**, the MRI was normal, and the screening markers were **normal ACE and lysozyme**; again, a mild sIL-2R elevation alone does not establish the diagnosis.

Only after those mimics were excluded, and once the patient declared himself with **oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and folliculitis-like skin lesions**, did the diagnosis of **definite Behçet disease with acute neuro-Behçet involvement** become the best unifying explanation.

### [Clinical Pearls] In a euvolemic patient with serum sodium 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, and urine sodium 88 mmol/L, what criteria establish SIADH, and which endocrine, renal, or medication-related mimics must be excluded first?

This is the classic laboratory pattern of **SIADH**: **true hypotonic hyponatremia** (Na 124 mmol/L with serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg), **clinical euvolemia**, **inappropriately concentrated urine** despite hyponatremia (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), and **urine sodium that is not low** (88 mmol/L), reflecting continued renal sodium excretion rather than sodium retention. In the case report, the diagnosis was further supported by **normal renal function**, **normal TSH/free T4**, and **normal cortisol**. An elevated ADH level is compatible with SIADH, but it is **not required** for diagnosis; SIADH is a **diagnosis of exclusion**.

Before making that diagnosis, you must first exclude the common mimics:

- **Endocrine causes:**  
  - **Adrenal insufficiency**  
  - **Hypothyroidism**

- **Renal/volume-related causes:**  
  - **Renal failure or markedly impaired free-water excretion**  
  - **Hypovolemic hyponatremia** and salt-wasting states

- **Medication-related causes of SIADH or hyponatremia:**  
  - **Thiazide diuretics**  
  - **SSRIs/SNRIs**  
  - **Carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine**  
  - **Antipsychotics**  
  - **Cyclophosphamide**  
  - **Vincristine/ifosfamide**  
  - **Opioids** and sometimes **NSAIDs**

In practice, the report’s approach was appropriate: once thyroid, adrenal, and renal disease were excluded, and no medication trigger was evident, the pattern of hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia with concentrated urine established **SIADH**, in this case likely related to acute neuro-Behçet inflammation.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed mechanism by which acute neuro-Behçet disease could cause SIADH, and how might IL-6-mediated neuroinflammation lead to inappropriate ADH release even without a hypothalamic lesion visible on MRI?

The proposed mechanism is **cytokine-driven, functional hypothalamic–neurohypophyseal dysregulation rather than a visible structural lesion**. In acute neuro-Behçet disease, CNS inflammation can produce **aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis** and activate monocytes and other inflammatory cells to release **IL-6**. IL-6 has been linked to **nonosmotic vasopressin (ADH) secretion**: it may act on hypothalamic osmoregulatory pathways and the posterior pituitary, lowering the threshold for ADH release and causing water retention with euvolemic hyponatremia.

Importantly, this can occur **even when MRI shows no hypothalamic abnormality**, because the disturbance may be **microscopic, diffuse, or cytokine-mediated** rather than a mass-like lesion detectable on imaging. In this case, the patient had clear biochemical SIADH—hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, inappropriately concentrated urine, elevated urine sodium, and elevated ADH—together with CSF pleocytosis consistent with neuroinflammation, and the sodium and ADH normalized as the inflammatory disease improved. That temporal relationship supports the idea that **neuroinflammation itself, likely through IL-6, can trigger SIADH without an MRI-visible hypothalamic lesion**.

### [Pathophysiology] This patient had persistent hiccups and an elevated creatine kinase level in the setting of marked hyponatremia. What bedside teaching points do these findings illustrate, and what mechanisms link severe hyponatremia to hiccups and rhabdomyolysis?

This case teaches two useful bedside lessons.

First, **persistent hiccups are a clue to serious underlying disease, not a benign nuisance**. In a patient with headache, fever, and euvolemic hyponatremia, hiccups should prompt you to think about **CNS pathology and SIADH**, especially when there is evidence of meningeal inflammation. Here, the hiccups improved as the hyponatremia and neuroinflammation resolved, supporting that they were part of the SIADH/neuro-Behçet’s presentation. Severe hyponatremia can trigger hiccups by disturbing the central “hiccup reflex” circuitry in the brainstem and by altering neuronal excitability in the medulla, where the reflex arc is organized.

Second, **an elevated creatine kinase in profound hyponatremia should raise concern for rhabdomyolysis, even if the patient has no focal weakness**. Hyponatremia is an underrecognized cause of muscle injury. The proposed mechanisms are osmotic and ionic: low extracellular sodium causes **myocyte swelling**, impaired membrane function, and disruption of the **Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange**, leading to intracellular calcium accumulation, activation of proteases and phospholipases, and muscle fiber necrosis. The CK elevation in this case, which normalized with correction of the hyponatremia and treatment of the underlying inflammatory disorder, is consistent with that mechanism.

So the teaching point is: **persistent hiccups and CK elevation can be manifestations of severe hyponatremia**. In a euvolemic patient, that should immediately bring SIADH into the differential, and in this case it was ultimately linked to acute neuro-Behçet’s disease.

### [Management] What is the usual first-line treatment for acute neuro-Behçet disease, and how would you justify or question colchicine monotherapy in a patient with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms but no focal deficits or MRI lesion?

The usual first-line treatment for **acute neuro-Behçet disease** is **systemic corticosteroid therapy**, typically high-dose glucocorticoids, after infection has been reasonably excluded. This is especially true for the classic **parenchymal/meningoencephalitic** form of neuro-Behçet, in which most patients are treated with steroids and, if disease is severe, relapsing, or steroid-dependent, a steroid-sparing immunosuppressant such as **azathioprine** is often added.

In this case, **colchicine monotherapy** is best viewed as an **exceptional, nonstandard observation rather than established therapy**. Colchicine is well supported for the **mucocutaneous** manifestations of Behçet disease, but the case report itself notes that systematic reviews generally find it **ineffective for acute neuro-Behçet** and that it is usually used as an adjunct, not as sole treatment. The patient’s improvement is intriguing, but it does not prove colchicine was the active treatment; this episode may have been a **mild, self-limited nonparenchymal meningoencephalitis**, and spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded.

So, how do you justify or question it in a patient like this?

- **Justify it only cautiously** if the presentation is mild, infection has been excluded, MRI is normal, there are **no focal deficits**, and there is a strong concurrent systemic Behçet picture, as in this report.
- **Question it strongly** because a **normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet**, especially early, and the absence of focal deficits does not make CNS inflammation benign. If CSF shows aseptic meningitis and the clinical syndrome fits, the standard approach is still **corticosteroids**, not colchicine alone.

In short: **steroids are the usual first-line therapy; colchicine monotherapy is not standard and should be interpreted as anecdotal in this case, not practice-changing evidence.**

### [Management] How should SIADH due to suspected CNS inflammation be managed during the acute hospitalization, including fluid restriction, indications for hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists, and safeguards against overly rapid correction of sodium?

In this case, the hyponatremia was consistent with **euvolemic SIADH from CNS inflammation**, so the acute inpatient approach is **supportive water restriction plus close sodium surveillance while treating the neurologic/inflammatory trigger**. In the report, the team used **fluid restriction to <800 mL/day**, followed the **serum sodium daily**, and the sodium normalized as the neuroinflammatory process improved; **hypertonic saline and vasopressin antagonists were not needed**.

The first-line treatment for SIADH is therefore **fluid restriction**, usually enough when the patient is **awake, hemodynamically stable, and only mildly symptomatic or asymptomatic**. If the underlying CNS inflammation begins to resolve, endogenous water excretion can improve quickly, so the sodium may rise faster than expected; that is why **frequent reassessment is essential**.

**Hypertonic saline (3%) is reserved for severe or symptomatic hyponatremia**—for example, **seizure, coma, marked confusion, or other serious neurologic symptoms** attributable to hyponatremia, or when the sodium is profoundly low and the clinical situation is unstable. In that setting, the goal is not normalization, but a **small prompt increase** in sodium to reverse cerebral edema and neurologic symptoms, followed by slow controlled correction.

**Vasopressin antagonists (“vaptans”) are not first-line in acute hospitalized SIADH**, especially when the cause is a potentially reversible CNS inflammatory process. They can produce **unpredictable aquaresis and overcorrection**, so they are generally reserved for **selected refractory cases** when fluid restriction fails and the patient is stable enough for very close monitoring. They are **not appropriate as the initial therapy for severe symptomatic hyponatremia**, where hypertonic saline is preferred.

The major safeguard is to **avoid overly rapid correction**, because of the risk of osmotic demyelination. Practically, that means:
- **Check serum sodium frequently** during active correction, often every 4–6 hours if the sodium is changing or if any therapy is escalating.
- Aim for only a **modest initial rise** if symptomatic, then slow the pace.
- Do **not exceed about 8 mmol/L in 24 hours** in most patients; use even more caution in those at high risk for osmotic demyelination.
- Watch for a sudden water diuresis as the inflammatory trigger improves.
- If sodium is rising too quickly, be prepared to **stop the offending therapy, give desmopressin, and/or replace free water with D5W** to relower or “clamp” the correction.

So, for a patient like the one in the report, the key inpatient management is **treat the CNS inflammatory disorder, restrict free water, monitor sodium closely, and reserve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms or unstable profound hyponatremia**—all while ensuring that correction remains slow and controlled.

### [Clinical Pearls] When screening broadly for alternative inflammatory or malignant causes of aseptic meningitis, how should normal ACE and lysozyme levels and a mildly elevated soluble IL-2 receptor be interpreted, and what is the pitfall of relying on these nonspecific tests in place of clinicoradiologic correlation?

Normal serum ACE and lysozyme levels should be interpreted as **not supporting sarcoidosis**, but they **do not exclude** it; ACE in particular has **limited sensitivity**. A **mildly elevated soluble IL-2 receptor** is even less specific: in this setting it is best viewed as a **nonspecific marker of immune activation**, not evidence of lymphoma by itself.

The key pitfall is that these are **screening adjuncts, not diagnostic tests**. If one relies on them without clinicoradiologic correlation, one can be misled by a **false-negative** sarcoid workup or a **false-positive** malignancy signal and miss the real diagnosis. In this case, there were **no clinical, CSF, or MRI features** to support sarcoidosis or CNS lymphoma, whereas the evolving **oral aphthae, genital ulcer, folliculitis-like rash, and aseptic meningitis** established Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. The lesson is that broad laboratory screening should never replace careful synthesis of the **syndrome, imaging, and cerebrospinal fluid profile**.
