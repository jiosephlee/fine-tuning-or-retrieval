Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning: Establishing Definite Behçet’s Disease, Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease, and SIADH

The diagnostic challenge in this case began with a patient who had fever, headache, malaise, and marked hyponatraemia. The first step was to determine the mechanism of the low sodium. The serum osmolality was low, confirming true hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia rather than pseudohyponatraemia or hypertonic hyponatraemia. At the same time, the urine was inappropriately concentrated and the urine sodium was elevated, while the patient was clinically euvolemic and had normal renal function. This pattern is classic for impaired free-water excretion and strongly suggests syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, or SIADH, rather than hypovolaemic or hypervolaemic hyponatraemia.

Before diagnosing SIADH, clinicians must exclude endocrine causes that can mimic it, especially hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency. In this case, thyroid-stimulating hormone, free T4, and serum cortisol were all normal, making those alternative explanations unlikely. The measured antidiuretic hormone level was elevated despite hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, which is physiologically inappropriate because ADH secretion should be suppressed when serum osmolality is low. Although ADH levels are not always required in routine practice, in this patient they supported the diagnosis by showing that water retention was being driven by persistent antidiuretic activity rather than by an appropriate renal response to volume depletion.

Because the patient also had fever and worsening headache, the clinicians had to evaluate for central nervous system infection or another inflammatory process. Normal head CT and brain MRI were helpful in excluding large structural lesions, hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, or an obvious parenchymal brainstem lesion, but normal imaging does not rule out early inflammatory meningoencephalitis or acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. The lumbar puncture was therefore crucial. Cerebrospinal fluid showed mildly increased opening pressure, elevated protein, and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, a pattern consistent with aseptic meningitis rather than pyogenic bacterial infection. This CSF profile fits inflammatory meningitis and is compatible with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, even when MRI is unrevealing.

The next task was to exclude common infectious mimics. Bacterial meningitis was unlikely because the CSF did not show neutrophilic predominance and cultures were negative. HSV and VZV encephalitis were considered early because of fever and headache, but CSF testing for viral pathogens was negative. Tuberculous meningitis was also excluded by negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing, and cryptococcal infection was ruled out by negative cryptococcal antigen testing. In parallel, the team considered malignant and inflammatory disorders that can present with chronic or subacute meningoencephalitis. CSF cytology did not show malignant cells, arguing against leptomeningeal malignancy or lymphoma, and MRI did not show a mass lesion. Mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor and normal ACE were not persuasive for lymphoma or sarcoidosis; both tests are non-specific screening markers and must be interpreted in the clinical context. Likewise, there was no constellation of findings to support a connective tissue disease such as lupus or vasculitis.

A key turning point came when a painful genital ulcer appeared on day 5. In a patient who already had oral aphthae and a folliculitis-like rash, this new lesion shifted the diagnostic focus toward Behçet’s disease. Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, oral aphthosis contributes 2 points, genital aphthosis 2 points, and skin lesions 1 point. This patient therefore reached 5 points, exceeding the threshold of 4 points for definite Behçet’s disease. The genital ulcer was particularly important because genital aphthosis is a relatively specific clinical clue and is much more convincing than the earlier nonspecific febrile inflammatory picture.

Once Behçet’s disease was established, the neurological syndrome was reinterpreted as acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. This diagnosis does not require a characteristic MRI lesion if the clinical context and CSF findings are supportive. Acute neuro-Behçet’s disease often manifests as meningoencephalitis-like illness, with headache, fever, and CSF pleocytosis, and MRI can be normal early in the course or when there is no focal parenchymal involvement. Thus, the combination of definite systemic Behçet’s disease, acute meningeal inflammation on CSF analysis, and compatible neurological symptoms provided a coherent diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease despite the absence of MRI abnormalities.

HLA-B51 positivity in this patient supported the diagnosis but was not essential. HLA-B51 is associated with Behçet’s disease risk, yet it is not part of the criteria required to establish the diagnosis. Its main value here was confirmatory rather than determinative; the diagnosis rested on clinical features and exclusion of mimics.

Overall, the most parsimonious synthesis was concurrent systemic Behçet’s disease, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, and SIADH. The hyponatraemia was best explained by inappropriate ADH-mediated water retention in the setting of CNS inflammation, while the mucocutaneous lesions and CSF findings anchored the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease with neurological involvement. This case illustrates how careful stepwise reasoning, rather than any single test, is often required to diagnose neuro-Behçet’s disease when MRI is nondiagnostic and the presentation initially resembles infection or another systemic inflammatory disorder.
