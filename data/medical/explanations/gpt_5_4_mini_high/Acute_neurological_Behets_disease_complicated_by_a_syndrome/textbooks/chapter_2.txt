Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## From First Symptoms to a Focused Clinical Problem Representation

This patient’s illness began as a short, acute febrile syndrome: 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia involving the shoulders and ankles, with oral aphthae already present at presentation. For the learner, the important first step is to avoid treating these as unrelated complaints. Taken together, they suggest a single inflammatory process rather than isolated symptoms. Fever, headache, and malaise raise concern for infection or inflammatory disease; arthralgia adds systemic involvement; and oral aphthae immediately introduce a mucocutaneous clue that should prompt consideration of Behçet’s disease even before the diagnosis becomes classifiable.

The early physical examination sharpened that suspicion. He had oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk, but no focal neurological deficits, no muscle weakness, and no peripheral edema. That combination matters. Oral and cutaneous lesions are common in Behçet’s disease, whereas the absence of focal deficits does not exclude central nervous system involvement, especially in acute meningoencephalitic presentations that may begin with nonspecific symptoms such as headache or vertigo. His clinically euvolemic status is also essential because it frames the hyponatremia as a water-balance problem rather than a depletion state from vomiting, diuretics, or overt volume loss.

The initial laboratory profile reinforced the impression of active systemic inflammation. He had marked hyponatremia, very high CRP and ESR, mild transaminitis, elevated creatine kinase, and poor glycemic control with hyperglycemia. In an acutely ill patient, hyponatremia should never be dismissed as incidental. Mild hyperglycemia can lower the measured sodium, but it does not explain the entire picture here, especially when the broader clinical context suggests inflammation and the patient appears euvolemic. The elevated creatine kinase broadened the differential further, because it suggested muscle injury or rhabdomyolysis, a possible complication of severe hyponatremia or systemic inflammatory illness. Mild AST and ALT elevation are nonspecific but fit with a generalized inflammatory syndrome.

Persistent hiccups deserve special attention because they are often overlooked. In an acutely ill patient, hiccups can be a clue to diaphragmatic irritation, metabolic derangement, or central nervous system pathology. In this case, they fit with the possibility of CNS inflammation and, in retrospect, were compatible with the hyponatremic state that later proved to be due to SIADH. When hiccups occur together with headache, fever, and hyponatremia, they should prompt clinicians to think beyond gastrointestinal causes and consider neurologic or electrolyte-related explanations.

A useful initial problem representation at this stage would be: an older man with an acute febrile inflammatory illness, mucocutaneous lesions, headache/vertigo, persistent hiccups, arthralgia, and euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia. Before the genital ulcer appeared, the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease was not yet definite, so the early differential reasonably included CNS infection, inflammatory meningitis or encephalitis, autoimmune disease, malignancy, and evolving Behçet’s disease. The oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash were the key early clues that the illness was systemic and inflammatory, while the hyponatremia and headache signaled that CNS involvement had to remain high on the list.
