Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Broad Differential, Narrow Thinking: Diagnostic Stewardship in Fever, Headache, and Aseptic Meningitis

## Why this case is worth your attention

This is the kind of patient that forces everyone to widen their lens: fever, headache, tachycardia, marked inflammation, hyponatremia, and a CSF profile that ultimately fit aseptic meningitis. Early on, he looked infectious. He also looked inflammatory. And because CNS disease was in play, malignancy stayed on the table too.

The surprise is not just the final diagnosis — concurrent new systemic Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s, complicated by SIADH — but how easy it would have been to get lost in the noise. The case is a great reminder that a broad differential is good medicine. A broad, unfocused workup is not.

## What the first few hours got right

On arrival, the team did what you want done in a sick febrile patient with possible CNS involvement: they treated the dangerous stuff while the diagnosis was still being assembled. Blood cultures were obtained and empiric cefepime was started because bacterial infection had not yet been excluded. That’s reasonable bridge therapy when the patient has fever, headache, high inflammatory markers, and no clear source.

When the headache and vertigo worsened, empiric acyclovir was added. Also reasonable. HSV and VZV encephalitis can present before the picture is obvious, and early MRI can be unrevealing. In other words: you do not need a perfect diagnosis to justify early antiviral coverage when encephalitis is on the table.

The key is what happens next. Empiric therapy is not a commitment to diagnostic inertia. Once CSF testing, cultures, and viral studies fail to support those diagnoses, de-escalation matters.

## A broad differential is not the same thing as a broad panel

This case also shows how quickly “I’ve ordered a lot of tests” can masquerade as good diagnostic strategy.

The team appropriately considered other inflammatory and malignant causes of CNS disease, including sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancy. But the supporting evidence was thin: brain CT and MRI were unrevealing, CSF cytology was negative, and there were no imaging or clinical features that really made neurosarcoid or lymphoma jump out.

That’s where low-pretest-probability testing gets dangerous. Serum ACE and soluble IL-2 receptor were checked, but neither meaningfully advanced the diagnosis. That’s not a knock on the tests themselves; it’s a reminder of where they belong.

ACE has limited sensitivity for sarcoidosis. sIL-2R can be elevated in lymphoma, but mild elevations are nonspecific and can reflect inflammation. In a patient with CNS symptoms and systemic inflammation, a borderline abnormal result can create more heat than light. The test result starts driving the narrative instead of the phenotype.

A good rule: if the clinical syndrome doesn’t already point toward sarcoid or lymphoma, a weak biomarker is rarely the thing that should pull you there.

## The clue was in the skin and mucosa

The turning point here was not a clever lab panel. It was pattern recognition.

He already had oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash on presentation. Then a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on hospital day 5. Put those together and Behçet’s rises quickly on the list. That’s the kind of clue you don’t get from a single blood test; you get it from repeated, careful examination and from refusing to let one organ system own the case.

Behçet’s is still a clinical diagnosis. HLA-B51 was positive here, but that supports the picture; it does not make the diagnosis by itself. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease were met once the mucocutaneous findings were assembled.

The teaching point is simple: if a patient with fever and headache has oral ulcers, a skin eruption, and especially genital aphthosis, stop thinking about “mysterious fever” in the abstract and start thinking syndromically.

## Aseptic meningitis, normal MRI, and SIADH

By the time CSF was obtained, the picture had shifted toward aseptic meningitis: mildly elevated opening pressure, elevated protein, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, with negative bacterial, mycobacterial, fungal, and viral studies. Brain MRI was normal. That normal MRI matters, because it can falsely reassure clinicians into backing away from neuro-Behçet’s too early.

Acute neuro-Behçet’s can present as meningoencephalitis without a striking MRI lesion, especially early. So a clean scan does not exclude the diagnosis if the clinical syndrome fits.

The hyponatremia was not a side quest. Serum osmolality was low, urine osmolality was high, urine sodium was elevated, thyroid and cortisol were normal, and ADH was inappropriately elevated — all consistent with SIADH. Inflammatory CNS disease is a classic trigger. The exact mechanism in this case remains unproven, but the temporal association is hard to ignore.

This matters because symptoms like hiccups or elevated CK/rhabdomyolysis can be easy to dismiss as incidental. Sometimes they are the hyponatremia talking.

## Take-home points

- A broad differential is appropriate in fever, headache, and possible meningitis — but testing should still be hypothesis-driven.
- Empiric antibiotics and acyclovir are reasonable early bridges when bacterial meningitis or HSV/VZV encephalitis is plausible.
- Normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially when CSF suggests aseptic meningitis.
- ACE and sIL-2R are easy to send and easy to overvalue; in low-pretest-probability settings, mild abnormalities are often just noise.
- Oral aphthae, skin lesions, and genital ulcers are the syndromic clue to Behçet’s — don’t wait for the “classic” textbook presentation.
- Hyponatremia with a SIADH pattern in a CNS inflammatory syndrome should prompt you to look for an inflammatory neurologic driver, not just a sodium problem.
