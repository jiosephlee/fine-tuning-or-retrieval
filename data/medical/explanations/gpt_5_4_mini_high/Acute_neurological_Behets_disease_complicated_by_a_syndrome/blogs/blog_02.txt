Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Normal MRI, Inflamed CNS: How to Recognize Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Anyway

## The MRI was clean. The CNS clearly wasn’t.

Here’s the diagnostic trap: a man in his 60s shows up with fever, headache, vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and hyponatremia. His brain MRI is unrevealing. If you stop there, you may miss the real story.

This case is a reminder that **acute neuro-Behçet’s disease can present like aseptic meningitis with a normal MRI**, especially early on or when the process is non-parenchymal. In other words: a normal scan does not exclude inflammatory CNS disease when the clinical phenotype is doing the talking.

## The case in brief

This patient presented with a 3-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia. He also had oral aphthae. On exam, he had oral ulcers and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. A few days later, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared.

His initial workup showed:

- **Hyponatremia**: 124 mmol/L
- **Hypo-osmolar serum**
- **Concentrated urine** with high urine sodium
- **Elevated ADH**
- Marked systemic inflammation: CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70 mm/h
- Normal renal, thyroid, and cortisol testing

That pattern fit **SIADH**.

When his headache and vertigo worsened, CSF showed:

- Mildly elevated opening pressure
- **Protein 0.97 g/L**
- **Lymphocytic pleocytosis** (11 cells/µL)
- Negative infectious studies, including bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal testing

MRI of the brain was normal.

The key turning point was the evolving mucocutaneous picture: oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, and folliculitis-like skin lesions. With HLA-B51 positivity, he met criteria for **definite Behçet’s disease**. Putting the systemic findings together with the aseptic meningitis picture, the team diagnosed **acute neuro-Behçet’s disease**, complicated by **SIADH**.

He improved with **colchicine monotherapy** plus fluid restriction for the hyponatremia.

## Why this presentation is so slippery

Behçet’s disease is a multisystem inflammatory disorder, but neuro involvement doesn’t usually announce itself on day one. In many patients, neurologic disease comes years after the initial Behçet diagnosis. This case is unusual because **the systemic disease and the neuro-Behçet’s were recognized concurrently**.

That matters, because acute neuro-Behçet’s often doesn’t arrive as a classic focal deficit or a dramatic MRI lesion. It can look like:

- Headache
- Fever
- Meningitis-like symptoms
- Vertigo
- Confusion or behavioral change
- Nonspecific neurologic complaints

And sometimes the MRI is normal.

That doesn’t mean the CNS is fine. It means the inflammation may be **too early, too subtle, or too non-parenchymal** to show up radiographically.

## Why a normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s

This is the big take-home.

Classic neuro-Behçet’s often involves the brainstem, basal ganglia, or thalamus, but acute disease can also present as **aseptic meningoencephalitis without obvious parenchymal lesions**. In early disease, MRI may be unrevealing even when symptoms are real and CSF is inflammatory.

So if you have:

- Compatible systemic Behçet features
- CNS symptoms
- CSF inflammation
- Negative infectious and malignant workup

…a normal MRI should not close the case.

Think of MRI as one piece of evidence, not a veto.

## The CSF is doing the heavy lifting

In this case, the CSF pattern mattered:

- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- Elevated protein
- No organisms
- No malignant cells

That’s not specific for neuro-Behçet’s, but it is highly supportive of an **aseptic inflammatory CNS process** once infection and malignancy are reasonably excluded.

The workup here appropriately considered viral encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, lymphoma, sarcoidosis, and autoimmune disease. That’s the right approach. But once the infectious studies were negative and the Behçet phenotype emerged, the diagnosis became much more coherent.

## Behçet’s is a clinical diagnosis first

This case also reminds us not to get hypnotized by labs that are only indirectly helpful.

The diagnosis of Behçet’s here came from the bedside pattern:

- Oral aphthae
- Genital aphthosis
- Folliculitis-like skin lesions

That gave him a score consistent with **definite Behçet’s disease** by the International Criteria. HLA-B51 was positive, but that was supportive rather than decisive.

A practical pearl: when you’re trying to unify fever, headache, CSF pleocytosis, and hyponatremia, **look carefully for mucocutaneous clues**. Genital ulcers and recurrent oral ulcers are easy to miss unless you ask directly.

## SIADH may be the clue that something inflammatory is happening in the CNS

The hyponatremia here wasn’t incidental. It fit SIADH:

- Low serum osmolality
- High urine osmolality
- High urine sodium
- Elevated ADH
- Clinically euvolemic

CNS inflammation is a well-known trigger for SIADH, and acute neuroinflammatory syndromes can do this. In this case, the authors suggest a possible inflammatory link between neuro-Behçet’s and SIADH, potentially mediated by cytokines such as IL-6, though that mechanism was not directly measured.

Two clinical pearls here:

1. **Don’t dismiss persistent hiccups** in an acutely ill patient with hyponatremia. They can be a clue to CNS irritation and/or electrolyte disturbance.
2. **Follow sodium carefully**. The team treated conservatively with fluid restriction and avoided rapid correction, hypertonic saline, and vaptans.

Also worth noting: the elevated CK likely reflected rhabdomyolysis, which can accompany severe hyponatremia and is easy to overlook if you don’t check it.

## Treatment: when the diagnosis drives the therapy

Empiric cefepime and acyclovir were reasonable while infection was still in play. Once the workup pointed away from infection and toward Behçet’s, colchicine was started.

What happened next is interesting:

- Fever improved the day after colchicine
- Appetite returned
- Vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and skin/mucosal lesions improved over 2 weeks
- Sodium normalized by day 16
- ADH fell to normal
- No corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were used

That is not the usual textbook course for acute neuro-Behçet’s, where corticosteroids are generally considered mainstay therapy. So this should not be overgeneralized. But it is a reminder that **case-based decisions sometimes happen in the gray zone**, and that some patients may improve without steroids, especially when the presentation is mild, self-limited, or caught early.

## What to do at the bedside

When you see headache + fever + hyponatremia + normal MRI, don’t reflexively back away from the neurologic differential. Instead, ask:

- Is there **CSF inflammation**?
- Are there **mucocutaneous signs of Behçet’s**?
- Is the hyponatremia consistent with **SIADH**?
- Have infection and malignancy been reasonably excluded?

If the answer to those questions points toward inflammatory neuro-Behçet’s, the diagnosis can be made even without MRI abnormalities.

## Take-home points

- **Acute neuro-Behçet’s can present with meningitis-like symptoms and a normal MRI.**
- **Aseptic CSF pleocytosis/protein elevation plus Behçet mucocutaneous findings can justify the diagnosis** after infection and malignancy are excluded.
- **Normal MRI does not rule out early or non-parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s.**
- **Hyponatremia should trigger a search for SIADH and CNS inflammation**, not just volume status.
- **Look for oral aphthae and genital ulcers early**—they may be the clue that unifies the whole case.

If you remember one thing: **the scan can be normal while the CNS is very much inflamed**. In the right clinical setting, Behçet’s is a bedside diagnosis supported by CSF, not excluded by MRI.
