Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Steroids Optional? What to Make of Colchicine-Only Treatment in Acute Neuro-Behçet’s

## The part of this case that should make you pause

Behçet’s disease is already a slippery diagnosis. Add acute neuro-Behçet’s, hyponatremia from SIADH, a normal brain MRI, and then a clinical recovery without corticosteroids, and suddenly you’ve got a case that challenges your reflexes.

That’s exactly what happened here: a man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, hiccups, malaise, oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, and later a painful genital ulcer. CSF showed an aseptic meningitis pattern, brain MRI was unrevealing, and the labs met SIADH criteria. He improved after colchicine was started — without steroids, without cyclophosphamide, without biologics.

That is unusual. It is also not the same as proof that colchicine is a standard neuro-Behçet’s treatment.

## What makes acute neuro-Behçet’s tricky

Acute neuro-Behçet’s often presents as a meningoencephalitic syndrome, sometimes with focal deficits and often with parenchymal brain lesions, classically involving the brainstem. But not always. A normal MRI, especially early, does not rule it out. In this case, the CSF told the story: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, negative infectious studies, and a clinical picture that fit inflammatory CNS disease in the setting of new definite Behçet’s.

The sodium abnormality matters too. SIADH can be a consequence of CNS inflammation, and this patient had the full biochemical pattern: low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, high urine sodium, and elevated ADH. The hiccups were a nice clue that something central and metabolic was going on — not just “a bad viral illness.”

## What is the usual treatment for acute neuro-Behçet’s?

In most cases, corticosteroids are the backbone of acute neuro-Behçet’s treatment. The goal is to suppress CNS inflammation quickly and prevent neurological injury. Depending on severity, phenotype, and relapse risk, clinicians often add steroid-sparing immunosuppressants.

That’s why this case stands out: the patient got colchicine monotherapy after the diagnosis was made, and he got better. But colchicine is classically a drug for mucocutaneous Behçet’s, not a proven rescue therapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s.

So the real question is not, “Can colchicine ever work?” It’s, “Did colchicine treat the CNS disease, or did this patient have a milder, self-limited inflammatory phenotype that was already on the way down?”

## Colchicine-only: true effect or natural history?

There are a few reasons to be cautious about overcalling efficacy here.

First, acute neuro-Behçet’s can be self-limited in some meningoencephalitic presentations. The report itself acknowledges that spontaneous improvement cannot be excluded.

Second, the patient was not treated in a vacuum. He also had fluid restriction for SIADH, careful sodium monitoring, and supportive care. Sodium correction and symptom improvement happened over time, not overnight.

Third, colchicine was started on hospital day 8, after the illness had already been evolving for several days. The temporal association is suggestive, but it is still only temporal.

That said, the case is not empty of signal. Fever improved the day after colchicine started, and over the next two weeks his vertigo, malaise, hiccups, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatremia all resolved. Four years of follow-up without recurrence is also reassuring. So while this does not prove colchicine is a neuro-Behçet’s standard, it does suggest that some patients with a limited inflammatory phenotype may do better than expected without steroids.

## When might a steroid-sparing approach be reasonable?

This is where clinical judgment matters.

A steroid-sparing strategy may be reasonable only in very selected situations, such as a patient with:

- mild, nonfocal neuro symptoms
- no parenchymal lesion on MRI
- CSF supportive of aseptic inflammation but no red flags for infection or malignancy
- clear mucocutaneous Behçet’s features establishing the diagnosis
- close inpatient or very close outpatient follow-up
- a low threshold to escalate if neurologic status worsens

In other words: if the patient is stable, the diagnosis is secure, and the course is already improving, you might be able to watch carefully rather than reach immediately for high-dose steroids.

That is very different from saying colchicine is an evidence-based substitute for corticosteroids in acute neuro-Behçet’s. It isn’t.

## When should you still reach for steroids?

Do not let this case tempt you into under-treating the usual neuro-Behçet’s patient.

Steroids are still the safer default when you have:

- focal deficits
- encephalopathy
- brainstem involvement
- clear parenchymal MRI lesions
- progressive symptoms
- severe headache or meningoencephalitis that is not improving
- uncertainty about infection that has not yet been reasonably excluded

This case had no focal deficits, no MRI lesion, and a CSF picture more consistent with aseptic meningitis than destructive parenchymal disease. That likely lowered the threshold for conservative management. It also means you should be careful about generalizing the result to more severe neuro-Behçet’s phenotypes.

## The practical bedside pearl

If a Behçet’s patient shows up with headache and hyponatremia, don’t stop at “SIADH.” Ask why the ADH is high. In this case, the answer may have been CNS inflammation from acute neuro-Behçet’s.

And if you’re considering a steroid-sparing strategy, don’t ask whether colchicine is “good enough” in the abstract. Ask whether the patient’s disease is mild, clearly inflammatory, already improving, and safe to observe closely.

## Take-home points

- Acute neuro-Behçet’s is usually treated with corticosteroids; colchicine is not established standard therapy for CNS disease.
- This case is interesting because the patient improved with colchicine monotherapy, but spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded.
- A normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially early.
- Hyponatremia from SIADH can be a clue to CNS inflammation in Behçet’s, not just an incidental lab abnormality.
- A steroid-sparing approach may be reasonable only in carefully selected, stable, improving patients with close monitoring and a low threshold to escalate.
