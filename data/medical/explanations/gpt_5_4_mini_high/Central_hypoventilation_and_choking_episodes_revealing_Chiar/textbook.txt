Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Chiari Malformation Type I as a Cause of Brainstem-Mediated Respiratory and Bulbar Dysfunction

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is defined radiologically by descent of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. However, the imaging threshold alone does not determine clinical significance. CMI is relatively common as an incidental finding, whereas symptomatic disease, sometimes called Chiari syndrome, is much less frequent. The key clinical question is not simply how far the tonsils descend, but whether the craniocervical junction is crowded enough to distort cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow or compress adjacent neural structures. In this case, the patient’s 11 mm tonsillar descent and crowded foramen magnum were anatomically compatible with symptomatic CMI.

The usual anatomic basis is a small posterior fossa with a mismatch between available space and the volume of cerebellar tissue and other hindbrain structures. This crowding can narrow the foramen magnum and impair normal CSF circulation at the craniocervical junction. Disturbed CSF flow may contribute to pressure dissociation across this region and to intermittent symptoms that are provoked by coughing, straining, or positional changes. Even when MRI findings are stable, the functional effect of this crowding can vary, which is why some patients remain asymptomatic while others develop headache, bulbar dysfunction, or respiratory abnormalities.

Headache is the most common symptom of symptomatic CMI and is classically occipital or suboccipital, often worsened by Valsalva maneuvers such as coughing, laughing, or straining. The mechanism is thought to relate to transient rises in intracranial pressure acting on a crowded posterior fossa and on pain-sensitive structures around the foramen magnum. The patient in this case had chronic occipital and vertex pain, although her headaches were atypical because they were not clearly Valsalva triggered. Scalp allodynia is not a classic hallmark of CMI, but irritation of occipital and upper cervical sensory pathways can produce cutaneous hypersensitivity or neuralgia-like pain patterns.

Bulbar symptoms such as dysphagia, choking, gagging, or nasal regurgitation arise when the medulla, lower cranial nerve nuclei, or their exiting fibers are compressed or distorted. Swallowing requires precise coordination between cortical input and brainstem pattern generators, along with intact function of cranial nerves IX, X, and XII. In CMI, this coordination may fail even when endoscopy and laryngoscopy are normal, because the problem is neurologic rather than structural within the esophagus or larynx. That is why a patient may report choking on both solids and liquids, reflecting neurogenic dysphagia rather than a fixed mechanical obstruction.

Respiratory dysfunction in CMI is especially important because it can be subtle, misleading, and potentially dangerous. The medulla contains the automatic respiratory centers that generate baseline breathing rhythm, particularly during sleep. Compression at the craniocervical junction can disrupt these centers and produce central sleep apnea or central hypoventilation, in which ventilatory effort briefly stops or becomes inadequate without airway obstruction. A useful conceptual distinction is between voluntary and automatic breathing: cortical pathways allow a patient to breathe consciously during wakefulness, but brainstem control may fail during sleep or quiet rest. This explains why patients can appear comfortable and without dyspnea despite marked oxygen desaturation, as in this case.

The respiratory phenotype in structural brainstem disease is sometimes described as an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, resembling congenital central hypoventilation in the pattern of impaired automatic drive but differing in cause. In congenital disease, the defect is usually genetic, whereas in CMI the problem is mechanical compression or distortion of medullary respiratory pathways. The result may be central apneas, hypoventilation, or episodic unexplained hypoxemia. Because symptoms can be intermittent and polysomnography may underestimate severity if sleep is not fully measured, a normal or mildly abnormal study does not exclude clinically important brainstem-related respiratory dysfunction.

Finally, CMI should be understood as a disorder that may be congenital, acquired, or “unmasked” by trauma or other changes in the craniocervical region. In patients like this one, a prior head injury may have revealed a previously silent anatomic predisposition or may have altered CSF dynamics enough to make the malformation symptomatic. This framework is important because it shifts the interpretation from a purely incidental MRI finding to a potentially explanatory structural lesion. When occipital headache, neurogenic dysphagia, and unexplained sleep-related or episodic hypoxemia occur together, CMI becomes a unifying diagnosis worth actively pursuing.

## Clinical Presentation and Early Pattern Recognition

This case is best understood by following the symptom chronology rather than looking for a single classic presentation. The patient’s illness began with persistent headaches after a minor motor vehicle accident, then evolved over years into recurrent choking episodes, nocturnal awakenings with gasping, and intermittent severe oxygen desaturation. That gradual progression is important: symptomatic Chiari malformation type I can emerge as a slow, multisystem pattern involving pain, bulbar dysfunction, and respiratory control abnormalities, even when no single symptom is pathognomonic. In primary care or emergency settings, these complaints are often evaluated separately, which delays recognition of a unifying brainstem or craniocervical junction problem.

Her headache history was not typical of the textbook “Chiari cough headache.” Instead, she had chronic occipital and vertex pain described as pressure-like and persistent, with marked scalp allodynia. The detail that even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort suggests cutaneous hypersensitivity, which in this case could reflect occipital neuralgia, a burning vertex-type syndrome, or an unrelated primary headache disorder. The teaching point is not that vertex allodynia is specific for Chiari malformation, but that chronic posterior head pain plus scalp sensitivity should raise suspicion when it occurs alongside bulbar or respiratory symptoms. In other words, the headache phenotype alone may be nonspecific, but it becomes much more meaningful in combination with the rest of the history.

Her dysphagia pattern also pointed toward neurologic localization. She reported unpredictable choking with both solids and liquids, without a clear mechanical progression and with repeatedly normal laryngoscopy and upper endoscopy. Equal difficulty with liquids and solids is less suggestive of a fixed obstructing lesion and more consistent with neurogenic oropharyngeal dysfunction, especially when accompanied by coughing, choking, and a normal structural workup. In Chiari-related disease, this can reflect dysfunction of lower cranial nerve nuclei or impaired coordination at the level of the medulla and cervicomedullary junction.

Nocturnal choking episodes are another major clue. Awakening from sleep with gasping or coughing suggests that the problem is not simply swallowing difficulty during meals, but also sleep-related impairment of respiratory control. In this patient, the nocturnal episodes occurred several times per week and were later linked to desaturation on home monitoring. That combination should prompt consideration of central sleep-disordered breathing, particularly when there is a compatible neurologic context. The absence of wheezing, chest disease, or a structural upper-airway lesion makes a central mechanism more likely than a purely pulmonary or otolaryngologic explanation.

The home pulse oximetry findings were especially revealing because they showed severe desaturation to the 70%–80% range without dyspnea, panic, or obvious respiratory effort. This mismatch between physiologic compromise and symptom awareness is a key sign of impaired automatic breathing control. The airport episode illustrated the same phenomenon in a striking way: she became dizzy and nauseated, had an oxygen saturation of 78%, improved rapidly with supplemental oxygen, and did not report the expected distress of air hunger. Such episodes are difficult to explain by anxiety alone and should prompt neurologic localization, especially when they occur without an obvious cardiopulmonary cause.

The psychosocial burden was substantial and clinically important. Recurrent choking led her to avoid restaurants and social gatherings, and the fear of public episodes contributed to withdrawal and emotional distress. That burden matters because chronic unexplained symptoms are often minimized when repeated ENT or gastrointestinal studies are normal. In practice, the social consequences of these episodes can be as disabling as the physiologic events themselves and may be one of the reasons patients continue to seek care despite nondiagnostic evaluations.

The pattern that should trigger neurologic thinking is the combination seen here: chronic posterior head pain, neurogenic-appearing dysphagia, nocturnal choking, and unexplained hypoxemia without an appropriate cardiopulmonary explanation. When laryngoscopy, endoscopy, chest imaging, and pulmonary testing are unrevealing, clinicians should step back and ask whether the symptoms localize to the brainstem or craniocervical junction. Atypical Chiari presentations are often missed because each symptom is referred to a different specialty, and the disorder may not be considered unless the clinician connects the headache, bulbar symptoms, and respiratory findings into one syndrome.

## Diagnostic Workup: From Exclusion of Common Causes to Confirmation of Structural Disease

This case illustrates a common diagnostic pattern in patients with unexplained choking and intermittent hypoxemia: the initial workup is often directed at the most common ENT, gastrointestinal, pulmonary, and neuromuscular causes, and only later does a structural neurologic lesion emerge as the unifying explanation. The patient’s symptoms were especially puzzling because choking occurred with both solids and liquids, nocturnal episodes were frequent, and repeated events were not accompanied by obvious distress. That combination should raise concern for oropharyngeal dysfunction or impaired automatic breathing rather than a simple esophageal obstruction.

The first major step was exclusion of local upper aerodigestive causes. Multiple flexible laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies were normal, and reflux testing did not support laryngopharyngeal reflux. These normal findings matter because they argue against a fixed structural lesion, significant mucosal disease, or severe reflux-related laryngeal irritation as the cause of recurrent choking. In a patient whose symptoms affect both solids and liquids equally, a normal ENT and gastrointestinal evaluation should shift attention away from mechanical obstruction and toward neurologic or functional swallowing disorders.

Pulmonary evaluation was also unrevealing. Chest imaging and pulmonary function testing were normal, with preserved spirometric values and respiratory muscle pressures that did not suggest intrinsic lung disease or major ventilatory muscle weakness. This is important because recurrent desaturation can otherwise be misattributed to asthma, parenchymal lung disease, or restrictive mechanics. A normal pulmonary workup does not exclude intermittent sleep-related hypoventilation, but it makes primary pulmonary pathology much less likely.

The laboratory screen similarly helped narrow the differential. Thyroid function, vitamin B12, inflammatory markers, and antibodies associated with myasthenia gravis, including acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibodies, were all normal or negative. Together with the lack of fatigability and diurnal fluctuation, these results made a neuromuscular junction disorder unlikely. In a patient with choking, bulbar complaints, or intermittent respiratory symptoms, this kind of screening is useful because it helps rule out treatable systemic causes before pursuing a more specialized neurologic explanation.

Her arterial blood gas on room air was normal, which may seem at odds with the reported desaturation episodes. In intermittent or sleep-related hypoventilation, however, a daytime ABG can be entirely normal because it captures only a brief physiologic snapshot. Normal pH and PaCO₂ do not exclude episodic central respiratory dysfunction, particularly when the abnormality occurs during sleep or under specific circumstances that reduce voluntary respiratory drive. In this case, the normal ABG was therefore reassuring but not definitive.

The swallowing evaluation was another important part of the workup. A videofluoroscopic modified barium swallow is designed to show the oral and pharyngeal phases of swallowing and can reveal delayed initiation, poor bolus propulsion, residue, penetration, or aspiration—findings that often point to neurogenic dysphagia. In patients whose choking is unexplained by endoscopy, this study can identify impaired coordination or airway protection even when the anatomy looks normal. It is particularly valuable when solids and liquids are affected equally, because that pattern is more consistent with a neurologic than a purely mechanical problem.

The decisive clue came from revisiting older imaging. A CT scan obtained 14 years earlier after the motor vehicle accident had been read as normal, but careful re-review showed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. This kind of retrospective imaging review is often what unlocks a diagnosis after years of negative testing, especially when symptoms are progressive and involve the brainstem. The later MRI of the brain and cervical spine confirmed Chiari malformation type I, demonstrating 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent with craniocervical crowding. Imaging alone does not define clinical significance; many people have mild tonsillar ectopia without symptoms. In contrast, a patient with compatible symptoms, loss of posterior fossa CSF space, and brainstem-level complaints is much more likely to have true Chiari syndrome rather than an incidental anatomic variant.

Sleep testing provided physiologic support for the neurologic hypothesis. The type III study showed only mild overall sleep apnea by apnea-hypopnea index, but the majority of events were central rather than obstructive. That pattern fits impaired central respiratory drive better than upper-airway collapse. The study result also needs to be interpreted in context: type III testing does not include EEG, so true sleep time is not measured and the apnea-hypopnea index may be underestimated because the denominator includes wakefulness. It also cannot assess arousals or sleep staging, which limits its ability to characterize central hypoventilation fully. For that reason, when suspicion remains high—as it did here—full polysomnography with EEG, capnography, and video recording is preferred to better define sleep-related breathing abnormalities and nocturnal CO₂ retention.

Taken together, the workup shows how old imaging, symptom chronology, and targeted re-evaluation can transform a long string of negative tests into a coherent diagnosis. The patient’s recurrent choking, unexplained desaturation, chronic occipital pain, normal ENT and pulmonary studies, and MRI evidence of craniocervical crowding all pointed to Chiari malformation type I as the structural lesion underlying her symptoms. The key diagnostic lesson is that persistent choking or hypoxia with normal local evaluations should not be dismissed; it should prompt reconsideration of a neurologic cause, especially when symptoms span swallowing, sleep, and respiratory control.

## Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Reasoning Toward a Unifying Diagnosis

The key to this case is problem representation. This patient had a long history of chronic posterior head pain, recurrent choking episodes during both meals and sleep, and documented episodes of profound oxygen desaturation without an obvious pulmonary explanation. Taken together, these symptoms point away from a single isolated local disorder and toward a lesion affecting the lower brainstem and craniovertebral junction, where pathways for swallowing, airway protection, and automatic respiration are closely linked.

Her “choking” history is especially important because it sounds neurogenic rather than purely gastrointestinal. Oropharyngeal dysphagia typically presents with difficulty initiating a swallow, coughing or choking immediately after swallowing, nasal regurgitation, or a sensation of aspiration. In contrast, esophageal dysphagia usually produces a feeling of food sticking lower in the chest after swallowing has already begun. The fact that she choked on both solids and liquids equally, and that episodes occurred not only at meals but also during sleep, argues against a fixed esophageal obstruction. A mechanical narrowing generally causes progressive difficulty first with solids and later with liquids; equal difficulty from the outset suggests impaired coordination of the swallow rather than luminal blockage.

Gastroesophageal reflux disease and laryngopharyngeal reflux are common initial considerations in patients with choking, cough, and nocturnal symptoms, so it was reasonable that they were investigated first. However, this patient had multiple normal laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies, and there was no supportive objective evidence of reflux-related injury. More importantly, reflux would not explain the combination of chronic occipital headache, recurrent unexplained desaturation, and central events on sleep testing. In other words, reflux might mimic one part of the story, but it does not unify the entire syndrome.

Myasthenia gravis and related neuromuscular junction disorders were also appropriate to consider because they can cause bulbar symptoms, dysphagia, and respiratory complaints. Yet myasthenia usually produces fluctuating fatigable weakness, often with ocular findings such as ptosis or diplopia, and symptoms commonly worsen with exertion or later in the day. None of those classic features were present here. In addition, the negative acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibodies, together with the lack of clear fatigability, made this diagnosis unlikely. Other neuromuscular disorders would similarly be expected to show broader weakness, abnormal motor examination, or more definitive pulmonary muscle impairment than was seen in this patient.

Primary pulmonary disease was another necessary branch in the differential because it is the most common explanation for hypoxemia. But normal chest imaging, normal spirometry, and preserved inspiratory and expiratory pressures made intrinsic lung disease or respiratory muscle failure less likely. Her arterial blood gas was also reassuring at baseline. The key abnormality was not chronic daytime respiratory failure but intermittent desaturation, often without distress, which is a pattern that should prompt consideration of a central control problem rather than a primary lung disorder.

The repeated attribution of her episodes to anxiety is a common diagnostic pitfall. Anxiety can amplify symptom perception and may coexist with unexplained dyspnea, but it does not explain objectively documented oxygen saturations in the 70%–80% range. When physiologic events are measured and reproducible, clinicians should resist the temptation to stop at a psychiatric explanation, especially when the pattern includes nocturnal events, central apnoeas, and a structural abnormality at the craniocervical junction.

This is where acquired central hypoventilation syndrome becomes important. Central hypoventilation refers to impaired automatic respiratory drive from the brainstem, with relative preservation of voluntary breathing. Patients may breathe normally when awake and alert, but lose automatic ventilatory control during sleep or when attention is diverted. Structural brainstem lesions, including compression at the foramen magnum, can produce this phenotype. In this patient, the combination of nocturnal choking, severe desaturation without marked dyspnea, and predominantly central events on sleep study strongly supported dysfunction of automatic respiratory control rather than a peripheral pulmonary problem.

Once a brainstem process is suspected, the differential broadens to include mass lesions, demyelinating disease, stroke, and craniovertebral junction abnormalities. In this case, the historic CT re-review and confirmatory MRI were decisive. They showed a crowded foramen magnum and 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils, consistent with Chiari malformation type I. This anatomy provides a parsimonious explanation for the entire presentation: posterior fossa crowding can cause chronic occipital headache, lower cranial nerve dysfunction with choking and dysphagia, and compression or distortion of medullary respiratory centers leading to central sleep-disordered breathing or acquired hypoventilation.

The apparent mismatch between her severe symptoms and a mildly abnormal sleep study is also explainable. A type III study does not include EEG, so it cannot accurately measure true sleep time and tends to underestimate the apnea-hypopnea index. It also lacks the detailed staging and respiratory characterization of full polysomnography. In this case, the central predominance of events was more informative than the overall index alone. The clinically important point is not that the sleep study looked dramatic on its own, but that it supported a central pattern in the setting of objective desaturations and a structural lesion.

Finally, the temporal relationship to her prior motor vehicle accident cannot prove causation. The accident may have caused the Chiari malformation, worsened a pre-existing but silent congenital narrowing, or simply brought attention to a lesion that was already present. Because no pre-injury imaging exists, the exact causality cannot be established. What matters clinically is that the subsequent symptom complex fits symptomatic Chiari syndrome far better than the competing diagnoses, making Chiari malformation type I with brainstem dysfunction the most coherent unifying diagnosis.

## Management, Monitoring, and Clinical Lessons

The management goal in symptomatic Chiari malformation type I is not simply to label the anatomic abnormality, but to reverse the physiology it creates. In patients with bulbar or respiratory manifestations, treatment is directed at relieving crowding at the craniocervical junction, restoring cerebrospinal fluid flow, and reducing compression of the medulla and lower cranial nerves. This is why posterior fossa decompression is the principal neurosurgical option when symptoms are attributable to Chiari malformation. In practice, the presence of choking, dysphagia, central apneas, or unexplained hypoxemia makes the diagnosis more clinically urgent than an incidental MRI finding with tonsillar descent alone.

Respiratory symptoms are especially important because they suggest brainstem involvement. Central sleep-disordered breathing, episodic desaturation, and apparent “forgetting to breathe” are concerning for disruption of the automatic respiratory centers in the medulla. In this case, the sleep study showed only mild overall sleep apnea, but the events were predominantly central, and the patient had documented saturations in the 70%–80% range without dyspnea. That combination should not be dismissed as mild disease. Even when polysomnography is limited by technique, recurrent central events plus severe desaturation are enough to justify prompt neurosurgical assessment, because posterior fossa decompression may improve or reverse the respiratory pattern by relieving the underlying compression.

Because the patient chose to defer surgery, conservative management became a careful risk-management strategy rather than definitive therapy. For her, this meant symptom coping measures, dietary modification to reduce choking risk, and ongoing counseling about warning signs that should trigger urgent reassessment. Conservative care can be reasonable when a patient needs time before deciding on an operation, but it should never imply that the problem has resolved. In a patient with bulbar symptoms, conservative management must be paired with explicit safety advice, including attention to aspiration risk, recurrent nocturnal choking, and any worsening episodes of desaturation or neurologic symptoms.

The follow-up plan in this case was deliberately structured: monthly clinical review, periodic overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance. That approach reflects an important principle in Chiari management—symptoms and anatomy must be followed over time, because either can evolve. A stable interval does not exclude ongoing risk, especially when the clinical picture includes central hypoventilation or dysphagia. A more definitive type I polysomnography with EEG and capnography was also planned, since the initial type III study could underestimate severity by using recording time rather than true sleep time. Repeated reassessment is essential because the decision to proceed to surgery may change if symptoms worsen, desaturation becomes more frequent or more severe, new neurologic deficits appear, or imaging shows progression.

This case also illustrates the value of multidisciplinary care. Family medicine served as the point of coordination, but the diagnostic and management pathway depended on input from neurology, pulmonology, sleep medicine, radiology, otolaryngology, gastroenterology, and neurosurgery. ENT and GI investigations were useful in excluding mucosal or structural causes of choking, pulmonology helped rule out intrinsic lung disease, sleep medicine characterized the breathing abnormality, and radiology made the critical diagnosis by re-reviewing prior imaging. Neurosurgery then framed the findings in terms of potential benefit from decompression. This is a good example of how a localized structural problem can present to multiple specialties before the unifying diagnosis is recognized.

The major diagnostic lesson is that negative endoscopy or laryngoscopy does not end the workup when the symptoms remain unexplained. Equal difficulty with solids and liquids, recurrent choking during sleep, central desaturation without dyspnea, and long-standing occipital headache should prompt consideration of a structural brainstem or craniocervical junction disorder. In this patient, the overlooked CT scan from 14 years earlier was decisive once it was revisited with the right clinical question in mind. When the phenotype suggests a neurologic localization, historical imaging should be re-examined rather than assumed to be definitively normal. For trainees, the key message is to think symptom-based: in Chiari malformation, the anatomic finding matters most when it explains a coherent pattern of brainstem and cranial nerve dysfunction.
