Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] In an adult with recurrent choking to both solids and liquids, coughing during meals, and repeatedly normal laryngoscopy and upper endoscopy, what feature most strongly suggests a neurogenic oropharyngeal dysphagia rather than a mechanical oesophageal lesion?

The key clue is **equivalent difficulty with both solids and liquids from the outset, especially when it is accompanied by coughing or choking during the act of swallowing**. That pattern favors **oropharyngeal neurogenic dysphagia**—impaired swallow initiation/airway protection—rather than a mechanical esophageal obstruction, which classically causes **progressive dysphagia for solids first and liquids later**. In this case, the repeatedly normal laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies further argue against a fixed structural lesion and point toward a bulbar or brainstem process, as ultimately seen with Chiari I–related lower cranial nerve dysfunction.

### [Morning Report] A woman has chronic occipital headaches, recurrent nocturnal choking, severe oxygen desaturations with little subjective dyspnoea, and a normal pulmonary evaluation. What unifying neurological diagnosis should be considered, and what imaging study best confirms it?

The unifying diagnosis is **Chiari malformation type I with brainstem compression causing acquired central hypoventilation syndrome**—the adult form of “Ondine’s curse.” In this setting, **occipital headaches, nocturnal choking, central apneic/desaturation events, and a normal pulmonary workup** point away from primary lung disease and toward a **craniocervical junction lesion**.

The **best confirmatory study is MRI of the brain and cervical spine**, with sagittal images through the **foramen magnum/craniocervical junction**. MRI is the diagnostic standard because it directly shows **cerebellar tonsillar descent, crowding of the posterior fossa, and loss of CSF space**, as in this case where MRI demonstrated **11 mm tonsillar herniation**. CT may suggest the diagnosis retrospectively, but **MRI confirms it**.

### [Clinical Pearls] What radiographic criterion defines Chiari malformation type I on sagittal MRI, and what historical CT finding at the craniocervical junction should raise suspicion even if the original report was interpreted as normal?

On sagittal MRI, Chiari malformation type I is defined by **caudal descent of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum**. In this case, MRI showed **11 mm of tonsillar descent**.

On historical CT of the craniocervical junction, the clue that should prompt suspicion is a **crowded foramen magnum with effacement or loss of the normal CSF space at the cervicomedullary junction**—in this patient, the re-reviewed CT also showed **cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion**. Even if the original CT report was read as normal, that pattern should raise concern for Chiari I and prompt MRI confirmation.

### [Pathophysiology] How can Chiari malformation type I produce an acquired central hypoventilation phenotype with preserved voluntary breathing but impaired automatic breathing during sleep or distraction?

Chiari malformation type I can cause an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome because the herniated cerebellar tonsils crowd the foramen magnum and compress the medulla, where the automatic respiratory network is located. That network — including the pre-Bötzinger complex, ventrolateral medulla, and nucleus tractus solitarius — generates the unconscious rhythm of breathing and mediates the ventilatory response to rising CO₂, especially during sleep.

Voluntary breathing, however, is largely cortically driven and can temporarily bypass this injured automatic circuitry. That is why patients may breathe adequately when awake, instructed, or strongly focused, yet develop hypoventilation or central apneas when asleep, drowsy, or distracted. In this case, the patient’s normal daytime gas exchange and preserved respiratory mechanics contrasted with predominantly central events on sleep study and profound desaturation during the airport episode, when attention was diverted.

The same brainstem involvement may also blunt ascending respiratory sensation, so patients can be strikingly hypoxemic without feeling dyspneic. In other words, Chiari I can create an acquired “Ondine’s curse”: preserved voluntary breathing with failure of automatic breathing when cortical drive falls away.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which findings in this case argue against gastro-oesophageal reflux or laryngopharyngeal reflux, myasthenia gravis, and intrinsic pulmonary disease as the primary explanation for the choking and hypoxaemia?

Several features in this case make reflux, myasthenia gravis, and intrinsic lung disease unlikely as the primary explanation.

For **gastro-oesophageal reflux or laryngopharyngeal reflux**, the strongest arguments against it were the **repeatedly normal evaluations**: three flexible laryngoscopies, two upper endoscopies, and normal reflux testing, together with **no response to maximal acid suppression**. In addition, the symptoms were not just postprandial throat irritation but **recurrent choking during both meals and sleep**, affecting **solids and liquids equally**, which is less typical of a primary reflux disorder.

For **myasthenia gravis**, the case lacked the usual clinical pattern of **fluctuating fatigable weakness** and **diurnal variation**. The **acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibodies were negative**, and there was no evidence of a broader neuromuscular junction syndrome. The equal difficulty with solids and liquids could suggest bulbar dysfunction, but the absence of fatigability and the negative serology argue strongly against MG as the unifying diagnosis.

For **intrinsic pulmonary disease**, the respiratory workup was essentially normal: **chest imaging and pulmonary function tests were normal**, including **normal FEV1, FVC, maximal inspiratory pressure, and maximal expiratory pressure**, and the **arterial blood gas on room air was normal** between episodes. The hypoxaemia was **episodic and sometimes profound without respiratory distress**, which is not the pattern of primary parenchymal lung disease. Together, these findings point away from a pulmonary gas-exchange problem and toward a central cause of the desaturation and choking.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do acquired central hypoventilation syndrome and congenital central hypoventilation syndrome differ in pathogenesis, and why does this patient fit the acquired form rather than the congenital PHOX2B-associated disorder?

Acquired and congenital central hypoventilation syndromes share the same physiologic defect — failure of automatic ventilatory control, especially during sleep, with relative preservation of voluntary breathing — but their pathogenesis is different.

**Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS)** is a **genetic autonomic disorder**, classically caused by **PHOX2B mutations**. It reflects abnormal development and function of the brainstem autonomic respiratory network, so hypoventilation is usually present **from birth or early infancy**, often accompanied by other autonomic features.

**Acquired central hypoventilation syndrome** is **not genetic**; it results from **injury or compression of the medullary respiratory centers or their connections**. Causes include stroke, trauma, tumors, demyelination, and, as in this case, **Chiari malformation with craniocervical junction/brainstem compression**. The mechanism is loss of automatic respiratory drive, particularly during sleep, while wakeful, voluntary breathing may remain intact.

This patient fits the **acquired** form rather than PHOX2B-associated CCHS for several reasons. First, her symptoms began **in adulthood**, not in infancy or childhood. Second, the course followed a **motor vehicle accident**, and imaging later showed **Chiari I malformation with 11-mm tonsillar descent and a crowded foramen magnum**, providing a structural explanation for brainstem dysfunction. Third, her presentation is exactly what one expects from acquired central hypoventilation due to brainstem compression: **nocturnal central events, episodic profound desaturation, and preserved daytime pulmonary function**. There is no history of lifelong ventilatory failure or syndromic autonomic disease that would suggest congenital PHOX2B-related CCHS.

### [Pathophysiology] What brainstem mechanisms could explain why a patient with central hypoventilation develops profound oxygen desaturation without obvious respiratory distress or a sensation of air hunger?

In this patient, the most likely explanation is **compression of the dorsal medulla at the craniocervical junction by the Chiari I malformation**, disrupting both **automatic respiratory drive** and the **brainstem-to-cortex pathways that generate the conscious sensation of dyspnea**.

The key brainstem structures are the **medullary respiratory pattern generator**—including the **pre-Bötzinger complex** and adjacent ventrolateral medulla—and the **nucleus tractus solitarius**, which integrates afferent input from peripheral chemoreceptors. When these regions are compressed, the patient may **lose involuntary ventilatory responses to hypoxia and hypercapnia**, especially during sleep or when attention is diverted, while **voluntary breathing remains intact** because cortical motor pathways are preserved. That is why patients with acquired central hypoventilation can appear completely comfortable yet still stop breathing adequately.

The lack of “air hunger” is also important. The sensation of breathlessness is not simply a reflex to low oxygen; it requires intact **interoceptive signaling through the brainstem to higher cortical centers**, particularly the **insula and anterior cingulate cortex**. If those afferent signals are interrupted by brainstem dysfunction, the cortex may never receive the mismatch signal that normally produces dyspnea. In other words, the patient can become profoundly hypoxemic **without the expected warning symptom**.

So, in this case, the Chiari-related brainstem compression likely caused an acquired form of central hypoventilation—“Ondine’s curse”—with **failure of automatic breathing plus impaired conscious perception of respiratory distress**, explaining the severe desaturations seen at home and in the airport despite little obvious respiratory effort.

### [Clinical Pearls] In suspected central sleep-disordered breathing, what is the major limitation of a type III sleep study compared with full polysomnography with EEG and capnography, and how can this lead to underestimation of disease severity?

The key limitation of a type III study is that it does not measure EEG sleep staging, and often does not measure CO₂. In suspected central sleep-disordered breathing, that matters because respiratory event indices are then calculated using total recording time rather than true total sleep time, so periods of wakefulness dilute the apnea-hypopnea index and make the disorder look milder than it is.  

In addition, without capnography, type III testing can miss sustained nocturnal hypoventilation and hypercapnia, which are especially important in central hypoventilation syndromes. Thus, a patient may appear to have only “mild” sleep apnea by AHI, as in this case, while still having clinically significant central respiratory failure with profound desaturations and brainstem-related dysregulation. Full polysomnography with EEG and capnography is therefore the preferred study when central hypoventilation is suspected.

### [Management] In a symptomatic adult with Chiari malformation type I, bulbar symptoms, and predominantly central respiratory events, what clinical features favor posterior fossa decompression, and what follow-up strategy is reasonable if the patient initially defers surgery?

Posterior fossa decompression is favored when Chiari I is not merely an imaging finding but a symptomatic brainstem syndrome. In this patient, several features point in that direction: long-standing occipital headaches; progressive bulbar symptoms with choking and dysphagia for both solids and liquids; nocturnal choking; and objective respiratory abnormalities with profound desaturations and sleep-disordered breathing that is predominantly central. Coupled with MRI evidence of a crowded foramen magnum and 11 mm tonsillar descent, these findings suggest cervicomedullary compression affecting lower cranial nerve and respiratory control centers. That constellation generally supports decompression, because it is the only treatment that addresses the anatomic cause and may improve both swallowing and central hypoventilation.

If the patient initially declines surgery, a structured surveillance plan is reasonable, rather than passive observation. In the case report, this was done with monthly clinical reassessment, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance, with a low threshold to revisit surgery if symptoms worsen. It is also appropriate to complete a more definitive sleep evaluation when feasible, ideally full polysomnography with EEG and capnography, because type III testing can underestimate the severity of central events. Any increase in choking, new neurologic deficits, worsening desaturation, or evidence of hypercapnia should prompt renewed surgical discussion.
