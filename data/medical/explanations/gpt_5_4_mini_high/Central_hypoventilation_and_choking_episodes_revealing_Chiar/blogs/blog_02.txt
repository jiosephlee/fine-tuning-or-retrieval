Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Choking on Normal Scopes: Neurogenic Dysphagia in Chiari I

## The part that should make you stop and reframe the case

A woman in her early 40s had 7 years of choking on both solids and liquids, three normal flexible laryngoscopies, two normal upper endoscopies, and no obvious explanation for why she kept “choking” without ever having a true airway obstruction. Then came the bigger clue: 14 years of chronic occipital/vertex headaches, nocturnal choking, and home pulse-ox readings in the 70s–80s without much distress. When her old trauma CT was re-reviewed, the “normal” scan turned out to show a crowded foramen magnum. MRI later confirmed Chiari I with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent.

That is the kind of case that reminds you: normal scopes do not equal a normal swallow.

## Why the story matters more than the scopes

Flexible laryngoscopy and EGD are anatomy tests. They can exclude a mass, stricture, mucosal lesion, or obvious laryngeal pathology. What they cannot tell you is whether the swallow is actually coordinated.

The history here is the giveaway. Choking on solids and liquids equally is not the classic pattern of a fixed obstructing lesion. When patients describe immediate coughing, gagging, or “the swallow goes wrong,” you need to think about the oropharyngeal phase and the neurologic control of swallowing.

A simple heuristic:

- **Solids first** tends to suggest a mechanical problem.
- **Solids and liquids together** pushes you toward **neurogenic dysphagia** or a motility disorder.
- **Coughing/choking during the swallow** is an oropharyngeal problem until proven otherwise.

In this case, the repeated normal ENT and GI scopes were reassuring only in one narrow sense: there was no visible structural lesion. They did not explain the physiology.

## Why Chiari belongs on the list

Chiari I is defined by cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum, but the number on the MRI is not the whole story. Symptoms come from crowding at the craniocervical junction, with compression of the medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei.

That matters because the swallow is a brainstem job.

Lower cranial nerve involvement in Chiari is reported in a meaningful minority of patients, often presenting as oropharyngeal dysfunction such as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia. In other words, the patient may not have an “esophageal problem” at all — she may have a problem with the neural circuitry that coordinates swallowing.

This case is also a good reminder that Chiari does not always show up with the textbook cough/Valsalva occipital headache. Her headaches were chronic, occipital/vertex, and not clearly provoked by straining. If you wait for the classic presentation, you will miss the odd ones.

And Chiari can be bigger than dysphagia. In this patient, the nocturnal choking and unexplained desaturation episodes suggested that the same brainstem process could also be affecting automatic breathing.

## Where videofluoroscopic swallow study fits

If the history says “oropharyngeal,” the next useful test is often a **videofluoroscopic swallow study** (modified barium swallow).

That study gives you something scopes cannot: **physiology**.

It can show:

- delayed swallow initiation
- poor laryngeal elevation
- penetration or aspiration
- pharyngeal residue
- impaired upper esophageal sphincter opening
- discoordination between swallow phases

That is exactly why VFSS is so useful when the patient keeps choking despite normal ENT/GI anatomy. It helps you localize the problem to the swallow mechanism rather than the esophagus or airway lumen.

In this case report, the key educational point is not a specific fluoroscopic pattern — it’s that persistent choking with normal scopes should have prompted a physiologic swallow evaluation and a neurologic search much earlier.

## When to stop thinking ENT/GI and start thinking brainstem

The clinical pivot happens when symptoms refuse to behave like a local throat or esophageal disorder.

I would push neurologic workup when you see any combination of:

- choking with **both solids and liquids**
- cough/gag during or right after swallowing
- **nocturnal choking**
- unexplained desaturation or hypoventilation
- occipital headache or other posterior fossa symptoms
- repeatedly normal laryngoscopy/endoscopy
- any hint of bulbar dysfunction

At that point, MRI of the brain and craniocervical junction is the right move. If older imaging exists, re-review it yourself or ask radiology to take a second look. In this case, the answer had been sitting in an old CT for 14 years.

That’s a very Chiari lesson: sometimes the diagnosis is not missed because the image was absent — it’s missed because nobody thought to ask the right question of the image.

## A few practical pearls

The swallow story here was clinically “neurogenic” long before the MRI made it obvious. The combination of equal difficulty with solids and liquids, repeated normal scopes, nocturnal symptoms, and unexplained hypoxia should make you think beyond reflux, stricture, and laryngeal irritation.

Also worth remembering: symptomatic Chiari is treated by treating the anatomy. Posterior fossa decompression is the intervention that addresses the crowding at the foramen magnum, and it can improve brainstem-related symptoms when they are truly Chiari-driven.

## Take-home points

- **Equal trouble with solids and liquids** should make you think neurogenic dysphagia or motility, not just a mechanical obstruction.
- **Normal laryngoscopy and EGD do not rule out a swallowing disorder**; they only rule out visible structural disease.
- **Videofluoroscopic swallow study is the key physiologic test** when the history suggests oropharyngeal dysphagia.
- **Chiari I can present with dysphagia/choking from lower cranial nerve and brainstem compression**, even without classic cough headache.
- **Persistent choking plus unexplained respiratory symptoms should trigger MRI of the craniocervical junction**, not endless repeat scopes.
