Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# When a “Mild” Sleep Study Isn’t Mild: The Type III PSG Trap

## The number that almost let this case slip through

An AHI of 6.0 usually sounds reassuring. Mild sleep apnea, maybe observe, maybe weight loss, maybe CPAP discussion depending on symptoms. But in this case, that number was deeply misleading.

This woman had 7 years of recurrent choking during meals and sleep, repeated nocturnal desaturations into the 70s–80s, chronic occipital/vertex headaches, and ultimately an 11 mm Chiari I malformation at the craniocervical junction. Her sleep study was labeled “mild,” yet 72% of events were central. That combination should make you pause.

Because the issue here was not “a little sleep apnea.” The issue was likely brainstem-related sleep-disordered breathing, with a study that was not designed to fully characterize it.

## Why a type III study can undercall severity

Type III sleep studies are useful, but they are not full polysomnography. They typically measure airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and heart rate. What they do not measure is just as important: EEG.

No EEG means no true sleep staging and no direct way to know how much of the night the patient was actually asleep. That matters because AHI is supposed to be events per hour of sleep. In many type III studies, the denominator is effectively total recording time, not true sleep time. If the patient is awake for long stretches, the event rate gets diluted.

So a study may report an apparently low AHI even when the patient’s sleep itself is quite abnormal.

That is the trap.

In this case, the reported AHI of 6.0 likely underestimated the burden of sleep-disordered breathing. The patient’s symptoms, the desaturations, and the anatomy all argued that the physiology was more serious than the number suggested.

## Central events are the clue, not the footnote

The real red flag wasn’t just the low AHI. It was the phenotype:

- recurrent nocturnal choking
- severe desaturation without obvious dyspnea
- predominantly central events
- normal pulmonary workup
- structural abnormality at the foramen magnum

That is not the usual obstructive sleep apnea story.

Central events point you away from a floppy airway and toward impaired respiratory drive or impaired brainstem control. In Chiari I, cerebellar tonsillar descent can crowd the posterior fossa and affect medullary respiratory centers. That can produce a central hypoventilation pattern, sometimes clinically resembling acquired Ondine’s curse.

And in that setting, a “mild” home-style sleep study can be falsely calming.

## Why the absence of EEG matters so much

Without EEG, you lose several things that matter in suspected central hypoventilation:

- true total sleep time
- sleep stage information
- arousal scoring
- better discrimination between wake breathing and sleep breathing
- more accurate characterization of respiratory instability

That means a type III study may detect desaturation, but miss the full story behind it.

This is especially important when symptoms are intermittent, nocturnal, or brainstem-driven. If the patient has fragmented sleep, frequent awakenings, or prolonged wake during the test, the reported index can look deceptively low.

So when the story and the number don’t match, trust the story.

## Don’t let “mild” override severe physiology

A few practical clinical pearls from this case:

If a patient has unexplained nocturnal choking or desaturation, ask whether the pattern is central, obstructive, or mixed. Equal difficulty with solids and liquids, normal laryngoscopy, and normal endoscopy should push you to think beyond local ENT or GI pathology.

If desaturation is profound but the patient is not dyspneic, think about impaired central perception of respiratory distress, not just “anxiety.”

If there are chronic occipital headaches, especially with a long and confusing diagnostic path, don’t forget the craniocervical junction.

And if the sleep study is type III, remember that it is a screening tool, not the final word, particularly when you suspect central hypoventilation or brainstem pathology.

## Where capnography fits in

Oxygen saturation tells you about oxygenation. It does not tell you whether the patient is retaining carbon dioxide or hypoventilating through the night.

That is where end-tidal or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring becomes valuable. In suspected central hypoventilation, capnography helps identify sustained hypoventilation that may not be obvious on SpO₂ alone, especially if supplemental oxygen is used or if desaturation is intermittent.

For this reason, full attended polysomnography with EEG plus capnography is the better test when the concern is central apnea or hypoventilation rather than straightforward obstructive sleep apnea.

In this case, a type I PSG with EEG, capnography, and video was planned because the type III study was not enough to close the book.

## The bigger lesson: anatomy can trump a number

This patient’s eventual diagnosis came from looking back at an old head CT and recognizing a crowded foramen magnum, then confirming Chiari I on MRI. That’s the clinical lesson here: when physiology is strange, don’t stop at a mildly abnormal sleep study.

A low AHI does not rule out serious sleep-related breathing pathology if:

- the events are mostly central
- the desaturations are deep
- the symptoms are dramatic
- the test lacks EEG
- the anatomy suggests brainstem compression

## Take-home points

- A type III sleep study can underestimate severity because it lacks EEG and may use recording time rather than true sleep time.
- A “mild” AHI is not reassuring when most events are central and the patient has severe desaturations.
- In suspected central hypoventilation, add capnography and get full attended polysomnography if you can.
- Persistent nocturnal choking plus occipital headaches should prompt consideration of craniocervical pathology, including Chiari I.
- Trust the clinical syndrome, not the label on the sleep report.
