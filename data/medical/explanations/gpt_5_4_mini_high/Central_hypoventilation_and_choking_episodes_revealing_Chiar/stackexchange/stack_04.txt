Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Pathophysiology] How can Chiari malformation type I produce an acquired central hypoventilation phenotype with preserved voluntary breathing but impaired automatic breathing during sleep or distraction?

Chiari malformation type I can cause an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome because the herniated cerebellar tonsils crowd the foramen magnum and compress the medulla, where the automatic respiratory network is located. That network — including the pre-Bötzinger complex, ventrolateral medulla, and nucleus tractus solitarius — generates the unconscious rhythm of breathing and mediates the ventilatory response to rising CO₂, especially during sleep.

Voluntary breathing, however, is largely cortically driven and can temporarily bypass this injured automatic circuitry. That is why patients may breathe adequately when awake, instructed, or strongly focused, yet develop hypoventilation or central apneas when asleep, drowsy, or distracted. In this case, the patient’s normal daytime gas exchange and preserved respiratory mechanics contrasted with predominantly central events on sleep study and profound desaturation during the airport episode, when attention was diverted.

The same brainstem involvement may also blunt ascending respiratory sensation, so patients can be strikingly hypoxemic without feeling dyspneic. In other words, Chiari I can create an acquired “Ondine’s curse”: preserved voluntary breathing with failure of automatic breathing when cortical drive falls away.
