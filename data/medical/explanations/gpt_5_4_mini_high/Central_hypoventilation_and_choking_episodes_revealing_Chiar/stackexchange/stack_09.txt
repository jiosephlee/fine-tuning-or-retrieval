Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Management] In a symptomatic adult with Chiari malformation type I, bulbar symptoms, and predominantly central respiratory events, what clinical features favor posterior fossa decompression, and what follow-up strategy is reasonable if the patient initially defers surgery?

Posterior fossa decompression is favored when Chiari I is not merely an imaging finding but a symptomatic brainstem syndrome. In this patient, several features point in that direction: long-standing occipital headaches; progressive bulbar symptoms with choking and dysphagia for both solids and liquids; nocturnal choking; and objective respiratory abnormalities with profound desaturations and sleep-disordered breathing that is predominantly central. Coupled with MRI evidence of a crowded foramen magnum and 11 mm tonsillar descent, these findings suggest cervicomedullary compression affecting lower cranial nerve and respiratory control centers. That constellation generally supports decompression, because it is the only treatment that addresses the anatomic cause and may improve both swallowing and central hypoventilation.

If the patient initially declines surgery, a structured surveillance plan is reasonable, rather than passive observation. In the case report, this was done with monthly clinical reassessment, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance, with a low threshold to revisit surgery if symptoms worsen. It is also appropriate to complete a more definitive sleep evaluation when feasible, ideally full polysomnography with EEG and capnography, because type III testing can underestimate the severity of central events. Any increase in choking, new neurologic deficits, worsening desaturation, or evidence of hypercapnia should prompt renewed surgical discussion.
