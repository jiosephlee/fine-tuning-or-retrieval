Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# When the Patient “Forgets to Breathe”: Chiari I as Acquired Central Hypoventilation

## The clue that should stop you in your tracks

This case is a great reminder that not every choking episode is an esophageal problem, and not every hypoxemic patient is short of breath.

A woman in her early 40s had years of recurrent choking with both solids and liquids, frequent nocturnal choking, chronic occipital headaches, and repeated oxygen saturations in the 70%–80% range — yet she often looked surprisingly comfortable. She had already been through the usual gauntlet: multiple laryngoscopies, upper endoscopies, pulmonary testing, chest imaging. All unrevealing.

The eventual unifying diagnosis was Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm tonsillar descent and crowding at the foramen magnum, producing a phenotype of acquired central hypoventilation. In other words: preserved voluntary breathing, impaired automatic ventilation, especially during sleep or when attention drifted away from breathing.

That is the headline lesson here. Profound desaturation without proportional dyspnea should make you think beyond lung parenchyma, beyond reflux, and beyond simple “anxiety.”

## Why Chiari I can masquerade as ENT, GI, or pulmonary disease

Chiari I is not just “tonsils low on MRI.” The modern view is more nuanced: some people have an anatomic variant, while others have a clinically meaningful craniocervical junction problem with brainstem compression and cerebrospinal fluid flow disruption.

In symptomatic Chiari syndrome, headache and bulbar symptoms are the big clues. This patient had both:

- Chronic occipital/vertex headaches
- Recurrent choking episodes affecting solids and liquids equally
- Nocturnal choking and gasping
- Unexplained hypoxemia
- Central events on sleep testing

That combination should push you toward a neuroanatomic cause.

The swallowing complaint is especially important. Equal difficulty with solids and liquids is often a neuromuscular or neurogenic pattern, not a mechanical obstruction. In this case, repeated ENT and GI workups were normal, which is exactly the kind of result that should trigger a broader differential rather than reassurance.

Chiari can affect lower cranial nerve function and medullary respiratory centers, so the patient may present with what looks like dysphagia, laryngospasm, aspiration, or sleep-disordered breathing. The body can look “pulmonary,” but the problem lives at the craniocervical junction.

## “She forgets to breathe” — what that really means

The phrase is catchy, but the physiology matters.

Respiration has both voluntary and automatic control:

- **Voluntary breathing**: cortical, conscious, and often preserved
- **Automatic breathing**: brainstem-driven, essential during sleep and when attention is elsewhere

Chiari-related medullary compression can selectively impair the automatic pathway. That explains why a patient may breathe adequately when awake and focused, but desaturate during sleep or even during quiet distraction.

This case had a vivid example of that pattern: at an airport, she became dizzy and nauseated, and medical staff found her saturation at 78%. Supplemental oxygen improved her symptoms within minutes. The striking part is not just the number — it’s the relative lack of subjective distress despite major hypoxemia.

That mismatch is a red flag.

## Don’t let a mild sleep study fool you

The sleep study here showed only mild sleep apnea by AHI, but 72% of events were central. That combination should not be brushed off.

Two important caveats:

1. **Type III sleep studies can underestimate severity**  
   Without EEG, total sleep time is estimated from recording time, which can dilute the AHI.

2. **AHI does not tell the whole story**  
   Central predominance, significant desaturations, and anatomic brainstem compression matter just as much as the headline AHI.

So if the story screams hypoventilation but the sleep study looks “mild,” don’t stop there. The pattern still matters.

## When should you image the brain and craniocervical junction?

This is the practical FOAM point.

If a patient has unexplained hypoxemia, central apneas, or choking episodes — especially when ENT/GI/pulmonary testing is unrevealing — MRI of the brain and cervicomedullary junction deserves serious consideration.

In this case, the diagnosis was hiding in plain sight: an old CT done after trauma had already shown a crowded foramen magnum, but it was missed initially. A later MRI confirmed Chiari I.

That retrospective review is a good reminder: old imaging is not just history, it can be the diagnosis.

### Think about Chiari when you see:

- Central apneas or central hypoventilation
- Desaturation out of proportion to distress
- Choking/dysphagia with normal endoscopy or laryngoscopy
- Occipital headaches, especially with posterior fossa symptoms
- Brainstem-type complaints with otherwise unrevealing workup

## A few clinical pearls from this case

The case also highlights a common diagnostic trap: symptoms get routed into separate silos — ENT for choking, GI for dysphagia, pulmonary for hypoxemia, neuro only after everything else is negative. Chiari is one of those diagnoses that can connect all the dots if you step back early enough.

It also reinforces an important nuance: Chiari I on imaging is not automatically the cause of symptoms. The diagnosis is clinical-radiographic. In symptomatic patients, the imaging finding has to fit the story.

In this patient, it did.

Posterior fossa decompression was recommended because of the symptomatic brainstem compression, but she chose conservative management for now, with structured follow-up and surveillance. That’s a separate reminder that diagnosis does not always equal immediate surgery — but it does change the conversation.

## Take-home points

- Profound hypoxemia with little or no dyspnea should prompt you to think about central hypoventilation, not just pulmonary disease.
- Recurrent choking with both solids and liquids, normal endoscopy/laryngoscopy, and chronic occipital headaches should raise suspicion for a neuroanatomic cause.
- Chiari I can impair automatic breathing while preserving voluntary ventilation, creating a “forgets to breathe” phenotype.
- A “mild” sleep study does not exclude clinically important disease, especially if it is type III and central events predominate.
- When unexplained central apneas or hypoxemia persist, image the brain and craniocervical junction early.



# Choking on Normal Scopes: Neurogenic Dysphagia in Chiari I

## The part that should make you stop and reframe the case

A woman in her early 40s had 7 years of choking on both solids and liquids, three normal flexible laryngoscopies, two normal upper endoscopies, and no obvious explanation for why she kept “choking” without ever having a true airway obstruction. Then came the bigger clue: 14 years of chronic occipital/vertex headaches, nocturnal choking, and home pulse-ox readings in the 70s–80s without much distress. When her old trauma CT was re-reviewed, the “normal” scan turned out to show a crowded foramen magnum. MRI later confirmed Chiari I with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent.

That is the kind of case that reminds you: normal scopes do not equal a normal swallow.

## Why the story matters more than the scopes

Flexible laryngoscopy and EGD are anatomy tests. They can exclude a mass, stricture, mucosal lesion, or obvious laryngeal pathology. What they cannot tell you is whether the swallow is actually coordinated.

The history here is the giveaway. Choking on solids and liquids equally is not the classic pattern of a fixed obstructing lesion. When patients describe immediate coughing, gagging, or “the swallow goes wrong,” you need to think about the oropharyngeal phase and the neurologic control of swallowing.

A simple heuristic:

- **Solids first** tends to suggest a mechanical problem.
- **Solids and liquids together** pushes you toward **neurogenic dysphagia** or a motility disorder.
- **Coughing/choking during the swallow** is an oropharyngeal problem until proven otherwise.

In this case, the repeated normal ENT and GI scopes were reassuring only in one narrow sense: there was no visible structural lesion. They did not explain the physiology.

## Why Chiari belongs on the list

Chiari I is defined by cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum, but the number on the MRI is not the whole story. Symptoms come from crowding at the craniocervical junction, with compression of the medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei.

That matters because the swallow is a brainstem job.

Lower cranial nerve involvement in Chiari is reported in a meaningful minority of patients, often presenting as oropharyngeal dysfunction such as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia. In other words, the patient may not have an “esophageal problem” at all — she may have a problem with the neural circuitry that coordinates swallowing.

This case is also a good reminder that Chiari does not always show up with the textbook cough/Valsalva occipital headache. Her headaches were chronic, occipital/vertex, and not clearly provoked by straining. If you wait for the classic presentation, you will miss the odd ones.

And Chiari can be bigger than dysphagia. In this patient, the nocturnal choking and unexplained desaturation episodes suggested that the same brainstem process could also be affecting automatic breathing.

## Where videofluoroscopic swallow study fits

If the history says “oropharyngeal,” the next useful test is often a **videofluoroscopic swallow study** (modified barium swallow).

That study gives you something scopes cannot: **physiology**.

It can show:

- delayed swallow initiation
- poor laryngeal elevation
- penetration or aspiration
- pharyngeal residue
- impaired upper esophageal sphincter opening
- discoordination between swallow phases

That is exactly why VFSS is so useful when the patient keeps choking despite normal ENT/GI anatomy. It helps you localize the problem to the swallow mechanism rather than the esophagus or airway lumen.

In this case report, the key educational point is not a specific fluoroscopic pattern — it’s that persistent choking with normal scopes should have prompted a physiologic swallow evaluation and a neurologic search much earlier.

## When to stop thinking ENT/GI and start thinking brainstem

The clinical pivot happens when symptoms refuse to behave like a local throat or esophageal disorder.

I would push neurologic workup when you see any combination of:

- choking with **both solids and liquids**
- cough/gag during or right after swallowing
- **nocturnal choking**
- unexplained desaturation or hypoventilation
- occipital headache or other posterior fossa symptoms
- repeatedly normal laryngoscopy/endoscopy
- any hint of bulbar dysfunction

At that point, MRI of the brain and craniocervical junction is the right move. If older imaging exists, re-review it yourself or ask radiology to take a second look. In this case, the answer had been sitting in an old CT for 14 years.

That’s a very Chiari lesson: sometimes the diagnosis is not missed because the image was absent — it’s missed because nobody thought to ask the right question of the image.

## A few practical pearls

The swallow story here was clinically “neurogenic” long before the MRI made it obvious. The combination of equal difficulty with solids and liquids, repeated normal scopes, nocturnal symptoms, and unexplained hypoxia should make you think beyond reflux, stricture, and laryngeal irritation.

Also worth remembering: symptomatic Chiari is treated by treating the anatomy. Posterior fossa decompression is the intervention that addresses the crowding at the foramen magnum, and it can improve brainstem-related symptoms when they are truly Chiari-driven.

## Take-home points

- **Equal trouble with solids and liquids** should make you think neurogenic dysphagia or motility, not just a mechanical obstruction.
- **Normal laryngoscopy and EGD do not rule out a swallowing disorder**; they only rule out visible structural disease.
- **Videofluoroscopic swallow study is the key physiologic test** when the history suggests oropharyngeal dysphagia.
- **Chiari I can present with dysphagia/choking from lower cranial nerve and brainstem compression**, even without classic cough headache.
- **Persistent choking plus unexplained respiratory symptoms should trigger MRI of the craniocervical junction**, not endless repeat scopes.



# When a “Mild” Sleep Study Isn’t Mild: The Type III PSG Trap

## The number that almost let this case slip through

An AHI of 6.0 usually sounds reassuring. Mild sleep apnea, maybe observe, maybe weight loss, maybe CPAP discussion depending on symptoms. But in this case, that number was deeply misleading.

This woman had 7 years of recurrent choking during meals and sleep, repeated nocturnal desaturations into the 70s–80s, chronic occipital/vertex headaches, and ultimately an 11 mm Chiari I malformation at the craniocervical junction. Her sleep study was labeled “mild,” yet 72% of events were central. That combination should make you pause.

Because the issue here was not “a little sleep apnea.” The issue was likely brainstem-related sleep-disordered breathing, with a study that was not designed to fully characterize it.

## Why a type III study can undercall severity

Type III sleep studies are useful, but they are not full polysomnography. They typically measure airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and heart rate. What they do not measure is just as important: EEG.

No EEG means no true sleep staging and no direct way to know how much of the night the patient was actually asleep. That matters because AHI is supposed to be events per hour of sleep. In many type III studies, the denominator is effectively total recording time, not true sleep time. If the patient is awake for long stretches, the event rate gets diluted.

So a study may report an apparently low AHI even when the patient’s sleep itself is quite abnormal.

That is the trap.

In this case, the reported AHI of 6.0 likely underestimated the burden of sleep-disordered breathing. The patient’s symptoms, the desaturations, and the anatomy all argued that the physiology was more serious than the number suggested.

## Central events are the clue, not the footnote

The real red flag wasn’t just the low AHI. It was the phenotype:

- recurrent nocturnal choking
- severe desaturation without obvious dyspnea
- predominantly central events
- normal pulmonary workup
- structural abnormality at the foramen magnum

That is not the usual obstructive sleep apnea story.

Central events point you away from a floppy airway and toward impaired respiratory drive or impaired brainstem control. In Chiari I, cerebellar tonsillar descent can crowd the posterior fossa and affect medullary respiratory centers. That can produce a central hypoventilation pattern, sometimes clinically resembling acquired Ondine’s curse.

And in that setting, a “mild” home-style sleep study can be falsely calming.

## Why the absence of EEG matters so much

Without EEG, you lose several things that matter in suspected central hypoventilation:

- true total sleep time
- sleep stage information
- arousal scoring
- better discrimination between wake breathing and sleep breathing
- more accurate characterization of respiratory instability

That means a type III study may detect desaturation, but miss the full story behind it.

This is especially important when symptoms are intermittent, nocturnal, or brainstem-driven. If the patient has fragmented sleep, frequent awakenings, or prolonged wake during the test, the reported index can look deceptively low.

So when the story and the number don’t match, trust the story.

## Don’t let “mild” override severe physiology

A few practical clinical pearls from this case:

If a patient has unexplained nocturnal choking or desaturation, ask whether the pattern is central, obstructive, or mixed. Equal difficulty with solids and liquids, normal laryngoscopy, and normal endoscopy should push you to think beyond local ENT or GI pathology.

If desaturation is profound but the patient is not dyspneic, think about impaired central perception of respiratory distress, not just “anxiety.”

If there are chronic occipital headaches, especially with a long and confusing diagnostic path, don’t forget the craniocervical junction.

And if the sleep study is type III, remember that it is a screening tool, not the final word, particularly when you suspect central hypoventilation or brainstem pathology.

## Where capnography fits in

Oxygen saturation tells you about oxygenation. It does not tell you whether the patient is retaining carbon dioxide or hypoventilating through the night.

That is where end-tidal or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring becomes valuable. In suspected central hypoventilation, capnography helps identify sustained hypoventilation that may not be obvious on SpO₂ alone, especially if supplemental oxygen is used or if desaturation is intermittent.

For this reason, full attended polysomnography with EEG plus capnography is the better test when the concern is central apnea or hypoventilation rather than straightforward obstructive sleep apnea.

In this case, a type I PSG with EEG, capnography, and video was planned because the type III study was not enough to close the book.

## The bigger lesson: anatomy can trump a number

This patient’s eventual diagnosis came from looking back at an old head CT and recognizing a crowded foramen magnum, then confirming Chiari I on MRI. That’s the clinical lesson here: when physiology is strange, don’t stop at a mildly abnormal sleep study.

A low AHI does not rule out serious sleep-related breathing pathology if:

- the events are mostly central
- the desaturations are deep
- the symptoms are dramatic
- the test lacks EEG
- the anatomy suggests brainstem compression

## Take-home points

- A type III sleep study can underestimate severity because it lacks EEG and may use recording time rather than true sleep time.
- A “mild” AHI is not reassuring when most events are central and the patient has severe desaturations.
- In suspected central hypoventilation, add capnography and get full attended polysomnography if you can.
- Persistent nocturnal choking plus occipital headaches should prompt consideration of craniocervical pathology, including Chiari I.
- Trust the clinical syndrome, not the label on the sleep report.



# The Old CT That Wasn’t Normal: Diagnostic Delay and the Crowded Foramen Magnum

## A “normal” head CT that changed everything

Here’s the part of this case that should make every clinician pause: a head CT done after a motor vehicle accident was read as normal at the time, but 14 years later a careful re-review showed it wasn’t normal at all. It showed a crowded foramen magnum, cerebellar tissue extending down to the level of the opisthion, and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. MRI later confirmed Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm of tonsillar descent.

That missed clue sat there for years while the patient bounced between ENT, GI, pulmonary, and “it’s probably anxiety” pathways for choking, nocturnal episodes, headaches, and unexplained desaturation.

This is the kind of case that reminds us: sometimes the diagnosis is in the old imaging, not in the next test.

## Why this case was so easy to miss

The symptoms did not announce themselves as “Chiari” on day one.

She had:

- recurrent choking with both solids and liquids
- nocturnal choking episodes several times per week
- chronic occipital/vertex headaches
- unexplained oxygen desaturations into the 70%–80% range without obvious distress
- normal laryngoscopies, endoscopies, chest imaging, pulmonary function tests, and routine labs

That’s exactly how diagnostic delay happens: each specialty sees a normal test in its own lane, but no one is forced to reconcile the whole pattern.

The pattern that should make you uncomfortable is not one symptom. It’s the combination:

- bulbar symptoms without a structural GI/ENT explanation
- chronic occipital headache
- sleep-related breathing events
- unexplained hypoxemia
- a history that suggests possible brainstem or craniocervical junction involvement

## What the old CT was really telling us

CT is not the gold standard for Chiari I. MRI is.

But CT can still whisper the diagnosis if you know what to look for.

In this case, the old CT showed:

- a crowded foramen magnum
- cerebellar tissue low at the craniocervical junction
- loss of the normal CSF buffer space there

That’s the practical lesson: if the posterior fossa looks tight on CT, especially in a patient with compatible symptoms, don’t dismiss it just because the scan was obtained for trauma or was initially read as “no acute findings.”

A “normal” CT read years ago may simply mean “no bleed, no fracture, no acute emergency.” It does not mean “the craniocervical junction was carefully interrogated for Chiari.”

## When to re-open the image stack

This case is a great example of when prior imaging deserves a second look:

- symptoms persist despite negative workup
- multiple specialties have evaluated the patient without a unifying diagnosis
- the symptom pattern suggests neurogenic dysphagia or central sleep-disordered breathing
- there are posterior fossa clues, especially occipital headache or choking with both solids and liquids

If you’re worried, ask for a targeted re-review of the old study with a specific question:  
**Is there craniocervical junction crowding or tonsillar ectopia?**

That question changes what the radiologist looks at.

## The cognitive traps here are familiar

This case had all the ingredients for anchoring and specialty siloing:

- **Anchoring on the first label**: nummular headache, anxiety, reflux, and “normal” evaluations all became competing explanations.
- **Diagnostic momentum**: once a test is called normal, it tends to stay normal in everyone’s mind.
- **Siloed thinking**: choking gets sent to ENT or GI; desaturation to pulmonary; headache to neurology. But the diagnosis lived at the intersection of all four.
- **False reassurance from normal tests**: repeated normal laryngoscopy and endoscopy do not exclude a neurologic cause of choking.

## Practical CT clues that should raise suspicion for Chiari I

On CT, think about Chiari I when you see:

- a crowded or tight foramen magnum
- effacement/loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction
- low-lying cerebellar tissue at the foramen magnum
- an anatomic impression that there is simply “not enough room” at the skull base

CT won’t confirm the diagnosis, but it can absolutely raise the flag that should trigger MRI.

## A few bedside pearls from this case

The symptoms also teach us a lot.

Equal difficulty with solids and liquids is a red flag for neurogenic dysphagia, not a simple mechanical obstruction. Add nocturnal choking and unexplained desaturation, and the differential shifts away from reflux alone.

The sleep study in this case showed only mild sleep apnea by AHI, but it was mostly central events and was performed as a type III study without EEG. That matters, because type III studies can underestimate event burden. The physiology here still strongly suggested central hypoventilation from brainstem compression, even before full polysomnography.

And one more subtle point: the patient had severe desaturation without much dyspnea. That fits a brainstem/interoceptive problem better than a typical “air hunger” complaint.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that diagnostic delay is sometimes caused by a single missed radiologic clue hiding in plain sight.

The patient didn’t need a brand-new diagnosis as much as she needed someone to re-examine the old one with the right question.

## Take-home points

- A prior “normal” head CT is not truly normal if the craniocervical junction was never scrutinized.
- In a patient with occipital headache, choking/dysphagia, and unexplained nocturnal desaturation, think beyond ENT/GI/pulmonary causes.
- CT can suggest Chiari I when it shows foramen magnum crowding or loss of CSF space, but MRI is the confirmatory study.
- Re-review old imaging when the story doesn’t fit the negative workup.
- Specialty siloing is a major cause of delay when a brainstem problem presents like an airway, swallowing, or sleep disorder.



# Chiari I Is Not a Number: Incidental Tonsillar Ectopia vs Symptomatic Chiari Syndrome

## The MRI finding that wasn’t “just incidental”

A woman in her early 40s spent 14 years being evaluated for headaches, choking, and unexplained desaturation before anyone put the pieces together. She had normal laryngoscopies. Normal endoscopies. Normal pulmonary testing. Even a historical head CT from after a motor vehicle accident had been read as normal.

The clue was hiding in plain sight: a crowded foramen magnum, later confirmed on MRI as Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm of tonsillar descent. Once the anatomy was recognized, the symptoms suddenly made sense: chronic occipital/vertex pain, recurrent choking with solids and liquids, nocturnal choking, and episodes of severe hypoxemia with predominantly central events on sleep testing.

That’s the core lesson here: **Chiari I is not a diagnosis you make by millimeters alone**.

## Tonsillar descent is a finding; Chiari syndrome is a clinical problem

Chiari I is commonly defined radiographically as cerebellar tonsillar descent of at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum. But that threshold is a starting point, not a verdict. In the modern MRI era, tonsillar ectopia is not rare, and not every patient with descent has symptomatic disease.

The important distinction is between:

- **Incidental tonsillar ectopia**: an anatomic imaging finding without a compatible clinical syndrome
- **Symptomatic Chiari syndrome**: a pattern of symptoms and/or neurologic dysfunction attributable to crowding at the craniocervical junction and brainstem compromise

That difference matters, because surgery is not justified by a number in isolation. It’s justified when anatomy and physiology line up.

## What made this case feel like “real” Chiari disease?

This patient had several features that should make anyone think beyond an incidental MRI finding.

### 1) Chronic occipital headaches
Chiari headaches are often suboccipital, but this case is a reminder that the phenotype can be messy. She had 14 years of persistent occipital/vertex pain, with scalp allodynia so marked that even hair weight hurt. The headaches weren’t classic cough/Valsalva headaches, which likely contributed to diagnostic drift, but the location and chronicity still fit the broader Chiari spectrum.

### 2) Bulbar symptoms without a structural ENT explanation
She had 7 years of recurrent choking on both solids and liquids, plus nocturnal choking episodes. Multiple laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies were normal.

That “equal difficulty with solids and liquids” is a clue. Mechanical obstruction usually doesn’t treat both equally. When endoscopy is unrevealing, think neurogenic dysphagia and remember that lower cranial nerve dysfunction can be part of Chiari syndrome.

### 3) Respiratory dysfunction that looked neurologic, not pulmonary
The most striking part of the case may be the home oximetry and the airport episode: severe desaturation into the 70%–80% range without overt respiratory distress. She could look surprisingly comfortable while objectively hypoxemic.

That pattern should raise concern for impaired automatic breathing, especially when pulmonary workup is unrevealing. In this case, sleep testing showed mild sleep apnea by AHI, but the majority of events were central. The authors appropriately note a key limitation: the study was type III, without EEG, so the AHI likely underestimated true severity.

## Why millimeters don’t tell the whole story

This is where Chiari gets tricky. A person can have measurable tonsillar descent and be asymptomatic. Another patient can have lesser descent but clear symptoms from crowding, CSF flow obstruction, or brainstem distortion.

So what should drive referral and concern?

Not just the measurement. Look for the syndrome:

- **Symptoms**: occipital headache, choking, dysphagia, sleep-disordered breathing, dizziness, imbalance
- **Anatomic crowding**: loss of CSF space at the foramen magnum, tight posterior fossa, cervicomedullary compression
- **Physiologic impact**: central apneas, hypoventilation, unexplained desaturation
- **Objective neurologic findings**: lower cranial nerve dysfunction, syrinx, brainstem signs

In this case, the re-review of the old CT was pivotal. The original report missed the crowded foramen magnum; retrospectively, the anatomy had been there all along.

## Respiratory symptoms are a real Chiari clue

One of the most useful things to remember from this case is that Chiari can present as a respiratory disorder.

In adults, sleep-disordered breathing is not rare in Chiari I, and central events can occur when the medulla or respiratory control pathways are affected. The clinical picture may look like:

- nocturnal choking or gasping
- central apneas on sleep study
- episodic hypoxemia out of proportion to pulmonary findings
- preserved voluntary breathing but impaired automatic breathing

That last point is especially important. Patients may look “fine” when awake, then deteriorate during sleep or distraction. The case’s airport event is a vivid example: once oxygen was given, symptoms resolved quickly, and the episode looked less like anxiety and more like transient failure of automatic respiratory control.

This is the kind of presentation that can be mislabeled for years unless someone keeps a structural neurologic cause on the table.

## Don’t overcall the MRI, but don’t undercall the syndrome

The modern tension in Chiari care is real. Overdiagnosis is possible if every 5-mm descent becomes a neurosurgical referral. Underdiagnosis is equally dangerous if symptomatic patients are dismissed because the MRI “isn’t that impressive.”

The safest approach is to ask:

- Does the symptom pattern fit Chiari?
- Is there crowding at the craniocervical junction?
- Is there evidence of brainstem or lower cranial nerve dysfunction?
- Is there sleep-related breathing abnormality, especially central events?
- Is there another explanation that truly accounts for everything?

In this patient, the answer to the last question was no. That’s why the Chiari diagnosis became unifying.

## Practical takeaways for bedside and clinic

A few clinical pearls from this case:

- **Do not equate tonsillar descent with disease.** Chiari I is a radiographic descriptor; Chiari syndrome is a clinical diagnosis.
- **Choking with both solids and liquids should make you think neurogenic dysphagia**, especially if ENT/GI workups are unrevealing.
- **Unexplained nocturnal desaturation or central apneas deserve a neurologic differential**, not just a pulmonary one.
- **Re-review old imaging when the story doesn’t fit.** A “normal” CT report can hide craniocervical crowding.
- **Type III sleep studies can underestimate severity** because they lack EEG and may dilute the AHI by using recording time instead of true sleep time.
- **Symptoms and functional impact matter as much as millimeters.** The question is not “How far did the tonsils descend?” but “What is the patient’s brainstem doing?”

## Bottom line

Chiari I is not a number. It’s a clinical syndrome in the right patient with the right symptoms, the right anatomy, and the right physiologic consequences.

This case is a reminder that when occipital headache, dysphagia/choking, and unexplained hypoxemia show up together, the foramen magnum deserves a second look.

## Take-home points

- **Incidental tonsillar ectopia is common; symptomatic Chiari syndrome is not.**
- **Crowding at the foramen magnum and brainstem-related symptoms should outweigh millimeters alone.**
- **Choking/dysphagia, especially with normal ENT/GI workup, can be neurologic.**
- **Central sleep-disordered breathing or unexplained desaturation should prompt consideration of Chiari.**
- **If the story fits, re-review imaging and think beyond the original report.**



# Did Trauma Cause It or Unmask It? Post-Traumatic Chiari Phenotypes

## The case that looked like “everything else”
This is the kind of patient that gets labeled early and then carried, problem by problem, through years of separate workups.

A woman in her early 40s had 7 years of recurrent choking episodes with both solids and liquids, frequent nocturnal choking, and 14 years of occipital/vertex headache after a minor motor vehicle accident. ENT and GI evaluations were unrevealing. Her home pulse oximeter later showed repeated silent desaturations into the 70s and 80s, and an airport event finally documented an SpO₂ of 78% with rapid improvement on supplemental oxygen. Chest imaging, pulmonary function testing, and broad lab work were normal.

The unifying diagnosis only emerged when an old head CT was re-reviewed: a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. MRI confirmed Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent. Sleep testing showed mild sleep apnea overall, but most events were central.

The surprise here isn’t just that Chiari was missed. It’s that the case was hiding in plain sight behind a post-traumatic headache story.

## Did trauma cause the Chiari, or just reveal it?
That question is clinically important — and often unanswerable.

Chiari I can be congenital, but contemporary classifications also recognize secondary or acquired forms, including those related to trauma or altered intracranial pressure. In this case, the first CT was done after the accident and already showed a crowded foramen magnum, but there was no pre-trauma imaging. So we cannot prove whether the accident caused the tonsillar descent, worsened a pre-existing anatomic crowding, or simply brought symptoms to attention.

That uncertainty matters less at the bedside than the pattern recognition:
- symptoms began after trauma
- they progressed over years
- they involved headache plus bulbar/respiratory complaints
- routine airway, GI, and pulmonary workups were normal

That combination should push you away from a simple “post-traumatic headache” frame and toward a posterior fossa problem.

## Why the headache story was such a trap
This patient’s pain history had multiple features that could pull clinicians in the wrong direction: persistent occipital and vertex pain, localized scalp allodynia, and relief after cutting her hair short. She was even diagnosed with nummular headache and treated with local injections.

That is a classic cognitive trap. Focal scalp pain after trauma is easy to anchor on as a primary headache syndrome or local neuralgia. But Chiari-related pain is not always textbook cough headache. Yes, the classic teaching is Valsalva-provoked occipital pain. But absence of that feature does not exclude Chiari, especially when there are other posterior fossa clues.

The scalp allodynia is interesting here. It may reflect occipital neuralgia-like symptoms, a separate primary headache disorder, or some poorly defined Chiari-associated pain phenotype. The key point is not to over-interpret it. Hair sensitivity does not make the diagnosis benign.

## The bigger red flag wasn’t the headache — it was the choking
The most diagnostic part of the story may be the least glamorous: recurrent choking with solids and liquids, plus nocturnal choking, plus unexplained hypoxemia.

That is not typical reflux until proven otherwise.

Normal laryngoscopy and endoscopy do not rule out neurogenic dysphagia. In this case, the swallowing symptoms affected solids and liquids equally, which should raise concern for a neuromuscular or central process rather than a mechanical obstruction. In Chiari I, lower cranial nerve involvement can produce oropharyngeal dysfunction, and the brainstem itself may be involved in respiratory control.

The sleep story is also telling. She had repeated desaturations without distress, and the sleep study showed predominantly central events. A type III study can underestimate severity because it lacks EEG and therefore cannot measure true sleep time. So the reported AHI of 6.0 may have underestimated the real burden of sleep-disordered breathing.

Put bluntly: if a patient with chronic occipital headache is also choking at night and silently desaturating, stop thinking in organ systems and start thinking at the craniocervical junction.

## What this case teaches about “post-traumatic” symptoms
Minor trauma is often blamed for persistent headache, dizziness, or throat symptoms. Sometimes that’s correct. Sometimes trauma is just the event that reveals a vulnerable anatomy.

This case argues for a practical middle ground:

- trauma may cause secondary Chiari in some patients
- trauma may worsen CSF flow dynamics across a crowded foramen magnum
- trauma may simply unmask symptoms from a previously silent Chiari I
- and sometimes the trauma is a red herring that delays recognition of the real lesion

The mistake is not choosing the wrong theory of causation. The mistake is stopping the workup once the story sounds “post-traumatic.”

## Practical bedside takeaways
If you see a patient with chronic headache after trauma, ask yourself whether the picture is really headache-only. Chiari should move up the list when any of the following are present:
- occipital or vertex pain that keeps evolving
- scalp allodynia or “odd” head pain that resists simple headache labels
- dysphagia or choking, especially with both solids and liquids
- nocturnal choking, unexplained desaturation, or central events on sleep testing
- normal ENT/GI/pulmonary workup despite persistent symptoms

MRI of the brain and cervical spine is the right test. If an old CT already exists, re-review it — especially for a crowded foramen magnum or loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. That historical image may be the clue everyone missed.

## Take-home points
- Trauma does not automatically explain persistent headache, choking, or nocturnal desaturation.
- Chiari I may be congenital, acquired, or unmasked by trauma — and you often cannot prove which.
- Focal scalp pain and allodynia can misdirect clinicians toward primary headache syndromes.
- Dysphagia/choking with solids and liquids, plus silent desaturation, should trigger a posterior fossa/brainstem differential.
- Normal laryngoscopy, endoscopy, chest imaging, and PFTs do not exclude neurogenic swallowing or central hypoventilation.
- A crowded foramen magnum on old imaging is not a trivial incidental finding when the symptom story fits.
