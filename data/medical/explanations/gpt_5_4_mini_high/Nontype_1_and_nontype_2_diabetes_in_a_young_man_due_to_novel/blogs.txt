# Young Adult, Big A1c, Negative Antibodies: When to Stop Calling It Type 1 or Type 2

## Why this case is worth a second look

A young adult shows up on routine screening with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. He’s not in DKA, he’s not obese, he has no acanthosis nigricans, and his family history is loaded with early-onset diabetes across multiple generations. That’s the kind of case that should make you uncomfortable with the usual reflex labels.

This is exactly where MODY lives: in the gap between “too young for type 2” and “doesn’t look like classic type 1.” In this case, the eventual diagnosis was HNF1A-MODY, confirmed by a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant, p.N270S (c.809A>G). The big lesson isn’t just genetic trivia. It’s that the right diagnosis changes treatment, prognostication, and family counseling.

## The bedside clues that should trigger a MODY workup

The phenotype here is the key. He had marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2-hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L), but no diabetic ketoacidosis. He also reported polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss — symptoms that can overlap with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, which is why these cases get mislabeled so often.

But the pattern was not typical for either.

He had:
- a strong autosomal dominant family history: maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at 20
- negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A)
- preserved endogenous insulin secretion on glucagon stimulation testing
- glycosuria
- no acanthosis nigricans
- BMI 24.6 kg/m², so not obese, though mild metabolic noise was present with high triglycerides and low HDL

That combination should move MODY way up the list.

A useful pearl: glycosuria is not just “diabetes showing up in the urine.” In HNF1A-MODY, the renal threshold for glucose is reduced because HNF1A affects renal glucose handling, including SGLT2 expression. So glycosuria can be an early clue, not just a consequence of severe hyperglycaemia.

## How to approach suspected MODY at the bedside

The practical approach is simple: don’t let the patient get trapped in the type 1/type 2 binary before you’ve asked the right questions.

Start with the phenotype:
- young onset, especially before 25
- multigenerational diabetes with an autosomal dominant pattern
- absence of ketosis or DKA
- lack of strong insulin resistance features
- postprandial hyperglycaemia and/or glycosuria
- negative islet antibodies

Then check physiology:
- autoantibodies: GADA, IA-2A, and ideally ZnT8 if available
- C-peptide, ideally interpreted in context and sometimes repeated over time

This case is a good reminder that C-peptide is helpful, but not absolute. A preserved C-peptide argues against absolute insulin deficiency, but early autoimmune diabetes can still have measurable secretion. So the question is not “Does one test prove MODY?” It’s “Do the clinical pieces fit well enough to send genetics?”

If the answer is yes, send molecular testing. NGS panels have made this far more feasible. If you want a decision aid, the Exeter MODY calculator can help, but remember its performance was derived largely in White European populations and may not translate perfectly across ancestries. In this case, the calculator estimated a substantial probability of MODY, which supported moving forward.

## Why HNF1A-MODY matters clinically

HNF1A is not just a gene on a report. It’s a transcription factor expressed in the pancreas, liver, and kidney, and mutations cause progressive beta-cell dysfunction. That means the diabetes often starts subtly and worsens over time.

Treatment matters because HNF1A-MODY is not just “type 2 diabetes in a thin person.” Classic teaching says these patients are particularly sensitive to sulfonylureas. That said, this case shows a real-world alternative: after genetic confirmation, insulin was discontinued and replaced with dulaglutide, with metformin continued. HbA1c improved from 10.2% to 6.0% over follow-up, and control remained good on maintenance therapy.

That’s the practical takeaway: once you know the genotype, you can stop treating the phenotype blindly.

A few treatment pearls:
- sulfonylureas are still the classic first-line option in HNF1A-MODY
- GLP-1 receptor agonists may be a useful alternative when hypoglycaemia risk or beta-cell decline makes sulfonylureas less attractive
- SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution in HNF1A-MODY, given the already low renal glucose threshold and the concern for dehydration and ketoacidosis described in the literature
- insulin may be needed temporarily, but it should not be the default endpoint if the diagnosis turns out to be monogenic diabetes

The other big win is family medicine, not just glucose control. A molecular diagnosis gives you a chance to screen relatives, explain the inheritance pattern, and avoid years of misclassification.

## The practical lesson

This case should change how you think about “young adult diabetes.”

If the story is autosomal dominant, antibody-negative, non-ketotic, and C-peptide-positive, stop forcing the patient into type 1 or type 2. Think MODY early, send the genetics, and let the result guide therapy.

## Take-home points

- Young-onset diabetes is not automatically type 1 or type 2.
- Three-generation early-onset diabetes is a huge MODY clue.
- Negative islet antibodies plus preserved C-peptide should push you away from absolute insulin deficiency.
- Glycosuria and postprandial hyperglycaemia are classic HNF1A-MODY clues.
- Genetic testing changes treatment, family counseling, and long-term planning.
- A novel HNF1A variant can still be clinically actionable when the phenotype and segregation fit.



# The Type 2 Look-Alike Trap: Why BMI, Triglycerides and Mild Metabolic Features Don’t Exclude MODY

## The case that should make you pause

A young adult shows up for a routine health exam and gets flagged for hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. Nothing dramatic on exam: BMI 24.6 kg/m², blood pressure normal, no acanthosis nigricans, no ketoacidosis. If you stopped there, this could easily get filed under “early type 2 diabetes” and never looked at again.

But the details matter.

He had a strong autosomal-dominant-looking family history of early diabetes: maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at 20. His HbA1c was 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and 2-hour OGTT glucose 19.8 mmol/L. He also had glycosuria and classic osmotic symptoms: thirst, polyphagia, and weight loss. Autoantibodies were negative. Glucagon-stimulated C-peptide was still present, but not robust. Genetic testing ultimately found a novel heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variant, confirming HNF1A-MODY.

That’s the trap: the patient didn’t look “classic MODY” enough to be obvious, but he also didn’t really fit type 2 diabetes cleanly. He sat in the growing overlap zone where diagnostic anchoring can send you in the wrong direction.

## Why this matters: MODY is losing its “thin, perfect phenotype” stereotype

We’ve traditionally been taught to think of MODY as a lean young person with diabetes plus a striking multigenerational family history. Helpful? Sure. Complete? Not even close.

As obesity and metabolic syndrome become more common in younger people, the phenotype of MODY is getting noisier. A mildly elevated BMI, higher triglycerides, or even a general “metabolic syndrome vibe” no longer safely excludes monogenic diabetes. That’s exactly why HNF1A-MODY gets missed: the clinical picture can overlap with type 2 diabetes enough to feel familiar, but the underlying biology is different enough that treatment, prognosis, and family counseling all change.

This patient had triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L and HDL 1.01 mmol/L, which could easily nudge clinicians toward a type 2 narrative. But the family history and negative autoimmunity should keep MODY on the table.

## What HNF1A-MODY looks like when it doesn’t read the textbook

HNF1A-MODY is the most common MODY subtype. It’s caused by mutations in a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. The result is progressive beta-cell dysfunction and impaired insulin secretion.

A few features matter clinically:

- Hyperglycaemia often appears in adolescence or early adulthood
- Glycosuria is common and can be striking
- Ketoacidosis is usually absent at presentation
- Family history is often autosomal dominant
- Autoantibodies are usually negative
- C-peptide may still be present early on, but beta-cell function declines over time

This case had a particularly useful clue: glycosuria. In HNF1A-MODY, reduced renal threshold for glucose can lead to urinary glucose loss even when the rest of the picture is still evolving. That’s a clue worth noticing, especially in a young person without clear insulin resistance features.

## When the workup starts to point away from type 1 and type 2

The diagnostic approach here was sensible and very FOAM-worthy: don’t overcommit early, but don’t ignore the pattern.

This patient had:

- Negative GADA and IA-2A
- Preserved but inadequate glucagon-stimulated C-peptide
- Strong family history of early-onset diabetes
- No DKA
- Glycosuria
- Young age at presentation

That combination should trigger consideration of MODY.

The glucagon stimulation test is useful as a support tool, not a standalone answer. A stimulated C-peptide above roughly 0.60 nmol/L suggests preserved endogenous insulin secretion, but interpretation depends on context. In a young person with negative antibodies and a dominant family history, “some insulin production” does not equal type 2 diabetes.

One practical point: MODY probability calculators can help, but they’re not gospel. In this report, the Exeter calculator suggested a substantial probability of MODY, but these tools were derived largely from White European populations and may not generalize perfectly across ethnic groups.

## The genetics mattered because the phenotype was not enough

One month after diagnosis, NGS identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant: p.N270S (c.809A>G), in exon 4. It wasn’t in ClinVar or VarSome, but the authors classified it as pathogenic/likely pathogenic using ACMG criteria, supported by:

- Location in the DNA-binding domain
- High AlphaMissense score
- Segregation with diabetes in the family
- Biological plausibility

This is the key precision-medicine point: the diagnosis was not made because the phenotype was textbook. It was made because the phenotype triggered testing, and the testing clarified the biology.

## Treatment: why the correct diagnosis changes what you do

He was initially treated like early type 2 diabetes: basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin. That’s understandable given the degree of hyperglycaemia at presentation.

But once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed, insulin was stopped and therapy shifted to dulaglutide with metformin. HbA1c improved from 10.2% to 6.0% over 6 months, and glycaemic control stayed under 6.5% on follow-up. The case supports a few practical treatment lessons:

In HNF1A-MODY, sulfonylureas are often first-line because these patients tend to be quite sensitive to insulin secretagogues. In this report, however, a GLP-1 receptor agonist was used successfully, which is especially relevant when beta-cell function is already declining or when hypoglycaemia risk is a concern.

This is also a reminder that getting the diagnosis right can prevent unnecessary long-term insulin use. That’s not just about convenience — it affects quality of life, hypoglycaemia risk, and cost.

## Don’t let metabolic features prematurely close the differential

This is the practical takeaway from the case: a mildly elevated BMI and dyslipidaemia do not rule out MODY. They may simply be background noise.

What should keep you honest?

- Diabetes diagnosed young
- Family history spanning multiple generations
- Negative islet autoantibodies
- Glycosuria
- C-peptide that is present but not robust
- No convincing features of marked insulin resistance
- A course that doesn’t fit the expected type 1 or type 2 story

In other words, don’t let triglycerides and BMI do your thinking for you.

## A useful mental model

When a young person presents with diabetes, ask yourself three questions:

1. **Is there evidence of autoimmunity?**  
   If yes, type 1 rises. If no, keep moving.

2. **Is there evidence of insulin resistance severe enough to explain it?**  
   A BMI of 24.6 and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L may raise suspicion for type 2, but they’re not decisive.

3. **Does the family history look dominant rather than simply “lots of diabetes in the family”?**  
   Early-onset diabetes across multiple generations should make MODY hard to ignore.

If the answer set is “no autoimmunity, not much insulin resistance, strong family history,” you should be thinking genetic testing.

## Take-home points

- **MODY can look like type 2 diabetes**, especially as obesity and dyslipidaemia become more common in younger patients.
- **BMI and triglycerides are not exclusion criteria** for MODY.
- **Negative autoantibodies plus preserved C-peptide plus a strong family history** should prompt consideration of monogenic diabetes.
- **Glycosuria in a young patient is a clue**, particularly in HNF1A-MODY.
- **Genetic confirmation changes management** and opens the door to family screening.
- **Precision matters**: this patient’s novel HNF1A variant clarified the diagnosis and supported a successful treatment change.

This case is a good reminder that diabetes phenotypes are getting messier, not cleaner. The job at the bedside is to resist premature closure — especially when the story is trying to tell you “type 2” before the biology has had its say.



# Precision Diabetes in Real Time: How a Genetic Diagnosis Changed Therapy

## The case that should make you stop and think

A young adult shows up on a routine exam with hyperglycemia and glycosuria. No DKA. No acanthosis. BMI 24.6 kg/m². Family history lights up across generations: grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at 20.

That’s not the usual “new type 2 diabetes” story.

He had symptomatic hyperglycemia with HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and a 2-hour OGTT of 19.8 mmol/L. Autoantibodies were negative. C-peptide was not absent, but it wasn’t reassuringly robust either. Genetic testing later identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant, consistent with HNF1A-MODY.

And that changed the treatment story in a big way: insulin was stopped, dulaglutide was started, and his HbA1c fell to 6.0%.

That’s the clinical lesson here. Precision medicine is useful when it changes the next prescription.

## Why this looked like diabetes, but not quite the usual kind

MODY sits in the awkward middle ground between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. It’s monogenic, autosomal dominant, and caused by a primary beta-cell defect. HNF1A-MODY is one of the commonest forms, and it often presents in adolescence or young adulthood.

The clues in this case were classic enough to raise suspicion, but not so classic that the diagnosis was obvious:

- early-onset diabetes in multiple relatives
- glycosuria
- negative islet autoantibodies
- preserved but insufficient endogenous insulin secretion
- no DKA
- no strong insulin-resistant phenotype

The glycosuria matters. In HNF1A-MODY, the kidney’s glucose threshold is lowered because HNF1A regulates renal glucose handling, including SGLT2 expression. So glycosuria can show up early and sometimes seems out of proportion to the clinical picture.

One useful pearl: HNF1A-MODY doesn’t always come wrapped in the stereotype of a very lean, perfectly “non–type 2” patient. This patient had a BMI in the normal-to-overweight range and triglycerides that could easily pull a clinician toward type 2 diabetes. That’s exactly why pattern recognition has to beat stereotype recognition.

## What the bedside workup is trying to answer

The practical question is not “Does this patient have a rare gene mutation?”

It’s: **Is this really autoimmune beta-cell destruction, insulin resistance, or an inherited beta-cell defect that will respond better to another therapy?**

In this case, several bedside and lab features pointed away from classic type 1 diabetes:

- negative GADA and IA-2A
- no ketoacidosis
- stimulated C-peptide still present at 0.83 nmol/L
- family history across generations

The glucagon stimulation test is helpful here because it tells you whether there’s still endogenous insulin production. A stimulated C-peptide above about 0.60 nmol/L generally suggests meaningful residual secretion. That said, it’s supportive, not definitive. You still need the phenotype, antibody testing, and—when suspicion is high—genetic testing.

Also worth remembering: a normal C-peptide does not permanently exclude type 1 diabetes early on. So serial context matters. But in a patient like this, the combination of family history, negative antibodies, and preserved C-peptide should move MODY much higher on the differential.

## Why the diagnosis changes treatment

This is where HNF1A-MODY really matters at the bedside.

HNF1A-MODY is not just a diagnostic label; it predicts drug response.

Historically, **low-dose sulfonylureas** are considered first-line because these patients still have beta cells that can be stimulated. The defect is not absence of beta cells—it’s impaired transcriptional programming and progressive beta-cell dysfunction. Sulfonylureas can “push” insulin secretion effectively.

But there are tradeoffs:

- hypoglycemia risk can be significant
- beta-cell function tends to decline over time
- some patients may need alternative or adjunctive agents

In this case, after genetic confirmation and early improvement in glucose control, insulin was stopped and dulaglutide was started. That’s a very reasonable precision-medicine move. GLP-1 receptor agonists can improve glycemic control without the same hypoglycemia burden as sulfonylureas, and the literature supports their use as an alternative in HNF1A-MODY.

The case report notes a small randomized crossover trial in HNF1A-MODY where liraglutide and glimepiride both improved glycemia, with less mild hypoglycemia on the GLP-1 receptor agonist. So while sulfonylureas remain the classic answer, GLP-1 therapy is a legitimate modern option—especially when you want a safer fit for someone with residual but limited beta-cell function.

This patient’s course shows the practical point: once the diagnosis was clarified, the team could stop using insulin as a default and choose therapy that matched the underlying biology.

## What about metformin, DPP-4 inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors?

Metformin was part of his regimen from the start and remained in his long-term maintenance plan, which is sensible if there’s any concomitant insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome physiology. In real life, many patients do not fit a single tidy category.

The case also used saxagliptin after the initial GLP-1 receptor agonist phase. That’s not the classic teaching for HNF1A-MODY, but it reflects the broader point: management can be individualized once you know what you’re treating.

SGLT2 inhibitors are where the biology gets interesting. Since HNF1A affects renal glucose handling and these patients already have glycosuria, adding an SGLT2 inhibitor may be a poor physiologic fit. The case report highlights concerns about dehydration, genital infections, vascular collapse, and diabetic ketoacidosis. In other words: just because an agent lowers glucose doesn’t mean it’s the right tool for this subtype.

## The real takeaway: don’t wait for genetics to think correctly

The most useful part of this case is not the novel variant itself. It’s the bedside consequence of getting the diagnosis right:

- insulin was started when the patient was clearly hyperglycemic
- the phenotype raised suspicion for MODY
- genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY
- therapy was de-escalated and aligned with the underlying disease
- control improved substantially

That is precision medicine done well.

And it’s a good reminder that young-onset diabetes is not a single disease. If you see early diabetes plus a strong autosomal-dominant family history, negative antibodies, glycosuria, and some preserved C-peptide, don’t let the chart settle too quickly on “type 2.” That’s the moment to think MODY and send the genetic test.

## Take-home points

- HNF1A-MODY should be on the differential in young-onset diabetes with dominant family history, glycosuria, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide.
- A glucagon-stimulated C-peptide is supportive, but it’s not the whole diagnosis.
- Confirming HNF1A-MODY can change management: insulin may be temporary, and therapy can be tailored to beta-cell physiology.
- Sulfonylureas remain the classic first-line therapy, but GLP-1 receptor agonists are a reasonable alternative in selected patients.
- Don’t let BMI or mild metabolic syndrome features exclude MODY; real patients are messier than textbook phenotypes.
- SGLT2 inhibitors may be a poor fit in HNF1A-MODY because of the renal glucose-handling defect and glycosuria.

This is what precision medicine should look like at the bedside: not genetic testing for curiosity, but genetic testing that changes the next decision.



# Glycosuria as a Genotype Clue: The Renal Physiology of HNF1A-MODY and Why SGLT2 Inhibitors May Be a Bad Fit

## The case that should make you stop and think

A young man came in for routine health screening and was found to have hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. Not DKA. Not obvious type 1 diabetes. Not a classic insulin-resistant type 2 phenotype either.

He had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans, and a striking family history: diabetes in his maternal grandmother in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. His HbA1c was 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and 2-hour OGTT glucose 19.8 mmol/L. Autoantibodies were negative, and a glucagon stimulation test showed residual but insufficient insulin secretion.

Genetic testing later revealed a novel heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variant, confirming HNF1A-MODY.

That’s the punchline: the urine glucose wasn’t just a consequence of high serum glucose. It was a clue to the genotype.

## Why glycosuria is a bigger deal than it looks

We usually think of glycosuria as “blood sugar got high enough to spill.” That’s true in ordinary diabetes. But in HNF1A-MODY, the kidney has its own piece of the pathology.

HNF1A is expressed in multiple tissues, including the pancreas and kidney. In the proximal tubule, HNF1A helps regulate SGLT2 expression, and when HNF1A is mutated, renal glucose reabsorption is impaired. The result is a reduced renal threshold for glucose: patients may spill glucose earlier and more readily than you’d expect for their measured plasma glucose.

So when a young person with diabetes has glycosuria out of proportion to the usual “type 2” story, think beyond insulin resistance.

This is especially relevant when the rest of the phenotype points away from type 2 diabetes:

- early-onset diabetes across generations
- negative islet autoantibodies
- preserved C-peptide, even if not fully normal
- no acanthosis or marked obesity
- glycosuria on routine urinalysis

That combination should trigger a MODY workup.

## The diagnostic trap: looking like type 2, behaving like monogenic diabetes

This case had a few features that could have anchored the team on type 2 diabetes: hyperglycaemia, elevated triglycerides, and a need for initial insulin because the HbA1c was high. But the family history and antibody/C-peptide pattern told a different story.

A preserved C-peptide is helpful, but it’s not the whole answer. Early type 1 diabetes can still have measurable endogenous insulin production. That’s why the full pattern matters: family history, antibodies, body habitus, glycosuria, and beta-cell reserve all feed the differential.

Once the HNF1A mutation was identified, the management changed. He was transitioned off insulin and treated with dulaglutide plus metformin, with HbA1c falling to 6.0% over follow-up. He has remained under surveillance without microalbuminuria or retinopathy to date.

## Why this matters for prescribing: SGLT2 inhibitors are not a neutral choice here

This is where the renal physiology becomes clinically actionable.

SGLT2 inhibitors work by intentionally increasing urinary glucose loss. That’s useful in many patients with type 2 diabetes, and for some it’s a major win. But in HNF1A-MODY, the baseline physiology is already shifted toward glycosuria because SGLT2 expression is reduced.

So what happens if you add an SGLT2 inhibitor on top of that?

You may be stacking glucosuric mechanisms on top of each other. In a patient who already has limited beta-cell reserve, that can mean:

- more osmotic diuresis
- more volume depletion risk
- more concern for ketosis, including euglycaemic DKA
- uncertain incremental glycaemic benefit relative to the risk

This is not a blanket “never use an SGLT2 inhibitor in any monogenic diabetes” statement. The evidence base is limited. But the physiology is uncomfortable enough that HNF1A-MODY should make you pause before reaching for the class reflexively.

In other words: if the kidney is already behaving like it has partial SGLT2 inhibition built in, adding a drug that does exactly that may be a poor fit.

## What to do instead

For HNF1A-MODY, classic teaching still favors low-dose sulfonylureas because these patients often remain responsive to insulin secretagogues. In this case, the team used a GLP-1 receptor agonist, and the patient improved substantially.

That’s a useful reminder that treatment in MODY is not just “diabetes control.” It’s matching therapy to mechanism:

- HNF1A-MODY = beta-cell dysfunction, not primary insulin resistance
- Sulfonylureas can work very well
- GLP-1 receptor agonists may be a reasonable alternative in selected patients, especially if hypoglycaemia is a concern or beta-cell function is waning
- SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution because the renal phenotype may already be “pre-loaded” toward glucosuria and dehydration

## Clinical pearl

A urine dipstick with glucose in a young patient is not automatically a “type 2 diabetes” finding. In the right context, it’s a genotype clue.

If the patient is young, has autosomal-dominant family history, lacks autoimmunity, and has preserved C-peptide, think MODY before you settle on an ordinary diabetes label.

## Take-home points

- Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is often a clue to a lowered renal glucose threshold, not just severe hyperglycaemia.
- HNF1A affects both beta-cell function and renal glucose handling.
- Negative autoantibodies and preserved C-peptide should push you toward monogenic diabetes when the family history fits.
- SGLT2 inhibitors may be a physiologic mismatch in HNF1A-MODY because they can amplify an already glucosuric, volume-depleting phenotype.
- Genetic diagnosis changes treatment: it can move patients away from insulin and toward therapies that better match the underlying defect.



# From Suspicion to Sequencing: When to Order MODY Testing and How to Interpret a Novel Variant

## The case that should make you pause

This is the kind of diabetes case that slips through the usual type 1 / type 2 binning process.

A young adult showed up for routine screening with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. He wasn’t ketotic, he wasn’t markedly obese, and he didn’t have acanthosis nigricans. His A1c was 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and 2-hour OGTT glucose 19.8 mmol/L. The family history was the real clue: diabetes across three generations, with onset in the 20s and 30s. Autoantibodies were negative, and C-peptide was still present but not robust. Genetic testing later found a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant, c.809A>G (p.N270S), consistent with HNF1A-MODY.

The surprise here isn’t just the diagnosis. It’s how often a case like this gets labeled “type 2” because the patient is not in DKA and still has some endogenous insulin production. That shortcut can delay the right test, the right counseling, and the right treatment.

## When “young diabetes” should trigger MODY thinking

MODY is not rare enough to ignore, and not obvious enough to catch without a deliberate pause. The textbook pattern is autosomal dominant diabetes presenting at a young age, often before 25, with non-autoimmune features and preserved C-peptide. But real life is messier.

In this case, several features pointed toward monogenic diabetes:

- Young age at diagnosis
- Clear multigenerational family history
- No DKA
- Negative GADA and IA-2A
- Preserved, though insufficient, beta-cell function on glucagon stimulation
- Glycosuria out of proportion to the usual “type 2” story

That last clue matters in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A regulates renal glucose handling, and reduced SGLT2 expression lowers the renal threshold for glycosuria. So if you see glycosuria early, especially with a family history that looks autosomal dominant, don’t just assume “early type 2.”

Also worth remembering: BMI and metabolic syndrome do not exclude MODY. This patient’s BMI was 24.6 kg/m² and his triglycerides were elevated, but that did not make this type 2 diabetes. As obesity becomes more common, phenotype alone gets less useful.

## What should you order, and when?

The practical sequence is usually:

1. Confirm diabetes and assess severity.
2. Check islet autoantibodies.
3. Assess endogenous insulin secretion with C-peptide, ideally in a clinically useful context.
4. If the story still doesn’t fit type 1 or type 2, order monogenic diabetes testing.

In a patient like this, an NGS monogenic diabetes panel is often the most efficient test. Why? Because MODY is genetically heterogeneous, with more than 15 genes linked to the phenotype, and the clinical overlap is real. Serial single-gene testing can be slow and expensive, especially when the phenotype is not absolutely classic.

The case also shows a common real-world pattern: you often have to treat hyperglycaemia before the genetics come back. That’s appropriate. The key is to keep the differential open while you stabilize the patient.

A useful reminder from this case: the glucagon stimulation test was supportive, not definitive. A stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L suggests residual endogenous insulin secretion, which argues against classic long-standing type 1 diabetes, but it does not by itself “diagnose” MODY. Use it as one piece of the puzzle.

## The MODY calculator: helpful, but not gospel

Tools like the Exeter MODY probability calculator are useful for triage. They can help identify who should get genetic testing, especially when you’re balancing cost and access.

But they have limits.

This calculator was derived largely from White European populations, so it may not generalize cleanly to other ancestries. In this case, the calculated probability was 62.4%, which is certainly high enough to justify sequencing. Still, the number should support clinical judgment, not replace it.

That’s the key principle: use calculators to sharpen suspicion, not to decide the diagnosis.

## Interpreting a novel variant without overcalling it

Here’s where many clinicians get nervous: the sequencing comes back, and the variant is “novel.” It’s not in ClinVar, not in VarSome, and not in the literature. What now?

First, novel does not mean benign. It just means unreported.

To interpret a variant like HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S), you have to think like a clinician-geneticist and ask a few questions:

- Does the gene match the phenotype?
- Is the variant in a functionally important region?
- Does it segregate with disease in the family?
- Do in silico predictions support functional impact?
- Is the phenotype consistent with what we know about this gene?

In this case, the answer was yes across multiple fronts:

- HNF1A is a well-established MODY gene
- The variant is in the DNA-binding domain
- It lies adjacent to known pathogenic residues
- AlphaMissense scored it highly deleterious
- The family history fit autosomal dominant diabetes
- The clinical phenotype fit HNF1A-MODY

That’s the logic behind ACMG-based interpretation. You do not need a ClinVar entry to call a variant clinically relevant if multiple independent lines of evidence point the same way. Database absence is not a veto.

This is also why segregation data matter so much. If the variant tracks with diabetes across affected relatives, that significantly strengthens the case for pathogenicity or likely pathogenicity. In monogenic diabetes, family studies can be as valuable as any single lab annotation.

## Why the diagnosis changes treatment

The treatment implications are where MODY becomes more than an academic label.

HNF1A-MODY is classically sulfonylurea-responsive because the core problem is impaired insulin secretion rather than insulin resistance. But this case shows that treatment can be individualized beyond the traditional “sulfonylurea only” mindset.

He initially received basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin while the diagnosis was being clarified. After genetic confirmation and improving glycaemia, insulin was stopped and dulaglutide was started. His A1c fell from 10.2% to 6.0%, and he later remained well controlled on metformin plus saxagliptin.

That’s a nice reminder that precision medicine is not just about naming the disease. It’s about using the diagnosis to de-escalate unnecessary insulin, avoid hypoglycaemia, and choose therapy that fits the biology.

A few practical points from this case:

- GLP-1 receptor agonists can be useful in HNF1A-MODY, particularly when beta-cell function is declining and hypoglycaemia is a concern.
- Sulfonylureas remain a classic first-line option, but they are not the only reasonable choice.
- SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution in HNF1A-MODY because of baseline glycosuria and concern for dehydration and ketoacidosis risk.
- Metformin may still have a role, especially if there are features of insulin resistance or mixed physiology.

## The bigger lesson

This case is a good example of why “young diabetes” should not be a reflex diagnosis.

If the story includes early-onset diabetes, autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, and a phenotype that doesn’t quite fit type 1 or type 2, MODY testing is worth pursuing. And if the genetic result is a novel variant, don’t stop at “it’s not in ClinVar.” Use ACMG reasoning, phenotype matching, and segregation to decide whether the variant is clinically meaningful.

## Take-home points

- Young-onset diabetes with a strong family history and negative autoantibodies should trigger MODY thinking early.
- Glycosuria can be a clue in HNF1A-MODY, not just a nonspecific finding.
- BMI and metabolic syndrome features do not rule out MODY.
- MODY probability calculators are useful for triage, but they were built in specific populations and may not generalize well.
- A novel variant is not automatically uncertain or benign; interpretation should incorporate ACMG criteria, segregation, domain location, and phenotype fit.
- Genetic confirmation matters because it can change therapy, reduce unnecessary insulin use, and guide family counseling.
