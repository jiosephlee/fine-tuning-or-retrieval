Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Pathophysiology] How do HNF1A mutations lead to both progressive pancreatic beta-cell dysfunction and glycosuria, and why is postprandial hyperglycaemia often prominent early in the disease?

HNF1A-MODY is fundamentally a **transcriptional disease**. HNF1A encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor-1α, a transcription factor expressed in **pancreatic β cells** and **renal tubular cells**. Heterozygous loss-of-function mutations reduce expression of genes needed for normal glucose sensing and insulin secretion in β cells, including **insulin itself, GLUT2, and enzymes involved in glucose metabolism such as pyruvate kinase**. The result is a **progressive secretory defect**, not abrupt β-cell destruction. That is why patients like the young man in the case can still have measurable C-peptide early on, yet show an **inadequate stimulated insulin response** that worsens over time.

The **glycosuria** is a separate but related manifestation of HNF1A dysfunction in the kidney. HNF1A normally helps regulate **SGLT2** expression in the proximal tubule. When HNF1A function is reduced, the **renal threshold for glucose falls**, so glucose spills into the urine at lower plasma glucose levels than expected. This explains why glycosuria may be an early clue, as in the case report.

**Postprandial hyperglycaemia is often prominent early** because the earliest defect is an impaired ability to mount a **rapid insulin response to a glucose load**. Basal fasting insulin secretion may remain relatively preserved at first, so fasting glucose can be less striking than the **2-hour OGTT glucose**, which was markedly elevated in this patient. Over time, as β-cell dysfunction progresses, fasting hyperglycaemia becomes more evident and the phenotype begins to resemble other forms of diabetes.
