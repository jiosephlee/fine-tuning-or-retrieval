Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 4: ## Genetic Confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and Interpretation of a Novel Variant

In this patient, the diagnosis of MODY was not made purely on clinical grounds; it was confirmed by molecular testing after the phenotype raised suspicion for a monogenic form of diabetes. Genetic testing is most useful when diabetes begins at a young age, runs strongly in successive generations, and is accompanied by features that do not fit classic autoimmune type 1 diabetes or typical insulin-resistant type 2 diabetes. In this case, the patient had early-onset hyperglycaemia, negative islet autoantibodies, measurable endogenous insulin secretion, glycosuria, and a striking maternal family history of early diabetes. These findings made an NGS-based MODY panel an appropriate next step, because MODY is genetically heterogeneous and a multigene panel is more efficient than single-gene testing when the specific subtype is not yet known.

The gene identified here, HNF1A, encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha, a transcription factor expressed in pancreatic β-cells as well as in the liver and kidney. The gene contains 10 exons and the encoded protein has important functional regions involved in DNA binding and transcriptional activation. In HNF1A-MODY, pathogenic variants impair transcriptional regulation of genes needed for β-cell function and glucose homeostasis, leading to progressive insulin secretory failure. The renal manifestation is also clinically relevant: reduced HNF1A activity lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, which helps explain why glycosuria can be an early and prominent clue in these patients.

The variant found in this case was a heterozygous c.809A>G substitution in exon 4, resulting in p.N270S. Its location is important because amino acid 270 lies within the DNA-binding region of HNF1A, where even single-amino-acid changes can alter transcription factor function. A variant in this domain is therefore biologically plausible as disease-causing, especially when the clinical picture fits HNF1A-MODY. The report also notes that the altered residue lies near previously recognized pathogenic residues, adding further support that this position is functionally important.

Interpreting a previously unreported variant requires structured use of ACMG criteria rather than reliance on a single datapoint. In this case, several lines of evidence point toward pathogenic or likely pathogenic significance: the variant was absent from ClinVar, VarSome, and the published literature, which supports rarity; it affected a critical functional domain of the protein, which supports biological plausibility; in silico prediction was strongly unfavorable; and the family history showed a pattern of diabetes across multiple generations that is consistent with autosomal dominant segregation. Together, these features support the conclusion that the variant is clinically meaningful. Importantly, absence from a database does not imply benignity. It often means only that the variant is novel, underreported, or not yet submitted. Many truly disease-causing variants are “private” to a family or population and are recognized only when clinical phenotype and molecular data are considered together.

This case also illustrates the heterogeneity of HNF1A-MODY. Although HNF1A mutations classically cause non-obese, insulin-sensitive diabetes with early glycosuria, real-world presentations can overlap with type 2 diabetes, including higher BMI or metabolic features, as seen here. Genetic confirmation therefore has practical consequences: it strengthens diagnostic confidence, helps guide therapy, and enables cascade testing of relatives who may carry the same autosomal dominant variant. For the family, this allows earlier screening, counselling about inheritance risk, and individualized treatment before complications develop. More broadly, discovery of a novel HNF1A variant expands the mutational spectrum of HNF1A-MODY and supports the role of precision medicine in young-onset diabetes.
