Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 5: ## Precision Management, Therapeutic Transitions, and Long-Term Clinical Lessons

At presentation, the diabetes subtype was still uncertain, so the initial regimen was chosen pragmatically rather than as a disease-specific plan. The patient had marked hyperglycaemia, a very high HbA1c, symptoms of catabolism, and no diabetic ketoacidosis, so basal insulin was reasonable to achieve rapid control and reverse glucotoxicity while the workup was underway. Metformin and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin were added because type 2 diabetes remained in the differential at that point, and these agents can support glycaemic control without immediately committing the patient to a permanent insulin-dependent strategy.

The later diagnostic data shifted the management framework toward HNF1A-MODY. The combination of early-onset diabetes in multiple generations, negative islet autoantibodies, and measurable but insufficient C-peptide suggested that the patient still had residual β-cell function, but not enough to maintain normal glycaemia. That distinction matters because HNF1A-MODY is a progressive secretory defect rather than an autoimmune β-cell destruction syndrome, so treatment should be matched to the amount of remaining endogenous insulin secretion and the risk of hypoglycaemia rather than to a presumed need for lifelong insulin.

In HNF1A-MODY, low-dose sulfonylureas are traditionally considered first-line because these patients are often unusually sensitive to insulin secretagogues. In this case, however, the clinicians chose a GLP-1 receptor agonist after genetic confirmation and early improvement in HbA1c. That decision is clinically sensible: GLP-1 receptor agonists enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, reduce postprandial hyperglycaemia, and generally carry less hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas. They can be especially useful when β-cell reserve is declining or when clinicians want a safer long-term regimen that avoids unnecessary hypoglycaemia.

The therapeutic transition also illustrates why genetic diagnosis changes care. Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed and glycaemic control had begun to improve, insulin was discontinued and dulaglutide was started. The patient’s HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 9.1% and then to 6.0%, after which therapy was simplified to metformin plus saxagliptin for maintenance. This course shows that temporary insulin may be appropriate at diagnosis, but it does not have to become the default long-term treatment when the diabetes is monogenic and β-cell function is still partially preserved. In this patient, the eventual oral regimen was sufficient to maintain HbA1c below 6.5% on follow-up.

Lifestyle measures remain important adjuncts in monogenic diabetes. Dietary counseling and regular physical activity were advised here, but as in most patients with established HNF1A-MODY, lifestyle alone would not be expected to normalize glycaemia. The goal is therefore to combine behavioural measures with pharmacotherapy that fits the underlying molecular defect and the patient’s remaining insulin secretory capacity.

This case also highlights medication safety. Because HNF1A regulates renal glucose handling, glycosuria is part of the phenotype, and SGLT2 inhibitors may pose disproportionate risks in HNF1A-MODY, including dehydration and ketoacidosis. For that reason, the report cautions against routine use of this class. Long-term follow-up should also include surveillance for microvascular complications and for progressive β-cell decline. In this patient, the absence of microalbuminuria and retinopathy is reassuring, but the abnormal sudomotor testing reminds clinicians that neuropathic involvement may already be present and should be monitored over time.

The main lesson is that young adults with atypical diabetes should not be forced into a type 1 or type 2 label when the phenotype does not fit. Strategic testing—autoantibodies, C-peptide, family history, and then genetic analysis—can confirm MODY, prevent unnecessary insulin exposure, reduce medication risk, and allow therapy to be tailored to the molecular diagnosis.
