Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Diagnosis of Early-Onset Nonautoimmune Diabetes

In a young adult with new hyperglycaemia, the initial differential diagnosis is broader than type 2 diabetes alone. Clinicians must consider autoimmune diabetes, including classic type 1 diabetes and slowly progressive adult-onset autoimmune diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and monogenic diabetes such as MODY. Less common secondary causes, such as pancreatitis-related diabetes, endocrinopathies, medication-induced hyperglycaemia, and syndromic or mitochondrial diabetes, should also be kept in mind when the presentation is atypical. In this case, the patient’s age, multigenerational family history, glycosuria, and absence of ketoacidosis quickly raised concern that the usual type 1/type 2 framework might not fully explain the disease.

The absence of diabetic ketoacidosis was an important early clue. DKA does not absolutely exclude type 1 diabetes, but in a newly diagnosed young patient it lowers the likelihood of severe absolute insulin deficiency at presentation. Here, the patient had marked hyperglycaemia and symptoms of diabetes, yet no DKA. That pattern is compatible with MODY, particularly HNF1A-MODY, which often presents with progressive hyperglycaemia rather than abrupt metabolic decompensation. The presence of glycosuria also fits HNF1A-MODY, because HNF1A mutations reduce the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption and can cause urinary glucose loss at blood glucose levels that might not always produce glycosuria in other forms of diabetes.

Testing for islet autoantibodies is a key step in distinguishing autoimmune diabetes from nonautoimmune forms. In practice, glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), IA-2 antibodies, and ideally ZnT8 antibodies are used to increase sensitivity. In this patient, GADA and IA-2A were negative, which supports a nonautoimmune process and therefore increases the pretest probability of MODY. However, negative antibodies do not by themselves prove MODY, because antibody-negative type 1 diabetes exists and antibody results can vary with disease stage and the breadth of the assay panel. For that reason, autoantibody testing should be interpreted together with the clinical phenotype rather than used as a standalone discriminator.

Assessment of endogenous insulin secretion adds another layer of diagnostic information. C-peptide is released in equimolar amounts with endogenous insulin and is therefore a useful marker of residual β-cell function, unlike exogenous insulin. In this case, fasting C-peptide was low-normal, and the glucagon-stimulated value rose to 0.83 nmol/L. A stimulated C-peptide above about 0.6 nmol/L generally suggests preserved endogenous insulin secretion, but the clinical meaning depends on the context. Here, the key point was not that β-cell function was normal, but that it was present yet insufficient for the degree of hyperglycaemia. That pattern argues against absolute insulinopenia typical of established type 1 diabetes and is more compatible with progressive β-cell dysfunction, as seen in HNF1A-MODY or, less specifically, in some patients with type 2 diabetes. The glucagon stimulation test is therefore best understood as supportive evidence: it helps quantify residual reserve, but it cannot definitively classify diabetes type on its own.

The 75 g OGTT was especially informative because it showed severe postprandial hyperglycaemia, a classic feature of HNF1A-MODY. Many patients with HNF1A mutations have disproportionately high post-meal glucose excursions before fasting glucose becomes markedly abnormal. This pattern, together with glycosuria, is a useful clinical clue. At the same time, the patient’s BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and dyslipidaemia could have suggested type 2 diabetes, but modern practice shows that BMI and metabolic syndrome are imperfect exclusion criteria for MODY. Weight status has become less discriminating as obesity becomes more common in the general population, and some patients with MODY may also have coincident insulin resistance or dyslipidaemia. Thus, a borderline or even elevated BMI should not prevent clinicians from considering MODY when other features point in that direction.

Family history was one of the strongest reasons to suspect MODY in this case. Diabetes had occurred across successive generations: the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother were all affected at relatively young ages. Such vertical transmission is characteristic of autosomal dominant inheritance and raises suspicion for MODY more than for sporadic type 2 diabetes. Still, family history alone is not definitive, because type 2 diabetes is common and can cluster in families. The diagnostic value of family history is highest when onset is early, disease appears in multiple generations, and insulin resistance features are absent or mild, all of which applied here.

Clinical prediction tools can help decide who should undergo genetic testing. The Exeter MODY probability calculator is widely used to estimate the likelihood of MODY from clinical variables such as age at diagnosis, BMI, treatment pattern, and family history. In this patient, the calculated probability was 62.4%, strongly supporting further evaluation. However, this tool has limitations: it was derived largely from White European cohorts and may not be perfectly calibrated in Asian populations, so it should guide—not replace—clinical judgment. A high score is most useful when it aligns with the bedside phenotype, as it did in this case.

Once the phenotype suggests MODY and autoimmune diabetes is not supported by antibody testing, molecular genetic testing becomes the decisive step. In this report, the clinicians appropriately moved from syndromic suspicion to next-generation sequencing, which identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant consistent with HNF1A-MODY. This step is important because a genetic diagnosis does not merely label the diabetes subtype; it changes management, informs prognosis, and allows targeted family counselling and cascade testing of relatives.
