Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Monogenic Diabetes in Context: MODY and the Clinical Relevance of HNF1A

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a form of monogenic diabetes caused by a single-gene defect that impairs pancreatic β-cell development or function. Unlike type 1 diabetes, it is not autoimmune, and unlike typical type 2 diabetes, it is not primarily driven by insulin resistance. MODY is classically inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, so diabetes often appears in successive generations at a young age. Although it is uncommon compared with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, it is clinically important because a meaningful fraction of young-onset diabetes cases is due to MODY, and the exact proportion is often underestimated when genetic testing is not routinely performed.

Several genes can cause MODY, including HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, and HNF1B, among others. Among these, HNF1A is one of the most important clinically because it is relatively common and because the diagnosis can directly affect treatment choice. HNF1A-MODY accounts for a substantial share of MODY cases and often presents with a clear family history of early-onset diabetes, as in this patient’s maternal line. Recognizing this subtype matters because patients may respond differently to glucose-lowering therapies than those with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

HNF1A encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha, a transcription factor expressed in pancreatic β-cells as well as in the liver and kidney. In β-cells, HNF1A regulates genes involved in insulin production and glucose sensing. In normal physiology, this transcriptional program supports appropriate insulin secretion in response to rising glucose. When one HNF1A allele carries a pathogenic variant, the result is progressive β-cell dysfunction: insulin secretion becomes insufficient over time, and glucose intolerance worsens gradually. This is why many patients are not diagnosed in childhood but instead are identified in adolescence or early adulthood, sometimes only after routine laboratory testing reveals hyperglycaemia.

The renal phenotype of HNF1A-MODY is also characteristic. HNF1A helps regulate expression of renal glucose transport pathways, including SGLT2-related glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule. When HNF1A function is impaired, the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption is reduced, so glycosuria can occur at plasma glucose levels that might not produce such marked urinary glucose loss in other forms of diabetes. In practice, this can be a useful clue: a patient with unexpected glycosuria, especially when paired with young age and family history, should prompt consideration of MODY. In severe hyperglycaemia, glycosuria may also contribute to osmotic symptoms such as polyuria, polydipsia, and sometimes weight loss.

The typical clinical picture of HNF1A-MODY is therefore one of early-onset diabetes in multiple generations, postprandial hyperglycaemia, and evidence of endogenous insulin production that is still present, at least early in the disease course. Autoimmune markers of type 1 diabetes, such as GAD, IA-2, and ZnT8 antibodies, are usually absent. C-peptide is often preserved initially because β-cells are not destroyed abruptly, although secretion declines progressively. This combination can be diagnostically confusing: the patient may appear “too young” for type 2 diabetes, yet not fit type 1 diabetes because ketoacidosis is absent, autoantibodies are negative, and some insulin secretion remains. That is exactly the diagnostic problem illustrated by this case.

Misclassification is common in routine practice because MODY shares features with both major diabetes types. A young person with marked hyperglycaemia, weight loss, and symptoms of polyuria or polydipsia may be presumed to have type 1 diabetes, while a patient with a mildly elevated body mass index or dyslipidaemia may be labelled as type 2 diabetes. Family history can be helpful, but it is not always obvious, and obesity or metabolic syndrome can coexist and obscure the picture. For this reason, MODY should be considered when young-onset diabetes occurs with autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide, especially if glycosuria seems disproportionate or there is little evidence of insulin resistance.

An accurate diagnosis is not just academic. Identifying HNF1A-MODY informs prognosis, because the natural history is one of progressive β-cell failure rather than autoimmune destruction or primary insulin resistance. It also guides treatment selection, since some therapies are more effective than others in this subtype. In addition, the diagnosis has implications for family counselling, because first-degree relatives may carry the same variant and benefit from targeted testing and early surveillance. In a case like this one, genetic confirmation changes how the diabetes is understood, how it is managed, and how the patient and family are followed over time.

## Recognizing the Clinical Pattern in the Index Patient

The patient came to medical attention not because of an acute diabetic emergency, but because incidental hyperglycaemia and glycosuria were found during a routine annual health examination. This mode of presentation is an important clue in young-onset diabetes: monogenic diabetes, especially MODY, is often detected unexpectedly before a patient has been labelled as having type 1 or type 2 diabetes. In this case, the incidental finding was accompanied by classic hyperglycaemic symptoms—thirst, increased appetite, and recent weight loss—showing that the disorder had already become clinically significant, but without progression to diabetic ketoacidosis. The absence of ketoacidosis is not diagnostic by itself, but it makes classic new-onset type 1 diabetes less likely and suggests that the patient retained enough endogenous insulin to avoid catabolic decompensation.

The physical examination also pointed away from straightforward type 2 diabetes. His BMI was 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure was normal, and there was no acanthosis nigricans, which is a common marker of insulin resistance. A normal body habitus does not exclude type 2 diabetes, but in a young adult it should prompt the clinician to look more carefully for alternative explanations, especially when other insulin-resistance stigmata are absent. The family history was particularly revealing: diabetes had occurred in the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother, all at an early age. This vertical transmission across successive generations, affecting both sexes, is the classic pedigree pattern that should raise suspicion for autosomal dominant inheritance and therefore MODY.

The biochemical profile confirmed unequivocal diabetes. His HbA1c was 10.2%, the fasting glucose was 12.4 mmol/L, and the 2-hour value after a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test was 19.8 mmol/L. These results show sustained hyperglycaemia, not just a transient abnormality. Glycosuria in a young person is especially noteworthy in HNF1A-MODY because HNF1A mutations lower the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, so glucose may appear in the urine disproportionately early. In other words, glycosuria in this setting is not merely a consequence of high plasma glucose; it can be a characteristic clue to HNF1A-related disease. The elevated triglycerides and low HDL could suggest metabolic syndrome at first glance, but dyslipidaemia is also common in uncontrolled diabetes and should not override the broader phenotype. In this patient, the lipid pattern was therefore a potential distractor rather than a definitive argument for type 2 diabetes.

Early complication screening added useful context. No microalbuminuria was detected, indicating that overt diabetic nephropathy was not yet present at diagnosis. By contrast, sudomotor testing showed severely reduced function in the hands, consistent with autonomic small-fibre dysfunction. This does not distinguish MODY from other diabetes types, but it reminds the learner that clinically silent complications can already be present when hyperglycaemia has likely been evolving for some time. The main value of these findings is temporal: they support a diagnosis of established diabetes rather than a fleeting laboratory abnormality.

Taken together, the case had several features arguing against classic type 1 diabetes: no ketoacidosis, preserved if limited endogenous insulin secretion later demonstrated by C-peptide testing, negative islet autoantibodies, and a strong multigenerational family history. It also lacked the usual phenotype of straightforward type 2 diabetes: the patient was not obese, had no acanthosis nigricans, and was relatively young for typical insulin-resistant diabetes. The correct clinical response to this pattern is to recognize that the patient fits a “non-classical” diabetes phenotype and should be evaluated for MODY rather than assuming the common autoimmune or metabolic categories.

## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Diagnosis of Early-Onset Nonautoimmune Diabetes

In a young adult with new hyperglycaemia, the initial differential diagnosis is broader than type 2 diabetes alone. Clinicians must consider autoimmune diabetes, including classic type 1 diabetes and slowly progressive adult-onset autoimmune diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and monogenic diabetes such as MODY. Less common secondary causes, such as pancreatitis-related diabetes, endocrinopathies, medication-induced hyperglycaemia, and syndromic or mitochondrial diabetes, should also be kept in mind when the presentation is atypical. In this case, the patient’s age, multigenerational family history, glycosuria, and absence of ketoacidosis quickly raised concern that the usual type 1/type 2 framework might not fully explain the disease.

The absence of diabetic ketoacidosis was an important early clue. DKA does not absolutely exclude type 1 diabetes, but in a newly diagnosed young patient it lowers the likelihood of severe absolute insulin deficiency at presentation. Here, the patient had marked hyperglycaemia and symptoms of diabetes, yet no DKA. That pattern is compatible with MODY, particularly HNF1A-MODY, which often presents with progressive hyperglycaemia rather than abrupt metabolic decompensation. The presence of glycosuria also fits HNF1A-MODY, because HNF1A mutations reduce the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption and can cause urinary glucose loss at blood glucose levels that might not always produce glycosuria in other forms of diabetes.

Testing for islet autoantibodies is a key step in distinguishing autoimmune diabetes from nonautoimmune forms. In practice, glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), IA-2 antibodies, and ideally ZnT8 antibodies are used to increase sensitivity. In this patient, GADA and IA-2A were negative, which supports a nonautoimmune process and therefore increases the pretest probability of MODY. However, negative antibodies do not by themselves prove MODY, because antibody-negative type 1 diabetes exists and antibody results can vary with disease stage and the breadth of the assay panel. For that reason, autoantibody testing should be interpreted together with the clinical phenotype rather than used as a standalone discriminator.

Assessment of endogenous insulin secretion adds another layer of diagnostic information. C-peptide is released in equimolar amounts with endogenous insulin and is therefore a useful marker of residual β-cell function, unlike exogenous insulin. In this case, fasting C-peptide was low-normal, and the glucagon-stimulated value rose to 0.83 nmol/L. A stimulated C-peptide above about 0.6 nmol/L generally suggests preserved endogenous insulin secretion, but the clinical meaning depends on the context. Here, the key point was not that β-cell function was normal, but that it was present yet insufficient for the degree of hyperglycaemia. That pattern argues against absolute insulinopenia typical of established type 1 diabetes and is more compatible with progressive β-cell dysfunction, as seen in HNF1A-MODY or, less specifically, in some patients with type 2 diabetes. The glucagon stimulation test is therefore best understood as supportive evidence: it helps quantify residual reserve, but it cannot definitively classify diabetes type on its own.

The 75 g OGTT was especially informative because it showed severe postprandial hyperglycaemia, a classic feature of HNF1A-MODY. Many patients with HNF1A mutations have disproportionately high post-meal glucose excursions before fasting glucose becomes markedly abnormal. This pattern, together with glycosuria, is a useful clinical clue. At the same time, the patient’s BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and dyslipidaemia could have suggested type 2 diabetes, but modern practice shows that BMI and metabolic syndrome are imperfect exclusion criteria for MODY. Weight status has become less discriminating as obesity becomes more common in the general population, and some patients with MODY may also have coincident insulin resistance or dyslipidaemia. Thus, a borderline or even elevated BMI should not prevent clinicians from considering MODY when other features point in that direction.

Family history was one of the strongest reasons to suspect MODY in this case. Diabetes had occurred across successive generations: the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother were all affected at relatively young ages. Such vertical transmission is characteristic of autosomal dominant inheritance and raises suspicion for MODY more than for sporadic type 2 diabetes. Still, family history alone is not definitive, because type 2 diabetes is common and can cluster in families. The diagnostic value of family history is highest when onset is early, disease appears in multiple generations, and insulin resistance features are absent or mild, all of which applied here.

Clinical prediction tools can help decide who should undergo genetic testing. The Exeter MODY probability calculator is widely used to estimate the likelihood of MODY from clinical variables such as age at diagnosis, BMI, treatment pattern, and family history. In this patient, the calculated probability was 62.4%, strongly supporting further evaluation. However, this tool has limitations: it was derived largely from White European cohorts and may not be perfectly calibrated in Asian populations, so it should guide—not replace—clinical judgment. A high score is most useful when it aligns with the bedside phenotype, as it did in this case.

Once the phenotype suggests MODY and autoimmune diabetes is not supported by antibody testing, molecular genetic testing becomes the decisive step. In this report, the clinicians appropriately moved from syndromic suspicion to next-generation sequencing, which identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant consistent with HNF1A-MODY. This step is important because a genetic diagnosis does not merely label the diabetes subtype; it changes management, informs prognosis, and allows targeted family counselling and cascade testing of relatives.

## Genetic Confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and Interpretation of a Novel Variant

In this patient, the diagnosis of MODY was not made purely on clinical grounds; it was confirmed by molecular testing after the phenotype raised suspicion for a monogenic form of diabetes. Genetic testing is most useful when diabetes begins at a young age, runs strongly in successive generations, and is accompanied by features that do not fit classic autoimmune type 1 diabetes or typical insulin-resistant type 2 diabetes. In this case, the patient had early-onset hyperglycaemia, negative islet autoantibodies, measurable endogenous insulin secretion, glycosuria, and a striking maternal family history of early diabetes. These findings made an NGS-based MODY panel an appropriate next step, because MODY is genetically heterogeneous and a multigene panel is more efficient than single-gene testing when the specific subtype is not yet known.

The gene identified here, HNF1A, encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha, a transcription factor expressed in pancreatic β-cells as well as in the liver and kidney. The gene contains 10 exons and the encoded protein has important functional regions involved in DNA binding and transcriptional activation. In HNF1A-MODY, pathogenic variants impair transcriptional regulation of genes needed for β-cell function and glucose homeostasis, leading to progressive insulin secretory failure. The renal manifestation is also clinically relevant: reduced HNF1A activity lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, which helps explain why glycosuria can be an early and prominent clue in these patients.

The variant found in this case was a heterozygous c.809A>G substitution in exon 4, resulting in p.N270S. Its location is important because amino acid 270 lies within the DNA-binding region of HNF1A, where even single-amino-acid changes can alter transcription factor function. A variant in this domain is therefore biologically plausible as disease-causing, especially when the clinical picture fits HNF1A-MODY. The report also notes that the altered residue lies near previously recognized pathogenic residues, adding further support that this position is functionally important.

Interpreting a previously unreported variant requires structured use of ACMG criteria rather than reliance on a single datapoint. In this case, several lines of evidence point toward pathogenic or likely pathogenic significance: the variant was absent from ClinVar, VarSome, and the published literature, which supports rarity; it affected a critical functional domain of the protein, which supports biological plausibility; in silico prediction was strongly unfavorable; and the family history showed a pattern of diabetes across multiple generations that is consistent with autosomal dominant segregation. Together, these features support the conclusion that the variant is clinically meaningful. Importantly, absence from a database does not imply benignity. It often means only that the variant is novel, underreported, or not yet submitted. Many truly disease-causing variants are “private” to a family or population and are recognized only when clinical phenotype and molecular data are considered together.

This case also illustrates the heterogeneity of HNF1A-MODY. Although HNF1A mutations classically cause non-obese, insulin-sensitive diabetes with early glycosuria, real-world presentations can overlap with type 2 diabetes, including higher BMI or metabolic features, as seen here. Genetic confirmation therefore has practical consequences: it strengthens diagnostic confidence, helps guide therapy, and enables cascade testing of relatives who may carry the same autosomal dominant variant. For the family, this allows earlier screening, counselling about inheritance risk, and individualized treatment before complications develop. More broadly, discovery of a novel HNF1A variant expands the mutational spectrum of HNF1A-MODY and supports the role of precision medicine in young-onset diabetes.

## Precision Management, Therapeutic Transitions, and Long-Term Clinical Lessons

At presentation, the diabetes subtype was still uncertain, so the initial regimen was chosen pragmatically rather than as a disease-specific plan. The patient had marked hyperglycaemia, a very high HbA1c, symptoms of catabolism, and no diabetic ketoacidosis, so basal insulin was reasonable to achieve rapid control and reverse glucotoxicity while the workup was underway. Metformin and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin were added because type 2 diabetes remained in the differential at that point, and these agents can support glycaemic control without immediately committing the patient to a permanent insulin-dependent strategy.

The later diagnostic data shifted the management framework toward HNF1A-MODY. The combination of early-onset diabetes in multiple generations, negative islet autoantibodies, and measurable but insufficient C-peptide suggested that the patient still had residual β-cell function, but not enough to maintain normal glycaemia. That distinction matters because HNF1A-MODY is a progressive secretory defect rather than an autoimmune β-cell destruction syndrome, so treatment should be matched to the amount of remaining endogenous insulin secretion and the risk of hypoglycaemia rather than to a presumed need for lifelong insulin.

In HNF1A-MODY, low-dose sulfonylureas are traditionally considered first-line because these patients are often unusually sensitive to insulin secretagogues. In this case, however, the clinicians chose a GLP-1 receptor agonist after genetic confirmation and early improvement in HbA1c. That decision is clinically sensible: GLP-1 receptor agonists enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, reduce postprandial hyperglycaemia, and generally carry less hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas. They can be especially useful when β-cell reserve is declining or when clinicians want a safer long-term regimen that avoids unnecessary hypoglycaemia.

The therapeutic transition also illustrates why genetic diagnosis changes care. Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed and glycaemic control had begun to improve, insulin was discontinued and dulaglutide was started. The patient’s HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 9.1% and then to 6.0%, after which therapy was simplified to metformin plus saxagliptin for maintenance. This course shows that temporary insulin may be appropriate at diagnosis, but it does not have to become the default long-term treatment when the diabetes is monogenic and β-cell function is still partially preserved. In this patient, the eventual oral regimen was sufficient to maintain HbA1c below 6.5% on follow-up.

Lifestyle measures remain important adjuncts in monogenic diabetes. Dietary counseling and regular physical activity were advised here, but as in most patients with established HNF1A-MODY, lifestyle alone would not be expected to normalize glycaemia. The goal is therefore to combine behavioural measures with pharmacotherapy that fits the underlying molecular defect and the patient’s remaining insulin secretory capacity.

This case also highlights medication safety. Because HNF1A regulates renal glucose handling, glycosuria is part of the phenotype, and SGLT2 inhibitors may pose disproportionate risks in HNF1A-MODY, including dehydration and ketoacidosis. For that reason, the report cautions against routine use of this class. Long-term follow-up should also include surveillance for microvascular complications and for progressive β-cell decline. In this patient, the absence of microalbuminuria and retinopathy is reassuring, but the abnormal sudomotor testing reminds clinicians that neuropathic involvement may already be present and should be monitored over time.

The main lesson is that young adults with atypical diabetes should not be forced into a type 1 or type 2 label when the phenotype does not fit. Strategic testing—autoantibodies, C-peptide, family history, and then genetic analysis—can confirm MODY, prevent unnecessary insulin exposure, reduce medication risk, and allow therapy to be tailored to the molecular diagnosis.
