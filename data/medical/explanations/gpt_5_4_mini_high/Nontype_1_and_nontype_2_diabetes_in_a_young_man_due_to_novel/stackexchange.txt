Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, BMI 24.6 kg/m2, negative GAD and IA-2 antibodies, preserved stimulated C-peptide, and diabetes affecting multiple successive relatives diagnosed before age 30, what specific form of diabetes should be suspected, and what inheritance pattern is typical?

This pattern should raise strong suspicion for **maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY)**, and in this case most specifically **HNF1A-MODY**. The key clues are the **young age at onset**, **absence of islet autoantibodies**, **preserved endogenous insulin secretion on C-peptide testing**, **lack of marked obesity/insulin resistance**, and a **clear multigenerational family history of diabetes diagnosed before age 30**. Glycosuria is also a classic clue in **HNF1A-MODY**, because of a reduced renal threshold for glucose.

The typical inheritance pattern is **autosomal dominant**, with diabetes often appearing in **successive generations** of a family.

### [Morning Report] Which clinical and laboratory features in this case make HNF1A-MODY more likely than classic type 1 diabetes and more likely than typical type 2 diabetes?

This case has the classic pattern that should prompt consideration of **HNF1A-MODY**: **young onset diabetes in several successive generations, no ketosis, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved but insufficient endogenous insulin secretion**.

More specifically, it is **less consistent with type 1 diabetes** because he had:

- **No diabetic ketoacidosis**
- **Negative GAD and IA-2 antibodies**
- **Detectable glucagon-stimulated C-peptide** (0.83 nmol/L), showing retained β-cell function rather than the near-absolute insulin deficiency typical of classic type 1 diabetes

It is **less consistent with typical type 2 diabetes** because he had:

- **Early-onset diabetes** in a **clear autosomal dominant family pattern**: grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, brother at 20
- **No obesity** (BMI 24.6 kg/m²)
- **No acanthosis nigricans** or obvious insulin-resistance phenotype
- **Glycosuria and marked postprandial hyperglycemia**, which are particularly suggestive of HNF1A-MODY because HNF1A variants lower the renal threshold for glucose

So the key teaching point is: **early-onset familial diabetes, autoantibody negativity, preserved C-peptide, absence of ketosis, and lack of a strong insulin-resistance phenotype** should move HNF1A-MODY ahead of both type 1 and typical type 2 diabetes in the differential.

### [Morning Report] When MODY is suspected in a patient with early-onset diabetes, what initial workup should be obtained before genetic testing, and how do autoantibody testing and C-peptide measurement help determine whether sequencing is warranted?

When MODY is suspected, the first step is not genetic testing but a careful phenotypic workup to exclude autoimmune type 1 diabetes and obvious type 2 diabetes. In practice, this means documenting the family history across generations, looking for features of insulin resistance such as obesity or acanthosis, and obtaining basic glycemic testing, along with pancreatic autoantibodies and C-peptide measurement.

The key autoantibodies are GAD65, IA-2, and ZnT8, with insulin autoantibody sometimes added in younger patients. If these are negative, autoimmune diabetes becomes much less likely. C-peptide then helps assess endogenous insulin secretion: a preserved fasting or, better, stimulated C-peptide supports an endogenous insulin-producing state and makes MODY more likely. In the case report, the patient had negative GAD and IA-2 antibodies and a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, which indicated residual beta-cell function and supported sequencing for MODY.

The combination matters: negative autoantibodies plus measurable C-peptide in a young patient with early-onset diabetes and a strong autosomal dominant family history is the pattern that should prompt genetic testing. By contrast, positive islet autoantibodies and absent/very low C-peptide point toward type 1 diabetes, making MODY sequencing much less likely to be useful. C-peptide should be interpreted cautiously, however, because early type 1 diabetes can still have preserved levels; it is supportive, not definitive.

### [Pathophysiology] How do HNF1A mutations lead to both progressive pancreatic beta-cell dysfunction and glycosuria, and why is postprandial hyperglycaemia often prominent early in the disease?

HNF1A-MODY is fundamentally a **transcriptional disease**. HNF1A encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor-1α, a transcription factor expressed in **pancreatic β cells** and **renal tubular cells**. Heterozygous loss-of-function mutations reduce expression of genes needed for normal glucose sensing and insulin secretion in β cells, including **insulin itself, GLUT2, and enzymes involved in glucose metabolism such as pyruvate kinase**. The result is a **progressive secretory defect**, not abrupt β-cell destruction. That is why patients like the young man in the case can still have measurable C-peptide early on, yet show an **inadequate stimulated insulin response** that worsens over time.

The **glycosuria** is a separate but related manifestation of HNF1A dysfunction in the kidney. HNF1A normally helps regulate **SGLT2** expression in the proximal tubule. When HNF1A function is reduced, the **renal threshold for glucose falls**, so glucose spills into the urine at lower plasma glucose levels than expected. This explains why glycosuria may be an early clue, as in the case report.

**Postprandial hyperglycaemia is often prominent early** because the earliest defect is an impaired ability to mount a **rapid insulin response to a glucose load**. Basal fasting insulin secretion may remain relatively preserved at first, so fasting glucose can be less striking than the **2-hour OGTT glucose**, which was markedly elevated in this patient. Over time, as β-cell dysfunction progresses, fasting hyperglycaemia becomes more evident and the phenotype begins to resemble other forms of diabetes.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should a glucagon-stimulated C-peptide increase from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L be interpreted in this patient, and why is this test supportive rather than definitive for distinguishing MODY from early type 1 diabetes?

The rise in C-peptide from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon indicates **preserved endogenous insulin secretion with impaired, not absent, β-cell reserve**. In this case, the stimulated value crosses the commonly used threshold of about **0.6 nmol/L**, which argues against absolute insulin deficiency and is therefore **compatible with a non–type 1 diabetes process such as MODY**, especially when paired with the negative GADA and IA-2A results and the strong autosomal dominant family history.

But the test is **supportive rather than definitive** because **early type 1 diabetes can still show measurable, even “normal,” C-peptide** before β-cell failure becomes complete. Conversely, some MODY patients have reduced C-peptide as their disease progresses. So glucagon-stimulated C-peptide tells you about **residual β-cell function**, not the **cause** of the diabetes. It cannot by itself distinguish **monogenic β-cell dysfunction** from **early autoimmune β-cell loss**.

In short: **0.83 nmol/L means there is still insulin production, making classic type 1 diabetes less likely, but only genetic testing can confirm MODY**, as it did here with the pathogenic **HNF1A** variant.

### [Clinical Pearls] A previously unreported HNF1A missense variant was identified in this patient. What types of evidence can support classifying a novel variant as pathogenic or likely pathogenic under ACMG principles, even if it is absent from ClinVar?

Absence from ClinVar does **not** mean a variant is benign; it usually means only that the variant is **novel or not yet curated**. Under ACMG/AMP principles, a previously unreported **HNF1A missense** variant can still be classified as **likely pathogenic** or **pathogenic** if multiple independent lines of evidence converge.

In this case, the strongest supporting evidence would be the following: **rarity or absence from population databases** (PM2); **segregation with diabetes in the family** in a pattern consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance (PP1); a **phenotype highly specific for HNF1A-MODY**—early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, glycosuria, and a strong multigenerational family history (PP4); placement in a **critical functional domain or mutational hot spot**, or near other known pathogenic residues, such as the **DNA-binding domain** in this report (PM1/PM5); and **computational evidence** suggesting deleterious impact, such as conservation and high pathogenicity scores like the reported AlphaMissense score (PP3). If available, **functional studies** showing impaired HNF1A transcriptional activity would add strong evidence (PS3), and a true **de novo** event would also be powerful evidence in the right context (PS2).

So, the key teaching point is that **ACMG classification is evidence-based, not database-based**. In this patient, the combination of classic MODY phenotype, family segregation, and the variant’s location in a biologically important region supported a **likely pathogenic** interpretation even though ClinVar did not yet contain the variant.

### [Management] After genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY in a patient initially treated with basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor, how should therapy be reconsidered, and why can insulin often be discontinued once glycaemic control is achieved?

Once **HNF1A-MODY** is genetically confirmed, the treatment plan should be **reframed from “type 1 or type 2 diabetes” to monogenic, β-cell–dysfunction diabetes**. In practice, that means insulin should be **reassessed and often tapered off**, provided there is **no diabetic ketoacidosis, no absolute insulin deficiency, and glycaemic control can be maintained with noninsulin therapy**.  

In this case, the patient had **negative islet autoantibodies** and **preserved stimulated C-peptide**, indicating **residual endogenous insulin secretion**. That is the key reason insulin can often be discontinued in HNF1A-MODY: the disorder is **not autoimmune β-cell destruction**, as in type 1 diabetes, but a **transcription-factor defect causing progressive, partial impairment of insulin secretion**. As long as some β-cell function remains, patients may do well on agents that enhance endogenous insulin release or incretin effect.  

For HNF1A-MODY, **low-dose sulfonylureas are classically first-line**, because these patients are highly sulfonylurea sensitive. In the reported case, insulin was stopped after genetic confirmation and glycaemic improvement, and therapy was shifted to **dulaglutide**, with later simplification to **metformin plus a DPP-4 inhibitor**. That is reasonable when sulfonylureas are not preferred or when a GLP-1 receptor agonist is better tolerated or considered safer.  

So the teaching point is: **after genetic diagnosis, do not keep insulin by default.** If the patient has **residual C-peptide and stable glycaemia**, insulin can usually be withdrawn and replaced with **MODY-directed therapy**, because the underlying problem is **insufficient but not absent insulin secretion**.

### [Management] Why are low-dose sulfonylureas traditionally considered first-line therapy in HNF1A-MODY, and in what clinical circumstances might a GLP-1 receptor agonist be a preferable alternative?

In HNF1A-MODY, the primary problem is **progressive β-cell dysfunction with impaired glucose-stimulated insulin secretion**, not insulin resistance. Low-dose sulfonylureas are traditionally first-line because they **bypass the defective glucose-sensing pathway** and directly stimulate insulin release by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels. Patients with HNF1A-MODY are often **unusually sensitive** to sulfonylureas, so small doses can produce marked glycemic improvement, but the same sensitivity also means a higher risk of hypoglycemia if standard type 2 diabetes doses are used.

A **GLP-1 receptor agonist** may be preferable when **β-cell reserve is declining** and sulfonylurea therapy is less attractive because of **hypoglycemia risk, intolerance, or inadequate control**. This is exactly the scenario suggested by the case: the patient had **negative autoantibodies, preserved but insufficient C-peptide, and strong family history**, and after genetic confirmation, insulin was stopped and **dulaglutide** was used successfully, with HbA1c falling to **6.0%**. As the report notes, GLP-1 receptor agonists can be a **safer alternative to sulfonylureas** in HNF1A-MODY, particularly in patients prone to hypoglycemia, and small trial data suggest efficacy comparable to sulfonylureas with **less mild hypoglycemia**.

So, the practical pearl is:  
- **Start with low-dose sulfonylurea** in classic HNF1A-MODY because these patients are highly responsive.  
- **Consider a GLP-1 receptor agonist** when sulfonylureas pose a hypoglycemia risk, are poorly tolerated, or when residual β-cell function is limited but not absent, as in this case.

### [Management] Why should SGLT2 inhibitors be used cautiously or avoided in patients with HNF1A-MODY?

SGLT2 inhibitors should be used cautiously, and often avoided, in **HNF1A-MODY** because these patients already have **reduced renal glucose reabsorption at baseline**. In HNF1A-MODY, the **HNF1A transcription factor normally regulates SGLT2 expression in the proximal tubule**; when HNF1A is mutated, SGLT2 expression is diminished, which explains the characteristic **glycosuria** even at relatively modest glucose levels.

Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor in this setting can push a patient who already has a **low renal glucose threshold** into **excessive glycosuria and osmotic diuresis**, increasing the risk of **volume depletion, severe dehydration, and possibly vascular collapse**. The case report also highlights concern for **genital infections** and **diabetic ketoacidosis**, including potentially **euglycemic DKA**, because these patients have **progressive β-cell dysfunction and limited insulin reserve** rather than the insulin resistance typical of type 2 diabetes.

So the key point is: in HNF1A-MODY, SGLT2 inhibition may amplify an existing tubular defect and create disproportionate harm relative to benefit. In this case, the authors appropriately favored **metformin and a GLP-1 receptor agonist** rather than an SGLT2 inhibitor.

### [Management] Once HNF1A-MODY is diagnosed in one patient, what is the rationale for cascade testing and counselling of family members, and which relatives should be prioritised?

Once HNF1A-MODY is diagnosed in one patient, cascade testing is recommended because this is an **autosomal dominant** disorder: each first-degree relative has a **50% chance** of carrying the familial variant. The point is not only to label relatives, but to **find affected family members early**, before or at the onset of hyperglycemia, so they can receive the correct diagnosis, appropriate glucose monitoring, and MODY-directed therapy rather than being treated as having type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Counseling also matters because it clarifies **inheritance risk, reproductive risk, and long-term surveillance** for microvascular complications.

The relatives to **prioritize first** are the **first-degree relatives** of the proband: **parents, siblings, and children**. In this case, the pedigree already suggests **vertical transmission on the maternal side**, so the **mother, younger brother, and other maternal relatives with early-onset diabetes** would be the highest-yield family members to test first. If the familial mutation is confirmed in them, testing can then be extended to additional at-risk relatives in that branch of the family.
