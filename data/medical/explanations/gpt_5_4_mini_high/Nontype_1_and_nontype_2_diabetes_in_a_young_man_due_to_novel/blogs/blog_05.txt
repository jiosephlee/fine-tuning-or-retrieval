Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# From Suspicion to Sequencing: When to Order MODY Testing and How to Interpret a Novel Variant

## The case that should make you pause

This is the kind of diabetes case that slips through the usual type 1 / type 2 binning process.

A young adult showed up for routine screening with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. He wasn’t ketotic, he wasn’t markedly obese, and he didn’t have acanthosis nigricans. His A1c was 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and 2-hour OGTT glucose 19.8 mmol/L. The family history was the real clue: diabetes across three generations, with onset in the 20s and 30s. Autoantibodies were negative, and C-peptide was still present but not robust. Genetic testing later found a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant, c.809A>G (p.N270S), consistent with HNF1A-MODY.

The surprise here isn’t just the diagnosis. It’s how often a case like this gets labeled “type 2” because the patient is not in DKA and still has some endogenous insulin production. That shortcut can delay the right test, the right counseling, and the right treatment.

## When “young diabetes” should trigger MODY thinking

MODY is not rare enough to ignore, and not obvious enough to catch without a deliberate pause. The textbook pattern is autosomal dominant diabetes presenting at a young age, often before 25, with non-autoimmune features and preserved C-peptide. But real life is messier.

In this case, several features pointed toward monogenic diabetes:

- Young age at diagnosis
- Clear multigenerational family history
- No DKA
- Negative GADA and IA-2A
- Preserved, though insufficient, beta-cell function on glucagon stimulation
- Glycosuria out of proportion to the usual “type 2” story

That last clue matters in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A regulates renal glucose handling, and reduced SGLT2 expression lowers the renal threshold for glycosuria. So if you see glycosuria early, especially with a family history that looks autosomal dominant, don’t just assume “early type 2.”

Also worth remembering: BMI and metabolic syndrome do not exclude MODY. This patient’s BMI was 24.6 kg/m² and his triglycerides were elevated, but that did not make this type 2 diabetes. As obesity becomes more common, phenotype alone gets less useful.

## What should you order, and when?

The practical sequence is usually:

1. Confirm diabetes and assess severity.
2. Check islet autoantibodies.
3. Assess endogenous insulin secretion with C-peptide, ideally in a clinically useful context.
4. If the story still doesn’t fit type 1 or type 2, order monogenic diabetes testing.

In a patient like this, an NGS monogenic diabetes panel is often the most efficient test. Why? Because MODY is genetically heterogeneous, with more than 15 genes linked to the phenotype, and the clinical overlap is real. Serial single-gene testing can be slow and expensive, especially when the phenotype is not absolutely classic.

The case also shows a common real-world pattern: you often have to treat hyperglycaemia before the genetics come back. That’s appropriate. The key is to keep the differential open while you stabilize the patient.

A useful reminder from this case: the glucagon stimulation test was supportive, not definitive. A stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L suggests residual endogenous insulin secretion, which argues against classic long-standing type 1 diabetes, but it does not by itself “diagnose” MODY. Use it as one piece of the puzzle.

## The MODY calculator: helpful, but not gospel

Tools like the Exeter MODY probability calculator are useful for triage. They can help identify who should get genetic testing, especially when you’re balancing cost and access.

But they have limits.

This calculator was derived largely from White European populations, so it may not generalize cleanly to other ancestries. In this case, the calculated probability was 62.4%, which is certainly high enough to justify sequencing. Still, the number should support clinical judgment, not replace it.

That’s the key principle: use calculators to sharpen suspicion, not to decide the diagnosis.

## Interpreting a novel variant without overcalling it

Here’s where many clinicians get nervous: the sequencing comes back, and the variant is “novel.” It’s not in ClinVar, not in VarSome, and not in the literature. What now?

First, novel does not mean benign. It just means unreported.

To interpret a variant like HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S), you have to think like a clinician-geneticist and ask a few questions:

- Does the gene match the phenotype?
- Is the variant in a functionally important region?
- Does it segregate with disease in the family?
- Do in silico predictions support functional impact?
- Is the phenotype consistent with what we know about this gene?

In this case, the answer was yes across multiple fronts:

- HNF1A is a well-established MODY gene
- The variant is in the DNA-binding domain
- It lies adjacent to known pathogenic residues
- AlphaMissense scored it highly deleterious
- The family history fit autosomal dominant diabetes
- The clinical phenotype fit HNF1A-MODY

That’s the logic behind ACMG-based interpretation. You do not need a ClinVar entry to call a variant clinically relevant if multiple independent lines of evidence point the same way. Database absence is not a veto.

This is also why segregation data matter so much. If the variant tracks with diabetes across affected relatives, that significantly strengthens the case for pathogenicity or likely pathogenicity. In monogenic diabetes, family studies can be as valuable as any single lab annotation.

## Why the diagnosis changes treatment

The treatment implications are where MODY becomes more than an academic label.

HNF1A-MODY is classically sulfonylurea-responsive because the core problem is impaired insulin secretion rather than insulin resistance. But this case shows that treatment can be individualized beyond the traditional “sulfonylurea only” mindset.

He initially received basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin while the diagnosis was being clarified. After genetic confirmation and improving glycaemia, insulin was stopped and dulaglutide was started. His A1c fell from 10.2% to 6.0%, and he later remained well controlled on metformin plus saxagliptin.

That’s a nice reminder that precision medicine is not just about naming the disease. It’s about using the diagnosis to de-escalate unnecessary insulin, avoid hypoglycaemia, and choose therapy that fits the biology.

A few practical points from this case:

- GLP-1 receptor agonists can be useful in HNF1A-MODY, particularly when beta-cell function is declining and hypoglycaemia is a concern.
- Sulfonylureas remain a classic first-line option, but they are not the only reasonable choice.
- SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution in HNF1A-MODY because of baseline glycosuria and concern for dehydration and ketoacidosis risk.
- Metformin may still have a role, especially if there are features of insulin resistance or mixed physiology.

## The bigger lesson

This case is a good example of why “young diabetes” should not be a reflex diagnosis.

If the story includes early-onset diabetes, autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, and a phenotype that doesn’t quite fit type 1 or type 2, MODY testing is worth pursuing. And if the genetic result is a novel variant, don’t stop at “it’s not in ClinVar.” Use ACMG reasoning, phenotype matching, and segregation to decide whether the variant is clinically meaningful.

## Take-home points

- Young-onset diabetes with a strong family history and negative autoantibodies should trigger MODY thinking early.
- Glycosuria can be a clue in HNF1A-MODY, not just a nonspecific finding.
- BMI and metabolic syndrome features do not rule out MODY.
- MODY probability calculators are useful for triage, but they were built in specific populations and may not generalize well.
- A novel variant is not automatically uncertain or benign; interpretation should incorporate ACMG criteria, segregation, domain location, and phenotype fit.
- Genetic confirmation matters because it can change therapy, reduce unnecessary insulin use, and guide family counseling.
