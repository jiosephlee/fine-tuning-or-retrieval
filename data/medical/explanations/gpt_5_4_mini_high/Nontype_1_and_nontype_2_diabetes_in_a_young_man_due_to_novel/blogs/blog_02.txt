Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The Type 2 Look-Alike Trap: Why BMI, Triglycerides and Mild Metabolic Features Don’t Exclude MODY

## The case that should make you pause

A young adult shows up for a routine health exam and gets flagged for hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. Nothing dramatic on exam: BMI 24.6 kg/m², blood pressure normal, no acanthosis nigricans, no ketoacidosis. If you stopped there, this could easily get filed under “early type 2 diabetes” and never looked at again.

But the details matter.

He had a strong autosomal-dominant-looking family history of early diabetes: maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at 20. His HbA1c was 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and 2-hour OGTT glucose 19.8 mmol/L. He also had glycosuria and classic osmotic symptoms: thirst, polyphagia, and weight loss. Autoantibodies were negative. Glucagon-stimulated C-peptide was still present, but not robust. Genetic testing ultimately found a novel heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variant, confirming HNF1A-MODY.

That’s the trap: the patient didn’t look “classic MODY” enough to be obvious, but he also didn’t really fit type 2 diabetes cleanly. He sat in the growing overlap zone where diagnostic anchoring can send you in the wrong direction.

## Why this matters: MODY is losing its “thin, perfect phenotype” stereotype

We’ve traditionally been taught to think of MODY as a lean young person with diabetes plus a striking multigenerational family history. Helpful? Sure. Complete? Not even close.

As obesity and metabolic syndrome become more common in younger people, the phenotype of MODY is getting noisier. A mildly elevated BMI, higher triglycerides, or even a general “metabolic syndrome vibe” no longer safely excludes monogenic diabetes. That’s exactly why HNF1A-MODY gets missed: the clinical picture can overlap with type 2 diabetes enough to feel familiar, but the underlying biology is different enough that treatment, prognosis, and family counseling all change.

This patient had triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L and HDL 1.01 mmol/L, which could easily nudge clinicians toward a type 2 narrative. But the family history and negative autoimmunity should keep MODY on the table.

## What HNF1A-MODY looks like when it doesn’t read the textbook

HNF1A-MODY is the most common MODY subtype. It’s caused by mutations in a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. The result is progressive beta-cell dysfunction and impaired insulin secretion.

A few features matter clinically:

- Hyperglycaemia often appears in adolescence or early adulthood
- Glycosuria is common and can be striking
- Ketoacidosis is usually absent at presentation
- Family history is often autosomal dominant
- Autoantibodies are usually negative
- C-peptide may still be present early on, but beta-cell function declines over time

This case had a particularly useful clue: glycosuria. In HNF1A-MODY, reduced renal threshold for glucose can lead to urinary glucose loss even when the rest of the picture is still evolving. That’s a clue worth noticing, especially in a young person without clear insulin resistance features.

## When the workup starts to point away from type 1 and type 2

The diagnostic approach here was sensible and very FOAM-worthy: don’t overcommit early, but don’t ignore the pattern.

This patient had:

- Negative GADA and IA-2A
- Preserved but inadequate glucagon-stimulated C-peptide
- Strong family history of early-onset diabetes
- No DKA
- Glycosuria
- Young age at presentation

That combination should trigger consideration of MODY.

The glucagon stimulation test is useful as a support tool, not a standalone answer. A stimulated C-peptide above roughly 0.60 nmol/L suggests preserved endogenous insulin secretion, but interpretation depends on context. In a young person with negative antibodies and a dominant family history, “some insulin production” does not equal type 2 diabetes.

One practical point: MODY probability calculators can help, but they’re not gospel. In this report, the Exeter calculator suggested a substantial probability of MODY, but these tools were derived largely from White European populations and may not generalize perfectly across ethnic groups.

## The genetics mattered because the phenotype was not enough

One month after diagnosis, NGS identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant: p.N270S (c.809A>G), in exon 4. It wasn’t in ClinVar or VarSome, but the authors classified it as pathogenic/likely pathogenic using ACMG criteria, supported by:

- Location in the DNA-binding domain
- High AlphaMissense score
- Segregation with diabetes in the family
- Biological plausibility

This is the key precision-medicine point: the diagnosis was not made because the phenotype was textbook. It was made because the phenotype triggered testing, and the testing clarified the biology.

## Treatment: why the correct diagnosis changes what you do

He was initially treated like early type 2 diabetes: basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin. That’s understandable given the degree of hyperglycaemia at presentation.

But once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed, insulin was stopped and therapy shifted to dulaglutide with metformin. HbA1c improved from 10.2% to 6.0% over 6 months, and glycaemic control stayed under 6.5% on follow-up. The case supports a few practical treatment lessons:

In HNF1A-MODY, sulfonylureas are often first-line because these patients tend to be quite sensitive to insulin secretagogues. In this report, however, a GLP-1 receptor agonist was used successfully, which is especially relevant when beta-cell function is already declining or when hypoglycaemia risk is a concern.

This is also a reminder that getting the diagnosis right can prevent unnecessary long-term insulin use. That’s not just about convenience — it affects quality of life, hypoglycaemia risk, and cost.

## Don’t let metabolic features prematurely close the differential

This is the practical takeaway from the case: a mildly elevated BMI and dyslipidaemia do not rule out MODY. They may simply be background noise.

What should keep you honest?

- Diabetes diagnosed young
- Family history spanning multiple generations
- Negative islet autoantibodies
- Glycosuria
- C-peptide that is present but not robust
- No convincing features of marked insulin resistance
- A course that doesn’t fit the expected type 1 or type 2 story

In other words, don’t let triglycerides and BMI do your thinking for you.

## A useful mental model

When a young person presents with diabetes, ask yourself three questions:

1. **Is there evidence of autoimmunity?**  
   If yes, type 1 rises. If no, keep moving.

2. **Is there evidence of insulin resistance severe enough to explain it?**  
   A BMI of 24.6 and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L may raise suspicion for type 2, but they’re not decisive.

3. **Does the family history look dominant rather than simply “lots of diabetes in the family”?**  
   Early-onset diabetes across multiple generations should make MODY hard to ignore.

If the answer set is “no autoimmunity, not much insulin resistance, strong family history,” you should be thinking genetic testing.

## Take-home points

- **MODY can look like type 2 diabetes**, especially as obesity and dyslipidaemia become more common in younger patients.
- **BMI and triglycerides are not exclusion criteria** for MODY.
- **Negative autoantibodies plus preserved C-peptide plus a strong family history** should prompt consideration of monogenic diabetes.
- **Glycosuria in a young patient is a clue**, particularly in HNF1A-MODY.
- **Genetic confirmation changes management** and opens the door to family screening.
- **Precision matters**: this patient’s novel HNF1A variant clarified the diagnosis and supported a successful treatment change.

This case is a good reminder that diabetes phenotypes are getting messier, not cleaner. The job at the bedside is to resist premature closure — especially when the story is trying to tell you “type 2” before the biology has had its say.
