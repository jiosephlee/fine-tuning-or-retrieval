Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Precision Diabetes in Real Time: How a Genetic Diagnosis Changed Therapy

## The case that should make you stop and think

A young adult shows up on a routine exam with hyperglycemia and glycosuria. No DKA. No acanthosis. BMI 24.6 kg/m². Family history lights up across generations: grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at 20.

That’s not the usual “new type 2 diabetes” story.

He had symptomatic hyperglycemia with HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and a 2-hour OGTT of 19.8 mmol/L. Autoantibodies were negative. C-peptide was not absent, but it wasn’t reassuringly robust either. Genetic testing later identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant, consistent with HNF1A-MODY.

And that changed the treatment story in a big way: insulin was stopped, dulaglutide was started, and his HbA1c fell to 6.0%.

That’s the clinical lesson here. Precision medicine is useful when it changes the next prescription.

## Why this looked like diabetes, but not quite the usual kind

MODY sits in the awkward middle ground between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. It’s monogenic, autosomal dominant, and caused by a primary beta-cell defect. HNF1A-MODY is one of the commonest forms, and it often presents in adolescence or young adulthood.

The clues in this case were classic enough to raise suspicion, but not so classic that the diagnosis was obvious:

- early-onset diabetes in multiple relatives
- glycosuria
- negative islet autoantibodies
- preserved but insufficient endogenous insulin secretion
- no DKA
- no strong insulin-resistant phenotype

The glycosuria matters. In HNF1A-MODY, the kidney’s glucose threshold is lowered because HNF1A regulates renal glucose handling, including SGLT2 expression. So glycosuria can show up early and sometimes seems out of proportion to the clinical picture.

One useful pearl: HNF1A-MODY doesn’t always come wrapped in the stereotype of a very lean, perfectly “non–type 2” patient. This patient had a BMI in the normal-to-overweight range and triglycerides that could easily pull a clinician toward type 2 diabetes. That’s exactly why pattern recognition has to beat stereotype recognition.

## What the bedside workup is trying to answer

The practical question is not “Does this patient have a rare gene mutation?”

It’s: **Is this really autoimmune beta-cell destruction, insulin resistance, or an inherited beta-cell defect that will respond better to another therapy?**

In this case, several bedside and lab features pointed away from classic type 1 diabetes:

- negative GADA and IA-2A
- no ketoacidosis
- stimulated C-peptide still present at 0.83 nmol/L
- family history across generations

The glucagon stimulation test is helpful here because it tells you whether there’s still endogenous insulin production. A stimulated C-peptide above about 0.60 nmol/L generally suggests meaningful residual secretion. That said, it’s supportive, not definitive. You still need the phenotype, antibody testing, and—when suspicion is high—genetic testing.

Also worth remembering: a normal C-peptide does not permanently exclude type 1 diabetes early on. So serial context matters. But in a patient like this, the combination of family history, negative antibodies, and preserved C-peptide should move MODY much higher on the differential.

## Why the diagnosis changes treatment

This is where HNF1A-MODY really matters at the bedside.

HNF1A-MODY is not just a diagnostic label; it predicts drug response.

Historically, **low-dose sulfonylureas** are considered first-line because these patients still have beta cells that can be stimulated. The defect is not absence of beta cells—it’s impaired transcriptional programming and progressive beta-cell dysfunction. Sulfonylureas can “push” insulin secretion effectively.

But there are tradeoffs:

- hypoglycemia risk can be significant
- beta-cell function tends to decline over time
- some patients may need alternative or adjunctive agents

In this case, after genetic confirmation and early improvement in glucose control, insulin was stopped and dulaglutide was started. That’s a very reasonable precision-medicine move. GLP-1 receptor agonists can improve glycemic control without the same hypoglycemia burden as sulfonylureas, and the literature supports their use as an alternative in HNF1A-MODY.

The case report notes a small randomized crossover trial in HNF1A-MODY where liraglutide and glimepiride both improved glycemia, with less mild hypoglycemia on the GLP-1 receptor agonist. So while sulfonylureas remain the classic answer, GLP-1 therapy is a legitimate modern option—especially when you want a safer fit for someone with residual but limited beta-cell function.

This patient’s course shows the practical point: once the diagnosis was clarified, the team could stop using insulin as a default and choose therapy that matched the underlying biology.

## What about metformin, DPP-4 inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors?

Metformin was part of his regimen from the start and remained in his long-term maintenance plan, which is sensible if there’s any concomitant insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome physiology. In real life, many patients do not fit a single tidy category.

The case also used saxagliptin after the initial GLP-1 receptor agonist phase. That’s not the classic teaching for HNF1A-MODY, but it reflects the broader point: management can be individualized once you know what you’re treating.

SGLT2 inhibitors are where the biology gets interesting. Since HNF1A affects renal glucose handling and these patients already have glycosuria, adding an SGLT2 inhibitor may be a poor physiologic fit. The case report highlights concerns about dehydration, genital infections, vascular collapse, and diabetic ketoacidosis. In other words: just because an agent lowers glucose doesn’t mean it’s the right tool for this subtype.

## The real takeaway: don’t wait for genetics to think correctly

The most useful part of this case is not the novel variant itself. It’s the bedside consequence of getting the diagnosis right:

- insulin was started when the patient was clearly hyperglycemic
- the phenotype raised suspicion for MODY
- genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY
- therapy was de-escalated and aligned with the underlying disease
- control improved substantially

That is precision medicine done well.

And it’s a good reminder that young-onset diabetes is not a single disease. If you see early diabetes plus a strong autosomal-dominant family history, negative antibodies, glycosuria, and some preserved C-peptide, don’t let the chart settle too quickly on “type 2.” That’s the moment to think MODY and send the genetic test.

## Take-home points

- HNF1A-MODY should be on the differential in young-onset diabetes with dominant family history, glycosuria, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide.
- A glucagon-stimulated C-peptide is supportive, but it’s not the whole diagnosis.
- Confirming HNF1A-MODY can change management: insulin may be temporary, and therapy can be tailored to beta-cell physiology.
- Sulfonylureas remain the classic first-line therapy, but GLP-1 receptor agonists are a reasonable alternative in selected patients.
- Don’t let BMI or mild metabolic syndrome features exclude MODY; real patients are messier than textbook phenotypes.
- SGLT2 inhibitors may be a poor fit in HNF1A-MODY because of the renal glucose-handling defect and glycosuria.

This is what precision medicine should look like at the bedside: not genetic testing for curiosity, but genetic testing that changes the next decision.
