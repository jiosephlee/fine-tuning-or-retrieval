Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Glycosuria as a Genotype Clue: The Renal Physiology of HNF1A-MODY and Why SGLT2 Inhibitors May Be a Bad Fit

## The case that should make you stop and think

A young man came in for routine health screening and was found to have hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. Not DKA. Not obvious type 1 diabetes. Not a classic insulin-resistant type 2 phenotype either.

He had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans, and a striking family history: diabetes in his maternal grandmother in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. His HbA1c was 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and 2-hour OGTT glucose 19.8 mmol/L. Autoantibodies were negative, and a glucagon stimulation test showed residual but insufficient insulin secretion.

Genetic testing later revealed a novel heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variant, confirming HNF1A-MODY.

That’s the punchline: the urine glucose wasn’t just a consequence of high serum glucose. It was a clue to the genotype.

## Why glycosuria is a bigger deal than it looks

We usually think of glycosuria as “blood sugar got high enough to spill.” That’s true in ordinary diabetes. But in HNF1A-MODY, the kidney has its own piece of the pathology.

HNF1A is expressed in multiple tissues, including the pancreas and kidney. In the proximal tubule, HNF1A helps regulate SGLT2 expression, and when HNF1A is mutated, renal glucose reabsorption is impaired. The result is a reduced renal threshold for glucose: patients may spill glucose earlier and more readily than you’d expect for their measured plasma glucose.

So when a young person with diabetes has glycosuria out of proportion to the usual “type 2” story, think beyond insulin resistance.

This is especially relevant when the rest of the phenotype points away from type 2 diabetes:

- early-onset diabetes across generations
- negative islet autoantibodies
- preserved C-peptide, even if not fully normal
- no acanthosis or marked obesity
- glycosuria on routine urinalysis

That combination should trigger a MODY workup.

## The diagnostic trap: looking like type 2, behaving like monogenic diabetes

This case had a few features that could have anchored the team on type 2 diabetes: hyperglycaemia, elevated triglycerides, and a need for initial insulin because the HbA1c was high. But the family history and antibody/C-peptide pattern told a different story.

A preserved C-peptide is helpful, but it’s not the whole answer. Early type 1 diabetes can still have measurable endogenous insulin production. That’s why the full pattern matters: family history, antibodies, body habitus, glycosuria, and beta-cell reserve all feed the differential.

Once the HNF1A mutation was identified, the management changed. He was transitioned off insulin and treated with dulaglutide plus metformin, with HbA1c falling to 6.0% over follow-up. He has remained under surveillance without microalbuminuria or retinopathy to date.

## Why this matters for prescribing: SGLT2 inhibitors are not a neutral choice here

This is where the renal physiology becomes clinically actionable.

SGLT2 inhibitors work by intentionally increasing urinary glucose loss. That’s useful in many patients with type 2 diabetes, and for some it’s a major win. But in HNF1A-MODY, the baseline physiology is already shifted toward glycosuria because SGLT2 expression is reduced.

So what happens if you add an SGLT2 inhibitor on top of that?

You may be stacking glucosuric mechanisms on top of each other. In a patient who already has limited beta-cell reserve, that can mean:

- more osmotic diuresis
- more volume depletion risk
- more concern for ketosis, including euglycaemic DKA
- uncertain incremental glycaemic benefit relative to the risk

This is not a blanket “never use an SGLT2 inhibitor in any monogenic diabetes” statement. The evidence base is limited. But the physiology is uncomfortable enough that HNF1A-MODY should make you pause before reaching for the class reflexively.

In other words: if the kidney is already behaving like it has partial SGLT2 inhibition built in, adding a drug that does exactly that may be a poor fit.

## What to do instead

For HNF1A-MODY, classic teaching still favors low-dose sulfonylureas because these patients often remain responsive to insulin secretagogues. In this case, the team used a GLP-1 receptor agonist, and the patient improved substantially.

That’s a useful reminder that treatment in MODY is not just “diabetes control.” It’s matching therapy to mechanism:

- HNF1A-MODY = beta-cell dysfunction, not primary insulin resistance
- Sulfonylureas can work very well
- GLP-1 receptor agonists may be a reasonable alternative in selected patients, especially if hypoglycaemia is a concern or beta-cell function is waning
- SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution because the renal phenotype may already be “pre-loaded” toward glucosuria and dehydration

## Clinical pearl

A urine dipstick with glucose in a young patient is not automatically a “type 2 diabetes” finding. In the right context, it’s a genotype clue.

If the patient is young, has autosomal-dominant family history, lacks autoimmunity, and has preserved C-peptide, think MODY before you settle on an ordinary diabetes label.

## Take-home points

- Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is often a clue to a lowered renal glucose threshold, not just severe hyperglycaemia.
- HNF1A affects both beta-cell function and renal glucose handling.
- Negative autoantibodies and preserved C-peptide should push you toward monogenic diabetes when the family history fits.
- SGLT2 inhibitors may be a physiologic mismatch in HNF1A-MODY because they can amplify an already glucosuric, volume-depleting phenotype.
- Genetic diagnosis changes treatment: it can move patients away from insulin and toward therapies that better match the underlying defect.
