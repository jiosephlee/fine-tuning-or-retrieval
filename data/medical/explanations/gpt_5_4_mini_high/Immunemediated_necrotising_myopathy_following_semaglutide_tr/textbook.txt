Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Foundations: GLP-1 Receptor Agonists, Drug-Associated Myopathy, and Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are widely used for type 2 diabetes and obesity and are increasingly prescribed because they lower glucose, promote weight loss, and can improve metabolic risk factors that overlap with fatty liver disease. Their most common adverse effects are gastrointestinal, especially nausea, vomiting, early satiety, and diarrhea. In most patients these are self-limited, but severe intolerance can be clinically important because persistent poor intake and volume depletion may lead to dehydration, acute kidney injury, and metabolic derangements, as happened early in this case. When a patient deteriorates after starting one of these agents, it is essential not to attribute all symptoms to “expected side effects,” because more serious drug-related or drug-unmasked pathology may be present.

Muscle-related adverse events reported with GLP-1-based therapies span a spectrum. Some patients experience weight-loss–associated reduction in lean mass, which is not the same as primary myopathy. Rarely, patients may develop marked creatine kinase (CK) elevation and rhabdomyolysis, a toxic muscle injury that typically presents acutely during exposure and improves rapidly after stopping the drug and giving supportive care. That pattern differs from an autoimmune process, in which weakness and CK elevation persist or worsen despite discontinuation. The case in this report is notable because the patient’s CK and weakness progressed over months rather than resolving quickly, making a simple toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis less likely.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct inflammatory myopathy characterized clinically by subacute, usually symmetric proximal muscle weakness and often very high CK levels, frequently in the thousands. Patients may also develop dysphagia or dysphonia when bulbar muscles are involved, reflecting more severe disease. Histologically, IMNM is defined by prominent myofibre necrosis and regeneration with relatively little lymphocytic inflammation, although macrophages are often present because they clear damaged fibers. This distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis, which classically shows endomysial CD8-positive T-cell invasion of non-necrotic fibers, and from dermatomyositis, which more often causes perifascicular atrophy, skin disease, and a complement-mediated microangiopathic pattern. A toxic myopathy can also produce necrotic fibers, but it generally lacks the autoimmune features and tends to improve once the offending exposure is removed.

Two autoantibody-associated IMNM subtypes are well recognized: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (anti-HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP). Anti-HMGCR disease is classically linked to statin exposure, while anti-SRP disease often causes severe weakness and CK elevation without a clear medication trigger. A substantial minority of patients, however, are seronegative, meaning that testing does not identify a known myositis autoantibody. Seronegative IMNM is diagnostically challenging because there is no single blood test to confirm the diagnosis, so clinicians must rely on the combination of clinical pattern, CK elevation, MRI evidence of muscle edema, exclusion of mimics, and muscle biopsy. In this patient, the negative HMGCR and SRP testing did not exclude IMNM; rather, it made tissue diagnosis especially important.

The pathology of IMNM supports an immune-mediated necrotizing process. Muscle biopsy typically shows necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage-predominant inflammation, and myophagocytosis, with only sparse T-cell infiltrates. Membrane attack complex deposition on the sarcolemma can also be seen and suggests complement-mediated injury. These findings indicate that the immune system is participating in ongoing muscle damage rather than the injury being purely toxic or metabolic. In the present case, the biopsy pattern of necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage staining and membrane attack complex deposition was therefore highly consistent with IMNM.

The concept of drug-triggered autoimmunity is important to understanding why a medication exposure may precede a sustained myopathy. Statin-associated necrotizing myopathy is the classic example: some patients do not simply improve after stopping the statin, but instead develop a self-sustaining autoimmune process that requires immunosuppression. This precedent shows that a drug can act as a trigger rather than a direct toxin. In the case under discussion, the temporal relationship to semaglutide, the progressive weakness after the initial exposure, and the response to corticosteroids and IVIG all fit better with an immune-mediated syndrome than with short-lived toxic rhabdomyolysis. Because the patient was seronegative, the biopsy and clinical course were central to the diagnosis, illustrating why seronegative IMNM remains an important and sometimes easily missed entity.

## From First Dose to Progressive Neuromuscular Syndrome: Reconstructing the Clinical Course

This patient’s course is best understood as two temporally linked but clinically distinct phases. She was a woman in her 60s with obesity and several common comorbidities—hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote history of venous thromboembolism—who had been functionally independent before this illness. Her pre-existing medical problems matter because they created diagnostic “noise”: in particular, she already had mildly elevated transaminases and a prior liver biopsy showing non-alcoholic steatohepatitis. That background made later AST and ALT abnormalities harder to interpret, since both liver disease and muscle injury can raise aminotransferases. In a patient like this, one must be careful not to assume that all laboratory abnormalities point in the same direction.

Semaglutide was started for obesity and fatty liver disease, which is a typical clinical use for a GLP-1 receptor agonist. Shortly after the first and only dose, she developed prominent gastrointestinal intolerance with nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury, and severe metabolic acidosis. This early presentation is most consistent with an acute adverse drug reaction complicated by volume depletion rather than primary myositis. The creatine kinase at that time was only modestly elevated, and there were no classic rhabdomyolysis clues such as diffuse myalgias or cola-colored urine. For learners, this distinction is important: a mildly elevated CK during an acute dehydrating illness does not by itself establish inflammatory myopathy, especially when the dominant syndrome is gastrointestinal and renal.

The more diagnostically important phase came later. Over the next four months, despite having stopped semaglutide after that single exposure, she developed progressive weakness that no longer fit simple deconditioning. The red flags were striking: she became unable to sit or stand without assistance, and she developed dysphagia and dysphonia. Those bulbar symptoms, together with severe proximal limb weakness, should immediately raise concern for a serious myopathic process. Inflammatory myopathies often present with difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, lifting the arms, or maintaining head and trunk control; when swallowing and voice are affected, the disease is more severe and the risk of aspiration and respiratory compromise increases.

Her examination supported a muscle-localized process rather than a neuropathic or central neurologic disorder. The weakness was greatest in proximal muscle groups, and there were no rashes, sensory deficits, synovitis, or other focal neurologic findings to suggest dermatomyositis, neuropathy, or a central lesion. In addition, the absence of myalgias does not exclude myopathy; immune-mediated necrotising myopathy can produce profound weakness with limited pain. By the time she presented for tertiary evaluation, the CK had risen into the thousands, which fits a true muscle injury pattern and is far more concerning than the earlier mild elevation. The combination of progressive proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, and substantial CK elevation made the syndrome urgent, not merely chronic or post-hospital deconditioning.

Several features also suggested possible severe systemic involvement. Her cardiac biomarkers were abnormal, and imaging raised concern for myocardial inflammation, although this was not definitive. In a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy, any evidence of cardiac involvement heightens urgency because myocardium and skeletal muscle can be affected together. Even if the cardiac findings ultimately prove equivocal, they reinforce the need to think broadly about inflammatory disease rather than attributing the entire syndrome to weakness from illness or immobility.

Overall, the timeline is what makes this case clinically instructive. An initial acute intolerance syndrome followed by a delayed, progressive neuromuscular decline after a single semaglutide dose is not the pattern of uncomplicated drug side effects or transient rhabdomyolysis. Instead, it should prompt clinicians to consider an autoimmune-pattern myopathy, especially when weakness is proximal, functionally disabling, and accompanied by bulbar symptoms and rising CK.

## Diagnostic Evaluation: Laboratory, Imaging, and Histopathologic Confirmation

The workup began with the clinical pattern of an acquired myopathy: progressive symmetric proximal weakness with dysphagia and dysphonia, together with a clear creatine kinase (CK) rise. In suspected muscle disease, CK is the most useful enzyme marker because it reflects skeletal muscle injury directly. This case also illustrates why AST and ALT cannot be assumed to be liver-specific. Both enzymes may rise in myopathy, especially when muscle necrosis is active. Here, the patient already had known hepatic steatosis, so her chronic transaminase elevation was a potential red herring. The normal bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, and platelets, along with later biopsy-proven steatotic liver disease, made chronic fatty liver a better explanation for the transaminases than the acute muscle process. The key point was the mismatch between a modest, chronic liver enzyme abnormality and a much more striking CK elevation, which strongly favored muscle injury.

Inflammatory markers were only mildly elevated, with a modestly raised ESR and CRP. That pattern is compatible with inflammatory myopathy but is nonspecific, so these tests serve only as supportive evidence. A normal TSH was important because hypothyroid myopathy is a common and reversible cause of proximal weakness and CK elevation. The broad autoimmune screen was unrevealing, including ANA, ENA antibodies, ANCA, and other connective tissue disease serologies. This reduced the likelihood of lupus, vasculitis, or another overlap syndrome as the explanation for her presentation. Her myositis panel was also negative, including anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR. Those two antibodies are closely associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, but their absence does not exclude the diagnosis, because a substantial subset of patients are seronegative and must be diagnosed clinically and histologically. The isolated low-titre SS-B positivity was not compelling evidence of a Sjögren-related process in the absence of ANA or SS-A positivity, sicca symptoms, or a compatible systemic phenotype.

MRI of the thighs provided supportive evidence of active muscle inflammation. It showed symmetric oedema in the proximal quadriceps and vastus lateralis muscles, which is the typical pattern seen in inflammatory myositis. MRI does not establish the exact subtype, but it is useful because it confirms that weakness is associated with active muscle disease and helps select the best biopsy site. In this patient, MRI also helped exclude a focal structural injury. High-resolution chest CT was negative for interstitial lung disease, which was helpful because ILD would have suggested a myositis overlap syndrome such as antisynthetase disease rather than isolated IMNM.

The diagnosis was confirmed on muscle biopsy. The right vastus lateralis specimen showed necrotic and regenerating fibres with myophagocytosis, indicating active fibre destruction and repair. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated CD68-positive macrophages and acid phosphatase staining in areas of fibre breakdown, consistent with macrophage-mediated cleanup of necrotic tissue. Membrane attack complex staining was present on necrotic and non-necrotic myofibres, supporting complement-mediated injury. Only rare CD3-positive T cells were seen, so the inflammatory infiltrate was sparse rather than the dense lymphocytic pattern expected in polymyositis. This histology is characteristic of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy: prominent necrosis and regeneration with little lymphocytic invasion. In a seronegative patient, biopsy is especially important because it anchors the diagnosis when antibody testing is unrevealing.

Because she had dysphagia, dysphonia, dyspnoea, and borderline cardiac biomarker abnormalities, the clinicians also looked for extramuscular involvement. Dysphagia in myositis suggests involvement of the striated muscles of swallowing and often signals more severe disease. Her elevated high-sensitivity troponin and BNP justified cardiac evaluation, since myocarditis or myocardial injury can occur in inflammatory myopathies. Echocardiography showed preserved systolic function, while cardiac MRI showed mild regional hypokinesis and myocardial oedema without definite late gadolinium enhancement. That pattern was suggestive but not diagnostic of myocarditis or stress-related myocardial injury; importantly, there was no clear scar or infarction. The cardiac findings therefore required caution in interpretation rather than overcalling definite myocardial disease.

Repeat abdominal imaging and liver biopsy were also diagnostically useful. They showed macrovesicular steatosis with little portal inflammation and no interface hepatitis, confirming persistent steatotic liver disease rather than autoimmune hepatitis. Abdominal ultrasound, Doppler imaging, and MRI showed hepatosteatosis and hepatomegaly without a mass or lymphadenopathy, which was reassuring against another occult hepatic or malignant process. This was important because chronic liver disease could have distracted attention from the muscle source of the abnormal enzymes. Overall, the diagnosis of IMNM rested on the combination of progressive proximal weakness, CK elevation, supportive MRI, exclusion of common mimics, and biopsy-proven necrotising myopathy despite negative myositis antibodies.

## Clinical Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis: Why This Was Seronegative IMNM Probably Triggered by Semaglutide

This patient’s presentation should first be recognized as a myopathic syndrome rather than a primary neurologic or rheumatologic disorder. Progressive proximal weakness, inability to rise or sit upright, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a creatine kinase elevation into the thousands together point to active skeletal muscle injury. In that setting, the initial differential includes inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), polymyositis, and dermatomyositis; toxic or drug-induced rhabdomyolysis; endocrine myopathy; infectious myositis; hereditary/metabolic myopathies; and less likely critical-illness myopathy or a motor neuron process. The bulbar symptoms and the degree of functional decline signaled a severe process and justified urgent inpatient evaluation.

The main diagnostic challenge was distinguishing IMNM from a direct toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis related to semaglutide. In acute toxic rhabdomyolysis, muscle injury generally appears close to the exposure, CK rises sharply, and improvement is expected after stopping the offending agent plus hydration. This patient’s course was different. Her first semaglutide dose caused a dramatic gastrointestinal and volume-depletion illness with acute kidney injury, and a mild CK elevation was seen at that time, but the later neuromuscular syndrome evolved over months after she was already off the drug. The progressive weakness, bulbar involvement, and rising CK over four months are not typical of a self-limited toxic rhabdomyolysis. Her need for pulse corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin also argues against a purely toxic process. This distinction is important because a previously reported GLP-1-related myotoxicity case with tirzepatide behaved like classic rhabdomyolysis: very high CK at presentation and rapid biochemical improvement after drug withdrawal and hydration, without immunosuppression.

The muscle biopsy was the key evidence supporting IMNM. IMNM is characterized histologically by necrotic and regenerating myofibers with relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation; macrophages are often present as they clear necrotic muscle, and complement activation may be demonstrable. That is essentially what this biopsy showed: necrotic and regenerating fibers, myophagocytosis with CD68-positive macrophages, membrane attack complex staining, and only rare CD3-positive cells. This pattern is much more consistent with necrotising autoimmune myopathy than with polymyositis, which classically shows endomysial CD8-predominant inflammation, or dermatomyositis, which typically shows perifascicular atrophy, perivascular/perifascicular inflammation, and usually a compatible rash. The MRI findings of symmetric proximal muscle edema also supported an active inflammatory myopathy.

Statin-associated IMNM was an important alternative because it is the best-known trigger for anti-HMGCR myopathy, but it was not supported here. The patient denied statin exposure, did not use red yeast rice or other relevant supplements, and her myositis panel was negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies. A negative HMGCR result makes statin-associated disease less likely, though it does not exclude IMNM overall. In the same way, the negative SRP result does not rule out IMNM, because a substantial subset of cases are seronegative. That is why the diagnosis in this case rests on the clinical phenotype plus biopsy, not on autoantibody positivity alone.

Other causes of proximal weakness were considered and were less convincing. Hypothyroid myopathy was unlikely because the thyroid-stimulating hormone was normal. Her long-standing transaminase elevation could have raised concern for muscle-derived AST/ALT, but liver disease had been documented years before semaglutide, liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis, and the aminotransferases remained abnormal even after the CK normalized. That makes chronic liver disease the more plausible explanation for the transaminitis. Critical-illness myopathy also fit poorly, because the weakness progressed after discharge over months rather than during prolonged intensive care exposure. Her history did not suggest a hereditary or exertional rhabdomyolysis syndrome, and there was no compelling evidence for infectious myositis or another systemic inflammatory condition.

Her cardiac abnormalities required interpretation in context. Mild troponin and BNP elevations, with cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema and transient hypokinesis but no definite late gadolinium enhancement, suggested either mild myocardial inflammation or stress-related dysfunction, but not a definitive separate cardiomyopathy. Because the abnormalities later normalized, they were treated as part of the systemic illness rather than as the primary explanation for her weakness. Similarly, the absence of interstitial lung disease, rash, synovitis, sicca features, or cytopenias made a broader connective tissue disease much less likely.

Importantly, seronegative IMNM is a valid diagnosis. About one-third of IMNM cases lack the usual autoantibodies, so negative standard myositis serologies do not exclude the disease. In seronegative patients, the combination of proximal weakness, elevated CK, necrotising biopsy, and response to immunosuppression becomes especially important. Her sustained improvement after corticosteroids and IVIG, along with CK normalization and recovery after semaglutide avoidance, further supported an immune-mediated process rather than an isolated toxic injury.

The association with semaglutide must be described as probable, not proven. Using WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre logic, the case had a reasonable time relationship to exposure, no better alternative cause was found, and there was a clinically plausible response after withdrawal and immunosuppression. However, causality cannot be certain because there was no pre-semaglutide CK to exclude smoldering disease, and rechallenge would have been unsafe and unethical. For that reason, the most accurate interpretation is that semaglutide was a probable trigger or contributor to a seronegative IMNM in a susceptible patient, rather than a definitively proven cause.

## Treatment, Recovery, and Practice Implications

This patient’s treatment was appropriately escalated because she presented with severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM): she had marked proximal weakness, difficulty swallowing, dysphonia, and a substantially elevated creatine kinase (CK), all of which suggest active muscle injury with potential for rapid functional decline. In this setting, initial therapy with pulse-dose intravenous methylprednisolone followed by high-dose oral prednisone is standard practice because glucocorticoids provide prompt broad suppression of the immune-mediated muscle inflammation. Her clinical severity and bulbar symptoms also justified inpatient monitoring rather than outpatient treatment, since dysphagia in IMNM raises concern for aspiration and, in more advanced cases, respiratory compromise.

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added because she remained significantly weak after steroids and had severe features. In IMNM, IVIG is commonly used when disease is severe, when there is prominent bulbar involvement, or when a faster response is needed than steroids alone can provide. In this case, IVIG was given as a one-time induction course, and the combination of corticosteroids plus IVIG was followed by a clear biochemical response, with CK falling markedly over about 2 weeks and strength improving enough to permit discharge and later outpatient recovery.

Supportive care was an important part of her management, even though it is often less visible than immunosuppression. Her dysphagia and dysphonia required attention to swallowing safety and close monitoring for any deterioration in breathing or ability to protect the airway. Her profound weakness also meant that mobility support and rehabilitation planning were necessary to prevent deconditioning and help her regain independence. Because transaminases can rise from muscle injury, serial CK and clinical strength testing were more useful than liver enzymes alone for following treatment response in this case.

Her recovery was tracked by both laboratory and functional improvement. The most reassuring markers were normalization of CK and steady improvement in proximal strength, which paralleled her return to baseline activities. Once her CK remained normal and strength continued to improve, prednisone was tapered relatively quickly rather than according to a fixed long-term schedule. This is an important management point: tapering in IMNM is guided by sustained remission, not by an arbitrary time frame, and the goal is to reduce steroid exposure once the disease is clearly controlled. She did not require a steroid-sparing agent or repeat IVIG because the disease remained quiescent after the initial induction treatment and semaglutide discontinuation. In other words, her course behaved more like a drug-associated inflammatory episode that settled after withdrawal of the suspected trigger than like classic idiopathic IMNM, which often needs prolonged immunosuppression.

Her follow-up course also illustrates why these patients need ongoing vigilance for complications of treatment. About 2 weeks after discharge, she developed septic shock from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection while still in the period of recent immunosuppression. This kind of event is a reminder that high-dose steroids and IVIG may be necessary to control severe myositis, but they also increase infection risk, so clinicians should maintain a low threshold to evaluate fever, weakness, hemodynamic instability, or new laboratory abnormalities during recovery. She also had initial cardiac biomarker elevation and imaging abnormalities concerning for possible myocarditis or stress-related myocardial dysfunction, but these resolved on follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI, and her atrial fibrillation did not recur. For learners, this underscores that systemic inflammatory presentations can have overlapping cardiac findings that warrant evaluation when biomarkers are abnormal, even if the final cardiac diagnosis remains uncertain.

By 10 months after presentation, she had regained baseline functional status and had been off immunosuppression for several months with continued normal CK. She also continued to avoid GLP-1 receptor agonists. The practical implication of this case is not that semaglutide has been proven to cause IMNM, but that clinicians should recognize a plausible temporal association when progressive weakness and CK elevation follow GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure and persist after the drug is stopped. In that situation, the differential should extend beyond simple rhabdomyolysis to include autoimmune necrotising myopathy, because delayed recognition can postpone immunosuppressive treatment. As use of semaglutide and related agents expands, suspected cases should be documented and reported to pharmacovigilance systems, and patients with a concerning prior reaction should be counseled to avoid rechallenge unless there is compelling reason to reconsider and the risk-benefit balance is carefully reviewed.
