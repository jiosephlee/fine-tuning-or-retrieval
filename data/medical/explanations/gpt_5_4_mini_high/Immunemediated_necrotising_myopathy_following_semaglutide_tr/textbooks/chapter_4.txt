Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 4: ## Clinical Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis: Why This Was Seronegative IMNM Probably Triggered by Semaglutide

This patient’s presentation should first be recognized as a myopathic syndrome rather than a primary neurologic or rheumatologic disorder. Progressive proximal weakness, inability to rise or sit upright, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a creatine kinase elevation into the thousands together point to active skeletal muscle injury. In that setting, the initial differential includes inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), polymyositis, and dermatomyositis; toxic or drug-induced rhabdomyolysis; endocrine myopathy; infectious myositis; hereditary/metabolic myopathies; and less likely critical-illness myopathy or a motor neuron process. The bulbar symptoms and the degree of functional decline signaled a severe process and justified urgent inpatient evaluation.

The main diagnostic challenge was distinguishing IMNM from a direct toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis related to semaglutide. In acute toxic rhabdomyolysis, muscle injury generally appears close to the exposure, CK rises sharply, and improvement is expected after stopping the offending agent plus hydration. This patient’s course was different. Her first semaglutide dose caused a dramatic gastrointestinal and volume-depletion illness with acute kidney injury, and a mild CK elevation was seen at that time, but the later neuromuscular syndrome evolved over months after she was already off the drug. The progressive weakness, bulbar involvement, and rising CK over four months are not typical of a self-limited toxic rhabdomyolysis. Her need for pulse corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin also argues against a purely toxic process. This distinction is important because a previously reported GLP-1-related myotoxicity case with tirzepatide behaved like classic rhabdomyolysis: very high CK at presentation and rapid biochemical improvement after drug withdrawal and hydration, without immunosuppression.

The muscle biopsy was the key evidence supporting IMNM. IMNM is characterized histologically by necrotic and regenerating myofibers with relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation; macrophages are often present as they clear necrotic muscle, and complement activation may be demonstrable. That is essentially what this biopsy showed: necrotic and regenerating fibers, myophagocytosis with CD68-positive macrophages, membrane attack complex staining, and only rare CD3-positive cells. This pattern is much more consistent with necrotising autoimmune myopathy than with polymyositis, which classically shows endomysial CD8-predominant inflammation, or dermatomyositis, which typically shows perifascicular atrophy, perivascular/perifascicular inflammation, and usually a compatible rash. The MRI findings of symmetric proximal muscle edema also supported an active inflammatory myopathy.

Statin-associated IMNM was an important alternative because it is the best-known trigger for anti-HMGCR myopathy, but it was not supported here. The patient denied statin exposure, did not use red yeast rice or other relevant supplements, and her myositis panel was negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies. A negative HMGCR result makes statin-associated disease less likely, though it does not exclude IMNM overall. In the same way, the negative SRP result does not rule out IMNM, because a substantial subset of cases are seronegative. That is why the diagnosis in this case rests on the clinical phenotype plus biopsy, not on autoantibody positivity alone.

Other causes of proximal weakness were considered and were less convincing. Hypothyroid myopathy was unlikely because the thyroid-stimulating hormone was normal. Her long-standing transaminase elevation could have raised concern for muscle-derived AST/ALT, but liver disease had been documented years before semaglutide, liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis, and the aminotransferases remained abnormal even after the CK normalized. That makes chronic liver disease the more plausible explanation for the transaminitis. Critical-illness myopathy also fit poorly, because the weakness progressed after discharge over months rather than during prolonged intensive care exposure. Her history did not suggest a hereditary or exertional rhabdomyolysis syndrome, and there was no compelling evidence for infectious myositis or another systemic inflammatory condition.

Her cardiac abnormalities required interpretation in context. Mild troponin and BNP elevations, with cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema and transient hypokinesis but no definite late gadolinium enhancement, suggested either mild myocardial inflammation or stress-related dysfunction, but not a definitive separate cardiomyopathy. Because the abnormalities later normalized, they were treated as part of the systemic illness rather than as the primary explanation for her weakness. Similarly, the absence of interstitial lung disease, rash, synovitis, sicca features, or cytopenias made a broader connective tissue disease much less likely.

Importantly, seronegative IMNM is a valid diagnosis. About one-third of IMNM cases lack the usual autoantibodies, so negative standard myositis serologies do not exclude the disease. In seronegative patients, the combination of proximal weakness, elevated CK, necrotising biopsy, and response to immunosuppression becomes especially important. Her sustained improvement after corticosteroids and IVIG, along with CK normalization and recovery after semaglutide avoidance, further supported an immune-mediated process rather than an isolated toxic injury.

The association with semaglutide must be described as probable, not proven. Using WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre logic, the case had a reasonable time relationship to exposure, no better alternative cause was found, and there was a clinically plausible response after withdrawal and immunosuppression. However, causality cannot be certain because there was no pre-semaglutide CK to exclude smoldering disease, and rechallenge would have been unsafe and unethical. For that reason, the most accurate interpretation is that semaglutide was a probable trigger or contributor to a seronegative IMNM in a susceptible patient, rather than a definitively proven cause.
