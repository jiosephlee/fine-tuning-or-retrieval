Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 5: ## Treatment, Recovery, and Practice Implications

This patient’s treatment was appropriately escalated because she presented with severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM): she had marked proximal weakness, difficulty swallowing, dysphonia, and a substantially elevated creatine kinase (CK), all of which suggest active muscle injury with potential for rapid functional decline. In this setting, initial therapy with pulse-dose intravenous methylprednisolone followed by high-dose oral prednisone is standard practice because glucocorticoids provide prompt broad suppression of the immune-mediated muscle inflammation. Her clinical severity and bulbar symptoms also justified inpatient monitoring rather than outpatient treatment, since dysphagia in IMNM raises concern for aspiration and, in more advanced cases, respiratory compromise.

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added because she remained significantly weak after steroids and had severe features. In IMNM, IVIG is commonly used when disease is severe, when there is prominent bulbar involvement, or when a faster response is needed than steroids alone can provide. In this case, IVIG was given as a one-time induction course, and the combination of corticosteroids plus IVIG was followed by a clear biochemical response, with CK falling markedly over about 2 weeks and strength improving enough to permit discharge and later outpatient recovery.

Supportive care was an important part of her management, even though it is often less visible than immunosuppression. Her dysphagia and dysphonia required attention to swallowing safety and close monitoring for any deterioration in breathing or ability to protect the airway. Her profound weakness also meant that mobility support and rehabilitation planning were necessary to prevent deconditioning and help her regain independence. Because transaminases can rise from muscle injury, serial CK and clinical strength testing were more useful than liver enzymes alone for following treatment response in this case.

Her recovery was tracked by both laboratory and functional improvement. The most reassuring markers were normalization of CK and steady improvement in proximal strength, which paralleled her return to baseline activities. Once her CK remained normal and strength continued to improve, prednisone was tapered relatively quickly rather than according to a fixed long-term schedule. This is an important management point: tapering in IMNM is guided by sustained remission, not by an arbitrary time frame, and the goal is to reduce steroid exposure once the disease is clearly controlled. She did not require a steroid-sparing agent or repeat IVIG because the disease remained quiescent after the initial induction treatment and semaglutide discontinuation. In other words, her course behaved more like a drug-associated inflammatory episode that settled after withdrawal of the suspected trigger than like classic idiopathic IMNM, which often needs prolonged immunosuppression.

Her follow-up course also illustrates why these patients need ongoing vigilance for complications of treatment. About 2 weeks after discharge, she developed septic shock from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection while still in the period of recent immunosuppression. This kind of event is a reminder that high-dose steroids and IVIG may be necessary to control severe myositis, but they also increase infection risk, so clinicians should maintain a low threshold to evaluate fever, weakness, hemodynamic instability, or new laboratory abnormalities during recovery. She also had initial cardiac biomarker elevation and imaging abnormalities concerning for possible myocarditis or stress-related myocardial dysfunction, but these resolved on follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI, and her atrial fibrillation did not recur. For learners, this underscores that systemic inflammatory presentations can have overlapping cardiac findings that warrant evaluation when biomarkers are abnormal, even if the final cardiac diagnosis remains uncertain.

By 10 months after presentation, she had regained baseline functional status and had been off immunosuppression for several months with continued normal CK. She also continued to avoid GLP-1 receptor agonists. The practical implication of this case is not that semaglutide has been proven to cause IMNM, but that clinicians should recognize a plausible temporal association when progressive weakness and CK elevation follow GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure and persist after the drug is stopped. In that situation, the differential should extend beyond simple rhabdomyolysis to include autoimmune necrotising myopathy, because delayed recognition can postpone immunosuppressive treatment. As use of semaglutide and related agents expands, suspected cases should be documented and reported to pharmacovigilance systems, and patients with a concerning prior reaction should be counseled to avoid rechallenge unless there is compelling reason to reconsider and the risk-benefit balance is carefully reviewed.
