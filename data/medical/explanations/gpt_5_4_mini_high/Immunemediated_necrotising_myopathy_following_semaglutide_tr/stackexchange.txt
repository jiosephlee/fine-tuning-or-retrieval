Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a woman in her 60s with 4 months of progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a creatine kinase level near 4000 U/L after exposure to semaglutide, what is the focused differential diagnosis for severe acquired myopathy, and which features would make immune-mediated necrotising myopathy more likely than toxic rhabdomyolysis, polymyositis, dermatomyositis, hypothyroid myopathy, or inclusion body myositis?

In a patient like this, the focused differential for **severe acquired myopathy with CK in the thousands** should center on **toxic muscle injury, inflammatory myopathy, and endocrine myopathy**. The key bedside question is whether the course is **acute and self-limited** or **progressive and immune-mediated**.

**The main possibilities are:**
- **Toxic rhabdomyolysis** from a drug or metabolic insult
- **Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)**
- **Polymyositis**
- **Dermatomyositis**
- **Hypothyroid myopathy**
- **Inclusion body myositis (IBM)**

In this case, several features make **IMNM** more likely than the others:

1. **Subacute, progressive course over 4 months**  
   IMNM typically causes **rapidly progressive proximal weakness** that continues even after the inciting exposure has stopped. That pattern is different from **toxic rhabdomyolysis**, which is usually **acute**, often with myalgias or dark urine, and improves quickly with drug withdrawal and hydration.

2. **Marked proximal weakness with bulbar involvement**  
   Her **proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia** fit a severe inflammatory myopathy. Bulbar symptoms can occur in IMNM, especially in more aggressive disease.

3. **CK in the thousands but not a classic rhabdomyolysis picture**  
   CK around 4000 U/L is certainly elevated, but the important clue is the **persistent rise and ongoing weakness over time**, rather than a single acute spike with rapid normalization.

4. **No rash, no arthritis, no interstitial lung disease, and no thyroid abnormality**  
   That makes **dermatomyositis** and **hypothyroid myopathy** less likely. Dermatomyositis usually has **cutaneous findings** and biopsy features of **perifascicular injury** rather than pure necrosis.

5. **Pattern not typical for inclusion body myositis**  
   IBM is usually **slowly progressive**, often **asymmetric**, and classically affects **finger flexors and quadriceps**; CK is often only modestly elevated. Dysphagia can occur, but the overall pattern here—**symmetric proximal weakness with CK nearly 4000 and biopsy-proven necrotizing myopathy**—is much more consistent with IMNM.

6. **Muscle biopsy is the diagnostic pivot**  
   The biopsy showing **necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition, and only rare lymphocytes** is the hallmark of **necrotizing myopathy**. That histology strongly favors **IMNM** over polymyositis, which should show **endomysial CD8+ T-cell invasion of non-necrotic fibers**, and over dermatomyositis, which shows **perifascicular atrophy and complement-mediated microangiopathy**.

7. **Response to immunosuppression rather than supportive care alone**  
   Her improvement with **high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG**, along with sustained normalization of CK after stopping semaglutide, supports an immune process. Toxic rhabdomyolysis would be expected to improve primarily with **removal of the toxin and hydration**, not IVIG.

So, the teaching point is this: **progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms, persistently elevated CK, and a necrotizing biopsy with minimal lymphocytic inflammation should prompt strong consideration of IMNM**, even when an exposure raises the possibility of drug toxicity. In this case, the **course, biopsy, and steroid/IVIG responsiveness** argue much more for **immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy** than for rhabdomyolysis, polymyositis, dermatomyositis, hypothyroid myopathy, or inclusion body myositis.

### [Clinical Pearls] What aspects of this patient’s clinical course argue for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy rather than a direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from semaglutide alone?

Several features of her course favor **immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)** over a simple, direct **semaglutide-induced rhabdomyolysis**:

First, the **time course was wrong for toxic rhabdomyolysis**. Her initial semaglutide reaction was acute gastrointestinal intolerance with dehydration and acute kidney injury, and her CK at that time was only mildly elevated (337 U/L). In direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, CK typically rises **acutely during exposure** and then begins to fall promptly after the drug is stopped and hydration is given. Instead, she **continued to worsen over 4 months after a single dose**, with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK that rose to nearly 4000 U/L.

Second, the **clinical phenotype was that of inflammatory myopathy**, not isolated muscle breakdown. She developed **symmetric proximal weakness** with bulbar involvement, which is characteristic of IMNM. She did not have the typical rhabdomyolysis clues of severe myalgias, cola-colored urine, or a brief self-limited course.

Third, the **muscle biopsy was diagnostic of a necrotising autoimmune process**: dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex deposition, with only scant lymphocytic inflammation. That histology is much more consistent with IMNM than with nonspecific toxic myonecrosis.

Fourth, her **response to treatment** supports an immune mechanism. She improved with **pulse corticosteroids and IVIG**, and her CK normalized over weeks with sustained recovery off immunosuppression. A direct toxic rhabdomyolysis would be expected to improve mainly with drug withdrawal and supportive care, not to require immunotherapy.

So the key teaching point is that **persistent, progressive weakness and CK elevation after the offending drug has been stopped, plus biopsy showing necrotising myopathy, argue strongly for IMNM** rather than straightforward semaglutide-related rhabdomyolysis.

### [Pathophysiology] Which autoantibodies are classically associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and why does a negative anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP panel not exclude the diagnosis in a patient with compatible weakness, creatine kinase elevation, and biopsy findings?

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy is classically associated with **anti-HMGCR** and **anti-SRP** autoantibodies. Anti-HMGCR is the subtype most often linked to prior statin exposure, whereas anti-SRP is also strongly associated with a severe necrotizing phenotype and marked CK elevation.

A **negative anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP panel does not exclude IMNM** because IMNM is a **clinicopathologic diagnosis**, not a purely serologic one. As the case report notes, **about one-third of patients are seronegative**. In those patients, the diagnosis rests on the combination of **compatible proximal weakness, elevated CK, and biopsy findings**—namely **necrotic and regenerating myofibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation**, often with complement membrane attack complex deposition, exactly as seen in this patient’s muscle biopsy. Negative antibodies may reflect **assay limitations** or **autoantibody targets that have not yet been identified**. Thus, when the phenotype and histology fit, seronegativity should not delay treatment.

### [Pathophysiology] What muscle biopsy findings are most characteristic of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and how do necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage-predominant myophagocytosis and sparse lymphocytic infiltrates help distinguish it from other inflammatory myopathies?

The muscle biopsy most characteristic of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) shows **dispersed necrotic and regenerating myofibers with prominent myophagocytosis by macrophages and only sparse lymphocytic inflammation**. In the case you provided, the biopsy of the vastus lateralis demonstrated exactly this pattern: **necrotic and regenerating fibers, CD68-positive macrophages engulfing damaged fibers, membrane attack complex staining on necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, and only rare CD3-positive T cells**.

This histology is important because IMNM is defined by **myofiber injury out of proportion to inflammation**. The dominant macrophage infiltrate reflects cleanup of necrotic fibers rather than a dense T-cell–mediated attack. That helps distinguish IMNM from other inflammatory myopathies:

- **Polymyositis** typically shows **endomyosial CD8-positive T cells invading non-necrotic fibers**.
- **Dermatomyositis** more often shows **perivascular/perimysial inflammation, perifascicular atrophy, and capillary injury**, rather than widespread necrosis with minimal lymphocytes.
- **Inclusion body myositis** has **endomysial inflammation plus rimmed vacuoles and chronic degenerative changes**.

So, in practice, a biopsy with **necrosis and regeneration, macrophage-predominant myophagocytosis, and scant lymphocytes** strongly points to IMNM, especially when paired with proximal weakness and marked CK elevation, as in this case.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should persistent AST and ALT elevation be interpreted in a patient with both biopsy-proven steatotic liver disease and active myopathy, and what bedside or laboratory clues help distinguish hepatic transaminitis from muscle-derived enzyme elevation?

Persistent AST and ALT elevation in this setting should be interpreted as potentially **mixed, but not assumed to be purely hepatic**. AST and ALT are “transaminases,” not liver-specific enzymes; injured skeletal muscle can release both, especially AST, during active myopathy. In this case, the patient had **biopsy-proven steatotic liver disease years before semaglutide** with a mild ALT-predominant transaminitis, normal bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, and platelets — a pattern that supports a **true hepatic source**. When she later developed immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, the AST/ALT rise likely had a **muscle contribution**, but the fact that her borderline transaminase elevation **persisted after CK normalized** strongly argues that her chronic aminotransferase abnormality was still primarily hepatic rather than due to muscle breakdown.

The practical bedside clues are the **pattern and parallelism**:

- **Suggest muscle-derived elevation** when AST/ALT rise with **proximal weakness, myalgias, dysphagia, or dark urine**, and when they track with **CK** (often with LDH and aldolase also elevated). AST is often greater than ALT, and **bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, GGT, INR, and albumin are usually normal**.
- **Suggest hepatic transaminitis** when the abnormality **predates myopathy**, persists **after CK falls**, or is accompanied by **abnormal liver imaging/biopsy** or cholestatic/synthetic dysfunction.

A useful teaching point is that if the aminotransferases do not move with the CK, or if they remain elevated once the myopathy improves, you should keep looking for a liver source — as happened here, where repeat biopsy confirmed ongoing steatotic liver disease.

### [Morning Report] In an inflammatory myopathy patient with elevated high-sensitivity troponin, markedly elevated BNP, atrial fibrillation, and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema without clear late gadolinium enhancement, how would you assess for possible cardiac involvement and how would the result affect management?

In an inflammatory myopathy, I would take the elevated hs-troponin and markedly elevated BNP seriously, but I would not assume they prove myocarditis. The first step is to determine whether the biomarker pattern reflects true myocardial injury or nonspecific release/strain in the setting of skeletal muscle disease, atrial fibrillation, and acute illness.

Practically, I would assess cardiac involvement with a cardiology co-evaluation, serial ECGs and telemetry, repeat troponin and BNP trends, and transthoracic echocardiography for ventricular function and wall-motion abnormality. Cardiac MRI is the key noninvasive test here: myocardial edema suggests active inflammation, but the absence of clear late gadolinium enhancement argues against established scar or infarction and makes the diagnosis less definitive. If the diagnosis remains uncertain and would change treatment, endomyocardial biopsy is the diagnostic standard, though it is usually reserved for cases with hemodynamic compromise, progressive dysfunction, or persistent diagnostic ambiguity. One important teaching point is that troponin T can be elevated from regenerating skeletal muscle; troponin I is more specific for myocardial injury when available.

In this case, the MRI finding of edema without definite late enhancement, together with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction on echo and later normalization of the cardiac abnormalities, supports possible transient myocardial involvement or stress-related injury rather than definite fulminant myocarditis. That distinction matters for management: confirmed cardiac involvement would prompt closer cardiology follow-up, continued rhythm management for atrial fibrillation, and a lower threshold to intensify immunosuppression and treat any heart-failure physiology. If the cardiac findings are equivocal and the patient is stable, I would continue treatment directed at the inflammatory myopathy itself, monitor closely, and repeat cardiac imaging and biomarkers after treatment, as was done in this case.

### [Management] What initial treatment strategy is appropriate for severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy presenting with marked proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms, and what is the rationale for using pulse methylprednisolone followed by high-dose prednisone and IVIG in this case?

In **severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)** with **profound proximal weakness and bulbar involvement** such as dysphagia and dysphonia, the appropriate initial strategy is **prompt, aggressive immunosuppression** rather than watchful waiting. In practice, that means:

1. **Pulse IV methylprednisolone** for rapid disease control,  
2. Transition to **high-dose oral prednisone** (about **1 mg/kg/day**) to maintain suppression, and  
3. **Early IVIG** (typically **2 g/kg over 2–5 days**) when weakness is severe, bulbar function is impaired, or a rapid response is needed.

The rationale is that IMNM is an **immune-driven necrotizing myopathy**, and in severe presentations the priority is to **halt ongoing muscle injury quickly** and prevent aspiration, respiratory decline, and further functional loss. **Pulse steroids** provide immediate anti-inflammatory effect; **oral prednisone** sustains that effect after the pulse. **IVIG** is often added early because severe IMNM may respond incompletely or too slowly to glucocorticoids alone, and IVIG can produce a **faster clinical and CK improvement**, especially when there is marked weakness or swallowing difficulty.

That is exactly the logic in this case: the patient had **rapidly progressive, disabling proximal weakness with dysphagia/dysphonia and CK in the thousands**, so she was treated with **IV methylprednisolone followed by high-dose prednisone plus IVIG**, with subsequent improvement in strength and normalization of CK.

### [Morning Report] This patient improved after withdrawal of semaglutide and a relatively short course of immunosuppression, then remained in remission off therapy. How does that outcome inform your thinking about drug-triggered versus idiopathic immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and why would rechallenge with a GLP-1 receptor agonist be inappropriate?

Her course makes a **drug-triggered immune-mediated necrotising myopathy more plausible than classic idiopathic IMNM**, though it does not prove causality. In idiopathic IMNM, the autoimmune process is often **persistent and treatment-intensive**: patients commonly require prolonged immunosuppression, and relapse is frequent when therapy is withdrawn. By contrast, this patient had a clear temporal association with semaglutide exposure, biopsy-proven necrotising myopathy, and then—most importantly—**sustained remission after semaglutide withdrawal and only a short course of corticosteroids plus IVIG**, remaining well and off immunosuppression for months. That pattern argues that semaglutide may have served as a trigger that initiated an immune process that became quiescent once the inciting exposure was removed, which is more consistent with a **drug-associated autoimmune myopathy** than with an ongoing idiopathic one.

A rechallenge with a GLP-1 receptor agonist would be inappropriate because this was a **severe, likely immune-mediated adverse drug reaction**: she developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, marked CK elevation, and biopsy findings consistent with IMNM. Re-exposure could re-activate the same immune pathway and precipitate **more rapid and more severe recurrence**, potentially with bulbar or systemic complications. In pharmacovigilance terms, the dechallenge response is already informative; **rechallenge is not ethically justified when the suspected reaction is serious and potentially disabling**. Moreover, there is no clinical necessity to risk recurrent myopathy when alternative strategies for weight and metabolic control exist.
