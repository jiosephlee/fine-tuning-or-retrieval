Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# AST, ALT, and the Muscle-Liver Trap: Don’t Miss Myopathy in the Patient with “Known Fatty Liver”

## The setup: a liver diagnosis that almost explains too much

It’s very easy to see a patient with obesity, hepatosteatosis, and chronic mild transaminitis and stop thinking. The chart already has an answer: NAFLD. But this case is a good reminder that a liver label can become a diagnostic blindfold.

A woman in her 60s had biopsy-proven fatty liver and mildly elevated AST/ALT for years. Then, after a single semaglutide dose, she developed a cascade of symptoms that were initially easy to anchor on as medication intolerance and deconditioning: nausea, vomiting, AKI, metabolic acidosis, then progressive weakness. Four months later, she came back unable to stand or sit upright without help, with dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK near 4000. Muscle biopsy ultimately showed immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and she improved with corticosteroids and IVIG.

The big teaching point isn’t semaglutide itself. It’s this: **pre-existing fatty liver should never stop you from asking whether AST/ALT are coming from muscle.**

## AST and ALT are not “liver-only” numbers

We all learn that AST and ALT are aminotransferases, but in practice they often get treated like liver-specific biomarkers. They aren’t.

Skeletal muscle injury can raise both AST and ALT, sometimes substantially. AST is especially nonspecific, but ALT can also rise with myopathy, inflammatory myositis, rhabdomyolysis, and other muscle injury states. That means a patient with weakness and “transaminitis” may actually have a muscle problem first and a liver problem second — or both.

In this case, that distinction mattered because she already carried a diagnosis of NAFLD. Her earlier AST/ALT elevations were plausibly hepatic, and her repeat liver biopsy did show steatosis and progression of fatty liver. But when she later developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a marked CK rise, the liver story could no longer explain the whole picture.

## When transaminitis is really a clue to myopathy

There are a few practical clues that should make you widen the differential beyond hepatitis, fatty liver, or “medication side effect”:

- **Weakness out of proportion to liver disease**
- **Bulbar symptoms** such as dysphagia or dysphonia
- **CK elevation** alongside AST/ALT elevation
- **Normal bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and often GGT**
- **A temporal relationship with a drug exposure** that persists after the drug is stopped
- **Failure of AST/ALT and CK to behave like a simple toxic injury**

In muscle-driven aminotransferase elevation, the AST/ALT trend often parallels the CK trend, at least initially. If the CK improves but AST/ALT remain mildly elevated, that may point back toward underlying liver disease — which is exactly what happened here. Her longstanding transaminase abnormalities were probably hepatic, while the later CK rise and weakness reflected a separate muscle process.

That’s the trap: **a patient can have both NAFLD and myositis.** One diagnosis does not cancel out the other.

## Why this case should make you think beyond “toxic myopathy”

The clinical course here is what pushed the diagnosis away from simple rhabdomyolysis. She had an immediate intolerance-type reaction to semaglutide with vomiting, AKI, and a mild CK elevation during the initial hospitalization. But then, while off semaglutide, she continued to worsen over months, with a 12-fold rise in CK and progressive proximal weakness plus dysphagia/dysphonia.

That pattern is much more compatible with an immune-mediated myopathy than with direct drug toxicity. In toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis, you expect the CK to start falling after the offending agent is removed and hydration is provided. Here, the process kept evolving.

Her muscle MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema, and biopsy demonstrated necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — all supportive of IMNM. Serologies were negative for HMGCR and SRP, which does not exclude IMNM, since a substantial minority of cases are seronegative.

## The practical bedside lesson: don’t let NAFLD become a stopping rule

This case is especially relevant because NAFLD is so common. Once a patient has “fatty liver” in the chart, future AST/ALT abnormalities often get attributed to the same bucket without a fresh look. That is how myopathy gets missed.

If a patient with known NAFLD develops any combination of weakness, fatigue that sounds neuromuscular, dysphagia, dark urine, myalgias, or unexplained functional decline, check a CK early. Don’t wait for the liver workup to loop around again. If AST/ALT are elevated and the patient can’t climb stairs, rise from a chair, or swallow normally, muscle has to be on the table.

Also remember the reverse: a normal CK doesn’t always exclude muscle disease if the story is compelling, but a rising CK makes the case much harder to ignore. In this patient, the CK was the clue that forced the right workup.

## A note on the semaglutide association

The report frames semaglutide as a probable contributor to IMNM, but causality is not proven. That matters. The educational point is not “GLP-1 receptor agonists cause myositis.” The point is that a drug exposure can create diagnostic distraction, and a seemingly routine metabolic-liver patient can actually be evolving a serious autoimmune myopathy.

Whether semaglutide was trigger, contributor, or coincidence, the clinical lesson stands: **when the weakness keeps progressing after the drug is stopped, think autoimmune, not just toxic.**

## Take-home points

- AST and ALT are not liver-specific; skeletal muscle injury can raise both.
- In a patient with weakness, dysphagia, or falling function, always think about muscle as a source of “transaminitis.”
- Known NAFLD should not be used to explain away new neuromuscular symptoms or a rising CK.
- A CK that keeps climbing after drug withdrawal argues against simple toxic myopathy and should prompt evaluation for IMNM or other inflammatory myopathies.
- If the liver story and the muscle story don’t match, keep both in play — patients can have fatty liver and myopathy at the same time.
