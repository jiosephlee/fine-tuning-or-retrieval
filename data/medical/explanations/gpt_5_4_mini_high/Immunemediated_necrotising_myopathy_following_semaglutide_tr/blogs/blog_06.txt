Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# One Dose, Four Months Later: Pharmacovigilance Lessons from a Possible Semaglutide-Triggered Autoimmune Myopathy

## Why this case matters

Semaglutide has become a household name in obesity and diabetes care, and for good reason: it works, it’s widely used, and most of the adverse effects we see are the expected GI kind. That’s what makes this case so striking. A woman in her 60s received a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide, had an immediate severe GI illness with AKI and metabolic derangements, and then—after seeming to recover—progressed over four months to profound proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and CK elevation into the thousands. Muscle biopsy ultimately supported immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and she improved with corticosteroids, IVIG, and avoidance of GLP-1 receptor agonists.

This is not a “semaglutide causes myopathy” case. It is a reminder that when drugs become ubiquitous, rare serious adverse events will eventually appear in temporal proximity. The challenge is deciding when a temporal association deserves a pharmacovigilance alarm.

## The clinical pattern: not just rhabdomyolysis

The first temptation here is to anchor on toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis. That would fit the initial semaglutide-associated hospitalization: nausea, vomiting, poor intake, acute renal failure, severe metabolic acidosis, and a modest CK elevation of 337 U/L. But the later course does not behave like uncomplicated drug toxicity.

Four months later, she had a very different syndrome:

- Progressive proximal weakness
- Dysphagia and dysphonia
- CK 3992 U/L
- MRI with symmetric muscle edema
- Muscle biopsy showing necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage/myophagocytic activity and membrane attack complex staining

That pattern is much more consistent with IMNM than with simple rhabdomyolysis. In toxic muscle injury, CK usually falls after the insult is removed and hydration is provided. In this case, the muscle disease evolved long after the single exposure and required immunosuppression.

A key bedside lesson: if a patient has weakness plus dysphagia/dysphonia and CK elevation that persists or worsens well after the medication exposure, stop thinking “deconditioning” and start thinking inflammatory myopathy.

## Why the biopsy matters

IMNM is a distinct autoimmune myopathy characterized by severe proximal weakness, necrotizing myofibers, and relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation. Anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies are classic associations, but seronegativity is common enough that a negative panel does not exclude the diagnosis.

That matters here because this patient’s myositis panel was negative for HMGCR, SRP, and a broad range of other antibodies. The biopsy carried the diagnosis:

- Dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers
- Myophagocytosis with CD68-positive macrophages
- Membrane attack complex deposition
- Only rare CD3-positive cells

In other words, the tissue looked like immune-mediated muscle destruction, not a bland toxic injury.

Practical pearl: if the phenotype screams inflammatory myopathy, a negative antibody panel should not end the workup.

## Causation versus correlation: what can we really say?

This is where pharmacovigilance gets uncomfortable. The authors call this a probable association, and that is the right level of caution.

The argument for semaglutide as a contributor is:

- Clear temporal relationship
- No statin exposure or other obvious classic trigger
- No convincing alternative connective tissue disease
- CK and weakness progressed after the exposure rather than resolving
- Improvement followed withdrawal of semaglutide plus a short course of immunosuppression

But there are real limitations:

- No CK level before semaglutide, so a smoldering process cannot be fully excluded
- Seronegative IMNM exists, so antibody negativity does not prove drug causation
- The patient had pre-existing transaminase elevation, though liver biopsy and persistent AST/ALT abnormalities suggest NAFLD rather than muscle as the source

This is the central pharmacovigilance lesson: a case report can create a safety signal, but it cannot prove causality.

## How to think about WHO-UMC causality here

The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre framework is a helpful way to discipline our thinking. In this case, the relationship is best viewed as “probable” rather than “certain.”

Why probable?

- Reasonable time relationship
- Other likely causes are not compelling
- There was a clinically reasonable response after withdrawal
- Rechallenge would be unsafe and unethical

That last point matters. In severe suspected drug reactions, rechallenge is often not just unwise—it is morally indefensible. If a patient develops a life-threatening myopathy, we do not need a second exposure to “prove” the point.

A practical way to say it in real life: “This may be drug-related, but we are not claiming proof. We are acting on a plausible safety signal.”

## What makes this different from the tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis report?

The report discusses a prior case of rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide. That comparison is useful because it highlights how differently toxic muscle injury behaves from immune-mediated disease.

The tirzepatide case had:

- Markedly elevated CK at presentation
- Improvement within days of stopping the drug and giving supportive care
- No need for immunosuppression

This semaglutide case behaved differently:

- CK rose over months, not hours to days
- Weakness progressed despite drug cessation
- Biopsy showed autoimmune necrotizing features
- Improvement required steroids and IVIG

That distinction is clinically important. A CK of 3000–4000 in a patient on or after a GLP-1 receptor agonist should not be reflexively labeled “rhabdo” without asking whether the trajectory fits immune myopathy.

## What should clinicians do with a case report like this?

Case reports should change how we think, not how we overreact. They should prompt better surveillance, better counseling, and a lower threshold to investigate suspicious symptoms—not blanket avoidance of an otherwise effective medication in everyone.

In practical terms:

- Ask about new proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, or dark urine after GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure
- If symptoms are present, check CK and liver enzymes, but remember AST/ALT can be muscle-derived
- Don’t let a negative antibody panel stop the evaluation if the phenotype is concerning
- Consider MRI and biopsy when weakness/CK elevation persists or evolves
- If serious immune-mediated toxicity is suspected, document the reaction clearly and avoid rechallenge

Counseling should be nuanced. Patients should know that severe immune myopathy appears rare, causality is not established, and the overall safety profile of semaglutide remains favorable. But clinicians should also recognize that “rare” is not “never,” especially once a drug is used at massive scale.

## A word on transaminases, troponin, and the diagnostic noise

This case also reminds us how messy these evaluations can get.

Her AST/ALT were elevated before semaglutide and remained abnormal even after CK normalized, which supports underlying NAFLD rather than muscle as the primary source. Her troponin and BNP elevations, plus cardiac MRI findings, raised concern for myocardial involvement or stress cardiomyopathy, but the cardiac findings were ultimately inconclusive and later normalized.

The takeaway is not that every biomarker abnormality in myositis means myocarditis. It’s that systemic autoimmune muscle disease can create a lot of diagnostic static, and you need to interpret biomarkers in clinical context.

## Bottom line

This report is best read as a pharmacovigilance signal, not a verdict. A single semaglutide dose was followed by a severe, delayed, biopsy-supported IMNM that improved after drug avoidance and immunosuppression. That sequence is unusual enough to matter, especially in the era of widespread GLP-1 receptor agonist use.

## Take-home points

- Temporal association is not causation, but it is enough to trigger careful scrutiny.
- Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and rising CK after drug exposure should raise concern for IMNM, not just rhabdomyolysis.
- Negative HMGCR/SRP testing does not exclude immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy.
- WHO-UMC criteria can help frame “probable” drug causality when rechallenge is unsafe.
- Rechallenge after a severe suspected immune-mediated adverse reaction is usually unethical.
- Case reports should guide vigilance and counseling, not overstate certainty.
