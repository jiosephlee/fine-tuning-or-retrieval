Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Troponin, BNP, and a Weak Patient: Sorting Cardiac Involvement in Necrotising Myopathy

## Why this case is worth a second look

Most of us are trained to treat a troponin like a siren: hear it, chase it. But in inflammatory myopathy, that reflex can get you into trouble.

This patient had biopsy-proven immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, plus a mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin, a markedly elevated proBNP, a brief episode of atrial fibrillation during her earlier critical illness, and a cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema with mild regional hypokinesis but no definite late gadolinium enhancement. That is exactly the kind of “is this myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or just biomarker noise?” scenario that shows up on the wards and in the ED.

The useful lesson here is not that semaglutide causes myocarditis or that every troponin in myositis is cardiac. It’s that cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathy lives on a spectrum, and the workup has to be matched to the phenotype.

## The cardiac story in this patient

The skeletal muscle diagnosis was clear: progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, CK elevation, MRI muscle edema, and biopsy with necrotic/regenerating fibers, macrophage activity, and membrane attack complex staining.

The heart was murkier.

At the tertiary evaluation, she had:
- High-sensitivity troponin elevation to 62 pg/mL
- proBNP of 4358 pg/mL
- Normal creatinine
- Transthoracic echo with preserved LVEF (65%–70%)
- RVSP 46 mm Hg
- Cardiac MRI with mild mid-anteroseptal/anterior hypokinesis and myocardial edema
- No definite late gadolinium enhancement
- Later normalization of echo and MRI findings

That pattern argues against a straightforward infarct. It also doesn’t give you a clean “yes” for fulminant myocarditis. What it does do is tell you that something transient was happening to the myocardium, or at least to the biomarkers we use to interrogate it.

## Troponin in myositis: useful, but only if you know what you’re measuring

One of the biggest traps in myositis is assuming all troponins mean the same thing.

In inflammatory muscle disease, troponin interpretation depends heavily on the assay. Troponin T can be less specific in skeletal muscle disorders, whereas troponin I is generally more cardiac-specific. If the assay isn’t specified, you have to be cautious about overcalling myocardial injury.

In this case, the troponin was only modestly elevated. That’s not the pattern of a large infarct. It may reflect:
- Mild myocardial involvement
- Physiologic stress
- Atrial fibrillation-related demand
- Systemic inflammation
- Or a combination of all of the above

The key point: a small troponin rise in a patient with severe systemic illness and myositis should trigger thoughtful cardiac evaluation, not automatic ACS labeling.

## BNP is a wall-stress biomarker, not a heart-failure diagnosis

The proBNP here was strikingly elevated, but BNP is not a synonym for decompensated heart failure.

BNP rises with:
- Myocardial stretch
- Atrial fibrillation
- Pulmonary hypertension or elevated right-sided pressures
- Renal dysfunction
- Critical illness
- Sepsis or inflammatory stress

This patient had several possible BNP drivers. She had a recent severe hospitalization, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and mildly elevated RVSP on echo. Her BNP is therefore best interpreted as a marker of cardiac stress, not proof of overt pump failure.

That distinction matters because a preserved EF does not exclude myocardial injury, but it also means you should not anchor on “heart failure” just because the BNP is high.

## Myocarditis versus stress cardiomyopathy versus nonspecific leak

This is the real diagnostic fork in the road.

### Myocarditis
Myocarditis becomes more likely when you have:
- Troponin elevation
- New arrhythmia or conduction abnormality
- Symptoms out of proportion to skeletal weakness
- Cardiac MRI abnormalities consistent with inflammation
- Sometimes reduced EF, though not always

In this patient, CMR showed myocardial edema and mild hypokinesis, which keeps myocarditis on the table. But the lack of definite late gadolinium enhancement and the later normalization make this less than definitive.

### Stress cardiomyopathy
Stress cardiomyopathy is a very reasonable alternative here.

She had a major physiologic insult: severe GI symptoms, dehydration, AKI, acidosis, ICU-level care, then progressive systemic inflammatory disease. Stress cardiomyopathy can present with:
- Modest troponin elevation
- Elevated BNP
- Regional wall motion abnormalities
- Myocardial edema on MRI
- Recovery over time

That fits the trajectory here surprisingly well, especially given the normalization on follow-up imaging.

### Nonspecific biomarker leak
Sometimes the right answer is not a named cardiomyopathy at all. Troponin and BNP can leak in systemic illness without a primary cardiac syndrome. In a patient with severe inflammatory myopathy, that possibility has to stay open unless the clinical picture becomes more convincing.

## Why the normal EF doesn’t close the case

A normal EF is reassuring, but it doesn’t rule out early or mild myocardial involvement.

Echo is a blunt tool. It tells you about pump function and gross structure, not the whole inflammatory story. Cardiac MRI is better for tissue characterization, and in this case it gave you the clue that the myocardium was involved somehow, even if the exact label remained uncertain.

The later normalization of echo and MRI is important. It supports a transient process rather than established cardiomyopathy or infarction. That makes myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or inflammatory myocardial injury more plausible than a chronic structural problem.

## The rhythm piece: atrial fibrillation may be the messenger, not the diagnosis

Her paroxysmal atrial fibrillation occurred during the earlier hospitalization for semaglutide-related GI toxicity, AKI, and metabolic derangement. That timing matters.

AF in this setting may reflect:
- Acute physiologic stress
- Volume shifts
- Electrolyte/metabolic disturbance
- Atrial stretch
- Systemic inflammation

It may also be a clue to transient myocardial irritation, but AF alone does not prove myocarditis. In many sick patients, AF is the smoke rather than the fire.

## A practical approach when the myositis patient has abnormal cardiac biomarkers

If you’re caring for a patient with inflammatory myopathy and an elevated troponin or BNP, a sensible approach is:

1. Confirm the assay and trend it.
   - If you can get troponin I rather than T, that may help interpretation in myositis.

2. Look for physiology, not just numbers.
   - Hemodynamics, oxygenation, volume status, ECG changes, arrhythmia, chest symptoms, and signs of shock matter more than a single biomarker.

3. Get an echo early if biomarkers are abnormal or there is dyspnea, arrhythmia, or hypotension.

4. Use cardiac MRI when the patient is stable enough and the diagnosis remains unclear.
   - CMR helps separate inflammation, ischemia, and stress cardiomyopathy.

5. Don’t overcall myocarditis from MRI edema alone.
   - Edema without LGE and with later normalization can fit several transient processes.

6. Reassess after the acute illness resolves.
   - In this case, repeat imaging normalized, which is often the most clinically useful clue of all.

## Take-home points

- Troponin and BNP abnormalities in inflammatory myopathy are not automatically myocarditis or heart failure.
- Troponin assay matters; troponin I is generally more specific for cardiac injury than troponin T in myositis.
- BNP can rise from atrial fibrillation, renal dysfunction, pulmonary pressures, and systemic illness.
- Cardiac MRI edema with no definite LGE suggests acute myocardial stress/injury, but it is not diagnostic by itself.
- Normalization of echo and MRI supports a transient process such as stress cardiomyopathy or reversible inflammatory myocardial involvement.
- In weak patients with IMNM, keep a broad differential: myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, and nonspecific biomarker leak can all look similar at first glance.
