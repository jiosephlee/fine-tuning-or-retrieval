# When CK Won’t Behave: Distinguishing GLP-1–Associated Rhabdomyolysis from Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy

## Why this case is interesting

A CK bump after semaglutide might make you think “drug-related rhabdo, hydrate, stop the medication, move on.” This case refuses to fit that tidy story.

Instead, a woman in her 60s had an initial GI/dehydration/AKI presentation after a single semaglutide dose, then over the next four months developed relentless proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK that climbed to nearly 4,000 despite being off the drug. Muscle biopsy later showed necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex staining — in other words, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), not simple toxic muscle injury.

That distinction matters, because the management and the prognosis are very different.

## Rhabdomyolysis vs IMNM: the pattern is the diagnosis

With GLP-1 receptor agonists, the default adverse effect profile is usually gastrointestinal. Muscle toxicity is not the classic headline. But when muscle injury does happen, you need to decide whether you’re dealing with:

- **Direct toxic muscle injury / rhabdomyolysis**
- **An autoimmune inflammatory myopathy such as IMNM**

They can both raise CK. They are not the same disease.

A useful mental model:

- **Rhabdomyolysis burns hot and fast**
- **IMNM smolders, progresses, and keeps going**

In the prior tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis report cited by the authors, CK was massively elevated and improved rapidly after stopping the drug and giving supportive care. That’s the classic toxic pattern.

This case looked different:

- CK was only mildly elevated during the initial semaglutide-associated hospitalization
- The patient then **worsened over months**
- Weakness became **progressive and proximal**
- She developed **bulbar symptoms**: dysphagia and dysphonia
- CK stayed elevated and then rose substantially **well after drug discontinuation**
- She improved only after **immunosuppression plus IVIG**

That trajectory should make you think beyond rhabdo.

## Clues that this was not simple rhabdomyolysis

Several details push away from a one-off toxic myopathy:

First, the time course was wrong for straightforward rhabdomyolysis. A drug-triggered toxic injury should generally improve after stopping the agent and correcting volume depletion. Here, the patient kept declining over four months.

Second, the weakness pattern was classic for inflammatory myopathy: **proximal > distal**, with enough severity that she could not sit or stand upright without help.

Third, the bulbar involvement matters. Dysphagia and dysphonia are red flags. When a “CK problem” starts affecting swallowing and voice, you need to widen the lens.

Fourth, there was no strong alternative explanation. She denied statin exposure, supplements such as red yeast rice or mushrooms, heavy exertion, alcohol, or illicit drugs. Her thyroid function was normal. Her serologies were unrevealing for connective tissue disease. She was also seronegative for the usual IMNM antibodies, including **HMGCR** and **SRP** — which does **not** exclude IMNM.

Finally, the tissue told the story. MRI showed mild symmetric muscular edema, and muscle biopsy demonstrated necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage activity and membrane attack complex staining, all consistent with IMNM.

## Why the biopsy matters

Serology helps, but biopsy is often what breaks the tie when the clinical picture is murky.

IMNM is defined by:
- aggressive proximal weakness
- markedly elevated CK
- necrotic myofibers with relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation

This patient’s biopsy fit that pattern. Importantly, seronegative IMNM is real; a negative myositis panel does not clear the patient. That’s especially important in a case like this, where the diagnosis might otherwise get mislabeled as “rhabdo from semaglutide” and dismissed.

## Don’t let the initial CK anchor you

One of the most useful lessons here is that the **first CK is not the final diagnosis**.

At her initial semaglutide-related hospitalization, the CK was only 337 U/L. That could easily be written off as mild muscle injury from dehydration, critical illness, or immobility. But the later evolution showed that this was not the end of the story.

That’s the trap:
- early GI toxicity and AKI can distract from
- a separate, evolving neuromuscular process

Also, remember that AST and ALT can rise with muscle disease, but this patient had biopsy-proven steatohepatitis before semaglutide, and the transaminase elevation persisted even after CK normalized. That makes a liver source more plausible than muscle-only transaminitis.

## Treatment: when severe IMNM is on the table, escalate early

This patient was treated like severe inflammatory myopathy:
- IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 5 days
- then oral prednisone 1 mg/kg daily
- plus IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days because of persistent weakness and severity

That’s a reasonable move when there is:
- profound weakness
- bulbar involvement
- high CK
- concern for ongoing immune-mediated muscle injury

She improved clinically and biochemically, was rapidly weaned off prednisone, and remained well off immunosuppression with continued avoidance of semaglutide. That response supports an immune-mediated process more than a toxic one.

## Practical bedside takeaways

- **Persistent or rising CK after stopping the suspected drug is a warning sign** — especially if weakness is worsening.
- **Proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms are not typical “simple rhabdo” features.**
- **Negative HMGCR/SRP antibodies do not rule out IMNM.**
- **Muscle biopsy can be decisive** when the clinical course is atypical.
- **If the patient is failing to improve, don’t keep calling it rhabdo. Reframe the diagnosis.**

## Bottom line

In GLP-1 receptor agonist–exposed patients, not every CK elevation is a benign toxic myopathy. A delayed, progressive course with proximal and bulbar weakness, persistent CK elevation off drug, MRI muscle edema, and biopsy showing necrotising myopathy should push you toward IMNM — even if the serologies are negative.

This case is a reminder that the diagnosis lives in the **trajectory**, not just the lab value.

## Take-home points

- **Rhabdomyolysis = acute injury, rapid improvement after drug withdrawal and hydration.**
- **IMNM = progressive weakness, persistent CK elevation, and need for immunosuppression.**
- **Bulbar symptoms should raise the stakes immediately.**
- **A negative myositis panel does not exclude IMNM.**
- **When CK won’t behave, think beyond rhabdo.**



# Seronegative but Not Benign: Diagnosing IMNM When HMGCR and SRP Are Negative

A single dose of semaglutide is not the kind of exposure you’d expect to end up explaining a necrotizing myopathy months later. But that’s exactly what made this case interesting: a woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK in the thousands after an initial semaglutide-associated illness. The twist? Her standard myositis antibodies were negative, including HMGCR and SRP.

That’s the teaching point here: **a negative antibody panel does not rule out immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)**. In the right clinical context, the diagnosis is still very much on the table.

## Why this case should change how you think about “negative myositis testing”

IMNM is one of those diseases where the phenotype often matters more than the send-out panel. The classic picture is:

- subacute, progressive **symmetric proximal weakness**
- **CK elevation**, often marked
- bulbar symptoms such as **dysphagia** or **dysphonia**
- little in the way of rash, synovitis, or other connective-tissue disease clues

This patient fit that pattern well. She was previously independent, then deteriorated over months with worsening weakness and swallowing difficulty. Her CK rose from a modest elevation early on to nearly 4000 U/L four months later. That time course is a major clue: it is not what you expect from simple dehydration-related rhabdomyolysis.

The other thing that makes IMNM easy to miss is that a substantial minority of patients are **seronegative**. So if HMGCR and SRP are negative, you have not crossed IMNM off the list. You’ve just lost one diagnostic shortcut.

## Start with the clinical trajectory, not the antibody panel

In this case, the timing mattered. She had an acute GI illness, AKI, and a mildly elevated CK shortly after semaglutide, which could make you think “toxic myopathy” or “rhabdo.” But then she didn’t bounce back. Instead, she got progressively weaker over months, eventually developing bulbar involvement and a much higher CK.

That pattern should push you away from transient toxicity and toward autoimmune muscle disease.

A practical way to frame it:

- **Toxic rhabdomyolysis** usually improves after stopping the trigger and giving supportive care.
- **IMNM** keeps progressing, often even after the suspected trigger is removed.
- **Bulbar symptoms** and inability to sit or stand without help are red flags that this is not a benign self-limited process.

This patient’s course was much more consistent with the second pattern.

## MRI is not the diagnosis — but it is incredibly useful

Muscle MRI does not prove IMNM, but it helps in two important ways:

1. **It shows active muscle involvement**
2. **It helps target the biopsy**

Her MRI of the thighs showed mild, symmetric muscular edema, especially in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps. That’s exactly the kind of finding that supports an active myopathic process and helps you choose a biopsy site with the best chance of yielding something useful.

A few practical points:

- MRI edema supports **ongoing inflammation or injury**
- It is not specific for IMNM
- It is most useful when the serologies are unrevealing and you need to decide where to biopsy

In other words, MRI is a roadmap, not the destination.

## The biopsy is where seronegative IMNM usually declares itself

When antibodies are negative, the biopsy becomes the anchor.

This patient’s right vastus lateralis biopsy showed:

- **necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers**
- **myophagocytosis**
- **CD68-positive macrophages**
- **membrane attack complex positivity**
- only **rare CD3-positive T cells**
- some **perivascular inflammatory cells**

That is very compatible with IMNM.

The key histologic idea in IMNM is that the muscle is being destroyed and repaired, with relatively little of the dense lymphocytic infiltrate you might expect in other inflammatory myopathies. Macrophages and complement deposition fit the pattern well.

If you only remember one pathology pearl, make it this:  
**IMNM is a clinicopathologic diagnosis. The biopsy often matters more than the antibody panel.**

## Don’t let low-yield serologies hijack the case

This patient had a low-titer isolated **SS-B** positivity, with negative ANA and negative SS-A, and no sicca symptoms or other connective-tissue disease features.

That should not be over-interpreted.

Isolated low-titer SS-B in this setting is a weak signal. It is not enough to explain a necrotizing myopathy on its own, and it should not distract you from the stronger evidence:

- progressive proximal weakness
- bulbar symptoms
- CK elevation
- MRI muscle edema
- biopsy-proven necrotizing myopathy

A good rule: if the serology does not match the phenotype, **trust the phenotype**.

## Semaglutide: trigger, coincidence, or both?

The authors appropriately stop short of claiming causality. That matters. The temporal association is striking — her symptoms began after a single semaglutide dose — but that does not prove semaglutide caused the IMNM.

What the case does show is that there may have been a **drug-associated trigger** in a patient who then developed a sustained autoimmune myopathy. Her course was not typical for rhabdomyolysis, and she improved with immunosuppression plus avoidance of GLP-1 receptor agonists.

So the teaching point is not “semaglutide causes IMNM.”  
It’s: **when the clinical course doesn’t fit a toxic myopathy, keep autoimmune disease in play even if the trigger is a new medication.**

## A practical bedside approach

If you’re faced with suspected seronegative IMNM, think in this order:

1. **Recognize the phenotype**  
   Progressive symmetric proximal weakness, CK elevation, dysphagia/dysphonia.

2. **Don’t stop at a negative antibody panel**  
   HMGCR and SRP are helpful, but they are not the whole disease.

3. **Use MRI to find active muscle**  
   Helpful for biopsy targeting and for confirming there is a real myopathic process.

4. **Biopsy if serologies are unrevealing or the presentation is atypical**  
   Look for necrosis, regeneration, macrophage-predominant inflammation, and complement deposition.

5. **Treat severe disease in parallel with the workup**  
   Bulbar symptoms and severe weakness are not a “wait and see” situation.

## Take-home points

- **Negative HMGCR and SRP do not exclude IMNM.**
- **A progressive course with CK rising over weeks to months is more concerning for IMNM than for transient rhabdomyolysis.**
- **MRI is useful for identifying active muscle and guiding biopsy, but it is not diagnostic by itself.**
- **Biopsy findings of necrotic/regenerating fibers with macrophage-predominant inflammation and membrane attack complex deposition support IMNM.**
- **Isolated low-titer SS-B positivity, especially with negative ANA/SSA and no connective-tissue features, is low-yield and should not override the clinicopathologic picture.**
- **When weakness is severe or bulbar symptoms are present, diagnostic workup and treatment should happen at the same time.**

This case is a good reminder that seronegative IMNM is not benign just because the antibody panel is. If the story fits, keep digging — and let the muscle, not the serology, tell you the diagnosis.



# Bulbar Weakness in Myositis: When Dysphagia and Dysphonia Should Escalate Your Treatment

## The case that should make you pause

A woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia over four months after a single dose of semaglutide. By the time she reached care, she could no longer sit or stand without help, and her CK had climbed to nearly 4,000 U/L. Muscle biopsy showed necrotizing myopathy with inflammatory features, and she improved after pulse-dose steroids plus IVIG.

The semaglutide connection is interesting, but the bigger teaching point is this: **bulbar symptoms in inflammatory myopathy are not a side note. They are a severity marker.** Dysphagia and dysphonia should immediately raise your concern for aspiration, rapid clinical decline, and the need to escalate therapy early.

## Bulbar symptoms change the game

In myositis, proximal weakness is common. Bulbar involvement is what makes the case higher risk.

Dysphagia and dysphonia suggest involvement of the oropharyngeal and pharyngeal muscles, and that matters because these patients can deteriorate in ways CK alone won’t tell you. A patient may look “stable” on paper yet be unable to safely swallow liquids, manage secretions, or protect their airway.

In this case, the patient had:
- Progressive proximal weakness
- Dysphagia to both liquids and solids
- Dysphonia
- Severe functional loss, ultimately unable to sit or stand unassisted

That combination should push you toward a more urgent myositis pathway, even before every antibody result is back.

## Why this often justifies pulse steroids and early IVIG

When inflammatory myopathy presents with severe weakness and bulbar features, waiting for a slow outpatient-style response is often the wrong move.

There are a few reasons clinicians escalate early:
1. **Bulbar symptoms can worsen quickly**
2. **Aspiration risk is real**
3. **Respiratory decline may follow**
4. **Some myositis phenotypes respond incompletely to steroids alone**

In this case, the team treated with:
- IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 5 days
- Then prednisone 1 mg/kg daily
- Plus IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 4 days because of persistent weakness and severe features

That’s a very reasonable pattern for a patient with high-risk myositis features. The improvement in CK and function followed.

The practical FOAM lesson: **if someone with suspected inflammatory myopathy has dysphagia/dysphonia and marked proximal weakness, don’t wait for “proof” that they are sick enough. They already are.**

## What to monitor at the bedside

Bulbar involvement means you need to think beyond the muscle exam.

Things to actively watch for:
- Inability to swallow safely
- Coughing with liquids or secretions
- Weak voice or worsening dysphonia
- Pooling secretions
- Dyspnea or shallow breathing
- Any trajectory suggesting impending respiratory compromise

A few practical moves are worth making early:
- Keep aspiration risk front and center
- Get speech/swallow evaluation when available
- Monitor respiratory status closely if bulbar weakness is present
- Trend CK, but don’t let a “moderate” CK falsely reassure you
- Reassess frequently for progression rather than relying on a single snapshot

CK is helpful, but it is not the whole story. This patient’s muscle disease declared itself with function and swallowing first, and the lab only confirmed what the exam already suggested.

## The biopsy matters, but the bedside comes first

This patient’s workup ultimately fit immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy: necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage activity, membrane attack complex staining, and minimal lymphocytic inflammation. Her myositis panel, including SRP and HMGCR, was negative.

That’s another useful reminder: **seronegative IMNM exists.** You cannot rule it out just because the antibody panel is unrevealing.

But in the acute setting, treatment urgency should be driven by the phenotype:
- progressive weakness
- bulbar dysfunction
- rising CK
- functional collapse

The biopsy helps confirm the diagnosis. The swallowing problem is what should make you act.

## A word on the semaglutide connection

This report describes a probable association between semaglutide and IMNM, but causality is not proven. The point isn’t to over-interpret one case. It’s to remember that new metabolic therapies can have unexpected adverse effects, and that prolonged weakness after an initial drug reaction should not be dismissed as “deconditioning.”

In this patient, the initial GI illness and AKI happened soon after semaglutide, but the later course was not a transient toxic myopathy. She kept worsening off the drug, with a marked CK rise and biopsy-proven necrotizing myopathy. That’s the kind of pattern that should make you think immune-mediated disease, not just drug intolerance.

## Take-home points

- **Dysphagia and dysphonia in myositis are red flags, not minor symptoms.**
- **Bulbar weakness should trigger urgent concern for aspiration and respiratory decline.**
- **Severe myositis with bulbar involvement often justifies early pulse-dose steroids and consideration of IVIG.**
- **CK is useful, but clinical trajectory matters more than the number alone.**
- **Negative myositis antibodies do not exclude IMNM.**
- **If the patient is getting weaker and can’t swallow safely, don’t wait for outpatient-style progression. Escalate.**



# AST, ALT, and the Muscle-Liver Trap: Don’t Miss Myopathy in the Patient with “Known Fatty Liver”

## The setup: a liver diagnosis that almost explains too much

It’s very easy to see a patient with obesity, hepatosteatosis, and chronic mild transaminitis and stop thinking. The chart already has an answer: NAFLD. But this case is a good reminder that a liver label can become a diagnostic blindfold.

A woman in her 60s had biopsy-proven fatty liver and mildly elevated AST/ALT for years. Then, after a single semaglutide dose, she developed a cascade of symptoms that were initially easy to anchor on as medication intolerance and deconditioning: nausea, vomiting, AKI, metabolic acidosis, then progressive weakness. Four months later, she came back unable to stand or sit upright without help, with dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK near 4000. Muscle biopsy ultimately showed immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and she improved with corticosteroids and IVIG.

The big teaching point isn’t semaglutide itself. It’s this: **pre-existing fatty liver should never stop you from asking whether AST/ALT are coming from muscle.**

## AST and ALT are not “liver-only” numbers

We all learn that AST and ALT are aminotransferases, but in practice they often get treated like liver-specific biomarkers. They aren’t.

Skeletal muscle injury can raise both AST and ALT, sometimes substantially. AST is especially nonspecific, but ALT can also rise with myopathy, inflammatory myositis, rhabdomyolysis, and other muscle injury states. That means a patient with weakness and “transaminitis” may actually have a muscle problem first and a liver problem second — or both.

In this case, that distinction mattered because she already carried a diagnosis of NAFLD. Her earlier AST/ALT elevations were plausibly hepatic, and her repeat liver biopsy did show steatosis and progression of fatty liver. But when she later developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a marked CK rise, the liver story could no longer explain the whole picture.

## When transaminitis is really a clue to myopathy

There are a few practical clues that should make you widen the differential beyond hepatitis, fatty liver, or “medication side effect”:

- **Weakness out of proportion to liver disease**
- **Bulbar symptoms** such as dysphagia or dysphonia
- **CK elevation** alongside AST/ALT elevation
- **Normal bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and often GGT**
- **A temporal relationship with a drug exposure** that persists after the drug is stopped
- **Failure of AST/ALT and CK to behave like a simple toxic injury**

In muscle-driven aminotransferase elevation, the AST/ALT trend often parallels the CK trend, at least initially. If the CK improves but AST/ALT remain mildly elevated, that may point back toward underlying liver disease — which is exactly what happened here. Her longstanding transaminase abnormalities were probably hepatic, while the later CK rise and weakness reflected a separate muscle process.

That’s the trap: **a patient can have both NAFLD and myositis.** One diagnosis does not cancel out the other.

## Why this case should make you think beyond “toxic myopathy”

The clinical course here is what pushed the diagnosis away from simple rhabdomyolysis. She had an immediate intolerance-type reaction to semaglutide with vomiting, AKI, and a mild CK elevation during the initial hospitalization. But then, while off semaglutide, she continued to worsen over months, with a 12-fold rise in CK and progressive proximal weakness plus dysphagia/dysphonia.

That pattern is much more compatible with an immune-mediated myopathy than with direct drug toxicity. In toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis, you expect the CK to start falling after the offending agent is removed and hydration is provided. Here, the process kept evolving.

Her muscle MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema, and biopsy demonstrated necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — all supportive of IMNM. Serologies were negative for HMGCR and SRP, which does not exclude IMNM, since a substantial minority of cases are seronegative.

## The practical bedside lesson: don’t let NAFLD become a stopping rule

This case is especially relevant because NAFLD is so common. Once a patient has “fatty liver” in the chart, future AST/ALT abnormalities often get attributed to the same bucket without a fresh look. That is how myopathy gets missed.

If a patient with known NAFLD develops any combination of weakness, fatigue that sounds neuromuscular, dysphagia, dark urine, myalgias, or unexplained functional decline, check a CK early. Don’t wait for the liver workup to loop around again. If AST/ALT are elevated and the patient can’t climb stairs, rise from a chair, or swallow normally, muscle has to be on the table.

Also remember the reverse: a normal CK doesn’t always exclude muscle disease if the story is compelling, but a rising CK makes the case much harder to ignore. In this patient, the CK was the clue that forced the right workup.

## A note on the semaglutide association

The report frames semaglutide as a probable contributor to IMNM, but causality is not proven. That matters. The educational point is not “GLP-1 receptor agonists cause myositis.” The point is that a drug exposure can create diagnostic distraction, and a seemingly routine metabolic-liver patient can actually be evolving a serious autoimmune myopathy.

Whether semaglutide was trigger, contributor, or coincidence, the clinical lesson stands: **when the weakness keeps progressing after the drug is stopped, think autoimmune, not just toxic.**

## Take-home points

- AST and ALT are not liver-specific; skeletal muscle injury can raise both.
- In a patient with weakness, dysphagia, or falling function, always think about muscle as a source of “transaminitis.”
- Known NAFLD should not be used to explain away new neuromuscular symptoms or a rising CK.
- A CK that keeps climbing after drug withdrawal argues against simple toxic myopathy and should prompt evaluation for IMNM or other inflammatory myopathies.
- If the liver story and the muscle story don’t match, keep both in play — patients can have fatty liver and myopathy at the same time.



# Troponin, BNP, and a Weak Patient: Sorting Cardiac Involvement in Necrotising Myopathy

## Why this case is worth a second look

Most of us are trained to treat a troponin like a siren: hear it, chase it. But in inflammatory myopathy, that reflex can get you into trouble.

This patient had biopsy-proven immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, plus a mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin, a markedly elevated proBNP, a brief episode of atrial fibrillation during her earlier critical illness, and a cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema with mild regional hypokinesis but no definite late gadolinium enhancement. That is exactly the kind of “is this myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or just biomarker noise?” scenario that shows up on the wards and in the ED.

The useful lesson here is not that semaglutide causes myocarditis or that every troponin in myositis is cardiac. It’s that cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathy lives on a spectrum, and the workup has to be matched to the phenotype.

## The cardiac story in this patient

The skeletal muscle diagnosis was clear: progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, CK elevation, MRI muscle edema, and biopsy with necrotic/regenerating fibers, macrophage activity, and membrane attack complex staining.

The heart was murkier.

At the tertiary evaluation, she had:
- High-sensitivity troponin elevation to 62 pg/mL
- proBNP of 4358 pg/mL
- Normal creatinine
- Transthoracic echo with preserved LVEF (65%–70%)
- RVSP 46 mm Hg
- Cardiac MRI with mild mid-anteroseptal/anterior hypokinesis and myocardial edema
- No definite late gadolinium enhancement
- Later normalization of echo and MRI findings

That pattern argues against a straightforward infarct. It also doesn’t give you a clean “yes” for fulminant myocarditis. What it does do is tell you that something transient was happening to the myocardium, or at least to the biomarkers we use to interrogate it.

## Troponin in myositis: useful, but only if you know what you’re measuring

One of the biggest traps in myositis is assuming all troponins mean the same thing.

In inflammatory muscle disease, troponin interpretation depends heavily on the assay. Troponin T can be less specific in skeletal muscle disorders, whereas troponin I is generally more cardiac-specific. If the assay isn’t specified, you have to be cautious about overcalling myocardial injury.

In this case, the troponin was only modestly elevated. That’s not the pattern of a large infarct. It may reflect:
- Mild myocardial involvement
- Physiologic stress
- Atrial fibrillation-related demand
- Systemic inflammation
- Or a combination of all of the above

The key point: a small troponin rise in a patient with severe systemic illness and myositis should trigger thoughtful cardiac evaluation, not automatic ACS labeling.

## BNP is a wall-stress biomarker, not a heart-failure diagnosis

The proBNP here was strikingly elevated, but BNP is not a synonym for decompensated heart failure.

BNP rises with:
- Myocardial stretch
- Atrial fibrillation
- Pulmonary hypertension or elevated right-sided pressures
- Renal dysfunction
- Critical illness
- Sepsis or inflammatory stress

This patient had several possible BNP drivers. She had a recent severe hospitalization, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and mildly elevated RVSP on echo. Her BNP is therefore best interpreted as a marker of cardiac stress, not proof of overt pump failure.

That distinction matters because a preserved EF does not exclude myocardial injury, but it also means you should not anchor on “heart failure” just because the BNP is high.

## Myocarditis versus stress cardiomyopathy versus nonspecific leak

This is the real diagnostic fork in the road.

### Myocarditis
Myocarditis becomes more likely when you have:
- Troponin elevation
- New arrhythmia or conduction abnormality
- Symptoms out of proportion to skeletal weakness
- Cardiac MRI abnormalities consistent with inflammation
- Sometimes reduced EF, though not always

In this patient, CMR showed myocardial edema and mild hypokinesis, which keeps myocarditis on the table. But the lack of definite late gadolinium enhancement and the later normalization make this less than definitive.

### Stress cardiomyopathy
Stress cardiomyopathy is a very reasonable alternative here.

She had a major physiologic insult: severe GI symptoms, dehydration, AKI, acidosis, ICU-level care, then progressive systemic inflammatory disease. Stress cardiomyopathy can present with:
- Modest troponin elevation
- Elevated BNP
- Regional wall motion abnormalities
- Myocardial edema on MRI
- Recovery over time

That fits the trajectory here surprisingly well, especially given the normalization on follow-up imaging.

### Nonspecific biomarker leak
Sometimes the right answer is not a named cardiomyopathy at all. Troponin and BNP can leak in systemic illness without a primary cardiac syndrome. In a patient with severe inflammatory myopathy, that possibility has to stay open unless the clinical picture becomes more convincing.

## Why the normal EF doesn’t close the case

A normal EF is reassuring, but it doesn’t rule out early or mild myocardial involvement.

Echo is a blunt tool. It tells you about pump function and gross structure, not the whole inflammatory story. Cardiac MRI is better for tissue characterization, and in this case it gave you the clue that the myocardium was involved somehow, even if the exact label remained uncertain.

The later normalization of echo and MRI is important. It supports a transient process rather than established cardiomyopathy or infarction. That makes myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or inflammatory myocardial injury more plausible than a chronic structural problem.

## The rhythm piece: atrial fibrillation may be the messenger, not the diagnosis

Her paroxysmal atrial fibrillation occurred during the earlier hospitalization for semaglutide-related GI toxicity, AKI, and metabolic derangement. That timing matters.

AF in this setting may reflect:
- Acute physiologic stress
- Volume shifts
- Electrolyte/metabolic disturbance
- Atrial stretch
- Systemic inflammation

It may also be a clue to transient myocardial irritation, but AF alone does not prove myocarditis. In many sick patients, AF is the smoke rather than the fire.

## A practical approach when the myositis patient has abnormal cardiac biomarkers

If you’re caring for a patient with inflammatory myopathy and an elevated troponin or BNP, a sensible approach is:

1. Confirm the assay and trend it.
   - If you can get troponin I rather than T, that may help interpretation in myositis.

2. Look for physiology, not just numbers.
   - Hemodynamics, oxygenation, volume status, ECG changes, arrhythmia, chest symptoms, and signs of shock matter more than a single biomarker.

3. Get an echo early if biomarkers are abnormal or there is dyspnea, arrhythmia, or hypotension.

4. Use cardiac MRI when the patient is stable enough and the diagnosis remains unclear.
   - CMR helps separate inflammation, ischemia, and stress cardiomyopathy.

5. Don’t overcall myocarditis from MRI edema alone.
   - Edema without LGE and with later normalization can fit several transient processes.

6. Reassess after the acute illness resolves.
   - In this case, repeat imaging normalized, which is often the most clinically useful clue of all.

## Take-home points

- Troponin and BNP abnormalities in inflammatory myopathy are not automatically myocarditis or heart failure.
- Troponin assay matters; troponin I is generally more specific for cardiac injury than troponin T in myositis.
- BNP can rise from atrial fibrillation, renal dysfunction, pulmonary pressures, and systemic illness.
- Cardiac MRI edema with no definite LGE suggests acute myocardial stress/injury, but it is not diagnostic by itself.
- Normalization of echo and MRI supports a transient process such as stress cardiomyopathy or reversible inflammatory myocardial involvement.
- In weak patients with IMNM, keep a broad differential: myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, and nonspecific biomarker leak can all look similar at first glance.



# One Dose, Four Months Later: Pharmacovigilance Lessons from a Possible Semaglutide-Triggered Autoimmune Myopathy

## Why this case matters

Semaglutide has become a household name in obesity and diabetes care, and for good reason: it works, it’s widely used, and most of the adverse effects we see are the expected GI kind. That’s what makes this case so striking. A woman in her 60s received a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide, had an immediate severe GI illness with AKI and metabolic derangements, and then—after seeming to recover—progressed over four months to profound proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and CK elevation into the thousands. Muscle biopsy ultimately supported immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and she improved with corticosteroids, IVIG, and avoidance of GLP-1 receptor agonists.

This is not a “semaglutide causes myopathy” case. It is a reminder that when drugs become ubiquitous, rare serious adverse events will eventually appear in temporal proximity. The challenge is deciding when a temporal association deserves a pharmacovigilance alarm.

## The clinical pattern: not just rhabdomyolysis

The first temptation here is to anchor on toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis. That would fit the initial semaglutide-associated hospitalization: nausea, vomiting, poor intake, acute renal failure, severe metabolic acidosis, and a modest CK elevation of 337 U/L. But the later course does not behave like uncomplicated drug toxicity.

Four months later, she had a very different syndrome:

- Progressive proximal weakness
- Dysphagia and dysphonia
- CK 3992 U/L
- MRI with symmetric muscle edema
- Muscle biopsy showing necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage/myophagocytic activity and membrane attack complex staining

That pattern is much more consistent with IMNM than with simple rhabdomyolysis. In toxic muscle injury, CK usually falls after the insult is removed and hydration is provided. In this case, the muscle disease evolved long after the single exposure and required immunosuppression.

A key bedside lesson: if a patient has weakness plus dysphagia/dysphonia and CK elevation that persists or worsens well after the medication exposure, stop thinking “deconditioning” and start thinking inflammatory myopathy.

## Why the biopsy matters

IMNM is a distinct autoimmune myopathy characterized by severe proximal weakness, necrotizing myofibers, and relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation. Anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies are classic associations, but seronegativity is common enough that a negative panel does not exclude the diagnosis.

That matters here because this patient’s myositis panel was negative for HMGCR, SRP, and a broad range of other antibodies. The biopsy carried the diagnosis:

- Dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers
- Myophagocytosis with CD68-positive macrophages
- Membrane attack complex deposition
- Only rare CD3-positive cells

In other words, the tissue looked like immune-mediated muscle destruction, not a bland toxic injury.

Practical pearl: if the phenotype screams inflammatory myopathy, a negative antibody panel should not end the workup.

## Causation versus correlation: what can we really say?

This is where pharmacovigilance gets uncomfortable. The authors call this a probable association, and that is the right level of caution.

The argument for semaglutide as a contributor is:

- Clear temporal relationship
- No statin exposure or other obvious classic trigger
- No convincing alternative connective tissue disease
- CK and weakness progressed after the exposure rather than resolving
- Improvement followed withdrawal of semaglutide plus a short course of immunosuppression

But there are real limitations:

- No CK level before semaglutide, so a smoldering process cannot be fully excluded
- Seronegative IMNM exists, so antibody negativity does not prove drug causation
- The patient had pre-existing transaminase elevation, though liver biopsy and persistent AST/ALT abnormalities suggest NAFLD rather than muscle as the source

This is the central pharmacovigilance lesson: a case report can create a safety signal, but it cannot prove causality.

## How to think about WHO-UMC causality here

The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre framework is a helpful way to discipline our thinking. In this case, the relationship is best viewed as “probable” rather than “certain.”

Why probable?

- Reasonable time relationship
- Other likely causes are not compelling
- There was a clinically reasonable response after withdrawal
- Rechallenge would be unsafe and unethical

That last point matters. In severe suspected drug reactions, rechallenge is often not just unwise—it is morally indefensible. If a patient develops a life-threatening myopathy, we do not need a second exposure to “prove” the point.

A practical way to say it in real life: “This may be drug-related, but we are not claiming proof. We are acting on a plausible safety signal.”

## What makes this different from the tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis report?

The report discusses a prior case of rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide. That comparison is useful because it highlights how differently toxic muscle injury behaves from immune-mediated disease.

The tirzepatide case had:

- Markedly elevated CK at presentation
- Improvement within days of stopping the drug and giving supportive care
- No need for immunosuppression

This semaglutide case behaved differently:

- CK rose over months, not hours to days
- Weakness progressed despite drug cessation
- Biopsy showed autoimmune necrotizing features
- Improvement required steroids and IVIG

That distinction is clinically important. A CK of 3000–4000 in a patient on or after a GLP-1 receptor agonist should not be reflexively labeled “rhabdo” without asking whether the trajectory fits immune myopathy.

## What should clinicians do with a case report like this?

Case reports should change how we think, not how we overreact. They should prompt better surveillance, better counseling, and a lower threshold to investigate suspicious symptoms—not blanket avoidance of an otherwise effective medication in everyone.

In practical terms:

- Ask about new proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, or dark urine after GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure
- If symptoms are present, check CK and liver enzymes, but remember AST/ALT can be muscle-derived
- Don’t let a negative antibody panel stop the evaluation if the phenotype is concerning
- Consider MRI and biopsy when weakness/CK elevation persists or evolves
- If serious immune-mediated toxicity is suspected, document the reaction clearly and avoid rechallenge

Counseling should be nuanced. Patients should know that severe immune myopathy appears rare, causality is not established, and the overall safety profile of semaglutide remains favorable. But clinicians should also recognize that “rare” is not “never,” especially once a drug is used at massive scale.

## A word on transaminases, troponin, and the diagnostic noise

This case also reminds us how messy these evaluations can get.

Her AST/ALT were elevated before semaglutide and remained abnormal even after CK normalized, which supports underlying NAFLD rather than muscle as the primary source. Her troponin and BNP elevations, plus cardiac MRI findings, raised concern for myocardial involvement or stress cardiomyopathy, but the cardiac findings were ultimately inconclusive and later normalized.

The takeaway is not that every biomarker abnormality in myositis means myocarditis. It’s that systemic autoimmune muscle disease can create a lot of diagnostic static, and you need to interpret biomarkers in clinical context.

## Bottom line

This report is best read as a pharmacovigilance signal, not a verdict. A single semaglutide dose was followed by a severe, delayed, biopsy-supported IMNM that improved after drug avoidance and immunosuppression. That sequence is unusual enough to matter, especially in the era of widespread GLP-1 receptor agonist use.

## Take-home points

- Temporal association is not causation, but it is enough to trigger careful scrutiny.
- Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and rising CK after drug exposure should raise concern for IMNM, not just rhabdomyolysis.
- Negative HMGCR/SRP testing does not exclude immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy.
- WHO-UMC criteria can help frame “probable” drug causality when rechallenge is unsafe.
- Rechallenge after a severe suspected immune-mediated adverse reaction is usually unethical.
- Case reports should guide vigilance and counseling, not overstate certainty.
