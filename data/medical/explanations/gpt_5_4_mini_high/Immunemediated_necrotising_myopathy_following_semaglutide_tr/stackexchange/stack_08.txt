Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] This patient improved after withdrawal of semaglutide and a relatively short course of immunosuppression, then remained in remission off therapy. How does that outcome inform your thinking about drug-triggered versus idiopathic immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and why would rechallenge with a GLP-1 receptor agonist be inappropriate?

Her course makes a **drug-triggered immune-mediated necrotising myopathy more plausible than classic idiopathic IMNM**, though it does not prove causality. In idiopathic IMNM, the autoimmune process is often **persistent and treatment-intensive**: patients commonly require prolonged immunosuppression, and relapse is frequent when therapy is withdrawn. By contrast, this patient had a clear temporal association with semaglutide exposure, biopsy-proven necrotising myopathy, and then—most importantly—**sustained remission after semaglutide withdrawal and only a short course of corticosteroids plus IVIG**, remaining well and off immunosuppression for months. That pattern argues that semaglutide may have served as a trigger that initiated an immune process that became quiescent once the inciting exposure was removed, which is more consistent with a **drug-associated autoimmune myopathy** than with an ongoing idiopathic one.

A rechallenge with a GLP-1 receptor agonist would be inappropriate because this was a **severe, likely immune-mediated adverse drug reaction**: she developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, marked CK elevation, and biopsy findings consistent with IMNM. Re-exposure could re-activate the same immune pathway and precipitate **more rapid and more severe recurrence**, potentially with bulbar or systemic complications. In pharmacovigilance terms, the dechallenge response is already informative; **rechallenge is not ethically justified when the suspected reaction is serious and potentially disabling**. Moreover, there is no clinical necessity to risk recurrent myopathy when alternative strategies for weight and metabolic control exist.
