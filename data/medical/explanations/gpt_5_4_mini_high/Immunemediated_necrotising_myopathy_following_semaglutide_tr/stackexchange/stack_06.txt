Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] In an inflammatory myopathy patient with elevated high-sensitivity troponin, markedly elevated BNP, atrial fibrillation, and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema without clear late gadolinium enhancement, how would you assess for possible cardiac involvement and how would the result affect management?

In an inflammatory myopathy, I would take the elevated hs-troponin and markedly elevated BNP seriously, but I would not assume they prove myocarditis. The first step is to determine whether the biomarker pattern reflects true myocardial injury or nonspecific release/strain in the setting of skeletal muscle disease, atrial fibrillation, and acute illness.

Practically, I would assess cardiac involvement with a cardiology co-evaluation, serial ECGs and telemetry, repeat troponin and BNP trends, and transthoracic echocardiography for ventricular function and wall-motion abnormality. Cardiac MRI is the key noninvasive test here: myocardial edema suggests active inflammation, but the absence of clear late gadolinium enhancement argues against established scar or infarction and makes the diagnosis less definitive. If the diagnosis remains uncertain and would change treatment, endomyocardial biopsy is the diagnostic standard, though it is usually reserved for cases with hemodynamic compromise, progressive dysfunction, or persistent diagnostic ambiguity. One important teaching point is that troponin T can be elevated from regenerating skeletal muscle; troponin I is more specific for myocardial injury when available.

In this case, the MRI finding of edema without definite late enhancement, together with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction on echo and later normalization of the cardiac abnormalities, supports possible transient myocardial involvement or stress-related injury rather than definite fulminant myocarditis. That distinction matters for management: confirmed cardiac involvement would prompt closer cardiology follow-up, continued rhythm management for atrial fibrillation, and a lower threshold to intensify immunosuppression and treat any heart-failure physiology. If the cardiac findings are equivocal and the patient is stable, I would continue treatment directed at the inflammatory myopathy itself, monitor closely, and repeat cardiac imaging and biomarkers after treatment, as was done in this case.
