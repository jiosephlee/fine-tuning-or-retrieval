Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] A woman in her 60s develops hypotension, hypoxemia, generalized urticaria, facial swelling, flushing, and throat tightness 1 hour after taking her first dose of clarithromycin. What clinical features establish the diagnosis of anaphylaxis, and what findings specifically support calling this anaphylactic shock?

This case meets the clinical diagnosis of **anaphylaxis** on the basis of its **rapid onset after exposure to a likely trigger** and the involvement of **multiple organ systems**. She developed symptoms **within 1 hour of the first dose of clarithromycin**, with clear **cutaneous/mucosal findings** — generalized urticaria, flushing, erythema, and facial swelling — plus **airway/breathing symptoms** — throat tightness and hypoxemia. In practice, anaphylaxis is a **clinical diagnosis**; no laboratory test is required to establish it in the acute setting.

What makes this **anaphylactic shock** rather than “anaphylaxis without shock” is the presence of **cardiovascular collapse**, specifically **hypotension**. Her blood pressure of **74/50 mm Hg** is the key finding supporting shock, especially in the context of concurrent respiratory compromise. The report also describes progression to **deep hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea** during recurrent episodes, which further reinforces that this was not a limited allergic reaction but a severe systemic event with hemodynamic instability.

So, in short: **anaphylaxis** is established by the acute, multisystem allergic syndrome; **anaphylactic shock** is established when that syndrome includes **hypotension and circulatory compromise**, as in this patient.

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient developed a rash after 5 days of clindamycin, then had severe systemic symptoms 1 hour after her first dose of clarithromycin. Which antibiotic is the more likely trigger of the anaphylactic event, and what aspects of timing and symptom pattern help distinguish an immediate drug allergy from a delayed cutaneous drug eruption?

The **more likely trigger of the anaphylactic event is clarithromycin**, not clindamycin.

In this case, the key discriminator is **timing plus symptom pattern**. The rash that appeared **after 5 days of clindamycin** is much more consistent with a **delayed cutaneous drug eruption**—typically a T-cell–mediated exanthem that develops **days after exposure** and is usually limited to the skin. By contrast, her severe illness began **1 hour after the first dose of clarithromycin** and included **pruritus, flushing, generalized urticaria, facial swelling, throat symptoms, hypotension, and hypoxemia**—the classic pattern of **immediate anaphylaxis**, which occurs **within minutes to a few hours** of drug exposure and involves **more than one organ system**, especially skin plus airway/breathing/circulation.

So, in practical terms:  
- **Delayed rash after clindamycin** → suggests a **delayed hypersensitivity eruption**  
- **Rapid multisystem collapse after clarithromycin** → strongly supports **immediate drug allergy/anaphylaxis**

This is exactly the distinction highlighted by the case: the clindamycin rash was a separate, delayed cutaneous reaction, whereas clarithromycin was the likely precipitant of the anaphylactic episode.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Several hours after complete initial improvement and without further clarithromycin exposure, the patient had recurrent episodes of hypotension, dyspnea, rash, and pruritus. How would you differentiate biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis from refractory anaphylaxis and from persistent symptoms due to ongoing allergen absorption?

The key distinction is the presence or absence of a true symptom-free interval after adequate treatment. In **biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis**, the patient has **complete or near-complete initial resolution** of hypotension, dyspnea, rash, and pruritus, and then develops **new recurrent episodes hours later without re-exposure** to the trigger. In this case, the patient improved after the first epinephrine dose, had **no further clarithromycin exposure**, and then had **multiple distinct relapses over the next 12 hours**, which is most consistent with **multiphasic anaphylaxis**.

By contrast, **refractory anaphylaxis** is not a relapse after recovery; it is **persistent anaphylaxis that does not adequately respond to correctly administered epinephrine**, or only transiently improves before immediately deteriorating again. The patient never truly stabilizes. So if the shock, bronchospasm, or cutaneous findings never fully clear, think refractory disease rather than biphasic or multiphasic reaction.

Finally, **ongoing allergen absorption** should be considered when the trigger was **ingested or slow-release**, and symptoms continue because the allergen is still entering the circulation. That scenario usually produces **persistent or waxing-and-waning symptoms without a clean asymptomatic interval**. In this case, although the trigger was an oral drug, the report emphasizes **complete initial improvement** before the later episodes, which argues against ongoing absorption and favors **multiphasic anaphylaxis**.

### [Management] For a 104-kg adult with anaphylaxis, what is the recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose, how is it derived from weight-based dosing, and why might repeated use of a 0.3 mg auto-injector represent clinically important underdosing?

For a 104-kg adult with anaphylaxis, the recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose is **0.5 mg**.

The usual guideline dose is **0.01 mg/kg IM**, but this is **capped at a maximum of 0.5 mg** in adults. In this patient, the weight-based calculation would be 0.01 × 104 = **1.04 mg**, but because of the adult maximum, the correct initial IM dose is **0.5 mg**, not 1 mg.

Repeated use of a **0.3 mg auto-injector** is clinically important underdosing because it delivers only **60% of the recommended adult dose** for a patient of this size. In other words, it is **40% below the guideline dose** of 0.5 mg. As the case report emphasizes, auto-injectors containing 0.3 mg are common but may be **potentially critical underdosing in adults over 50 kg**, and insufficient initial dosing can contribute to **incomplete reversal of vasodilation, bronchospasm, and hypotension**, increasing the likelihood that additional epinephrine doses or escalation of care will be needed—as occurred in this patient’s multiphasic course.

### [Management] After the first relapse of anaphylactic shock on the ward, what factors justify escalation to ICU-level monitoring, and under what circumstances would you consider switching from repeated intramuscular epinephrine to a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion?

After the first relapse, the threshold for ICU-level monitoring should be low because the patient has already declared a **severe multiphasic course**: she had initial anaphylactic shock with **hypotension and hypoxemia**, then recurrent shock within hours despite an initial response, and she required **repeated epinephrine**. Those features imply a meaningful risk of another abrupt recurrence during the first 12–24 hours, when biphasic/multiphasic reactions most commonly occur. In the case report, ICU transfer was justified not by the diagnosis alone, but by the combination of **hemodynamic instability, repeated relapse, and the need for closer monitoring than the ward could provide**. ICU-level care offers continuous blood-pressure and oxygenation monitoring, rapid fluid resuscitation, and immediate access to airway support and vasopressors.

I would consider moving from repeated intramuscular epinephrine to a **continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion** only if the reaction is becoming **refractory or functionally persistent**—that is, if shock or respiratory compromise **does not stabilize durably after correctly administered IM epinephrine and fluids**, or if the patient has **frequent, closely spaced recurrences** such that intermittent IM dosing is no longer maintaining control. In other words, IV epinephrine is for **ongoing or rapidly recurrent anaphylaxis**, not for routine first-line treatment. Because of its narrower safety margin, it should be used only with **infusion pump titration and continuous monitoring**, typically in the ICU.

In this case, the team appropriately considered an IV infusion after multiple relapses, but because the episodes were becoming less intense and then stopped, repeated IM epinephrine with close ICU observation was sufficient.

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical factors in this case increase the risk of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and therefore support prolonged observation beyond the traditional 4- to 6-hour emergency department monitoring period?

The key reasons in this case are the **severity of the index anaphylaxis** and the **early recurrence after apparent response**.

She presented with **true anaphylactic shock** — marked **hypotension (74/50 mm Hg)** and **hypoxemia (SpO₂ 88%)** — along with diffuse flushing, urticaria, and facial swelling. Severe cardiovascular and respiratory involvement is one of the clearest clinical signals for a higher risk of biphasic or multiphasic disease. In addition, she **required repeated intramuscular epinephrine** for recurrent episodes, with **four total anaphylactic episodes** over the first 12 hours. The need for **more than one epinephrine dose** and the fact that she had **already relapsed after initial improvement** are strong practical reasons to continue monitored observation rather than discharge after a routine 4- to 6-hour period.

Two additional features heighten concern. First, there was some **delay from symptom onset to definitive treatment**, which is a recognized risk factor for recurrence. Second, the later workup suggested a **mast-cell disorder with elevated tryptase and probable systemic mastocytosis**; a high mast-cell burden plausibly predisposes to more severe and recurrent mediator release.

So, in this patient, the combination of **shock-level severity, recurrent symptoms, repeated epinephrine requirement, possible delayed treatment, and suspected mastocytosis** supports prolonged observation — and, once relapse occurred, ICU-level monitoring was appropriate.

### [Management] What is the current evidence-based role of corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis and in prevention of biphasic reactions, and how should that evidence influence management in a patient with recurrent anaphylactic episodes?

Corticosteroids should **not** be considered a primary treatment for acute anaphylaxis, and the current evidence does **not** support their routine use to prevent biphasic reactions. Their onset is too slow to reverse the immediate airway, breathing, or circulatory compromise of anaphylaxis, and the best available data do not show that they reduce recurrence. As the case report notes, a systematic review found **no convincing benefit** for biphasic prevention, and subsequent observational studies have even raised concern for greater resource use or harm with prehospital steroid administration.

The management implication is straightforward: in a patient with recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis, **epinephrine remains the treatment that matters**. Corticosteroids should never delay repeat intramuscular epinephrine, aggressive supportive care, or escalation to an intravenous epinephrine infusion if shock is persistent or refractory. In the reported patient, relapse occurred despite corticosteroids, which illustrates the key point: steroids did not prevent further episodes.

So, in practice, recurrent anaphylaxis should prompt:
- **Prompt repeat IM epinephrine** for each recurrence, at guideline dose
- **IV fluids and oxygen** as needed
- **Close observation, often in ICU**, when there is hypotension, repeated epinephrine requirement, or early relapse
- **Search for an underlying predisposition** when the course is unusually severe or prolonged, as in this case, where mast-cell disease was suspected

In short, corticosteroids are at most an adjunct for selected associated problems, but they are **not evidence-based prophylaxis against biphasic anaphylaxis**. The presence of recurrent episodes should shift attention to adequate epinephrine treatment and vigilant monitoring, not to steroid escalation.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should serum tryptase be measured after suspected anaphylaxis in terms of number and timing of samples, and what is the clinical significance of a persistently elevated baseline tryptase level after recovery?

After suspected anaphylaxis, serum tryptase should be drawn serially: obtain an initial sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset (and no later than 4 hours, when tryptase typically peaks), and a third sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish the baseline. In the case report, the authors note that guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three, timed samples because interpretation depends on comparing the acute rise with the recovery baseline.

A persistently elevated baseline tryptase after recovery is clinically important because it suggests an underlying mast-cell disorder rather than just transient mediator release from the anaphylactic event. In this case, the elevated baseline tryptase, which remained increased on follow-up, raised a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, even though formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met. Practically, a high baseline tryptase should prompt further allergologic evaluation and consideration of mast-cell disease, especially in patients with unusually severe, recurrent, or multiphasic anaphylaxis.

### [Pathophysiology] How can a clonal mast cell disorder such as systemic mastocytosis predispose to unusually severe or recurrent anaphylaxis, and what clues in this patient prompted evaluation for mastocytosis despite not meeting full WHO diagnostic criteria?

Systemic mastocytosis predisposes to severe or recurrent anaphylaxis because the patient has an expanded, clonally abnormal mast-cell population that is more easily activated and able to release a larger burst of mediators — especially histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins — when exposed to a trigger. In practical terms, the threshold for degranulation is lower, the hemodynamic consequences are greater, and the reaction may be more prolonged or recur after apparent improvement. This is why patients with clonal mast-cell disease can have disproportionately severe hypotension, flushing, urticaria, and repeated episodes of anaphylaxis after relatively ordinary exposures such as drugs or Hymenoptera venom.

In this case, mastocytosis was suspected not because she met full WHO criteria, but because the clinical pattern was atypical for a simple drug allergy: she had multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single clarithromycin dose, with four episodes despite initial treatment, each requiring epinephrine. The suspicion was strengthened by persistently elevated tryptase levels during and after the event, suggesting increased baseline mast-cell burden, as well as a history of a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, hepatomegaly on ultrasound, and possible neuropsychiatric symptoms. Although bone marrow material was insufficient to assess the major criterion and she had only two minor WHO criteria, the overall picture was sufficiently suggestive of a clonal mast-cell disorder to prompt further evaluation.
