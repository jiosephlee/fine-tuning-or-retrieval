Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# How Long Should You Watch Them? Disposition After Severe Anaphylaxis

## The case that makes disposition feel impossible

The hard part of anaphylaxis is usually not the diagnosis. It’s the part that comes after the epinephrine: **is this patient truly safe, or are we just in the calm before the next wave?**

This case is a perfect example. A woman in her 60s took a single oral dose of clarithromycin and developed classic severe anaphylaxis: pruritus, flushing, generalized erythema and urticaria, facial swelling, agitation, hypoxemia, and hypotension to 74/50 mm Hg. She improved after IM epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, and antihistamine. Then she relapsed. And relapsed again. In total, she had **four anaphylactic episodes** despite no further allergen exposure.

That’s the uncomfortable reality: some anaphylaxis is not a one-and-done event. A patient can look better, even dramatically better, and still crash again hours later.

## Biphasic, multiphasic, refractory — the labels matter less than the physiology

We tend to talk about **biphasic anaphylaxis** as recurrence after apparent resolution, and **multiphasic anaphylaxis** when there are multiple relapses. **Refractory anaphylaxis** is a different beast: persistent symptoms that never really respond adequately to correctly given epinephrine.

In the real world, these categories blur. A patient who improves after epinephrine, then deteriorates again, may have true biphasic or multiphasic disease — or may simply have ongoing mediator release with transient improvement. Either way, the disposition problem is the same:

- Does this person need prolonged monitored observation?
- Do they need step-up care?
- Is the floor enough?
- Should ICU be involved early?

This case argues for humility. The first response can be deceiving.

## The observation clock is useful, but it is not magic

We still do not have a perfect observation duration for anaphylaxis. Traditional practice has often been **4–6 hours after symptom resolution**, but that window is not a guarantee. Reported biphasic reactions vary widely, and many occur within the first 6–12 hours — but some happen later, even well beyond the classic ED observation period.

That’s why disposition should be **risk-based**, not purely time-based.

The patient in this case had several features that should make you uneasy:

- **Severe initial presentation** with hypotension and hypoxemia
- **Need for epinephrine**
- **Recurrence after initial improvement**
- Ultimately, **multiple epinephrine doses**
- A later workup suggesting a **mast cell disorder phenotype** (high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though formal criteria were not fully met)

These are not just academic details. They are the exact features that should push you away from “looks good, discharge” thinking.

## What should make you worry about recurrence?

The literature does not give us a validated crystal ball, but the repeatedly suggested risk markers are familiar:

- Delayed epinephrine administration
- Severe initial reaction
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses
- Prior biphasic reactions

None of these perfectly predicts recurrence, but they all tell the same story: the patient has already declared themselves to be higher risk.

This case also highlights a practical issue that gets overlooked: **dose matters**. She weighed 104 kg, and the initial epi doses came from a 0.3 mg auto-injector because that was the fastest available option. That is understandable in time-sensitive care, but it’s also a reminder that many adult auto-injectors underdose larger patients compared with the commonly recommended adult maximum dose of 0.5 mg.

Fast epinephrine is better than perfect epinephrine delayed. But if you’re deciding whether a patient is truly out of the woods, you should know exactly what they received.

## Ward or ICU?

This is where the art comes in.

A patient with resolved, low-risk anaphylaxis may be appropriate for a brief monitored observation period and discharge with education, an epinephrine auto-injector, and follow-up. But once the case becomes severe, recurrent, or hemodynamically unstable, the floor may not be enough.

In this report, the patient initially went to an acute internal ward without telemetry after seeming to respond. She then relapsed within 12 hours and required ICU transfer. That sequence is the lesson: **a patient who has already relapsed is no longer in the “simple observation” category.**

A practical way to think about disposition:

- **ED observation / ward care** may be reasonable when symptoms resolve promptly after a single dose, there is no recurrence, and the clinical course is otherwise reassuring.
- **Step-up or ICU-level monitoring** becomes much more appropriate when there is shock, hypoxemia, airway concern, repeated epinephrine requirement, or early relapse.
- If you are even considering an epinephrine infusion, that patient is already in escalation territory.

Importantly, this is not about punishing the patient with ICU. It’s about matching the monitoring environment to the possibility of abrupt deterioration.

## Steroids: don’t let them fool you

This case received corticosteroids after the first recurrence, which is still common in many protocols. But the evidence base for steroids preventing biphasic reactions is weak at best, and more recent thinking has moved away from routine steroid use in anaphylaxis.

That matters for disposition because steroids can create a false sense of security.

Steroids may still be used for selected reasons, but they should **not** be the reason you shorten observation or downgrade monitoring. They do not replace epinephrine, and they do not reliably prevent relapse.

## Don’t forget the long game: mast cell disease and allergy follow-up

This patient’s prolonged and unusually recurrent course triggered a workup that showed a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met. Baseline and episode tryptase were elevated, and there were other features that fit the broader mast-cell-disorder story.

That doesn’t mean every severe anaphylaxis patient has mastocytosis. It does mean that **atypical, recurrent, or outsized reactions deserve further evaluation**. If the course is strange enough to make you say, “this isn’t behaving like routine anaphylaxis,” you should listen to that instinct.

Also worth noting: tryptase is useful, but only if you collect it correctly. In this case, the recommended early sampling window was missed initially. If you suspect severe anaphylaxis, get serial tryptase without delaying treatment, and make sure the timing actually helps interpret the event later.

## The bottom line

This is not a story about a rare allergy to clarithromycin. It’s a story about the messiness of anaphylaxis disposition.

A patient can improve after epinephrine and still relapse hours later. A patient can have more than one recurrence. And the usual “observe for a few hours” rule is too blunt to handle every case.

## Take-home points

- **Anaphylaxis disposition should be risk-based, not timer-based.**
- **Severe initial shock, hypoxemia, and need for repeated epinephrine should push you toward prolonged monitored care.**
- **Biphasic and multiphasic reactions are real, and recurrence can happen after a full initial response.**
- **Steroids should not be relied on to prevent relapse.**
- **If the course is atypical or recurrent, think about mast cell disease and arrange allergy follow-up.**
- **The moment symptoms recur, the patient is no longer “simple observation” — they need reassessment and likely escalation.**
