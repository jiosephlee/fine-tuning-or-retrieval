Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Repeat IM or Start an Infusion? Managing Recurrent Anaphylaxis at the Bedside

## The case that should make you sit up

This is the kind of anaphylaxis case that makes you uncomfortable in the right way.

A woman in her 60s took a first dose of oral clarithromycin and, within an hour, developed classic severe anaphylaxis: pruritus, flushing, generalized erythema, urticaria, throat swelling sensation, hypotension, and hypoxaemia. She responded to intramuscular epinephrine, oxygen, and fluids. Then she relapsed. And relapsed again. Four anaphylactic episodes in all, with ICU transfer after the first recurrence.

That’s the dilemma: once the patient has responded, do you keep treating each relapse with IM epinephrine, or do you move earlier to a continuous IV epinephrine infusion?

## Why this case matters

Most anaphylaxis cases are straightforward in principle: give epinephrine early, support the ABCs, and watch for improvement. The hard part is when the story doesn’t end after the first response.

Biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon, but they matter because they create a false sense of security. The patient looks better, the blood pressure normalizes, the rash settles, and everyone starts thinking about disposition. Then the reaction returns without re-exposure.

In this case, the challenge was even more practical than semantic. Was this truly multiphasic anaphylaxis, or a smoldering reaction with temporary response to epinephrine? The distinction is fuzzy at the bedside, and the management question is the same either way: what do you do when the patient keeps declaring themselves unstable?

## IM epinephrine still owns the front door

The first move doesn’t change just because the reaction recurs: epinephrine remains the treatment.

For adults, the guideline dose is 0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg. In this patient, the first two doses were 0.3 mg auto-injectors, which the authors explicitly note were used for speed and familiarity in a time-sensitive setting. That makes sense operationally, but it also highlights an important bedside truth: a 0.3 mg auto-injector may underdose a large adult. This patient weighed 104 kg.

The reassuring part of the case is that each episode responded to IM epinephrine. That matters. If the reaction is recurrent but still epinephrine-responsive, repeating IM dosing is not “failing”; it is appropriate resuscitation.

## So when do you think about an infusion?

This is where the evidence gets thin and the judgment gets thick.

A continuous IV epinephrine infusion is attractive when the patient keeps relapsing, especially if the episodes are severe or the hemodynamics are not staying stable. In principle, it gives you titratability and the ability to stay ahead of recurrent symptoms rather than chasing them.

But the drip comes with real costs:

- it requires close monitoring
- it needs a team comfortable with vasoactive infusions
- it carries a higher risk of dosing error than a simple IM injection
- it usually pushes you into an ICU or equivalent monitored setting

That’s why this is not a casual ward decision. A patient who is having recurrent anaphylactic shock may absolutely deserve an epinephrine infusion, but only in the right environment.

In this case, the patient was admitted first to an acute internal medicine ward without telemetry, then transferred to ICU after the first relapse. That escalation was reasonable. Once a patient has demonstrated repeated shock, the question is no longer “Can we observe them?” but “Can we safely observe them where they are?”

## The tricky part: the episodes got milder

One of the most human parts of this case is that the episodes became less intense over time. That can tempt you toward watchful waiting. Allergology even considered two options: start a continuous epinephrine infusion preemptively, or take an expectant approach and see if the relapses would burn out on their own.

Both options had risk, and that’s exactly the point. The literature does not give us a clean algorithm for multiphasic anaphylaxis. In rare cases like this, you’re balancing two bad outcomes:

- overtreating with a drip and ICU-level care that may not be needed
- undertreating and being surprised by another crash

The patient ultimately stopped relapsing after the fourth episode, so neither escalation strategy was required. But that doesn’t make the dilemma go away for the next patient.

## Don’t let antihistamines and steroids steal the spotlight

This case also shows how easily adjuncts can create therapeutic noise.

She received clemastine for cutaneous symptoms, and steroids were added after recurrence because of their traditional role in preventing biphasic reactions. That’s a common reflex, but the evidence base is weak. The case report is consistent with the broader literature: epinephrine is the intervention that changes physiology; antihistamines and steroids do not rescue shock.

Steroids may still show up in local protocols, but they should not distract from the main event. If the patient is hypotensive or hypoxaemic, epinephrine is the move. Full stop.

## The mast cell clue you don’t want to miss

This patient later had a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. That is a big deal, because mast cell disorders can predispose to severe or recurrent anaphylaxis.

A couple of practical pearls fall out of that:

- recurrent or unusually severe anaphylaxis should make you think about underlying mast cell disease
- baseline tryptase matters
- tryptase timing matters even more

The report notes that the recommended sampling window was not followed perfectly. Ideally, you want an early sample without delaying treatment, a second sample around 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and a baseline sample after full resolution. If you miss the early samples, you lose part of the physiologic story.

## Bedside takeaways

- Recurrent anaphylaxis after apparent improvement is real; don’t assume the first response is the last event.
- Repeat IM epinephrine promptly for each relapse.
- A 0.3 mg auto-injector is fast and familiar, but it may underdose larger adults compared with the usual 0.5 mg adult target.
- IV epinephrine infusion is a reasonable escalation for recurrent or persistent severe anaphylaxis, but only where close monitoring and infusion expertise are available.
- Steroids and antihistamines are adjuncts, not substitutes for epinephrine, and steroids have not convincingly been shown to prevent biphasic reactions.
- Severe or recurrent anaphylaxis should prompt consideration of mast cell disease and properly timed tryptase sampling.

## Bottom line

This case doesn’t give us a neat protocol for multiphasic anaphylaxis, because the literature still doesn’t have one. What it does give us is a very real bedside lesson: when the patient keeps relapsing, you need to think beyond “did they respond?” and ask “can this setting safely handle the next relapse?”

Sometimes the answer is another IM dose. Sometimes it’s ICU and an infusion. The key is not to wait until the decision is made for you.
