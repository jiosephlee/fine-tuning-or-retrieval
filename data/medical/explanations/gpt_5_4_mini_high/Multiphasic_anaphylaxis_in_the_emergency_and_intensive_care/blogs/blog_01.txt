Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# When Anaphylaxis Comes Back: Biphasic, Multiphasic, or Just Not Finished?

## The part of this case that should make you sit up

A woman in her 60s took her first dose of oral clarithromycin and, within an hour, developed pruritus, flushing, diaphoresis, agitation, a sensation of throat swelling, hypotension, and hypoxemia. She improved after intramuscular epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, and an H1 blocker.

Then she relapsed. And relapsed again. And again.

By the end, she had had four episodes of anaphylaxis, each separated by apparent improvement and each responding to epinephrine. That is the clinical trap: the first response can look like the finish line, when in fact the process is still unfolding.

## Why the labels matter

We tend to talk about recurrent anaphylaxis as if the categories are neat. In practice, they aren’t.

- **Biphasic anaphylaxis**: symptoms recur after apparent resolution, without re-exposure.
- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis**: more than one recurrence.
- **Refractory anaphylaxis**: ongoing symptoms that do not adequately respond to appropriately administered epinephrine.
- **Ongoing antigen absorption**: especially with oral exposures, the reaction may not truly be “over” even if the patient briefly looks better.

This case lives in that gray zone. The trigger was a single oral dose of clarithromycin, so ongoing absorption is at least part of the differential early on. But the bedside reality was more important than the semantic debate: she had recurrent shock physiology after transient improvement.

That’s the key lesson. Improvement after epinephrine is reassuring, but it is not proof of resolution.

## What happened here, clinically

The patient had a known beta-lactam allergy and had recently been treated with clindamycin for erysipelas. After a rash developed, her antibiotic was switched to clarithromycin, which she had not previously received.

About an hour after taking clarithromycin, she presented with a classic severe anaphylaxis picture:

- hypotension
- hypoxemia
- facial swelling
- generalized erythema and urticaria
- agitation and diaphoresis

She received:

- **IM epinephrine 0.3 mg** via auto-injector
- **clemastine**
- **15 L oxygen**
- **500 mL normal saline**

She initially improved and was admitted to a monitored ward, but then developed recurrent episodes within the first 12 hours. After the first relapse, she went to the ICU. Each episode required epinephrine to settle. The first two doses were 0.3 mg auto-injector doses; the later two were 0.5 mg prepared IM doses.

The episodes became less intense over time, which can be falsely reassuring. Less dramatic does not mean resolved.

## Why transient improvement is not enough

Epinephrine reverses the dangerous physiology of anaphylaxis: vasodilation, capillary leak, bronchospasm, and mucosal edema. It does not remove the trigger. It does not guarantee the mast-cell activation cascade is finished.

That’s why a patient can look better, then suddenly decompensate again.

In this case, the relapses were separated by symptom-free intervals, which argues against simple treatment failure. Because each episode responded to epinephrine, this was not classic refractory anaphylaxis either. The practical concern is recurrent anaphylaxis with a multiphasic course.

## What to do when the reaction keeps coming back

The core treatment doesn’t change: **epinephrine first, epinephrine again if needed**.

A few bedside points matter:

- **Do not anchor on antihistamines or steroids.** They may help skin symptoms, but they do not treat the life-threatening part of anaphylaxis.
- **Fluids matter** when there is hypotension.
- **Airway and oxygenation monitoring matter** even if the first response is good.
- **Escalate monitoring** when there is shock, hypoxemia, or repeated epinephrine requirement.

This case also highlights a practical dosing issue. Adult guidelines commonly cite **0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg**. Many auto-injectors available in practice deliver **0.3 mg**, which can be a meaningful underdose in larger adults. The tradeoff is real: auto-injectors are fast, familiar, and less likely to be delayed in time-sensitive settings, but they may not match guideline adult dosing.

The team also considered a more aggressive strategy, including an IV epinephrine infusion, versus watching closely for further relapse. That’s a very honest reflection of the evidence gap: there is no strong playbook for multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## Observation, documentation, and the tryptase problem

This is where the case gets especially educational.

Observation after anaphylaxis is common because relapse can occur after the initial episode seems to have settled. Traditional observation windows have often been **4 to 6 hours**, but recurrent reactions may occur later, and published timing is variable. Severe initial presentation, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and delayed treatment are all reasons to think harder about monitoring intensity.

Just as important is documentation. In recurrent cases, the chart should clearly capture:

- timing of symptom onset
- exact epinephrine dose and route
- response to treatment
- timing and pattern of relapse
- any potential for ongoing exposure or absorption
- disposition and level of monitoring

Tryptase can help support the diagnosis and point toward mast-cell disease, but only if it is drawn correctly. The usual approach is:

- a sample as soon as feasible, without delaying treatment
- a repeat sample about **1–2 hours** after symptom onset
- a baseline sample **at least 24 hours after full resolution**

In this case, the initial tryptase sampling was not done at the ideal time, which is a common real-world miss. Later work-up showed elevated baseline and event tryptase, and the patient had a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria.

That matters because mast-cell disorders raise the stakes for severe or recurrent anaphylaxis.

## Steroids: still common, still controversial

The patient received corticosteroids after recurrence, reflecting older practice patterns and local protocols. But this is a good place to update our mental model: the evidence that steroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis is weak, and contemporary guidelines increasingly move away from routine steroid use.

So if you give steroids, don’t let that create a false sense of security. They are not the rescue therapy. Epinephrine is.

## Take-home points

- Apparent improvement after epinephrine does **not** mean the reaction is over.
- Recurrent symptoms after a symptom-free interval raise concern for **biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis**.
- If symptoms never truly clear, think **refractory anaphylaxis**; if the trigger may still be absorbing, think **ongoing antigen absorption**.
- Repeated epinephrine requirement, hypotension, and hypoxemia should lower your threshold for **ICU-level monitoring**.
- Tryptase is useful, but only if it is drawn at the right times — and never at the expense of prompt treatment.
- In adults, be aware that a **0.3 mg auto-injector** may underdose compared with the commonly cited **0.5 mg** adult dose.
- Steroids may still appear in protocols, but they should not distract from the main event: **epinephrine, fluids, and observation**.

This case is a good reminder that anaphylaxis is sometimes less a single event than a sequence. The patient may look better, the rash may fade, and the blood pressure may normalize — only to crash again. When that happens, the right response is not to debate the label first. It is to recognize that the danger may still be active.
