Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# 0.3 mg Is Not 0.5 mg: Adult Epinephrine Dosing and the Hidden Cost of Convenience

## A case that should make us uncomfortable

This case is interesting because it looks, at first, like a routine anaphylaxis resuscitation — and then it refuses to stay routine.

A woman in her 60s, weighing 104 kg, took her first dose of clarithromycin and within an hour developed pruritus, flushing, diaphoresis, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. When she arrived in the ED she was in shock: BP 74/50 mm Hg, SpO2 88% on room air, with facial swelling, generalized erythema, and urticaria.

She received 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via auto-injector, IV clemastine, oxygen, and fluids. She improved. Then the story turned: despite an initial response, she had recurrent episodes of anaphylaxis over the next 12 hours — four episodes in total — each requiring epinephrine. She was ultimately admitted to the ICU, and later the episodes stopped.

That is the clinical gut-punch here: the first dose was fast, familiar, and convenient — but in a 104 kg adult, it was not the guideline-supported adult dose.

## The dosing problem: convenience is not a treatment strategy

For adult anaphylaxis, epinephrine is still the treatment that matters most. The standard IM dose is 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum of 0.5 mg. In a 104 kg patient, the calculated dose exceeds the cap, so the practical adult target is 0.5 mg IM.

That means 0.3 mg is not a trivial difference. It is a 40% shortfall versus the adult maximum dose.

And that matters because epinephrine is not a drug where “close enough” is always close enough. In a crashing patient, we absolutely want speed — but speed should not quietly become underdosing. This case shows the temptation clearly: auto-injectors are easy, immediate, and reduce hesitation, especially in settings where epinephrine is used infrequently. The authors even note that this is why their team prefers them in time-sensitive situations or where staff may be less comfortable preparing vasoactive medication.

That logic is understandable. But once the patient is already in the ED, “easy to give” should not be the same thing as “good enough.”

## Multiphasic anaphylaxis: when the first improvement lies to you

This case also highlights the other trap: a good initial response does not mean the reaction is over.

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is defined by recurrence after apparent resolution without re-exposure to the allergen. It is uncommon, the predictors are unreliable, and the clinical course can be frustratingly messy. In this patient, the recurrent episodes got milder over time — from deep hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea to later episodes dominated by dyspnea, rash, and pruritus — but they still happened.

That’s the key bedside lesson: improvement after epinephrine does not equal safety. If a patient has severe initial anaphylaxis, needs repeated epinephrine, or relapses after seeming to stabilize, they have earned close monitoring and a low threshold for escalation.

In this case, the team considered two imperfect options after the first relapse: a continuous epinephrine infusion versus an expectant approach. That uncertainty reflects the broader problem — multiphasic anaphylaxis is real, but evidence-based management pathways are thin.

## Steroids: still widely used, not strongly supported

This patient received corticosteroids after recurrence, which is still common in many hospitals. But the evidence base is weak at best. Current literature has not shown corticosteroids to reliably prevent biphasic reactions, and some data even suggest harm or increased admission.

So the practical message is simple:

Epinephrine is the treatment.  
Steroids are not a substitute for epinephrine.  
Steroids are not a reason to feel reassured.

If you use them, use them as adjunctive therapy — not as the thing you’re hoping will save the day.

## Don’t miss the “why did this happen?” question

Because this course was unusually prolonged and recurrent, the team looked for an underlying predisposition. Their evaluation suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not met. Baseline and episode-related tryptase were elevated, and the patient had other findings that fit with mast cell disease, including hepatomegaly.

That matters because recurrent or unusually severe anaphylaxis should make you think beyond the trigger alone. Mast cell disorders can amplify the severity of reactions and may help explain why some patients behave so dramatically.

One more practical pearl from the report: tryptase timing matters, and it was not ideal here. If you think this is a true anaphylaxis case with a strange course, get the acute tryptase early, repeat it at the right interval, and remember to obtain a baseline later. That data can be invaluable when the diagnosis is not straightforward.

## Clinical pearls from the case

- In adults, anaphylaxis is treated with IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg.
- A 0.3 mg auto-injector is better than delay — but it is still an underdose for many adults, especially larger patients.
- Initial improvement does not rule out biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.
- Recurrent symptoms after apparent resolution should prompt close observation and consideration of ICU-level monitoring.
- Corticosteroids should not be relied on to prevent recurrence.
- Unusually severe or recurrent anaphylaxis should trigger thought about mast cell disorders, including systemic mastocytosis.

## Take-home points

- **0.3 mg is not 0.5 mg.** In a 104 kg adult, the difference is clinically meaningful.
- **Auto-injectors are a speed tool, not a dosing ideal.** Great for rapid access, but don’t let convenience become a ceiling.
- **Anaphylaxis can recur without re-exposure.** Multiphasic disease is rare, but real.
- **Epinephrine remains the core therapy.** Repeat it promptly when needed.
- **Steroids are not proven to prevent biphasic reactions.**
- **Think mast cell disease** when the course is unusually severe, recurrent, or prolonged.

The uncomfortable truth from this case is that the first dose of epinephrine may have been enough to produce a response, but not enough to deliver closure. In adult anaphylaxis, especially in heavier patients, the hidden cost of convenience is underdosing — and an underdosed anaphylaxis patient is one who can come back to bite you.
