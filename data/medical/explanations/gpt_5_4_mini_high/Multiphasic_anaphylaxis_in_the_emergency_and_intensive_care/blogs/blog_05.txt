Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Steroids in Anaphylaxis: Tradition, Evidence Drift, and Why Practice Hasn’t Caught Up

## The case that exposes the problem

A woman in her 60s took a single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin and, within an hour, developed full-throated anaphylaxis: hypotension, hypoxaemia, flushing, urticaria, facial swelling, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. She improved after IM epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, and clemastine.

Then she relapsed.

Not once. Multiple times. Over the first 12 hours, she had recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock, ultimately totaling four anaphylactic episodes. After the first recurrence, corticosteroids were added because that’s what the older anaphylaxis playbook has long suggested. In fact, she ended up receiving two different steroid regimens because the ward and ICU protocols didn’t even agree with each other.

That’s the teaching point: steroids are still embedded in anaphylaxis care, but the rationale has outpaced the evidence.

## Why steroids ever became part of anaphylaxis treatment

The historical appeal is easy to understand. Anaphylaxis has an acute phase and can have a delayed or biphasic recurrence, so corticosteroids seemed like the obvious way to blunt “the second wave.” They’re familiar, easy to prescribe, and often perceived as harmless.

But the story is more biologically satisfying than evidence-based.

Steroids are not rescue drugs for the acute airway, breathing, and circulation collapse of anaphylaxis. They act on a slower genomic timeline, which means they are not going to fix shock or laryngeal edema in the moment. Epinephrine is the lifesaving medication. Steroids are, at best, an adjunct for a separate indication.

## What the evidence actually says about biphasic prevention

The evidence for corticosteroids preventing biphasic anaphylaxis is weak to absent.

A 2017 systematic review looked at 22 studies: only one supported corticosteroids, while 21 did not show evidence that steroids reduce biphasic reactions. More recent work has pushed practice even further away from routine steroid use. One 2019 study even found an association between prehospital corticosteroid treatment and higher ICU/ward admission rates, although observational data like that can be confounded by severity.

The honest bottom line is simple: we do not have good evidence that corticosteroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. And if the data are thin for biphasic reactions, they are even thinner for multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## Why this case is especially instructive

This patient’s course was unusually prolonged and relapse-prone, and later workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. That kind of mast-cell disease may help explain why she behaved the way she did.

But that does not rescue steroids.

A patient with a mast-cell disorder may be more vulnerable to severe or recurrent mediator release, but that is not evidence that corticosteroids prevent recurrence. If anything, this case reminds us how tempting it is to keep adding medications when the patient keeps relapsing, even when the data behind those medications are shaky.

It also illustrates a very real systems problem: protocol inertia. In this case, steroids were given because they were part of traditional anaphylaxis management, then continued because different hospital protocols pointed in different directions. That’s common. It doesn’t make the practice right.

## What should we actually do at the bedside?

The first move in anaphylaxis is still the same: IM epinephrine early, repeat it if needed, give oxygen and fluids when indicated, and monitor appropriately for relapse.

For steroids, the key question is not “Do we give them by default?” It’s “What problem am I trying to solve?”

If the goal is preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, the answer from current evidence is: probably not much. If there is another steroid-responsive process going on—like concomitant asthma—that’s a different conversation. But that’s not the same as saying steroids are routine anaphylaxis therapy.

The practical trap is letting steroids become a substitute for real resuscitation. They aren’t.

## Take-home points

- Epinephrine is the treatment that changes outcomes in anaphylaxis; steroids do not replace it.
- The traditional idea that corticosteroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis is not supported by strong evidence.
- Contemporary guidance is moving away from routine steroid use in anaphylaxis.
- Relapsing anaphylaxis should trigger repeat epinephrine, reassessment, and longer observation—not reflex steroid escalation.
- In rare multiphasic cases, think about underlying mast cell disease, but don’t assume steroids will prevent recurrence.
