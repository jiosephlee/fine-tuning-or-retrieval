Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Understanding Complex Anaphylaxis: Core Concepts Needed for This Case

Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis made when a reaction begins suddenly and progresses rapidly, with involvement of the skin and/or mucosa plus compromise of the airway, breathing, or circulation, with or without gastrointestinal symptoms. In practice, the combination of flushing, urticaria, angioedema, throat tightness, dyspnea, wheeze, hypotension, syncope, or severe gastrointestinal symptoms after a likely trigger should prompt immediate recognition. The key point is that anaphylaxis is defined by the pattern and speed of illness, not by waiting for every possible organ system to become involved. In this case, the patient’s pruritus, generalized erythema and urticaria, facial swelling, hypoxemia, and marked hypotension fit a severe anaphylactic presentation with shock.

The underlying mechanism is acute mast-cell and basophil activation, most often through IgE-mediated allergen cross-linking, although non-IgE pathways can also contribute. Activated cells rapidly release preformed mediators such as histamine and tryptase and generate lipid mediators such as leukotrienes and prostaglandins. These substances cause vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, smooth muscle contraction, and mucosal edema. The result is plasma leakage into tissues, reduced effective circulating volume, airway swelling, bronchospasm, and impaired oxygenation. This explains the patient’s hypotension, dyspnea, hypoxemia, flushing, urticaria, and the sense of throat swelling. Tryptase is a useful laboratory marker because it reflects mast-cell activation, but it is supportive rather than diagnostic on its own and must be interpreted in relation to timing and the baseline level.

Drug-triggered anaphylaxis is common in acute care, and antibiotics are important triggers. Beta-lactams are classic causes, but other antibiotic classes, including macrolides such as clarithromycin, can also provoke severe reactions. A history of “first exposure” to the culprit drug does not exclude allergy. Patients may have been previously sensitized through an unrecognized exposure, cross-reactivity with related compounds, or a prior immune event that has gone unnoticed. In some drug reactions, non-IgE mechanisms may also produce an anaphylaxis-like picture. For this case, the absence of prior clarithromycin exposure does not make the diagnosis less plausible; the temporal relationship—symptom onset about an hour after ingestion—strongly supports a drug-induced anaphylactic reaction.

Complex anaphylaxis becomes challenging when symptoms recur after apparent resolution. A uniphasic reaction resolves with treatment and does not recur. Biphasic anaphylaxis refers to a second wave of symptoms after a symptom-free interval and without re-exposure to the trigger. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, as in this case, means more than one recurrence after the initial episode, again without new allergen exposure. This must be distinguished from sustained anaphylaxis, in which symptoms never fully clear but may appear to improve transiently, and from refractory anaphylaxis, in which the reaction persists despite appropriate epinephrine treatment. In this patient, each episode responded to epinephrine and there were symptom-free intervals, making a multiphasic pattern more likely than refractory disease.

Biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but clinically important, and reported incidence varies widely because studies use different definitions and observation periods. Most estimates fall around 4% to 6%, although published rates range from well under 1% to more than 20%. Recurrence usually occurs within the first 24 hours, but later relapses have been reported. Multiphasic reactions are rarer still and are not well quantified, which is why the literature offers limited guidance for cases like this one. Several features have been associated with a higher risk of recurrence, including severe initial presentation, delayed epinephrine administration, hypotension, and the need for multiple epinephrine doses. This patient had several of those risk features from the start, including shock and repeated epinephrine requirements.

These uncertainties explain why observation protocols differ and why management of complex anaphylaxis is difficult. Most patients with anaphylaxis do not relapse, so prolonged monitoring of everyone would be inefficient, but premature discharge risks missing a recurrent episode. Current guidelines therefore emphasize observation after symptom resolution, with longer monitoring for higher-risk presentations, yet they do not provide a perfect strategy for predicting who will relapse. The same uncertainty affects escalation decisions when symptoms recur: clinicians must choose between repeated rescue treatment, continuous vasopressor or epinephrine infusion, or watchful waiting. This case illustrates how quickly care can move from routine anaphylaxis treatment to a difficult ICU-level monitoring problem.

Finally, mast cell disorders are important because they can amplify anaphylaxis severity. In systemic mastocytosis, the body contains an abnormally increased mast-cell burden, which can lower the threshold for mediator release and make reactions more severe or prolonged. Elevated baseline tryptase and a heightened tryptase response during an event may suggest this biology, even if formal diagnostic criteria are not fully met. In a patient with unusually severe or recurrent anaphylaxis, especially with elevated baseline tryptase, clinicians should consider an underlying mast-cell disorder as a possible contributor to the clinical course.

## From First Dose to Shock: Clinical Presentation and Initial Emergency Assessment

This patient was a woman in her 60s with several chronic conditions, including type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. Her weight was 104 kg, which matters when thinking about epinephrine dosing because adult intramuscular doses are weight-based. She also had a history suggestive of atopy or drug sensitivity, including a remote reaction to amoxicillin with facial swelling and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, although neither event had previously caused a true systemic anaphylactic episode. Her regular medications did not obviously explain the acute presentation, and her last hydrochlorothiazide dose had been taken more than 12 hours earlier, making an immediate medication effect from her chronic drugs unlikely.

The antibiotic history is important for understanding trigger attribution. She had first been treated with clindamycin for erysipelas and developed a rash after several days, which was interpreted by her general practitioner as a delayed allergic reaction. On that basis, clindamycin was stopped and replaced with clarithromycin, a drug to which she had no known prior exposure. She then developed symptoms about 1 hour after the first oral dose of clarithromycin. That tight temporal relationship strongly supports clarithromycin as the likely trigger for the acute reaction, even though the preceding rash raises some uncertainty about whether the earlier antibiotic course had already caused immune activation or whether the patient was generally predisposed to drug hypersensitivity.

At presentation, the clinical picture was classic for severe anaphylaxis. She had pruritus, flushing, diaphoresis, agitation, a sensation of throat swelling, generalized erythema, urticaria, and facial swelling. More importantly, she was already hemodynamically and respiratorily compromised, with hypotension of 74/50 mm Hg and hypoxemia with an oxygen saturation of 88% on room air. In anaphylaxis, the diagnosis is made clinically: acute onset after exposure to a likely trigger, involvement of skin and mucosal findings, and compromise of the airway, breathing, or circulation. The absence of visible tongue or oropharyngeal swelling does not exclude anaphylaxis, because upper-airway edema can be present even when it is not obvious on inspection, and shock can occur without dramatic airway findings. Her normal heart and lung auscultation and sinus rhythm on ECG did not lower concern, because anaphylactic shock may present without wheeze or chest findings.

At the bedside, the main differential diagnosis included other causes of distributive shock and acute distress, such as sepsis, vasovagal syncope, panic reaction, isolated urticaria or angioedema, asthma exacerbation, or less likely cardiogenic causes. However, the combination of rapid onset after a new antibiotic, cutaneous manifestations, throat discomfort, hypoxemia, and hypotension makes anaphylaxis the most parsimonious diagnosis. The presence of shock distinguished this from a limited allergic rash or mild angioedema and made urgent treatment mandatory before any confirmatory testing.

Management priorities in this setting are straightforward: remove the suspected trigger, give intramuscular epinephrine immediately, support oxygenation and circulation, and monitor closely. Clarithromycin was stopped after the single initial dose, so no further exposure occurred. She received 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly via auto-injector, supplemental oxygen, and intravenous crystalloid. Epinephrine is the key treatment because it reverses vasodilation, reduces mucosal edema, supports blood pressure, and helps relieve bronchospasm; antihistamines and steroids do not replace it. Clemetastine was given for cutaneous symptoms, but this should be understood as adjunctive therapy only. In adults, the usual intramuscular epinephrine dose is 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg, which means that a 104 kg adult would generally be dosed at the upper limit. In practice, however, 0.3 mg auto-injectors are often used first in time-sensitive situations because they are immediately available and reduce treatment delay, even though they may underdose larger adults.

Although she improved initially, admission was still appropriate. Severe anaphylaxis can recur after apparent recovery, and the initial presentation already included shock, which places the patient at higher risk for further instability. Early hospital monitoring therefore remains essential even when the first epinephrine dose appears to work, because apparent recovery can be followed by relapse into biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## When Symptoms Return: Diagnostic Reasoning and Management of Multiphasic Anaphylaxis in the Ward and ICU

This patient’s course is best understood as a true multiphasic anaphylactic reaction: after the initial emergency department presentation, she improved with epinephrine and supportive care, but then developed three further episodes of anaphylaxis within the first 12 hours, for a total of four attacks. A useful teaching point is that the later episodes were less severe than the first, but that does not exclude multiphasic anaphylaxis. The defining feature is not severity, but recurrence after an apparent clinical response and a symptom-free interval, without deliberate re-exposure to the allergen.

In practice, the key diagnostic challenge is distinguishing multiphasic anaphylaxis from persistent or refractory disease. Refractory anaphylaxis implies ongoing symptoms despite correctly administered epinephrine, whereas this patient repeatedly returned to a stable state after each intramuscular dose and then relapsed later. That pattern argues against epinephrine failure and supports separate recurrences. Because the trigger was an orally administered drug, ongoing absorption was a theoretical alternative explanation, but the clinical picture of full transient responses followed by new episodes made multiphasic anaphylaxis the more appropriate working diagnosis.

The first recurrence was a major escalation point. She had already shown an initial episode of shock with hypoxemia, and relapse in that setting raised concern for further hemodynamic collapse and respiratory compromise. Transfer to the ICU was therefore appropriate for closer observation and rapid access to repeat treatment; the acute ward could provide increased nursing surveillance, but not the same intensity of monitoring. In anaphylaxis, ICU-level care is not required for every patient, but becomes reasonable when reactions recur, when shock has occurred, or when repeated epinephrine administration is anticipated.

Management in this case remained anchored in standard anaphylaxis treatment: prompt intramuscular epinephrine, oxygen when needed, and isotonic fluids for circulatory support. Each recurrence was treated with epinephrine, which is the correct response when symptoms return. The initial use of a 0.3 mg auto-injector reflects a common real-world compromise in time-sensitive settings, but the case also illustrates why this can be suboptimal in a heavier adult: at 104 kg, the guideline dose of 0.01 mg/kg would reach the adult maximum of 0.5 mg. That is why later doses were increased to 0.5 mg intramuscularly. The lesson is that underdosing may contribute to incomplete control or prompt the need for additional doses, particularly in larger adults.

Supportive therapy remained important throughout. Hypotension in anaphylaxis is driven by vasodilation and capillary leak, so intravenous crystalloid boluses are part of first-line care, not just an afterthought. Likewise, supplemental oxygen is appropriate when there is hypoxemia or respiratory distress, and in this patient oxygen was restarted during recurrent episodes as needed. These measures do not replace epinephrine, but they stabilize the ABCs while epinephrine takes effect.

Corticosteroids were added after the first relapse, reflecting older practice and local protocols that still aim to prevent biphasic reactions. This is a common real-world decision, but it should be interpreted critically. Current evidence does not show that steroids reliably prevent biphasic anaphylaxis, and contemporary guidance increasingly discourages routine use for that purpose. In this case, steroid administration was protocol-driven rather than evidence-based, and the use of both prednisone and dexamethasone also exposed inconsistency between ward and ICU pathways. That inconsistency is itself an important systems lesson from the case.

The treating team also considered more advanced strategies, including a pre-emptive intravenous epinephrine infusion versus watchful waiting as the later episodes became somewhat less intense. This dilemma highlights the lack of evidence for multiphasic anaphylaxis specifically. Standard guidelines largely address single-episode anaphylaxis or general observation after resolution, not repeated relapses over many hours. In such situations, clinicians must individualize care, balancing the risk of another shock episode against the risks of continuous vasoactive treatment.

Finally, the observation period needs to be interpreted in light of the recurrence pattern. After the last episode, she remained stable for about 24 hours before being moved back to standard care, which was appropriate given her prior relapses. Traditional 4- to 6-hour observation windows may be adequate for many patients after complete symptom resolution, but they are not reassuring once a patient has already demonstrated a multiphasic course. This case therefore illustrates why recurrent anaphylaxis requires longer monitoring, repeated reassessment, and careful coordination across the ED, ward, and ICU.

## Searching for the Underlying Cause: Trigger Attribution, Tryptase Interpretation, and Possible Systemic Mastocytosis

Once the patient had been stabilized, the key diagnostic question was not only what had caused the initial reaction, but also why the course was unusually prolonged and recurrent. In this case, clarithromycin was the leading suspected trigger because the first episode began about 1 hour after the first dose, which is the classic time course for an IgE-mediated or otherwise immediate anaphylactic reaction. The preceding abdominal rash after several days of clindamycin was less convincing as the cause of the shock-like episodes, because it was delayed, poorly characterized, and not followed by systemic collapse until after clarithromycin exposure. In other words, the temporal relationship strongly supported clarithromycin as the culprit, but without confirmatory testing the attribution remained clinical rather than definitive.

An atypical anaphylactic course, especially one with recurrent relapses after apparently adequate treatment, should prompt additional etiologic workup after the acute phase. The purpose is twofold: to document mast-cell activation and to look for a predisposition to severe reactions. This is particularly relevant when the presentation is severe, requires repeated epinephrine, or shows a multiphasic pattern. In such patients, clinicians should consider whether the severity is explained solely by the trigger, or whether there is an underlying mast-cell disorder or other condition that amplifies mediator release.

Serum tryptase is the most commonly used laboratory marker of mast-cell activation. It is released from mast cells during degranulation, rises within a few hours of symptom onset, and then gradually returns to baseline. For that reason, interpretation depends heavily on sample timing. The recommended approach is to obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, ideally within 1 to 2 hours of symptom onset and no later than 4 hours, followed by a baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical recovery. In this case, the first tryptase was drawn only after the third anaphylactic episode, and the baseline sample was drawn 27 hours after the final recurrence. That means the earliest episodes were not captured optimally, so the laboratory data could not be used to reconstruct the full tryptase pattern across all relapses. Even so, an elevated event-related tryptase together with a persistently elevated baseline tryptase supports true mast-cell activation and suggests that the patient may have an increased resting mast-cell burden.

Persistently elevated baseline tryptase is not specific for systemic mastocytosis. The differential diagnosis includes hereditary alpha-tryptasemia, chronic kidney disease, certain hematologic or myeloid disorders, and other mast-cell diseases. Therefore, baseline tryptase should be viewed as a clue that justifies further evaluation, not as a standalone diagnosis. In this patient, the persistently elevated tryptase values made a mast-cell disorder plausible, but they did not by themselves establish the exact cause.

The formal WHO diagnostic framework for systemic mastocytosis requires either the major criterion plus one minor criterion, or at least three minor criteria. The major criterion is multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone marrow or another extracutaneous organ. The minor criteria include abnormal mast-cell morphology, an activating KIT mutation, aberrant expression of mast-cell markers such as CD25 or CD2, and a baseline serum tryptase level above 20 ng/mL. In this case, the bone marrow sample was limited, so the major criterion could not be adequately assessed. The report states that only two minor criteria were met. As a result, the patient had a high suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, but she did not fulfill formal diagnostic criteria.

Other findings were suggestive but not diagnostic. Hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound can be seen in systemic mast-cell disease, and the history of depression and memory problems raised the possibility of broader systemic involvement, but these features are nonspecific. They can support clinical suspicion, yet they cannot confirm mastocytosis on their own. This is a common issue in real practice: the available evidence may point toward a mast-cell disorder, but insufficient tissue or incomplete criteria prevent a definitive label.

Formal allergy confirmation testing was not pursued because the risk-benefit balance was unfavorable. Skin testing or drug provocation after a severe, recurrent anaphylactic reaction can provoke another life-threatening event, and in this case avoidance of clarithromycin was straightforward. When the suspected drug can simply be omitted and the likelihood of a dangerous re-exposure is high, it is reasonable to defer confirmatory testing. That approach accepts some diagnostic uncertainty in exchange for patient safety.

Finally, the case highlights an important pathophysiologic idea: mastocytosis and related mast-cell disorders can increase the severity of anaphylaxis by providing a larger pool of reactive mast cells and promoting exaggerated mediator release. That makes severe or recurrent reactions more plausible. However, whether mastocytosis specifically predisposes to biphasic or multiphasic recurrence is still uncertain. The association is biologically plausible, but the evidence remains too limited to draw firm conclusions.

## Outcome, Secondary Prevention, and Clinical Lessons for Practice

In this case, the patient’s anaphylaxis ultimately settled after several relapses, and no further recurrence occurred after roughly 24 hours of stability. She was stepped down from the ICU back to a standard ward and discharged after 3 days in hospital. Because the episode was severe, unusual, and multiphasic, discharge planning focused not only on immediate recovery but also on reducing the risk of future episodes and ensuring that she could respond quickly if symptoms recurred outside the hospital.

Secondary prevention in anaphylaxis begins with avoidance of the suspected trigger. Here, clarithromycin was stopped after a single dose and should be avoided in the future unless an allergist later determines otherwise. At discharge, she was given levocetirizine and, more importantly, prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector and taught how to use it. Patient education is essential: she needed to recognize early symptoms such as flushing, pruritus, throat tightness, dyspnoea, dizziness, or swelling, and understand that epinephrine is the first-line treatment and should be administered immediately if symptoms suggest recurrent anaphylaxis. Antihistamines may help with cutaneous symptoms, but they do not prevent airway compromise or shock.

This case also illustrates why follow-up after severe or atypical anaphylaxis should extend beyond routine discharge advice. Referral to allergy/immunology is appropriate for trigger assessment, counseling about future drug avoidance, and evaluation for underlying predisposition. In this patient, the prolonged and recurrent course, elevated tryptase values, hepatomegaly, and other features prompted investigation for a mast cell disorder. Even though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis, the case shows that unusual anaphylaxis should raise suspicion for an underlying mast-cell–driven condition and justify specialist follow-up.

For disposition decisions, the key lesson is that some patients require prolonged observation rather than short ED monitoring. Biphasic or multiphasic relapse may occur after initial improvement, and the first recurrence often happens within the first 6 to 12 hours, although later recurrence can occur. This patient’s repeated relapses within the first 12 hours justified escalation to ICU-level monitoring. At the same time, this case underscores the limits of current risk stratification: severe initial presentation, need for repeated epinephrine, and possible mast cell disease may suggest higher risk, but no bedside rule reliably predicts which patients will relapse.

A practical point highlighted by the report is epinephrine dosing. Guidelines recommend intramuscular epinephrine at 0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg in adults. In larger adults, a 0.3 mg auto-injector can represent underdosing, although it may still be the fastest and most practical way to deliver epinephrine when immediate treatment is needed. The central principle is not to delay epinephrine while waiting for a “perfect” dose or formulation; however, clinicians should recognize that standard adult care often requires 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine when prepared and administered in a monitored setting.

The case also reflects the current evidence-based shift away from relying on antihistamines and corticosteroids as preventive therapy. H1-antihistamines can be useful for urticaria and pruritus, but they are adjunctive only. Corticosteroids were historically used to prevent biphasic reactions, yet evidence has not shown clear benefit, and they should not replace epinephrine or delay its administration. In complex courses like this one, supportive treatment remains secondary to repeated, prompt epinephrine and close observation.

Finally, the report highlights broader evidence gaps. Biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis remain difficult to define consistently, which makes studies hard to compare and weakens guideline recommendations. Better standardized definitions, multicentre registries, and consensus management strategies are needed, particularly for patients with recurrent episodes despite appropriate treatment. Until then, this case supports a cautious, individualized approach: treat early with epinephrine, observe longer when the initial reaction is severe or recurrent, arrange specialist follow-up, and ensure the patient leaves with both education and the means to treat a future episode immediately.
