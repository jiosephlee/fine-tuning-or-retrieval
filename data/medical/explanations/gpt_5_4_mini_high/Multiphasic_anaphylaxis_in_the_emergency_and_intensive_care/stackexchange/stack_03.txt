Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Several hours after complete initial improvement and without further clarithromycin exposure, the patient had recurrent episodes of hypotension, dyspnea, rash, and pruritus. How would you differentiate biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis from refractory anaphylaxis and from persistent symptoms due to ongoing allergen absorption?

The key distinction is the presence or absence of a true symptom-free interval after adequate treatment. In **biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis**, the patient has **complete or near-complete initial resolution** of hypotension, dyspnea, rash, and pruritus, and then develops **new recurrent episodes hours later without re-exposure** to the trigger. In this case, the patient improved after the first epinephrine dose, had **no further clarithromycin exposure**, and then had **multiple distinct relapses over the next 12 hours**, which is most consistent with **multiphasic anaphylaxis**.

By contrast, **refractory anaphylaxis** is not a relapse after recovery; it is **persistent anaphylaxis that does not adequately respond to correctly administered epinephrine**, or only transiently improves before immediately deteriorating again. The patient never truly stabilizes. So if the shock, bronchospasm, or cutaneous findings never fully clear, think refractory disease rather than biphasic or multiphasic reaction.

Finally, **ongoing allergen absorption** should be considered when the trigger was **ingested or slow-release**, and symptoms continue because the allergen is still entering the circulation. That scenario usually produces **persistent or waxing-and-waning symptoms without a clean asymptomatic interval**. In this case, although the trigger was an oral drug, the report emphasizes **complete initial improvement** before the later episodes, which argues against ongoing absorption and favors **multiphasic anaphylaxis**.
