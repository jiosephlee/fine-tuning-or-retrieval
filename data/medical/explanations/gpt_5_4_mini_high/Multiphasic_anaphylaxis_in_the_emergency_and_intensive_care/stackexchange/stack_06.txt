Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical factors in this case increase the risk of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and therefore support prolonged observation beyond the traditional 4- to 6-hour emergency department monitoring period?

The key reasons in this case are the **severity of the index anaphylaxis** and the **early recurrence after apparent response**.

She presented with **true anaphylactic shock** — marked **hypotension (74/50 mm Hg)** and **hypoxemia (SpO₂ 88%)** — along with diffuse flushing, urticaria, and facial swelling. Severe cardiovascular and respiratory involvement is one of the clearest clinical signals for a higher risk of biphasic or multiphasic disease. In addition, she **required repeated intramuscular epinephrine** for recurrent episodes, with **four total anaphylactic episodes** over the first 12 hours. The need for **more than one epinephrine dose** and the fact that she had **already relapsed after initial improvement** are strong practical reasons to continue monitored observation rather than discharge after a routine 4- to 6-hour period.

Two additional features heighten concern. First, there was some **delay from symptom onset to definitive treatment**, which is a recognized risk factor for recurrence. Second, the later workup suggested a **mast-cell disorder with elevated tryptase and probable systemic mastocytosis**; a high mast-cell burden plausibly predisposes to more severe and recurrent mediator release.

So, in this patient, the combination of **shock-level severity, recurrent symptoms, repeated epinephrine requirement, possible delayed treatment, and suspected mastocytosis** supports prolonged observation — and, once relapse occurred, ICU-level monitoring was appropriate.
