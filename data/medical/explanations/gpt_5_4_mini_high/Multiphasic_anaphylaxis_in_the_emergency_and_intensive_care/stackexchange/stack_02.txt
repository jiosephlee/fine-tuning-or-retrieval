Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient developed a rash after 5 days of clindamycin, then had severe systemic symptoms 1 hour after her first dose of clarithromycin. Which antibiotic is the more likely trigger of the anaphylactic event, and what aspects of timing and symptom pattern help distinguish an immediate drug allergy from a delayed cutaneous drug eruption?

The **more likely trigger of the anaphylactic event is clarithromycin**, not clindamycin.

In this case, the key discriminator is **timing plus symptom pattern**. The rash that appeared **after 5 days of clindamycin** is much more consistent with a **delayed cutaneous drug eruption**—typically a T-cell–mediated exanthem that develops **days after exposure** and is usually limited to the skin. By contrast, her severe illness began **1 hour after the first dose of clarithromycin** and included **pruritus, flushing, generalized urticaria, facial swelling, throat symptoms, hypotension, and hypoxemia**—the classic pattern of **immediate anaphylaxis**, which occurs **within minutes to a few hours** of drug exposure and involves **more than one organ system**, especially skin plus airway/breathing/circulation.

So, in practical terms:  
- **Delayed rash after clindamycin** → suggests a **delayed hypersensitivity eruption**  
- **Rapid multisystem collapse after clarithromycin** → strongly supports **immediate drug allergy/anaphylaxis**

This is exactly the distinction highlighted by the case: the clindamycin rash was a separate, delayed cutaneous reaction, whereas clarithromycin was the likely precipitant of the anaphylactic episode.
