Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] What is the current evidence-based role of corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis and in prevention of biphasic reactions, and how should that evidence influence management in a patient with recurrent anaphylactic episodes?

Corticosteroids should **not** be considered a primary treatment for acute anaphylaxis, and the current evidence does **not** support their routine use to prevent biphasic reactions. Their onset is too slow to reverse the immediate airway, breathing, or circulatory compromise of anaphylaxis, and the best available data do not show that they reduce recurrence. As the case report notes, a systematic review found **no convincing benefit** for biphasic prevention, and subsequent observational studies have even raised concern for greater resource use or harm with prehospital steroid administration.

The management implication is straightforward: in a patient with recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis, **epinephrine remains the treatment that matters**. Corticosteroids should never delay repeat intramuscular epinephrine, aggressive supportive care, or escalation to an intravenous epinephrine infusion if shock is persistent or refractory. In the reported patient, relapse occurred despite corticosteroids, which illustrates the key point: steroids did not prevent further episodes.

So, in practice, recurrent anaphylaxis should prompt:
- **Prompt repeat IM epinephrine** for each recurrence, at guideline dose
- **IV fluids and oxygen** as needed
- **Close observation, often in ICU**, when there is hypotension, repeated epinephrine requirement, or early relapse
- **Search for an underlying predisposition** when the course is unusually severe or prolonged, as in this case, where mast-cell disease was suspected

In short, corticosteroids are at most an adjunct for selected associated problems, but they are **not evidence-based prophylaxis against biphasic anaphylaxis**. The presence of recurrent episodes should shift attention to adequate epinephrine treatment and vigilant monitoring, not to steroid escalation.
