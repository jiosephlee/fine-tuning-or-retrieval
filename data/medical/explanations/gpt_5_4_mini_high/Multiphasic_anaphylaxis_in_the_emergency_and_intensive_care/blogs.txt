Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# When Anaphylaxis Comes Back: Biphasic, Multiphasic, or Just Not Finished?

## The part of this case that should make you sit up

A woman in her 60s took her first dose of oral clarithromycin and, within an hour, developed pruritus, flushing, diaphoresis, agitation, a sensation of throat swelling, hypotension, and hypoxemia. She improved after intramuscular epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, and an H1 blocker.

Then she relapsed. And relapsed again. And again.

By the end, she had had four episodes of anaphylaxis, each separated by apparent improvement and each responding to epinephrine. That is the clinical trap: the first response can look like the finish line, when in fact the process is still unfolding.

## Why the labels matter

We tend to talk about recurrent anaphylaxis as if the categories are neat. In practice, they aren’t.

- **Biphasic anaphylaxis**: symptoms recur after apparent resolution, without re-exposure.
- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis**: more than one recurrence.
- **Refractory anaphylaxis**: ongoing symptoms that do not adequately respond to appropriately administered epinephrine.
- **Ongoing antigen absorption**: especially with oral exposures, the reaction may not truly be “over” even if the patient briefly looks better.

This case lives in that gray zone. The trigger was a single oral dose of clarithromycin, so ongoing absorption is at least part of the differential early on. But the bedside reality was more important than the semantic debate: she had recurrent shock physiology after transient improvement.

That’s the key lesson. Improvement after epinephrine is reassuring, but it is not proof of resolution.

## What happened here, clinically

The patient had a known beta-lactam allergy and had recently been treated with clindamycin for erysipelas. After a rash developed, her antibiotic was switched to clarithromycin, which she had not previously received.

About an hour after taking clarithromycin, she presented with a classic severe anaphylaxis picture:

- hypotension
- hypoxemia
- facial swelling
- generalized erythema and urticaria
- agitation and diaphoresis

She received:

- **IM epinephrine 0.3 mg** via auto-injector
- **clemastine**
- **15 L oxygen**
- **500 mL normal saline**

She initially improved and was admitted to a monitored ward, but then developed recurrent episodes within the first 12 hours. After the first relapse, she went to the ICU. Each episode required epinephrine to settle. The first two doses were 0.3 mg auto-injector doses; the later two were 0.5 mg prepared IM doses.

The episodes became less intense over time, which can be falsely reassuring. Less dramatic does not mean resolved.

## Why transient improvement is not enough

Epinephrine reverses the dangerous physiology of anaphylaxis: vasodilation, capillary leak, bronchospasm, and mucosal edema. It does not remove the trigger. It does not guarantee the mast-cell activation cascade is finished.

That’s why a patient can look better, then suddenly decompensate again.

In this case, the relapses were separated by symptom-free intervals, which argues against simple treatment failure. Because each episode responded to epinephrine, this was not classic refractory anaphylaxis either. The practical concern is recurrent anaphylaxis with a multiphasic course.

## What to do when the reaction keeps coming back

The core treatment doesn’t change: **epinephrine first, epinephrine again if needed**.

A few bedside points matter:

- **Do not anchor on antihistamines or steroids.** They may help skin symptoms, but they do not treat the life-threatening part of anaphylaxis.
- **Fluids matter** when there is hypotension.
- **Airway and oxygenation monitoring matter** even if the first response is good.
- **Escalate monitoring** when there is shock, hypoxemia, or repeated epinephrine requirement.

This case also highlights a practical dosing issue. Adult guidelines commonly cite **0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg**. Many auto-injectors available in practice deliver **0.3 mg**, which can be a meaningful underdose in larger adults. The tradeoff is real: auto-injectors are fast, familiar, and less likely to be delayed in time-sensitive settings, but they may not match guideline adult dosing.

The team also considered a more aggressive strategy, including an IV epinephrine infusion, versus watching closely for further relapse. That’s a very honest reflection of the evidence gap: there is no strong playbook for multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## Observation, documentation, and the tryptase problem

This is where the case gets especially educational.

Observation after anaphylaxis is common because relapse can occur after the initial episode seems to have settled. Traditional observation windows have often been **4 to 6 hours**, but recurrent reactions may occur later, and published timing is variable. Severe initial presentation, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and delayed treatment are all reasons to think harder about monitoring intensity.

Just as important is documentation. In recurrent cases, the chart should clearly capture:

- timing of symptom onset
- exact epinephrine dose and route
- response to treatment
- timing and pattern of relapse
- any potential for ongoing exposure or absorption
- disposition and level of monitoring

Tryptase can help support the diagnosis and point toward mast-cell disease, but only if it is drawn correctly. The usual approach is:

- a sample as soon as feasible, without delaying treatment
- a repeat sample about **1–2 hours** after symptom onset
- a baseline sample **at least 24 hours after full resolution**

In this case, the initial tryptase sampling was not done at the ideal time, which is a common real-world miss. Later work-up showed elevated baseline and event tryptase, and the patient had a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria.

That matters because mast-cell disorders raise the stakes for severe or recurrent anaphylaxis.

## Steroids: still common, still controversial

The patient received corticosteroids after recurrence, reflecting older practice patterns and local protocols. But this is a good place to update our mental model: the evidence that steroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis is weak, and contemporary guidelines increasingly move away from routine steroid use.

So if you give steroids, don’t let that create a false sense of security. They are not the rescue therapy. Epinephrine is.

## Take-home points

- Apparent improvement after epinephrine does **not** mean the reaction is over.
- Recurrent symptoms after a symptom-free interval raise concern for **biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis**.
- If symptoms never truly clear, think **refractory anaphylaxis**; if the trigger may still be absorbing, think **ongoing antigen absorption**.
- Repeated epinephrine requirement, hypotension, and hypoxemia should lower your threshold for **ICU-level monitoring**.
- Tryptase is useful, but only if it is drawn at the right times — and never at the expense of prompt treatment.
- In adults, be aware that a **0.3 mg auto-injector** may underdose compared with the commonly cited **0.5 mg** adult dose.
- Steroids may still appear in protocols, but they should not distract from the main event: **epinephrine, fluids, and observation**.

This case is a good reminder that anaphylaxis is sometimes less a single event than a sequence. The patient may look better, the rash may fade, and the blood pressure may normalize — only to crash again. When that happens, the right response is not to debate the label first. It is to recognize that the danger may still be active.



# 0.3 mg Is Not 0.5 mg: Adult Epinephrine Dosing and the Hidden Cost of Convenience

## A case that should make us uncomfortable

This case is interesting because it looks, at first, like a routine anaphylaxis resuscitation — and then it refuses to stay routine.

A woman in her 60s, weighing 104 kg, took her first dose of clarithromycin and within an hour developed pruritus, flushing, diaphoresis, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. When she arrived in the ED she was in shock: BP 74/50 mm Hg, SpO2 88% on room air, with facial swelling, generalized erythema, and urticaria.

She received 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via auto-injector, IV clemastine, oxygen, and fluids. She improved. Then the story turned: despite an initial response, she had recurrent episodes of anaphylaxis over the next 12 hours — four episodes in total — each requiring epinephrine. She was ultimately admitted to the ICU, and later the episodes stopped.

That is the clinical gut-punch here: the first dose was fast, familiar, and convenient — but in a 104 kg adult, it was not the guideline-supported adult dose.

## The dosing problem: convenience is not a treatment strategy

For adult anaphylaxis, epinephrine is still the treatment that matters most. The standard IM dose is 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum of 0.5 mg. In a 104 kg patient, the calculated dose exceeds the cap, so the practical adult target is 0.5 mg IM.

That means 0.3 mg is not a trivial difference. It is a 40% shortfall versus the adult maximum dose.

And that matters because epinephrine is not a drug where “close enough” is always close enough. In a crashing patient, we absolutely want speed — but speed should not quietly become underdosing. This case shows the temptation clearly: auto-injectors are easy, immediate, and reduce hesitation, especially in settings where epinephrine is used infrequently. The authors even note that this is why their team prefers them in time-sensitive situations or where staff may be less comfortable preparing vasoactive medication.

That logic is understandable. But once the patient is already in the ED, “easy to give” should not be the same thing as “good enough.”

## Multiphasic anaphylaxis: when the first improvement lies to you

This case also highlights the other trap: a good initial response does not mean the reaction is over.

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is defined by recurrence after apparent resolution without re-exposure to the allergen. It is uncommon, the predictors are unreliable, and the clinical course can be frustratingly messy. In this patient, the recurrent episodes got milder over time — from deep hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea to later episodes dominated by dyspnea, rash, and pruritus — but they still happened.

That’s the key bedside lesson: improvement after epinephrine does not equal safety. If a patient has severe initial anaphylaxis, needs repeated epinephrine, or relapses after seeming to stabilize, they have earned close monitoring and a low threshold for escalation.

In this case, the team considered two imperfect options after the first relapse: a continuous epinephrine infusion versus an expectant approach. That uncertainty reflects the broader problem — multiphasic anaphylaxis is real, but evidence-based management pathways are thin.

## Steroids: still widely used, not strongly supported

This patient received corticosteroids after recurrence, which is still common in many hospitals. But the evidence base is weak at best. Current literature has not shown corticosteroids to reliably prevent biphasic reactions, and some data even suggest harm or increased admission.

So the practical message is simple:

Epinephrine is the treatment.  
Steroids are not a substitute for epinephrine.  
Steroids are not a reason to feel reassured.

If you use them, use them as adjunctive therapy — not as the thing you’re hoping will save the day.

## Don’t miss the “why did this happen?” question

Because this course was unusually prolonged and recurrent, the team looked for an underlying predisposition. Their evaluation suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not met. Baseline and episode-related tryptase were elevated, and the patient had other findings that fit with mast cell disease, including hepatomegaly.

That matters because recurrent or unusually severe anaphylaxis should make you think beyond the trigger alone. Mast cell disorders can amplify the severity of reactions and may help explain why some patients behave so dramatically.

One more practical pearl from the report: tryptase timing matters, and it was not ideal here. If you think this is a true anaphylaxis case with a strange course, get the acute tryptase early, repeat it at the right interval, and remember to obtain a baseline later. That data can be invaluable when the diagnosis is not straightforward.

## Clinical pearls from the case

- In adults, anaphylaxis is treated with IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg.
- A 0.3 mg auto-injector is better than delay — but it is still an underdose for many adults, especially larger patients.
- Initial improvement does not rule out biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.
- Recurrent symptoms after apparent resolution should prompt close observation and consideration of ICU-level monitoring.
- Corticosteroids should not be relied on to prevent recurrence.
- Unusually severe or recurrent anaphylaxis should trigger thought about mast cell disorders, including systemic mastocytosis.

## Take-home points

- **0.3 mg is not 0.5 mg.** In a 104 kg adult, the difference is clinically meaningful.
- **Auto-injectors are a speed tool, not a dosing ideal.** Great for rapid access, but don’t let convenience become a ceiling.
- **Anaphylaxis can recur without re-exposure.** Multiphasic disease is rare, but real.
- **Epinephrine remains the core therapy.** Repeat it promptly when needed.
- **Steroids are not proven to prevent biphasic reactions.**
- **Think mast cell disease** when the course is unusually severe, recurrent, or prolonged.

The uncomfortable truth from this case is that the first dose of epinephrine may have been enough to produce a response, but not enough to deliver closure. In adult anaphylaxis, especially in heavier patients, the hidden cost of convenience is underdosing — and an underdosed anaphylaxis patient is one who can come back to bite you.



# How Long Should You Watch Them? Disposition After Severe Anaphylaxis

## The case that makes disposition feel impossible

The hard part of anaphylaxis is usually not the diagnosis. It’s the part that comes after the epinephrine: **is this patient truly safe, or are we just in the calm before the next wave?**

This case is a perfect example. A woman in her 60s took a single oral dose of clarithromycin and developed classic severe anaphylaxis: pruritus, flushing, generalized erythema and urticaria, facial swelling, agitation, hypoxemia, and hypotension to 74/50 mm Hg. She improved after IM epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, and antihistamine. Then she relapsed. And relapsed again. In total, she had **four anaphylactic episodes** despite no further allergen exposure.

That’s the uncomfortable reality: some anaphylaxis is not a one-and-done event. A patient can look better, even dramatically better, and still crash again hours later.

## Biphasic, multiphasic, refractory — the labels matter less than the physiology

We tend to talk about **biphasic anaphylaxis** as recurrence after apparent resolution, and **multiphasic anaphylaxis** when there are multiple relapses. **Refractory anaphylaxis** is a different beast: persistent symptoms that never really respond adequately to correctly given epinephrine.

In the real world, these categories blur. A patient who improves after epinephrine, then deteriorates again, may have true biphasic or multiphasic disease — or may simply have ongoing mediator release with transient improvement. Either way, the disposition problem is the same:

- Does this person need prolonged monitored observation?
- Do they need step-up care?
- Is the floor enough?
- Should ICU be involved early?

This case argues for humility. The first response can be deceiving.

## The observation clock is useful, but it is not magic

We still do not have a perfect observation duration for anaphylaxis. Traditional practice has often been **4–6 hours after symptom resolution**, but that window is not a guarantee. Reported biphasic reactions vary widely, and many occur within the first 6–12 hours — but some happen later, even well beyond the classic ED observation period.

That’s why disposition should be **risk-based**, not purely time-based.

The patient in this case had several features that should make you uneasy:

- **Severe initial presentation** with hypotension and hypoxemia
- **Need for epinephrine**
- **Recurrence after initial improvement**
- Ultimately, **multiple epinephrine doses**
- A later workup suggesting a **mast cell disorder phenotype** (high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though formal criteria were not fully met)

These are not just academic details. They are the exact features that should push you away from “looks good, discharge” thinking.

## What should make you worry about recurrence?

The literature does not give us a validated crystal ball, but the repeatedly suggested risk markers are familiar:

- Delayed epinephrine administration
- Severe initial reaction
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses
- Prior biphasic reactions

None of these perfectly predicts recurrence, but they all tell the same story: the patient has already declared themselves to be higher risk.

This case also highlights a practical issue that gets overlooked: **dose matters**. She weighed 104 kg, and the initial epi doses came from a 0.3 mg auto-injector because that was the fastest available option. That is understandable in time-sensitive care, but it’s also a reminder that many adult auto-injectors underdose larger patients compared with the commonly recommended adult maximum dose of 0.5 mg.

Fast epinephrine is better than perfect epinephrine delayed. But if you’re deciding whether a patient is truly out of the woods, you should know exactly what they received.

## Ward or ICU?

This is where the art comes in.

A patient with resolved, low-risk anaphylaxis may be appropriate for a brief monitored observation period and discharge with education, an epinephrine auto-injector, and follow-up. But once the case becomes severe, recurrent, or hemodynamically unstable, the floor may not be enough.

In this report, the patient initially went to an acute internal ward without telemetry after seeming to respond. She then relapsed within 12 hours and required ICU transfer. That sequence is the lesson: **a patient who has already relapsed is no longer in the “simple observation” category.**

A practical way to think about disposition:

- **ED observation / ward care** may be reasonable when symptoms resolve promptly after a single dose, there is no recurrence, and the clinical course is otherwise reassuring.
- **Step-up or ICU-level monitoring** becomes much more appropriate when there is shock, hypoxemia, airway concern, repeated epinephrine requirement, or early relapse.
- If you are even considering an epinephrine infusion, that patient is already in escalation territory.

Importantly, this is not about punishing the patient with ICU. It’s about matching the monitoring environment to the possibility of abrupt deterioration.

## Steroids: don’t let them fool you

This case received corticosteroids after the first recurrence, which is still common in many protocols. But the evidence base for steroids preventing biphasic reactions is weak at best, and more recent thinking has moved away from routine steroid use in anaphylaxis.

That matters for disposition because steroids can create a false sense of security.

Steroids may still be used for selected reasons, but they should **not** be the reason you shorten observation or downgrade monitoring. They do not replace epinephrine, and they do not reliably prevent relapse.

## Don’t forget the long game: mast cell disease and allergy follow-up

This patient’s prolonged and unusually recurrent course triggered a workup that showed a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met. Baseline and episode tryptase were elevated, and there were other features that fit the broader mast-cell-disorder story.

That doesn’t mean every severe anaphylaxis patient has mastocytosis. It does mean that **atypical, recurrent, or outsized reactions deserve further evaluation**. If the course is strange enough to make you say, “this isn’t behaving like routine anaphylaxis,” you should listen to that instinct.

Also worth noting: tryptase is useful, but only if you collect it correctly. In this case, the recommended early sampling window was missed initially. If you suspect severe anaphylaxis, get serial tryptase without delaying treatment, and make sure the timing actually helps interpret the event later.

## The bottom line

This is not a story about a rare allergy to clarithromycin. It’s a story about the messiness of anaphylaxis disposition.

A patient can improve after epinephrine and still relapse hours later. A patient can have more than one recurrence. And the usual “observe for a few hours” rule is too blunt to handle every case.

## Take-home points

- **Anaphylaxis disposition should be risk-based, not timer-based.**
- **Severe initial shock, hypoxemia, and need for repeated epinephrine should push you toward prolonged monitored care.**
- **Biphasic and multiphasic reactions are real, and recurrence can happen after a full initial response.**
- **Steroids should not be relied on to prevent relapse.**
- **If the course is atypical or recurrent, think about mast cell disease and arrange allergy follow-up.**
- **The moment symptoms recur, the patient is no longer “simple observation” — they need reassessment and likely escalation.**



# Repeat IM or Start an Infusion? Managing Recurrent Anaphylaxis at the Bedside

## The case that should make you sit up

This is the kind of anaphylaxis case that makes you uncomfortable in the right way.

A woman in her 60s took a first dose of oral clarithromycin and, within an hour, developed classic severe anaphylaxis: pruritus, flushing, generalized erythema, urticaria, throat swelling sensation, hypotension, and hypoxaemia. She responded to intramuscular epinephrine, oxygen, and fluids. Then she relapsed. And relapsed again. Four anaphylactic episodes in all, with ICU transfer after the first recurrence.

That’s the dilemma: once the patient has responded, do you keep treating each relapse with IM epinephrine, or do you move earlier to a continuous IV epinephrine infusion?

## Why this case matters

Most anaphylaxis cases are straightforward in principle: give epinephrine early, support the ABCs, and watch for improvement. The hard part is when the story doesn’t end after the first response.

Biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon, but they matter because they create a false sense of security. The patient looks better, the blood pressure normalizes, the rash settles, and everyone starts thinking about disposition. Then the reaction returns without re-exposure.

In this case, the challenge was even more practical than semantic. Was this truly multiphasic anaphylaxis, or a smoldering reaction with temporary response to epinephrine? The distinction is fuzzy at the bedside, and the management question is the same either way: what do you do when the patient keeps declaring themselves unstable?

## IM epinephrine still owns the front door

The first move doesn’t change just because the reaction recurs: epinephrine remains the treatment.

For adults, the guideline dose is 0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg. In this patient, the first two doses were 0.3 mg auto-injectors, which the authors explicitly note were used for speed and familiarity in a time-sensitive setting. That makes sense operationally, but it also highlights an important bedside truth: a 0.3 mg auto-injector may underdose a large adult. This patient weighed 104 kg.

The reassuring part of the case is that each episode responded to IM epinephrine. That matters. If the reaction is recurrent but still epinephrine-responsive, repeating IM dosing is not “failing”; it is appropriate resuscitation.

## So when do you think about an infusion?

This is where the evidence gets thin and the judgment gets thick.

A continuous IV epinephrine infusion is attractive when the patient keeps relapsing, especially if the episodes are severe or the hemodynamics are not staying stable. In principle, it gives you titratability and the ability to stay ahead of recurrent symptoms rather than chasing them.

But the drip comes with real costs:

- it requires close monitoring
- it needs a team comfortable with vasoactive infusions
- it carries a higher risk of dosing error than a simple IM injection
- it usually pushes you into an ICU or equivalent monitored setting

That’s why this is not a casual ward decision. A patient who is having recurrent anaphylactic shock may absolutely deserve an epinephrine infusion, but only in the right environment.

In this case, the patient was admitted first to an acute internal medicine ward without telemetry, then transferred to ICU after the first relapse. That escalation was reasonable. Once a patient has demonstrated repeated shock, the question is no longer “Can we observe them?” but “Can we safely observe them where they are?”

## The tricky part: the episodes got milder

One of the most human parts of this case is that the episodes became less intense over time. That can tempt you toward watchful waiting. Allergology even considered two options: start a continuous epinephrine infusion preemptively, or take an expectant approach and see if the relapses would burn out on their own.

Both options had risk, and that’s exactly the point. The literature does not give us a clean algorithm for multiphasic anaphylaxis. In rare cases like this, you’re balancing two bad outcomes:

- overtreating with a drip and ICU-level care that may not be needed
- undertreating and being surprised by another crash

The patient ultimately stopped relapsing after the fourth episode, so neither escalation strategy was required. But that doesn’t make the dilemma go away for the next patient.

## Don’t let antihistamines and steroids steal the spotlight

This case also shows how easily adjuncts can create therapeutic noise.

She received clemastine for cutaneous symptoms, and steroids were added after recurrence because of their traditional role in preventing biphasic reactions. That’s a common reflex, but the evidence base is weak. The case report is consistent with the broader literature: epinephrine is the intervention that changes physiology; antihistamines and steroids do not rescue shock.

Steroids may still show up in local protocols, but they should not distract from the main event. If the patient is hypotensive or hypoxaemic, epinephrine is the move. Full stop.

## The mast cell clue you don’t want to miss

This patient later had a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. That is a big deal, because mast cell disorders can predispose to severe or recurrent anaphylaxis.

A couple of practical pearls fall out of that:

- recurrent or unusually severe anaphylaxis should make you think about underlying mast cell disease
- baseline tryptase matters
- tryptase timing matters even more

The report notes that the recommended sampling window was not followed perfectly. Ideally, you want an early sample without delaying treatment, a second sample around 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and a baseline sample after full resolution. If you miss the early samples, you lose part of the physiologic story.

## Bedside takeaways

- Recurrent anaphylaxis after apparent improvement is real; don’t assume the first response is the last event.
- Repeat IM epinephrine promptly for each relapse.
- A 0.3 mg auto-injector is fast and familiar, but it may underdose larger adults compared with the usual 0.5 mg adult target.
- IV epinephrine infusion is a reasonable escalation for recurrent or persistent severe anaphylaxis, but only where close monitoring and infusion expertise are available.
- Steroids and antihistamines are adjuncts, not substitutes for epinephrine, and steroids have not convincingly been shown to prevent biphasic reactions.
- Severe or recurrent anaphylaxis should prompt consideration of mast cell disease and properly timed tryptase sampling.

## Bottom line

This case doesn’t give us a neat protocol for multiphasic anaphylaxis, because the literature still doesn’t have one. What it does give us is a very real bedside lesson: when the patient keeps relapsing, you need to think beyond “did they respond?” and ask “can this setting safely handle the next relapse?”

Sometimes the answer is another IM dose. Sometimes it’s ICU and an infusion. The key is not to wait until the decision is made for you.



# Steroids in Anaphylaxis: Tradition, Evidence Drift, and Why Practice Hasn’t Caught Up

## The case that exposes the problem

A woman in her 60s took a single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin and, within an hour, developed full-throated anaphylaxis: hypotension, hypoxaemia, flushing, urticaria, facial swelling, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. She improved after IM epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, and clemastine.

Then she relapsed.

Not once. Multiple times. Over the first 12 hours, she had recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock, ultimately totaling four anaphylactic episodes. After the first recurrence, corticosteroids were added because that’s what the older anaphylaxis playbook has long suggested. In fact, she ended up receiving two different steroid regimens because the ward and ICU protocols didn’t even agree with each other.

That’s the teaching point: steroids are still embedded in anaphylaxis care, but the rationale has outpaced the evidence.

## Why steroids ever became part of anaphylaxis treatment

The historical appeal is easy to understand. Anaphylaxis has an acute phase and can have a delayed or biphasic recurrence, so corticosteroids seemed like the obvious way to blunt “the second wave.” They’re familiar, easy to prescribe, and often perceived as harmless.

But the story is more biologically satisfying than evidence-based.

Steroids are not rescue drugs for the acute airway, breathing, and circulation collapse of anaphylaxis. They act on a slower genomic timeline, which means they are not going to fix shock or laryngeal edema in the moment. Epinephrine is the lifesaving medication. Steroids are, at best, an adjunct for a separate indication.

## What the evidence actually says about biphasic prevention

The evidence for corticosteroids preventing biphasic anaphylaxis is weak to absent.

A 2017 systematic review looked at 22 studies: only one supported corticosteroids, while 21 did not show evidence that steroids reduce biphasic reactions. More recent work has pushed practice even further away from routine steroid use. One 2019 study even found an association between prehospital corticosteroid treatment and higher ICU/ward admission rates, although observational data like that can be confounded by severity.

The honest bottom line is simple: we do not have good evidence that corticosteroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. And if the data are thin for biphasic reactions, they are even thinner for multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## Why this case is especially instructive

This patient’s course was unusually prolonged and relapse-prone, and later workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. That kind of mast-cell disease may help explain why she behaved the way she did.

But that does not rescue steroids.

A patient with a mast-cell disorder may be more vulnerable to severe or recurrent mediator release, but that is not evidence that corticosteroids prevent recurrence. If anything, this case reminds us how tempting it is to keep adding medications when the patient keeps relapsing, even when the data behind those medications are shaky.

It also illustrates a very real systems problem: protocol inertia. In this case, steroids were given because they were part of traditional anaphylaxis management, then continued because different hospital protocols pointed in different directions. That’s common. It doesn’t make the practice right.

## What should we actually do at the bedside?

The first move in anaphylaxis is still the same: IM epinephrine early, repeat it if needed, give oxygen and fluids when indicated, and monitor appropriately for relapse.

For steroids, the key question is not “Do we give them by default?” It’s “What problem am I trying to solve?”

If the goal is preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, the answer from current evidence is: probably not much. If there is another steroid-responsive process going on—like concomitant asthma—that’s a different conversation. But that’s not the same as saying steroids are routine anaphylaxis therapy.

The practical trap is letting steroids become a substitute for real resuscitation. They aren’t.

## Take-home points

- Epinephrine is the treatment that changes outcomes in anaphylaxis; steroids do not replace it.
- The traditional idea that corticosteroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis is not supported by strong evidence.
- Contemporary guidance is moving away from routine steroid use in anaphylaxis.
- Relapsing anaphylaxis should trigger repeat epinephrine, reassessment, and longer observation—not reflex steroid escalation.
- In rare multiphasic cases, think about underlying mast cell disease, but don’t assume steroids will prevent recurrence.



# Don’t Miss the Tryptase Window: Post-Anaphylaxis Testing and the Mast Cell Workup

## The case that should make you pause

Anaphylaxis is usually a “treat-now, sort-it-out-later” ED problem. This case reminds us that the “later” part matters.

A woman in her 60s developed severe anaphylaxis after her first dose of oral clarithromycin, with hypotension, hypoxemia, flushing, urticaria, and facial swelling. She initially responded to epinephrine, oxygen, and fluids — then relapsed again, and again, for a total of four anaphylactic episodes. That’s not just dramatic clinically; it’s the kind of course that should immediately raise the question: is this simply a bad drug reaction, or is there an underlying mast-cell disorder making her unusually vulnerable?

That’s where tryptase comes in. And the key lesson is simple: if you don’t collect the sample in the right window, you lose one of the few objective clues you have.

## Tryptase is supportive, not definitive — but timing is everything

Anaphylaxis remains a clinical diagnosis. You do not wait for a lab to treat it, and a normal tryptase does not rule it out. But tryptase is still very useful when the presentation is severe, recurrent, atypical, or diagnostically messy.

The practical problem is that tryptase is time-sensitive. In this case, the first tryptase was not drawn during the initial episodes; the first sample came about 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and the baseline sample was drawn 27 hours after the last relapse. That was still informative, but it illustrates the central pitfall: the diagnostic window is easy to miss.

The usual approach is:

- draw an acute tryptase as soon as feasible after symptom onset, without delaying treatment
- aim for the 1–2 hour mark after onset, and certainly within 4 hours
- obtain a true baseline sample after complete clinical resolution, ideally at least 24 hours later

If the patient has recurrent episodes, each new episode is another chance to get an acute sample. In multiphasic anaphylaxis, that matters.

A useful interpretive rule is the “20% + 2” concept: an acute tryptase above baseline × 1.2 + 2 ng/mL supports mast-cell activation. The exact threshold is less important than the principle — compare the acute value to that patient’s own baseline, not to a generic normal range alone.

## When the baseline is high, think beyond “allergy”

In this case, both acute and baseline tryptase were elevated, and follow-up workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. That is the real teaching point: a persistently elevated baseline tryptase is not just a leftover lab abnormality. It is a clue.

You should start thinking about clonal mast-cell disease when anaphylaxis is:

- unusually severe
- recurrent or biphasic/multiphasic
- associated with hypotension or syncope
- triggered by a drug or venom exposure in a patient with a history suggestive of mast-cell activation
- accompanied by persistently elevated baseline tryptase

This patient had a severe immediate reaction, repeated relapses, a prior strong local reaction to a wasp sting, and later workup that raised suspicion for a mast-cell disorder. The authors also note hepatomegaly and possible neuropsychiatric symptoms, which added to the concern, though none of those findings alone establish the diagnosis.

The important nuance: elevated baseline tryptase is a clue, not a diagnosis. But it should move the patient out of the “simple allergy” bucket and into specialist follow-up.

## What the outpatient workup should accomplish

For the ED and ICU teams, the acute job is stabilization. For the outpatient team, the job is risk stratification and explanation.

That means:

- confirming the likely trigger if possible
- documenting the episode carefully, including timing of onset, peak severity, epinephrine doses, and response
- ensuring a true baseline tryptase is obtained after recovery
- referring to allergy/immunology when the reaction is severe, recurrent, or unexplained
- considering evaluation for clonal mast-cell disease when baseline tryptase stays elevated or the clinical picture is suspicious

In this case, no allergy confirmation test was done, and that makes sense. When the culprit drug is obvious and re-exposure could be dangerous, the point is not to “prove” the allergy at all costs. The better question is whether there is an underlying host factor that explains the severity and recurrence.

## Don’t let steroids distract you from the real post-anaphylaxis question

This case also reflects a common real-world pattern: steroids were added after recurrence because many local protocols still include them. But steroids are not the answer to a missed diagnosis, and they are not a substitute for proper observation or for collecting the right labs.

The harder and more important question is this: was this a one-off drug reaction, or a patient with a mast-cell disorder who needs a broader workup and different future risk counseling?

That question cannot be answered by looking at the rash alone. It starts with the right tryptase samples.

## Practical take-home from this case

- Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis; epinephrine comes first.
- Acute tryptase should be drawn as soon as possible after onset, ideally within 1–2 hours and within 4 hours.
- Always pair the acute sample with a true baseline sample after recovery, ideally ≥24 hours later.
- Compare the acute value to the patient’s own baseline; a persistently elevated baseline is a red flag.
- Severe, recurrent, biphasic, or multiphasic anaphylaxis should prompt allergy/immunology follow-up and consideration of clonal mast-cell disease.
- A “negative” or late tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis — but a properly timed tryptase can change the entire downstream workup.
