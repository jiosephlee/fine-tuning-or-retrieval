Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient initially had a positive direct antiglobulin test for IgG, but corticosteroids did not improve the hemolysis and repeat testing became negative. What features argue against autoimmune hemolytic anemia and instead support thrombotic microangiopathy as the cause of hemolysis?

Several features point away from autoimmune hemolytic anemia and toward a thrombotic microangiopathy.

First, the hemolysis was **microangiopathic in pattern**: the peripheral smear showed **15% to 20% schistocytes**, with **rising LDH** and **undetectable haptoglobin**. That combination is classic for red-cell fragmentation from intravascular shear stress, not for warm AIHA, which more often shows **spherocytes**.

Second, the hemolysis occurred together with **persistent thrombocytopenia** and **severe acute kidney injury progressing to anuria and dialysis dependence**. That triad—**MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and renal injury**—strongly supports TMA. AIHA alone does not explain the platelet consumption or the degree of renal failure.

Third, there was **no clinical response to high-dose corticosteroids**, which you would expect in warm AIHA. Instead, the process continued until the TMA workup was pursued.

Fourth, the **repeat direct antiglobulin test became negative**, and in this setting the initial IgG-positive DAT was most likely a **false-positive** related to **penicillin exposure or transfusion** rather than true immune hemolysis.

Finally, the **kidney biopsy demonstrated fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli**, confirming TMA as the pathologic substrate of the hemolysis.

So the key teaching point is that a positive DAT does not override the broader syndrome: **schistocytosis, thrombocytopenia, AKI, steroid nonresponse, and biopsy evidence of microvascular thrombosis** argue strongly for TMA rather than autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
