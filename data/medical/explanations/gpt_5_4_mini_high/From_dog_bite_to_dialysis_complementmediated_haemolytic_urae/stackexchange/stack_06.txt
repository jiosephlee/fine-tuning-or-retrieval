Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Pathophysiology] Why do normal C3 and C4 levels not exclude complement-mediated HUS, and what is the underlying pathophysiologic mechanism by which infection can trigger this disorder even in the absence of an identified pathogenic complement gene variant?

Normal C3 and C4 levels do **not** exclude complement-mediated HUS because this is usually **not a quantitative deficiency of complement proteins**; it is a disorder of **complement regulation and local overactivation**, most often of the **alternative pathway**. In other words, the problem is not that C3 or C4 are absent in the blood, but that complement is being **inappropriately amplified on endothelial surfaces**, especially in the renal microvasculature. Because C4 is primarily a marker of the **classical pathway**, it is often normal in complement-mediated HUS, and C3 may also remain in the reference range if hepatic synthesis keeps pace or if activation is largely **tissue-localized rather than reflected in circulating levels**. That is why this diagnosis remains a **clinicopathologic one** and cannot be ruled out by normal C3/C4.

The pathophysiology is a “**loss of complement control**” at the endothelial surface, leading to unchecked alternative-pathway C3 convertase activity, downstream generation of C5b-9, endothelial injury, platelet activation, and thrombotic microangiopathy. In the case reported, the patient had a severe **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** infection, and the key clue was that the hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia appeared **after the sepsis began to improve**, which is typical of a superimposed complement-mediated process rather than DIC alone. Infection can act as the **triggering second hit** by strongly activating complement through microbial surfaces and systemic inflammation, thereby overwhelming the patient’s complement regulatory capacity—even when no pathogenic complement gene variant is identified. This may reflect **incomplete penetrance**, an **undetected genetic or acquired predisposition**, or simply **transient infection-driven complement activation** sufficient to precipitate HUS.

So the teaching point is: **normal C3/C4 do not rule out CM-HUS**, because the disorder is defined by **dysregulated alternative-pathway activation at the endothelium**, and infection can precipitate the syndrome by tipping that balance, even in an apparently genetically negative patient.
