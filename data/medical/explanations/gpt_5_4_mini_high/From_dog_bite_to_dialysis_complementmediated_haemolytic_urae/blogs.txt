Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Dog Bite, White Count Down: Recognizing Capnocytophaga Before the Culture Turns Positive

## Why this case matters

Dog bites are common. Fulminant **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** sepsis is not.

What makes this case striking is how quickly a previously healthy woman went from a lip bite to **high fever, refractory hypoglycaemia, pancytopenia, DIC, AKI, acral ischaemia, and dialysis dependence**. Her blood cultures only turned positive **7 days later**. That delay is exactly why this organism is easy to miss: the patient can look like “just another septic patient” long before the microbiology catches up.

The big teaching point is simple: **recent dog exposure plus a very sick patient should immediately put Capnocytophaga on the short list — even if the host is immunocompetent**.

## Capnocytophaga canimorsus: the organism behind the bite

C. canimorsus is a **slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod** found in the normal oral flora of dogs and cats. Transmission can occur via **bite, scratch, or close contact**. It is classically associated with severe infection in patients with asplenia, alcohol use disorder, or immunosuppression, but this case is a good reminder that **“healthy” does not mean protected**.

Clinical presentations range from mild bacteremia to **fulminant sepsis, DIC, multiorgan failure, and thrombotic microangiopathy**. Mortality remains substantial, reported to approach **30%** despite broad antibiotic susceptibility.

## The bedside pattern you should not ignore

This case had several red flags that should make any ED/ICU clinician pause:

- **Recent dog bite**, especially to the face/mucosa
- **Rapid deterioration over days**, not weeks
- **Fever and septic appearance**
- **Leukopenia/pancytopenia** rather than the more familiar leukocytosis
- **Coagulopathy/DIC**
- **AKI with proteinuria/haematuria**
- **Hypoglycaemia**
- **Acral mottling/ischaemia** of feet, toes, fingers

That combination should make you think beyond routine cellulitis or “bite wound infection.” In severe Capnocytophaga infection, the early picture can be dominated by **sepsis physiology and DIC**, which can hide what is actually a rapidly progressive invasive zoonosis.

Acral changes deserve special attention. Mottled, cool, cyanotic, or ischaemic digits in a septic patient should trigger concern for **microthrombotic disease**. In this case, the extremity findings initially fit the DIC picture, but the later course also reminded everyone that a superimposed **TMA** can emerge as the sepsis settles.

## Why the white count can be low

One of the more counterintuitive clues here was the **marked leukopaenia/pancytopenia**. We often picture severe bacterial infection as a high WBC count, but overwhelming sepsis can go the other way. In the right context, a low white count is not reassuring — it can be a marker of **severe systemic illness**.

Add **hypoglycaemia** to that picture and the alarm bells should get louder. In a septic patient, especially one with poor intake, low glucose can be multifactorial. But when it is profound and hard to correct, it often reflects **major physiologic stress** rather than simple starvation.

## Treat first, prove later

The right move in this case was broad empiric coverage with **piperacillin/tazobactam**, which is a very reasonable choice for a critically ill patient with a dog-bite source and suspected zoonotic gram-negative infection.

The key is not to wait for the culture result before treating aggressively. Capnocytophaga is **fastidious and slow-growing**, so early cultures may be unrevealing. If the patient is crashing and the story fits, you need to act on the bedside pattern.

Once C. canimorsus is identified and the patient stabilizes, therapy can often be **narrowed**. In this case, antibiotics were de-escalated to **ampicillin** and later oral **penicillin** based on culture results.

## Why cultures may lag behind the patient

This is a classic organism where the lab may be behind the clinical picture. Blood cultures can take days to turn positive, and that delay can be clinically dangerous if the diagnosis is not considered early.

That is where **molecular diagnostics** are increasingly important. In some centers, methods such as **real-time nanopore sequencing** or other rapid molecular techniques may identify fastidious organisms sooner than routine culture. You don’t need them to treat the patient, but they can be helpful when:

- cultures are negative early,
- antibiotics have already been started,
- or the clinical picture strongly suggests an unusual pathogen.

Bottom line: **a negative early culture does not rule out Capnocytophaga**.

## Don’t anchor on “just a bite”

One of the traps in this case would have been to focus only on the local wound. The patient didn’t present with a dramatic infected laceration; she presented with **systemic collapse**. That’s the key distinction.

When a dog-bite patient comes in with shock physiology, cytopenias, DIC, renal injury, and hypoglycaemia, think beyond routine bite-wound management. This is not about suturing the wound or outpatient oral antibiotics. This is an **ICU-level invasive infection** until proven otherwise.

## A final twist: when sepsis improves but the labs don’t

This case also had a second teaching point: after the initial septic phase improved, the patient developed **new isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, and biopsy-proven thrombotic microangiopathy**, ultimately diagnosed as **complement-mediated HUS**.

That’s not the usual Capnocytophaga story, but it matters because it reminds us that severe infection can be the trigger for a second process. If the platelet count, haemolysis, or kidney injury stop behaving like straightforward sepsis, keep the differential open.

## Take-home points

- **Recent dog exposure + severe sepsis = think Capnocytophaga canimorsus early**, even in immunocompetent adults.
- **Leukopenia/pancytopenia, DIC, hypoglycaemia, AKI, and acral ischaemia** are major bedside clues.
- **Do not wait for culture positivity** to start treatment; broad IV beta-lactam coverage is appropriate in severe disease.
- **Capnocytophaga is slow-growing**, so early negative cultures do not exclude it.
- **Molecular diagnostics** are increasingly useful when suspicion is high and cultures lag.
- If the patient’s course becomes atypical after the sepsis improves, **reconsider TMA/CM-HUS** rather than assuming the job is done.



# DIC or Something Else? Spotting Sepsis-Triggered TMA Hiding in Plain Sight

## The twist in this case

This is one of those cases that starts out looking straightforward and then quietly changes the rules.

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s was bitten on the upper lip by her dog, did not start the prescribed antibiotics, and returned 4 days later with fever >40°C, malaise, mottled feet, hypoglycaemia, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, and acute kidney injury. That is absolutely a septic coagulopathy picture — and it behaved like one. Blood cultures later grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a classic but uncommon dog-associated pathogen that can cause fulminant sepsis, DIC, and multiorgan failure.

But then the case pivoted.

As the infection and coagulation abnormalities began to settle, the patient developed a new problem: isolated haemoglobin drop, persistent thrombocytopenia, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes on smear, and worsening dialysis-dependent kidney injury. That is the moment this stops being “just sepsis” and starts looking like thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The big lesson here is simple: **improving sepsis markers can falsely reassure you while a second process is evolving underneath.**

## When DIC isn’t the whole story

Early on, DIC was a reasonable working diagnosis. She was febrile, inflamed, pancytopenic, coagulopathic, and had AKI with lactic acidosis. She also had distal mottling and later skin bleeding/ecchymoses, which fit a septic coagulopathy / microthrombotic picture. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were the right move, and *C. canimorsus* was eventually confirmed by blood cultures.

But DIC and TMA are not mutually exclusive, and this is where bedside pattern recognition matters.

What changed was the trajectory:

- inflammatory markers improved
- coagulation parameters normalised
- platelets stayed low
- haemoglobin fell sharply without bleeding
- haemolysis became obvious
- schistocytes appeared on smear
- kidney injury did not recover, and she became anuric/dialysis-dependent

That combination should make you stop anchoring on DIC alone.

A useful mental model:  
**DIC = consumption problem**  
**TMA = shearing/endothelial injury problem**

In real life, they can overlap, especially in severe infection. The challenge is recognising when the “DIC explanation” no longer fits the whole story.

## The diagnostic pivot: think MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI

Once haemolysis showed up, the case became a TMA workup.

The key lab and clinical clues were:

- sudden haemoglobin drop without overt bleeding
- low/absent haptoglobin
- elevated LDH
- persistent thrombocytopenia
- schistocytes on peripheral smear
- progressive renal failure
- renal biopsy later showing fibrin thrombi and ischaemic glomeruli

That is microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) until proven otherwise.

One early red herring here was the positive DAT (Coombs) test, which raised concern for autoimmune haemolysis. But the smear told the real story: schistocytes, not spherocytes. The DAT later proved misleading, likely a false positive in the setting of penicillin exposure and/or transfusion.

## Rule out TTP fast, then keep moving

In any adult with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) has to be addressed immediately because it is the “don’t miss” diagnosis.

In this case:

- PLASMIC score was 4/7, which was low risk
- ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%
- that effectively ruled out TTP, since TTP typically has ADAMTS13 activity <10%

Plasma exchange was started while awaiting the full picture, which is exactly the right bridge when TTP is still on the table. But the important point is this: **PEX is definitive therapy for TTP, not for CM-HUS.**

Stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin, which argued against STEC-HUS. With TTP and STEC-HUS off the list, the biopsy-supported diagnosis became complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), likely triggered by the infection.

## Why normal complement levels don’t save you

A classic trap in CM-HUS is expecting low C3/C4.

Don’t.

Normal complement levels do **not** exclude CM-HUS. This patient’s standard complement studies were normal. That does not rule out complement dysregulation, and it’s one reason CM-HUS remains a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion.

Her renal biopsy helped a lot: severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli, fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescents — all consistent with TMA.

Another nuance: extended complement testing was done after plasma exchange and after eculizumab had already been started, so some of those functional complement results are hard to interpret cleanly. That’s reality in sick patients — we often treat before the full genetics are back.

## Treatment: bridge first, then block complement

This case highlights the therapeutic dilemma in infection-triggered CM-HUS.

Eculizumab is the standard treatment for CM-HUS and works by inhibiting terminal complement activation. But giving complement blockade during active bacteraemia is not trivial, because terminal complement is part of host defence against encapsulated organisms.

That is why timing matters.

In this case:

- she received broad-spectrum antibiotics early
- cultures later confirmed *C. canimorsus*
- PEX was used as a bridge while the diagnosis was being clarified
- eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative
- meningococcal vaccination was initiated, and antibiotic prophylaxis was given because the vaccines had not been given 2 weeks before complement blockade

Her diuresis returned after the PEX sessions, but sustained haematologic and renal improvement came only after eculizumab. That’s a pretty classic “temporary stabilization vs disease control” distinction.

## Clinical pearls from the case

A few take-home lessons are worth keeping in your pocket:

- **DIC and TMA can coexist.** Don’t assume the first explanation explains everything.
- **A falling platelet count that fails to recover, plus new haemolysis, should trigger a TMA reassessment.**
- **Schistocytes matter.** If the smear shows MAHA, keep digging.
- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS.**
- **Exclude TTP quickly with ADAMTS13; don’t wait if suspicion is high.**
- **Infection-triggered CM-HUS can emerge as the sepsis phase improves.** That timing is a clue, not a contradiction.
- **Eculizumab is the real disease-modifying therapy for CM-HUS, but timing, infection control, and vaccination/prophylaxis are critical.**

## Bottom line

This was not just dog-bite sepsis with DIC. It was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis complicated by a superimposed complement-mediated TMA.

The diagnostic pivot came from noticing that the infection was improving while haemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and kidney failure were not. That disconnect is the red flag.

When the labs stop moving together, assume there may be more than one process in play.



# The DAT Trap: When a Positive Coombs Test Distracts from MAHA

## A dog bite, sepsis, then a Coombs test that almost pulled the team the wrong way

This case starts like a classic “bad infection, bad labs” story and then takes a dangerous turn.

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s developed fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite. She arrived with pancytopenia, coagulopathy, AKI, lactic acidosis, mottled extremities, and later became anuric and dialysis-dependent. Blood cultures grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a rare but nasty dog-associated pathogen.

Then came the hematology curveball: after her sepsis began to improve, her hemoglobin dropped sharply, platelets stayed low, haptoglobin became undetectable, LDH rose, and the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG. That is the kind of result that can make you reach for steroids and label the patient with autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

But the blood smear told a different story: 15%–20% schistocytes. This was not warm autoimmune hemolysis. This was microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) from thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), ultimately confirmed on kidney biopsy and treated as complement-mediated HUS.

That’s the lesson here: a positive DAT does not end the hemolysis workup. Sometimes it just complicates it.

## Why the DAT can mislead you

The DAT is useful, but it is not a diagnosis. It tells you whether immunoglobulin and/or complement is coating the red cell surface. That can happen in true autoimmune hemolytic anemia, but also in a bunch of situations where the DAT is positive and hemolysis is not primarily immune-mediated.

In this case, there were two big confounders:

- **Recent penicillin exposure**
- **Multiple transfusions during critical illness**

Both can muddy the waters.

Beta-lactam antibiotics, including penicillins, can be associated with a positive DAT. Sometimes that reflects true drug-induced immune hemolysis; sometimes it’s a positive test without the full clinical syndrome of autoimmune hemolysis. Transfusions can also complicate serologic testing by introducing donor RBCs, alloantibodies, or nonspecific coating that makes interpretation messy.

So when the DAT is positive in an ICU patient, the key question is not “Is the DAT positive?” It is “Does the whole picture actually fit immune hemolysis?”

In this patient, the picture did not fit well enough.

## The smear is the tie-breaker

If you take one thing from this case, make it this: **the peripheral blood smear is not optional in suspected hemolysis**.

Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia usually gives you spherocytes, polychromasia, and a DAT that fits the story. MAHA gives you fragmented red cells — schistocytes — because the RBCs are being mechanically shredded as they pass through microthrombi and damaged microvasculature.

This patient’s smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes. That is a major clue. In the setting of:

- thrombocytopenia
- AKI
- rising LDH
- undetectable haptoglobin
- anemia without bleeding

…schistocytes should push you hard toward TMA, even if the DAT is positive.

The distinction matters because the treatment pathways are very different. Steroids may help warm AIHA. They do not fix complement-mediated TMA.

## Why steroids are such a tempting reflex

Once the DAT came back positive, high-dose prednisolone was started. That is understandable. In a sick patient with falling hemoglobin, it is hard not to treat the most familiar immune diagnosis in front of you.

But this case is a reminder that **a positive DAT can anchor you to the wrong mechanism**.

A few practical cautions:

- If the anemia is accompanied by **schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, and AKI**, think TMA first.
- If the hemoglobin drop is **out of proportion to bleeding**, do not stop at the DAT.
- If steroids do not produce the expected response, reassess early.

In this patient, the initial DAT was later felt to be a false positive, likely influenced by penicillin exposure or transfusion history. The repeat DAT was negative. The hemolysis pattern and smear were far more consistent with MAHA than with autoimmune hemolysis.

## Sepsis, DIC, and TMA can blur together

This is where the case gets especially tricky.

Early on, the patient looked like severe sepsis with DIC: fever, leukopaenia, coagulopathy, AKI, liver injury, and later skin bleeding and distal ischemic changes. That is a very plausible first interpretation. And it may have been correct for the early phase.

But then the story changed. As the inflammatory picture improved, she developed:

- sudden isolated anemia
- persistent thrombocytopenia
- undetectable haptoglobin
- rising LDH
- schistocytes

That shift should make you think that **something more than sepsis-associated coagulopathy is going on**.

The overlap between DIC and TMA can be brutal at the bedside. Both can present with thrombocytopenia and organ dysfunction. But persistent MAHA with schistocytes, especially as sepsis is improving, should raise concern for a superimposed TMA.

## The TMA workup that mattered here

Once TMA was on the table, the team did the right exclusion workup:

- **ADAMTS13 activity** was 43.5%, making TTP unlikely
- **PLASMIC score** was low-intermediate, not screaming TTP
- **Shiga toxin PCR** was negative, arguing against STEC-HUS
- **Kidney biopsy** showed TMA features: fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli, severe acute tubular injury

That constellation supported complement-mediated HUS.

A few important nuances from this case:

- **Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS**
- Complement testing can be hard to interpret during active illness and after plasma exchange or eculizumab
- In adults with MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI, you still have to think through TTP first because it is the “don’t miss” diagnosis

Plasma exchange was used as a bridge while the diagnosis was clarified, and eculizumab was started after vaccines and negative blood cultures, which is the right balancing act in a patient with recent bacteremia.

## Why this case matters beyond the rare diagnosis

Most of us will not see *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered complement-mediated HUS every year. But the diagnostic trap is common.

The lesson is broader:

- A **positive DAT does not equal autoimmune hemolysis**
- **Schistocytes trump the Coombs test** when the clinical picture points to MAHA
- **Recent antibiotics and transfusions can confuse serology**
- In critically ill patients, **hemolysis is often multifactorial**, and anchoring on one lab result is dangerous

This case also shows how infection can be the trigger that unmasks a complement-mediated TMA, even in someone without an obvious prior hematologic history.

## Practical bedside approach

When a critically ill patient has anemia and a positive DAT, pause and ask:

1. **Is there objective hemolysis?**
   - LDH up
   - haptoglobin down
   - bilirubin may help
   - reticulocytes may or may not be appropriately elevated in critical illness

2. **What does the smear show?**
   - Spherocytes? think immune hemolysis
   - Schistocytes? think MAHA/TMA

3. **Are platelets and kidneys involved?**
   - Thrombocytopenia + AKI should make TMA jump up the list

4. **What is the TTP risk?**
   - Send ADAMTS13 early
   - Use PLASMIC as a triage tool, not as truth

5. **Could the DAT be a red herring?**
   - Recent penicillin
   - Recent transfusions
   - Critical illness

## Take-home points

- A positive DAT is **not** synonymous with autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
- In a patient with anemia, thrombocytopenia, AKI, and hemolysis, **look at the smear early**.
- **Schistocytes** should shift you toward MAHA/TMA, even if the DAT is positive.
- Recent **penicillin exposure** and **transfusions** can make the DAT hard to interpret.
- If the clinical picture fits TMA, don’t let a positive Coombs test delay the broader workup for **TTP, STEC-HUS, and complement-mediated HUS**.
- In this case, the real diagnosis was not autoimmune hemolysis — it was **infection-triggered complement-mediated TMA**.



# Normal Complement, Abnormal Endothelium: Diagnosing Complement-Mediated HUS in Adults

## The case that started as a dog bite and ended in dialysis

This is the kind of case that tricks you twice.

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s was bitten on the lip by her dog, didn’t start the prescribed penicillin, and returned four days later with fulminant sepsis, hypoglycaemia, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, and AKI. Blood cultures later grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Early on, “sepsis with DIC” was a very reasonable working diagnosis.

Then the story changed.

As the infection and coagulopathy began to settle, she became anuric, needed continuous renal replacement therapy, and then developed a new isolated haemolytic picture: falling haemoglobin without bleeding, persistent thrombocytopenia, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and 15%–20% schistocytes on smear. That pivot from sepsis/DIC to microangiopathic haemolysis is the red flag for thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The final diagnosis was complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), apparently triggered by infection, with only partial renal recovery despite antibiotics, plasma exchange, and eculizumab.

The teaching point: in adults, TMA is often a moving target, and sepsis can both mimic and trigger it.

## First question: is this TMA, DIC, or both?

Early in this case, the patient really did look like she had sepsis-associated coagulopathy. Fever, thrombocytopenia, abnormal coagulation studies, organ dysfunction, and mottled extremities all fit.

But DIC should not be a diagnostic dead end. If the platelets stay low, creatinine climbs, and haemolysis emerges, you need to reopen the file.

When you suspect TMA, look for:
- falling haemoglobin without bleeding
- elevated LDH
- low or absent haptoglobin
- schistocytes on peripheral smear
- thrombocytopenia
- AKI

Once that cluster appears, TMA is in play until proven otherwise.

## Step 1 in adult TMA: rule out TTP fast

TTP is the first diagnosis to exclude because delay can be fatal. In practice, you usually won’t have ADAMTS13 back when you need to act, so PLASMIC score helps estimate pretest probability while you wait.

In this case, the PLASMIC score was 4/7, which is low risk for TTP. ADAMTS13 activity later returned at 43.5%, which makes severe ADAMTS13 deficiency very unlikely and effectively argues against TTP. For reference, TTP usually has ADAMTS13 activity below 10%.

A practical approach:
- send ADAMTS13 immediately
- use PLASMIC as a triage tool, not a final diagnosis
- if suspicion is high, start plasma exchange while awaiting results

## Step 2: don’t miss STEC-HUS

Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS is the other big one to exclude. It’s classically diarrhoeal, but adult presentations can be messy.

Here, stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative, and there was no prodromal enteric illness. That makes STEC-HUS much less likely.

The point is simple: if the history fits at all, send stool PCR early. A negative result is useful, but only if you actually looked.

## Normal C3 and C4 do not rule out CM-HUS

This is the trap.

Complement-mediated HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion, and basic complement levels can be completely normal. In this patient, C3 and C4 were normal, and even functional assays of the classical, alternative and lectin pathways were initially normal.

That does not exclude CM-HUS.

Why? Because complement-mediated TMA is often trigger-dependent and dynamic. A single serum snapshot may miss the biology entirely. So if you have persistent MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, a non-TTP ADAMTS13 result, and STEC testing is negative, CM-HUS stays on the table even with normal complement levels.

## Kidney biopsy can be the tie-breaker

Biopsy isn’t mandatory in every TMA, but it can be very helpful when the diagnosis is muddy or the kidney injury is dominating the course.

In this case, biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation, consistent with TMA. Immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy didn’t suggest a primary glomerulopathy.

That doesn’t tell you the exact flavour of TMA, but it confirms the endothelium has taken a hit.

## Timing matters: treatment can blur later complement testing

This is a big practical lesson from the case.

Extended complement testing was sent after plasma exchange sessions and after the first eculizumab dose. The alternative pathway activity was markedly reduced, but that result is hard to interpret because both plasma exchange and complement blockade can distort assay results.

Same problem with genetic testing: if you sample after treatment has started, you may still get useful information, but the interpretation of functional complement studies becomes trickier.

The takeaway:
- draw complement and genetic samples early if you can
- don’t wait for perfect lab timing if the patient is deteriorating
- treat first, then interpret the tests in context

## Infection-triggered CM-HUS can happen without a known genetic variant

This case is unusual because the trigger was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a dog-associated organism, and no pathogenic complement gene variant was identified.

That matters. CM-HUS is often framed as a genetic predisposition plus a “second hit,” but this case shows that a severe infection alone may be enough to unmask complement-mediated endothelial injury, even when no inherited variant is found.

It also reminds us of the paradox of complement: it helps clear the infection, but if regulation is off, the same system can drive TMA.

## Treatment: bridge when you must, block complement when you can

Plasma exchange was used here as bridging therapy while TTP was being excluded and the diagnosis was clarified. That makes sense. PEX is lifesaving in TTP.

But PEX is not definitive therapy for CM-HUS. In this case, the durable haematologic recovery and partial renal improvement followed the combination of supportive care, plasma exchange, and eculizumab.

Eculizumab is the key drug for CM-HUS, but it raises a real concern in active infection because terminal complement is important for defence against encapsulated organisms. In this patient, eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative, with meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis in place.

## A practical adult TMA framework

When an adult presents with thrombocytopenia, AKI, and evidence of haemolysis, I’d work through this sequence:

1. Confirm TMA on labs and smear.
2. Look for DIC and sepsis, but don’t stop there.
3. Calculate PLASMIC and send ADAMTS13 immediately.
4. Send stool PCR for Shiga toxin if there’s any diarrhoeal story.
5. If ADAMTS13 is not severely low and STEC is negative, think CM-HUS and other secondary TMAs.
6. Send complement and genetic testing early if feasible, but don’t over-trust normal C3/C4.
7. Consider kidney biopsy if the diagnosis remains unclear or renal failure is prominent.
8. Start eculizumab when CM-HUS is the leading diagnosis and infection is controlled.

## Take-home points

- In adults, MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI = TMA until proven otherwise.
- Use PLASMIC to estimate TTP risk, but let ADAMTS13 decide the diagnosis; severe deficiency is usually <10%.
- Negative stool PCR plus no diarrhoeal prodrome makes STEC-HUS less likely.
- Normal C3 and C4 do not rule out CM-HUS.
- Plasma exchange and eculizumab can confound later complement studies, so sample early if possible.
- If TTP and STEC-HUS are excluded, persistent TMA after infection should keep CM-HUS high on the list — even with an unusual trigger like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.



# Biopsy After the Bleeding Stops: When Dialysis-Dependent Septic AKI Needs Kidney Histology

## Why this case is worth remembering

This is one of those cases where the kidney story looked like it had an obvious explanation — until it didn’t.

A previously healthy woman developed fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite. Blood cultures later grew **Capnocytophaga canimorsus**, and the early course was dominated by the familiar septic package: DIC, pancytopenia, lactic acidosis, and rapidly worsening AKI. She even became dialysis-dependent. If you stopped thinking there, you could easily label this as severe septic ATN and move on.

But the case turned when the “expected” course didn’t happen. As the sepsis and coagulopathy improved, she developed **persistent thrombocytopenia, sudden hemolysis, and ongoing renal failure**. That’s when the team stopped asking, “How bad is the sepsis?” and started asking, “Is there a second kidney process here?”

That question — asked at the right time — is what made the kidney biopsy so important.

## The diagnostic trap: sepsis, DIC, and delayed recognition of TMA

Sepsis can blur the entire hematologic picture. DIC can explain thrombocytopenia, bleeding, kidney injury, and abnormal coagulation. Severe infection can also cause ATN. So early on, it was completely reasonable to think this was just overwhelming septic physiology.

But several features should make you pause when AKI doesn’t improve as the infection settles:

- **Persistent dialysis-dependent AKI**
- **Platelets that don’t normalize as expected**
- **New hemolysis** after the septic phase starts to improve
- **Schistocytes** on smear
- **Protein and blood on urinalysis**
- Ischemic peripheral changes that are more than just “shock liver, shock kidney, shock everything”

In this patient, the real pivot was the delayed appearance of **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)**. Her hemoglobin fell, haptoglobin became undetectable, LDH rose, and the smear showed **15–20% schistocytes**. That shifted the differential from pure sepsis/DIC toward **thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)**.

At that point the key question became: is this TTP, STEC-HUS, or complement-mediated HUS?

The workup helped narrow it:

- **ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%**, which argues strongly against TTP
- **Stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin**, making STEC-HUS unlikely
- **C3/C4 were normal**, which does **not** exclude CM-HUS
- And importantly, the kidney wasn’t recovering the way isolated septic ATN usually does

## Why wait for the platelets to recover?

This is where timing matters.

Kidney biopsy during active DIC and severe thrombocytopenia is a bad idea. But once the coagulopathy started to resolve and the platelet count recovered enough for a safer procedure, biopsy became the next logical move. In a patient with ongoing anuric AKI, it can provide the missing tissue diagnosis when labs are confusing and the clinical course is mixed.

That’s the practical lesson: **biopsy is not for the bleeding phase; it’s for the point when bleeding risk has eased but the diagnosis is still unclear and management would change.**

In other words, don’t biopsy the patient in the middle of consumptive coagulopathy — but don’t miss the window once the bleeding stops and the kidney still isn’t waking up.

## What the biopsy showed

The renal biopsy did not show a single-process explanation like “just ATN.”

It showed:

- **Severe acute tubular necrosis**
- **Ischaemic glomeruli**
- **Fibrin thrombi**
- **Scattered crescent formation**

That combination is the clue. It tells you this wasn’t simply tubular injury from sepsis. There was also **renal TMA**.

Immunofluorescence was negative for immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy did not support a primary glomerulopathy. So the biopsy didn’t just say “kidney injury” — it ruled in the relevant pathology and ruled out a few important mimics.

This is the point where histology earns its keep. A biopsy can show you that the patient’s AKI is not just a slow recovery from septic ATN; it’s a microvascular thrombotic process layered on top of tubular injury.

## How the biopsy changed management

The biopsy mattered because it reinforced the move toward **complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)**.

That mattered for two reasons:

1. **PEX was only a bridge**
   Plasma exchange is lifesaving in TTP, but it’s not definitive therapy for CM-HUS. In this case, PEX was started while the team was still sorting out the TMA differential and awaiting ADAMTS13. Reasonable move. But the biopsy and clinical course made it clear that ongoing recovery would need more than PEX.

2. **Eculizumab became the key therapy**
   Once blood cultures had cleared, the team initiated meningococcal vaccination, gave prophylactic antibiotics, and started **eculizumab**. After that, hematologic remission occurred and kidney function partially recovered.

This is the classic “you need the right diagnosis to justify the right treatment” moment. If the biopsy had shown only ATN, the plan might have stayed supportive. Because it showed TMA, the management shifted toward complement blockade.

## A few bedside pearls from this case

The biggest trap here is anchoring on sepsis alone. Severe infection can absolutely cause AKI, DIC, and thrombocytopenia — but if the kidney remains offline and hemolysis appears, you need to reopen the differential.

Kidney biopsy is especially useful when:

- AKI is **persistent or dialysis-dependent** after the initial septic shock phase
- Coagulopathy is improving and biopsy is becoming safer
- There is **ongoing thrombocytopenia or hemolysis**
- You need to distinguish **ATN alone** from **TMA**, because the treatment would change

And remember: **normal complement levels do not rule out CM-HUS**. Tissue diagnosis and the overall trajectory matter more than a reassuring C3/C4.

## Take-home points

- **Not all severe septic AKI is “just ATN.”** If the course is atypical, keep TMA on the table.
- **Wait for the bleeding risk to improve, then biopsy the kidney if the diagnosis is still unclear.**
- **Renal biopsy can reveal mixed pathology** — here, ATN plus fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli consistent with TMA.
- **A biopsy result can change treatment**, especially when it supports CM-HUS and justifies complement blockade.
- **Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM-HUS.** Don’t let a normal complement panel falsely reassure you.

Sometimes the kidney tells the real story only after the coagulopathy settles down. In this case, biopsy turned a “slow recovery from sepsis” narrative into a diagnosis of **complement-mediated TMA** — and that changed everything.



# Blocking Complement After Bacteraemia: Eculizumab, Plasma Exchange, and the Infection Tradeoff

## The surprising part of this case

A dog bite led to septic shock, DIC-like physiology, dialysis-dependent AKI, and then — just when the sepsis picture started to settle — a thrombotic microangiopathy declared itself.

That’s the interesting pivot here. The initial story looked like severe Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with coagulopathy. But the later story was different: falling hemoglobin, persistent thrombocytopenia, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes, and kidney biopsy showing TMA. That’s the moment you have to stop calling it “just sepsis” and start asking whether complement-mediated HUS is in play.

And once you get there, the treatment question becomes tricky: how do you give eculizumab — a drug that blocks terminal complement — in someone who just had bacteraemia from an encapsulated organism?

## When sepsis and TMA overlap, don’t anchor early

This case is a good reminder that septic shock can blur the borders between DIC and TMA.

Early on, the patient had fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, AKI, and mottled extremities. DIC from bacteraemia was a reasonable first framework. But the key is to keep re-evaluating the pattern as the story evolves. In this case, coagulation parameters normalized while haemolysis persisted and platelets stayed low. That mismatch should make you think beyond DIC.

A few practical points:

- **TTP must be excluded quickly** in any adult with MAHA + thrombocytopenia + organ injury.
- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS.**
- **ADAMTS13 is decisive for TTP**, not complement levels.
- **A positive DAT does not end the workup** if the smear shows schistocytes and the clinical picture fits TMA.

This patient’s PLASMIC score was low, and ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5%, making TTP unlikely. That opened the door to a CM-HUS diagnosis.

## Plasma exchange: useful as a bridge, not the destination

Plasma exchange has a very different role in TTP versus CM-HUS.

In **TTP**, plasma exchange is life-saving because it removes inhibitory anti-ADAMTS13 antibodies and replenishes ADAMTS13. In **CM-HUS**, it may temporarily improve the situation by removing inflammatory mediators, complement fragments, or autoantibodies — but it does not fix the underlying complement dysregulation.

That distinction matters.

In this case, plasma exchange was started while the team was still sorting out the TMA differential. That’s a defensible move when TTP hasn’t been excluded yet. But once the diagnosis points away from TTP and toward CM-HUS, PEX is best thought of as a **bridge**, not definitive therapy.

One of the subtle traps here is that transient clinical improvement after PEX can be misleading. This patient’s urine output returned after the final PEX sessions, but sustained haematologic and renal improvement came only after complement blockade was started.

## Eculizumab: the treatment that actually changes the disease

Eculizumab targets C5 and shuts down terminal complement activation. In CM-HUS, that’s the disease-modifying move. If you wait too long, renal recovery is often incomplete.

But in the setting of recent bacteraemia, you have to balance benefit against risk.

Why the concern? Because terminal complement is important for host defence against **encapsulated bacteria**, including **Neisseria** and, relevant here, **Capnocytophaga**. Blocking C5 in someone with active infection is not a trivial decision.

What did the team do here?

- They treated the infection first with broad-spectrum antibiotics, then narrowed therapy once **C. canimorsus** was confirmed.
- They started eculizumab **after blood cultures became negative**.
- They gave **Menveo** and **Bexsero** before induction.
- Because vaccines had not been given 2 weeks before complement blockade, they used **antibiotic prophylaxis with Apocillin**.

That is the practical compromise most of us are aiming for: get infection control as far along as possible, vaccinate if you can, and don’t leave the kidney to burn if CM-HUS is clearly evolving.

## Timing matters: don’t wait for genetics to act

This is one of the biggest take-home points.

CM-HUS is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion. Genetic testing can help explain susceptibility and guide long-term risk, but it is not what should determine whether you treat an acutely deteriorating patient.

In this case:

- the infection trigger was clear,
- schistocytes were present,
- kidney biopsy supported TMA,
- STEC was excluded,
- TTP was unlikely,
- and the clinical course improved after eculizumab.

That is enough to justify treatment.

The complement workup later showed no pathogenic variant, which supports an infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a clearly inherited complement defect. But a negative panel does **not** mean the complement system was innocent — it just means the genetics didn’t hand you a simple explanation.

## How long do you continue eculizumab when genetics are negative?

There isn’t a one-size-fits-all answer, and this case shows why.

The patient was discharged on maintenance eculizumab, with a plan for reassessment if renal function stabilized. That is a sensible, individualized approach when:

- the trigger was severe,
- kidney recovery is incomplete,
- and no pathogenic complement variant has been identified.

If a high-risk complement mutation is found, the argument for longer-term therapy becomes stronger. If genetics are negative and the trigger is clearly transient, stopping therapy may eventually be reasonable — but that decision has to be made cautiously, with close monitoring for relapse.

## Practical take-home points

- **Sepsis does not exclude TMA.** If platelets stay low and haemolysis appears after the shock phase, pivot.
- **Use plasma exchange as a bridge when TTP is still on the table.** It’s not definitive therapy for CM-HUS.
- **Eculizumab is the disease-modifying treatment for CM-HUS**, but infection risk is the price tag.
- **Try to start complement blockade after culture clearance if possible.**
- **Vaccinate against meningococcus and use antibiotic prophylaxis** if eculizumab must start before the vaccine window is complete.
- **Negative complement genetics do not rule out infection-triggered CM-HUS** and do not automatically tell you when to stop therapy.

This case is a good reminder that sometimes the hardest part of treating CM-HUS is not recognizing the TMA — it’s knowing when the infection is controlled enough to safely turn off complement.
