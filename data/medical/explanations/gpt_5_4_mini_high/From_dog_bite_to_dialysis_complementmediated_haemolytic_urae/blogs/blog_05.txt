Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Biopsy After the Bleeding Stops: When Dialysis-Dependent Septic AKI Needs Kidney Histology

## Why this case is worth remembering

This is one of those cases where the kidney story looked like it had an obvious explanation — until it didn’t.

A previously healthy woman developed fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite. Blood cultures later grew **Capnocytophaga canimorsus**, and the early course was dominated by the familiar septic package: DIC, pancytopenia, lactic acidosis, and rapidly worsening AKI. She even became dialysis-dependent. If you stopped thinking there, you could easily label this as severe septic ATN and move on.

But the case turned when the “expected” course didn’t happen. As the sepsis and coagulopathy improved, she developed **persistent thrombocytopenia, sudden hemolysis, and ongoing renal failure**. That’s when the team stopped asking, “How bad is the sepsis?” and started asking, “Is there a second kidney process here?”

That question — asked at the right time — is what made the kidney biopsy so important.

## The diagnostic trap: sepsis, DIC, and delayed recognition of TMA

Sepsis can blur the entire hematologic picture. DIC can explain thrombocytopenia, bleeding, kidney injury, and abnormal coagulation. Severe infection can also cause ATN. So early on, it was completely reasonable to think this was just overwhelming septic physiology.

But several features should make you pause when AKI doesn’t improve as the infection settles:

- **Persistent dialysis-dependent AKI**
- **Platelets that don’t normalize as expected**
- **New hemolysis** after the septic phase starts to improve
- **Schistocytes** on smear
- **Protein and blood on urinalysis**
- Ischemic peripheral changes that are more than just “shock liver, shock kidney, shock everything”

In this patient, the real pivot was the delayed appearance of **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)**. Her hemoglobin fell, haptoglobin became undetectable, LDH rose, and the smear showed **15–20% schistocytes**. That shifted the differential from pure sepsis/DIC toward **thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)**.

At that point the key question became: is this TTP, STEC-HUS, or complement-mediated HUS?

The workup helped narrow it:

- **ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%**, which argues strongly against TTP
- **Stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin**, making STEC-HUS unlikely
- **C3/C4 were normal**, which does **not** exclude CM-HUS
- And importantly, the kidney wasn’t recovering the way isolated septic ATN usually does

## Why wait for the platelets to recover?

This is where timing matters.

Kidney biopsy during active DIC and severe thrombocytopenia is a bad idea. But once the coagulopathy started to resolve and the platelet count recovered enough for a safer procedure, biopsy became the next logical move. In a patient with ongoing anuric AKI, it can provide the missing tissue diagnosis when labs are confusing and the clinical course is mixed.

That’s the practical lesson: **biopsy is not for the bleeding phase; it’s for the point when bleeding risk has eased but the diagnosis is still unclear and management would change.**

In other words, don’t biopsy the patient in the middle of consumptive coagulopathy — but don’t miss the window once the bleeding stops and the kidney still isn’t waking up.

## What the biopsy showed

The renal biopsy did not show a single-process explanation like “just ATN.”

It showed:

- **Severe acute tubular necrosis**
- **Ischaemic glomeruli**
- **Fibrin thrombi**
- **Scattered crescent formation**

That combination is the clue. It tells you this wasn’t simply tubular injury from sepsis. There was also **renal TMA**.

Immunofluorescence was negative for immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy did not support a primary glomerulopathy. So the biopsy didn’t just say “kidney injury” — it ruled in the relevant pathology and ruled out a few important mimics.

This is the point where histology earns its keep. A biopsy can show you that the patient’s AKI is not just a slow recovery from septic ATN; it’s a microvascular thrombotic process layered on top of tubular injury.

## How the biopsy changed management

The biopsy mattered because it reinforced the move toward **complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)**.

That mattered for two reasons:

1. **PEX was only a bridge**
   Plasma exchange is lifesaving in TTP, but it’s not definitive therapy for CM-HUS. In this case, PEX was started while the team was still sorting out the TMA differential and awaiting ADAMTS13. Reasonable move. But the biopsy and clinical course made it clear that ongoing recovery would need more than PEX.

2. **Eculizumab became the key therapy**
   Once blood cultures had cleared, the team initiated meningococcal vaccination, gave prophylactic antibiotics, and started **eculizumab**. After that, hematologic remission occurred and kidney function partially recovered.

This is the classic “you need the right diagnosis to justify the right treatment” moment. If the biopsy had shown only ATN, the plan might have stayed supportive. Because it showed TMA, the management shifted toward complement blockade.

## A few bedside pearls from this case

The biggest trap here is anchoring on sepsis alone. Severe infection can absolutely cause AKI, DIC, and thrombocytopenia — but if the kidney remains offline and hemolysis appears, you need to reopen the differential.

Kidney biopsy is especially useful when:

- AKI is **persistent or dialysis-dependent** after the initial septic shock phase
- Coagulopathy is improving and biopsy is becoming safer
- There is **ongoing thrombocytopenia or hemolysis**
- You need to distinguish **ATN alone** from **TMA**, because the treatment would change

And remember: **normal complement levels do not rule out CM-HUS**. Tissue diagnosis and the overall trajectory matter more than a reassuring C3/C4.

## Take-home points

- **Not all severe septic AKI is “just ATN.”** If the course is atypical, keep TMA on the table.
- **Wait for the bleeding risk to improve, then biopsy the kidney if the diagnosis is still unclear.**
- **Renal biopsy can reveal mixed pathology** — here, ATN plus fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli consistent with TMA.
- **A biopsy result can change treatment**, especially when it supports CM-HUS and justifies complement blockade.
- **Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM-HUS.** Don’t let a normal complement panel falsely reassure you.

Sometimes the kidney tells the real story only after the coagulopathy settles down. In this case, biopsy turned a “slow recovery from sepsis” narrative into a diagnosis of **complement-mediated TMA** — and that changed everything.
