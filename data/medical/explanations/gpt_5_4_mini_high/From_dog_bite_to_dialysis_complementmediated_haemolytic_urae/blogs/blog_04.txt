Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Normal Complement, Abnormal Endothelium: Diagnosing Complement-Mediated HUS in Adults

## The case that started as a dog bite and ended in dialysis

This is the kind of case that tricks you twice.

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s was bitten on the lip by her dog, didn’t start the prescribed penicillin, and returned four days later with fulminant sepsis, hypoglycaemia, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, and AKI. Blood cultures later grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Early on, “sepsis with DIC” was a very reasonable working diagnosis.

Then the story changed.

As the infection and coagulopathy began to settle, she became anuric, needed continuous renal replacement therapy, and then developed a new isolated haemolytic picture: falling haemoglobin without bleeding, persistent thrombocytopenia, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and 15%–20% schistocytes on smear. That pivot from sepsis/DIC to microangiopathic haemolysis is the red flag for thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The final diagnosis was complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), apparently triggered by infection, with only partial renal recovery despite antibiotics, plasma exchange, and eculizumab.

The teaching point: in adults, TMA is often a moving target, and sepsis can both mimic and trigger it.

## First question: is this TMA, DIC, or both?

Early in this case, the patient really did look like she had sepsis-associated coagulopathy. Fever, thrombocytopenia, abnormal coagulation studies, organ dysfunction, and mottled extremities all fit.

But DIC should not be a diagnostic dead end. If the platelets stay low, creatinine climbs, and haemolysis emerges, you need to reopen the file.

When you suspect TMA, look for:
- falling haemoglobin without bleeding
- elevated LDH
- low or absent haptoglobin
- schistocytes on peripheral smear
- thrombocytopenia
- AKI

Once that cluster appears, TMA is in play until proven otherwise.

## Step 1 in adult TMA: rule out TTP fast

TTP is the first diagnosis to exclude because delay can be fatal. In practice, you usually won’t have ADAMTS13 back when you need to act, so PLASMIC score helps estimate pretest probability while you wait.

In this case, the PLASMIC score was 4/7, which is low risk for TTP. ADAMTS13 activity later returned at 43.5%, which makes severe ADAMTS13 deficiency very unlikely and effectively argues against TTP. For reference, TTP usually has ADAMTS13 activity below 10%.

A practical approach:
- send ADAMTS13 immediately
- use PLASMIC as a triage tool, not a final diagnosis
- if suspicion is high, start plasma exchange while awaiting results

## Step 2: don’t miss STEC-HUS

Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS is the other big one to exclude. It’s classically diarrhoeal, but adult presentations can be messy.

Here, stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative, and there was no prodromal enteric illness. That makes STEC-HUS much less likely.

The point is simple: if the history fits at all, send stool PCR early. A negative result is useful, but only if you actually looked.

## Normal C3 and C4 do not rule out CM-HUS

This is the trap.

Complement-mediated HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion, and basic complement levels can be completely normal. In this patient, C3 and C4 were normal, and even functional assays of the classical, alternative and lectin pathways were initially normal.

That does not exclude CM-HUS.

Why? Because complement-mediated TMA is often trigger-dependent and dynamic. A single serum snapshot may miss the biology entirely. So if you have persistent MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, a non-TTP ADAMTS13 result, and STEC testing is negative, CM-HUS stays on the table even with normal complement levels.

## Kidney biopsy can be the tie-breaker

Biopsy isn’t mandatory in every TMA, but it can be very helpful when the diagnosis is muddy or the kidney injury is dominating the course.

In this case, biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation, consistent with TMA. Immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy didn’t suggest a primary glomerulopathy.

That doesn’t tell you the exact flavour of TMA, but it confirms the endothelium has taken a hit.

## Timing matters: treatment can blur later complement testing

This is a big practical lesson from the case.

Extended complement testing was sent after plasma exchange sessions and after the first eculizumab dose. The alternative pathway activity was markedly reduced, but that result is hard to interpret because both plasma exchange and complement blockade can distort assay results.

Same problem with genetic testing: if you sample after treatment has started, you may still get useful information, but the interpretation of functional complement studies becomes trickier.

The takeaway:
- draw complement and genetic samples early if you can
- don’t wait for perfect lab timing if the patient is deteriorating
- treat first, then interpret the tests in context

## Infection-triggered CM-HUS can happen without a known genetic variant

This case is unusual because the trigger was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a dog-associated organism, and no pathogenic complement gene variant was identified.

That matters. CM-HUS is often framed as a genetic predisposition plus a “second hit,” but this case shows that a severe infection alone may be enough to unmask complement-mediated endothelial injury, even when no inherited variant is found.

It also reminds us of the paradox of complement: it helps clear the infection, but if regulation is off, the same system can drive TMA.

## Treatment: bridge when you must, block complement when you can

Plasma exchange was used here as bridging therapy while TTP was being excluded and the diagnosis was clarified. That makes sense. PEX is lifesaving in TTP.

But PEX is not definitive therapy for CM-HUS. In this case, the durable haematologic recovery and partial renal improvement followed the combination of supportive care, plasma exchange, and eculizumab.

Eculizumab is the key drug for CM-HUS, but it raises a real concern in active infection because terminal complement is important for defence against encapsulated organisms. In this patient, eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative, with meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis in place.

## A practical adult TMA framework

When an adult presents with thrombocytopenia, AKI, and evidence of haemolysis, I’d work through this sequence:

1. Confirm TMA on labs and smear.
2. Look for DIC and sepsis, but don’t stop there.
3. Calculate PLASMIC and send ADAMTS13 immediately.
4. Send stool PCR for Shiga toxin if there’s any diarrhoeal story.
5. If ADAMTS13 is not severely low and STEC is negative, think CM-HUS and other secondary TMAs.
6. Send complement and genetic testing early if feasible, but don’t over-trust normal C3/C4.
7. Consider kidney biopsy if the diagnosis remains unclear or renal failure is prominent.
8. Start eculizumab when CM-HUS is the leading diagnosis and infection is controlled.

## Take-home points

- In adults, MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI = TMA until proven otherwise.
- Use PLASMIC to estimate TTP risk, but let ADAMTS13 decide the diagnosis; severe deficiency is usually <10%.
- Negative stool PCR plus no diarrhoeal prodrome makes STEC-HUS less likely.
- Normal C3 and C4 do not rule out CM-HUS.
- Plasma exchange and eculizumab can confound later complement studies, so sample early if possible.
- If TTP and STEC-HUS are excluded, persistent TMA after infection should keep CM-HUS high on the list — even with an unusual trigger like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.
