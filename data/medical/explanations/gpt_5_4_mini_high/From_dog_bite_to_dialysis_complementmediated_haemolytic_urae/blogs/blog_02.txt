Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# DIC or Something Else? Spotting Sepsis-Triggered TMA Hiding in Plain Sight

## The twist in this case

This is one of those cases that starts out looking straightforward and then quietly changes the rules.

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s was bitten on the upper lip by her dog, did not start the prescribed antibiotics, and returned 4 days later with fever >40°C, malaise, mottled feet, hypoglycaemia, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, and acute kidney injury. That is absolutely a septic coagulopathy picture — and it behaved like one. Blood cultures later grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a classic but uncommon dog-associated pathogen that can cause fulminant sepsis, DIC, and multiorgan failure.

But then the case pivoted.

As the infection and coagulation abnormalities began to settle, the patient developed a new problem: isolated haemoglobin drop, persistent thrombocytopenia, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes on smear, and worsening dialysis-dependent kidney injury. That is the moment this stops being “just sepsis” and starts looking like thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The big lesson here is simple: **improving sepsis markers can falsely reassure you while a second process is evolving underneath.**

## When DIC isn’t the whole story

Early on, DIC was a reasonable working diagnosis. She was febrile, inflamed, pancytopenic, coagulopathic, and had AKI with lactic acidosis. She also had distal mottling and later skin bleeding/ecchymoses, which fit a septic coagulopathy / microthrombotic picture. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were the right move, and *C. canimorsus* was eventually confirmed by blood cultures.

But DIC and TMA are not mutually exclusive, and this is where bedside pattern recognition matters.

What changed was the trajectory:

- inflammatory markers improved
- coagulation parameters normalised
- platelets stayed low
- haemoglobin fell sharply without bleeding
- haemolysis became obvious
- schistocytes appeared on smear
- kidney injury did not recover, and she became anuric/dialysis-dependent

That combination should make you stop anchoring on DIC alone.

A useful mental model:  
**DIC = consumption problem**  
**TMA = shearing/endothelial injury problem**

In real life, they can overlap, especially in severe infection. The challenge is recognising when the “DIC explanation” no longer fits the whole story.

## The diagnostic pivot: think MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI

Once haemolysis showed up, the case became a TMA workup.

The key lab and clinical clues were:

- sudden haemoglobin drop without overt bleeding
- low/absent haptoglobin
- elevated LDH
- persistent thrombocytopenia
- schistocytes on peripheral smear
- progressive renal failure
- renal biopsy later showing fibrin thrombi and ischaemic glomeruli

That is microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) until proven otherwise.

One early red herring here was the positive DAT (Coombs) test, which raised concern for autoimmune haemolysis. But the smear told the real story: schistocytes, not spherocytes. The DAT later proved misleading, likely a false positive in the setting of penicillin exposure and/or transfusion.

## Rule out TTP fast, then keep moving

In any adult with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) has to be addressed immediately because it is the “don’t miss” diagnosis.

In this case:

- PLASMIC score was 4/7, which was low risk
- ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%
- that effectively ruled out TTP, since TTP typically has ADAMTS13 activity <10%

Plasma exchange was started while awaiting the full picture, which is exactly the right bridge when TTP is still on the table. But the important point is this: **PEX is definitive therapy for TTP, not for CM-HUS.**

Stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin, which argued against STEC-HUS. With TTP and STEC-HUS off the list, the biopsy-supported diagnosis became complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), likely triggered by the infection.

## Why normal complement levels don’t save you

A classic trap in CM-HUS is expecting low C3/C4.

Don’t.

Normal complement levels do **not** exclude CM-HUS. This patient’s standard complement studies were normal. That does not rule out complement dysregulation, and it’s one reason CM-HUS remains a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion.

Her renal biopsy helped a lot: severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli, fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescents — all consistent with TMA.

Another nuance: extended complement testing was done after plasma exchange and after eculizumab had already been started, so some of those functional complement results are hard to interpret cleanly. That’s reality in sick patients — we often treat before the full genetics are back.

## Treatment: bridge first, then block complement

This case highlights the therapeutic dilemma in infection-triggered CM-HUS.

Eculizumab is the standard treatment for CM-HUS and works by inhibiting terminal complement activation. But giving complement blockade during active bacteraemia is not trivial, because terminal complement is part of host defence against encapsulated organisms.

That is why timing matters.

In this case:

- she received broad-spectrum antibiotics early
- cultures later confirmed *C. canimorsus*
- PEX was used as a bridge while the diagnosis was being clarified
- eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative
- meningococcal vaccination was initiated, and antibiotic prophylaxis was given because the vaccines had not been given 2 weeks before complement blockade

Her diuresis returned after the PEX sessions, but sustained haematologic and renal improvement came only after eculizumab. That’s a pretty classic “temporary stabilization vs disease control” distinction.

## Clinical pearls from the case

A few take-home lessons are worth keeping in your pocket:

- **DIC and TMA can coexist.** Don’t assume the first explanation explains everything.
- **A falling platelet count that fails to recover, plus new haemolysis, should trigger a TMA reassessment.**
- **Schistocytes matter.** If the smear shows MAHA, keep digging.
- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS.**
- **Exclude TTP quickly with ADAMTS13; don’t wait if suspicion is high.**
- **Infection-triggered CM-HUS can emerge as the sepsis phase improves.** That timing is a clue, not a contradiction.
- **Eculizumab is the real disease-modifying therapy for CM-HUS, but timing, infection control, and vaccination/prophylaxis are critical.**

## Bottom line

This was not just dog-bite sepsis with DIC. It was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis complicated by a superimposed complement-mediated TMA.

The diagnostic pivot came from noticing that the infection was improving while haemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and kidney failure were not. That disconnect is the red flag.

When the labs stop moving together, assume there may be more than one process in play.
