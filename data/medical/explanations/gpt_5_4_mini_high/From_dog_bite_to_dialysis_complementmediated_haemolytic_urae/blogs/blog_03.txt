Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# The DAT Trap: When a Positive Coombs Test Distracts from MAHA

## A dog bite, sepsis, then a Coombs test that almost pulled the team the wrong way

This case starts like a classic “bad infection, bad labs” story and then takes a dangerous turn.

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s developed fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite. She arrived with pancytopenia, coagulopathy, AKI, lactic acidosis, mottled extremities, and later became anuric and dialysis-dependent. Blood cultures grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a rare but nasty dog-associated pathogen.

Then came the hematology curveball: after her sepsis began to improve, her hemoglobin dropped sharply, platelets stayed low, haptoglobin became undetectable, LDH rose, and the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG. That is the kind of result that can make you reach for steroids and label the patient with autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

But the blood smear told a different story: 15%–20% schistocytes. This was not warm autoimmune hemolysis. This was microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) from thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), ultimately confirmed on kidney biopsy and treated as complement-mediated HUS.

That’s the lesson here: a positive DAT does not end the hemolysis workup. Sometimes it just complicates it.

## Why the DAT can mislead you

The DAT is useful, but it is not a diagnosis. It tells you whether immunoglobulin and/or complement is coating the red cell surface. That can happen in true autoimmune hemolytic anemia, but also in a bunch of situations where the DAT is positive and hemolysis is not primarily immune-mediated.

In this case, there were two big confounders:

- **Recent penicillin exposure**
- **Multiple transfusions during critical illness**

Both can muddy the waters.

Beta-lactam antibiotics, including penicillins, can be associated with a positive DAT. Sometimes that reflects true drug-induced immune hemolysis; sometimes it’s a positive test without the full clinical syndrome of autoimmune hemolysis. Transfusions can also complicate serologic testing by introducing donor RBCs, alloantibodies, or nonspecific coating that makes interpretation messy.

So when the DAT is positive in an ICU patient, the key question is not “Is the DAT positive?” It is “Does the whole picture actually fit immune hemolysis?”

In this patient, the picture did not fit well enough.

## The smear is the tie-breaker

If you take one thing from this case, make it this: **the peripheral blood smear is not optional in suspected hemolysis**.

Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia usually gives you spherocytes, polychromasia, and a DAT that fits the story. MAHA gives you fragmented red cells — schistocytes — because the RBCs are being mechanically shredded as they pass through microthrombi and damaged microvasculature.

This patient’s smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes. That is a major clue. In the setting of:

- thrombocytopenia
- AKI
- rising LDH
- undetectable haptoglobin
- anemia without bleeding

…schistocytes should push you hard toward TMA, even if the DAT is positive.

The distinction matters because the treatment pathways are very different. Steroids may help warm AIHA. They do not fix complement-mediated TMA.

## Why steroids are such a tempting reflex

Once the DAT came back positive, high-dose prednisolone was started. That is understandable. In a sick patient with falling hemoglobin, it is hard not to treat the most familiar immune diagnosis in front of you.

But this case is a reminder that **a positive DAT can anchor you to the wrong mechanism**.

A few practical cautions:

- If the anemia is accompanied by **schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, and AKI**, think TMA first.
- If the hemoglobin drop is **out of proportion to bleeding**, do not stop at the DAT.
- If steroids do not produce the expected response, reassess early.

In this patient, the initial DAT was later felt to be a false positive, likely influenced by penicillin exposure or transfusion history. The repeat DAT was negative. The hemolysis pattern and smear were far more consistent with MAHA than with autoimmune hemolysis.

## Sepsis, DIC, and TMA can blur together

This is where the case gets especially tricky.

Early on, the patient looked like severe sepsis with DIC: fever, leukopaenia, coagulopathy, AKI, liver injury, and later skin bleeding and distal ischemic changes. That is a very plausible first interpretation. And it may have been correct for the early phase.

But then the story changed. As the inflammatory picture improved, she developed:

- sudden isolated anemia
- persistent thrombocytopenia
- undetectable haptoglobin
- rising LDH
- schistocytes

That shift should make you think that **something more than sepsis-associated coagulopathy is going on**.

The overlap between DIC and TMA can be brutal at the bedside. Both can present with thrombocytopenia and organ dysfunction. But persistent MAHA with schistocytes, especially as sepsis is improving, should raise concern for a superimposed TMA.

## The TMA workup that mattered here

Once TMA was on the table, the team did the right exclusion workup:

- **ADAMTS13 activity** was 43.5%, making TTP unlikely
- **PLASMIC score** was low-intermediate, not screaming TTP
- **Shiga toxin PCR** was negative, arguing against STEC-HUS
- **Kidney biopsy** showed TMA features: fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli, severe acute tubular injury

That constellation supported complement-mediated HUS.

A few important nuances from this case:

- **Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS**
- Complement testing can be hard to interpret during active illness and after plasma exchange or eculizumab
- In adults with MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI, you still have to think through TTP first because it is the “don’t miss” diagnosis

Plasma exchange was used as a bridge while the diagnosis was clarified, and eculizumab was started after vaccines and negative blood cultures, which is the right balancing act in a patient with recent bacteremia.

## Why this case matters beyond the rare diagnosis

Most of us will not see *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered complement-mediated HUS every year. But the diagnostic trap is common.

The lesson is broader:

- A **positive DAT does not equal autoimmune hemolysis**
- **Schistocytes trump the Coombs test** when the clinical picture points to MAHA
- **Recent antibiotics and transfusions can confuse serology**
- In critically ill patients, **hemolysis is often multifactorial**, and anchoring on one lab result is dangerous

This case also shows how infection can be the trigger that unmasks a complement-mediated TMA, even in someone without an obvious prior hematologic history.

## Practical bedside approach

When a critically ill patient has anemia and a positive DAT, pause and ask:

1. **Is there objective hemolysis?**
   - LDH up
   - haptoglobin down
   - bilirubin may help
   - reticulocytes may or may not be appropriately elevated in critical illness

2. **What does the smear show?**
   - Spherocytes? think immune hemolysis
   - Schistocytes? think MAHA/TMA

3. **Are platelets and kidneys involved?**
   - Thrombocytopenia + AKI should make TMA jump up the list

4. **What is the TTP risk?**
   - Send ADAMTS13 early
   - Use PLASMIC as a triage tool, not as truth

5. **Could the DAT be a red herring?**
   - Recent penicillin
   - Recent transfusions
   - Critical illness

## Take-home points

- A positive DAT is **not** synonymous with autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
- In a patient with anemia, thrombocytopenia, AKI, and hemolysis, **look at the smear early**.
- **Schistocytes** should shift you toward MAHA/TMA, even if the DAT is positive.
- Recent **penicillin exposure** and **transfusions** can make the DAT hard to interpret.
- If the clinical picture fits TMA, don’t let a positive Coombs test delay the broader workup for **TTP, STEC-HUS, and complement-mediated HUS**.
- In this case, the real diagnosis was not autoimmune hemolysis — it was **infection-triggered complement-mediated TMA**.
