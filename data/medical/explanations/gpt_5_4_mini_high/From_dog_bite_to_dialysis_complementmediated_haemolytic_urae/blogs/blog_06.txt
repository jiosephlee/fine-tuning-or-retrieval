Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Blocking Complement After Bacteraemia: Eculizumab, Plasma Exchange, and the Infection Tradeoff

## The surprising part of this case

A dog bite led to septic shock, DIC-like physiology, dialysis-dependent AKI, and then — just when the sepsis picture started to settle — a thrombotic microangiopathy declared itself.

That’s the interesting pivot here. The initial story looked like severe Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with coagulopathy. But the later story was different: falling hemoglobin, persistent thrombocytopenia, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes, and kidney biopsy showing TMA. That’s the moment you have to stop calling it “just sepsis” and start asking whether complement-mediated HUS is in play.

And once you get there, the treatment question becomes tricky: how do you give eculizumab — a drug that blocks terminal complement — in someone who just had bacteraemia from an encapsulated organism?

## When sepsis and TMA overlap, don’t anchor early

This case is a good reminder that septic shock can blur the borders between DIC and TMA.

Early on, the patient had fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, AKI, and mottled extremities. DIC from bacteraemia was a reasonable first framework. But the key is to keep re-evaluating the pattern as the story evolves. In this case, coagulation parameters normalized while haemolysis persisted and platelets stayed low. That mismatch should make you think beyond DIC.

A few practical points:

- **TTP must be excluded quickly** in any adult with MAHA + thrombocytopenia + organ injury.
- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS.**
- **ADAMTS13 is decisive for TTP**, not complement levels.
- **A positive DAT does not end the workup** if the smear shows schistocytes and the clinical picture fits TMA.

This patient’s PLASMIC score was low, and ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5%, making TTP unlikely. That opened the door to a CM-HUS diagnosis.

## Plasma exchange: useful as a bridge, not the destination

Plasma exchange has a very different role in TTP versus CM-HUS.

In **TTP**, plasma exchange is life-saving because it removes inhibitory anti-ADAMTS13 antibodies and replenishes ADAMTS13. In **CM-HUS**, it may temporarily improve the situation by removing inflammatory mediators, complement fragments, or autoantibodies — but it does not fix the underlying complement dysregulation.

That distinction matters.

In this case, plasma exchange was started while the team was still sorting out the TMA differential. That’s a defensible move when TTP hasn’t been excluded yet. But once the diagnosis points away from TTP and toward CM-HUS, PEX is best thought of as a **bridge**, not definitive therapy.

One of the subtle traps here is that transient clinical improvement after PEX can be misleading. This patient’s urine output returned after the final PEX sessions, but sustained haematologic and renal improvement came only after complement blockade was started.

## Eculizumab: the treatment that actually changes the disease

Eculizumab targets C5 and shuts down terminal complement activation. In CM-HUS, that’s the disease-modifying move. If you wait too long, renal recovery is often incomplete.

But in the setting of recent bacteraemia, you have to balance benefit against risk.

Why the concern? Because terminal complement is important for host defence against **encapsulated bacteria**, including **Neisseria** and, relevant here, **Capnocytophaga**. Blocking C5 in someone with active infection is not a trivial decision.

What did the team do here?

- They treated the infection first with broad-spectrum antibiotics, then narrowed therapy once **C. canimorsus** was confirmed.
- They started eculizumab **after blood cultures became negative**.
- They gave **Menveo** and **Bexsero** before induction.
- Because vaccines had not been given 2 weeks before complement blockade, they used **antibiotic prophylaxis with Apocillin**.

That is the practical compromise most of us are aiming for: get infection control as far along as possible, vaccinate if you can, and don’t leave the kidney to burn if CM-HUS is clearly evolving.

## Timing matters: don’t wait for genetics to act

This is one of the biggest take-home points.

CM-HUS is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion. Genetic testing can help explain susceptibility and guide long-term risk, but it is not what should determine whether you treat an acutely deteriorating patient.

In this case:

- the infection trigger was clear,
- schistocytes were present,
- kidney biopsy supported TMA,
- STEC was excluded,
- TTP was unlikely,
- and the clinical course improved after eculizumab.

That is enough to justify treatment.

The complement workup later showed no pathogenic variant, which supports an infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a clearly inherited complement defect. But a negative panel does **not** mean the complement system was innocent — it just means the genetics didn’t hand you a simple explanation.

## How long do you continue eculizumab when genetics are negative?

There isn’t a one-size-fits-all answer, and this case shows why.

The patient was discharged on maintenance eculizumab, with a plan for reassessment if renal function stabilized. That is a sensible, individualized approach when:

- the trigger was severe,
- kidney recovery is incomplete,
- and no pathogenic complement variant has been identified.

If a high-risk complement mutation is found, the argument for longer-term therapy becomes stronger. If genetics are negative and the trigger is clearly transient, stopping therapy may eventually be reasonable — but that decision has to be made cautiously, with close monitoring for relapse.

## Practical take-home points

- **Sepsis does not exclude TMA.** If platelets stay low and haemolysis appears after the shock phase, pivot.
- **Use plasma exchange as a bridge when TTP is still on the table.** It’s not definitive therapy for CM-HUS.
- **Eculizumab is the disease-modifying treatment for CM-HUS**, but infection risk is the price tag.
- **Try to start complement blockade after culture clearance if possible.**
- **Vaccinate against meningococcus and use antibiotic prophylaxis** if eculizumab must start before the vaccine window is complete.
- **Negative complement genetics do not rule out infection-triggered CM-HUS** and do not automatically tell you when to stop therapy.

This case is a good reminder that sometimes the hardest part of treating CM-HUS is not recognizing the TMA — it’s knowing when the infection is controlled enough to safely turn off complement.
