Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Dog Bite, White Count Down: Recognizing Capnocytophaga Before the Culture Turns Positive

## Why this case matters

Dog bites are common. Fulminant **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** sepsis is not.

What makes this case striking is how quickly a previously healthy woman went from a lip bite to **high fever, refractory hypoglycaemia, pancytopenia, DIC, AKI, acral ischaemia, and dialysis dependence**. Her blood cultures only turned positive **7 days later**. That delay is exactly why this organism is easy to miss: the patient can look like “just another septic patient” long before the microbiology catches up.

The big teaching point is simple: **recent dog exposure plus a very sick patient should immediately put Capnocytophaga on the short list — even if the host is immunocompetent**.

## Capnocytophaga canimorsus: the organism behind the bite

C. canimorsus is a **slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod** found in the normal oral flora of dogs and cats. Transmission can occur via **bite, scratch, or close contact**. It is classically associated with severe infection in patients with asplenia, alcohol use disorder, or immunosuppression, but this case is a good reminder that **“healthy” does not mean protected**.

Clinical presentations range from mild bacteremia to **fulminant sepsis, DIC, multiorgan failure, and thrombotic microangiopathy**. Mortality remains substantial, reported to approach **30%** despite broad antibiotic susceptibility.

## The bedside pattern you should not ignore

This case had several red flags that should make any ED/ICU clinician pause:

- **Recent dog bite**, especially to the face/mucosa
- **Rapid deterioration over days**, not weeks
- **Fever and septic appearance**
- **Leukopenia/pancytopenia** rather than the more familiar leukocytosis
- **Coagulopathy/DIC**
- **AKI with proteinuria/haematuria**
- **Hypoglycaemia**
- **Acral mottling/ischaemia** of feet, toes, fingers

That combination should make you think beyond routine cellulitis or “bite wound infection.” In severe Capnocytophaga infection, the early picture can be dominated by **sepsis physiology and DIC**, which can hide what is actually a rapidly progressive invasive zoonosis.

Acral changes deserve special attention. Mottled, cool, cyanotic, or ischaemic digits in a septic patient should trigger concern for **microthrombotic disease**. In this case, the extremity findings initially fit the DIC picture, but the later course also reminded everyone that a superimposed **TMA** can emerge as the sepsis settles.

## Why the white count can be low

One of the more counterintuitive clues here was the **marked leukopaenia/pancytopenia**. We often picture severe bacterial infection as a high WBC count, but overwhelming sepsis can go the other way. In the right context, a low white count is not reassuring — it can be a marker of **severe systemic illness**.

Add **hypoglycaemia** to that picture and the alarm bells should get louder. In a septic patient, especially one with poor intake, low glucose can be multifactorial. But when it is profound and hard to correct, it often reflects **major physiologic stress** rather than simple starvation.

## Treat first, prove later

The right move in this case was broad empiric coverage with **piperacillin/tazobactam**, which is a very reasonable choice for a critically ill patient with a dog-bite source and suspected zoonotic gram-negative infection.

The key is not to wait for the culture result before treating aggressively. Capnocytophaga is **fastidious and slow-growing**, so early cultures may be unrevealing. If the patient is crashing and the story fits, you need to act on the bedside pattern.

Once C. canimorsus is identified and the patient stabilizes, therapy can often be **narrowed**. In this case, antibiotics were de-escalated to **ampicillin** and later oral **penicillin** based on culture results.

## Why cultures may lag behind the patient

This is a classic organism where the lab may be behind the clinical picture. Blood cultures can take days to turn positive, and that delay can be clinically dangerous if the diagnosis is not considered early.

That is where **molecular diagnostics** are increasingly important. In some centers, methods such as **real-time nanopore sequencing** or other rapid molecular techniques may identify fastidious organisms sooner than routine culture. You don’t need them to treat the patient, but they can be helpful when:

- cultures are negative early,
- antibiotics have already been started,
- or the clinical picture strongly suggests an unusual pathogen.

Bottom line: **a negative early culture does not rule out Capnocytophaga**.

## Don’t anchor on “just a bite”

One of the traps in this case would have been to focus only on the local wound. The patient didn’t present with a dramatic infected laceration; she presented with **systemic collapse**. That’s the key distinction.

When a dog-bite patient comes in with shock physiology, cytopenias, DIC, renal injury, and hypoglycaemia, think beyond routine bite-wound management. This is not about suturing the wound or outpatient oral antibiotics. This is an **ICU-level invasive infection** until proven otherwise.

## A final twist: when sepsis improves but the labs don’t

This case also had a second teaching point: after the initial septic phase improved, the patient developed **new isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, and biopsy-proven thrombotic microangiopathy**, ultimately diagnosed as **complement-mediated HUS**.

That’s not the usual Capnocytophaga story, but it matters because it reminds us that severe infection can be the trigger for a second process. If the platelet count, haemolysis, or kidney injury stop behaving like straightforward sepsis, keep the differential open.

## Take-home points

- **Recent dog exposure + severe sepsis = think Capnocytophaga canimorsus early**, even in immunocompetent adults.
- **Leukopenia/pancytopenia, DIC, hypoglycaemia, AKI, and acral ischaemia** are major bedside clues.
- **Do not wait for culture positivity** to start treatment; broad IV beta-lactam coverage is appropriate in severe disease.
- **Capnocytophaga is slow-growing**, so early negative cultures do not exclude it.
- **Molecular diagnostics** are increasingly useful when suspicion is high and cultures lag.
- If the patient’s course becomes atypical after the sepsis improves, **reconsider TMA/CM-HUS** rather than assuming the job is done.
