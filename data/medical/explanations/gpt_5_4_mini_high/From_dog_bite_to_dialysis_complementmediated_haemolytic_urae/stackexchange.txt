Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] A previously healthy adult develops fulminant sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation 4 days after a dog bite. Which zoonotic pathogen should be strongly suspected, and what microbiologic characteristic commonly delays culture-based diagnosis?

The pathogen to strongly suspect is **Capnocytophaga canimorsus**. In a previously healthy adult who develops **fulminant sepsis, DIC, and multiorgan failure within days of a dog bite**, this organism should be high on the differential, even without classic immunocompromising risk factors.

The microbiologic feature that commonly delays diagnosis is that **C. canimorsus is fastidious and slow-growing**—a **slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod** that may require **prolonged incubation** and can be difficult to recover on routine culture. As in the case report, blood cultures may not turn positive for several days, which is why clinical suspicion must prompt early empiric therapy.

### [Morning Report] In the initial phase of this case, what clinical and laboratory features supported sepsis-associated DIC rather than primary thrombotic microangiopathy, and why can the two processes be difficult to distinguish early?

In the initial phase, the picture fit **sepsis-associated DIC** much better than primary thrombotic microangiopathy. She had a clear infectious trigger after a dog bite, with **fulminant septic physiology**: high fever, marked inflammatory response, lactic acidosis, hypoglycemia, mottled/cool extremities, AKI, liver enzyme elevation, **pancytopenia**, and **coagulopathy** requiring platelet and fibrinogen support. Importantly, the early hematologic data did **not** yet show convincing microangiopathic hemolysis: haptoglobin was normal, the reticulocyte response was not appropriately elevated, and schistocytosis was not demonstrated until later. That pattern is much more typical of DIC in severe sepsis, where consumption of clotting factors and platelets accompanies systemic inflammation.

The two syndromes are hard to separate early because they **overlap clinically**: both can produce thrombocytopenia, anemia, AKI, and distal ischemic skin changes. In addition, **sepsis can cause DIC and can also trigger complement-mediated TMA**, so the hematologic picture may evolve over days. In this case, the diagnosis only became clear when the sepsis/coagulopathy improved but **isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, and biopsy-proven TMA** emerged later, revealing a superimposed complement-mediated HUS.

### [Differential Diagnosis] After the patient’s sepsis and coagulopathy began improving, she developed a new fall in hemoglobin, persistent thrombocytopenia, elevated LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, and 15%–20% schistocytes. What is the most important next diagnostic framework for this syndrome, and which major thrombotic microangiopathies must be rapidly differentiated in an adult with AKI?

The key next step is to recognize this as a **thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)**—that is, **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia plus thrombocytopenia and organ injury**—rather than persistent sepsis-related DIC or bleeding. In this case, the new isolated hemolysis with 15%–20% schistocytes, undetectable haptoglobin, and ongoing thrombocytopenia after the coagulopathy improved should immediately trigger the **TMA diagnostic framework**, with **urgent ADAMTS13 testing** to exclude TTP and parallel evaluation for Shiga toxin–associated HUS and complement-mediated HUS.

In an adult with AKI, the major TMAs that must be rapidly distinguished are:

1. **Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** — classically **severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%)**; this is the most time-critical diagnosis because it requires immediate plasma exchange.  
2. **Shiga toxin–associated HUS (STEC-HUS)** — usually linked to diarrheal illness and confirmed by stool PCR/toxin testing.  
3. **Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS, atypical HUS)** — a diagnosis of exclusion when TTP and STEC-HUS are ruled out, often presenting with prominent AKI; **normal C3/C4 do not exclude it**.

So, the most important diagnostic move is to **treat this as TMA until proven otherwise and rapidly separate TTP from STEC-HUS and CM-HUS**, because the management and urgency differ profoundly.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do ADAMTS13 activity testing and Shiga toxin testing help distinguish TTP, STEC-HUS, and complement-mediated HUS in an adult with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury?

In an adult with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and severe kidney injury, **ADAMTS13 activity and Shiga toxin testing are the key early branch points in the TMA differential**.

**ADAMTS13 activity testing distinguishes TTP from HUS.**  
Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura is defined by **severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, typically <10%**. If activity is that low, TTP is the leading diagnosis and plasma exchange should not be delayed. In this case, the ADAMTS13 activity was **43.5%**, which is **not compatible with TTP** and makes TTP very unlikely.

**Shiga toxin testing distinguishes STEC-HUS from complement-mediated HUS.**  
Shiga toxin–producing E. coli HUS is confirmed by **detecting Shiga toxin or its genes in stool by PCR/culture**. A **negative stool PCR** argues strongly against STEC-HUS, especially when there is no diarrheal prodrome, as in this case.

So when an adult has TMA with **ADAMTS13 not severely reduced** and **Shiga toxin testing negative**, the remaining major diagnosis is **complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)**—a **diagnosis of exclusion**. This fits the case: persistent MAHA, thrombocytopenia, dialysis-dependent AKI, kidney biopsy showing TMA, **ADAMTS13 43.5%**, and **negative Shiga toxin PCR**, all supporting CM-HUS rather than TTP or STEC-HUS. Normal C3/C4 do **not** exclude CM-HUS.

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient initially had a positive direct antiglobulin test for IgG, but corticosteroids did not improve the hemolysis and repeat testing became negative. What features argue against autoimmune hemolytic anemia and instead support thrombotic microangiopathy as the cause of hemolysis?

Several features point away from autoimmune hemolytic anemia and toward a thrombotic microangiopathy.

First, the hemolysis was **microangiopathic in pattern**: the peripheral smear showed **15% to 20% schistocytes**, with **rising LDH** and **undetectable haptoglobin**. That combination is classic for red-cell fragmentation from intravascular shear stress, not for warm AIHA, which more often shows **spherocytes**.

Second, the hemolysis occurred together with **persistent thrombocytopenia** and **severe acute kidney injury progressing to anuria and dialysis dependence**. That triad—**MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and renal injury**—strongly supports TMA. AIHA alone does not explain the platelet consumption or the degree of renal failure.

Third, there was **no clinical response to high-dose corticosteroids**, which you would expect in warm AIHA. Instead, the process continued until the TMA workup was pursued.

Fourth, the **repeat direct antiglobulin test became negative**, and in this setting the initial IgG-positive DAT was most likely a **false-positive** related to **penicillin exposure or transfusion** rather than true immune hemolysis.

Finally, the **kidney biopsy demonstrated fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli**, confirming TMA as the pathologic substrate of the hemolysis.

So the key teaching point is that a positive DAT does not override the broader syndrome: **schistocytosis, thrombocytopenia, AKI, steroid nonresponse, and biopsy evidence of microvascular thrombosis** argue strongly for TMA rather than autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

### [Pathophysiology] Why do normal C3 and C4 levels not exclude complement-mediated HUS, and what is the underlying pathophysiologic mechanism by which infection can trigger this disorder even in the absence of an identified pathogenic complement gene variant?

Normal C3 and C4 levels do **not** exclude complement-mediated HUS because this is usually **not a quantitative deficiency of complement proteins**; it is a disorder of **complement regulation and local overactivation**, most often of the **alternative pathway**. In other words, the problem is not that C3 or C4 are absent in the blood, but that complement is being **inappropriately amplified on endothelial surfaces**, especially in the renal microvasculature. Because C4 is primarily a marker of the **classical pathway**, it is often normal in complement-mediated HUS, and C3 may also remain in the reference range if hepatic synthesis keeps pace or if activation is largely **tissue-localized rather than reflected in circulating levels**. That is why this diagnosis remains a **clinicopathologic one** and cannot be ruled out by normal C3/C4.

The pathophysiology is a “**loss of complement control**” at the endothelial surface, leading to unchecked alternative-pathway C3 convertase activity, downstream generation of C5b-9, endothelial injury, platelet activation, and thrombotic microangiopathy. In the case reported, the patient had a severe **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** infection, and the key clue was that the hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia appeared **after the sepsis began to improve**, which is typical of a superimposed complement-mediated process rather than DIC alone. Infection can act as the **triggering second hit** by strongly activating complement through microbial surfaces and systemic inflammation, thereby overwhelming the patient’s complement regulatory capacity—even when no pathogenic complement gene variant is identified. This may reflect **incomplete penetrance**, an **undetected genetic or acquired predisposition**, or simply **transient infection-driven complement activation** sufficient to precipitate HUS.

So the teaching point is: **normal C3/C4 do not rule out CM-HUS**, because the disorder is defined by **dysregulated alternative-pathway activation at the endothelium**, and infection can precipitate the syndrome by tipping that balance, even in an apparently genetically negative patient.

### [Morning Report] What renal biopsy findings are most consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy in this case, and how can biopsy help when the diagnosis remains uncertain after laboratory evaluation?

The renal biopsy findings most consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy in this case were **fibrin thrombi within ischemic glomeruli**, accompanied by **severe acute tubular necrosis** and **scattered crescent formation**. On the report, **immunofluorescence showed no immune-complex deposition** and electron microscopy did not show a primary glomerulopathy, which supports a **microvascular thrombotic/endothelial injury pattern** rather than an immune-complex nephritis or another primary glomerular disease.

Biopsy is especially valuable when the laboratory evaluation is not definitive, as in this patient, whose course was initially confounded by **sepsis, DIC, AKI, thrombocytopenia, and hemolysis**. A kidney biopsy can **confirm that renal injury is due to TMA**, and it can also **exclude mimics** such as immune-complex glomerulonephritis, vasculitis, or another intrinsic renal process. In this case, the biopsy helped anchor the diagnosis of **TMA/CM-HUS** after **ADAMTS13 was not severely reduced** and routine complement testing was nondiagnostic, showing that tissue diagnosis can be decisive when the clinical and laboratory picture remains ambiguous.

### [Management] Why is plasma exchange often started empirically in adults presenting with thrombotic microangiopathy while ADAMTS13 results are pending, and why is its role different in complement-mediated HUS than in TTP?

Adults who present with the triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury should be treated as having **possible TTP until proven otherwise**, because **TTP is a hematologic emergency** and treatment delay can be fatal. The diagnosis is ultimately laboratory-based—**severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, typically <10%, confirms TTP**—but that result is often not immediately available. Therefore, if the clinical suspicion is meaningful, plasma exchange is started empirically as a **bridge while ADAMTS13 is pending**, rather than waiting and risking irreversible neurologic or organ injury.

The reason plasma exchange is so effective in **TTP** is mechanistic: it **removes circulating anti-ADAMTS13 autoantibodies and ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers** and **replaces deficient ADAMTS13** with donor plasma. In other words, it treats the core pathophysiology.

Its role is quite different in **complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)**. CM-HUS is driven by **dysregulated alternative complement activation**, not ADAMTS13 deficiency. Plasma exchange may provide only **transient benefit** by removing circulating complement fragments or, in some cases, autoantibodies, but it does **not correct the underlying complement abnormality**. That is why the definitive therapy in CM-HUS is **terminal complement blockade with eculizumab**, which in this case was associated with sustained hematologic recovery and partial renal recovery, whereas plasma exchange was only a temporary bridge. The case illustrates this well: ADAMTS13 later returned at **43.5%**, making TTP unlikely, and the patient’s durable improvement occurred only after complement inhibition, not plasma exchange alone.

### [Management] What factors should guide the timing of eculizumab initiation in a patient with suspected complement-mediated HUS who recently had documented Capnocytophaga bacteremia, and what infection-prevention measures are required when starting terminal complement blockade?

In a patient with suspected complement-mediated HUS after recent **Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia**, the timing of **eculizumab** should be guided by two competing risks: the need to **stop ongoing thrombotic microangiopathy early** to preserve renal function, and the need to **avoid blocking terminal complement during uncontrolled infection**. In practice, as in this case, it is reasonable to start eculizumab **after the bacteremia is clinically controlled and blood cultures are negative**, while the patient is receiving appropriate antibiotics and after **TTP has been effectively excluded** (ADAMTS13 not severely reduced). Plasma exchange can serve as a bridge while the diagnosis is clarified and infection control is established. The key point is not to wait for complete renal recovery; once ongoing MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI support CM-HUS, treatment should proceed promptly.

Before terminal complement blockade, **meningococcal prevention is mandatory**. Give **both MenACWY and MenB vaccines**, ideally **at least 2 weeks before** the first eculizumab dose. If urgent treatment must begin sooner, start eculizumab with **concurrent antibiotic prophylaxis**—in this report, **Apocillin/penicillin**—and continue prophylaxis until vaccine-induced protection is expected, per local protocol. Patients must also be counseled to seek **immediate care for fever or meningococcal symptoms**, and vaccine boosters should be kept up to date.
