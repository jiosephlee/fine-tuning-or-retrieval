Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Capnocytophaga canimorsus After Dog Bite: Microbiology, Transmission, and the Initial Septic Presentation

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a fastidious, slow-growing, gram-negative rod that colonizes the oral cavities of dogs and cats. In humans, infection usually follows a bite, scratch, or exposure of broken skin or mucosa to saliva, and even apparently minor or indirect contact can transmit the organism. Its encapsulated surface helps it evade host defences, including complement-mediated killing, which contributes to its ability to cause invasive disease. Although severe infection is classically associated with risk factors such as asplenia, alcohol use disorder, cirrhosis, or immunosuppression, this case illustrates an important teaching point: fulminant disease can also occur in previously healthy adults.

The clinical spectrum of *C. canimorsus* infection is broad. Some patients develop only a local wound infection or mild bacteraemia, whereas others progress rapidly to meningitis, septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure, and, more rarely, thrombotic microangiopathy. In practice, the organism is dangerous not because it is resistant to most antibiotics, but because recognition is often delayed. Its growth in culture is slow and may take several days, so the diagnosis frequently depends on clinical suspicion after a compatible exposure history.

This patient’s timeline is typical of an invasive zoonotic infection with a short incubation period. She was bitten on the upper lip by her dog and was prescribed penicillin prophylaxis, but treatment was not started. Within four days she developed high fever, malaise, poor oral intake, and then peripheral symptoms including numbness and discolouration of the feet, prompting emergency evaluation. The rapid deterioration from a seemingly localized animal bite to systemic illness should immediately raise concern for dog-associated sepsis, especially when symptoms appear within days of the exposure.

On arrival, her examination and laboratory profile were most consistent with severe sepsis. She was febrile and appeared vasoconstricted and poorly perfused, with mottled, cool extremities. Blood gas analysis showed metabolic acidosis and marked hyperlactataemia, reflecting tissue hypoperfusion. She also had pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy, all of which can occur in sepsis with DIC and evolving multiorgan dysfunction. The admission hypoglycaemia is also compatible with severe sepsis, in which impaired hepatic glucose production and increased metabolic demand can make glucose levels difficult to maintain. Urinalysis showing blood and protein supported concurrent renal involvement.

At this stage, the immediate differential was appropriately broad. The most likely diagnosis was septic DIC secondary to bacteraemia, but the differential also had to include necrotising soft-tissue infection because of the discoloured extremities, acute haematologic disease because of pancytopenia, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in the setting of systemic inflammation, and early thrombotic microangiopathy if haemolysis or renal injury evolved. In a patient with animal exposure and rapid shock physiology, empiric treatment should not wait for microbiologic confirmation. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics with reliable coverage of dog-bite pathogens, including *Capnocytophaga* species, are appropriate; piperacillin/tazobactam is a reasonable choice in this setting. Supportive care for sepsis and coagulopathy is equally important while the diagnostic picture becomes clearer.

This case also highlights the diagnostic limitations of *C. canimorsus*. Blood cultures ultimately confirmed the organism, but only after a delay of several days, which is common with fastidious bacteria. Thus, early recognition depends more on the clinical context than on initial microbiology. Newer molecular methods, including sequencing-based approaches, can shorten the time to identification in some centres, but standard blood cultures remain central to diagnosis when they become positive. For the clinician, the key lesson is that a recent dog bite followed by rapid onset of sepsis, coagulopathy, and organ dysfunction should prompt immediate empiric therapy for *C. canimorsus* even before cultures return.

## Early Multiorgan Failure: Sepsis, Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation, and Acute Kidney Injury

The patient’s early ICU course was dominated by fulminant sepsis from *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia after a dog bite. This organism can cause severe disease even in previously healthy adults, and the presentation may progress quickly from nonspecific fever and malaise to shock physiology, multiorgan dysfunction, and coagulopathy. In this case, the initial bedside picture was that of severe infection: fever, lactic acidosis, leukopaenia/pancytopenia, liver test abnormalities, acute kidney injury, and abnormal coagulation studies. At that stage, the overall pattern was most consistent with sepsis complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), rather than a primary thrombotic microangiopathy.

DIC is a consumptive coagulopathy driven by widespread activation of coagulation in response to sepsis and endothelial injury. Thrombin generation leads to fibrin deposition in the microcirculation, while platelets and clotting factors are consumed faster than they can be replaced. Clinically, this creates the paradox of simultaneous thrombosis and bleeding. The patient’s diffuse ecchymoses, skin bleeding, and distal ischemic colour changes of the fingers and toes fit this pattern of microvascular thrombosis and hemorrhagic tendency. Her mottled extremities were concerning, but surgical causes such as necrotizing fasciitis were excluded clinically and radiologically, making DIC-related microthrombotic ischemia the more plausible explanation at that point.

Marked cytopenias can occur in severe sepsis for several reasons: marrow suppression from inflammation, peripheral consumption in DIC, dilution from resuscitation, and occasionally hemophagocytic syndromes. Because pancytopenia is also seen in acute leukemia and other hematologic disorders, the differential diagnosis had to remain broad. This is why hematology was consulted early, flow cytometry was obtained, and bone marrow biopsy was considered. When flow cytometry did not show blasts or an abnormal immunophenotype, a primary marrow malignancy became less likely, and the biopsy was deferred. In a critically ill patient, this kind of stepwise narrowing of the differential is important, because severe infection can produce blood count abnormalities that mimic hematologic disease.

Her profound hypoglycaemia was another marker of severe systemic illness. In sepsis, low glucose can result from increased peripheral glucose consumption, impaired hepatic gluconeogenesis, depleted glycogen stores, and, in some cases, relative adrenal insufficiency or liver dysfunction. Unlike the more common stress hyperglycemia, hypoglycemia in sepsis is often a sign of advanced disease and requires urgent treatment with intravenous dextrose while the underlying cause is addressed. In the emergency department, persistent low glucose despite replacement reinforced the impression of severe physiological stress.

The kidney injury evolved from preserved urine output on arrival to complete anuria after ICU transfer. Septic acute kidney injury is usually multifactorial: renal hypoperfusion, vasodilation, inflammatory endothelial injury, microcirculatory dysfunction, and acute tubular necrosis all contribute. Importantly, creatinine rise and oliguria/anuria in sepsis do not necessarily reflect simple volume depletion; the renal tubules and microvasculature are often directly injured. The patient initially received aggressive fluid resuscitation, and loop diuretics were tried, but once anuria and fluid overload developed, these measures could not restore renal function. Her septic cardiomyopathy further limited tolerance of fluid loading and increased the need for close hemodynamic and volume management.

Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was appropriate once she became anuric with worsening fluid overload in the setting of critical illness. CRRT is often preferred over intermittent dialysis in unstable ICU patients because it allows gradual fluid and solute removal and better control of volume status. In this case, dialysis was needed not simply because kidney function had worsened, but because the combination of anuria, fluid overload, and cardiac dysfunction made ongoing conservative management unsafe. Supportive blood product transfusion was also reasonable in the setting of DIC, where bleeding risk and consumption coagulopathy may require replacement of platelets and fibrinogen, especially when there is active bleeding or a need for invasive procedures.

Stress-dose corticosteroids were used during the septic phase as part of ICU supportive care. They do not treat DIC or AKI directly, but are sometimes used in severe sepsis/shock physiology as an adjunct when clinicians are concerned about adrenal insufficiency or unstable hemodynamics. As in all severe infections, the patient also required continuous reassessment for alternative causes of her multiorgan failure. Autoimmune testing with ANA and ANCA was negative, helping to exclude vasculitic or immune-complex renal disease that might otherwise be considered in a patient with kidney injury and systemic symptoms.

A key teaching point from this phase is that DIC can obscure an evolving thrombotic microangiopathy. Both DIC and TMA can present with thrombocytopenia, organ dysfunction, skin ischemia, and hemolysis-related findings, but the underlying mechanisms differ. In classic DIC, the coagulopathy is prominent because clotting factors are consumed; in TMA, platelet-rich microthrombi and microangiopathic hemolysis dominate, often with less severe derangement of clotting tests. Early in this case, the profound sepsis, abnormal coagulation profile, and multiorgan dysfunction made DIC the most defensible diagnosis. The transition point came later, when overt sepsis and coagulopathy improved but the kidney failed to recover, setting the stage for recognition of a superimposed complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy.

## When the Story Changes: Recognizing Thrombotic Microangiopathy and Diagnosing Complement-Mediated HUS

In this case, the diagnostic pivot came after the patient’s initial sepsis began to improve. Her inflammatory markers and coagulation profile were recovering, but she then developed a sudden, isolated drop in haemoglobin without obvious bleeding, haemodynamic collapse, or another explanation such as major fluid dilution. In a patient who remained thrombocytopenic and had severe kidney injury, this pattern should prompt concern for a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) rather than ongoing sepsis alone. This is the key teaching point: when a critically ill patient stops behaving like a pure sepsis case and instead shows new haemolysis with persistent thrombocytopenia and renal failure, the differential must broaden.

The laboratory pattern supported haemolysis. Haptoglobin became undetectable, lactate dehydrogenase rose, and platelets remained low despite improvement in the infectious and coagulation parameters. These findings are typical of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), in which red cells are mechanically fragmented as they pass through small vessels containing platelet-rich thrombi. A positive direct antiglobulin test initially raised the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, but this was not sustained by the rest of the picture. In this setting, DAT positivity can be misleading, particularly after penicillin exposure or recent transfusions, both of which can produce a false-positive result or nonspecific serologic interference. The later negative repeat test and lack of steroid response argued strongly against a true immune haemolysis.

The peripheral blood smear was decisive. Schistocytes, especially when present in substantial numbers, are the morphologic hallmark of MAHA and point toward TMA. In this patient, 15%–20% schistocytes is a striking abnormality and is far beyond the small number of fragments sometimes seen in other critical illnesses. Together with thrombocytopenia and kidney injury, the smear shifted the diagnostic frame from “post-sepsis cytopenias” to a true TMA syndrome. This distinction matters because TMAs require targeted evaluation for the major adult syndromes: thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin-associated HUS (STEC-HUS), complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), secondary TMAs, and, in some patients, MAHA associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

TTP was the most urgent diagnosis to exclude because it is rapidly fatal if missed and requires immediate plasma exchange. Clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC can help estimate pre-test probability while awaiting definitive testing. In this case, the PLASMIC score was low and ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, which effectively ruled out TTP. Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, usually less than 10%, is the defining laboratory feature of TTP; values in the 40% range are not compatible with classic TTP. Plasma exchange was still reasonable as a bridge while the workup was pending, but the result made TTP very unlikely and redirected attention to other TMAs.

STEC-HUS also had to be considered, because it is another common cause of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury. However, this patient had no convincing diarrhoeal prodrome, and stool PCR for Shiga toxin-producing E. coli was negative. That combination made STEC-HUS unlikely. This left CM-HUS and secondary TMA as the main remaining possibilities. In adults with infection, severe systemic illness, and organ injury, there is often overlap between DIC and TMA, but the persistence of haemolysis, schistocytosis, and renal failure after the coagulation abnormalities improved supported a separate TMA process rather than DIC alone.

Complement testing can be helpful, but it has important limitations. Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS, because patients can have clinically significant complement dysregulation even when routine complement levels are within the reference range. Functional pathway assays may also be unrevealing, particularly if samples are obtained after plasma exchange or after complement inhibition has already begun. In other words, normal “screening” complement studies cannot rule out CM-HUS. This is why CM-HUS remains a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion, built from the overall clinical pattern rather than a single confirmatory blood test.

Kidney biopsy provided strong supportive evidence. The biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli, fibrin thrombi, and some crescent formation, all of which fit with renal TMA. Renal histology in TMA does not identify the cause by itself, but it demonstrates the target-organ injury expected in CM-HUS and helps separate TMA from primary glomerular disease or isolated acute tubular injury. In this case, the absence of immune-complex deposition and lack of evidence for a primary glomerulopathy also helped narrow the diagnosis.

Putting the pieces together, the final diagnosis of CM-HUS was reached by exclusion and by clinicopathologic correlation: persistent MAHA with schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, dialysis-requiring kidney injury, biopsy-proven renal TMA, negative Shiga toxin testing, and ADAMTS13 activity not consistent with TTP. The fact that haemolysis appeared as the septic phase was improving also suggested an infection-triggered complement-mediated process rather than simple sepsis-related cytopenia. This case illustrates why clinicians must revisit the diagnosis when the clinical course changes: in a patient who initially seems to have DIC from sepsis, a later pattern of isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia should trigger a structured TMA workup and consideration of CM-HUS.

## Pathophysiology of Complement-Mediated HUS and Infection-Triggered Endothelial Injury

What was historically called “atypical” haemolytic uraemic syndrome is now better termed complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), because the central problem in many patients is dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway rather than a purely descriptive “atypical” clinical pattern. This distinction matters in practice: CM-HUS is not just a diagnosis of exclusion, but a clinicopathological syndrome in which uncontrolled complement activation injures the endothelium, especially in the renal microvasculature. In this case, the diagnosis was considered only after the more common explanations for thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), particularly thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and Shiga toxin-associated HUS, were not supported by the laboratory workup.

In normal host defence, the alternative complement pathway is continuously active at a low level through “tick-over” of C3. C3b can bind microbial or host surfaces, then factor B binds and is cleaved, forming the alternative pathway C3 convertase (C3bBb), which amplifies complement activation. This system is kept under control by regulatory proteins. Factor H and factor I are soluble regulators: factor H accelerates decay of the convertase and serves as a cofactor for factor I, which cleaves C3b into inactive fragments. CD46, also called membrane cofactor protein, is a cell-surface regulator on host cells, including endothelium, that helps protect self-tissue by supporting factor I-mediated inactivation of complement. When these regulatory mechanisms are impaired by inherited variants or autoantibodies, complement activation becomes excessive on host endothelium. Downstream cleavage of C5 and formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) then promote cellular injury and inflammation.

The clinical consequence of this endothelial injury is TMA. Damaged microvascular endothelium becomes prothrombotic, with platelet adhesion and aggregation, fibrin deposition, and formation of small-vessel thrombi. Red blood cells passing through these partially obstructed vessels are mechanically fragmented, producing microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) with schistocytes, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, low haptoglobin, and falling haemoglobin. Platelets are consumed in the process, causing thrombocytopenia. The kidney is often the dominant target because of its dense microvascular network and susceptibility to endothelial injury, which explains the combination of acute kidney injury, proteinuria, haematuria, and, in severe cases, dialysis dependence seen in this patient.

Most patients with CM-HUS have some form of complement predisposition, but penetrance is incomplete. Variants in genes encoding factor H, factor I, factor B, CD46, and related regulators may be present, yet many carriers never develop disease. This is why CM-HUS is often described as requiring a “second hit.” A trigger such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disease, transplantation, or certain drugs can tip a previously compensated complement system into uncontrolled activation. In this case, the dog bite and ensuing Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia provided a biologically plausible trigger. The patient initially had a sepsis/DIC picture, but the later appearance of isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia, after the inflammatory state had begun to improve, is the pattern that raised concern for a superimposed complement-mediated TMA.

C. canimorsus is a particularly plausible trigger because it is a zoonotic, encapsulated gram-negative organism for which complement-mediated killing is an important defence mechanism. Encapsulated bacteria can evade immunity, and some C. canimorsus strains show relative serum resistance, while others are more complement sensitive. This creates a paradox: the alternative complement pathway is needed to control the infection, but strong complement activation during bacteraemia can also intensify endothelial stress and precipitate TMA in a susceptible host. The case therefore illustrates how the same immune pathway can be both protective and pathogenic. The severe sepsis likely created a proinflammatory, prothrombotic milieu, and the subsequent haemolytic phase is consistent with complement-driven endothelial injury emerging as a second process.

A normal C3 or C4 level does not exclude CM-HUS. This is an important teaching point, because routine complement tests are often misinterpreted as screening tests for complement-mediated disease. In reality, C3 and C4 are crude plasma concentrations and may remain within the reference range despite clinically important alternative pathway dysregulation. C4 is primarily a classical and lectin pathway component, so a normal C4 is not reassuring in a disease driven by the alternative pathway. Even C3 can be normal if consumption is modest, intermittent, compartmentalised to the endothelium, or balanced by hepatic synthesis. For that reason, CM-HUS remains a clinical diagnosis supported by exclusion of other TMAs, kidney biopsy when needed, and response to complement blockade.

Interpretation of complement testing is also complicated by treatment. Plasma exchange removes complement proteins and circulating antibodies, and eculizumab directly blocks terminal complement activation; both can distort functional assays. In this patient, extended complement studies were drawn after several plasma exchange sessions and after the first dose of eculizumab, so the reduced alternative pathway activity (low AH50) is best interpreted cautiously and is likely influenced by treatment rather than reflecting her untreated baseline. Her normal factor H, factor I, and factor B levels and negative anti-factor H antibodies argue against some common acquired or quantitative defects, and the absence of pathogenic variants on genetic testing makes a known inherited complement defect less likely. However, negative genetic testing does not eliminate CM-HUS; it simply means that no recognised pathogenic variant was identified with the testing performed.

This case fits the emerging literature that infections, including unusual zoonotic infections, can precipitate complement-mediated TMA even in adults without an obvious prior history of kidney disease or recurrent haemolysis. The fact that C. canimorsus is both a complement-sensitive pathogen and a trigger of complement activation makes it a biologically coherent precipitant. Clinically, the key lesson is that persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytosis, and worsening kidney injury after apparent control of sepsis should prompt reconsideration of CM-HUS. In such situations, normal routine complement levels should not provide false reassurance, and timely recognition matters because outcomes depend heavily on how quickly complement blockade is started once CM-HUS is recognised.

## Management, Recovery, and Clinical Lessons: From Plasma Exchange to Eculizumab

The patient’s treatment evolved in step with the diagnostic uncertainty of the case. Because she presented after a dog bite with fulminant sepsis, the first priority was to treat presumed Capnocytophaga-associated bacteraemia and stabilize organ failure. Empiric piperacillin/tazobactam was appropriate because it provides broad coverage for dog-bite pathogens, including C. canimorsus, while cultures were pending. Once blood cultures confirmed the organism and her condition stabilized, antibiotics were narrowed to ampicillin and then oral phenoxymethylpenicillin. This de-escalation reflects standard antimicrobial stewardship: initial broad therapy for life-threatening sepsis, followed by targeted treatment once the pathogen is identified and susceptibility is presumed.

Supportive care was equally important. During the early septic phase she required blood product support for coagulopathy, including thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions, consistent with management of disseminated intravascular coagulation. She also received albumin and careful hemodynamic monitoring. As renal failure progressed from oliguria to complete anuria, she developed fluid overload and septic cardiomyopathy, making continuous renal replacement therapy necessary. Her mottled, cyanotic digits were watched closely because they could represent either DIC-related microthrombosis or evolving thrombotic microangiopathy. In this case, the early ischemic changes were initially interpreted in the context of sepsis, which is a common diagnostic pitfall.

A key turning point was the development of isolated haemolysis after the septic picture began to improve. The initial direct antiglobulin test was positive, so autoimmune hemolytic anaemia was considered and high-dose prednisolone was started. This was a reasonable bridge while the team clarified the cause of the falling hemoglobin. However, the lack of hematologic response to corticosteroids, together with persistent thrombocytopenia, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and schistocytes on the blood film, shifted the diagnosis away from autoimmune hemolysis and toward microangiopathic haemolysis. The repeat negative Coombs test supported the interpretation that the first result was likely misleading, possibly related to penicillin exposure or transfusion.

Once thrombotic microangiopathy was suspected, plasma exchange was started before the diagnosis was fully settled. This is the correct emergency approach in an adult with haemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and kidney injury because thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura must be treated immediately and can be fatal without plasma exchange. In TTP, plasma exchange removes anti-ADAMTS13 antibodies and replenishes ADAMTS13, so it is lifesaving. In complement-mediated HUS, however, plasma exchange is only a temporizing measure. It may transiently reduce circulating inflammatory mediators or autoantibodies, but it does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation. In this patient, the return of spontaneous diuresis after plasma exchange was encouraging but did not by itself prove recovery from the disease process.

The decisive treatment was eculizumab. Eculizumab blocks complement component C5, preventing generation of the proinflammatory C5a fragment and formation of the membrane attack complex C5b-9. In complement-mediated HUS, this interrupts the cycle of endothelial injury, platelet activation, and microvascular thrombosis. The benefit is greatest when treatment is started early, before irreversible renal damage accumulates. In this case, eculizumab was introduced only after the team had excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura with ADAMTS13 testing and had ruled out Shiga toxin-producing E. coli by stool PCR. The subsequent haematologic remission and partial renal recovery support the diagnosis and illustrate why complement blockade, rather than continued plasma exchange alone, is the key therapy in CM-HUS.

Starting C5 blockade during or soon after bacteraemia is not straightforward. Terminal complement activity is important for host defense against encapsulated bacteria, especially Neisseria species, and the same principle applies to other encapsulated organisms such as Capnocytophaga. For that reason, the risk of worsening infection had to be balanced against the risk of ongoing thrombotic microangiopathy and irreversible kidney injury. In this case, eculizumab was started after blood cultures had become negative, which reduced the immediate infectious risk. Because vaccination ideally should be completed at least two weeks before complement inhibition, but treatment could not safely be delayed, she received MenACWY and MenB vaccines at the time of induction and was also given prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin. This approach reflects a pragmatic compromise used when urgent complement blockade is needed.

The patient’s clinical response was consistent with effective control of complement-mediated disease. After the second and third plasma exchange sessions, her spontaneous urine output returned, but the more sustained improvement occurred after eculizumab induction. Platelet counts recovered, haemolysis resolved, and kidney function partially improved, although she remained with residual chronic kidney impairment. This pattern is typical of CM-HUS: haematologic recovery may occur relatively quickly once complement activation is blocked, whereas renal recovery is often slower and incomplete if the kidney injury has already been severe.

Long-term follow-up in this case focused on whether complement blockade would need to continue. She was discharged on maintenance eculizumab, with plans to reassess discontinuation if kidney function stabilized and no evidence of hereditary complement abnormalities emerged. Because genetic testing did not identify a pathogenic variant, the working model was infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a clearly inherited complement disorder. That distinction matters prognostically: patients with an identifiable complement defect often have a higher relapse risk and may require longer treatment, whereas a single infection-triggered episode may allow eventual discontinuation with close surveillance. Ongoing follow-up therefore includes renal function monitoring, blood counts, and vigilance for recurrent haemolysis or thrombocytopenia.

The main lesson from this case is that TMA can emerge after the initial septic phase and be easy to miss. When a patient with sepsis develops persistent thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic haemolysis, and worsening kidney injury, the clinician must repeatedly reassess the differential diagnosis rather than attributing all abnormalities to DIC alone. Early exclusion of TTP is critical, but normal C3/C4 levels do not rule out complement-mediated HUS. Prognosis is shaped largely by the degree of kidney injury and the speed with which complement blockade is started. In that sense, the phrase “from dog bite to dialysis” captures both the severity of the initial infection and the importance of recognizing the later, treatable thrombotic microangiopathy that it triggered.
