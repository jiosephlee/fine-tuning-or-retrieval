Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 5: ## Outcome, Malignancy Surveillance, and Practice-Changing Lessons

This patient’s early response illustrated an important principle in dermatomyositis: clinical improvement can begin before every objective marker normalizes, but recovery is often staged. After high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab, his neck swelling improved substantially within the first 3 weeks and his proximal hip flexor strength recovered by 3 weeks after discharge. Creatine kinase normalized shortly thereafter, which was reassuring evidence that the acute muscle injury was coming under control. However, dysphagia lagged behind the biochemical response and required nasogastric feeding for about 2 months. This delay is not surprising, because swallowing dysfunction in dermatomyositis reflects inflammation and weakness of the pharyngeal and upper esophageal musculature, which may recover more slowly than serum muscle enzymes. In practice, a falling CK is helpful, but it does not by itself prove that airway protection and oral intake are safe.

His longitudinal follow-up also shows how immunosuppression is typically de-escalated only after sustained improvement. He remained on a prednisone taper while continuing steroid-sparing treatment with mycophenolate and scheduled IVIG, with rituximab already given as induction therapy. That approach reflects the need to maintain disease suppression until both symptoms and objective measures are stable, then reduce therapy cautiously to avoid relapse. In this case, the persistence of dysphagia, residual skin changes, and the severity of his initial presentation justified ongoing treatment rather than early withdrawal. For clinicians, the lesson is that dermatomyositis management is guided by the whole trajectory—strength, swallowing, CK, skin findings, and pulmonary status—not by one laboratory value alone.

His respiratory follow-up also needs to be interpreted in context. The initial chest CT showed patchy consolidation and nodules that were felt to represent aspiration-related change rather than established fibrotic interstitial lung disease, and the later imaging improved in parallel with swallowing recovery. The mild restrictive pattern on pulmonary function testing likewise improved, supporting the interpretation that part of the abnormality was driven by active myositis, upper airway dysfunction, and aspiration rather than irreversible parenchymal lung disease. This distinction matters because respiratory involvement in dermatomyositis may come from several mechanisms: respiratory muscle weakness, aspiration from dysphagia, or true interstitial lung disease. Serial clinical assessment and repeat imaging are what clarify which process is present.

Although the neck swelling was dramatic, its prognosis was better than its appearance suggested once the underlying inflammatory myopathy was recognized. Oedematous or “pseudoangioedema” presentations of dermatomyositis are uncommon, and they can be mistaken for infection or histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. The key point is that localized swelling does not exclude myositis; in fact, it may be a clue to severe active disease. In anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis, prominent skin involvement and dysphagia are common, and interstitial lung disease and malignancy can also occur. This patient’s favorable recovery likely reflected prompt recognition and aggressive treatment before irreversible weakness or lung injury developed.

A major practice-changing issue in dermatomyositis is cancer surveillance. Dermatomyositis is one of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies most strongly linked to malignancy, especially around the time of diagnosis. The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) approach stratifies patients by risk factors to determine how intensive the screening should be. In this patient, dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia were high-risk features, while male sex and SAE1 positivity added further concern. That combination justified comprehensive baseline evaluation with CT imaging, PET scanning, tumor markers, and planned endoscopic assessment, rather than relying on routine age-appropriate screening alone. Importantly, a negative initial workup does not eliminate risk; surveillance remains necessary because an occult malignancy may declare itself later. Ongoing follow-up should therefore include repeated history and examination, interval laboratory testing, and repeat imaging when indicated by risk profile or new symptoms.

For the bedside clinician, the most useful diagnostic pearl from this case is simple: unexplained localized “angioedema,” especially in the neck, should prompt a search for dermatomyositis. When swelling is nonurticarial, nonpruritic, unexplained by medication exposure, and accompanied by subtle periungual changes, facial erythema, rash, proximal weakness, or dysphagia, the evaluation should move away from a pure angioedema pathway. A creatine kinase level is an efficient next step, and elevated AST, ALT, and LDH can provide additional support for muscle injury. Nailfold inspection, a focused skin examination, myositis-specific antibodies, MRI, and biopsy can then confirm the diagnosis. In other words, when infection, angioedema, and structural ENT causes do not fit, inflammatory myopathy must enter the differential early—because timely treatment can prevent aspiration, airway compromise, and delayed cancer detection.
