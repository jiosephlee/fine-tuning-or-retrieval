Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Dermatomyositis as an Unusual Cause of Neck Swelling

Dermatomyositis is one of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, a group that also includes polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and inclusion body myositis. It is distinguished by its characteristic skin disease and by a microvascular immune injury pattern that preferentially affects muscle. In contrast to polymyositis, which is less clearly defined histologically, dermatomyositis classically shows perifascicular muscle injury, capillary damage, and complement-mediated vascular deposition. This case illustrates that the disease can present in a way that initially looks like an ENT infection or angioedema rather than a primary myopathy.

The underlying pathology of dermatomyositis is thought to be an immune-mediated microangiopathy. Small blood vessels in muscle become damaged by complement activation, leading to perifascicular ischemia and inflammation. This is why muscle biopsy often shows perifascicular necrosis or atrophy and deposition of the membrane attack complex, C5b-9, in endomysial capillaries. The biopsy findings in this patient fit that pattern and support the diagnosis much more strongly than the initial radiology-based concern for deep neck infection or oedema alone.

Clinically, dermatomyositis can involve the skin, proximal muscles, swallowing musculature, lungs, heart, and other systems. The classic skin signs are Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, shawl or V-neck distribution erythema, periungual erythema or telangiectasias, and occasionally holster sign on the lateral thighs. These cutaneous clues may be subtle or incomplete, as in this case, where classic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were absent but facial erythema, periungual change, and thigh rash provided important diagnostic hints. Proximal weakness, especially of the hip flexors and shoulder girdle, remains a key clue to inflammatory myopathy.

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is a recognized serologic subtype. SAE1 refers to the small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1, part of the SUMOylation pathway, which modifies proteins after translation. Patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies often have significant cutaneous disease and may also develop dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. In this case, the antibody result helped unify the presentation of neck swelling, myositis, and swallowing impairment into a single diagnosis. The serology was also useful because it made alternative myositis subtypes, such as antisynthetase syndrome or Mi-2 disease, less likely.

Neck swelling in dermatomyositis is unusual but clinically important because it may resemble angioedema. The swelling is not due to histamine release, mast cell activation, or bradykinin excess; rather, it reflects inflammatory soft-tissue oedema associated with myositis and vascular injury. Because this “pseudoangioedema” can be non-pitting, erythematous, and persistent, it is easily misclassified as idiopathic angioedema or a deep neck space process. That misclassification matters, because standard angioedema treatments do not address the underlying autoimmune inflammation.

Although uncommon, facial or neck oedema is a recognized presentation of dermatomyositis and may be the dominant early feature. Its rarity is precisely why it causes diagnostic delay. In a patient with isolated neck swelling, the absence of urticaria, hypotension, medication triggers, family history, or complement abnormalities should prompt reconsideration of angioedema. In this case, the combination of elevated creatine kinase, proximal weakness, and subtle skin findings made a muscle disease much more plausible than a histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated disorder.

Dysphagia and respiratory involvement are especially important because they signal clinically significant muscle disease and raise the risk of aspiration, airway compromise, and pulmonary complications. Dysphagia may arise from pharyngeal and upper oesophageal muscle weakness, and can lead to aspiration pneumonitis, as occurred here. Respiratory symptoms may also reflect interstitial lung disease or restrictive muscle involvement. For that reason, once dermatomyositis is suspected, it is not enough to identify the diagnosis; clinicians must assess swallowing safety, respiratory function, and overall disease severity promptly.

The major teaching point from this presentation is that unexplained neck swelling should not be assumed to be angioedema when the clinical picture is atypical. Recognizing dermatomyositis early allows timely immunosuppression and supportive care before aspiration, airway compromise, or irreversible organ damage develops. In a case like this, a simple creatine kinase measurement and careful skin examination can shift the diagnosis quickly and prevent dangerous delay.
