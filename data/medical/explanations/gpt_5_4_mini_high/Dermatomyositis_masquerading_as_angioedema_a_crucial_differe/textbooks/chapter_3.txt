Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Clinico-Pathologic Confirmation

This case shows why unexplained neck swelling should not be assumed to be angioedema when there are any accompanying features of muscle disease, rash, or dysphagia. The key pivot in the diagnostic workup was the early request for creatine kinase (CK), because the patient’s presentation was atypical for isolated angioedema: the swelling was persistent, non-tender, associated with proximal hip flexor weakness, and accompanied by subtle skin and nailfold changes. A markedly elevated CK in this context strongly shifts the differential toward inflammatory myopathy, especially dermatomyositis or another immune-mediated myositis. Inflammatory myopathies can also raise AST, ALT, and lactate dehydrogenase, because these enzymes are released from injured skeletal muscle. Recognizing this pattern is important so that “transaminitis” is not automatically attributed to primary hepatobiliary disease without considering muscle as the source.

The normal renal function and negative urine myoglobin were reassuring but did not exclude active myositis. These findings argue against severe rhabdomyolysis with pigment-induced kidney injury, but they do not rule out significant skeletal muscle inflammation or damage. In other words, the absence of myoglobinuria or acute kidney injury lowers concern for a rhabdomyolysis complication, not for the underlying muscle disorder itself.

Because the initial working diagnosis was angioedema, complement testing and C1 esterase studies were appropriate. Normal C3 and C4 levels, together with normal C1 esterase function and quantity, make C1-inhibitor deficiency syndromes much less likely and help move the evaluation away from hereditary or acquired bradykinin-mediated angioedema. In a patient with neck swelling, this is a useful exclusion step, but it does not provide a positive diagnosis; it simply narrows the differential and supports looking for other causes of edema, including inflammatory myopathy.

Serologic testing then provided an important immunologic clue. A high-titer antinuclear antibody supported an autoimmune process, and the myositis panel was decisive because it identified strongly positive anti-SAE1 antibodies while other common myositis antibodies were negative. Myositis-specific antibodies are not required to diagnose dermatomyositis, but they are very useful for phenotyping the disease, supporting the clinical impression, and helping with risk stratification. Anti-SAE1 positivity is a recognized dermatomyositis subtype and is often associated with prominent skin disease, dysphagia, and sometimes interstitial lung disease or malignancy. The negative Mi-2, TIF1-γ, Jo-1, and OJ results were also informative because they reduced support for alternative serologic subsets of myositis. Importantly, the diagnosis still depended on integrating the serology with the clinical picture and tissue findings rather than on the antibody result alone.

Subtle bedside findings mattered greatly in this case. Nailfold capillaroscopy showed microhemorrhages and avascular areas, and examination revealed periungual erythema. These microvascular abnormalities are classic supportive clues in dermatomyositis and reflect the vasculopathic component of the disease. They are easy to overlook, but in a patient with swelling and weakness they can be the difference between suspecting a localized neck problem and recognizing systemic inflammatory myopathy.

Imaging helped confirm that the swelling and weakness reflected diffuse inflammatory muscle disease rather than a focal neck process. MRI of the thighs demonstrated active myositis in multiple muscle groups, which is a useful, noninvasive way to identify inflamed muscle and choose a biopsy site. FDG-PET showed widespread abnormal uptake in skeletal muscle, especially in the head and neck and proximal limb girdles, consistent with metabolically active inflammation. In inflammatory myopathy, PET can show diffuse muscle uptake and is also valuable in the same examination for malignancy assessment, which is important because dermatomyositis can be a paraneoplastic syndrome. In this patient, PET did not reveal metabolically active cancer, which was reassuring but did not eliminate the need for ongoing cancer surveillance.

The dysphagia was evaluated with a videofluoroscopic swallow study, which demonstrated severe pharyngeal phase dysfunction with residue and penetration to the vocal cords. This finding explained the patient’s aspiration risk and the peribronchial consolidation seen on chest CT, which was interpreted as aspiration-related rather than primary interstitial lung disease. Pulmonary function testing showed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, which can be seen when swallowing or upper airway mechanics are affected, and the chest CT lacked the radiologic features of fibrotic interstitial lung disease. The non-cavitating nodules and consolidative changes were therefore considered in the broader inflammatory/aspiration context rather than as evidence of a separate primary pulmonary process.

The final clinico-pathologic confirmation came from muscle biopsy. The biopsy showed perifascicular necrosis and regeneration with scattered perimysial inflammation, and immunohistochemistry demonstrated C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This pattern is highly characteristic of dermatomyositis, reflecting complement-mediated microvascular injury with secondary perifascicular ischemic damage. When seen together with the clinical syndrome, elevated CK, supportive imaging, and anti-SAE1 positivity, the biopsy established the diagnosis with confidence.

Taken together, the data converged on moderate to severe anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis presenting unusually with inflammatory cervical oedema, marked myositis, and severe dysphagia. The case illustrates a practical diagnostic principle: when neck swelling is unexplained and accompanied by weakness, rash, or swallowing difficulty, clinicians should broaden the differential beyond angioedema and infection, check CK early, examine the nailfolds and skin carefully, and pursue muscle-directed imaging and biopsy when the laboratory and clinical clues point toward inflammatory myopathy.
