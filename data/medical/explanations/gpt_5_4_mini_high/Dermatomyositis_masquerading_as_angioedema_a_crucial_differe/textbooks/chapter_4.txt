Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 4: ## Management of Severe Dermatomyositis with Dysphagia and Airway-Adjacent Involvement

This patient’s management was driven by the severity of his presentation rather than by the final antibody result alone. In dermatomyositis, neck swelling can reflect inflammatory edema of the soft tissues and muscles rather than true allergic angioedema, and in this case it was accompanied by marked creatine kinase elevation, proximal myopathy, and, most importantly, severe pharyngeal dysphagia with evidence of aspiration. Those features place the patient at high risk for airway compromise, malnutrition, and aspiration pneumonitis, so treatment needed to be both prompt and aggressive.

High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone was used first because pulse glucocorticoids are the fastest way to suppress active muscle and skin inflammation in severe inflammatory myopathy. The goal is to rapidly reduce edema and inflammatory injury while more slowly acting steroid-sparing therapies take effect. Once the acute phase is controlled, patients are transitioned to oral prednisone and the dose is tapered gradually according to clinical response. In this case, tapering was guided by improvement in neck swelling, recovery of proximal strength, normalization of creatine kinase, and—critically—improving swallowing function. A slow taper is important because disease control often lags behind the initial clinical improvement, and overly rapid dose reduction can lead to relapse.

Because this was a severe presentation with dysphagia and extensive myositis, intravenous immunoglobulin was added early. IVIG is commonly used in dermatomyositis when there is significant bulbar involvement, refractory disease, or a need for a relatively rapid adjunctive effect. It can be particularly useful when swallowing is impaired, because it may help control inflammation while avoiding immediate further escalation of glucocorticoid exposure. In this patient, IVIG was given as loading therapy and then continued at regular intervals, reflecting the need for ongoing disease suppression.

Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen as a steroid-sparing agent to maintain control once the initial inflammatory burst had been treated. It is widely used in dermatomyositis because it is generally well tolerated and effective for muscle and systemic involvement, and it can help reduce dependence on prolonged high-dose prednisone. In this case, it also suited the need for a practical long-term oral regimen. Rituximab was added because the disease was severe and multisystemic, with significant edema, dysphagia, and muscle involvement. Although evidence is less robust than for glucocorticoids and some conventional steroid-sparing agents, B-cell–directed therapy is often considered in difficult or refractory inflammatory myopathy, especially when clinicians want to intensify treatment without relying solely on prolonged steroid exposure.

Methotrexate was avoided because the patient had abnormal pulmonary assessment, including a restrictive pattern and CT findings that required careful interpretation in the context of aspiration-related changes and possible lung involvement. Since methotrexate can be associated with pulmonary toxicity, it was not the most attractive option here. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were reasonable alternatives, but they were not selected because mycophenolate was already well tolerated and provided a suitable steroid-sparing strategy. This illustrates a common principle in dermatomyositis: there is often more than one acceptable maintenance agent, and the choice depends on comorbidities, organ involvement, toxicity concerns, and practicality.

Supportive care was equally important. The patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration risk, so nasogastric feeding was needed to maintain nutrition and allow medications to be administered safely. Swallow assessment by speech and language therapy and repeat swallowing studies were central to deciding when oral intake could resume. Because aspiration pneumonitis had already occurred radiologically, airway protection and feeding strategy were not secondary issues; they were part of the core management of the myositis itself.

Before intensive immunosuppression, a safety screen was performed to reduce the risk of reactivating latent infection or worsening an unrecognized infection. This included hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, varicella zoster, cytomegalovirus, and tuberculosis assessment. The indeterminate QuantiFERON result was handled in context with sputum testing for mycobacteria, which was negative. Such screening is standard before combining high-dose steroids with other immunosuppressive agents and IVIG, because the cumulative infection risk rises substantially.

Given the degree of immunosuppression, Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis was also appropriate. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is a common first-line agent, but this patient developed hemolytic anemia despite no G6PD deficiency, so prophylaxis was changed to nebulized pentamidine. This is a useful reminder that prophylaxis must be individualized, and adverse effects can limit the preferred agent.

Overall, the case shows why severe dermatomyositis requires coordinated multidisciplinary care. Emergency physicians, ENT surgeons, immunologists or rheumatologists, radiologists, pathologists, respiratory physicians, and allied health teams all contributed to diagnosis, airway and swallowing assessment, immunosuppressive treatment, and follow-up. In a presentation that initially resembled angioedema or deep neck infection, recognizing the inflammatory myopathy pattern allowed timely therapy and likely prevented further respiratory and bulbar complications.
