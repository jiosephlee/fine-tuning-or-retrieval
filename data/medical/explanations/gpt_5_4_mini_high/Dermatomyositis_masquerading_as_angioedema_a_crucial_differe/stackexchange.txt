Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient presenting with progressive neck swelling, what bedside clues should prompt you to order a creatine kinase level and consider dermatomyositis rather than treating presumed angioedema alone?

When neck swelling is **progressive over days to weeks** and does **not look like classic angioedema**, you should broaden the differential and check a **creatine kinase** promptly if there are any clues to inflammatory myopathy.

The bedside clues in this case were:

- **Subacute, persistent swelling** rather than abrupt episodic edema  
- **Non-tender, erythematous neck swelling** without urticaria or pruritus  
- **No typical angioedema trigger**: no ACE inhibitor, NSAID, DPP-4 inhibitor use, and no personal or family history of angioedema  
- **Associated muscle symptoms**: proximal hip flexor weakness and mild myalgia  
- **Bulbar symptoms**: dysphagia and coughing with nasendoscopy  
- **Subtle dermatomyositis skin/nail findings** on careful exam: facial erythema, periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages/capillary changes, and a rash on the thigh  
- **Absence of a convincing infectious or structural cause** after ENT evaluation

The key teaching point is that in dermatomyositis, “angioedema-like” neck swelling can actually reflect **inflammatory edema of the muscles and soft tissues**, not histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. So if the swelling is **atypical, persistent, and accompanied by weakness, dysphagia, rash, or nailfold changes**, order a **CK** and consider dermatomyositis early rather than treating presumed angioedema alone. In this patient, that bedside reasoning led to the diagnosis after CK returned markedly elevated at **16,184 U/L**.

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient had no urticaria, no ACE-inhibitor or NSAID exposure, no personal or family history of angioedema, and normal C4 and quantitative and functional C1 esterase inhibitor testing. How do these findings narrow the differential diagnosis of his neck swelling, and what alternative diagnoses should be prioritized?

These findings make **true angioedema much less likely**, especially the two major mechanistic categories:

1. **Histaminergic angioedema** is less likely because there was **no urticaria, pruritus, or allergic trigger**.
2. **Bradykinin-mediated angioedema** is also unlikely because he had **no ACE-inhibitor or NSAID exposure**, no personal or family history suggestive of hereditary angioedema, and—most importantly—**normal C4 with normal quantitative and functional C1 esterase inhibitor testing**. That profile argues strongly against both **hereditary** and most **acquired C1-INH–deficient** angioedema.

So the differential should shift away from angioedema and toward **mimics of neck swelling**, particularly those that fit the case’s subacute course and associated muscle/skin findings.

In this patient, the alternative diagnosis that should be prioritized is **dermatomyositis with pseudoangioedema**, as illustrated by the case: progressive neck edema, dysphagia, proximal weakness, elevated CK, subtle periungual and facial changes, and subsequent MRI/biopsy confirmation. In other words, the neck swelling was a manifestation of **inflammatory myositis and soft-tissue edema**, not mast-cell or bradykinin angioedema.

Other important mimics to consider, especially early in the workup, are:

- **Deep neck space infection/Ludwig angina**
- **Cellulitis/erysipelas or bacterial sialadenitis**
- **Superior vena cava syndrome**
- **Drug hypersensitivity syndromes** such as DRESS
- Less commonly, **orofacial granulomatosis**, **hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis**, and **neuromuscular disorders** such as myasthenia gravis when dysphagia is prominent

Clinically, the lesson from this case is that **unexplained neck swelling without urticaria and with normal complement/C1-INH studies should prompt a careful search for non-angioedema causes—especially inflammatory myopathy, and dermatomyositis in particular.**

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed pathophysiologic basis of 'pseudoangioedema' in dermatomyositis, and how does it differ mechanistically from histaminergic and bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

In dermatomyositis, “pseudoangioedema” is thought to be **inflammatory soft-tissue edema from active myositis and dermatomyositis-associated microangiopathy**, not true angioedema. The swelling reflects **capillary/endothelial injury with leakage into affected muscle and surrounding tissues**, likely driven by the same complement-mediated vascular injury seen in dermatomyositis; in this case, the markedly elevated CK, MRI/CT evidence of strap-muscle and retropharyngeal edema, and the biopsy showing **perifascicular C5b-9 deposition** all support that mechanism.

This differs from:
- **Histaminergic angioedema**: mast-cell degranulation with histamine release, causing rapid vasodilation and increased permeability, often with urticaria/pruritus and response to antihistamines, epinephrine, and steroids.
- **Bradykinin-mediated angioedema**: excess bradykinin from C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency or ACE-inhibitor effect, producing vascular permeability without urticaria, and typically not responsive to antihistamines or epinephrine.

So, the key distinction is that dermatomyositis-related “pseudoangioedema” is **myositis-driven inflammatory edema**, whereas true angioedema is **mediator-driven vascular leak**.

### [Morning Report] Which combination of laboratory, imaging, and pathology findings in this case most strongly supports the diagnosis of dermatomyositis, and which muscle-biopsy feature helps distinguish dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies?

The combination that most strongly supports dermatomyositis in this case is the **markedly elevated creatine kinase** with accompanying muscle enzyme elevation, the **MRI and FDG-PET evidence of active, multifocal skeletal muscle inflammation**, and the **myositis-serology positivity for anti-SAE1 antibody** in the setting of compatible clinical findings. In other words, the very high CK of 16,184 U/L, MRI showing active myositis in the pelvic and proximal thigh muscles, PET showing diffuse FDG uptake in multiple muscle groups, and a muscle biopsy with a classic dermatomyositis pattern together make the diagnosis highly convincing.

The biopsy feature that best distinguishes dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies is **perifascicular pathology with complement-mediated microangiopathy**—here, **perifascicular myofiber degeneration/atrophy with C5b-9 (membrane attack complex) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries**. That capillary complement deposition is a classic hallmark of dermatomyositis and helps separate it from immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, which is more diffusely necrotizing, and from inclusion body myositis, which has a different pattern of endomysial inflammation and rimmed vacuoles.

### [Clinical Pearls] When a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy has markedly elevated AST, ALT, and LDH, how can you determine whether these abnormalities reflect hepatocellular injury or skeletal muscle injury?

In suspected inflammatory myopathy, markedly elevated AST, ALT, and LDH often come from **skeletal muscle rather than the liver**. The key is to look for a **muscle pattern**, not interpret the transaminases in isolation.

The most useful step is to check a **creatine kinase (CK)** level, and if needed aldolase, and to correlate the aminotransferases with the clinical and laboratory picture. In the case report, the patient’s **CK was 16,184 U/L** with **AST predominance** (AST 396, ALT 143) and elevated LDH, which strongly supported **active myositis**. His MRI and muscle biopsy then confirmed inflammatory muscle disease. In myositis, AST and ALT can rise because these enzymes are present in muscle; AST is particularly nonspecific.

Features that favor **skeletal muscle injury** are:
- **Markedly elevated CK**
- AST often greater than ALT
- Normal or near-normal **bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, GGT, and INR** if assessed
- Improvement of AST/ALT as the **CK and myositis improve**

Features that favor **true hepatocellular injury** are:
- Disproportionate abnormalities in **bilirubin, GGT, INR**, or alkaline phosphatase
- A hepatic risk factor or a pattern inconsistent with active myositis
- Persistent transaminitis despite resolution of muscle inflammation

So, in inflammatory myopathy, the practical teaching point is: **don’t assume liver disease from AST/ALT alone; check CK and liver-specific tests, and follow the trend.**

### [Pathophysiology] What clinical phenotype is associated with anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, and which complications or associated conditions are especially important to anticipate in these patients?

Anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis most often has a **cutaneous-predominant phenotype**: patients commonly develop the typical dermatomyositis rash, including **heliotrope eruption, Gottron’s papules, V-neck/shawl sign, facial erythema, and periungual/nailfold changes**. In this case, the disease presented atypically with **marked inflammatory neck swelling (“pseudoangioedema”)** and only subtle early weakness, but the underlying process was still classic dermatomyositis with severe myositis and dysphagia.

The key complications and associated conditions to anticipate are **bulbar dysfunction and aspiration**, **interstitial lung disease**, and **malignancy**. In the review cited in the report, **dysphagia occurred in about 46%**, **interstitial lung disease in 43%**, and **malignancy in 18%** of SAE1-positive cases. Thus, these patients warrant close attention to swallowing/airway safety, pulmonary evaluation, and **systematic cancer screening with ongoing surveillance**.

### [Management] How would you acutely manage dermatomyositis presenting with marked cervical swelling, severe pharyngeal dysphagia, and concern for airway muscle involvement?

Acute management is to treat this as **severe dermatomyositis with potential airway threat**, not as histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

First, the patient should be **admitted to a monitored setting** with **urgent ENT/anesthesia involvement**. Flexible nasendoscopy is appropriate to assess the upper airway, but if there is **stridor, drooling, hypoxemia, rapidly progressive swelling, or inability to handle secretions**, the airway should be secured early rather than waiting for further evolution. In this case, the endoscopy showed no fixed obstruction, but there was substantial secretion burden and coughing, consistent with bulbar involvement.

Second, because the dysphagia is severe, the patient should be made **NPO** and protected from aspiration. If swallowing is unsafe, provide **enteral nutrition via nasogastric tube**; that was necessary in this case, and it is often the safest bridge while inflammation improves.

Third, the key disease-directed therapy is **prompt high-dose glucocorticoids**. For severe myositis with cervical and pharyngeal involvement, this case appropriately used **intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days**, then a **high-dose oral prednisone taper**.

Fourth, in marked bulbar disease or when a rapid response is needed, I would add **early IVIG**; in this case, **2 g/kg** was given over 5 days, with subsequent monthly cycles. That is a reasonable choice when dysphagia and airway muscle involvement are prominent.

Finally, once the patient is stabilized, add a **steroid-sparing immunosuppressant** such as **mycophenolate mofetil**; rituximab is reasonable for severe or refractory disease, as was done here. Supportive measures should include aspiration precautions, PJP prophylaxis if prolonged high-dose immunosuppression is planned, and malignancy screening.

The important pearl from this case is that **marked neck swelling in dermatomyositis can be inflammatory pseudoangioedema from myositis**, and the treatment is **rapid immunosuppression plus airway and swallowing support**, not antihistamines alone.

### [Management] In a patient with dermatomyositis and severe pharyngeal dysphagia, what complications should be actively evaluated for, what diagnostic studies are useful, and what supportive measures should be instituted while immunosuppressive therapy takes effect?

In dermatomyositis with **severe pharyngeal dysphagia**, the immediate concern is not simply difficulty eating—it is **unsafe swallowing** with risk of **aspiration**, **aspiration pneumonitis/pneumonia**, **airway compromise from pharyngeal or cervical muscle edema**, and downstream **dehydration and malnutrition**. In the reported case, this was not theoretical: flexible nasendoscopy showed retained secretions and coughing, chest CT suggested aspiration pneumonitis, and videofluoroscopy demonstrated severe pharyngeal residue with penetration to the vocal cords.

The most useful studies are those that directly assess swallow safety and airway anatomy: **ENT examination with flexible nasendoscopy** to exclude structural obstruction and assess secretion handling, and a **videofluoroscopic (barium) swallow study** to define the severity of pharyngeal dysfunction and aspiration risk. **Chest imaging** is important when aspiration is suspected, and in dermatomyositis, **pulmonary function testing** and chest CT help identify concomitant respiratory involvement. In parallel, CK and muscle imaging/biopsy help confirm active myositis, but the swallow studies are what guide immediate feeding decisions.

While immunosuppression takes effect, the patient should be managed with **airway vigilance and aspiration precautions** and should not be allowed to eat orally if swallowing is unsafe. **Enteral nutrition via nasogastric tube** is appropriate when dysphagia is severe, and medications can be given through the tube as needed; this was required in the case for several weeks. Early involvement of **speech-language pathology, nutrition, and ENT** is essential. The key principle is that dysphagia in dermatomyositis is a marker of severe disease and must be bridged with supportive feeding and aspiration prevention until the inflammatory myopathy responds to treatment.

### [Management] This patient was considered high risk for occult malignancy. Which features placed him in a high-risk cancer-screening group, and what baseline and interval malignancy evaluation is appropriate in dermatomyositis of this type?

He was considered **high risk for occult cancer because his dermatomyositis was accompanied by dysphagia**, which the IMACS guideline treats as a **high-risk feature**. In addition, he had **anti-SAE1 positivity** and **male sex**, both of which are **intermediate-risk features**; taken together with dermatomyositis itself, this moved him into the high-risk screening group. In the case report, this is exactly how the team framed his risk.

For **baseline malignancy evaluation** in this form of dermatomyositis, the appropriate approach is **enhanced screening at diagnosis**: careful history and physical examination, routine laboratory studies, and **age- and sex-appropriate cancer screening**, with **cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis**; in higher-risk patients, **FDG-PET/CT** is reasonable if conventional evaluation is unrevealing or suspicion remains high. The workup in the case—CT imaging, tumor markers, PET scan, and planned gastroscopy/colonoscopy—fits this strategy.

If the baseline evaluation is negative, the patient should undergo **interval surveillance for at least 3 years**, typically **annual reassessment** with symptom review, examination, routine blood tests, and repeat imaging or endoscopy **guided by symptoms, prior abnormalities, and age-appropriate screening needs**.
