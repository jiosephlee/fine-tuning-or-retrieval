Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient presenting with progressive neck swelling, what bedside clues should prompt you to order a creatine kinase level and consider dermatomyositis rather than treating presumed angioedema alone?

When neck swelling is **progressive over days to weeks** and does **not look like classic angioedema**, you should broaden the differential and check a **creatine kinase** promptly if there are any clues to inflammatory myopathy.

The bedside clues in this case were:

- **Subacute, persistent swelling** rather than abrupt episodic edema  
- **Non-tender, erythematous neck swelling** without urticaria or pruritus  
- **No typical angioedema trigger**: no ACE inhibitor, NSAID, DPP-4 inhibitor use, and no personal or family history of angioedema  
- **Associated muscle symptoms**: proximal hip flexor weakness and mild myalgia  
- **Bulbar symptoms**: dysphagia and coughing with nasendoscopy  
- **Subtle dermatomyositis skin/nail findings** on careful exam: facial erythema, periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages/capillary changes, and a rash on the thigh  
- **Absence of a convincing infectious or structural cause** after ENT evaluation

The key teaching point is that in dermatomyositis, “angioedema-like” neck swelling can actually reflect **inflammatory edema of the muscles and soft tissues**, not histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. So if the swelling is **atypical, persistent, and accompanied by weakness, dysphagia, rash, or nailfold changes**, order a **CK** and consider dermatomyositis early rather than treating presumed angioedema alone. In this patient, that bedside reasoning led to the diagnosis after CK returned markedly elevated at **16,184 U/L**.
