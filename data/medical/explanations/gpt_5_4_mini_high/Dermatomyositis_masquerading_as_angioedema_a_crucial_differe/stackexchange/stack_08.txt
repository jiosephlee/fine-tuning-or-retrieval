Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Management] In a patient with dermatomyositis and severe pharyngeal dysphagia, what complications should be actively evaluated for, what diagnostic studies are useful, and what supportive measures should be instituted while immunosuppressive therapy takes effect?

In dermatomyositis with **severe pharyngeal dysphagia**, the immediate concern is not simply difficulty eating—it is **unsafe swallowing** with risk of **aspiration**, **aspiration pneumonitis/pneumonia**, **airway compromise from pharyngeal or cervical muscle edema**, and downstream **dehydration and malnutrition**. In the reported case, this was not theoretical: flexible nasendoscopy showed retained secretions and coughing, chest CT suggested aspiration pneumonitis, and videofluoroscopy demonstrated severe pharyngeal residue with penetration to the vocal cords.

The most useful studies are those that directly assess swallow safety and airway anatomy: **ENT examination with flexible nasendoscopy** to exclude structural obstruction and assess secretion handling, and a **videofluoroscopic (barium) swallow study** to define the severity of pharyngeal dysfunction and aspiration risk. **Chest imaging** is important when aspiration is suspected, and in dermatomyositis, **pulmonary function testing** and chest CT help identify concomitant respiratory involvement. In parallel, CK and muscle imaging/biopsy help confirm active myositis, but the swallow studies are what guide immediate feeding decisions.

While immunosuppression takes effect, the patient should be managed with **airway vigilance and aspiration precautions** and should not be allowed to eat orally if swallowing is unsafe. **Enteral nutrition via nasogastric tube** is appropriate when dysphagia is severe, and medications can be given through the tube as needed; this was required in the case for several weeks. Early involvement of **speech-language pathology, nutrition, and ENT** is essential. The key principle is that dysphagia in dermatomyositis is a marker of severe disease and must be bridged with supportive feeding and aspiration prevention until the inflammatory myopathy responds to treatment.
