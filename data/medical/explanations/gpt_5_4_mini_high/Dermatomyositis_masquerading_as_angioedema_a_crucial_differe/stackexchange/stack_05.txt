Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] When a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy has markedly elevated AST, ALT, and LDH, how can you determine whether these abnormalities reflect hepatocellular injury or skeletal muscle injury?

In suspected inflammatory myopathy, markedly elevated AST, ALT, and LDH often come from **skeletal muscle rather than the liver**. The key is to look for a **muscle pattern**, not interpret the transaminases in isolation.

The most useful step is to check a **creatine kinase (CK)** level, and if needed aldolase, and to correlate the aminotransferases with the clinical and laboratory picture. In the case report, the patient’s **CK was 16,184 U/L** with **AST predominance** (AST 396, ALT 143) and elevated LDH, which strongly supported **active myositis**. His MRI and muscle biopsy then confirmed inflammatory muscle disease. In myositis, AST and ALT can rise because these enzymes are present in muscle; AST is particularly nonspecific.

Features that favor **skeletal muscle injury** are:
- **Markedly elevated CK**
- AST often greater than ALT
- Normal or near-normal **bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, GGT, and INR** if assessed
- Improvement of AST/ALT as the **CK and myositis improve**

Features that favor **true hepatocellular injury** are:
- Disproportionate abnormalities in **bilirubin, GGT, INR**, or alkaline phosphatase
- A hepatic risk factor or a pattern inconsistent with active myositis
- Persistent transaminitis despite resolution of muscle inflammation

So, in inflammatory myopathy, the practical teaching point is: **don’t assume liver disease from AST/ALT alone; check CK and liver-specific tests, and follow the trend.**
