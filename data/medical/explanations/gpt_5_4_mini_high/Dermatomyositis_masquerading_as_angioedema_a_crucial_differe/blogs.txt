Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When Angioedema Isn’t: Dermatomyositis in the Differential of Neck Swelling

## The twist in this case

A swollen neck in the ED usually sends everyone down one of a few well-worn paths: deep neck space infection, Ludwig’s angina, or angioedema. That’s exactly what happened here. But this patient’s “angioedema” turned out to be something much less common — and much easier to miss: dermatomyositis with pseudoangioedema.

That’s the clinical trap. Inflammatory myopathy can cause prominent cervical soft tissue edema that looks like angioedema, yet the underlying problem is not histamine, not bradykinin, and not a primary ENT process. It’s muscle inflammation, with soft tissue swelling as the loudest symptom.

## Why this presentation is easy to misread

This man in his 40s presented with a 3-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal hip flexor weakness with mild myalgia. Initial CT suggested diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space, so Ludwig’s angina was a reasonable first concern. He was transferred for ENT review, and flexible nasendoscopy didn’t show a structural explanation.

At that point, the working diagnosis shifted toward idiopathic angioedema. But the story didn’t fit cleanly:

- he was afebrile and oxygenating well
- there was no ACE inhibitor, DPP-4 inhibitor, NSAID, or other typical medication trigger
- he had no personal or family history of angioedema
- complement and C1 esterase studies were normal
- subtle skin and nailfold findings were present
- proximal weakness and dysphagia were already there, if you looked for them

That’s the key lesson: **when the “angioedema” history is odd, don’t stop at the airway.** Look for systemic disease.

## The red flags that should make you pause

Dermatomyositis doesn’t always come wrapped in classic Gottron’s papules and a heliotrope rash. In this case, those hallmark findings were absent. Instead, there was:

- facial erythema including the nasolabial folds
- an erythematous, swollen, non-tender neck
- periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy
- a right lateral thigh rash
- reduced hip flexor power
- dysphagia with aspiration risk

Then the lab that changed everything came back: **CK 16,184 U/L**.

That is not angioedema until proven otherwise.

A few other clues matter too:

- AST and ALT can be elevated in myositis and may reflect muscle injury rather than primary hepatic disease
- dysphagia is a major clue to inflammatory myopathy and also a malignancy-risk feature in dermatomyositis
- a normal complement profile helps move you away from hereditary/acquired C1-esterase deficiency, but does not explain the swelling by itself

## Pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis: what’s actually happening?

This is worth separating cleanly from the usual angioedema buckets.

In histaminergic angioedema, you expect allergy-type features and often a more rapid onset. In bradykinin-mediated disease, you think ACE inhibitors, hereditary angioedema, and low/abnormal C4 or C1 esterase testing in the right context.

In dermatomyositis, the swelling is different. It’s inflammatory edema associated with myositis and soft tissue involvement, often in the setting of other clues to autoimmune disease. It can involve the face, neck, and sometimes the periorbital tissues, and may masquerade as ENT pathology or angioedema. In this patient, the neck swelling was prominent enough to dominate the presentation.

The take-home is simple: **neck swelling can be a myositis phenotype.**

## A practical approach when the angioedema story doesn’t fit

For ED, ENT, and acute medicine clinicians, the first job is still airway safety. But once the airway is secure and the structural/infectious causes are not convincing, broaden the differential early.

A practical bedside approach:

1. **Reassess the timeline**
   Subacute progression over days to weeks is less typical for classic allergic angioedema.

2. **Check the usual triggers**
   ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, NSAIDs, prior episodes, family history, urticaria, anaphylaxis features.

3. **Look hard for inflammatory myopathy clues**
   Proximal weakness, dysphagia, myalgia, facial erythema, periungual changes, nailfold abnormalities, rash over the trunk or thigh.

4. **Send a CK early**
   If the swelling is atypical, CK is a cheap, fast discriminator. In this case it was the pivot point.

5. **Don’t be reassured by a “normal-ish” infection picture**
   This patient had a CRP of 55.6 mg/L and a normal WCC. Inflammatory myopathy can still look “hot.”

6. **Think beyond angioedema if complement studies are normal**
   Normal C3/C4 and C1 esterase make bradykinin-mediated disease less likely, but don’t explain the swelling.

7. **Escalate appropriately**
   MRI of muscle, myositis antibody testing, swallow assessment, and biopsy can all be part of confirmation once the diagnosis is suspected.

## What confirmed the diagnosis here?

The diagnosis of dermatomyositis was supported by multiple converging data points:

- markedly elevated CK
- positive ANA with a speckled pattern
- strongly positive **anti-SAE1 antibody**
- MRI showing active myositis in the pelvis and proximal lower limb muscles
- FDG-PET showing diffuse muscular uptake, especially in the head and neck and multiple muscle groups
- muscle biopsy with perifascicular pathology and C5b-9 deposition in capillaries, typical of dermatomyositis

There was also severe pharyngeal dysphagia on swallow study, with aspiration-related changes on chest imaging.

## Why SAE1 matters

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is a recognized subtype, and dysphagia and interstitial lung disease are both important features to watch for. Malignancy risk is also part of the picture, so these patients need thoughtful screening and surveillance.

This case also reminds us that while some dermatomyositis antibodies are more commonly linked to edema, neck swelling itself remains uncommon. So if you see it, it’s easy to miss — but hard to unsee once you’ve seen it.

## Management: why early recognition matters

Once dermatomyositis was recognized, the patient received aggressive immunosuppression:

- IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days
- prednisone taper
- IVIG
- mycophenolate mofetil
- rituximab

He improved substantially: proximal weakness resolved, CK normalized, neck swelling receded, and dysphagia eventually improved enough to remove the nasogastric tube. That matters because untreated dermatomyositis can progress, and dysphagia/respiratory involvement are not benign symptoms.

## Clinical pearls

- **Not all neck swelling is angioedema.**
- **Subacute cervical edema plus proximal weakness should trigger a CK.**
- **Look for subtle skin and nailfold changes even when classic dermatomyositis rash is absent.**
- **Normal complement/C1 esterase helps exclude bradykinin-mediated angioedema, but it doesn’t solve the case.**
- **Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is a major red flag for severity and aspiration risk.**
- **Dermatomyositis can mimic ENT pathology, deep neck infection, and idiopathic angioedema.**

## Take-home points

- If the “angioedema” history is atypical, broaden the differential early to inflammatory myopathy.
- In unexplained neck swelling, especially with weakness or dysphagia, order a **CK** and examine the skin and nailfolds carefully.
- Dermatomyositis can present with **pseudoangioedema** from inflammatory cervical edema rather than mast-cell or bradykinin-mediated swelling.
- Early recognition matters because these patients often need **prompt immunosuppression** and malignancy screening.



# The CK That Changed the Case: Bedside Clues to Occult Inflammatory Myopathy

## When “angioedema” isn’t angioedema

A previously well man in his 40s showed up with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling. That alone is enough to send most of us down the familiar pathways: deep neck space infection, Ludwig’s angina, angioedema, maybe even venous obstruction. In this case, the CT scan reinforced the trap — diffuse neck edema, strap muscle involvement, and retropharyngeal inflammation.

But the diagnosis was not histamine, not bradykinin, and not infection.

It was dermatomyositis.

What makes this case worth pausing over is not just the final diagnosis, but how close it came to being missed. The neck swelling was the headline, but the clues were quietly sitting in the margins: proximal weakness, subtle rash, nailfold abnormalities, and a lab pattern that should make you think muscle before liver.

## The bedside exam was doing the heavy lifting

This patient had more than neck swelling. He also had:

- progressive proximal hip flexor weakness
- mild myalgia
- dysphagia, later proven severe on swallow study
- facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds
- a rash on the right lateral thigh
- periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages/avascular areas
- coughing and increased secretions during nasendoscopy

None of these findings scream “classic dermatomyositis” on their own. He didn’t have Gottron’s papules or a heliotrope rash. That matters, because we can’t wait for the textbook version of a disease to show up before we believe it.

The lesson here is simple: neck swelling plus any hint of myopathy or dermatomyositis-type skin/nail findings should trigger a CK. Early. Not after the angioedema workup has gone nowhere. Not after the “transaminitis” gets attributed to something vague. Early.

In this case, CK came back at 16,184 U/L.

That was the pivot.

## “Transaminitis” can be a muscle signal

The chemistry panel also pointed away from a primary liver process:

- AST 396 U/L
- ALT 143 U/L
- LDH 692 U/L

This is a classic place to misread the room. In inflammatory myopathy, AST and ALT can rise because muscle is injured. AST in particular is not liver-specific, and a disproportionately elevated AST — especially with myalgias, weakness, or a high CK — should prompt you to think muscle first.

A normal creatinine and a negative urine myoglobin do not rescue you from missing severe muscle disease. They’re reassuring, but they don’t rule it out.

In this patient, the “transaminitis” was part of the myositis signal, not a separate hepatology problem.

## Why dermatomyositis can look like swelling from the ED or ENT chair

Dermatomyositis is usually taught as skin + proximal muscle disease, but the phenotype is broader than that. It can involve the lungs, GI tract, heart, and, as this case shows, the soft tissues of the neck in a way that masquerades as angioedema or an ENT infection.

That masquerade is dangerous because it delays the real treatment: immunosuppression.

The patient’s evaluation ultimately showed:

- normal C3/C4 and normal C1 esterase testing
- ANA 1:1280 speckled
- strongly positive anti-SAE1 antibody
- MRI evidence of active myositis
- FDG-PET showing diffuse muscular uptake without an occult malignancy signal
- muscle biopsy with perifascicular injury and C5b-9 deposition, typical of dermatomyositis

The anti-SAE1 result is a useful reminder that antibody subtype can help explain the phenotype. SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is associated with prominent skin disease, dysphagia, and interstitial lung disease in many reported cases, and malignancy screening still matters.

## The practical bedside move: don’t anchor on the swelling

In the ED, “neck swelling” often gets sorted into one of a few buckets:

- infection
- angioedema
- traumatic/iatrogenic edema
- vascular obstruction
- allergic reaction

Dermatomyositis is not usually near the top of that list. It should be when the swelling is atypical:

- non-tender or oddly indurated
- erythematous rather than pale or urticarial
- accompanied by proximal weakness
- associated with dysphagia or coughing with swallowing
- paired with rash, periungual changes, or nailfold capillary abnormalities
- paired with unexplained AST/ALT elevation

This case also highlights the value of actually looking at the nailfolds and skin. Nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas are not decorative findings — they’re clues to microvascular disease in connective tissue disorders. If you have them in front of you, use them.

## Why this patient improved

Once the diagnosis was made, treatment escalated appropriately: pulse IV methylprednisolone, then prednisone, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. He improved over weeks to months, with CK normalization, recovery of proximal strength, and eventual resolution of the neck swelling and dysphagia.

That part matters because inflammatory myopathy with pharyngeal and neck involvement is not a “watch and wait” diagnosis. Dysphagia raises aspiration risk. Neck and pharyngeal muscle involvement can be clinically significant even when the airway itself is not immediately failing.

## Take-home points

- **Neck swelling is not always angioedema or infection.** Dermatomyositis can present with pseudoangioedema of the face/neck.
- **Proximal weakness + dysphagia + subtle rash/nailfold changes = get a CK early.**
- **AST/ALT elevation may be muscle-derived.** A high AST, especially with a markedly elevated CK, should make you think myositis.
- **Don’t wait for classic Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash.** Dermatomyositis can be subtle at the bedside.
- **If the swelling is atypical, widen the frame.** Early recognition changes management and may prevent aspiration, progression, and delayed immunosuppression.

## Bottom line

This case is a great example of how small bedside findings prevent big diagnostic errors. The neck swelling got everyone’s attention, but the CK — prompted by proximal weakness, nailfold changes, facial erythema, and “transaminitis” — cracked the case open.

When the story doesn’t fit angioedema, check the muscle.



# Swelling, Dysphagia, and Silent Aspiration: Airway and Swallowing Pitfalls in Dermatomyositis

## The case that looked like angioedema — until it didn’t

A previously well man in his 40s arrived with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling. The first pass through the system made perfect sense: CT neck showed diffuse edema in the strap muscles with retropharyngeal inflammatory change, so Ludwig’s angina and deep neck infection were on the table. ENT scope did not show a structural airway lesion. Immunology then inherited a “possible angioedema” problem.

That’s where the case gets interesting. He wasn’t on an ACE inhibitor or other typical culprit drug, had no personal or family history of angioedema, and the neck swelling was non-tender and atypical for histamine- or bradykinin-mediated disease. A careful re-exam found subtle facial erythema, periungual changes, a thigh rash, and hip flexor weakness. Then the creatine kinase came back at 16,184 U/L.

This was not angioedema. It was SAE1-positive dermatomyositis with marked myositis, dysphagia, and inflammatory soft tissue swelling.

## Why dysphagia is the high-stakes symptom

Dermatomyositis is often taught as a skin-and-muscle disease, but the swallowing muscles are where things can get dangerous fast. Dysphagia is more than a nuisance symptom here — it can be the marker of significant pharyngeal involvement, aspiration risk, and impending nutritional failure.

This patient’s course shows the trap: he was saturating well, not tachypnoeic, and not toxic. Yet he had severe pharyngeal dysphagia on video fluoroscopy, with pyriform residue and post-swallow penetration to the vocal cords. CT chest also showed patchy peribronchial consolidation felt to represent aspiration pneumonitis. In other words, the respiratory system was already paying the price, even though the bedside exam looked relatively calm.

In inflammatory myopathy, the problem is often not the larynx itself but the swallowing musculature. A normal ENT scope can reassure you that there isn’t a mass, abscess, or obstructing lesion — but it does not rule out failure of the pharyngeal pump.

## Don’t anchor on “neck swelling”

Neck swelling usually drives us toward infection, angioedema, or venous obstruction. That’s appropriate — until the clinical picture stops fitting.

This case is a good reminder that dermatomyositis can masquerade as a neck-space problem because the “swelling” may be inflammatory edema in muscle and surrounding soft tissue rather than true angioedema. The patient’s neck was swollen, but the clue was that the swelling came with subtle systemic neuromuscular signs:

- proximal weakness
- rash and facial erythema
- periungual nailfold changes
- very high CK
- dysphagia

Those are the breadcrumbs. If you don’t look for them, you’ll keep walking down the wrong pathway.

## Bedside recognition: what should make you suspicious?

You do not need a textbook rash to suspect dermatomyositis. In this case, classic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were absent. Instead, the exam showed more subtle findings: facial erythema including over the nasolabial folds, periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages/avascular areas, and a thigh rash.

The broader lesson: when the neck is swollen and the story is subacute, examine beyond the neck.

Useful bedside clues include:

- proximal muscle weakness, especially hip flexors
- dysphagia or coughing with oral intake
- secretion handling problems
- subtle periungual or facial skin changes
- unexplained elevated AST/ALT with high CK
- aspiration on chest imaging without an obvious infectious source

If the exam and imaging don’t behave like infection or angioedema, think inflammatory myopathy early and send the CK.

## When should you order a swallow study?

Early.

If a patient with suspected dermatomyositis has dysphagia, coughing during eating, secretion burden, aspiration pneumonitis, or unexplained lower respiratory findings, formal swallow assessment is the right move. Bedside swallow screening may miss pharyngeal-phase dysfunction.

In this case, the video fluoroscopic swallow study was decisive. It demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia, marked residue in the pyriform fossae, and penetration to the vocal cords. That is not a “safe for oral intake” pattern.

A key practical point: improvement in limb strength does not guarantee that swallowing has recovered. Swallowing muscles may lag behind. Keep reassessing.

## Airway and nutrition planning: don’t wait for a crisis

Severe dysphagia in dermatomyositis should trigger urgent planning for airway safety and enteral nutrition. This patient required nasogastric feeding for two months and was discharged with medications via NG tube. That was appropriate — trying to “push through” oral feeding would have risked ongoing aspiration.

In real terms, the priorities are:

1. protect the airway from aspiration,
2. maintain nutrition/hydration,
3. treat the underlying inflammatory myopathy fast.

That often means involving speech pathology early, making the patient NPO or restricting diet pending formal assessment, and using enteral feeding when the swallow is unsafe.

The presence of aspiration pneumonitis on CT chest should raise the stakes further. It means the dysphagia is not theoretical — it is already injuring the lungs.

## Why urgent immunosuppression matters

Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is not just a symptom to document; it’s a disease severity marker. This patient received pulse methylprednisolone, then prednisone taper, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. He improved substantially: CK normalized, neck swelling resolved, swallowing recovered, and respiratory findings improved over time.

That response reinforces the point: if the swallowing muscles are inflamed, you need to treat the inflammation, not just the consequences.

Delayed treatment risks:

- ongoing aspiration
- malnutrition
- prolonged tube dependence
- respiratory complications
- further multiorgan involvement

## A note on malignancy risk

This patient was considered high risk for malignancy under IMACS guidance because he had dermatomyositis, dysphagia, SAE1 positivity, and male sex. Workup with CT imaging, PET, tumour markers, and plans for GI endoscopy were reassuring to date.

That matters because in dermatomyositis, especially when the presentation is atypical or severe, you should be thinking about cancer screening from day one.

## The practical takeaway

When a patient presents with “angioedema-like” neck swelling plus dysphagia, do not stop at the airway diagnosis. If the story is subacute, the swelling is non-tender, ENT doesn’t find a structural cause, and there are any hints of weakness or rash, check CK and examine the skin and nails carefully.

## Take-home points

- Dermatomyositis can present with neck swelling that mimics angioedema or deep neck infection.
- Dysphagia is a high-risk manifestation and may reflect pharyngeal muscle involvement, not ENT obstruction.
- A normal nasendoscopy does not exclude severe swallowing dysfunction.
- If dysphagia, secretion burden, or aspiration is present, get formal swallow testing early.
- Severe dysphagia should trigger urgent nutrition planning and prompt immunosuppressive treatment.
- Always look for subtle skin and nailfold clues and send a CK when the presentation doesn’t fit angioedema cleanly.
- Aspiration pneumonitis can be the first clue that swallowing failure is already harming the lungs.



# SAE1-Positive Dermatomyositis: What the Antibody Adds Beyond the Diagnosis

## When a “swollen neck” is not angioedema

A 40-something man walks into the ED with a progressively swollen neck. The instinctive differential is familiar: deep neck space infection, Ludwig’s angina, angioedema, maybe SVC syndrome if the story wanders that way. What made this case so instructive is that the real problem was neither histamine nor bradykinin. It was dermatomyositis — specifically anti-SAE1-positive dermatomyositis — presenting as pseudoangioedema with major muscle involvement and severe dysphagia.

That’s the trap: the airway-adjacent presentation can dominate the thinking, while the key clue is sitting a little lower on the body exam. In this case, the neck swelling was accompanied by proximal hip flexor weakness, subtle skin and nailfold abnormalities, and a very high CK. Once those pieces were connected, the diagnosis moved from “mystery swelling” to inflammatory myopathy requiring urgent treatment.

## Why SAE1 matters

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are one of the myositis-specific antibodies seen in dermatomyositis. Like the better-known anti-MDA5 or anti-TIF1-γ antibodies, SAE1 doesn’t just “confirm” dermatomyositis — it helps define the phenotype.

The anti-SAE1 phenotype has been associated with prominent cutaneous disease, dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and a meaningful malignancy signal. In one large literature review of 215 patients, dysphagia occurred in 46%, interstitial lung disease in 43%, and malignancy in 18%. So while the antibody is not a diagnosis by itself, it absolutely changes how seriously you think about systemic involvement and surveillance.

Edema is the oddball feature here. Subcutaneous swelling, especially of the face or neck, is uncommon in dermatomyositis and can masquerade as angioedema or ENT pathology. That makes this case particularly valuable: it reminds us that “angioedema” that doesn’t behave like angioedema deserves a broader lens.

## The clinical clues were there — just not in textbook form

This patient had 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling plus mild myalgia and proximal weakness. CT showed diffuse neck oedema involving the strap muscles with retropharyngeal inflammatory change, which understandably triggered concern for Ludwig’s angina. But he was afebrile, not toxic, and flexible nasendoscopy didn’t show a structural airway problem.

The pivot came on careful re-examination:

- generalized facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds
- an erythematous, swollen, non-tender neck
- subtle periungual changes on nailfold capillaroscopy, including microhaemorrhages and avascular areas
- a rash on the thigh
- hip flexor weakness
- severe dysphagia on swallow study

That combination should make anyone pause before settling on angioedema. The skin findings weren’t flashy — no classic Gottron’s papules, no heliotrope rash — but they were enough to support the bigger diagnosis when paired with the muscle disease.

One practical pearl: elevated AST and ALT don’t have to be hepatic. In inflammatory myopathy, transaminases can rise with muscle injury, especially when CK is massively elevated. In this case, CK was 16,184 U/L, with AST 396 U/L and ALT 143 U/L. That pattern should push you toward muscle, not away from it.

## What each test contributed

This case is a nice example of how serology, imaging, and histology each earned their place.

The angioedema workup was unrevealing: normal C3, C4, C1 esterase level, and C1 esterase function. That helped move the team away from hereditary or acquired C1-inhibitor deficiency.

The myositis panel then provided the antibody signal: strongly positive anti-SAE1, with other common myositis antibodies negative. The positive ANA at 1:1280 with a speckled pattern supported autoimmunity, but the myositis-specific antibody was what sharpened the phenotype.

MRI of the thighs showed active myositis, and FDG-PET demonstrated diffuse abnormal uptake in multiple muscle groups, especially the head and neck, deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis. Importantly, PET did not reveal a metabolically active malignancy. That matters because the antibody profile and clinical syndrome raised enough concern to justify a serious cancer screen.

Muscle biopsy sealed the diagnosis. The pathology showed perifascicular necrosis/regeneration and perimysial inflammation, with C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — classic dermatomyositis pathology.

That’s the hierarchy worth remembering:

- **Serology** helps define subtype and risk
- **MRI** shows active myositis and can guide biopsy
- **PET** can assess disease extent and screen for occult malignancy
- **Biopsy** confirms the tissue pattern when the diagnosis needs anchoring

## Why the antibody changes management, not just terminology

The biggest value of a myositis-specific antibody is not label-making. It’s risk stratification.

For SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, the signal is toward:

- significant cutaneous disease, sometimes subtle early on
- dysphagia
- interstitial lung disease or restrictive physiology
- malignancy surveillance, especially in higher-risk clinical contexts

This patient had several reasons to be treated as high risk: dermatomyositis itself, dysphagia, male sex, and anti-SAE1 positivity. He underwent CT imaging, PET, tumour markers, and planned endoscopic evaluation. That’s exactly how the antibody informs practice: not by replacing clinical judgment, but by telling you how hard to look and how long to keep looking.

The antibody also nudges you toward urgency. This wasn’t benign neck swelling waiting for outpatient follow-up; it was active inflammatory myopathy with severe pharyngeal dysfunction and aspiration risk.

## Treat the muscle, protect the airway

He received 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily, then prednisone taper, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and later rituximab. Because dysphagia was significant, he required nasogastric feeding for a period, and aspiration pneumonitis was part of the picture.

That treatment strategy reflects the severity of the disease rather than the antibody alone. But the antibody helped justify a more aggressive posture early, particularly in the setting of airway-adjacent swelling and dysphagia.

The response was encouraging: CK normalized, proximal strength recovered, neck swelling resolved, and swallowing improved over time. Respiratory status stabilized as the aspiration-related changes improved.

## The practical lesson

This is the kind of case that should change how you think when the diagnosis seems to be “angioedema” but the exam and labs don’t fit.

If the neck is swollen and the story is off, go back to basics:

- Look for proximal weakness.
- Examine the skin and nailfolds carefully.
- Check CK early.
- Don’t be reassured by absent Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash.
- Think about myositis-specific antibodies when dermatomyositis is on the table.
- Use MRI, PET, and biopsy as complementary tools, not competing ones.

## Take-home points

- **Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is a phenotype-defining antibody**, not just a confirmatory test.
- **Dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and malignancy risk** are key features to keep on your radar.
- **Neck swelling can be pseudoangioedema** from myositis-related soft tissue and muscle inflammation.
- **Textbook rashes may be absent**; subtle periungual and facial findings can be the only clue.
- **CK is a high-yield, low-cost test** in unexplained swelling plus weakness.
- **MRI, PET, and muscle biopsy each add different information** and together can confirm disease and assess extent.
- **Serology should change surveillance and urgency**, not just help you name the disease.



# Treating Severe Dermatomyositis Up Front: Why This Was More Than a Steroid-Only Disease

## The neck swelling that wasn’t angioedema

This case is a good reminder that not every swollen neck is infection, and not every “angioedema” behaves like angioedema. A previously well man in his 40s came in with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling, proximal hip flexor weakness, mild myalgia, and later-recognized dysphagia. The first pass looked like Ludwig’s angina; then the working diagnosis became idiopathic angioedema after ENT found no structural cause.

But the pattern was off. He was afebrile, oxygenating well, not tachypnoeic, and had no ACE inhibitor, DPP-4 inhibitor, NSAID exposure, or angioedema history. On exam there were subtler clues: facial erythema including the nasolabial folds, periungual/nailfold changes, a thigh rash, and reduced hip flexor power. The CK came back at 16,184 U/L.

That is the pivot point. Once you see neck “edema” plus muscle disease, this stops being a swelling problem and becomes an inflammatory myopathy problem.

## Why this wasn’t a steroid-only disease

The real danger here wasn’t just the neck appearance. It was the combination of cervical soft tissue swelling, severe pharyngeal dysphagia, coughing/secretions on nasendoscopy, and evidence of aspiration-related lung disease. Videofluoroscopy showed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue and penetration to the vocal cords. CT chest showed peribronchial consolidation felt to be aspiration pneumonitis. MRI and FDG-PET confirmed active multisite myositis.

In other words: this was severe dermatomyositis with bulbar and neck muscle involvement. In that setting, prednisone alone is usually not the whole answer. Steroids are the starting gun, not the finish line.

The tissue edema here is not histamine-driven or bradykinin-driven; it is part of the inflammatory myopathy itself. That matters because it changes both what you treat and how urgently you treat it.

## Building the regimen: each drug had a job

The team used a layered approach, and that makes sense when the disease is severe and time-sensitive.

He received 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily, then a prednisone taper starting at 60 mg daily. That is a reasonable “rescue” move when you have major muscle involvement and swallowing/airway concern. Oral prednisone alone can be too slow, and this patient already had significant functional compromise.

IVIG was added as well: a loading dose of 2 g/kg over 5 days, then 5 additional cycles 4 weeks apart. In severe myositis with dysphagia, IVIG is attractive because it can be used early as a bridge while longer-acting immunosuppressants take effect. In a patient who is aspirating, losing swallow, and needing nasogastric feeding, waiting weeks for a single agent to work is a risky game.

For steroid-sparing therapy, mycophenolate mofetil was chosen at 1 g twice daily. That choice was practical as much as theoretical: the patient had abnormal pulmonary function tests and there was concern about lung involvement, so methotrexate was avoided because of its association with interstitial lung disease/pulmonary fibrosis concerns. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not used because mycophenolate was tolerated and fit the plan well.

Rituximab was also given, 1 g IV twice, 2 weeks apart. This is where the “evidence is limited” reality of dermatomyositis comes in. There is no neat randomized trial that tells you the exact best combination for anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis with neck swelling and dysphagia. So the regimen is driven by phenotype severity, organ threat, and the need to get ahead of the disease. In this case, the team did not have the luxury of under-treating a patient who was already aspirating and tube-fed.

Anti-SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is also not a “mild skin disease” phenotype. In the literature, it has been associated with dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, and malignancy is not rare. That adds another reason clinicians tend to treat these patients aggressively while malignancy screening proceeds in parallel.

## Don’t forget the boring stuff that prevents bad outcomes

Once you start stacking immunosuppressants, prophylaxis matters. This patient was started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim for Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis, then developed hemolytic anemia despite no G6PD deficiency. He was switched to nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks, which he tolerated.

That’s a useful practical reminder: TMP-SMX is first-line, but alternatives matter when toxicity shows up.

Also, treatment was not happening in a vacuum. He underwent malignancy screening with CT imaging, PET scan, and tumour markers, with plans for upper and lower GI endoscopy. That’s appropriate in dermatomyositis, especially in a high-risk phenotype.

## What happened after the aggressive start?

The payoff for early combination therapy was real. After 3 weeks in hospital, his hip flexor weakness had improved and the neck swelling was much better, though not gone. He still needed nasogastric feeding at discharge because dysphagia persisted.

Three weeks after discharge, strength had fully recovered and CK normalized. By 3 months, the cervical swelling had completely resolved. Dysphagia improved enough for NG removal and soft solids, and prednisone was eventually stopped by 7 months. That’s the kind of trajectory you want in severe dermatomyositis: stabilize early, suppress hard, and then gradually peel back therapy once the disease is under control.

## Take-home points

- Dermatomyositis can masquerade as “angioedema” when neck muscle inflammation causes pseudoedema.
- If neck swelling comes with proximal weakness, dysphagia, nailfold/cutaneous clues, or a high CK, think inflammatory myopathy early.
- Severe bulbar or neck muscle involvement is a reason to escalate beyond steroids alone.
- IVIG is a useful early adjunct in severe dermatomyositis when swallowing/airway compromise makes you want a faster bridge.
- Choose steroid-sparing therapy with the lung and infection context in mind; mycophenolate is often practical when methotrexate is unattractive.
- Don’t forget PJP prophylaxis, and switch agents if toxicity appears.
- In dermatomyositis, treatment and malignancy screening should happen in parallel.



# Cancer Screening in Dermatomyositis: Applying Risk-Stratified Surveillance at the Bedside

## The surprise in this case

At first glance, this looked like an ENT problem: progressive neck swelling, a CT scan with diffuse neck oedema, and a differential that naturally leaned toward deep neck infection or angioedema. But the real teaching point is what happened once the diagnosis shifted to dermatomyositis.

This patient was a previously well man in his 40s with subacute neck swelling, proximal hip flexor weakness, dysphagia, CK 16,184 U/L, subtle nailfold changes, and eventually SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis confirmed on biopsy. That’s the moment the frame changes. Dermatomyositis is not just a skin-and-muscle diagnosis; it is also a malignancy-screening diagnosis.

The key is not to overreact with indiscriminate testing, but to recognize that some dermatomyositis phenotypes deserve a much more serious cancer search than others.

## Why dermatomyositis raises the cancer question

Dermatomyositis has a well-known association with malignancy, but the risk is not uniform across patients. The bedside job is to decide who is in the “look harder” bucket.

In this case, the patient had multiple features that pushed him into a higher-risk lane:

- Dermatomyositis itself
- Dysphagia
- Male sex
- SAE1 antibody positivity

Per the case report’s IMACS-style risk framing, he had two high-risk factors and two intermediate-risk factors, which placed him in the high-risk category for malignancy screening. That matters because the screening strategy should match the pretest probability.

SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is uncommon, and the literature in this report cited malignancy in 18% of patients in a large review. That is not a trivial number. It does not mean every SAE1-positive patient has cancer, but it does mean you should not wave away the diagnosis once the muscle biopsy comes back.

A useful bedside pearl: if the dermatomyositis is the diagnosis, the cancer question should be asked immediately, not weeks later in clinic.

## What “risk-stratified screening” looked like here

This is where the case is practical.

Once the team recognized that the neck swelling was atypical for histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, they expanded the workup. The patient underwent CT imaging of the chest and abdomen, tumour markers, FDG-PET, and was planned for upper and lower GI endoscopy. That is a reasonable high-risk workup: broad enough to look for occult malignancy, but still anchored to clinical risk rather than pure fishing expedition.

A few things are worth stressing.

First, normal tumour markers do not rule out malignancy. In this case, CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, and PSA were normal, but that should be viewed as adjunctive information, not a clean bill of health.

Second, CT and PET are complementary, not interchangeable magic tricks. CT gives you anatomy; PET helps detect metabolically active disease. Neither test is perfect, and neither should replace a careful history, exam, and routine cancer screening.

Third, age-appropriate screening still matters. The patient was scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult GI/colorectal malignancy. That is sensible. Dermatomyositis can coexist with cancers that are not obvious on cross-sectional imaging, and dysphagia makes upper GI evaluation especially reasonable in a high-risk patient.

If you want the FOAM version of the principle: don’t use a PET scan as a substitute for thinking, and don’t use a normal CT as a substitute for follow-up.

## The danger of stopping after a negative screen

One of the biggest cognitive traps in dermatomyositis is treating the first negative cancer workup as the end of the story.

It isn’t.

This patient’s initial malignancy evaluation was reassuring: no metabolically active disease on PET, no clear malignancy on CT, and no constitutional symptoms on follow-up. That is good news, but it does not erase the need for longitudinal surveillance. The case explicitly notes ongoing monitoring with symptoms, examination, blood tests, and serial chest imaging, plus planned endoscopy.

That is the right mindset. Dermatomyositis-associated malignancy can be occult at presentation, and in some patients it emerges later. So the question is not just “What did the first scan show?” It is “What is the surveillance plan over time?”

This is especially important when the myositis is severe, atypical, or refractory. Persistent dysphagia, ongoing weakness, unexplained weight loss, new anemia, or evolving systemic symptoms should all trigger a fresh malignancy review.

## Practical bedside approach

For residents and ED clinicians, the bedside algorithm is simpler than it sounds.

If dermatomyositis is on the table, ask:

- Is this patient high risk by phenotype?
- Are there red flags like dysphagia, older age, male sex, or concerning autoantibodies?
- Have I done age-appropriate screening?
- Do I need CT, PET, or both?
- Is there a plan to re-check if the initial workup is negative?

In this case, the answer to “Do we need a broader cancer search?” was yes. The answer to “Can we stop after one reassuring scan?” was no.

## Take-home points

- Dermatomyositis is a malignancy-associated myopathy until proven otherwise, especially in adults with red-flag features.
- Risk matters: this patient was high risk because of dermatomyositis, dysphagia, male sex, and SAE1 positivity.
- CT and PET are useful in higher-risk patients, but they should complement, not replace, age-appropriate screening and targeted endoscopy.
- Normal tumour markers are not enough to close the book.
- A negative initial screen lowers the probability of cancer, but it does not end surveillance.
- Think of cancer screening in dermatomyositis as a timeline, not a checkbox.
