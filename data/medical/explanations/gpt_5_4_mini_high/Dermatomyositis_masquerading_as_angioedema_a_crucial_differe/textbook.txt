Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Dermatomyositis as an Unusual Cause of Neck Swelling

Dermatomyositis is one of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, a group that also includes polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and inclusion body myositis. It is distinguished by its characteristic skin disease and by a microvascular immune injury pattern that preferentially affects muscle. In contrast to polymyositis, which is less clearly defined histologically, dermatomyositis classically shows perifascicular muscle injury, capillary damage, and complement-mediated vascular deposition. This case illustrates that the disease can present in a way that initially looks like an ENT infection or angioedema rather than a primary myopathy.

The underlying pathology of dermatomyositis is thought to be an immune-mediated microangiopathy. Small blood vessels in muscle become damaged by complement activation, leading to perifascicular ischemia and inflammation. This is why muscle biopsy often shows perifascicular necrosis or atrophy and deposition of the membrane attack complex, C5b-9, in endomysial capillaries. The biopsy findings in this patient fit that pattern and support the diagnosis much more strongly than the initial radiology-based concern for deep neck infection or oedema alone.

Clinically, dermatomyositis can involve the skin, proximal muscles, swallowing musculature, lungs, heart, and other systems. The classic skin signs are Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, shawl or V-neck distribution erythema, periungual erythema or telangiectasias, and occasionally holster sign on the lateral thighs. These cutaneous clues may be subtle or incomplete, as in this case, where classic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were absent but facial erythema, periungual change, and thigh rash provided important diagnostic hints. Proximal weakness, especially of the hip flexors and shoulder girdle, remains a key clue to inflammatory myopathy.

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is a recognized serologic subtype. SAE1 refers to the small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1, part of the SUMOylation pathway, which modifies proteins after translation. Patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies often have significant cutaneous disease and may also develop dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. In this case, the antibody result helped unify the presentation of neck swelling, myositis, and swallowing impairment into a single diagnosis. The serology was also useful because it made alternative myositis subtypes, such as antisynthetase syndrome or Mi-2 disease, less likely.

Neck swelling in dermatomyositis is unusual but clinically important because it may resemble angioedema. The swelling is not due to histamine release, mast cell activation, or bradykinin excess; rather, it reflects inflammatory soft-tissue oedema associated with myositis and vascular injury. Because this “pseudoangioedema” can be non-pitting, erythematous, and persistent, it is easily misclassified as idiopathic angioedema or a deep neck space process. That misclassification matters, because standard angioedema treatments do not address the underlying autoimmune inflammation.

Although uncommon, facial or neck oedema is a recognized presentation of dermatomyositis and may be the dominant early feature. Its rarity is precisely why it causes diagnostic delay. In a patient with isolated neck swelling, the absence of urticaria, hypotension, medication triggers, family history, or complement abnormalities should prompt reconsideration of angioedema. In this case, the combination of elevated creatine kinase, proximal weakness, and subtle skin findings made a muscle disease much more plausible than a histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated disorder.

Dysphagia and respiratory involvement are especially important because they signal clinically significant muscle disease and raise the risk of aspiration, airway compromise, and pulmonary complications. Dysphagia may arise from pharyngeal and upper oesophageal muscle weakness, and can lead to aspiration pneumonitis, as occurred here. Respiratory symptoms may also reflect interstitial lung disease or restrictive muscle involvement. For that reason, once dermatomyositis is suspected, it is not enough to identify the diagnosis; clinicians must assess swallowing safety, respiratory function, and overall disease severity promptly.

The major teaching point from this presentation is that unexplained neck swelling should not be assumed to be angioedema when the clinical picture is atypical. Recognizing dermatomyositis early allows timely immunosuppression and supportive care before aspiration, airway compromise, or irreversible organ damage develops. In a case like this, a simple creatine kinase measurement and careful skin examination can shift the diagnosis quickly and prevent dangerous delay.

## From Emergency Presentation to Differential Diagnosis

This patient first came to attention with a 3-week history of progressive neck swelling, mild myalgia, and proximal weakness in the hip flexors. In a previously well adult, this combination should immediately trigger concern for both airway-threatening disease and systemic inflammatory illness. Even before a firm diagnosis is established, the first priorities are to assess the airway, breathing, and circulation, look for signs of impending obstruction, and exclude deep neck infection or other urgent structural causes. The fact that his swelling was progressive rather than sudden, and that he also described proximal weakness, already made the presentation more complex than a simple isolated neck process.

Ludwig’s angina was a reasonable initial diagnosis because it is a classic emergency cause of submandibular and floor-of-mouth swelling with potential airway compromise. In practice, CT findings of diffuse cervical soft tissue edema and inflammatory change can resemble deep neck space infection, especially when the edema extends toward the retropharyngeal space. In this case, the scan showed diffuse swelling involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal inflammatory change, which appropriately raised concern for a serious infectious process. However, infection was less convincing clinically because he was afebrile, oxygenating well, and not tachypnoeic, and there was no convincing source of sepsis on initial assessment.

Multidisciplinary review was essential. ENT examination with flexible nasendoscopy is a key step in patients with neck swelling because it helps determine whether there is upper-airway compromise, a mucosal lesion, supraglottitis, abscess, or another structural problem that would require urgent intervention. In this patient, nasendoscopy showed no major abnormality in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region. That finding reduced the likelihood of a primary obstructive ENT lesion, although increased secretions and coughing during the procedure suggested possible swallowing dysfunction. In a patient with progressive cervical swelling, that kind of subtle functional clue should prompt reconsideration of diagnoses beyond infection.

Once a deep neck space infection was not clearly identified, angioedema became the next major consideration. Clinically, angioedema is often divided into histamine-mediated and bradykinin-mediated forms, with hereditary and acquired C1-esterase inhibitor deficiency and medication-related causes such as ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, and NSAIDs among the better-known triggers. Classic angioedema usually has a more acute or episodic course, often with identifiable triggers, and isolated swelling is the dominant feature. This patient’s presentation was atypical: his swelling had evolved over weeks, was persistent and non-tender, and there was no personal or family history of angioedema and no relevant drug exposure. Normal complement and C1 esterase studies later further argued against a bradykinin-mediated process.

The broader physical examination was what ultimately shifted the diagnostic thinking. Careful inspection showed facial erythema, subtle periungual erythema and nailfold abnormalities, a rash on the thigh, and reduced hip flexor power. These are exactly the kinds of findings that can be missed if attention remains focused only on the neck. In unexplained neck swelling, especially when the initial label of “angioedema” does not fit, the clinician should deliberately examine the skin, nailfolds, and proximal muscle groups and should obtain a creatine kinase level early. In this case, the markedly elevated CK later confirmed that the process was not isolated head-and-neck edema but part of an inflammatory myopathy.

At this stage, the differential diagnosis needed to broaden beyond angioedema and infection. Cellulitis, erysipelas, and bacterial sialadenitis can all cause neck swelling, as can noninfectious mimics such as DRESS, contact dermatitis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome. Because proximal weakness and dysphagia were emerging features, neuromuscular causes such as myasthenia gravis and other inflammatory myopathies also had to be considered. The key red flags favoring inflammatory myopathy over isolated ENT disease were the subacute course, concurrent proximal weakness, myalgia, subtle dysphagia, and skin/nailfold changes. Together, these clues made dermatomyositis the most coherent unifying diagnosis and explained why the neck swelling was not due to classic histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinico-Pathologic Confirmation

This case shows why unexplained neck swelling should not be assumed to be angioedema when there are any accompanying features of muscle disease, rash, or dysphagia. The key pivot in the diagnostic workup was the early request for creatine kinase (CK), because the patient’s presentation was atypical for isolated angioedema: the swelling was persistent, non-tender, associated with proximal hip flexor weakness, and accompanied by subtle skin and nailfold changes. A markedly elevated CK in this context strongly shifts the differential toward inflammatory myopathy, especially dermatomyositis or another immune-mediated myositis. Inflammatory myopathies can also raise AST, ALT, and lactate dehydrogenase, because these enzymes are released from injured skeletal muscle. Recognizing this pattern is important so that “transaminitis” is not automatically attributed to primary hepatobiliary disease without considering muscle as the source.

The normal renal function and negative urine myoglobin were reassuring but did not exclude active myositis. These findings argue against severe rhabdomyolysis with pigment-induced kidney injury, but they do not rule out significant skeletal muscle inflammation or damage. In other words, the absence of myoglobinuria or acute kidney injury lowers concern for a rhabdomyolysis complication, not for the underlying muscle disorder itself.

Because the initial working diagnosis was angioedema, complement testing and C1 esterase studies were appropriate. Normal C3 and C4 levels, together with normal C1 esterase function and quantity, make C1-inhibitor deficiency syndromes much less likely and help move the evaluation away from hereditary or acquired bradykinin-mediated angioedema. In a patient with neck swelling, this is a useful exclusion step, but it does not provide a positive diagnosis; it simply narrows the differential and supports looking for other causes of edema, including inflammatory myopathy.

Serologic testing then provided an important immunologic clue. A high-titer antinuclear antibody supported an autoimmune process, and the myositis panel was decisive because it identified strongly positive anti-SAE1 antibodies while other common myositis antibodies were negative. Myositis-specific antibodies are not required to diagnose dermatomyositis, but they are very useful for phenotyping the disease, supporting the clinical impression, and helping with risk stratification. Anti-SAE1 positivity is a recognized dermatomyositis subtype and is often associated with prominent skin disease, dysphagia, and sometimes interstitial lung disease or malignancy. The negative Mi-2, TIF1-γ, Jo-1, and OJ results were also informative because they reduced support for alternative serologic subsets of myositis. Importantly, the diagnosis still depended on integrating the serology with the clinical picture and tissue findings rather than on the antibody result alone.

Subtle bedside findings mattered greatly in this case. Nailfold capillaroscopy showed microhemorrhages and avascular areas, and examination revealed periungual erythema. These microvascular abnormalities are classic supportive clues in dermatomyositis and reflect the vasculopathic component of the disease. They are easy to overlook, but in a patient with swelling and weakness they can be the difference between suspecting a localized neck problem and recognizing systemic inflammatory myopathy.

Imaging helped confirm that the swelling and weakness reflected diffuse inflammatory muscle disease rather than a focal neck process. MRI of the thighs demonstrated active myositis in multiple muscle groups, which is a useful, noninvasive way to identify inflamed muscle and choose a biopsy site. FDG-PET showed widespread abnormal uptake in skeletal muscle, especially in the head and neck and proximal limb girdles, consistent with metabolically active inflammation. In inflammatory myopathy, PET can show diffuse muscle uptake and is also valuable in the same examination for malignancy assessment, which is important because dermatomyositis can be a paraneoplastic syndrome. In this patient, PET did not reveal metabolically active cancer, which was reassuring but did not eliminate the need for ongoing cancer surveillance.

The dysphagia was evaluated with a videofluoroscopic swallow study, which demonstrated severe pharyngeal phase dysfunction with residue and penetration to the vocal cords. This finding explained the patient’s aspiration risk and the peribronchial consolidation seen on chest CT, which was interpreted as aspiration-related rather than primary interstitial lung disease. Pulmonary function testing showed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, which can be seen when swallowing or upper airway mechanics are affected, and the chest CT lacked the radiologic features of fibrotic interstitial lung disease. The non-cavitating nodules and consolidative changes were therefore considered in the broader inflammatory/aspiration context rather than as evidence of a separate primary pulmonary process.

The final clinico-pathologic confirmation came from muscle biopsy. The biopsy showed perifascicular necrosis and regeneration with scattered perimysial inflammation, and immunohistochemistry demonstrated C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This pattern is highly characteristic of dermatomyositis, reflecting complement-mediated microvascular injury with secondary perifascicular ischemic damage. When seen together with the clinical syndrome, elevated CK, supportive imaging, and anti-SAE1 positivity, the biopsy established the diagnosis with confidence.

Taken together, the data converged on moderate to severe anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis presenting unusually with inflammatory cervical oedema, marked myositis, and severe dysphagia. The case illustrates a practical diagnostic principle: when neck swelling is unexplained and accompanied by weakness, rash, or swallowing difficulty, clinicians should broaden the differential beyond angioedema and infection, check CK early, examine the nailfolds and skin carefully, and pursue muscle-directed imaging and biopsy when the laboratory and clinical clues point toward inflammatory myopathy.

## Management of Severe Dermatomyositis with Dysphagia and Airway-Adjacent Involvement

This patient’s management was driven by the severity of his presentation rather than by the final antibody result alone. In dermatomyositis, neck swelling can reflect inflammatory edema of the soft tissues and muscles rather than true allergic angioedema, and in this case it was accompanied by marked creatine kinase elevation, proximal myopathy, and, most importantly, severe pharyngeal dysphagia with evidence of aspiration. Those features place the patient at high risk for airway compromise, malnutrition, and aspiration pneumonitis, so treatment needed to be both prompt and aggressive.

High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone was used first because pulse glucocorticoids are the fastest way to suppress active muscle and skin inflammation in severe inflammatory myopathy. The goal is to rapidly reduce edema and inflammatory injury while more slowly acting steroid-sparing therapies take effect. Once the acute phase is controlled, patients are transitioned to oral prednisone and the dose is tapered gradually according to clinical response. In this case, tapering was guided by improvement in neck swelling, recovery of proximal strength, normalization of creatine kinase, and—critically—improving swallowing function. A slow taper is important because disease control often lags behind the initial clinical improvement, and overly rapid dose reduction can lead to relapse.

Because this was a severe presentation with dysphagia and extensive myositis, intravenous immunoglobulin was added early. IVIG is commonly used in dermatomyositis when there is significant bulbar involvement, refractory disease, or a need for a relatively rapid adjunctive effect. It can be particularly useful when swallowing is impaired, because it may help control inflammation while avoiding immediate further escalation of glucocorticoid exposure. In this patient, IVIG was given as loading therapy and then continued at regular intervals, reflecting the need for ongoing disease suppression.

Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen as a steroid-sparing agent to maintain control once the initial inflammatory burst had been treated. It is widely used in dermatomyositis because it is generally well tolerated and effective for muscle and systemic involvement, and it can help reduce dependence on prolonged high-dose prednisone. In this case, it also suited the need for a practical long-term oral regimen. Rituximab was added because the disease was severe and multisystemic, with significant edema, dysphagia, and muscle involvement. Although evidence is less robust than for glucocorticoids and some conventional steroid-sparing agents, B-cell–directed therapy is often considered in difficult or refractory inflammatory myopathy, especially when clinicians want to intensify treatment without relying solely on prolonged steroid exposure.

Methotrexate was avoided because the patient had abnormal pulmonary assessment, including a restrictive pattern and CT findings that required careful interpretation in the context of aspiration-related changes and possible lung involvement. Since methotrexate can be associated with pulmonary toxicity, it was not the most attractive option here. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were reasonable alternatives, but they were not selected because mycophenolate was already well tolerated and provided a suitable steroid-sparing strategy. This illustrates a common principle in dermatomyositis: there is often more than one acceptable maintenance agent, and the choice depends on comorbidities, organ involvement, toxicity concerns, and practicality.

Supportive care was equally important. The patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration risk, so nasogastric feeding was needed to maintain nutrition and allow medications to be administered safely. Swallow assessment by speech and language therapy and repeat swallowing studies were central to deciding when oral intake could resume. Because aspiration pneumonitis had already occurred radiologically, airway protection and feeding strategy were not secondary issues; they were part of the core management of the myositis itself.

Before intensive immunosuppression, a safety screen was performed to reduce the risk of reactivating latent infection or worsening an unrecognized infection. This included hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, varicella zoster, cytomegalovirus, and tuberculosis assessment. The indeterminate QuantiFERON result was handled in context with sputum testing for mycobacteria, which was negative. Such screening is standard before combining high-dose steroids with other immunosuppressive agents and IVIG, because the cumulative infection risk rises substantially.

Given the degree of immunosuppression, Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis was also appropriate. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is a common first-line agent, but this patient developed hemolytic anemia despite no G6PD deficiency, so prophylaxis was changed to nebulized pentamidine. This is a useful reminder that prophylaxis must be individualized, and adverse effects can limit the preferred agent.

Overall, the case shows why severe dermatomyositis requires coordinated multidisciplinary care. Emergency physicians, ENT surgeons, immunologists or rheumatologists, radiologists, pathologists, respiratory physicians, and allied health teams all contributed to diagnosis, airway and swallowing assessment, immunosuppressive treatment, and follow-up. In a presentation that initially resembled angioedema or deep neck infection, recognizing the inflammatory myopathy pattern allowed timely therapy and likely prevented further respiratory and bulbar complications.

## Outcome, Malignancy Surveillance, and Practice-Changing Lessons

This patient’s early response illustrated an important principle in dermatomyositis: clinical improvement can begin before every objective marker normalizes, but recovery is often staged. After high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab, his neck swelling improved substantially within the first 3 weeks and his proximal hip flexor strength recovered by 3 weeks after discharge. Creatine kinase normalized shortly thereafter, which was reassuring evidence that the acute muscle injury was coming under control. However, dysphagia lagged behind the biochemical response and required nasogastric feeding for about 2 months. This delay is not surprising, because swallowing dysfunction in dermatomyositis reflects inflammation and weakness of the pharyngeal and upper esophageal musculature, which may recover more slowly than serum muscle enzymes. In practice, a falling CK is helpful, but it does not by itself prove that airway protection and oral intake are safe.

His longitudinal follow-up also shows how immunosuppression is typically de-escalated only after sustained improvement. He remained on a prednisone taper while continuing steroid-sparing treatment with mycophenolate and scheduled IVIG, with rituximab already given as induction therapy. That approach reflects the need to maintain disease suppression until both symptoms and objective measures are stable, then reduce therapy cautiously to avoid relapse. In this case, the persistence of dysphagia, residual skin changes, and the severity of his initial presentation justified ongoing treatment rather than early withdrawal. For clinicians, the lesson is that dermatomyositis management is guided by the whole trajectory—strength, swallowing, CK, skin findings, and pulmonary status—not by one laboratory value alone.

His respiratory follow-up also needs to be interpreted in context. The initial chest CT showed patchy consolidation and nodules that were felt to represent aspiration-related change rather than established fibrotic interstitial lung disease, and the later imaging improved in parallel with swallowing recovery. The mild restrictive pattern on pulmonary function testing likewise improved, supporting the interpretation that part of the abnormality was driven by active myositis, upper airway dysfunction, and aspiration rather than irreversible parenchymal lung disease. This distinction matters because respiratory involvement in dermatomyositis may come from several mechanisms: respiratory muscle weakness, aspiration from dysphagia, or true interstitial lung disease. Serial clinical assessment and repeat imaging are what clarify which process is present.

Although the neck swelling was dramatic, its prognosis was better than its appearance suggested once the underlying inflammatory myopathy was recognized. Oedematous or “pseudoangioedema” presentations of dermatomyositis are uncommon, and they can be mistaken for infection or histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. The key point is that localized swelling does not exclude myositis; in fact, it may be a clue to severe active disease. In anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis, prominent skin involvement and dysphagia are common, and interstitial lung disease and malignancy can also occur. This patient’s favorable recovery likely reflected prompt recognition and aggressive treatment before irreversible weakness or lung injury developed.

A major practice-changing issue in dermatomyositis is cancer surveillance. Dermatomyositis is one of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies most strongly linked to malignancy, especially around the time of diagnosis. The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) approach stratifies patients by risk factors to determine how intensive the screening should be. In this patient, dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia were high-risk features, while male sex and SAE1 positivity added further concern. That combination justified comprehensive baseline evaluation with CT imaging, PET scanning, tumor markers, and planned endoscopic assessment, rather than relying on routine age-appropriate screening alone. Importantly, a negative initial workup does not eliminate risk; surveillance remains necessary because an occult malignancy may declare itself later. Ongoing follow-up should therefore include repeated history and examination, interval laboratory testing, and repeat imaging when indicated by risk profile or new symptoms.

For the bedside clinician, the most useful diagnostic pearl from this case is simple: unexplained localized “angioedema,” especially in the neck, should prompt a search for dermatomyositis. When swelling is nonurticarial, nonpruritic, unexplained by medication exposure, and accompanied by subtle periungual changes, facial erythema, rash, proximal weakness, or dysphagia, the evaluation should move away from a pure angioedema pathway. A creatine kinase level is an efficient next step, and elevated AST, ALT, and LDH can provide additional support for muscle injury. Nailfold inspection, a focused skin examination, myositis-specific antibodies, MRI, and biopsy can then confirm the diagnosis. In other words, when infection, angioedema, and structural ENT causes do not fit, inflammatory myopathy must enter the differential early—because timely treatment can prevent aspiration, airway compromise, and delayed cancer detection.
