Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Telomere Biology Disorders and the Role of PARN

Telomeres are repetitive DNA-protein structures that cap the ends of chromosomes and prevent them from being recognized as broken DNA. They preserve genomic stability during cell division, but because conventional DNA polymerase cannot fully replicate chromosome ends, telomeres shorten with each round of cell division. When telomeres become critically short, cells enter senescence or undergo apoptosis. This is usually a normal part of replicative aging, but when telomere maintenance is impaired, tissues with high turnover lose their regenerative capacity prematurely and a multisystem “accelerated aging” phenotype can develop.

Telomere length is preserved by telomerase and by several accessory proteins that ensure proper telomere synthesis and maintenance. The core telomerase complex includes TERT, the reverse transcriptase, and TERC, the RNA template. Other genes support telomerase biogenesis, trafficking, processing, and telomere repair. Pathogenic variants in these genes cause telomere biology disorders (TBDs), a group of inherited syndromes characterized by abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres. The clinical consequence is not limited to one organ system; rather, failure of stem cell renewal and repair affects any tissue that relies on ongoing cellular turnover.

The tissues most often involved are those with high proliferative demand, especially bone marrow, mucosa, skin, hair, liver, and lung. This explains why TBDs can present with cytopenias from marrow failure, oral and skin abnormalities, premature graying or alopecia, hepatic fibrosis, and interstitial lung disease. Fibrosis is a common downstream pattern because chronic cell loss and impaired regeneration promote wound-healing responses and scarring. In young adults, the combination of unexplained cytopenias plus pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis should therefore prompt consideration of an inherited telomere disorder, even when the presentation initially resembles nutritional deficiency, immune cytopenia, or isolated organ disease.

Dyskeratosis congenita is the prototype TBD. Classically, it is defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation; in practice, two of these features together with bone marrow failure are highly suggestive. However, many patients do not have the full triad, especially when the disorder presents in adulthood. Severe childhood phenotypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome represent the more severe end of the same biological spectrum. Adult-onset disease may instead appear as organ-predominant pulmonary fibrosis, marrow failure, or hepatic fibrosis, which is why TBDs are often underrecognized outside specialty centers.

PARN is one of the telomere-associated genes with an important but somewhat different role from TERT and TERC. It encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease that mediates deadenylation, a key step in RNA processing and turnover. In telomere biology, PARN is needed for proper maturation of TERC, the RNA component of telomerase. When PARN function is impaired, TERC processing is disrupted, telomerase activity falls, and telomeres shorten over time. The downstream effect is similar to other telomere maintenance defects: reduced stem cell reserve, premature senescence, and organ fibrosis.

The inheritance pattern helps explain the clinical variability. Biallelic PARN variants are associated with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita and severe childhood-onset disease, whereas monoallelic variants more often present in adults with pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. This likely reflects haploinsufficiency, meaning that one normal copy of the gene is not always sufficient to maintain normal telomere function. Penetrance is variable, so some carriers develop disease while others remain minimally affected. De novo variants can also occur, and the same gene may produce different phenotypes depending on the organ most vulnerable and on environmental or hematologic stressors.

This framework is what makes the patient in the case report clinically important. A young adult with pancytopenia, premature graying, hepatic fibrosis, and interstitial lung disease fits the pattern of a telomere biology disorder even without the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. In such patients, the diagnosis is usually established by a combination of clinical suspicion, telomere length assessment when available, and sequencing of telomere-related genes. Recognizing the syndrome matters because it changes prognosis, prompts surveillance for marrow, liver, and lung progression, and guides genetic counseling and transplant planning if organ failure advances.

## Clinical Presentation: Recognizing a Syndromic Multisystem Disorder

The most important clue in this case is not a single abnormal symptom, but the tempo and breadth of the illness. This was a young woman with a 5-year history of gradually progressive fatigue and generalized weakness, followed by a more rapid decline over the preceding 3 months that left her dependent for self-care. In a patient in her late 20s, that pattern should immediately raise concern for something more than routine anemia or nutritional deficiency. The chronicity suggests an underlying systemic process, while the recent acceleration suggests either worsening organ dysfunction or loss of physiologic reserve. When symptoms evolve over years and begin to involve daily function, clinicians should step back and ask whether there is a unifying disorder affecting multiple tissues.

Several symptoms pointed beyond isolated hematologic disease. Exertional dyspnea and intermittent chest discomfort raised concern for cardiopulmonary involvement, while weight loss and anorexia suggested a chronic systemic illness rather than a simple deficiency state. These constitutional features are nonspecific, but in a young adult they become more meaningful when they occur alongside cytopenia and signs of premature aging. The combination should prompt a broader differential that includes inherited marrow failure syndromes, chronic inflammatory disease, occult malignancy, and telomere biology disorders.

The examination added further diagnostic clues. Pallor was expected in the setting of cytopenia, and icterus suggested either hemolysis or ineffective erythropoiesis with increased bilirubin turnover. However, the cutaneous findings were particularly important: premature greying of hair and knuckle hyperpigmentation are classic “premature aging” clues that should make the reader think of a telomere disorder. These findings are subtle and easy to overlook, but they are valuable because they link hematologic abnormalities to a multisystem inherited syndrome rather than an acquired isolated anemia. The bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae, in contrast, fit well with megaloblastic deficiency and helped explain part of the cytopenic picture, especially given the later documentation of severe vitamin B12 deficiency.

The musculoskeletal and neurologic findings broadened the picture further. Thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, recurrent bone pain, fractures, and later electrophysiologic evidence of axonal polyneuropathy suggest that the illness affected more than bone marrow. Some of these findings can be explained by nutritional deficiency and osteoporosis, but their presence alongside premature greying and chronic cytopenia makes a syndromic diagnosis more plausible. In telomere biology disorders, tissues with high proliferative demand are especially vulnerable, so bone marrow, skin, mucosa, and sometimes the skeleton may show early or disproportionate involvement. Neuropathy is not a defining feature of telomere disease, but in this case it contributed to the overall impression of a broad, progressive systemic disorder.

The abdominal and respiratory examination findings were especially important because they signaled visceral organ involvement. Hepatomegaly, later confirmed as hepatosplenomegaly, and reduced breath sounds at both lung bases suggested hepatic and pulmonary pathology rather than isolated hematologic failure. In a young patient with cytopenia, these findings should raise suspicion for infiltrative, fibrotic, inflammatory, or genetic causes. When they coexist with premature aging features, the clinician should think about a telomere biology disorder, because pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis are recognized extrahematologic manifestations of this group of diseases.

A useful problem representation for this patient is: a young woman with progressive pancytopenia, severe fatigue, premature greying, mucosal changes of megaloblastic deficiency, neuropathic and skeletal manifestations, and evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. This framing is more helpful than labeling her simply as a “pancytopenia case,” because it emphasizes the multisystem pattern that ultimately justified genetic testing. In adults, red flags for inherited marrow failure or telomere disease include cytopenias with low reticulocyte response, premature aging features, unexplained pulmonary or liver fibrosis, osteoporosis or recurrent fractures, and symptoms that are disproportionate to an apparent nutritional deficiency. Importantly, the absence of a family history does not exclude a genetic syndrome, because de novo variants, incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity, and unrecognized disease in relatives are all possible. Likewise, the absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad should not reassure the clinician when the overall phenotype suggests telomere dysfunction.

## Stepwise Diagnostic Workup of Pancytopenia with Hepatic and Pulmonary Fibrosis

The diagnostic approach began with a basic but crucial question: was the pancytopenia due to decreased marrow production, increased peripheral destruction, or ineffective hematopoiesis? The complete blood count showed pancytopenia with a very low reticulocyte count, which immediately argued against an adequate marrow response. In a patient with anemia, a low reticulocyte count indicates either marrow hypoproliferation or ineffective erythropoiesis rather than brisk peripheral loss. This is an important early clue, because it shifts the differential toward nutritional deficiency, marrow failure syndromes, infiltrative disease, or inherited disorders.

The peripheral smear provided the next major clue. Dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils are classic markers of megaloblastic change and ineffective erythropoiesis. Hypersegmented neutrophils are especially helpful because they point to impaired DNA synthesis, most often from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. The dimorphic red cell population reflected abnormal red cell production, while the basophilic stippling and Cabot rings reinforced the impression of disordered erythropoiesis rather than isolated hemolysis or blood loss. Serum studies then confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, providing a proximate explanation for much of the hematologic picture.

The iron profile further supported ineffective erythropoiesis. Elevated serum iron and ferritin are commonly seen when red cell precursors fail to mature normally, because iron is not efficiently incorporated into hemoglobin and may accumulate in the marrow and circulation. Bone marrow examination was therefore valuable both to support this interpretation and to exclude more ominous causes of pancytopenia. The marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, but no leukemia, dysplasia, or infiltrative process, and cytogenetics were normal. This pattern is much more consistent with megaloblastic anemia than with aplastic anemia or myelodysplastic syndrome.

Markers of hemolysis were also abnormal, with elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin. In megaloblastic anemia, these findings often reflect intramedullary destruction of abnormal erythroid precursors, sometimes called ineffective hemolysis, rather than true peripheral hemolysis. The direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG, which can initially raise concern for autoimmune hemolytic anemia. However, a positive DAT must be interpreted in context: autoimmune hemolysis usually produces an appropriate reticulocyte response unless marrow production is also impaired. Here, the markedly low reticulocyte count and marrow findings favored ineffective erythropoiesis as the dominant mechanism, making the DAT result less convincing as the primary explanation for the cytopenias.

Because severe B12 deficiency must be explained, the team evaluated for malabsorption and autoimmune gastritis. Endoscopy was macroscopically normal, but biopsy showed chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cell function, which could impair intrinsic factor production and contribute to B12 deficiency. Even so, the extent of systemic disease was broader than would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone. This is a key clinical lesson: once a reversible deficiency is identified, clinicians should still ask whether it fully accounts for the patient’s age, severity, and extrahematologic features. In this case, the answer was no.

The liver and lung findings were especially important because they pointed away from an isolated nutritional problem and toward a multisystem disorder. Ultrasound confirmed hepatosplenomegaly, while transient elastography showed hepatic steatosis and stiffness compatible with cirrhosis. In a woman in her late 20s, this degree of fibrosis requires evaluation for autoimmune, infectious, metabolic, and genetic causes. Autoimmune serologies were negative, and infectious studies for viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and other common triggers were unrevealing. A positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG reflected prior exposure rather than active disease. Positron emission tomography-CT then helped exclude occult malignancy, granulomatous disease, or other infiltrative pathology while also demonstrating diffuse marrow uptake and bilateral ground-glass and fibrotic lung changes, consistent with interstitial lung disease. Together, these studies documented the multisystem fibrotic phenotype and narrowed the differential away from acquired inflammatory disease.

Additional testing showed broader organ involvement that strengthened the suspicion for a syndromic process. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry confirmed osteoporosis, while vitamin D deficiency, hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia, and secondary hyperparathyroidism explained her bone pain and fracture risk. Electrodiagnostic studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes, adding another layer of unexplained multisystem disease. At this stage, the combination of cytopenias, premature greying, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy in a young adult made an inherited telomere biology disorder much more likely than a purely acquired condition.

That clinical suspicion justified escalation to genetic testing. Clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), a change affecting a highly conserved residue in a functionally important domain. Although the variant was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance under ACMG criteria, several features made it biologically plausible: it was rare, predicted to be damaging by multiple in silico tools, and occurred in a gene already known to cause telomere biology disorders. In suspected telomere biology disorders, telomere length testing by flow-FISH is the functional complement to sequencing. It measures age-adjusted leukocyte telomere length and can provide strong supportive evidence, especially when sequencing identifies a VUS rather than a clearly pathogenic variant. In practice, the diagnosis is strongest when the genotype, telomere length, and clinical phenotype align, as they did here.

This case illustrates the value of stepwise reasoning. The initial hematologic abnormality was largely explained by severe B12 deficiency and ineffective erythropoiesis, but the persistent and disproportionate liver, lung, bone, and neurologic abnormalities prompted a broader search. Recognizing when common diagnoses do not fully fit is essential, because young patients with unexplained multisystem fibrosis and premature ageing features may have an underlying telomere biology disorder even without the classic mucocutaneous signs of dyskeratosis congenita.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning: From Nutritional Deficiency to Telomere Biology Disorder

A young adult with pancytopenia should trigger a broad differential diagnosis, because the mechanism may lie in deficient production, peripheral destruction, sequestration, or marrow infiltration. In this patient, the initial possibilities reasonably included severe nutritional deficiency, primary bone marrow failure, hematologic malignancy, autoimmune cytopenia, chronic infection, hypersplenism, and inherited syndromic marrow failure. The low reticulocyte count immediately suggested impaired marrow output rather than a brisk compensatory response, making ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow failure more likely than isolated peripheral blood loss or hemolysis.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was an appropriate early working diagnosis because it can explain many of the presenting features in a unified way. Megaloblastic anemia classically causes pancytopenia, a low reticulocyte count, hypersegmented neutrophils, and a smear that may show dimorphic red cells, Cabot rings, and basophilic stippling. The patient also had glossitis with papillary atrophy and neuropathy, both well-recognized manifestations of B12 deficiency. Elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin also fit with ineffective erythropoiesis, in which many abnormal precursors die within the marrow before reaching the circulation, producing an “intramedullary hemolysis” picture.

However, nutritional deficiency alone did not fully explain the case. Even after deficiency states were identified, the broader phenotype was unusually severe for simple dietary or malabsorptive B12 deficiency in a woman in her 20s. Premature greying, osteoporosis, persistent cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis suggested a systemic process affecting multiple high-turnover tissues. This mismatch between a treatable deficiency and the extent of organ involvement is an important diagnostic clue: once a common cause has been addressed, persistent multisystem disease should prompt a search for an underlying unifying disorder.

The positive direct antiglobulin test introduced a possible autoimmune explanation, but the overall pattern argued against clinically significant immune hemolysis. A DAT can be positive in settings other than autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and it must be interpreted alongside the reticulocyte response, bilirubin pattern, LDH, peripheral smear, and marrow findings. Here, the low reticulocyte count and marrow evidence of erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective erythropoiesis were more consistent with intramedullary red cell destruction from megaloblastic anemia than with a peripheral immune process. In addition, autoimmune serologies were negative and there was no broader systemic autoimmune phenotype, making connective tissue disease or autoimmune cytopenia less likely.

Bone marrow examination also helped deprioritize malignant or infiltrative causes. The marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia, without dysplasia, infiltrative disease, or cytogenetic abnormality. That profile is not typical of acute leukemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, or marrow infiltration by granulomatous or metastatic disease. Similarly, imaging and PET-CT did not reveal evidence of overt malignancy or granulomatous infiltration. These studies narrowed the field toward a nonmalignant process, but they did not explain the hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, which required a second diagnostic framework.

In a young patient, unexplained hepatic fibrosis and interstitial lung disease should raise concern for inherited systemic disease after common acquired causes have been excluded. Autoimmune hepatitis, chronic viral hepatitis, drug-related injury, metabolic liver disease, and infection were considered and largely ruled out in this case. The combination of lung fibrosis, liver fibrosis, cytopenia, and premature aging features then became highly suggestive of a telomere biology disorder. This is a key cognitive step: telomere disorders are not limited to the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita and may instead present in adults with isolated or predominant bone marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, or cryptogenic liver disease.

Among inherited marrow failure and premature-aging syndromes, dyskeratosis congenita and related telomere biology disorders fit best. Fanconi anemia and other syndromic marrow failure conditions can also present with cytopenia, but they are usually accompanied by congenital anomalies, a more characteristic marrow failure pattern, or specific DNA repair features. In contrast, telomere biology disorders often involve tissues with high proliferative demand, including marrow, lung, liver, skin, and mucosa. Clinical clues in this patient—young age, premature greying, osteoporosis, cytopenia, and fibrotic involvement of both liver and lungs—aligned well with that biology.

The main diagnostic pitfall is that adult-onset telomere disorders may lack the classic mucocutaneous stigmata of dyskeratosis congenita. Absence of nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, or reticular pigmentation should not exclude the diagnosis when the organ pattern is suggestive. In such patients, genetic testing becomes central, but sequence results must be interpreted in clinical context. Here, exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN missense change, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which was classified as a variant of uncertain significance. That label means the sequence change cannot be called definitively pathogenic on laboratory criteria alone; it does not mean it is clinically irrelevant.

Phenotype-genotype correlation is therefore essential. PARN is a telomere-related gene, and monoallelic pathogenic variants have been associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. The reported variant affects a conserved residue in the R3H domain, lies in a critical functional region, is rare in population databases, and is predicted by multiple in silico tools to be deleterious. These features map to ACMG/AMP supportive criteria such as PM1, PM2, PP2, and PP3. While this is still insufficient to upgrade a VUS to a definitive pathogenic classification on its own, the molecular finding meaningfully strengthens the clinical impression when combined with the patient’s multisystem phenotype and exclusion of alternative explanations.

Thus, the final diagnostic formulation is best understood as a probable PARN-associated telomere biology disorder with hematologic vulnerability and hepatic-pulmonary fibrosis, rather than isolated nutritional anemia. In this case, severe vitamin B12 deficiency likely contributed substantially to the cytopenia and neuropathy, but it did not account for the entire syndrome. The broader lesson is that in a young adult with persistent cytopenia plus premature aging signs and fibrotic organ disease, clinicians should look beyond common reversible causes and consider an inherited telomere disorder even when genetic data are not fully definitive.

## Management, Follow-up, and Clinical Implications of PARN-Associated TBD

Management in this case began with correction of reversible contributors to her presentation, because severe vitamin B12 deficiency and folate deficiency were clearly documented and were sufficient to explain much of the ineffective erythropoiesis seen on the blood film and marrow examination. She received intravenous cyanocobalamin followed by a maintenance schedule, along with oral folic acid, nutritional repletion, and correction of electrolyte abnormalities. This step was important diagnostically as well as therapeutically: if cytopenias improve after vitamin replacement, that supports a major nutritional component, but persistence or recurrence of cytopenia after correction should prompt renewed concern for an underlying marrow disorder.

Her anemia was managed supportively with blood transfusion when symptomatic. In telomere biology disorders, transfusions are used for clinical indications rather than to normalize counts, because the underlying problem may be limited marrow reserve rather than isolated deficiency. Serial blood-count monitoring is essential after vitamin replacement, since a patient with a telomere disorder may initially appear to recover but later declare progressive bone marrow failure. In this case, the short-term hematologic response was stabilization rather than full normalization, which is appropriate to follow closely over time.

Bone and mineral abnormalities also required treatment. She had vitamin D deficiency, hypocalcaemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and osteoporosis, all of which can contribute to bone pain, fractures, and further functional decline. Replacement with vitamin D and calcium, together with electrolyte correction, addresses these reversible factors. Physiotherapy is a useful adjunct because weakness in this patient was multifactorial: anemia, neuropathy, deconditioning, and skeletal fragility all likely contributed to impaired mobility and activities of daily living. In young adults with inherited multisystem disease, rehabilitation is not ancillary—it is part of preserving independence.

The key management issue after stabilization is surveillance for progression of telomere-mediated organ disease. Even though this patient’s pancytopenia improved after correction of vitamin deficiencies, the PARN variant and the broader phenotype raise concern for an underlying telomere biology disorder. That means blood counts should be followed longitudinally for evolving marrow failure, and clinicians should remain alert for new macrocytosis, falling reticulocyte counts, transfusion dependence, or recurrent cytopenias that would suggest a progressive marrow phenotype rather than a purely nutritional problem.

Pulmonary follow-up is especially important because PARN-associated telomere disease is well recognized in the spectrum of telomere-related interstitial lung disease and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. In practice, this means periodic clinical review, pulmonary function testing, and interval imaging when indicated, with early referral to pulmonology if dyspnea worsens or lung function declines. The goal is not only diagnosis but also timely planning, because progressive fibrotic lung disease in telomere disorders can advance despite otherwise modest initial symptoms.

The same principle applies to the liver. Her transient elastography and imaging suggested fibrosis/cirrhosis, so ongoing hepatic surveillance is needed to monitor for progression, portal hypertension, ascites, and hepatic decompensation. Because liver disease in telomere disorders may be under-recognized, young adults with otherwise unexplained fibrosis should not be assumed to have only metabolic or inflammatory liver disease once common causes have been excluded. Serial liver assessment is particularly relevant here because hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis can coexist in the same telomere phenotype.

Genetic counseling is a crucial part of management. This patient had a monoallelic PARN missense variant classified as a variant of uncertain significance, so the result does not prove pathogenicity in isolation. However, the clinical context strongly suggested a telomere biology disorder. Counseling therefore has to address uncertainty: family history may be absent because of reduced penetrance, unrecognized disease in relatives, or a possible de novo event. Relatives may still warrant consideration for clinical review or targeted testing if the variant is reclassified in the future, but interpretation should be careful and based on genetics expertise.

If marrow failure becomes progressive, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may be considered in telomere biology disorders, but transplantation is more complex in this setting than in acquired marrow failure. Patients with telomere disease are often more vulnerable to conditioning-related toxicity, pulmonary complications, and hepatic injury, so transplant planning should occur at an experienced center with attention to donor selection, organ assessment, and reduced-intensity approaches when appropriate. For this reason, early diagnosis matters: it allows timely referral before organ dysfunction becomes too advanced for safe intervention.

Disease-modifying therapies remain limited. Androgens such as danazol or nandrolone have been explored in telomere biology disorders and may increase telomere length or improve blood counts in some patients, but the evidence is still limited and long-term benefit and safety are uncertain. These agents are therefore not a substitute for surveillance, supportive care, or transplant planning when indicated. In a patient like this one, they may be discussed only in specialized follow-up and typically in the context of progressive disease.

The short-term outcome in this case was favorable: after vitamin repletion and supportive care, her fatigue improved and blood counts stabilized over the brief follow-up period. That improvement should not be interpreted as resolution of the underlying disorder. Rather, it shows that reversible deficiencies were contributing substantially to the presentation while the telomere disorder likely explains the broader multisystem phenotype. Long-term prognosis depends on whether she develops progressive marrow failure, worsening pulmonary fibrosis, or advancing liver disease, which is why ongoing multidisciplinary follow-up is essential.

For trainees, the main clinical lesson is to think beyond common explanations when a young adult has cytopenia plus premature-aging features or fibrosis in more than one organ system. Severe vitamin deficiency can coexist with an inherited marrow-liver-lung syndrome, and a partial response to repletion should not end the diagnostic workup if the overall phenotype remains disproportionate. In that setting, telomere biology disorders should be part of the differential, and early genetic evaluation can change counseling, surveillance, and transplant planning.
