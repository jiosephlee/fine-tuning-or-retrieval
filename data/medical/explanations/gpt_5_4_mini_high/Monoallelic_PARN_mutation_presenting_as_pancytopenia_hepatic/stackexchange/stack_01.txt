Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] A young woman has pancytopenia, a reticulocyte count of 0.39%, hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings, basophilic stippling, elevated LDH, and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. What hematologic process best explains these findings, and why can it mimic hemolysis?

These findings are best explained by **megaloblastic anemia with ineffective erythropoiesis—often described as intramedullary hemolysis**—most likely from **severe vitamin B12/folate deficiency**.

The key defect is **impaired DNA synthesis** in rapidly dividing marrow precursors, which produces **nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony**. As a result, many abnormal erythroid precursors **die in the marrow before reaching the circulation**, so the patient develops **pancytopenia with a very low reticulocyte count** despite anemia. The smear findings of **hypersegmented neutrophils** and **Cabot rings** support megaloblastic dyserythropoiesis; basophilic stippling can also be seen in disordered erythropoiesis.

It can **mimic hemolysis** because the premature destruction of erythroid precursors releases **LDH** and increases **unconjugated bilirubin**, just as peripheral hemolysis does. The difference is that in true hemolysis the marrow responds with **reticulocytosis**, whereas in megaloblastic anemia the reticulocyte response is **blunted** because erythropoiesis is ineffective. That is exactly the pattern in this case report, where severe B12 deficiency and marrow erythroid hyperplasia explained the “hemolysis-like” laboratory profile.
