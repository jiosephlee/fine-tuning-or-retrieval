Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] A young woman has pancytopenia, a reticulocyte count of 0.39%, hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings, basophilic stippling, elevated LDH, and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. What hematologic process best explains these findings, and why can it mimic hemolysis?

These findings are best explained by **megaloblastic anemia with ineffective erythropoiesis—often described as intramedullary hemolysis**—most likely from **severe vitamin B12/folate deficiency**.

The key defect is **impaired DNA synthesis** in rapidly dividing marrow precursors, which produces **nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony**. As a result, many abnormal erythroid precursors **die in the marrow before reaching the circulation**, so the patient develops **pancytopenia with a very low reticulocyte count** despite anemia. The smear findings of **hypersegmented neutrophils** and **Cabot rings** support megaloblastic dyserythropoiesis; basophilic stippling can also be seen in disordered erythropoiesis.

It can **mimic hemolysis** because the premature destruction of erythroid precursors releases **LDH** and increases **unconjugated bilirubin**, just as peripheral hemolysis does. The difference is that in true hemolysis the marrow responds with **reticulocytosis**, whereas in megaloblastic anemia the reticulocyte response is **blunted** because erythropoiesis is ineffective. That is exactly the pattern in this case report, where severe B12 deficiency and marrow erythroid hyperplasia explained the “hemolysis-like” laboratory profile.

### [Differential Diagnosis] If the direct antiglobulin test is IgG-positive but the indirect antiglobulin test is negative, the reticulocyte response is blunted, and bone marrow shows erythroid hyperplasia without evidence of malignancy or dysplasia, why is autoimmune hemolytic anemia unlikely to be the main cause of this patient’s anemia?

Autoimmune hemolytic anemia is unlikely to be the main driver here because the overall pattern fits **ineffective erythropoiesis**, not **peripheral immune-mediated red cell destruction**.

In warm AIHA, a positive DAT is typically accompanied by a convincing hemolytic picture and a **robust reticulocytosis**, unless the marrow cannot respond. In this patient, however, the **reticulocyte response was blunted**, which argues against hemolysis as the dominant process. The bone marrow showed **erythroid hyperplasia without malignancy or dysplasia**, a classic compensatory but ineffective response seen in **megaloblastic anemia** from severe vitamin B12/folate deficiency. That mechanism produces elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin from **intramedullary destruction of erythroid precursors**, which can mimic hemolysis biochemically but is not immune hemolysis.

So the DAT IgG positivity is best interpreted as an **incidental or nonspecific finding** rather than the explanation for the anemia. In this case report, the anemia is better accounted for by **severe nutritional deficiency with ineffective erythropoiesis**, likely occurring on a background of **telomere biology disorder–related marrow vulnerability**, rather than primary autoimmune hemolysis.

### [Clinical Pearls] In a young adult with persistent cytopenias, premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic fibrosis, and interstitial lung disease, why should a telomere biology disorder be considered even when the classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad is absent?

A telomere biology disorder should be suspected because the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad is **specific but not sensitive**. Many adolescents and adults with telomere disorders never develop the full mucocutaneous phenotype, or they present so subtly that it is overlooked. Instead, the disorder may first appear as **bone marrow failure or persistent cytopenias, premature graying, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, and other features of accelerated aging**—exactly the multisystem pattern in this case.

The pathophysiology explains the pattern: pathogenic variants in telomere-maintenance genes cause **critically short telomeres**, leading to premature senescence of rapidly dividing tissues such as the **bone marrow, lungs, liver, and skin/hair**. Thus, a young adult with unexplained cytopenias plus **interstitial lung disease, hepatic fibrosis, hepatosplenomegaly, osteoporosis/fractures, and premature greying** should prompt consideration of a telomere disorder even if leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation are absent.

This case illustrates that point well: the patient’s persistent cytopenias were not explained by nutritional deficiency alone, and the combination of **early graying, fibrosis of both lung and liver, and bone disease** fit an inherited telomere syndrome. The monoallelic **PARN** variant is particularly relevant, because PARN-associated telomere disorders can present in adults with **pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure**, with variable penetrance and incomplete classic DC features.

In short, **absence of the mucocutaneous triad does not exclude a telomere biology disorder; the multisystem “premature aging” phenotype is often the clue.**

### [Morning Report] What major alternative diagnoses should be systematically excluded in a late-20s patient with pancytopenia, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly, and combined liver and lung abnormalities before diagnosing a telomere biology disorder, and which parts of the evaluation are most useful to rule them out?

In a late-20s patient like this, a telomere biology disorder should be a diagnosis of synthesis, not of first assumption. Before settling on TBD, you should systematically exclude the common acquired mimics: severe nutritional megaloblastic anemia or malabsorption states such as vitamin B12/folate deficiency and pernicious/atrophic gastritis; primary marrow disorders including aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, leukemia, lymphoma, or other infiltrative marrow disease; autoimmune disease and immune cytopenias, including connective-tissue disease and autoimmune hepatitis; chronic infections such as viral hepatitis and HIV, with other systemic infections as clinically indicated; and occult granulomatous or malignant disease that could explain weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly, and pulmonary findings.

The most useful parts of the evaluation are the ones that directly test those alternatives. In this case, the low reticulocyte count and smear, the marked B12/folate deficiency, and the upper endoscopy/duodenal biopsy addressed ineffective erythropoiesis and malabsorption; the bone marrow examination with normal cytogenetics was key to rule out leukemia, MDS, and infiltrative marrow disease; autoimmune serologies and the direct antiglobulin test helped assess immune causes, though a positive DAT alone does not prove autoimmune hemolysis when the marrow response is poor; viral serologies excluded common infectious liver disease; and PET-CT was useful to look for occult malignancy or granulomatous disease. Once those are unrevealing, the combination of premature greying, osteoporosis, pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis should prompt telomere length testing and germline sequencing.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the normal role of the PARN protein in telomerase RNA biology, and how can impaired PARN function lead to premature telomere shortening and a phenotype of pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and bone marrow vulnerability?

PARN is a **poly(A)-specific ribonuclease deadenylase** that has an important role in **telomerase RNA biogenesis**, especially in the maturation of **TERC**, the RNA template component of telomerase. In normal cells, PARN helps **remove aberrant post-transcriptional oligo(A) tails from TERC**, allowing proper 3′-end processing and protecting TERC from degradation. In this way, PARN supports an adequate pool of mature TERC and therefore **normal telomerase activity**.

When PARN function is impaired, **TERC maturation falls and TERC becomes less stable**, so telomerase activity declines. The result is **accelerated telomere shortening** with each cell division. Cells with high turnover or regenerative demand—especially **hematopoietic stem cells, alveolar epithelial cells, and hepatocytes**—are particularly vulnerable. Critically short telomeres activate DNA-damage responses, leading to **cellular senescence or apoptosis**, impaired tissue repair, and ultimately a phenotype of **bone marrow vulnerability/cytopenias, pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis**. That is the unifying biology in this case: the patient’s monoallelic PARN variant fits a **telomere biology disorder**, explaining her premature greying, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and susceptibility of the marrow, even though her pancytopenia was also compounded by severe vitamin B12 deficiency.

### [Morning Report] When a telomere biology disorder is suspected, what diagnostic tests are considered most informative, and how do next-generation sequencing and flow-FISH telomere length testing complement one another?

When a telomere biology disorder is suspected, the two most informative tests are:

1. **Germline next-generation sequencing (NGS)** of telomere-maintenance genes  
   - Typically via a targeted telomere panel, or broader exome/genome sequencing if the phenotype is unclear.  
   - This identifies pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in genes such as **TERT, TERC, DKC1, RTEL1, PARN**, and others.  
   - In the reported case, exome sequencing found a **monoallelic PARN missense variant**, which—when interpreted in the context of her premature greying, cytopenias, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—supported a telomere biology disorder even though the variant was classified as a VUS.

2. **Flow-FISH telomere length testing**  
   - This measures telomere length in specific leukocyte subsets and compares it with **age-matched controls**.  
   - Markedly shortened telomeres, especially below the expected range for age, provide strong functional evidence of a telomere maintenance defect.

These tests **complement each other**:  
- **NGS answers “which gene is altered?”** and is crucial for confirming the molecular cause, inheritance pattern, and genetic counseling.  
- **Flow-FISH answers “is telomere biology actually impaired?”** and is particularly helpful when sequencing reveals a **variant of uncertain significance**, as in this case, or when sequencing is negative but the phenotype is highly suggestive.

In practice, **the combination is most powerful**: a young patient with multisystem disease and very short telomeres on flow-FISH makes a telomere biology disorder much more likely, while NGS can then identify the responsible gene and refine prognosis and family counseling.

### [Clinical Pearls] Clinical exome sequencing identifies a monoallelic PARN missense variant classified as a variant of uncertain significance. What phenotype-genotype correlations and ACMG/AMP-style evidence can still support a working diagnosis of a PARN-associated telomere biology disorder?

A monoallelic **PARN** missense variant can still support a **working diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder** when the clinical picture fits the known heterozygous PARN phenotype. In the case described, the key correlation is **adult-onset, multisystem premature-aging disease**: premature greying, unexplained lung fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, cytopenias or marrow stress, osteoporosis, and the **absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad** of dyskeratosis congenita. That pattern is highly compatible with the recognized spectrum of **monoallelic PARN-related telomere disease**, which more often presents as **pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure**, whereas **biallelic PARN** variants classically cause dyskeratosis congenita/HH syndrome. The fact that other acquired causes were not sufficient to explain the global phenotype strengthens the gene–phenotype match.

Even if the specific variant remains a **VUS**, ACMG/AMP-style supporting evidence can still be persuasive: **PM1** because the change lies in a **critical conserved functional domain** (the R3H domain), **PM2** because it is **absent or extremely rare** in population databases, **PP3** because multiple in silico tools predict a damaging effect, and **PP2** because **PARN is a gene in which missense variation is a known disease mechanism and benign missense variation is uncommon**. If available, **short telomeres by flow-FISH** would be the strongest orthogonal support, with segregation or functional data as additional upgrade evidence. So the correct stance is not “pathogenic by sequence alone,” but rather **probable PARN-associated TBD based on a strong phenotype-genotype correlation**.

### [Management] How does recognizing a telomere biology disorder change management compared with treating vitamin B12 deficiency alone, particularly regarding surveillance for marrow failure and fibrotic organ disease, genetic counseling, and candidacy for hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation or androgen therapy?

Recognizing a telomere biology disorder changes the management fundamentally: you treat the vitamin B12 deficiency, but you do **not** stop there. In this case, the severe B12 deficiency explained the megaloblastic features and likely contributed to the pancytopenia, yet the **disproportionate multisystem phenotype**—premature graying, osteoporosis, neuropathy, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis in a woman in her 20s—signals an underlying defect in telomere maintenance. That diagnosis requires **lifelong surveillance for progressive marrow failure and fibrotic organ disease**, because short telomeres can predispose to recurrent or evolving cytopenias even after nutritional repletion. Practically, that means serial blood counts and reticulocyte monitoring, and continued assessment of the lungs and liver, as was planned in this case.

It also changes family management. Telomere biology disorders have **variable inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and possible de novo disease**, so the patient should receive **genetic counseling** and relatives should be considered for risk assessment and, when appropriate, testing. This is especially important in a case like this one, where a monoallelic **PARN** variant was found and the clinical picture supported a telomere disorder despite the variant being classified as uncertain.

Finally, recognition of a telomere disorder changes candidacy for advanced therapies. **Hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation** is considered when there is **progressive marrow failure or severe hematologic disease**, but it is approached cautiously because standard conditioning can be excessively toxic in patients with short telomeres, and coexisting lung or liver fibrosis increases risk. By contrast, **androgen therapy** may be used in selected patients with telomere-associated marrow failure as a medical strategy to improve counts or bridge to transplant, but the evidence is limited and it does **not** address pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis; in a patient with liver disease, potential hepatotoxicity is a major concern. So, compared with isolated B12 deficiency, a telomere biology disorder mandates **surveillance, counseling, and transplant-aware long-term planning**, not just vitamin replacement.
