Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Young, Fibrotic, and Unexplained: When Liver Plus Lung Disease Should Trigger a Telomere Workup

## The weird part of this case

A woman in her late 20s came in with five years of progressive fatigue, then a sharper decline over three months. On paper, the early workup looked like a hematology/nutrition problem: pancytopenia, a very low reticulocyte count, dimorphic smear, severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, and bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia without malignancy.

But the case stopped being “just” nutritional deficiency pretty quickly.

She also had premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, osteoporosis, neuropathy, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic fibrosis, and fibrotic lung changes on imaging. That combination should make you pause. In a young adult, combined liver + lung fibrosis is not a coincidence until proven otherwise.

This is the kind of patient who can drift through multiple diagnoses before anyone zooms out far enough to see the pattern.

## Why this pattern matters

Telomere biology disorders are the classic “don’t miss the syndrome hiding behind the organ problem” diagnosis. They can present without the textbook dyskeratosis congenita triad, and they often show up in adults as isolated or overlapping:

- bone marrow failure or cytopenias
- idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- subtle premature-ageing features

That matters because the individual pieces are easy to misattribute. Cytopenias get blamed on B12 deficiency. Dyspnea gets worked up as infection or interstitial lung disease. Liver disease gets labeled cryptogenic. And if the patient doesn’t have obvious nail dystrophy, leukoplakia, or striking skin changes, the inherited diagnosis gets missed.

This case is a good reminder that the “aha” moment in telomere syndromes is often not one finding — it’s the combination.

## Telomere biology disorders, without the textbook triad

Telomeres protect chromosome ends and shorten with each cell division. When telomere maintenance fails, high-turnover tissues take the hit first: marrow, skin, mucosa, lung, and liver.

Classically, dyskeratosis congenita is the prototype, but adult phenotypes are much broader. Patients may present with pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, marrow failure, or a mix of all three. The mucocutaneous triad may be absent.

In this case, exome sequencing found a monoallelic PARN missense variant, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. That label matters: a VUS is not proof of causality. But the clinical context is doing a lot of work here. PARN is already linked to telomere-related disease, and monoallelic variants are associated more often with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure phenotypes.

So the genetics didn’t create the diagnosis out of thin air — they helped unify a phenotype that already looked syndromic.

## What should make you think “telomere disorder” at the bedside?

A few clues in this case are worth memorizing.

First, age. A late-20s patient with pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis is unusual enough to demand an inherited or systemic explanation.

Second, the pattern. Lung + liver + marrow involvement is the big clue. Any two of those together, especially in a young person, should raise suspicion.

Third, the small “premature aging” signs. Premature greying is easy to dismiss because it’s nonspecific, but in the right setting it’s a real clue. Add osteoporosis, low body reserve, neuropathy, and chronic cytopenias, and the signal gets stronger.

Fourth, the marrow response. A low reticulocyte count argues against an appropriate marrow response and points toward ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow dysfunction. In this patient, severe B12 deficiency explained a lot of that picture, but not everything.

That’s another useful pearl: nutritional deficiency and telomere biology disorder are not mutually exclusive. One can mask the other.

## The diagnostic trap: explaining away too much

This case had several tempting distractions.

Severe B12 deficiency was real and clinically meaningful. The smear and marrow supported ineffective erythropoiesis. There was even a positive DAT for IgG, which can pull you toward immune-mediated hemolysis or autoimmune disease.

But the rest of the story didn’t fit neatly. The reticulocyte response was low, the marrow wasn’t malignant or dysplastic, autoimmune serologies were negative, and the degree of multisystem disease was out of proportion to vitamin deficiency alone.

That’s the key diagnostic discipline here: treat the obvious abnormality, but keep asking whether it explains the whole phenotype.

It didn’t here.

## How to approach the workup when the pattern shows up

Once you suspect a telomere biology disorder, the workup becomes less about chasing random abnormalities and more about documenting the extent of organ involvement and getting genetics involved early.

In this case, the evaluation included:

- CBC, reticulocyte count, smear
- bone marrow exam and cytogenetics
- nutritional studies
- GI evaluation for malabsorption
- liver imaging and transient elastography
- chest imaging showing fibrotic change
- EMG/NCS for neuropathy
- exome sequencing

The broader principle is simple: if a young patient has unexplained cytopenias plus pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, don’t stop at “cryptogenic.” Think inherited telomere disorder and consider:
- telomere length testing, if available
- a targeted telomere gene panel or exome sequencing
- genetic counseling
- multidisciplinary follow-up with hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, and genetics

## Management implications

There’s no quick fix for telomere biology disorders. Treatment is supportive and organ-specific: correct deficiencies, manage cytopenias, monitor pulmonary and liver disease, and follow patients longitudinally.

That said, making the diagnosis still changes management. It affects:
- surveillance planning
- family counseling
- transplant discussions if marrow failure progresses
- expectations around disease course

In this patient, the immediate management was correction of B12 and folate deficiency, transfusion support, vitamin D and calcium replacement, and physiotherapy. She improved symptomatically, but the telomere diagnosis means she still needs ongoing monitoring for progression of marrow, lung, and liver disease.

## Clinical pearls

- Young adult + pulmonary fibrosis + hepatic fibrosis = think inherited telomere disorder until proven otherwise.
- Absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad does not rule out a telomere biology disorder.
- Premature greying, osteoporosis, and cytopenias are “small clues” that become powerful when they cluster.
- Severe nutritional deficiency can coexist with an underlying genetic syndrome and obscure it.
- A VUS does not automatically diagnose disease, but it can support the clinical picture when the phenotype fits.

## Take-home points

- The biggest clue to a telomere biology disorder is often combined organ fibrosis, not the classic mucocutaneous triad.
- In a young patient, unexplained liver + lung disease should trigger a telomere workup.
- Cytopenias with low reticulocytes and premature-ageing features should make you zoom out beyond nutritional deficiency alone.
- Early recognition matters because it changes surveillance, counseling, and long-term planning.
