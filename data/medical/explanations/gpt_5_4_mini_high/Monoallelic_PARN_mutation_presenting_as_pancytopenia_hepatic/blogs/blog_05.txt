Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Why the Diagnosis Changes Everything: Management Pearls in Telomere Biology Disorders

## The surprising part of this case

A woman in her late 20s came in with what could have been written off as “just” severe nutritional deficiency: fatigue, weakness, pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, and profound vitamin B12/folate deficiency. But the deeper story was bigger than that. She also had premature greying, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, and fibrotic lung disease.

That combination is the red flag.

This is the kind of patient who forces you to stop thinking in single-organ silos. Once telomere biology disorder (TBD) enters the differential, the entire management frame changes — even if the genetic result is not a clean, slam-dunk pathogenic variant. In this case, the patient had a monoallelic PARN missense variant reported as a VUS, but the clinical picture strongly supported a telomere disorder.

That matters because TBD is not just a diagnostic label. It is a roadmap for surveillance, counseling, transplant planning, and long-term risk management.

## Don’t let the B12 deficiency close the case

This patient absolutely did have severe B12 deficiency, and that explained a lot of the hematologic picture: low reticulocytes, ineffective erythropoiesis, hypersegmented neutrophils, elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and marrow erythroid hyperplasia.

But the important teaching point is this: nutritional deficiency can coexist with inherited marrow vulnerability.

In a young adult with:

- cytopenias,
- premature aging features,
- pulmonary fibrosis,
- hepatic fibrosis,
- osteoporosis,
- and a family history that may be absent or unhelpful,

you need to think beyond acquired deficiency states. TBD can be clinically subtle, and classic dyskeratosis congenita findings may be completely absent.

The diagnosis is often missed because the “obvious” explanation is real — just incomplete.

## Why naming a telomere biology disorder changes management

The biggest mistake is assuming that “supportive care only” means the diagnosis doesn’t matter. It matters a lot.

Even when there is no disease-modifying therapy available right now, identifying TBD changes the way you follow the patient and the way you think about future deterioration.

### 1) You stop treating the marrow as the only problem

TBD is a multisystem disease. In this case, the marrow findings were only one part of the story. She also had pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis, and both may progress over time.

That means follow-up is not just about hemoglobin. It is about:

- serial CBCs and reticulocyte counts,
- monitoring for evolving marrow failure,
- surveillance of liver function and fibrosis progression,
- pulmonary follow-up for interstitial lung disease,
- and watching for new organ involvement.

A patient can look “hematologically better” after vitamin replacement and still be quietly progressing in other organs.

### 2) You become more alert to malignancy risk

TBDs carry increased risk of hematologic and solid malignancies. The case report doesn’t give a screening protocol — and there isn’t a simple one-size-fits-all answer — but the practical point is low threshold vigilance.

Unexplained worsening cytopenias, weight loss, constitutional symptoms, new mucosal lesions, or changing organ dysfunction should not be shrugged off.

### 3) You plan for transplant differently

This is one of the most important reasons to recognize TBD early.

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be curative for marrow failure, but telomere disorders are not standard bone marrow failure syndromes. The underlying biology makes patients more fragile, and transplant toxicity can be substantial if the diagnosis is missed.

Why does that matter?

Because if a patient with a short-telomere syndrome is transplanted using routine myeloablative thinking, the lungs, liver, and mucosa may not tolerate it well. Knowing the diagnosis early allows transplant teams to think carefully about:

- timing,
- conditioning intensity,
- organ toxicity risk,
- donor evaluation,
- and whether the patient is even a transplant candidate at that moment.

The big conceptual point: **HCT treats the marrow failure, not the telomere disorder as a whole.**

### 4) You change how you counsel the family

This patient had no obvious family history, but that does not rule out inherited disease. TBDs can show variable penetrance, de novo variants, and subtle manifestations in relatives.

That means genetic counseling is not an optional extra. It is part of the diagnosis.

Family counseling should cover:

- inheritance patterns,
- the possibility of asymptomatic or mildly affected carriers,
- implications for siblings and future children,
- and the limits of a VUS result.

In other words: a VUS does not always end the story if the phenotype is convincing.

## The phenotype to remember

The classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita — oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation — is useful, but it is not required for suspicion.

In adults, the clue may instead be a “premature aging” phenotype:

- early greying,
- osteoporosis,
- marrow cytopenias,
- pulmonary fibrosis,
- hepatic fibrosis,
- and sometimes neuropathy or other end-organ issues.

This case is a good reminder that telomere disorders often present outside the textbook description.

## Treatment: supportive now, but not passive

The patient improved symptomatically after:

- IV cyanocobalamin,
- folate,
- transfusion support,
- calcium and vitamin D repletion,
- electrolyte correction,
- physiotherapy,
- and genetic counseling.

That is exactly the right kind of treatment for the moment. But supportive care in TBD is not “do nothing.” It is active longitudinal management.

The practical approach is:

- correct reversible deficiencies,
- treat symptomatic cytopenias,
- optimize bone health,
- monitor for pulmonary and hepatic progression,
- and coordinate care across hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, and rehabilitation.

This is a disease where multidisciplinary follow-up is not a buzzword — it is the treatment plan.

## What about telomere-targeted therapies?

Androgen-based therapy, such as danazol or nandrolone, is often discussed because small studies suggest possible telomere lengthening or hematologic benefit. But this is still unsettled territory.

The short version: promising, not definitive.

At present, these therapies are not a universal answer and should be considered only in expert hands, with careful risk-benefit discussion and close monitoring. They do not replace the need for surveillance or transplant planning when disease progresses.

## Clinical pearls from this case

- In a young adult with cytopenias plus pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, think telomere biology disorder early — even without classic dyskeratosis congenita features.
- Correcting B12 deficiency may improve the blood counts, but it does not explain away premature greying, fibrosis, or a broader premature-aging phenotype.
- A VUS in a telomere gene can still be clinically meaningful when the phenotype fits.
- Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation requires special thought in short-telomere syndromes; the diagnosis should be known before transplant decisions are made.
- Family counseling matters because penetrance can be variable and family history may be deceptively negative.
- Supportive care is essential, but surveillance for marrow failure, lung disease, liver disease, and malignancy risk is what makes the diagnosis actionable.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder changes everything — not because we suddenly have a cure, but because we finally know what to watch for, what to avoid, and when to escalate.

That’s the whole point of making the diagnosis: it turns a confusing multisystem illness into a coordinated plan.
