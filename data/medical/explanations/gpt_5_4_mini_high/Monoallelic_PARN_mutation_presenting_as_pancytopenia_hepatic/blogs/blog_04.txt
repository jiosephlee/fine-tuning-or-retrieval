Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# PARN and the Problem of the VUS: How to Use Genotype-Phenotype Correlation in Real Patients

## The case that should make you pause

A woman in her late 20s comes in with 5 years of progressive fatigue, then a sharp decline over 3 months. She’s pale, icteric, prematurely grey, osteoporotic, neuropathic, hepatosplenic, and short of breath. Her labs show pancytopenia and severe B12/folate deficiency. So far, this sounds like “nutritional deficiency with complications.”

But then the story starts refusing to stay in one box: hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature ageing features, neuropathy, osteoporosis, and persistent multisystem disease that feels too big for B12 deficiency alone. Exome sequencing finds a monoallelic PARN missense variant, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), but it’s classified as a variant of uncertain significance.

That is the interesting part of this case: the lab report did not hand over a clean diagnosis, but the patient clearly had a syndrome.

## Why this looks like a telomere biology disorder

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are the archetypal “premature ageing” syndromes. They show up in rapidly dividing tissues: marrow, mucosa, skin, hair, liver, and lung. The classic teaching image is dyskeratosis congenita with oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation. Real life is messier.

Adults may present without the mucocutaneous triad at all. Instead, they show up with:
- unexplained cytopenias,
- pulmonary fibrosis,
- liver disease/fibrosis,
- premature greying,
- osteoporosis,
- and other signs of accelerated tissue failure.

That’s exactly why this case matters. The phenotype is the clue.

PARN is a particularly useful gene to know here. It encodes a deadenylase involved in RNA processing and in maturation of TERC, a key telomerase RNA component. Disruption of this pathway impairs telomere maintenance. Biallelic PARN variants are classically linked to dyskeratosis congenita; monoallelic variants are more often associated with adult telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. In other words: a single altered PARN allele can absolutely matter.

## What a VUS does — and does not — mean

A variant of uncertain significance is not a diagnosis. It should not, by itself, be used to make irreversible decisions or to label a patient with certainty.

But a VUS is also not a shrug.

In the right clinical context, a VUS can become a strong piece of a larger diagnostic puzzle. That is the key lesson here. This patient’s PARN variant was not “proven pathogenic,” but the overall story made a telomere biology disorder the best working diagnosis.

How do you do that responsibly?

You lean on genotype-phenotype correlation and on the biology of the gene:
- The variant is a missense change affecting a highly conserved residue.
- It sits in the R3H domain, a functionally important region.
- It is absent or extremely rare in population databases.
- Multiple in silico tools predict deleterious effect.
- PARN is a gene in which loss of function and damaging missense variants are a known mechanism of disease.
- The patient’s phenotype fits the broader PARN/TBD spectrum.

That doesn’t turn a VUS into pathogenic overnight. But it does justify a working diagnosis, closer surveillance, and a genetics-informed plan.

## Don’t let one correct finding stop the workup

This case also teaches a very common trap: anchoring on the first real abnormality.

Yes, the B12 deficiency was real. Yes, it likely contributed to the pancytopenia, dimorphic smear, hypersegmented neutrophils, and low reticulocyte count. The bone marrow showed ineffective erythropoiesis rather than malignancy or infiltrative disease. That part fits.

But the patient’s full syndrome did not fit B12 deficiency alone.

The red flags that should make you keep digging:
- premature greying in a woman in her 20s,
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis without a clear autoimmune or infectious cause,
- pulmonary fibrosis,
- osteoporosis and fractures,
- neuropathy,
- and a multisystem “accelerated ageing” look.

A telomere disorder may also make the marrow less resilient to hematologic stress. So severe B12 deficiency and a TBD can coexist, with the genetic problem lowering the reserve and the nutritional problem unmasking it.

## How to use ACMG logic without overcalling the variant

This is where the clinical side and the molecular side need to talk to each other.

The ACMG/AMP framework gives structure, but it is not meant to replace bedside reasoning. In this case, the variant met several supporting criteria:
- PM1: located in a critical functional domain
- PM2: absent or extremely rare in population datasets
- PP3: multiple computational tools suggest damage
- PP2: missense variation is a recognized disease mechanism in this gene

Helpful? Absolutely. Sufficient alone? No.

That’s why the final diagnosis in practice is often phrased as something like: **“clinical telomere biology disorder with a PARN VUS supporting the phenotype.”** That wording is honest, nuanced, and clinically useful.

## What would strengthen the diagnosis further?

For suspected TBD, telomere-length testing is a very reasonable next step if available. Flow-FISH is commonly used and can show whether telomeres are abnormally short for age. In a patient like this, telomere testing would not merely be academic — it could:
- support the diagnosis,
- help interpret the VUS,
- guide family counseling,
- and inform transplant risk if marrow failure progresses.

That matters because management is not generic. Patients with telomere disorders can have different risks with immunosuppression, transplantation, and pulmonary or hepatic complications. Getting the syndrome right changes the downstream plan.

## Practical bedside takeaways

- A VUS is not proof, but in the right phenotype it may be powerful supporting evidence.
- Premature greying, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, osteoporosis, and cytopenia in a young adult should trigger a telomere disorder workup.
- Severe B12 deficiency may explain part of the presentation, but don’t stop if the syndrome is bigger than the deficiency.
- PARN variants can present with adult-onset telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or marrow failure, even without classic dyskeratosis congenita features.
- Telomere-length testing and genetics follow-up are key when the gene result is uncertain but the phenotype is convincing.

The bigger lesson? Genetics doesn’t always arrive as a verdict. Sometimes it arrives as a clue. The clinician’s job is to know when that clue fits the patient in front of them.
