Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# When B12 Deficiency Isn’t the Whole Story: Pancytopenia with Premature Ageing Clues

## The trap: a diagnosis that is correct, but incomplete

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can absolutely explain pancytopenia, dimorphic red cells, hypersegmented neutrophils, low reticulocytes, and a marrow full of ineffective erythropoiesis. That part of this case fits beautifully.

But the danger is stopping there.

In this woman in her late 20s, the hematologic picture was only one piece of a much bigger syndrome: premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, neuropathy, osteoporosis, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Nutritional deficiency was real — but it did not explain the whole phenotype.

That’s the cognitive trap here: anchoring on a satisfying lab abnormality while missing the “why is this happening in a young adult?” question.

## What B12 deficiency explained — and what it didn’t

The initial workup made sense. She had pancytopenia, a very low reticulocyte count, smear findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis, and severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. Bone marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and no malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative process. In other words: the marrow was stressed, but not replaced by cancer or aplasia.

A positive direct antiglobulin test could have distracted the team toward immune hemolysis, but the low retic response and marrow findings argued against peripheral destruction as the main driver. The biochemical hemolysis markers were more in keeping with ineffective erythropoiesis than a primary autoimmune process.

So yes — treat the deficiency. But don’t let a good explanation for the anemia erase the bigger pattern.

## The red flags beyond nutrition

The case starts looking different when you zoom out:

- premature greying in a woman in her 20s
- generalized weakness over years, not weeks
- pulmonary fibrosis
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- osteoporosis and fractures
- neuropathy
- hepatosplenomegaly
- thoracic scoliosis and thenar atrophy

That combination should make you think beyond “malabsorption” or “poor diet” and ask whether you’re looking at an inherited premature-ageing syndrome.

In this setting, telomere biology disorder (TBD) is the diagnosis you don’t want to miss.

## Telomere biology disorders: the adult version can be sneaky

Telomeres protect chromosome ends. When telomere maintenance fails, the tissues that divide the most take the hit first: bone marrow, lung, liver, skin, mucosa, and hair.

The prototypical disorder is dyskeratosis congenita, but the classic mucocutaneous triad is often absent in adults. Instead, patients may show up with:

- marrow failure or unexplained cytopenias
- idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- hepatic fibrosis
- premature greying or other “older than they should be” features

That’s why TBDs are so easy to miss. They don’t always announce themselves with the textbook triad; they often arrive disguised as acquired disease.

## Why PARN mattered here

Genetic testing identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant: c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg). It was classified as a variant of uncertain significance, so this is not a “gene test equals diagnosis” story in the simplistic sense.

But the clinical-genetic fit was compelling.

PARN is a telomere-related gene, and monoallelic variants are most often associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. In this patient, the phenotype extended beyond the marrow and lungs to the liver as well, supporting a broader TBD spectrum.

The key lesson: a VUS is not nothing when the phenotype is screaming telomere disorder. It may not prove causality, but it can still unify the case and change how you follow the patient.

## Practical bedside thinking: when pancytopenia is not “just B12”

B12 deficiency can coexist with a genetic syndrome. In fact, that may be the point: the deficiency explains the anemia, while the underlying biology explains why the patient is presenting so young, so broadly, and so unusually.

A young adult with pancytopenia deserves a wider lens when you see any of the following:

- premature greying or other signs of accelerated ageing
- pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis
- osteoporosis, fractures, or other unusual systemic complications
- neuropathy or multisystem involvement out of proportion to the vitamin deficiency
- persistent disease after correction of nutritional deficits

Family history can help, but its absence does not rule this out. Incomplete penetrance, de novo variants, and variable expression are all part of the telomere story.

## What changed in management

Treatment of the deficiency was still necessary: IV cyanocobalamin, folate, transfusion support, calcium and vitamin D replacement, electrolyte correction, and physiotherapy. She improved symptomatically and her counts stabilized at short follow-up.

But the broader diagnosis changed the trajectory of care. She now needs surveillance for marrow failure, pulmonary disease, and liver disease, plus genetic counselling for the family.

That is the real payoff of not stopping early: you move from “fix the anemia” to “recognize a lifelong syndrome.”

## Take-home points

- A correct diagnosis can still be incomplete. Severe B12 deficiency explained the smear and pancytopenia, but not the whole phenotype.
- In a young adult, premature greying plus pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, osteoporosis, or neuropathy should trigger a broader search for an inherited disorder.
- Telomere biology disorders often lack the classic mucocutaneous triad and may present in adulthood as isolated marrow, lung, or liver disease.
- Monoallelic PARN variants are particularly associated with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, but the phenotype can be broader.
- Treat the nutritional deficiency, but keep asking whether it is the cause, the consequence, or a coexisting problem.
- When the pattern is multisystem and “too young to be this sick,” think genetics early.
