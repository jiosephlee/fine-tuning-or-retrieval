Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# A Positive DAT Does Not Equal Autoimmune Haemolysis

## The case that should make you slow down

A woman in her late 20s came in with years of progressive fatigue, worsening weakness, dyspnoea, weight loss, and a pancytopenic picture that would make most of us worry about marrow failure, autoimmune disease, or a haematologic malignancy.

Then the lab threw in a classic trap: a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), IgG 2+.

That’s the kind of result that can hijack your thinking. “Positive DAT” plus anaemia often gets mentally translated into “autoimmune haemolysis.” But this case is a good reminder that the test is not the diagnosis. You still have to ask whether the physiology actually fits peripheral immune-mediated haemolysis — or whether the patient is really dealing with ineffective erythropoiesis and intramedullary destruction.

In this patient, the answer was the latter.

## What was actually happening?

The smear and marrow told the story better than the DAT did.

She had:

- pancytopenia
- a very low reticulocyte count
- dimorphic red cells
- hypersegmented neutrophils
- basophilic stippling and Cabot rings
- severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency
- bone marrow with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores

That combination screams ineffective erythropoiesis.

In megaloblastic states, the marrow is working hard, but it is producing defective precursors that die before they ever become mature red cells. That means you can see biochemical “haemolysis” — elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia — without classic peripheral haemolysis. The destruction is largely intramedullary.

That distinction matters.

## Why the DAT can mislead you

A DAT tells you that immunoglobulin and/or complement is bound to the red cell surface. It does **not** automatically tell you that the patient has clinically important autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA).

In this case, the DAT was positive for IgG, but the rest of the physiology did not line up neatly with AIHA:

- the reticulocyte response was poor, not brisk
- the marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia rather than a pattern suggesting a peripheral haemolytic process
- the smear had megaloblastic features
- the clinical context strongly supported severe nutritional deficiency

AIHA is a peripheral destruction problem. If the marrow is intact and the patient is haemolysing, the reticulocyte count usually rises. When the retic count is low, you need to think harder about underproduction or ineffective production.

That’s the bedside pearl here: **in an anaemic patient, a low reticulocyte count should make you pause before you call haemolysis.**

## The physiology: intramedullary destruction vs peripheral haemolysis

This is the key conceptual split.

### Peripheral haemolysis
In AIHA, red cells are destroyed in the circulation or spleen. You expect the marrow to respond, assuming it can:

- reticulocytosis
- elevated LDH
- indirect hyperbilirubinaemia
- often supportive smear findings, depending on the type of haemolysis

### Ineffective erythropoiesis
In B12/folate deficiency, red cell precursors fail DNA synthesis and undergo apoptosis in the marrow. So the patient can look “haemolytic” on paper, but the problem is not autoimmune destruction of mature red cells.

You get:

- low reticulocytes despite anaemia
- marrow erythroid hyperplasia
- hypersegmented neutrophils
- elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin from precursor breakdown
- pancytopenia in more severe cases

That is exactly why severe megaloblastic anaemia can masquerade as haemolysis.

## How to avoid overcalling AIHA

When the DAT comes back positive, resist the urge to stop thinking.

Ask:

1. **Is there a marrow response?**  
   Reticulocytes are the hinge point. A low retic count pushes you toward marrow failure, ineffective erythropoiesis, or suppression.

2. **Does the smear fit megaloblastosis?**  
   Hypersegmented neutrophils and dimorphic red cells are strong clues that the issue is impaired production.

3. **Are there nutritional deficiencies that explain the picture?**  
   Severe B12 and folate deficiency can create a haemolysis-like lab profile.

4. **Does the marrow support the story?**  
   Erythroid hyperplasia with no malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative disease supports ineffective erythropoiesis.

5. **Is the DAT being over-weighted?**  
   A positive DAT is a data point, not a final diagnosis.

In this patient, the team appropriately looked beyond immune haemolysis. They corrected the vitamin deficiencies, supported her with transfusion and replacement therapy, and continued the workup for the broader multisystem syndrome.

## The bigger lesson from this case

The DAT issue was only one part of a much bigger story. This patient also had premature greying, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, neuropathy, and pancytopenia. The constellation eventually led to clinical exome sequencing, which found a monoallelic PARN variant, supporting a telomere biology disorder.

That matters because telomere disorders can present in adults without the classic mucocutaneous findings of dyskeratosis congenita. They can look like “just” unexplained cytopenias, fibrosis, or premature ageing — until you step back and connect the dots.

So yes, the positive DAT was a trap. But the even bigger teaching point is this: when the phenotype is out of proportion to the obvious deficiency, keep digging. Don’t let one positive test collapse your differential too early.

## Take-home points

- **A positive DAT does not diagnose AIHA by itself.** Always interpret it in clinical context.
- **Low reticulocytes argue against compensated peripheral haemolysis** and should make you think about underproduction or ineffective erythropoiesis.
- **Severe B12/folate deficiency can mimic haemolysis** with elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin because of intramedullary precursor destruction.
- **Hypersegmented neutrophils, dimorphic red cells, and marrow erythroid hyperplasia** are strong clues toward a megaloblastic process.
- **If the presentation is multisystem and disproportionate**, broaden the lens for inherited marrow failure or telomere biology disorders.
