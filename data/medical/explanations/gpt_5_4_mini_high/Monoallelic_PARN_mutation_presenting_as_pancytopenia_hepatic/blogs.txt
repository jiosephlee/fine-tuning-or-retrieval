# When B12 Deficiency Isn’t the Whole Story: Pancytopenia with Premature Ageing Clues

## The trap: a diagnosis that is correct, but incomplete

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can absolutely explain pancytopenia, dimorphic red cells, hypersegmented neutrophils, low reticulocytes, and a marrow full of ineffective erythropoiesis. That part of this case fits beautifully.

But the danger is stopping there.

In this woman in her late 20s, the hematologic picture was only one piece of a much bigger syndrome: premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, neuropathy, osteoporosis, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Nutritional deficiency was real — but it did not explain the whole phenotype.

That’s the cognitive trap here: anchoring on a satisfying lab abnormality while missing the “why is this happening in a young adult?” question.

## What B12 deficiency explained — and what it didn’t

The initial workup made sense. She had pancytopenia, a very low reticulocyte count, smear findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis, and severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. Bone marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and no malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative process. In other words: the marrow was stressed, but not replaced by cancer or aplasia.

A positive direct antiglobulin test could have distracted the team toward immune hemolysis, but the low retic response and marrow findings argued against peripheral destruction as the main driver. The biochemical hemolysis markers were more in keeping with ineffective erythropoiesis than a primary autoimmune process.

So yes — treat the deficiency. But don’t let a good explanation for the anemia erase the bigger pattern.

## The red flags beyond nutrition

The case starts looking different when you zoom out:

- premature greying in a woman in her 20s
- generalized weakness over years, not weeks
- pulmonary fibrosis
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- osteoporosis and fractures
- neuropathy
- hepatosplenomegaly
- thoracic scoliosis and thenar atrophy

That combination should make you think beyond “malabsorption” or “poor diet” and ask whether you’re looking at an inherited premature-ageing syndrome.

In this setting, telomere biology disorder (TBD) is the diagnosis you don’t want to miss.

## Telomere biology disorders: the adult version can be sneaky

Telomeres protect chromosome ends. When telomere maintenance fails, the tissues that divide the most take the hit first: bone marrow, lung, liver, skin, mucosa, and hair.

The prototypical disorder is dyskeratosis congenita, but the classic mucocutaneous triad is often absent in adults. Instead, patients may show up with:

- marrow failure or unexplained cytopenias
- idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- hepatic fibrosis
- premature greying or other “older than they should be” features

That’s why TBDs are so easy to miss. They don’t always announce themselves with the textbook triad; they often arrive disguised as acquired disease.

## Why PARN mattered here

Genetic testing identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant: c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg). It was classified as a variant of uncertain significance, so this is not a “gene test equals diagnosis” story in the simplistic sense.

But the clinical-genetic fit was compelling.

PARN is a telomere-related gene, and monoallelic variants are most often associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. In this patient, the phenotype extended beyond the marrow and lungs to the liver as well, supporting a broader TBD spectrum.

The key lesson: a VUS is not nothing when the phenotype is screaming telomere disorder. It may not prove causality, but it can still unify the case and change how you follow the patient.

## Practical bedside thinking: when pancytopenia is not “just B12”

B12 deficiency can coexist with a genetic syndrome. In fact, that may be the point: the deficiency explains the anemia, while the underlying biology explains why the patient is presenting so young, so broadly, and so unusually.

A young adult with pancytopenia deserves a wider lens when you see any of the following:

- premature greying or other signs of accelerated ageing
- pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis
- osteoporosis, fractures, or other unusual systemic complications
- neuropathy or multisystem involvement out of proportion to the vitamin deficiency
- persistent disease after correction of nutritional deficits

Family history can help, but its absence does not rule this out. Incomplete penetrance, de novo variants, and variable expression are all part of the telomere story.

## What changed in management

Treatment of the deficiency was still necessary: IV cyanocobalamin, folate, transfusion support, calcium and vitamin D replacement, electrolyte correction, and physiotherapy. She improved symptomatically and her counts stabilized at short follow-up.

But the broader diagnosis changed the trajectory of care. She now needs surveillance for marrow failure, pulmonary disease, and liver disease, plus genetic counselling for the family.

That is the real payoff of not stopping early: you move from “fix the anemia” to “recognize a lifelong syndrome.”

## Take-home points

- A correct diagnosis can still be incomplete. Severe B12 deficiency explained the smear and pancytopenia, but not the whole phenotype.
- In a young adult, premature greying plus pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, osteoporosis, or neuropathy should trigger a broader search for an inherited disorder.
- Telomere biology disorders often lack the classic mucocutaneous triad and may present in adulthood as isolated marrow, lung, or liver disease.
- Monoallelic PARN variants are particularly associated with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, but the phenotype can be broader.
- Treat the nutritional deficiency, but keep asking whether it is the cause, the consequence, or a coexisting problem.
- When the pattern is multisystem and “too young to be this sick,” think genetics early.



# A Positive DAT Does Not Equal Autoimmune Haemolysis

## The case that should make you slow down

A woman in her late 20s came in with years of progressive fatigue, worsening weakness, dyspnoea, weight loss, and a pancytopenic picture that would make most of us worry about marrow failure, autoimmune disease, or a haematologic malignancy.

Then the lab threw in a classic trap: a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), IgG 2+.

That’s the kind of result that can hijack your thinking. “Positive DAT” plus anaemia often gets mentally translated into “autoimmune haemolysis.” But this case is a good reminder that the test is not the diagnosis. You still have to ask whether the physiology actually fits peripheral immune-mediated haemolysis — or whether the patient is really dealing with ineffective erythropoiesis and intramedullary destruction.

In this patient, the answer was the latter.

## What was actually happening?

The smear and marrow told the story better than the DAT did.

She had:

- pancytopenia
- a very low reticulocyte count
- dimorphic red cells
- hypersegmented neutrophils
- basophilic stippling and Cabot rings
- severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency
- bone marrow with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores

That combination screams ineffective erythropoiesis.

In megaloblastic states, the marrow is working hard, but it is producing defective precursors that die before they ever become mature red cells. That means you can see biochemical “haemolysis” — elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia — without classic peripheral haemolysis. The destruction is largely intramedullary.

That distinction matters.

## Why the DAT can mislead you

A DAT tells you that immunoglobulin and/or complement is bound to the red cell surface. It does **not** automatically tell you that the patient has clinically important autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA).

In this case, the DAT was positive for IgG, but the rest of the physiology did not line up neatly with AIHA:

- the reticulocyte response was poor, not brisk
- the marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia rather than a pattern suggesting a peripheral haemolytic process
- the smear had megaloblastic features
- the clinical context strongly supported severe nutritional deficiency

AIHA is a peripheral destruction problem. If the marrow is intact and the patient is haemolysing, the reticulocyte count usually rises. When the retic count is low, you need to think harder about underproduction or ineffective production.

That’s the bedside pearl here: **in an anaemic patient, a low reticulocyte count should make you pause before you call haemolysis.**

## The physiology: intramedullary destruction vs peripheral haemolysis

This is the key conceptual split.

### Peripheral haemolysis
In AIHA, red cells are destroyed in the circulation or spleen. You expect the marrow to respond, assuming it can:

- reticulocytosis
- elevated LDH
- indirect hyperbilirubinaemia
- often supportive smear findings, depending on the type of haemolysis

### Ineffective erythropoiesis
In B12/folate deficiency, red cell precursors fail DNA synthesis and undergo apoptosis in the marrow. So the patient can look “haemolytic” on paper, but the problem is not autoimmune destruction of mature red cells.

You get:

- low reticulocytes despite anaemia
- marrow erythroid hyperplasia
- hypersegmented neutrophils
- elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin from precursor breakdown
- pancytopenia in more severe cases

That is exactly why severe megaloblastic anaemia can masquerade as haemolysis.

## How to avoid overcalling AIHA

When the DAT comes back positive, resist the urge to stop thinking.

Ask:

1. **Is there a marrow response?**  
   Reticulocytes are the hinge point. A low retic count pushes you toward marrow failure, ineffective erythropoiesis, or suppression.

2. **Does the smear fit megaloblastosis?**  
   Hypersegmented neutrophils and dimorphic red cells are strong clues that the issue is impaired production.

3. **Are there nutritional deficiencies that explain the picture?**  
   Severe B12 and folate deficiency can create a haemolysis-like lab profile.

4. **Does the marrow support the story?**  
   Erythroid hyperplasia with no malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative disease supports ineffective erythropoiesis.

5. **Is the DAT being over-weighted?**  
   A positive DAT is a data point, not a final diagnosis.

In this patient, the team appropriately looked beyond immune haemolysis. They corrected the vitamin deficiencies, supported her with transfusion and replacement therapy, and continued the workup for the broader multisystem syndrome.

## The bigger lesson from this case

The DAT issue was only one part of a much bigger story. This patient also had premature greying, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, neuropathy, and pancytopenia. The constellation eventually led to clinical exome sequencing, which found a monoallelic PARN variant, supporting a telomere biology disorder.

That matters because telomere disorders can present in adults without the classic mucocutaneous findings of dyskeratosis congenita. They can look like “just” unexplained cytopenias, fibrosis, or premature ageing — until you step back and connect the dots.

So yes, the positive DAT was a trap. But the even bigger teaching point is this: when the phenotype is out of proportion to the obvious deficiency, keep digging. Don’t let one positive test collapse your differential too early.

## Take-home points

- **A positive DAT does not diagnose AIHA by itself.** Always interpret it in clinical context.
- **Low reticulocytes argue against compensated peripheral haemolysis** and should make you think about underproduction or ineffective erythropoiesis.
- **Severe B12/folate deficiency can mimic haemolysis** with elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin because of intramedullary precursor destruction.
- **Hypersegmented neutrophils, dimorphic red cells, and marrow erythroid hyperplasia** are strong clues toward a megaloblastic process.
- **If the presentation is multisystem and disproportionate**, broaden the lens for inherited marrow failure or telomere biology disorders.



# Young, Fibrotic, and Unexplained: When Liver Plus Lung Disease Should Trigger a Telomere Workup

## The weird part of this case

A woman in her late 20s came in with five years of progressive fatigue, then a sharper decline over three months. On paper, the early workup looked like a hematology/nutrition problem: pancytopenia, a very low reticulocyte count, dimorphic smear, severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, and bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia without malignancy.

But the case stopped being “just” nutritional deficiency pretty quickly.

She also had premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, osteoporosis, neuropathy, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic fibrosis, and fibrotic lung changes on imaging. That combination should make you pause. In a young adult, combined liver + lung fibrosis is not a coincidence until proven otherwise.

This is the kind of patient who can drift through multiple diagnoses before anyone zooms out far enough to see the pattern.

## Why this pattern matters

Telomere biology disorders are the classic “don’t miss the syndrome hiding behind the organ problem” diagnosis. They can present without the textbook dyskeratosis congenita triad, and they often show up in adults as isolated or overlapping:

- bone marrow failure or cytopenias
- idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- subtle premature-ageing features

That matters because the individual pieces are easy to misattribute. Cytopenias get blamed on B12 deficiency. Dyspnea gets worked up as infection or interstitial lung disease. Liver disease gets labeled cryptogenic. And if the patient doesn’t have obvious nail dystrophy, leukoplakia, or striking skin changes, the inherited diagnosis gets missed.

This case is a good reminder that the “aha” moment in telomere syndromes is often not one finding — it’s the combination.

## Telomere biology disorders, without the textbook triad

Telomeres protect chromosome ends and shorten with each cell division. When telomere maintenance fails, high-turnover tissues take the hit first: marrow, skin, mucosa, lung, and liver.

Classically, dyskeratosis congenita is the prototype, but adult phenotypes are much broader. Patients may present with pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, marrow failure, or a mix of all three. The mucocutaneous triad may be absent.

In this case, exome sequencing found a monoallelic PARN missense variant, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. That label matters: a VUS is not proof of causality. But the clinical context is doing a lot of work here. PARN is already linked to telomere-related disease, and monoallelic variants are associated more often with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure phenotypes.

So the genetics didn’t create the diagnosis out of thin air — they helped unify a phenotype that already looked syndromic.

## What should make you think “telomere disorder” at the bedside?

A few clues in this case are worth memorizing.

First, age. A late-20s patient with pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis is unusual enough to demand an inherited or systemic explanation.

Second, the pattern. Lung + liver + marrow involvement is the big clue. Any two of those together, especially in a young person, should raise suspicion.

Third, the small “premature aging” signs. Premature greying is easy to dismiss because it’s nonspecific, but in the right setting it’s a real clue. Add osteoporosis, low body reserve, neuropathy, and chronic cytopenias, and the signal gets stronger.

Fourth, the marrow response. A low reticulocyte count argues against an appropriate marrow response and points toward ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow dysfunction. In this patient, severe B12 deficiency explained a lot of that picture, but not everything.

That’s another useful pearl: nutritional deficiency and telomere biology disorder are not mutually exclusive. One can mask the other.

## The diagnostic trap: explaining away too much

This case had several tempting distractions.

Severe B12 deficiency was real and clinically meaningful. The smear and marrow supported ineffective erythropoiesis. There was even a positive DAT for IgG, which can pull you toward immune-mediated hemolysis or autoimmune disease.

But the rest of the story didn’t fit neatly. The reticulocyte response was low, the marrow wasn’t malignant or dysplastic, autoimmune serologies were negative, and the degree of multisystem disease was out of proportion to vitamin deficiency alone.

That’s the key diagnostic discipline here: treat the obvious abnormality, but keep asking whether it explains the whole phenotype.

It didn’t here.

## How to approach the workup when the pattern shows up

Once you suspect a telomere biology disorder, the workup becomes less about chasing random abnormalities and more about documenting the extent of organ involvement and getting genetics involved early.

In this case, the evaluation included:

- CBC, reticulocyte count, smear
- bone marrow exam and cytogenetics
- nutritional studies
- GI evaluation for malabsorption
- liver imaging and transient elastography
- chest imaging showing fibrotic change
- EMG/NCS for neuropathy
- exome sequencing

The broader principle is simple: if a young patient has unexplained cytopenias plus pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, don’t stop at “cryptogenic.” Think inherited telomere disorder and consider:
- telomere length testing, if available
- a targeted telomere gene panel or exome sequencing
- genetic counseling
- multidisciplinary follow-up with hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, and genetics

## Management implications

There’s no quick fix for telomere biology disorders. Treatment is supportive and organ-specific: correct deficiencies, manage cytopenias, monitor pulmonary and liver disease, and follow patients longitudinally.

That said, making the diagnosis still changes management. It affects:
- surveillance planning
- family counseling
- transplant discussions if marrow failure progresses
- expectations around disease course

In this patient, the immediate management was correction of B12 and folate deficiency, transfusion support, vitamin D and calcium replacement, and physiotherapy. She improved symptomatically, but the telomere diagnosis means she still needs ongoing monitoring for progression of marrow, lung, and liver disease.

## Clinical pearls

- Young adult + pulmonary fibrosis + hepatic fibrosis = think inherited telomere disorder until proven otherwise.
- Absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad does not rule out a telomere biology disorder.
- Premature greying, osteoporosis, and cytopenias are “small clues” that become powerful when they cluster.
- Severe nutritional deficiency can coexist with an underlying genetic syndrome and obscure it.
- A VUS does not automatically diagnose disease, but it can support the clinical picture when the phenotype fits.

## Take-home points

- The biggest clue to a telomere biology disorder is often combined organ fibrosis, not the classic mucocutaneous triad.
- In a young patient, unexplained liver + lung disease should trigger a telomere workup.
- Cytopenias with low reticulocytes and premature-ageing features should make you zoom out beyond nutritional deficiency alone.
- Early recognition matters because it changes surveillance, counseling, and long-term planning.



# PARN and the Problem of the VUS: How to Use Genotype-Phenotype Correlation in Real Patients

## The case that should make you pause

A woman in her late 20s comes in with 5 years of progressive fatigue, then a sharp decline over 3 months. She’s pale, icteric, prematurely grey, osteoporotic, neuropathic, hepatosplenic, and short of breath. Her labs show pancytopenia and severe B12/folate deficiency. So far, this sounds like “nutritional deficiency with complications.”

But then the story starts refusing to stay in one box: hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature ageing features, neuropathy, osteoporosis, and persistent multisystem disease that feels too big for B12 deficiency alone. Exome sequencing finds a monoallelic PARN missense variant, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), but it’s classified as a variant of uncertain significance.

That is the interesting part of this case: the lab report did not hand over a clean diagnosis, but the patient clearly had a syndrome.

## Why this looks like a telomere biology disorder

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are the archetypal “premature ageing” syndromes. They show up in rapidly dividing tissues: marrow, mucosa, skin, hair, liver, and lung. The classic teaching image is dyskeratosis congenita with oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation. Real life is messier.

Adults may present without the mucocutaneous triad at all. Instead, they show up with:
- unexplained cytopenias,
- pulmonary fibrosis,
- liver disease/fibrosis,
- premature greying,
- osteoporosis,
- and other signs of accelerated tissue failure.

That’s exactly why this case matters. The phenotype is the clue.

PARN is a particularly useful gene to know here. It encodes a deadenylase involved in RNA processing and in maturation of TERC, a key telomerase RNA component. Disruption of this pathway impairs telomere maintenance. Biallelic PARN variants are classically linked to dyskeratosis congenita; monoallelic variants are more often associated with adult telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. In other words: a single altered PARN allele can absolutely matter.

## What a VUS does — and does not — mean

A variant of uncertain significance is not a diagnosis. It should not, by itself, be used to make irreversible decisions or to label a patient with certainty.

But a VUS is also not a shrug.

In the right clinical context, a VUS can become a strong piece of a larger diagnostic puzzle. That is the key lesson here. This patient’s PARN variant was not “proven pathogenic,” but the overall story made a telomere biology disorder the best working diagnosis.

How do you do that responsibly?

You lean on genotype-phenotype correlation and on the biology of the gene:
- The variant is a missense change affecting a highly conserved residue.
- It sits in the R3H domain, a functionally important region.
- It is absent or extremely rare in population databases.
- Multiple in silico tools predict deleterious effect.
- PARN is a gene in which loss of function and damaging missense variants are a known mechanism of disease.
- The patient’s phenotype fits the broader PARN/TBD spectrum.

That doesn’t turn a VUS into pathogenic overnight. But it does justify a working diagnosis, closer surveillance, and a genetics-informed plan.

## Don’t let one correct finding stop the workup

This case also teaches a very common trap: anchoring on the first real abnormality.

Yes, the B12 deficiency was real. Yes, it likely contributed to the pancytopenia, dimorphic smear, hypersegmented neutrophils, and low reticulocyte count. The bone marrow showed ineffective erythropoiesis rather than malignancy or infiltrative disease. That part fits.

But the patient’s full syndrome did not fit B12 deficiency alone.

The red flags that should make you keep digging:
- premature greying in a woman in her 20s,
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis without a clear autoimmune or infectious cause,
- pulmonary fibrosis,
- osteoporosis and fractures,
- neuropathy,
- and a multisystem “accelerated ageing” look.

A telomere disorder may also make the marrow less resilient to hematologic stress. So severe B12 deficiency and a TBD can coexist, with the genetic problem lowering the reserve and the nutritional problem unmasking it.

## How to use ACMG logic without overcalling the variant

This is where the clinical side and the molecular side need to talk to each other.

The ACMG/AMP framework gives structure, but it is not meant to replace bedside reasoning. In this case, the variant met several supporting criteria:
- PM1: located in a critical functional domain
- PM2: absent or extremely rare in population datasets
- PP3: multiple computational tools suggest damage
- PP2: missense variation is a recognized disease mechanism in this gene

Helpful? Absolutely. Sufficient alone? No.

That’s why the final diagnosis in practice is often phrased as something like: **“clinical telomere biology disorder with a PARN VUS supporting the phenotype.”** That wording is honest, nuanced, and clinically useful.

## What would strengthen the diagnosis further?

For suspected TBD, telomere-length testing is a very reasonable next step if available. Flow-FISH is commonly used and can show whether telomeres are abnormally short for age. In a patient like this, telomere testing would not merely be academic — it could:
- support the diagnosis,
- help interpret the VUS,
- guide family counseling,
- and inform transplant risk if marrow failure progresses.

That matters because management is not generic. Patients with telomere disorders can have different risks with immunosuppression, transplantation, and pulmonary or hepatic complications. Getting the syndrome right changes the downstream plan.

## Practical bedside takeaways

- A VUS is not proof, but in the right phenotype it may be powerful supporting evidence.
- Premature greying, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, osteoporosis, and cytopenia in a young adult should trigger a telomere disorder workup.
- Severe B12 deficiency may explain part of the presentation, but don’t stop if the syndrome is bigger than the deficiency.
- PARN variants can present with adult-onset telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or marrow failure, even without classic dyskeratosis congenita features.
- Telomere-length testing and genetics follow-up are key when the gene result is uncertain but the phenotype is convincing.

The bigger lesson? Genetics doesn’t always arrive as a verdict. Sometimes it arrives as a clue. The clinician’s job is to know when that clue fits the patient in front of them.



# Why the Diagnosis Changes Everything: Management Pearls in Telomere Biology Disorders

## The surprising part of this case

A woman in her late 20s came in with what could have been written off as “just” severe nutritional deficiency: fatigue, weakness, pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, and profound vitamin B12/folate deficiency. But the deeper story was bigger than that. She also had premature greying, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, and fibrotic lung disease.

That combination is the red flag.

This is the kind of patient who forces you to stop thinking in single-organ silos. Once telomere biology disorder (TBD) enters the differential, the entire management frame changes — even if the genetic result is not a clean, slam-dunk pathogenic variant. In this case, the patient had a monoallelic PARN missense variant reported as a VUS, but the clinical picture strongly supported a telomere disorder.

That matters because TBD is not just a diagnostic label. It is a roadmap for surveillance, counseling, transplant planning, and long-term risk management.

## Don’t let the B12 deficiency close the case

This patient absolutely did have severe B12 deficiency, and that explained a lot of the hematologic picture: low reticulocytes, ineffective erythropoiesis, hypersegmented neutrophils, elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and marrow erythroid hyperplasia.

But the important teaching point is this: nutritional deficiency can coexist with inherited marrow vulnerability.

In a young adult with:

- cytopenias,
- premature aging features,
- pulmonary fibrosis,
- hepatic fibrosis,
- osteoporosis,
- and a family history that may be absent or unhelpful,

you need to think beyond acquired deficiency states. TBD can be clinically subtle, and classic dyskeratosis congenita findings may be completely absent.

The diagnosis is often missed because the “obvious” explanation is real — just incomplete.

## Why naming a telomere biology disorder changes management

The biggest mistake is assuming that “supportive care only” means the diagnosis doesn’t matter. It matters a lot.

Even when there is no disease-modifying therapy available right now, identifying TBD changes the way you follow the patient and the way you think about future deterioration.

### 1) You stop treating the marrow as the only problem

TBD is a multisystem disease. In this case, the marrow findings were only one part of the story. She also had pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis, and both may progress over time.

That means follow-up is not just about hemoglobin. It is about:

- serial CBCs and reticulocyte counts,
- monitoring for evolving marrow failure,
- surveillance of liver function and fibrosis progression,
- pulmonary follow-up for interstitial lung disease,
- and watching for new organ involvement.

A patient can look “hematologically better” after vitamin replacement and still be quietly progressing in other organs.

### 2) You become more alert to malignancy risk

TBDs carry increased risk of hematologic and solid malignancies. The case report doesn’t give a screening protocol — and there isn’t a simple one-size-fits-all answer — but the practical point is low threshold vigilance.

Unexplained worsening cytopenias, weight loss, constitutional symptoms, new mucosal lesions, or changing organ dysfunction should not be shrugged off.

### 3) You plan for transplant differently

This is one of the most important reasons to recognize TBD early.

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be curative for marrow failure, but telomere disorders are not standard bone marrow failure syndromes. The underlying biology makes patients more fragile, and transplant toxicity can be substantial if the diagnosis is missed.

Why does that matter?

Because if a patient with a short-telomere syndrome is transplanted using routine myeloablative thinking, the lungs, liver, and mucosa may not tolerate it well. Knowing the diagnosis early allows transplant teams to think carefully about:

- timing,
- conditioning intensity,
- organ toxicity risk,
- donor evaluation,
- and whether the patient is even a transplant candidate at that moment.

The big conceptual point: **HCT treats the marrow failure, not the telomere disorder as a whole.**

### 4) You change how you counsel the family

This patient had no obvious family history, but that does not rule out inherited disease. TBDs can show variable penetrance, de novo variants, and subtle manifestations in relatives.

That means genetic counseling is not an optional extra. It is part of the diagnosis.

Family counseling should cover:

- inheritance patterns,
- the possibility of asymptomatic or mildly affected carriers,
- implications for siblings and future children,
- and the limits of a VUS result.

In other words: a VUS does not always end the story if the phenotype is convincing.

## The phenotype to remember

The classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita — oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation — is useful, but it is not required for suspicion.

In adults, the clue may instead be a “premature aging” phenotype:

- early greying,
- osteoporosis,
- marrow cytopenias,
- pulmonary fibrosis,
- hepatic fibrosis,
- and sometimes neuropathy or other end-organ issues.

This case is a good reminder that telomere disorders often present outside the textbook description.

## Treatment: supportive now, but not passive

The patient improved symptomatically after:

- IV cyanocobalamin,
- folate,
- transfusion support,
- calcium and vitamin D repletion,
- electrolyte correction,
- physiotherapy,
- and genetic counseling.

That is exactly the right kind of treatment for the moment. But supportive care in TBD is not “do nothing.” It is active longitudinal management.

The practical approach is:

- correct reversible deficiencies,
- treat symptomatic cytopenias,
- optimize bone health,
- monitor for pulmonary and hepatic progression,
- and coordinate care across hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, and rehabilitation.

This is a disease where multidisciplinary follow-up is not a buzzword — it is the treatment plan.

## What about telomere-targeted therapies?

Androgen-based therapy, such as danazol or nandrolone, is often discussed because small studies suggest possible telomere lengthening or hematologic benefit. But this is still unsettled territory.

The short version: promising, not definitive.

At present, these therapies are not a universal answer and should be considered only in expert hands, with careful risk-benefit discussion and close monitoring. They do not replace the need for surveillance or transplant planning when disease progresses.

## Clinical pearls from this case

- In a young adult with cytopenias plus pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, think telomere biology disorder early — even without classic dyskeratosis congenita features.
- Correcting B12 deficiency may improve the blood counts, but it does not explain away premature greying, fibrosis, or a broader premature-aging phenotype.
- A VUS in a telomere gene can still be clinically meaningful when the phenotype fits.
- Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation requires special thought in short-telomere syndromes; the diagnosis should be known before transplant decisions are made.
- Family counseling matters because penetrance can be variable and family history may be deceptively negative.
- Supportive care is essential, but surveillance for marrow failure, lung disease, liver disease, and malignancy risk is what makes the diagnosis actionable.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder changes everything — not because we suddenly have a cure, but because we finally know what to watch for, what to avoid, and when to escalate.

That’s the whole point of making the diagnosis: it turns a confusing multisystem illness into a coordinated plan.
