Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 3: ## Stepwise Diagnostic Workup of Pancytopenia with Hepatic and Pulmonary Fibrosis

The diagnostic approach began with a basic but crucial question: was the pancytopenia due to decreased marrow production, increased peripheral destruction, or ineffective hematopoiesis? The complete blood count showed pancytopenia with a very low reticulocyte count, which immediately argued against an adequate marrow response. In a patient with anemia, a low reticulocyte count indicates either marrow hypoproliferation or ineffective erythropoiesis rather than brisk peripheral loss. This is an important early clue, because it shifts the differential toward nutritional deficiency, marrow failure syndromes, infiltrative disease, or inherited disorders.

The peripheral smear provided the next major clue. Dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils are classic markers of megaloblastic change and ineffective erythropoiesis. Hypersegmented neutrophils are especially helpful because they point to impaired DNA synthesis, most often from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. The dimorphic red cell population reflected abnormal red cell production, while the basophilic stippling and Cabot rings reinforced the impression of disordered erythropoiesis rather than isolated hemolysis or blood loss. Serum studies then confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, providing a proximate explanation for much of the hematologic picture.

The iron profile further supported ineffective erythropoiesis. Elevated serum iron and ferritin are commonly seen when red cell precursors fail to mature normally, because iron is not efficiently incorporated into hemoglobin and may accumulate in the marrow and circulation. Bone marrow examination was therefore valuable both to support this interpretation and to exclude more ominous causes of pancytopenia. The marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, but no leukemia, dysplasia, or infiltrative process, and cytogenetics were normal. This pattern is much more consistent with megaloblastic anemia than with aplastic anemia or myelodysplastic syndrome.

Markers of hemolysis were also abnormal, with elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin. In megaloblastic anemia, these findings often reflect intramedullary destruction of abnormal erythroid precursors, sometimes called ineffective hemolysis, rather than true peripheral hemolysis. The direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG, which can initially raise concern for autoimmune hemolytic anemia. However, a positive DAT must be interpreted in context: autoimmune hemolysis usually produces an appropriate reticulocyte response unless marrow production is also impaired. Here, the markedly low reticulocyte count and marrow findings favored ineffective erythropoiesis as the dominant mechanism, making the DAT result less convincing as the primary explanation for the cytopenias.

Because severe B12 deficiency must be explained, the team evaluated for malabsorption and autoimmune gastritis. Endoscopy was macroscopically normal, but biopsy showed chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cell function, which could impair intrinsic factor production and contribute to B12 deficiency. Even so, the extent of systemic disease was broader than would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone. This is a key clinical lesson: once a reversible deficiency is identified, clinicians should still ask whether it fully accounts for the patient’s age, severity, and extrahematologic features. In this case, the answer was no.

The liver and lung findings were especially important because they pointed away from an isolated nutritional problem and toward a multisystem disorder. Ultrasound confirmed hepatosplenomegaly, while transient elastography showed hepatic steatosis and stiffness compatible with cirrhosis. In a woman in her late 20s, this degree of fibrosis requires evaluation for autoimmune, infectious, metabolic, and genetic causes. Autoimmune serologies were negative, and infectious studies for viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and other common triggers were unrevealing. A positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG reflected prior exposure rather than active disease. Positron emission tomography-CT then helped exclude occult malignancy, granulomatous disease, or other infiltrative pathology while also demonstrating diffuse marrow uptake and bilateral ground-glass and fibrotic lung changes, consistent with interstitial lung disease. Together, these studies documented the multisystem fibrotic phenotype and narrowed the differential away from acquired inflammatory disease.

Additional testing showed broader organ involvement that strengthened the suspicion for a syndromic process. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry confirmed osteoporosis, while vitamin D deficiency, hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia, and secondary hyperparathyroidism explained her bone pain and fracture risk. Electrodiagnostic studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes, adding another layer of unexplained multisystem disease. At this stage, the combination of cytopenias, premature greying, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy in a young adult made an inherited telomere biology disorder much more likely than a purely acquired condition.

That clinical suspicion justified escalation to genetic testing. Clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), a change affecting a highly conserved residue in a functionally important domain. Although the variant was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance under ACMG criteria, several features made it biologically plausible: it was rare, predicted to be damaging by multiple in silico tools, and occurred in a gene already known to cause telomere biology disorders. In suspected telomere biology disorders, telomere length testing by flow-FISH is the functional complement to sequencing. It measures age-adjusted leukocyte telomere length and can provide strong supportive evidence, especially when sequencing identifies a VUS rather than a clearly pathogenic variant. In practice, the diagnosis is strongest when the genotype, telomere length, and clinical phenotype align, as they did here.

This case illustrates the value of stepwise reasoning. The initial hematologic abnormality was largely explained by severe B12 deficiency and ineffective erythropoiesis, but the persistent and disproportionate liver, lung, bone, and neurologic abnormalities prompted a broader search. Recognizing when common diagnoses do not fully fit is essential, because young patients with unexplained multisystem fibrosis and premature ageing features may have an underlying telomere biology disorder even without the classic mucocutaneous signs of dyskeratosis congenita.
