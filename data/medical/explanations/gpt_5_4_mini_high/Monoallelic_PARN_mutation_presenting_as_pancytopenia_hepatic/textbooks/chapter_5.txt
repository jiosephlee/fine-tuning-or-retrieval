Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 5: ## Management, Follow-up, and Clinical Implications of PARN-Associated TBD

Management in this case began with correction of reversible contributors to her presentation, because severe vitamin B12 deficiency and folate deficiency were clearly documented and were sufficient to explain much of the ineffective erythropoiesis seen on the blood film and marrow examination. She received intravenous cyanocobalamin followed by a maintenance schedule, along with oral folic acid, nutritional repletion, and correction of electrolyte abnormalities. This step was important diagnostically as well as therapeutically: if cytopenias improve after vitamin replacement, that supports a major nutritional component, but persistence or recurrence of cytopenia after correction should prompt renewed concern for an underlying marrow disorder.

Her anemia was managed supportively with blood transfusion when symptomatic. In telomere biology disorders, transfusions are used for clinical indications rather than to normalize counts, because the underlying problem may be limited marrow reserve rather than isolated deficiency. Serial blood-count monitoring is essential after vitamin replacement, since a patient with a telomere disorder may initially appear to recover but later declare progressive bone marrow failure. In this case, the short-term hematologic response was stabilization rather than full normalization, which is appropriate to follow closely over time.

Bone and mineral abnormalities also required treatment. She had vitamin D deficiency, hypocalcaemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and osteoporosis, all of which can contribute to bone pain, fractures, and further functional decline. Replacement with vitamin D and calcium, together with electrolyte correction, addresses these reversible factors. Physiotherapy is a useful adjunct because weakness in this patient was multifactorial: anemia, neuropathy, deconditioning, and skeletal fragility all likely contributed to impaired mobility and activities of daily living. In young adults with inherited multisystem disease, rehabilitation is not ancillary—it is part of preserving independence.

The key management issue after stabilization is surveillance for progression of telomere-mediated organ disease. Even though this patient’s pancytopenia improved after correction of vitamin deficiencies, the PARN variant and the broader phenotype raise concern for an underlying telomere biology disorder. That means blood counts should be followed longitudinally for evolving marrow failure, and clinicians should remain alert for new macrocytosis, falling reticulocyte counts, transfusion dependence, or recurrent cytopenias that would suggest a progressive marrow phenotype rather than a purely nutritional problem.

Pulmonary follow-up is especially important because PARN-associated telomere disease is well recognized in the spectrum of telomere-related interstitial lung disease and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. In practice, this means periodic clinical review, pulmonary function testing, and interval imaging when indicated, with early referral to pulmonology if dyspnea worsens or lung function declines. The goal is not only diagnosis but also timely planning, because progressive fibrotic lung disease in telomere disorders can advance despite otherwise modest initial symptoms.

The same principle applies to the liver. Her transient elastography and imaging suggested fibrosis/cirrhosis, so ongoing hepatic surveillance is needed to monitor for progression, portal hypertension, ascites, and hepatic decompensation. Because liver disease in telomere disorders may be under-recognized, young adults with otherwise unexplained fibrosis should not be assumed to have only metabolic or inflammatory liver disease once common causes have been excluded. Serial liver assessment is particularly relevant here because hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis can coexist in the same telomere phenotype.

Genetic counseling is a crucial part of management. This patient had a monoallelic PARN missense variant classified as a variant of uncertain significance, so the result does not prove pathogenicity in isolation. However, the clinical context strongly suggested a telomere biology disorder. Counseling therefore has to address uncertainty: family history may be absent because of reduced penetrance, unrecognized disease in relatives, or a possible de novo event. Relatives may still warrant consideration for clinical review or targeted testing if the variant is reclassified in the future, but interpretation should be careful and based on genetics expertise.

If marrow failure becomes progressive, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may be considered in telomere biology disorders, but transplantation is more complex in this setting than in acquired marrow failure. Patients with telomere disease are often more vulnerable to conditioning-related toxicity, pulmonary complications, and hepatic injury, so transplant planning should occur at an experienced center with attention to donor selection, organ assessment, and reduced-intensity approaches when appropriate. For this reason, early diagnosis matters: it allows timely referral before organ dysfunction becomes too advanced for safe intervention.

Disease-modifying therapies remain limited. Androgens such as danazol or nandrolone have been explored in telomere biology disorders and may increase telomere length or improve blood counts in some patients, but the evidence is still limited and long-term benefit and safety are uncertain. These agents are therefore not a substitute for surveillance, supportive care, or transplant planning when indicated. In a patient like this one, they may be discussed only in specialized follow-up and typically in the context of progressive disease.

The short-term outcome in this case was favorable: after vitamin repletion and supportive care, her fatigue improved and blood counts stabilized over the brief follow-up period. That improvement should not be interpreted as resolution of the underlying disorder. Rather, it shows that reversible deficiencies were contributing substantially to the presentation while the telomere disorder likely explains the broader multisystem phenotype. Long-term prognosis depends on whether she develops progressive marrow failure, worsening pulmonary fibrosis, or advancing liver disease, which is why ongoing multidisciplinary follow-up is essential.

For trainees, the main clinical lesson is to think beyond common explanations when a young adult has cytopenia plus premature-aging features or fibrosis in more than one organ system. Severe vitamin deficiency can coexist with an inherited marrow-liver-lung syndrome, and a partial response to repletion should not end the diagnostic workup if the overall phenotype remains disproportionate. In that setting, telomere biology disorders should be part of the differential, and early genetic evaluation can change counseling, surveillance, and transplant planning.
