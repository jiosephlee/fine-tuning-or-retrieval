Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 4: ## Intervention: CT-Guided TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Planning and Execution

The third transcatheter intervention was planned as a carefully individualized redo procedure in a triple-valve anatomy, where the aims were not only to restore valve function but also to avoid coronary obstruction, preserve future coronary access, and obtain the largest possible residual effective orifice area. In this setting, the preprocedural CT study was central to decision-making. It was used to assess the geometry of the two existing transcatheter frames, the size of the remaining lumen, the coronary ostial relationship to the anticipated new leaflet plane, and the feasibility of a high implant strategy. Because the patient had completed antimicrobial therapy and had no clinical or imaging evidence of active endocarditis, the team treated the case as a structural valve failure problem rather than an active infection problem. The procedure was performed only after multidisciplinary heart-team review, with institutional approval for off-label use of the selected device and shared decision-making with the patient and family.

A self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve, size S, was chosen because its design was considered more favorable for a narrow, multiply stented aortic root. Supra-annular leaflet position can provide better hemodynamics in constrained anatomy by placing the functional valve plane higher and away from the smallest residual lumen. The relatively low frame height also helps when working within previously implanted transcatheter frames, where excessive bulk can worsen patient-prosthesis mismatch and complicate coronary access. These properties made the device attractive for a case in which the prior Evolut frame already imposed major spatial constraints and the CT showed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm.

The procedure was carried out under local anesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. This approach allowed diagnostic aortography and hemodynamic assessment while maintaining a minimally invasive strategy in a high-risk patient. Baseline aortography confirmed severe aortic regurgitation, consistent with the preprocedural echocardiographic assessment of failed prosthetic valve function. A Safari guidewire was positioned in the left ventricle through femoral access to provide stable support for delivery and retrieval maneuvers. Balloon predilation was then performed under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. In serial TAVI procedures, predilation serves not only to prepare the landing zone but also to test whether coronary flow remains safe under transient changes in leaflet and frame position; in this case, simultaneous aortography showed no coronary compromise.

The key technical decision was high implantation of the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets. This strategy was preplanned using CT and fluoroscopic landmarks to place the new prosthesis in a position that would pin the pre-existing leaflets outward against the stent frame rather than allowing them to fold inward and further narrow the orifice. By fixing the old leaflets more effectively and limiting the amount of new “neo-skirt” extending below the coronary ostia, the team aimed to preserve coronary perfusion and maximize the residual effective orifice area. Commissural alignment was also deliberately pursued, since the cumulative overlap of three valve frames can make future coronary reaccess extremely difficult. In patients with severe coronary disease, this issue is especially important because even if the immediate procedure is successful, later coronary interventions may become technically hazardous.

Although coronary obstruction is a major concern in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures, no coronary protection strategy or BASILICA was used here. That choice reflected the CT-based assessment that coronary clearance was acceptable, the planned high implantation strategy, and the observation during predilation that coronary flow remained unobstructed. In other words, the team judged that anatomy and implant technique reduced the predicted obstruction risk enough to proceed without prophylactic leaflet modification or coronary wiring. This is an important principle in serial redo TAVI: adjunctive protection is reserved for patients whose CT anatomy suggests truly high obstruction risk, whereas careful positioning may be sufficient when the anatomy is favorable enough.

After deployment of the Acurate neo2 valve, post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon was performed under rapid pacing to improve frame expansion and optimize leaflet opening within the prior prosthetic frames. In triple-stented anatomy, post-dilation can be particularly important because incomplete expansion may leave significant residual obstruction even when the valve appears well seated fluoroscopically. The final aortogram confirmed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and absence of both transvalvular and paravalvular regurgitation. The procedural endpoints therefore focused on safe implantation, stable valve position, and maintained coronary flow, while formal assessment of procedural success was made using Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 criteria, which provide a standardized framework for judging technical success, hemodynamic performance, and major procedure-related complications.

Even when the immediate implant appears technically successful, serial TAVI procedures carry important anticipated risks: coronary obstruction, vascular injury, embolization, residual stenosis or regurgitation, and increasingly difficult future coronary access. This case illustrates how CT-guided planning, deliberate implantation depth, commissural alignment, and careful intraprocedural hemodynamic testing can make a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach feasible in a patient who would otherwise have had extremely limited treatment options.
