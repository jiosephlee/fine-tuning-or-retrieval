Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 5: ## Results, Hemodynamic Interpretation, and Lifetime Management Lessons

The immediate postprocedural echocardiogram showed a valve that was mechanically in place and functioning in a basic sense: the new leaflets were mobile, the valve opened, and there was no residual aortic regurgitation. However, the hemodynamics were still poor, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, a calculated aortic valve area of 0.9 cm², an indexed valve area of 0.4 cm²/m², and a Doppler velocity index of 0.24. In a patient with three overlapping transcatheter frames, these findings are best interpreted as severe prosthesis-patient mismatch rather than outright procedural failure. The valve was sealed and not leaking, but the effective opening was too small for the patient’s body size and flow requirements.

This distinction is important for trainees: a high gradient after valve-in-valve therapy does not automatically mean the procedure has failed. Gradients are influenced by valve expansion, frame constraints, loading conditions, stroke volume, and the residual internal diameter created by the previous prostheses. In nested transcatheter valves, especially with multiple layers, the residual orifice can remain markedly restricted even when leaflet motion appears acceptable. An indexed effective orifice area below 0.65 cm²/m² and a DVI below 0.25 support severe obstruction; in this case, those numbers fit the diagnosis of severe PPM immediately after the third implantation.

The later improvement in hemodynamics illustrates why serial assessment matters. Over the following month, the mean gradient fell from 67 to 27 mm Hg and the valve area increased to 1.1 cm², with no recurrent regurgitation. This improvement is consistent with the mechanisms described in the report: better frame expansion after post-dilation, improved leaflet opening, more favorable flow dynamics, and the effects of left ventricular remodeling as the circulation adapted to the new valve. In other words, an early postprocedural gradient can overestimate the final steady-state result in complex valve-in-valve settings, particularly when the valve is still settling within a constrained frame.

At 3.5 months, the patient remained asymptomatic, and by 1 year he was still clinically stable and active, without dyspnea, heart failure, regurgitation, or evidence of recurrent endocarditis. Left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved at 65%, and the mean gradient remained stable at 26 mm Hg with a valve area of 1.1 cm². Although the prosthesis did not normalize completely, the durable reduction in symptoms and the stable echocardiographic profile indicate a meaningful and sustained clinical benefit from the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy.

This case also shows how follow-up after triple transcatheter reintervention should be structured. Serial transthoracic echocardiography is needed to track gradients, valve area, regurgitation, Doppler velocity index, and ventricular remodeling over time rather than relying on the immediate postimplant result alone. Clinical surveillance should include symptom review for dyspnea, angina, syncope, and heart failure, along with infection surveillance because of the prior episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis. Given the patient’s extensive coronary disease and the triple-frame anatomy, follow-up should also preserve awareness of coronary access, since any future coronary intervention may be technically difficult.

The broader lesson is that transcatheter reintervention after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis can be considered when infection has cleared and surgery is prohibitive, but only after careful confirmation that there is no active endocarditis. In this case, repeated cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and CT supported eradication of infection, allowing the heart team to focus on anatomy, hemodynamic severity, and operative risk. This is a lifetime-management problem, not a single-procedure problem: the first valve choice, the orientation of commissures, and the preservation of coronary access all affect what can be done later.

The case also highlights how limited future rescue options become once a third prosthesis has been implanted. Surgical explantation would be technically hazardous, especially with deep frame incorporation into the aortic root. Techniques such as valve fracture or leaflet-laceration strategies may be considered in selected settings, but their role in triple-stented anatomy remains uncertain and is not well established. For that reason, the procedural plan for the third valve had to balance immediate hemodynamic improvement against the reality that future interventions may be extremely constrained.

For trainees, the main teaching points are straightforward. Reintervention strategy in structural valve disease must be driven by anatomy, imaging, and heart-team judgment, not by symptoms alone. CT-based planning is essential to assess coronary risk, frame interaction, and the feasibility of implantation depth and commissural alignment. Finally, this case underscores the principle of lifetime management in TAVI: as patients live longer after their first transcatheter valve, each subsequent procedure becomes more complex, and each decision should preserve as many future options as possible.
