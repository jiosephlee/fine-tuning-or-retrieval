Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 1: ## Foundations: Severe Aortic Stenosis, TAVI Durability, and the Challenge of Serial Redo Procedures

Severe aortic stenosis is a fixed outflow obstruction that forces the left ventricle to generate progressively higher systolic pressure to maintain forward flow. Over time, this pressure overload leads to concentric hypertrophy, rising myocardial oxygen demand, reduced coronary reserve, and eventual decompensation with dyspnoea, angina, presyncope, or heart failure. In the present case, the patient’s exertional breathlessness, chest discomfort, and declining ejection fraction are typical consequences of advanced prosthetic aortic valve obstruction: once the valve area becomes critically small, stroke volume falls and symptoms can worsen quickly. Valve replacement is therefore the definitive treatment, because medical therapy cannot remove the fixed obstruction.

TAVI has become established because it provides effective relief of aortic stenosis without the trauma of open surgery. Initially reserved for patients at prohibitive or high surgical risk, it is now used across a broader risk spectrum as device profiles, imaging, and procedural techniques have improved. That expansion matters for this case because it has created a growing population of patients living long enough to experience structural valve deterioration and require repeat interventions. What was once a rare “rescue” therapy is increasingly becoming part of a lifetime treatment pathway.

The prostheses used in this patient illustrate why valve design matters in redo planning. The original Lotus valve was a mechanically expanded, fully repositionable and retrievable device with a sealing mechanism that helped limit paravalvular leak, but it also created a constrained internal geometry. The second valve, Evolut, is a self-expanding nitinol prosthesis with supra-annular leaflets and generally favourable haemodynamics, especially in smaller annuli, but it has a tall frame that can complicate future coronary access. The third valve, Acurate neo2, was chosen because its supra-annular leaflet position, relatively low frame height, and open upper-cell design can help preserve orifice area and facilitate commissural alignment. In a triple-valve anatomy, these architectural details become central to whether a third transcatheter implant is feasible and what haemodynamic result can be expected.

Transcatheter bioprosthetic valves can fail through several overlapping mechanisms. Structural degeneration may involve leaflet fibrosis, thickening, calcification, or tearing, leading to progressive stenosis, regurgitation, or both. In a layered valve system, additional contributors include incomplete expansion of one or more frames, inward leaflet folding, and restricted leaflet motion caused by the geometry of the prior prosthesis. In this case, the patient developed severe recurrent obstruction and later mixed stenosis-regurgitation, which is a common way that failing transcatheter valves declare themselves. The episode of infective endocarditis likely accelerated this process by damaging leaflet tissue and promoting inflammation and scarring, even after the infection itself had been treated.

When a transcatheter valve fails, redo strategies can include TAV-in-TAV, in which a second valve is implanted inside the first. This approach can restore leaflet function and improve haemodynamics while avoiding surgical explantation. A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration is much rarer and technically more demanding because each added frame further narrows the residual lumen and changes the geometry of the aortic root. The effective orifice area can become critically small, and the risk of prosthesis-patient mismatch rises as the combined frame layers obstruct flow. This explains why the patient initially had severe residual gradients after the third implant and why careful valve selection, implantation depth, and post-dilatation were needed to improve final haemodynamics.

Several anatomic hazards are amplified in serial redo procedures. Coronary obstruction is a major concern because displaced leaflets and overlapping frames can block the coronary ostia or create a “neo-skirt” that sits near the coronaries. Commissural misalignment can further impair future coronary cannulation even if immediate flow is preserved. Preprocedural CT is therefore essential: it helps define coronary heights, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances, frame interaction, and the likely position of the neo-skirt. In this case, CT-supported planning allowed the team to proceed without coronary protection, but only after confirming that the anticipated implant position would preserve coronary perfusion and leave some possibility of future access.

A history of prosthetic valve endocarditis adds another layer of complexity. Active PVE is diagnosed by a combination of clinical features, blood cultures, and imaging evidence such as vegetations, abscesses, or paravalvular complications. In active infection, surgery is often preferred when there is heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or destructive complications. Healed infection is different: once the patient has completed targeted antibiotics, blood cultures are negative, and TEE or CT shows no vegetations or perivalvular extension, the problem is no longer active infection but residual structural valve dysfunction. That distinction was crucial in this case, because the third TAVI was undertaken only after infection had been treated and imaging confirmed resolution, allowing the team to focus on anatomy and haemodynamics rather than uncontrolled sepsis.

The broader lesson is one of lifetime management. The first transcatheter valve should not be viewed as an isolated procedure but as the first step in a long sequence of possible interventions. In patients with limited surgical reserve and expected long-term survival, the heart team must consider not only the immediate result but also how a future redo might be performed, whether coronary access can be preserved, and how much effective orifice area will remain after one or more additional implants. This case shows that careful CT-guided planning and deliberate strategy can make even a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV feasible, but it also highlights how quickly the technical and haemodynamic stakes rise with each added valve frame.
