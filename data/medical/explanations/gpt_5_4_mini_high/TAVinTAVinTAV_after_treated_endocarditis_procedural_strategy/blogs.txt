Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# TAVI Is a Lifetime Strategy: Planning the Third Valve at the First Implant

## The surprising part of this case

A man in his late 70s had a **third transcatheter aortic valve implanted inside two prior transcatheter valves** after a treated episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis. That alone makes the case memorable. But the real teaching point is bigger: this wasn’t just a technical feat, it was a **lifetime-management problem**.

He had a Lotus valve in 2014, a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut in 2022, then developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis with restenosis. After antimicrobial treatment and confirmation that the infection had resolved, he underwent a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** in 2024 with an Acurate neo2. At 1 year, he remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function.

That’s the headline: **serial redo TAVI can be feasible, but only if you plan like the next procedure matters from day one.**

## Why this case matters now

We are no longer doing TAVI in a world where the first procedure is likely the last. Indications have expanded, and patients are living long enough to develop:

- structural valve degeneration
- prosthesis-patient mismatch
- coronary access problems
- valve-in-valve reintervention
- complex anatomy from overlapping frames

So the question is no longer simply, “Can I implant this valve today?”  
It’s also, “What will this valve do to the patient’s next 10 years?”

That shift changes how we think about:

- valve platform selection
- implant depth
- commissural alignment
- residual effective orifice area
- future coronary access
- the geometry of redo procedures

## What happened in this patient

This patient had substantial cardiovascular disease burden: hypertension, three-vessel CAD with multiple prior PCIs, complete heart block with pacemaker, diabetes, COPD, and recurrent GI bleeding. He was already a high-risk structural heart patient before the valve story got complicated.

In 2014, he underwent implantation of a **27 mm Lotus valve** for severe aortic stenosis. By early 2022, that prosthesis had failed with severe stenosis and a mean gradient of **83 mm Hg**, along with a drop in LVEF to **42%**. He then underwent a **TAV-in-TAV** with a **23 mm Evolut** in May 2022.

That procedure was not smooth—he developed a femoral pseudoaneurysm requiring open vascular surgery—but the early hemodynamic response was good, with LVEF improving back to **54%**.

Then came the infectious complication. Three months later he re-presented with fever and worsening heart failure symptoms. Blood cultures grew **Enterococcus faecalis**, and he was diagnosed with prosthetic valve endocarditis. He completed targeted therapy with **6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based treatment**, followed by oral therapy per infectious diseases guidance. Importantly, by the time the third valve was being considered, there was **no evidence of active infection**: blood cultures were negative, TEE was reassuring, and MSCT showed no active endocarditis or paravalvular complication.

By 2024, the valve had failed again, now with severe stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. TTE showed:

- mean gradient **49 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.5 cm²**
- LVEF recovered to **66%**

That’s the setup for the third intervention.

## The key preprocedural question: is a third TAVI even feasible?

This is where the case becomes especially instructive. The team did not simply decide “repeat TAVI” and move on. They used **multislice CT** to ask the questions that matter in serial valve procedures:

- What does the coronary anatomy look like?
- How tall are the sinuses?
- What is the valve-to-coronary distance?
- How are the previous frames interacting?
- Will the anticipated neo-skirt threaten coronary flow?
- Can the new valve be implanted high enough to preserve coronary access?

CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis**, confirming severe narrowing and helping guide the strategy. This is the kind of case where CT is not just a nice add-on; it is the roadmap.

Because CT suggested acceptable coronary clearance, the team proceeded **without coronary protection**. That’s a major point: in complex redo TAVI, coronary protection should be considered, but it doesn’t have to be reflexive if the anatomy is favorable and carefully assessed.

## How the procedure was actually done

The third procedure was performed under local anesthesia with left radial and left femoral arterial access. After aortography confirmed severe regurgitation, the team:

- crossed the valve with a Safari wire
- performed balloon pre-dilation under rapid pacing
- confirmed no coronary compromise during the procedure
- implanted an **Acurate neo2 size S**
- deliberately aligned the valve with the Evolut leaflets based on CT planning
- performed post-dilation to optimize expansion

The deployment strategy matters here. The valve was implanted **high enough to sit at the level of the Evolut leaflets**, which the authors used to help pin the prior leaflets against the frame and reduce further loss of effective orifice area. The goal was not just “put a valve in the valve,” but to do so in a way that preserved flow and future options.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen for a reason. In this anatomy, its:

- supra-annular leaflet position
- relatively low frame height
- favorable hemodynamics in narrow anatomy
- commissural alignment features

made it a sensible choice for a triple-stented situation.

## The hemodynamics were not perfect immediately — and that’s important

Right after the procedure, TTE showed:

- no aortic regurgitation
- mobile leaflets
- but a mean gradient of **67 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.9 cm²**
- DVI **0.24**

That sounds underwhelming at first glance, and it’s worth resisting the temptation to equate “successful deployment” with “excellent immediate gradient.” In layered valve anatomy, especially with pre-existing frame constraints, early hemodynamics can be misleading.

By 1 month, things had improved:

- mean gradient **27 mm Hg**
- AVA **1.1 cm²**
- no regurgitation
- reverse remodeling of the LV

At 1 year, the patient remained asymptomatic and active, with:

- mean gradient **26 mm Hg**
- AVA **1.1 cm²**
- LVEF **65%**
- no recurrent endocarditis

So this was not a perfect-pressure-gradient story. It was a **durable clinical success** in a patient with very limited options.

## What this teaches us about lifetime valve planning

This is the core FOAM lesson: **the first TAVI should be planned as the first move in a long game.**

Every valve platform creates a different future. Frame height, commissural orientation, leaflet position, and valve expansion all influence what happens when the valve eventually degenerates. In a patient who may live long enough to need redo intervention, you want to preserve:

- coronary access
- room for a future valve
- enough effective orifice area to avoid severe PPM
- a landing zone that allows another intervention if needed

That means thinking about redo feasibility at the index procedure, not as an afterthought.

In this case, the team also highlighted the importance of **commissural alignment**. That may sound like a technical detail, but in a patient with CAD and prior PCI, it can be the difference between manageable future coronary access and a nightmare.

## Endocarditis changes the rules — but not forever

This case should not be read as “TAVI is the answer to prosthetic valve endocarditis.” It isn’t.

Current guidance still generally favors surgery for prosthetic valve endocarditis, especially when infection is uncontrolled or heart failure is present. But once infection has been treated, blood cultures are negative, and imaging shows no active endocarditis, the problem becomes one of **structural valve failure in a high-risk patient**.

That distinction matters.

Here, the patient had:

- completed antimicrobial therapy
- no suppressive antibiotics ongoing
- negative follow-up assessment for active infection
- prohibitive surgical risk
- complex overlapping transcatheter frames

Given that combination, the heart team chose a transcatheter solution rather than a high-risk root/ascending aorta operation.

## Practical pearls from this case

- **TAVI is a lifetime strategy, not a one-time fix.** Plan the first valve with redo anatomy in mind.
- **CT is essential before serial redo TAVI.** Evaluate coronary height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and frame interaction.
- **Commissural alignment matters.** It affects future coronary access and reintervention options.
- **After treated endocarditis, the decision framework changes.** If there’s no active infection, the case becomes about anatomy, hemodynamics, and operative risk.
- **Don’t ignore the “ugly” immediate gradient.** In layered valve anatomy, the early post-procedure hemodynamic picture may improve over time.
- **Triple TAVI is possible, but it should be intentional.** These cases succeed because of planning, not improvisation.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that structural heart intervention is moving from “procedure” to **longitudinal management**. As TAVI expands to younger and longer-living patients, the first implant should be chosen with the next implant in mind.

The best TAVI is not always the one that looks easiest today.  
Sometimes it’s the one that leaves the most options tomorrow.



# CT Before the Third Valve: Imaging the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Patient

## Why this case is worth your attention

The striking thing about this case is not just that a third transcatheter aortic valve was implanted — it’s that the team did it after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, in a patient who already had two overlapping transcatheter prostheses.

That moves you out of “routine redo TAVR” territory and into lifetime structural heart management. At that point, the key question is no longer “can we put another valve in?” It’s “can CT show us a safe path through the metal, keep the coronaries open, and leave enough effective orifice area to make the result worthwhile?”

This is exactly where preprocedural imaging earns its keep.

## First, prove the infection is quiet

Before anyone starts arguing about valve type, implantation depth, or commissural alignment, the post-endocarditis question has to be answered: is the infection resolved?

In this patient, that workup was reassuring. Blood cultures, TEE, and MSCT showed no evidence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications after completion of targeted therapy for *Enterococcus faecalis* PVE. That matters because once active infection is off the table, the remaining problem is mechanical and anatomical — and that is where CT becomes the decision-maker.

## What CT had to answer

In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV patient, CT is not just “anatomy review.” It is a feasibility study.

For this case, the CT assessment focused on the things that actually change the procedure:

- coronary ostial height
- sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- valve-to-coronary distance
- interaction between the existing valve frames
- anticipated neo-skirt position
- residual lumen diameter inside the prior prosthesis

That last point is easy to underestimate. The MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the existing Evolut frame. That’s the kind of number that tells you there is very little room left for error — and even less room left for another valve to expand without worsening obstruction or patient-prosthesis mismatch.

In layered transcatheter valves, CT is doing two jobs at once:

1. **Telling you whether the coronaries are at risk**
2. **Telling you whether the next valve can actually function**

## The CT measurements that matter most

### Coronary ostial height and sinus size

These are the classic obstruction variables. If the coronaries are low and the sinuses are tight, displaced leaflets or a bulky neo-skirt can compromise flow. In redo TAVR, this becomes even more important because you are not just displacing native leaflets — you’re dealing with prior prosthetic frames and pinned leaflets.

### Valve-to-coronary distance

This is one of the most practical numbers in the whole workup. It gives you a sense of how much space you have between the planned prosthesis position and the coronary ostia.

In this case, the CT assessment of coronary clearance helped the team decide that the procedure could be performed **without coronary protection**. That is not a casual decision in a triple-layer anatomy; it is a CT-driven one.

### Anticipated neo-skirt

The neo-skirt is easy to ignore until it becomes the reason you can’t see — or reach — the coronaries later. In layered valves, the skirt effect can be just as important as the leaflets. If the anticipated skirt position would sit too close to the coronary ostia, you may be creating a future access problem even if the acute procedure goes well.

### Frame interaction and residual lumen

Multiple overlapping frames can behave like a rigid tunnel. CT helps you understand how the new valve will sit inside the old ones, whether the prior frame will constrain expansion, and where the final functional orifice is likely to be.

That matters because the goal is not simply “a valve is in.” The goal is “a valve is in, it opens, the coronaries are safe, and the patient has enough valve area to feel better.”

## How CT changed the procedural plan

Once CT suggested that coronary flow could be preserved, the team could choose a strategy rather than improvise one.

A self-expanding **Acurate neo2** valve was selected, specifically because its design offered:

- supra-annular leaflets
- a relatively low frame height
- favorable hemodynamics in a narrow anatomy

Just as importantly, the team used CT modeling to plan a **high implantation strategy**. The third valve was positioned at the level of the Evolut leaflets, with the idea of fixing the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame and avoiding further loss of effective orifice area.

That is the key concept in these cases: the implant depth is not just about anchoring. It is about geometry.

High implantation in this case aimed to:

- reduce further inward leaflet folding
- minimize the neo-skirt below the coronaries
- preserve future coronary access
- optimize the residual orifice in a very constrained tunnel

Commissural alignment also mattered. Even if you cannot perfect it, thinking about commissural position in a triple-frame anatomy is a sign you are planning for the patient’s next procedure, not just the current one.

And notably, this was done **without coronary protection**, because CT had already answered the question that matters most: is the coronary escape route still open?

## What happened after deployment

This case is also a good reminder that successful redo TAVI does not always look perfect on the first post-op echo.

Immediately after the third valve, there was no regurgitation, but the mean gradient was still high at 67 mm Hg, with severe patient-prosthesis mismatch. That is not surprising in a triple-layer anatomy with a small residual lumen.

The important part is what happened next:

- at 1 month, the mean gradient fell to 27 mm Hg and AVA improved to 1.1 cm²
- at 1 year, the gradient remained 26 mm Hg, AVA remained 1.1 cm², and EF was 65%
- the patient was asymptomatic, active, and had no recurrent endocarditis

So CT planning did not deliver a perfect valve. It delivered a feasible, safe, durable solution in a patient with extremely limited options.

That is a win.

## Practical pearls from this case

- In redo TAVR, CT is not an accessory test — it is the roadmap.
- Coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, neo-skirt position, and frame interaction are the measurements that actually change management.
- A small residual lumen inside the prior valve frame is a warning sign for both patient-prosthesis mismatch and future coronary-access problems.
- High implantation can be a deliberate strategy to preserve coronary flow and maximize the effective orifice area in layered prostheses.
- If CT shows adequate coronary clearance, it may be reasonable to proceed **without coronary protection** — but that decision should be anatomy-driven, not habit-driven.
- In treated PVE, once infection is confirmed resolved, the remaining decision is usually anatomical and hemodynamic, not infectious.

## Bottom line

This case is a strong reminder that the “third valve” problem is really a CT problem.

Before you deploy another prosthesis inside two old ones, the imaging questions are simple but unforgiving: Where are the coronaries? How big is the sinus? How much room is left inside the frame? Where will the skirt land? What will the commissures do to future access?

If you can answer those questions on CT, you can sometimes do what sounds almost impossible: safely place a third transcatheter valve, preserve coronary flow, and buy the patient another good year — or more.



# Redo TAVI After Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis: When Is Infection Truly “Over”?

## Why this case is worth your attention

A man in his late 70s ended up with a valve inside a valve inside a valve — after a treated episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis. That alone makes the case unusual. What makes it really interesting is the decision point: once the infection had been treated and no longer looked active, the team still had to decide whether the prior endocarditis should block another transcatheter intervention.

Short answer: not automatically.

This case is a good reminder that prosthetic valve endocarditis should raise the bar for any reintervention, but it does not necessarily shut the door on redo TAVI in a prohibitive-risk patient with resolved infection, hostile anatomy, and no good surgical escape route.

## The setup: why this patient was so challenging

The patient had a long cardiovascular history: hypertension, three-vessel CAD with multiple PCI procedures, a pacemaker for complete heart block, diabetes, COPD, and recurrent GI bleeding. He initially received a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014 for severe aortic stenosis. By 2022, that valve had degenerated severely, with a mean gradient up to 83 mm Hg and EF down to 42%, so he underwent TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut.

That 2022 procedure helped his LV function, but it came with a vascular complication: femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm requiring open vascular surgery.

Then came the major curveball. In September 2022 he re-presented with fever and worsening heart failure. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*, TTE showed severe restenosis, and he was diagnosed with prosthetic valve endocarditis. He completed 6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy, followed by oral therapy per ID recommendations. By the time of the third TAVI, antimicrobial treatment was complete and he was not on suppressive antibiotics.

So the team was not dealing with active infection. They were dealing with the aftermath of treated PVE plus recurrent severe prosthetic dysfunction.

## When is endocarditis “over” enough to proceed?

This is the real clinical question. In this case, the team did not rely on a single reassuring data point. They built a case that infection had resolved using multiple layers of evidence:

- completion of targeted antimicrobial therapy
- repeated blood cultures without evidence of ongoing bacteremia
- TEE showing no active endocarditis or paravalvular complications
- MSCT without evidence of active endocarditis
- no clinical signs of ongoing infection

That matters, because prior PVE should influence the threshold for redo intervention, but it should not function as an automatic lifetime contraindication if there is no active infection and the valve is failing again.

The case also reflects current guideline tension: surgery remains the preferred strategy for PVE, especially when there is heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But guidelines also emphasize individualization. Here, the infection was treated, and the anatomy and surgical risk were the real decision-makers.

## Why surgery was not the easy answer

This patient was not just “high risk.” He was extremely high risk.

His EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, and he had COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding, diffuse CAD, and two previously implanted transcatheter valves with deep stent integration into the aortic wall. The authors note that open surgery would likely have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement.

That’s the kind of scenario where the heart team has to ask a practical question: if surgery is technically possible, is it actually the best option for this patient? In this case, the answer favored a third transcatheter strategy.

## CT was not a nice-to-have; it was the roadmap

Redo TAVI gets progressively more dangerous when you start stacking valve frames. The big concerns are coronary obstruction, future coronary access, and patient-prosthesis mismatch from a shrinking effective orifice.

Preprocedural MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis. The team used CT to evaluate:

- coronary ostial height
- sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- valve-to-coronary distance
- interaction between the prior valve frames
- the anticipated neo-skirt and its relationship to the coronaries

This CT planning is what allowed them to proceed without coronary protection. It also influenced valve choice, implant depth, and the strategy of high implantation to preserve coronary flow and future coronary access.

That’s the big lesson here: in serial transcatheter reinterventions, “anatomy” is not a background detail. It is the procedure.

## The procedural strategy: high, aligned, and deliberate

The third TAVI was done under local anesthesia with left radial and left femoral access. Aortography confirmed severe regurgitation. The team pre-dilated with a 22/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing at 180 bpm, then implanted an Acurate neo2 size S.

The device was intentionally aligned with the Evolut leaflets, based on CT modeling, to maximize effective orifice area and reduce patient-prosthesis mismatch. Post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon followed.

A few technical ideas here are worth highlighting. The Acurate neo2 was selected because of its supra-annular leaflet position, low frame height, and favorable hemodynamics in narrow anatomies. Commissural alignment was also part of the plan, because preserving coronary access matters even if the patient may never need another coronary intervention. In a triple-stented anatomy, planning for the next procedure starts with this one.

The team’s strategy also aimed to keep the neo-skirt from sitting too low relative to the coronaries. In practical terms: implant high enough to protect coronary flow, but still secure enough to function well.

## The hemodynamics tell an important story

Immediately after implantation, the valve looked good on echo in terms of leaflet motion and regurgitation, but the gradients were still high: mean gradient 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm², DVI 0.24. That looked like severe patient-prosthesis mismatch.

But the story did not end there.

At 1 month, the mean gradient had fallen to 27 mm Hg and AVA improved to 1.1 cm², with LV remodeling and no regurgitation. At 1 year, the patient was asymptomatic, active, with mean gradient 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², and EF 65%. No recurrent endocarditis was seen.

So this was not just a technically successful implant; it was a durable clinical win in a patient with very limited options.

## What should we take from this?

Prior prosthetic valve endocarditis is a major warning flag, but it is not always a permanent stop sign.

What this case really teaches is that redo TAVI after treated PVE is a multidisciplinary, imaging-driven decision. If infection is truly quiescent, surgical risk is prohibitive, and CT shows a feasible path with acceptable coronary safety, a third transcatheter valve can be a reasonable option.

## Take-home points

- Prior PVE should raise caution, not automatically exclude redo TAVI.
- “Infection over” in this case meant: completed antibiotics, negative repeat cultures, and no active disease on TEE/MSCT.
- In stacked transcatheter valves, CT planning is the procedure before the procedure.
- Coronary obstruction and future coronary access must be explicitly modeled, not hand-waved.
- Early high gradients do not always equal failure; follow-up hemodynamics matter.
- In prohibitive-risk patients, a heart-team, anatomy-first approach can make otherwise impossible cases feasible.



# Why Was the Gradient 67 mm Hg Right After TAVI? Interpreting Hemodynamics in Triple-Valve Anatomy

## The part that should make you stop and think

A mean gradient of 67 mm Hg immediately after TAVI is the kind of number that makes everyone in the room sit up straight. But in this case, the story was not “the valve failed.” The valve was deployed successfully, leaflet motion looked good, there was no regurgitation, and this was a patient with **triple transcatheter valve anatomy** after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis.

Even more interesting: the gradient was **67 mm Hg right after the procedure**, then fell to **27 mm Hg at 1 month**, and remained **26 mm Hg at 1 year** with the patient asymptomatic and EF preserved at 65%.

So the real question is not just, “Why was the gradient high?” It’s, “What does a high early gradient mean in a layered prosthetic valve system, and how should we interpret it without overcalling failure?”

## This was not a native valve problem

This patient had a long structural heart timeline:

- 2014: Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis
- 2022: TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve
- Later in 2022: Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, treated successfully
- 2024: Third transcatheter valve, an Acurate neo2, implanted inside the prior two frames

By June 2024, CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis**. That number matters. In a native aortic valve, we think about annular sizing, calcium, and coronary height. In **triple-valve anatomy**, the limiting structure is often the functional lumen created by the older valve frames and leaflets.

That means the new valve is not expanding into a normal annulus. It is trying to work inside a rigid, pre-shaped, already-narrowed metal scaffold. In that setting, a high residual gradient may reflect:

- true **prosthesis-patient mismatch**
- **constrained frame expansion**
- persistent limitation from the old valve geometry
- altered flow conditions after the procedure
- less commonly, measurement artifact

In this case, the echo numbers were not just “a weird Doppler view.” The post-op TTE showed:

- mean gradient **67 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.9 cm²** indexed AVA **0.4 cm²/m²**
- DVI **0.24**
- no aortic regurgitation

That combination is important. A high gradient alone can mislead you. A high gradient **plus** small calculated AVA **plus** low DVI is much more convincing for a truly small functional orifice.

## When the gradient is high, don’t ask only “Is the valve seated?” Ask “What is limiting flow?”

This is the main teaching point.

In redo TAVI, especially valve-in-valve and valve-in-valve-in-valve cases, the hemodynamic result depends on more than leaflet function. You can have a technically successful implant with:

- good positioning
- mobile leaflets
- no paravalvular leak
- preserved coronary flow

…and still end up with a high gradient because the **effective orifice area is constrained by the surrounding frame geometry**.

That’s why the team’s strategy here mattered. They intentionally chose an **Acurate neo2** because of its supra-annular leaflet position and more favorable hemodynamics in narrow anatomy. They also aimed for **high implantation**, aligning the new valve with the Evolut leaflets to keep the old leaflets pinned out of the way and avoid further loss of functional area. Balloon post-dilation was then used to optimize expansion.

That combination tells you the team was not passively accepting the anatomy. They were trying to maximize the final orifice within a very unfavorable mechanical setup.

## Why can the gradient improve later?

This is the part that makes the case educational.

The immediate post-procedure echo looked bad, but the patient did not have immediate regurgitation or valve instability. Over the following weeks, the hemodynamics improved substantially.

Likely reasons, grounded in the case:

1. **Post-dilation mattered**
   - The procedure included balloon post-dilation.
   - The report suggests high-pressure ballooning probably contributed to further radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame.

2. **The frame may not be fully “settled” immediately**
   - In a constrained multi-frame system, the final functional geometry may evolve after the procedure.
   - Small changes in radial expansion can have a real effect on effective orifice area.

3. **Leaflet and flow dynamics can stabilize**
   - Once the valve is seated and early post-procedural conditions normalize, the flow profile can improve.

4. **The ventricle can remodel**
   - At 1 month, the patient had lower LV volumes and better gradients.
   - That suggests the hemodynamic burden was easing, not worsening.

The key is that the early gradient did **not** behave like an unstable or failing prosthesis. It behaved like a valve that was functioning, but inside a severely constrained scaffold, with some room for hemodynamic improvement over time.

## Echo pitfalls after redo TAVI

This case is also a reminder not to overread a single early postoperative echo in isolation.

A few practical issues come up in redo valve anatomy:

- **Gradient is flow-dependent**
  - A valve can look worse at higher forward flow.
  - The post-procedure hemodynamic state is not the same as the pre-procedure state.

- **Doppler is harder in stacked prostheses**
  - Multiple frames and shadowing can make interrogation trickier.
  - You need to be sure you’re sampling the highest velocity jet.

- **AVA calculation can be less forgiving**
  - Continuity equation measurements depend on accurate LVOT data.
  - In prosthetic anatomy, the LVOT geometry may not behave like native anatomy.

- **Use more than one metric**
  - Gradient, DVI, AVA, regurgitation, leaflet motion, and imaging context all matter.
  - In this case, the high gradient was supported by the low DVI and small AVA, so it was real — but not necessarily permanent.

## What this case teaches about lifetime valve planning

This is not just a story about one high gradient. It’s a lifetime management story.

The third valve was only possible because:

- infection had been treated and cleared
- TEE and CT showed no active endocarditis
- surgical risk was prohibitive
- CT suggested coronary clearance and feasible implantation strategy

That preprocedural CT was doing a lot of work here. It was not just about sizing the valve. It was about deciding whether a third transcatheter layer could be tolerated at all, whether coronary flow could be preserved, and how to align the valve to avoid painting the patient into a corner for future coronary access.

## Bottom line

This case is a nice reminder that “successful deployment” and “good hemodynamics” are related, but not identical.

A gradient of 67 mm Hg right after TAVI in triple-valve anatomy can reflect a mix of true fixed obstruction, prosthesis-patient mismatch, constrained frame expansion, and evolving post-procedural flow conditions. In the right clinical context — no active infection, good valve position, no regurgitation, CT-guided planning, and subsequent gradient improvement — it may be a marker of severe anatomic challenge rather than procedural failure.

## Take-home points

- A very high early post-TAVI gradient does **not** automatically mean the valve is failing.
- In valve-in-valve-in-valve anatomy, think about **frame constraint and functional orifice area**, not just leaflet motion.
- If the gradient is high, check whether the data support **real obstruction**: AVA, DVI, regurgitation, and imaging context all matter.
- **CT-guided planning** is essential in complex redo TAVI, especially when coronary access and future interventions are on the line.
- Some gradients improve after **post-dilation, frame expansion, and hemodynamic remodeling** — so repeat imaging matters.



# Choosing the Valve for a Valve-in-Valve-in-Valve: Why Acurate neo2 Made Sense Here

## Why this case is worth your attention

A third transcatheter aortic valve in the same patient is already a head-turner. Add a prior episode of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, a narrow triple-stented aortic root, severe prosthesis-patient mismatch risk, and the ever-present worry about coronary obstruction — and you’ve got a case that forces you to think beyond “can we place a valve?” toward “which valve, where exactly, and what does this do to future options?”

That’s the real lesson here. In serial redo TAVI, device selection is not just about getting something to sit in the annulus. It’s about preserving flow, preserving coronary access, and buying the patient as much hemodynamic runway as possible.

## The problem was not just valve failure — it was space failure

This patient had already accumulated a Lotus valve, then an Evolut valve inside it, and later developed recurrent severe prosthetic dysfunction after treated *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis. By the time of the third procedure, CT showed a minimum lumen diameter of just 13.7 mm in the Evolut frame. That’s a tiny working space for another valve.

In a setting like this, the major procedural hazards are predictable:

- further reduction in effective orifice area
- severe prosthesis-patient mismatch
- coronary obstruction or loss of coronary re-access
- poor leaflet expansion if the new device is forced into an already constrained frame

So the choice of valve has to answer a simple question: **which platform gives the best chance of opening well inside a crowded scaffold?**

## Why Acurate neo2 made sense here

The team chose an **Acurate neo2, size S**, and the rationale is easy to understand if you think in terms of geometry and hemodynamics.

This device was attractive because of:

**Supra-annular leaflet position**  
In a tight redo anatomy, anything that helps move the functional leaflets higher can improve the effective orifice area. Supra-annular leaflet positioning is one of the main reasons self-expanding platforms are often favored in valve-in-valve scenarios.

**Relatively low frame profile**  
In a triple-stented root, frame height matters. A lower-profile design may help reduce the amount of hardware crowding the coronary sinuses and can support a strategy aimed at preserving coronary flow and future access.

**Favorable hemodynamics in narrow anatomies**  
When the residual lumen is already small, the best valve is often the one that can deliver the least additional flow penalty. The team was clearly trying to mitigate the risk of a dead-end hemodynamic result after the third implant.

**Support for commissural alignment**  
Commissural alignment is not just a buzzword here. In a patient with known coronary disease and repeated transcatheter layers, preserving coronary access is part of lifetime planning. Acurate neo2 offered a design that supported this strategy.

The key point is that this was not a random platform choice. It was a deliberate effort to match device geometry to anatomy.

## Why implantation height mattered just as much as the valve

A valve can be the “right” one and still perform badly if it’s implanted poorly.

In this case, the team used a **high implantation strategy**, aligning the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut leaflets based on CT modeling. That choice had two big goals:

1. **Maximize effective orifice area**
2. **Minimize the neo-skirt burden below the coronaries**

By implanting high, the team aimed to pin the pre-existing leaflets against the frame rather than let them fold inward and steal lumen. That’s the kind of subtle, anatomy-driven maneuver that can make the difference between “technically successful” and “hemodynamically acceptable.”

This is also why redo TAVI planning can’t be reduced to fluoroscopy alone. The landing zone is a 3D problem, and in layered valves, the interaction between frames matters just as much as the annulus itself.

## What CT had to answer before the team could proceed

The preprocedural MSCT was doing a lot of work here. It wasn’t just confirming that the patient had a small lumen — it was helping answer whether the procedure was even worth attempting.

The CT assessment focused on:

- coronary ostial height
- sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- valve-to-coronary distance
- interaction between the existing valve frames
- anticipated relationship between the new valve’s neo-skirt and the coronaries

That evaluation allowed the operators to proceed **without coronary protection**, which is not something you’d do casually in a triple-layered root. The imaging suggested that the planned strategy could preserve coronary flow.

One important nuance: they did **not** use dedicated 3D expansion software for full modeling. Even so, the CT data were sufficient to guide a strategy-based intervention. That’s a useful reminder that good imaging is less about having every toy and more about asking the right questions.

## The hemodynamic tradeoff was real — and expected

The immediate post-procedure echo looked good from a regurgitation standpoint, but the mean gradient was still high at 67 mm Hg, with DVI 0.24 and AVA 0.9 cm². That’s severe PPM territory.

This is the uncomfortable truth about TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV: sometimes the best possible result is still not “normal.” The aim is often to convert catastrophic obstruction into acceptable function.

And that’s exactly what happened here. Over time, gradients improved to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and 26 mm Hg at 1 year, with AVA 1.1 cm² and preserved EF. The patient remained asymptomatic and free of recurrent infection.

So even though the immediate hemodynamics weren’t perfect, the longer-term result was clinically meaningful.

## A few practical pearls from this case

The procedural details matter because they show how this was actually made to work:

- local anesthesia, left radial plus left femoral access
- balloon pre-dilation under rapid pacing
- self-expanding valve deployment
- post-dilation to optimize expansion
- final aortography confirming no regurgitation and preserved coronary perfusion

The post-dilation deserves attention. In a constrained frame, balloon optimization may help achieve more radial expansion and better leaflet motion. In this case, it likely contributed to the later improvement in transvalvular gradient.

That said, there was **no intentional frame fracture**. The benefit came from strategy and optimization, not from aggressive structural disruption.

## What about the prior endocarditis?

This is important: the case was not about implanting a valve into active infection.

The patient had completed targeted antimicrobial therapy for *E. faecalis* PVE, had no suppressive antibiotics ongoing, and had repeat blood cultures, TEE, and CT confirming no evidence of active endocarditis. That distinction matters. The decision-making was driven primarily by anatomy, hemodynamics, and prohibitive surgical risk — not by untreated infection.

So the lesson is not “TAVI is fine in endocarditis.” The lesson is that **after infection has been treated and excluded as active, the heart team may still need to solve a structural valve problem using the least bad option available.**

## Bottom line

In a narrow triple-stented aortic root, the “best” valve is the one that gives you the best compromise between expansion, hemodynamics, and coronary safety. Here, Acurate neo2 made sense because it offered supra-annular leaflet function, a low-profile frame, and compatibility with a high implantation strategy designed to preserve effective orifice area and future coronary access.

## Take-home points

- In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, device selection is anatomy-driven, not brand-driven.
- Supra-annular leaflet position and lower frame profile are major advantages in a crowded redo root.
- High implantation can be a deliberate strategy to preserve EOA and coronary access.
- CT planning should answer coronary clearance, frame interaction, and neo-skirt risk before you go to the lab.
- An immediate high gradient after triple valve layering may still be an acceptable tradeoff if symptoms, regurgitation, and long-term valve function improve.
- Treated prosthetic valve endocarditis does not automatically rule out redo transcatheter therapy, but active infection must be excluded first.
