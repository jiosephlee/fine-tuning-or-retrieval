Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Why Was the Gradient 67 mm Hg Right After TAVI? Interpreting Hemodynamics in Triple-Valve Anatomy

## The part that should make you stop and think

A mean gradient of 67 mm Hg immediately after TAVI is the kind of number that makes everyone in the room sit up straight. But in this case, the story was not “the valve failed.” The valve was deployed successfully, leaflet motion looked good, there was no regurgitation, and this was a patient with **triple transcatheter valve anatomy** after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis.

Even more interesting: the gradient was **67 mm Hg right after the procedure**, then fell to **27 mm Hg at 1 month**, and remained **26 mm Hg at 1 year** with the patient asymptomatic and EF preserved at 65%.

So the real question is not just, “Why was the gradient high?” It’s, “What does a high early gradient mean in a layered prosthetic valve system, and how should we interpret it without overcalling failure?”

## This was not a native valve problem

This patient had a long structural heart timeline:

- 2014: Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis
- 2022: TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve
- Later in 2022: Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, treated successfully
- 2024: Third transcatheter valve, an Acurate neo2, implanted inside the prior two frames

By June 2024, CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis**. That number matters. In a native aortic valve, we think about annular sizing, calcium, and coronary height. In **triple-valve anatomy**, the limiting structure is often the functional lumen created by the older valve frames and leaflets.

That means the new valve is not expanding into a normal annulus. It is trying to work inside a rigid, pre-shaped, already-narrowed metal scaffold. In that setting, a high residual gradient may reflect:

- true **prosthesis-patient mismatch**
- **constrained frame expansion**
- persistent limitation from the old valve geometry
- altered flow conditions after the procedure
- less commonly, measurement artifact

In this case, the echo numbers were not just “a weird Doppler view.” The post-op TTE showed:

- mean gradient **67 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.9 cm²** indexed AVA **0.4 cm²/m²**
- DVI **0.24**
- no aortic regurgitation

That combination is important. A high gradient alone can mislead you. A high gradient **plus** small calculated AVA **plus** low DVI is much more convincing for a truly small functional orifice.

## When the gradient is high, don’t ask only “Is the valve seated?” Ask “What is limiting flow?”

This is the main teaching point.

In redo TAVI, especially valve-in-valve and valve-in-valve-in-valve cases, the hemodynamic result depends on more than leaflet function. You can have a technically successful implant with:

- good positioning
- mobile leaflets
- no paravalvular leak
- preserved coronary flow

…and still end up with a high gradient because the **effective orifice area is constrained by the surrounding frame geometry**.

That’s why the team’s strategy here mattered. They intentionally chose an **Acurate neo2** because of its supra-annular leaflet position and more favorable hemodynamics in narrow anatomy. They also aimed for **high implantation**, aligning the new valve with the Evolut leaflets to keep the old leaflets pinned out of the way and avoid further loss of functional area. Balloon post-dilation was then used to optimize expansion.

That combination tells you the team was not passively accepting the anatomy. They were trying to maximize the final orifice within a very unfavorable mechanical setup.

## Why can the gradient improve later?

This is the part that makes the case educational.

The immediate post-procedure echo looked bad, but the patient did not have immediate regurgitation or valve instability. Over the following weeks, the hemodynamics improved substantially.

Likely reasons, grounded in the case:

1. **Post-dilation mattered**
   - The procedure included balloon post-dilation.
   - The report suggests high-pressure ballooning probably contributed to further radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame.

2. **The frame may not be fully “settled” immediately**
   - In a constrained multi-frame system, the final functional geometry may evolve after the procedure.
   - Small changes in radial expansion can have a real effect on effective orifice area.

3. **Leaflet and flow dynamics can stabilize**
   - Once the valve is seated and early post-procedural conditions normalize, the flow profile can improve.

4. **The ventricle can remodel**
   - At 1 month, the patient had lower LV volumes and better gradients.
   - That suggests the hemodynamic burden was easing, not worsening.

The key is that the early gradient did **not** behave like an unstable or failing prosthesis. It behaved like a valve that was functioning, but inside a severely constrained scaffold, with some room for hemodynamic improvement over time.

## Echo pitfalls after redo TAVI

This case is also a reminder not to overread a single early postoperative echo in isolation.

A few practical issues come up in redo valve anatomy:

- **Gradient is flow-dependent**
  - A valve can look worse at higher forward flow.
  - The post-procedure hemodynamic state is not the same as the pre-procedure state.

- **Doppler is harder in stacked prostheses**
  - Multiple frames and shadowing can make interrogation trickier.
  - You need to be sure you’re sampling the highest velocity jet.

- **AVA calculation can be less forgiving**
  - Continuity equation measurements depend on accurate LVOT data.
  - In prosthetic anatomy, the LVOT geometry may not behave like native anatomy.

- **Use more than one metric**
  - Gradient, DVI, AVA, regurgitation, leaflet motion, and imaging context all matter.
  - In this case, the high gradient was supported by the low DVI and small AVA, so it was real — but not necessarily permanent.

## What this case teaches about lifetime valve planning

This is not just a story about one high gradient. It’s a lifetime management story.

The third valve was only possible because:

- infection had been treated and cleared
- TEE and CT showed no active endocarditis
- surgical risk was prohibitive
- CT suggested coronary clearance and feasible implantation strategy

That preprocedural CT was doing a lot of work here. It was not just about sizing the valve. It was about deciding whether a third transcatheter layer could be tolerated at all, whether coronary flow could be preserved, and how to align the valve to avoid painting the patient into a corner for future coronary access.

## Bottom line

This case is a nice reminder that “successful deployment” and “good hemodynamics” are related, but not identical.

A gradient of 67 mm Hg right after TAVI in triple-valve anatomy can reflect a mix of true fixed obstruction, prosthesis-patient mismatch, constrained frame expansion, and evolving post-procedural flow conditions. In the right clinical context — no active infection, good valve position, no regurgitation, CT-guided planning, and subsequent gradient improvement — it may be a marker of severe anatomic challenge rather than procedural failure.

## Take-home points

- A very high early post-TAVI gradient does **not** automatically mean the valve is failing.
- In valve-in-valve-in-valve anatomy, think about **frame constraint and functional orifice area**, not just leaflet motion.
- If the gradient is high, check whether the data support **real obstruction**: AVA, DVI, regurgitation, and imaging context all matter.
- **CT-guided planning** is essential in complex redo TAVI, especially when coronary access and future interventions are on the line.
- Some gradients improve after **post-dilation, frame expansion, and hemodynamic remodeling** — so repeat imaging matters.
