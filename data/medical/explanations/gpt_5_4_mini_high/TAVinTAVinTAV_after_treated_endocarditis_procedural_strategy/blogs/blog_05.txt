Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Choosing the Valve for a Valve-in-Valve-in-Valve: Why Acurate neo2 Made Sense Here

## Why this case is worth your attention

A third transcatheter aortic valve in the same patient is already a head-turner. Add a prior episode of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, a narrow triple-stented aortic root, severe prosthesis-patient mismatch risk, and the ever-present worry about coronary obstruction — and you’ve got a case that forces you to think beyond “can we place a valve?” toward “which valve, where exactly, and what does this do to future options?”

That’s the real lesson here. In serial redo TAVI, device selection is not just about getting something to sit in the annulus. It’s about preserving flow, preserving coronary access, and buying the patient as much hemodynamic runway as possible.

## The problem was not just valve failure — it was space failure

This patient had already accumulated a Lotus valve, then an Evolut valve inside it, and later developed recurrent severe prosthetic dysfunction after treated *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis. By the time of the third procedure, CT showed a minimum lumen diameter of just 13.7 mm in the Evolut frame. That’s a tiny working space for another valve.

In a setting like this, the major procedural hazards are predictable:

- further reduction in effective orifice area
- severe prosthesis-patient mismatch
- coronary obstruction or loss of coronary re-access
- poor leaflet expansion if the new device is forced into an already constrained frame

So the choice of valve has to answer a simple question: **which platform gives the best chance of opening well inside a crowded scaffold?**

## Why Acurate neo2 made sense here

The team chose an **Acurate neo2, size S**, and the rationale is easy to understand if you think in terms of geometry and hemodynamics.

This device was attractive because of:

**Supra-annular leaflet position**  
In a tight redo anatomy, anything that helps move the functional leaflets higher can improve the effective orifice area. Supra-annular leaflet positioning is one of the main reasons self-expanding platforms are often favored in valve-in-valve scenarios.

**Relatively low frame profile**  
In a triple-stented root, frame height matters. A lower-profile design may help reduce the amount of hardware crowding the coronary sinuses and can support a strategy aimed at preserving coronary flow and future access.

**Favorable hemodynamics in narrow anatomies**  
When the residual lumen is already small, the best valve is often the one that can deliver the least additional flow penalty. The team was clearly trying to mitigate the risk of a dead-end hemodynamic result after the third implant.

**Support for commissural alignment**  
Commissural alignment is not just a buzzword here. In a patient with known coronary disease and repeated transcatheter layers, preserving coronary access is part of lifetime planning. Acurate neo2 offered a design that supported this strategy.

The key point is that this was not a random platform choice. It was a deliberate effort to match device geometry to anatomy.

## Why implantation height mattered just as much as the valve

A valve can be the “right” one and still perform badly if it’s implanted poorly.

In this case, the team used a **high implantation strategy**, aligning the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut leaflets based on CT modeling. That choice had two big goals:

1. **Maximize effective orifice area**
2. **Minimize the neo-skirt burden below the coronaries**

By implanting high, the team aimed to pin the pre-existing leaflets against the frame rather than let them fold inward and steal lumen. That’s the kind of subtle, anatomy-driven maneuver that can make the difference between “technically successful” and “hemodynamically acceptable.”

This is also why redo TAVI planning can’t be reduced to fluoroscopy alone. The landing zone is a 3D problem, and in layered valves, the interaction between frames matters just as much as the annulus itself.

## What CT had to answer before the team could proceed

The preprocedural MSCT was doing a lot of work here. It wasn’t just confirming that the patient had a small lumen — it was helping answer whether the procedure was even worth attempting.

The CT assessment focused on:

- coronary ostial height
- sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- valve-to-coronary distance
- interaction between the existing valve frames
- anticipated relationship between the new valve’s neo-skirt and the coronaries

That evaluation allowed the operators to proceed **without coronary protection**, which is not something you’d do casually in a triple-layered root. The imaging suggested that the planned strategy could preserve coronary flow.

One important nuance: they did **not** use dedicated 3D expansion software for full modeling. Even so, the CT data were sufficient to guide a strategy-based intervention. That’s a useful reminder that good imaging is less about having every toy and more about asking the right questions.

## The hemodynamic tradeoff was real — and expected

The immediate post-procedure echo looked good from a regurgitation standpoint, but the mean gradient was still high at 67 mm Hg, with DVI 0.24 and AVA 0.9 cm². That’s severe PPM territory.

This is the uncomfortable truth about TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV: sometimes the best possible result is still not “normal.” The aim is often to convert catastrophic obstruction into acceptable function.

And that’s exactly what happened here. Over time, gradients improved to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and 26 mm Hg at 1 year, with AVA 1.1 cm² and preserved EF. The patient remained asymptomatic and free of recurrent infection.

So even though the immediate hemodynamics weren’t perfect, the longer-term result was clinically meaningful.

## A few practical pearls from this case

The procedural details matter because they show how this was actually made to work:

- local anesthesia, left radial plus left femoral access
- balloon pre-dilation under rapid pacing
- self-expanding valve deployment
- post-dilation to optimize expansion
- final aortography confirming no regurgitation and preserved coronary perfusion

The post-dilation deserves attention. In a constrained frame, balloon optimization may help achieve more radial expansion and better leaflet motion. In this case, it likely contributed to the later improvement in transvalvular gradient.

That said, there was **no intentional frame fracture**. The benefit came from strategy and optimization, not from aggressive structural disruption.

## What about the prior endocarditis?

This is important: the case was not about implanting a valve into active infection.

The patient had completed targeted antimicrobial therapy for *E. faecalis* PVE, had no suppressive antibiotics ongoing, and had repeat blood cultures, TEE, and CT confirming no evidence of active endocarditis. That distinction matters. The decision-making was driven primarily by anatomy, hemodynamics, and prohibitive surgical risk — not by untreated infection.

So the lesson is not “TAVI is fine in endocarditis.” The lesson is that **after infection has been treated and excluded as active, the heart team may still need to solve a structural valve problem using the least bad option available.**

## Bottom line

In a narrow triple-stented aortic root, the “best” valve is the one that gives you the best compromise between expansion, hemodynamics, and coronary safety. Here, Acurate neo2 made sense because it offered supra-annular leaflet function, a low-profile frame, and compatibility with a high implantation strategy designed to preserve effective orifice area and future coronary access.

## Take-home points

- In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, device selection is anatomy-driven, not brand-driven.
- Supra-annular leaflet position and lower frame profile are major advantages in a crowded redo root.
- High implantation can be a deliberate strategy to preserve EOA and coronary access.
- CT planning should answer coronary clearance, frame interaction, and neo-skirt risk before you go to the lab.
- An immediate high gradient after triple valve layering may still be an acceptable tradeoff if symptoms, regurgitation, and long-term valve function improve.
- Treated prosthetic valve endocarditis does not automatically rule out redo transcatheter therapy, but active infection must be excluded first.
