Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# TAVI Is a Lifetime Strategy: Planning the Third Valve at the First Implant

## The surprising part of this case

A man in his late 70s had a **third transcatheter aortic valve implanted inside two prior transcatheter valves** after a treated episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis. That alone makes the case memorable. But the real teaching point is bigger: this wasn’t just a technical feat, it was a **lifetime-management problem**.

He had a Lotus valve in 2014, a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut in 2022, then developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis with restenosis. After antimicrobial treatment and confirmation that the infection had resolved, he underwent a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** in 2024 with an Acurate neo2. At 1 year, he remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function.

That’s the headline: **serial redo TAVI can be feasible, but only if you plan like the next procedure matters from day one.**

## Why this case matters now

We are no longer doing TAVI in a world where the first procedure is likely the last. Indications have expanded, and patients are living long enough to develop:

- structural valve degeneration
- prosthesis-patient mismatch
- coronary access problems
- valve-in-valve reintervention
- complex anatomy from overlapping frames

So the question is no longer simply, “Can I implant this valve today?”  
It’s also, “What will this valve do to the patient’s next 10 years?”

That shift changes how we think about:

- valve platform selection
- implant depth
- commissural alignment
- residual effective orifice area
- future coronary access
- the geometry of redo procedures

## What happened in this patient

This patient had substantial cardiovascular disease burden: hypertension, three-vessel CAD with multiple prior PCIs, complete heart block with pacemaker, diabetes, COPD, and recurrent GI bleeding. He was already a high-risk structural heart patient before the valve story got complicated.

In 2014, he underwent implantation of a **27 mm Lotus valve** for severe aortic stenosis. By early 2022, that prosthesis had failed with severe stenosis and a mean gradient of **83 mm Hg**, along with a drop in LVEF to **42%**. He then underwent a **TAV-in-TAV** with a **23 mm Evolut** in May 2022.

That procedure was not smooth—he developed a femoral pseudoaneurysm requiring open vascular surgery—but the early hemodynamic response was good, with LVEF improving back to **54%**.

Then came the infectious complication. Three months later he re-presented with fever and worsening heart failure symptoms. Blood cultures grew **Enterococcus faecalis**, and he was diagnosed with prosthetic valve endocarditis. He completed targeted therapy with **6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based treatment**, followed by oral therapy per infectious diseases guidance. Importantly, by the time the third valve was being considered, there was **no evidence of active infection**: blood cultures were negative, TEE was reassuring, and MSCT showed no active endocarditis or paravalvular complication.

By 2024, the valve had failed again, now with severe stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. TTE showed:

- mean gradient **49 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.5 cm²**
- LVEF recovered to **66%**

That’s the setup for the third intervention.

## The key preprocedural question: is a third TAVI even feasible?

This is where the case becomes especially instructive. The team did not simply decide “repeat TAVI” and move on. They used **multislice CT** to ask the questions that matter in serial valve procedures:

- What does the coronary anatomy look like?
- How tall are the sinuses?
- What is the valve-to-coronary distance?
- How are the previous frames interacting?
- Will the anticipated neo-skirt threaten coronary flow?
- Can the new valve be implanted high enough to preserve coronary access?

CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis**, confirming severe narrowing and helping guide the strategy. This is the kind of case where CT is not just a nice add-on; it is the roadmap.

Because CT suggested acceptable coronary clearance, the team proceeded **without coronary protection**. That’s a major point: in complex redo TAVI, coronary protection should be considered, but it doesn’t have to be reflexive if the anatomy is favorable and carefully assessed.

## How the procedure was actually done

The third procedure was performed under local anesthesia with left radial and left femoral arterial access. After aortography confirmed severe regurgitation, the team:

- crossed the valve with a Safari wire
- performed balloon pre-dilation under rapid pacing
- confirmed no coronary compromise during the procedure
- implanted an **Acurate neo2 size S**
- deliberately aligned the valve with the Evolut leaflets based on CT planning
- performed post-dilation to optimize expansion

The deployment strategy matters here. The valve was implanted **high enough to sit at the level of the Evolut leaflets**, which the authors used to help pin the prior leaflets against the frame and reduce further loss of effective orifice area. The goal was not just “put a valve in the valve,” but to do so in a way that preserved flow and future options.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen for a reason. In this anatomy, its:

- supra-annular leaflet position
- relatively low frame height
- favorable hemodynamics in narrow anatomy
- commissural alignment features

made it a sensible choice for a triple-stented situation.

## The hemodynamics were not perfect immediately — and that’s important

Right after the procedure, TTE showed:

- no aortic regurgitation
- mobile leaflets
- but a mean gradient of **67 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.9 cm²**
- DVI **0.24**

That sounds underwhelming at first glance, and it’s worth resisting the temptation to equate “successful deployment” with “excellent immediate gradient.” In layered valve anatomy, especially with pre-existing frame constraints, early hemodynamics can be misleading.

By 1 month, things had improved:

- mean gradient **27 mm Hg**
- AVA **1.1 cm²**
- no regurgitation
- reverse remodeling of the LV

At 1 year, the patient remained asymptomatic and active, with:

- mean gradient **26 mm Hg**
- AVA **1.1 cm²**
- LVEF **65%**
- no recurrent endocarditis

So this was not a perfect-pressure-gradient story. It was a **durable clinical success** in a patient with very limited options.

## What this teaches us about lifetime valve planning

This is the core FOAM lesson: **the first TAVI should be planned as the first move in a long game.**

Every valve platform creates a different future. Frame height, commissural orientation, leaflet position, and valve expansion all influence what happens when the valve eventually degenerates. In a patient who may live long enough to need redo intervention, you want to preserve:

- coronary access
- room for a future valve
- enough effective orifice area to avoid severe PPM
- a landing zone that allows another intervention if needed

That means thinking about redo feasibility at the index procedure, not as an afterthought.

In this case, the team also highlighted the importance of **commissural alignment**. That may sound like a technical detail, but in a patient with CAD and prior PCI, it can be the difference between manageable future coronary access and a nightmare.

## Endocarditis changes the rules — but not forever

This case should not be read as “TAVI is the answer to prosthetic valve endocarditis.” It isn’t.

Current guidance still generally favors surgery for prosthetic valve endocarditis, especially when infection is uncontrolled or heart failure is present. But once infection has been treated, blood cultures are negative, and imaging shows no active endocarditis, the problem becomes one of **structural valve failure in a high-risk patient**.

That distinction matters.

Here, the patient had:

- completed antimicrobial therapy
- no suppressive antibiotics ongoing
- negative follow-up assessment for active infection
- prohibitive surgical risk
- complex overlapping transcatheter frames

Given that combination, the heart team chose a transcatheter solution rather than a high-risk root/ascending aorta operation.

## Practical pearls from this case

- **TAVI is a lifetime strategy, not a one-time fix.** Plan the first valve with redo anatomy in mind.
- **CT is essential before serial redo TAVI.** Evaluate coronary height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and frame interaction.
- **Commissural alignment matters.** It affects future coronary access and reintervention options.
- **After treated endocarditis, the decision framework changes.** If there’s no active infection, the case becomes about anatomy, hemodynamics, and operative risk.
- **Don’t ignore the “ugly” immediate gradient.** In layered valve anatomy, the early post-procedure hemodynamic picture may improve over time.
- **Triple TAVI is possible, but it should be intentional.** These cases succeed because of planning, not improvisation.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that structural heart intervention is moving from “procedure” to **longitudinal management**. As TAVI expands to younger and longer-living patients, the first implant should be chosen with the next implant in mind.

The best TAVI is not always the one that looks easiest today.  
Sometimes it’s the one that leaves the most options tomorrow.
