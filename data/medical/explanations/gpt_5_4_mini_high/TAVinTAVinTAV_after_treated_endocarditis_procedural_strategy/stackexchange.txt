Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Morning Report] In a man in his late 70s with two prior transcatheter aortic valves who developed recurrent dyspnea and fatigue, what echocardiographic and CT findings would establish severe prosthetic valve dysfunction severe enough to justify reintervention?

In this case, the decision to reintervene was supported by **clear hemodynamic evidence of severe transcatheter valve failure on echocardiography**, together with **CT confirmation of a critically narrowed functional valve lumen**.

On **TTE**, the key findings were:

- **Mean transvalvular gradient 49 mm Hg** — this is in the severe range
- **Aortic valve area 0.5 cm²** by continuity equation — also clearly severe
- **Persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation**
- Visualization of **two overlapping bioprosthetic valves**, consistent with complex prosthetic obstruction

These findings established **severe prosthetic valve dysfunction**, not just mild degeneration.

On **MSCT**, the key finding was:

- **Minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis**

That CT measurement confirmed marked narrowing of the effective orifice and helped show that the valve anatomy was severely compromised, while also guiding procedural planning and coronary-risk assessment.

So, in practical terms, **symptomatic recurrent dyspnea plus a high Doppler gradient, very small AVA, significant intraprosthetic regurgitation, and CT evidence of a severely reduced internal lumen** would justify repeat intervention.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Three months after TAV-in-TAV, the patient presented with persistent fever, worsening heart failure symptoms, and a new increase in transprosthetic gradient. What differential diagnosis should be considered for early failure of a transcatheter bioprosthesis, and what findings in this case made Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis the leading diagnosis?

In a patient with **early transcatheter bioprosthesis failure**—that is, failure within months rather than years—the differential should include:

- **Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)**
- **Leaflet thrombosis / hypoattenuated leaflet thickening**
- **Early structural valve deterioration** or leaflet injury
- **Prosthesis underexpansion or malexpansion**
- **Severe patient–prosthesis mismatch**
- Less commonly, **pannus** or other mechanical obstruction

In this case, **Enterococcus faecalis PVE** became the leading diagnosis because the presentation was not just hemodynamic but also infectious: he had **persistent fever**, **worsening heart failure symptoms**, and a **new increase in transprosthetic gradient** on transthoracic echocardiography, indicating new prosthetic obstruction. Most importantly, **blood cultures were positive for Enterococcus faecalis**, providing microbiologic confirmation. The timing—only **3 months after TAV-in-TAV**—also argued against ordinary late structural degeneration as the sole explanation.

So the key teaching point is that **early prosthetic dysfunction plus fever should be presumed infective endocarditis until proven otherwise**, and in this patient the combination of **fever, rising gradient, decompensated heart failure, and positive E. faecalis blood cultures** made PVE the most compelling diagnosis.

### [Management] Before offering a third transcatheter aortic valve procedure to a patient with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis, what clinical, microbiologic, and imaging evidence should be present to support that the infection has been adequately treated and is no longer active?

Before a third transcatheter valve procedure is offered after prosthetic valve endocarditis, you want evidence that the infection has been **clinically quiescent, microbiologically cleared, and anatomically inactive**.

In practical terms, that means:

- **Clinical evidence:** the patient should be **afebrile and without ongoing signs of infection**—no persistent sepsis, no new embolic phenomena, and no other features suggesting uncontrolled endocarditis. Residual heart-failure symptoms can still be present if they are explained by **structural prosthetic failure**, not infection.
- **Microbiologic evidence:** **targeted antimicrobial therapy should be completed**, and **repeat blood cultures should be negative**, showing clearance of the prior pathogen. In the case report, the patient had completed a full course for *Enterococcus faecalis* PVE and was **not on chronic suppressive antibiotics** at the time of reintervention.
- **Imaging evidence:** **TEE is essential**, and CT is helpful, to show **no vegetations, no periannular abscess, no prosthetic dehiscence, and no paravalvular complications**. In this case, both **TEE and MSCT confirmed resolution of infection** and no evidence of active endocarditis.

So the decision to proceed should rest on a **concordant picture**: the patient is clinically well from an infectious standpoint, cultures have cleared, and TEE/CT show **no active endocarditis**. Only then should the remaining valve problem be treated as **degenerative prosthetic dysfunction**, as was done here.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which preprocedural CT measurements are most important when planning a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, and how do they help predict coronary obstruction, neo-skirt risk, and future coronary access?

The most important preprocedural CT measurements are the **coronary ostial height**, **sinus of Valsalva size** (and, when relevant, the **sinotubular junction**), and the **valve-to-coronary distance**—that is, how close the planned transcatheter frame and leaflets will sit to the coronary ostia. In this case, the team also assessed the **interaction between the two existing valve frames** and the anticipated relationship of the **neo-skirt** to the coronaries.

These measurements answer three practical questions. First, they predict **coronary obstruction**: low coronary heights, small sinuses, and a short valve-to-coronary distance mean the displaced leaflets and new frame are more likely to cover the ostium. Second, they predict **neo-skirt risk**: if the skirt or leaflet plane of the new valve will lie at or above the coronary level, the ostia may be functionally “jail­ed,” even if the valve is implanted successfully. Third, they help anticipate **future coronary access**: by mapping the prior frame geometry, commissural orientation, and feasibility of **high implantation**, CT can identify whether a catheter will still be able to reach the coronaries after the third valve is placed.

That is exactly how CT was used in this report—to choose valve type, implantation depth, and commissural strategy, and to proceed **without coronary protection** because the anatomy was judged favorable for preserved coronary flow and reaccess.

### [Management] In this patient with treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, COPD, diabetes, recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, prior PCI, a pacemaker, and two deeply integrated transcatheter valves, what factors made surgical explantation and redo root surgery less favorable than a third transcatheter intervention?

Surgical explantation and redo root surgery were less favorable because this was no longer a case of uncontrolled endocarditis, but rather of **treated infection with residual prosthetic dysfunction in a patient with prohibitive operative risk and hostile anatomy**.

Several factors pushed the heart team toward a third transcatheter intervention:

- **Very high surgical risk**: he was in his late 70s with a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, COPD, diabetes, severe coronary artery disease with multiple prior PCIs, recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, and a pacemaker.
- **No active infection at the time of reintervention**: he had completed targeted therapy for *Enterococcus faecalis* PVE, and repeat blood cultures, TEE, and CT showed **no vegetations, no paravalvular complications, and no evidence of ongoing endocarditis**. Thus, the main indication was prosthetic degeneration, not uncontrolled infection.
- **Technical difficulty of surgery**: the two prior transcatheter valves were **deeply integrated into the aortic root/aortic wall**. The report notes that surgical removal would likely have required **total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement**, with substantial risk of annular/root destruction, bleeding, and coronary reimplantation injury.
- **Coronary and reoperative complexity**: prior coronary disease and PCI made preservation of coronary access important, and redo root surgery in the setting of multiple layered transcatheter frames would have been especially hazardous.
- **Feasibility of a catheter-based solution**: CT showed an anatomy that permitted careful planning of a third valve with acceptable coronary clearance, making a less invasive transcatheter strategy both possible and preferable.

So the key principle is that, once endocarditis was cured, **the decision was driven primarily by surgical prohibitive risk and extreme anatomic complexity**, not by infection itself.

### [Management] What was the procedural rationale for choosing a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve and implanting it high, aligned with the prior Evolut leaflets, during the third valve procedure?

The rationale was to optimize hemodynamics and preserve coronary safety in a triple-valve, very small residual lumen. The Acurate neo2 was chosen because its supra-annular leaflet position and relatively low frame profile offered better expected effective orifice area in a narrow, already layered anatomy, with the added advantage of commissural alignment. The valve was implanted high, at the level of the prior Evolut leaflets, deliberately to pin those leaflets outward against the stent frame and prevent further inward folding, which would have worsened obstruction and patient–prosthesis mismatch. A high implant also helped keep the new “neo-skirt” lower relative to the coronary ostia, thereby preserving coronary flow and future coronary access. In short, the strategy was CT-guided: maximize the residual opening, avoid coronary compromise, and achieve the best possible hemodynamics in a complex TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration.

### [Pathophysiology] By what mechanisms can healed prosthetic valve endocarditis lead later to mixed prosthetic stenosis and regurgitation even after blood cultures have cleared and imaging shows no active infection?

Healed prosthetic valve endocarditis can leave behind **permanent mechanical damage** even when the infection is sterilized. The key point is that a negative blood culture and the disappearance of vegetations on TEE/CT mean the infection is no longer active, **not** that the valve has recovered.

In the case you provided, the later mixed prosthetic dysfunction was most plausibly due to three linked processes:

1. **Direct leaflet injury from infection** — endocarditis can damage, perforate, or weaken prosthetic leaflets, leading to **regurgitation** when coaptation is no longer complete.  
2. **Healed inflammation with fibrosis/scarring** — as the infection resolves, the repair response can thicken and stiffen the leaflets, or create pannus-like tissue, which **restricts leaflet opening** and produces **stenosis**.  
3. **Accelerated structural valve degeneration and mechanical restriction** — in transcatheter bioprostheses, prior infection can hasten leaflet degeneration and impair motion within the valve frame. In this patient’s valve-in-valve anatomy, the authors specifically thought that prior leaflet fibrosis and probable mechanical restriction, superimposed on progressive degeneration, explained the later **combined severe stenosis and intraprosthetic regurgitation**.

So the essence is: **infection may be cured, but the valve can be left scarred, distorted, and mechanically failed**—and that structural failure manifests as mixed stenosis and regurgitation.

### [Clinical Pearls] Immediately after the third valve implantation, transthoracic echocardiography showed no regurgitation but a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 0.9 cm2, and a Doppler velocity index of 0.24; one month later the mean gradient had fallen to 27 mm Hg. How would you interpret this early physiology, and what mechanisms could explain the subsequent improvement?

This early post-implant study is best interpreted as a **competent but functionally constrained valve**: there was **no regurgitation and the leaflets were mobile**, but the **very high gradient, low DVI, and small calculated valve area** indicated **severe residual patient–prosthesis mismatch / functional underexpansion** in a heavily layered TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV anatomy, rather than intrinsic valve failure.

The subsequent fall in gradient is most plausibly explained by a combination of factors described in the case: **continued expansion and settling of the self-expanding Acurate neo2**, **high-pressure post-dilatation**, and **partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame**, which improved leaflet excursion and increased the effective orifice. **Commissural alignment** likely also improved central flow dynamics and reduced turbulence. In addition, **left ventricular reverse remodeling** after relief of the obstruction can lower transvalvular flow demands and contribute to a lower Doppler gradient over time.

So, in short: the immediate physiology reflected a **severely constrained but well-seated valve**, and the later improvement reflected **delayed hemodynamic optimization of the nested valve frames** rather than a new structural change in the prosthesis.
