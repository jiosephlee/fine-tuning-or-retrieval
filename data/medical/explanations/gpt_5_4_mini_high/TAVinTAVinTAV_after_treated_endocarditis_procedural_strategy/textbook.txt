Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Foundations: Severe Aortic Stenosis, TAVI Durability, and the Challenge of Serial Redo Procedures

Severe aortic stenosis is a fixed outflow obstruction that forces the left ventricle to generate progressively higher systolic pressure to maintain forward flow. Over time, this pressure overload leads to concentric hypertrophy, rising myocardial oxygen demand, reduced coronary reserve, and eventual decompensation with dyspnoea, angina, presyncope, or heart failure. In the present case, the patient’s exertional breathlessness, chest discomfort, and declining ejection fraction are typical consequences of advanced prosthetic aortic valve obstruction: once the valve area becomes critically small, stroke volume falls and symptoms can worsen quickly. Valve replacement is therefore the definitive treatment, because medical therapy cannot remove the fixed obstruction.

TAVI has become established because it provides effective relief of aortic stenosis without the trauma of open surgery. Initially reserved for patients at prohibitive or high surgical risk, it is now used across a broader risk spectrum as device profiles, imaging, and procedural techniques have improved. That expansion matters for this case because it has created a growing population of patients living long enough to experience structural valve deterioration and require repeat interventions. What was once a rare “rescue” therapy is increasingly becoming part of a lifetime treatment pathway.

The prostheses used in this patient illustrate why valve design matters in redo planning. The original Lotus valve was a mechanically expanded, fully repositionable and retrievable device with a sealing mechanism that helped limit paravalvular leak, but it also created a constrained internal geometry. The second valve, Evolut, is a self-expanding nitinol prosthesis with supra-annular leaflets and generally favourable haemodynamics, especially in smaller annuli, but it has a tall frame that can complicate future coronary access. The third valve, Acurate neo2, was chosen because its supra-annular leaflet position, relatively low frame height, and open upper-cell design can help preserve orifice area and facilitate commissural alignment. In a triple-valve anatomy, these architectural details become central to whether a third transcatheter implant is feasible and what haemodynamic result can be expected.

Transcatheter bioprosthetic valves can fail through several overlapping mechanisms. Structural degeneration may involve leaflet fibrosis, thickening, calcification, or tearing, leading to progressive stenosis, regurgitation, or both. In a layered valve system, additional contributors include incomplete expansion of one or more frames, inward leaflet folding, and restricted leaflet motion caused by the geometry of the prior prosthesis. In this case, the patient developed severe recurrent obstruction and later mixed stenosis-regurgitation, which is a common way that failing transcatheter valves declare themselves. The episode of infective endocarditis likely accelerated this process by damaging leaflet tissue and promoting inflammation and scarring, even after the infection itself had been treated.

When a transcatheter valve fails, redo strategies can include TAV-in-TAV, in which a second valve is implanted inside the first. This approach can restore leaflet function and improve haemodynamics while avoiding surgical explantation. A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration is much rarer and technically more demanding because each added frame further narrows the residual lumen and changes the geometry of the aortic root. The effective orifice area can become critically small, and the risk of prosthesis-patient mismatch rises as the combined frame layers obstruct flow. This explains why the patient initially had severe residual gradients after the third implant and why careful valve selection, implantation depth, and post-dilatation were needed to improve final haemodynamics.

Several anatomic hazards are amplified in serial redo procedures. Coronary obstruction is a major concern because displaced leaflets and overlapping frames can block the coronary ostia or create a “neo-skirt” that sits near the coronaries. Commissural misalignment can further impair future coronary cannulation even if immediate flow is preserved. Preprocedural CT is therefore essential: it helps define coronary heights, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances, frame interaction, and the likely position of the neo-skirt. In this case, CT-supported planning allowed the team to proceed without coronary protection, but only after confirming that the anticipated implant position would preserve coronary perfusion and leave some possibility of future access.

A history of prosthetic valve endocarditis adds another layer of complexity. Active PVE is diagnosed by a combination of clinical features, blood cultures, and imaging evidence such as vegetations, abscesses, or paravalvular complications. In active infection, surgery is often preferred when there is heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or destructive complications. Healed infection is different: once the patient has completed targeted antibiotics, blood cultures are negative, and TEE or CT shows no vegetations or perivalvular extension, the problem is no longer active infection but residual structural valve dysfunction. That distinction was crucial in this case, because the third TAVI was undertaken only after infection had been treated and imaging confirmed resolution, allowing the team to focus on anatomy and haemodynamics rather than uncontrolled sepsis.

The broader lesson is one of lifetime management. The first transcatheter valve should not be viewed as an isolated procedure but as the first step in a long sequence of possible interventions. In patients with limited surgical reserve and expected long-term survival, the heart team must consider not only the immediate result but also how a future redo might be performed, whether coronary access can be preserved, and how much effective orifice area will remain after one or more additional implants. This case shows that careful CT-guided planning and deliberate strategy can make even a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV feasible, but it also highlights how quickly the technical and haemodynamic stakes rise with each added valve frame.

## Clinical Course: From First TAVI to Post-Endocarditis Failure of Two Prior Prostheses

The patient was a man in his late 70s who presented with progressive exertional dyspnoea, left-sided chest discomfort on walking 200–300 m, dizziness, and fatigue. His background immediately signaled a high-risk structural heart disease patient: long-standing hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions, complete atrioventricular block requiring pacemaker implantation, type 2 diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. This burden of comorbidity is important because it made repeated open surgery less attractive and increased the complexity of any future valve reintervention.

In 2014, he underwent his first transcatheter aortic valve implantation for severe native aortic stenosis, with a 27 mm Lotus prosthesis. For several years after that procedure, the valve functioned adequately, but by late 2021 he again developed exertional symptoms and echocardiography showed progressive prosthetic obstruction. The clinical pattern was typical of structural valve deterioration: worsening dyspnoea accompanied by a rising transvalvular gradient and declining haemodynamic performance of the prosthesis. By early 2022, the mean gradient had reached 83 mm Hg and left ventricular ejection fraction had fallen to 42% from 54% in December 2021, indicating severe stenosis of the transcatheter valve with secondary left ventricular decompensation.

Because the first transcatheter valve had failed and the patient remained a poor surgical candidate, he underwent a second transcatheter procedure in May 2022, a TAV-in-TAV with implantation of a 23 mm Evolut valve. The immediate haemodynamic effect was favourable, with improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction from 42% to 54%, illustrating the expected benefit of relieving severe prosthetic outflow obstruction. The procedure, however, was complicated by a femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm that required open vascular repair, underscoring that even access-site management can be difficult in patients with extensive vascular disease and repeated interventions.

Only a few months later, in September 2022, the clinical picture changed again. The patient was rehospitalised with persistent fever and worsening heart failure symptoms, and transthoracic echocardiography showed severe restenosis of the aortic prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis, and prosthetic valve endocarditis was diagnosed. In a patient with a transcatheter prosthesis, fever, bacteremia, and new or worsening valve dysfunction should always raise concern for endocarditis, because infection can damage leaflet tissue, worsen obstruction, and accelerate subsequent degeneration. He completed a full targeted antimicrobial course, including 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral treatment as advised by infectious disease specialists. After treatment, repeat blood cultures and imaging showed resolution of infection, and no suppressive antibiotic therapy was continued.

Despite microbiologic cure, the prosthesis did not remain stable over time. By January 2024, follow-up demonstrated recurrent severe aortic prosthetic dysfunction with a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, moderate-to-severe regurgitation, and recovered left ventricular ejection fraction of 66%. On admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography again showed marked prosthetic failure: grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation, a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, and an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm², with two overlapping transcatheter valves visible. This combination of persistent obstruction and regurgitation reflected failure of the layered prosthetic system after prior degeneration and treated endocarditis, and it created the clinical scenario that ultimately led to consideration of a third transcatheter valve intervention.

## Preprocedural Evaluation: Defining Valve Failure, Excluding Active Infection, and Determining Feasibility

When this patient re-presented with dyspnoea, exertional chest discomfort, dizziness, and fatigue after two prior transcatheter aortic valve procedures and treated enterococcal prosthetic valve endocarditis, the team had three immediate questions: what exactly was failing, was infection still active, and could a third catheter-based intervention be performed safely? In a patient with multiple overlapping valve frames, recurrent symptoms do not automatically mean one simple mechanism of failure. The differential included structural degeneration of the transcatheter valves, leaflet injury or scarring related to prior endocarditis, thrombosis, underexpansion of the existing prostheses, and pre-existing or acquired prosthesis-patient mismatch. Because the treatment choice would depend on this distinction, the evaluation combined echocardiography, microbiology, transoesophageal imaging, and multislice CT, followed by multidisciplinary review.

Transthoracic echocardiography was the first key test because it provides the haemodynamic signature of prosthetic dysfunction. In this case, TTE showed a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm², persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation, and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 66%. Together, these findings were consistent with severe mixed prosthetic valve dysfunction: significant obstruction plus significant regurgitation. In prosthetic valves, a high gradient alone can reflect stenosis, but the diagnosis is strengthened when the gradient is accompanied by a small calculated valve area and a low Doppler velocity index. The aortic valve area was calculated by the continuity equation, which compares flow in the left ventricular outflow tract with flow across the prosthetic valve. In practical terms, this method uses the left ventricular outflow tract diameter and Doppler velocity-time integral to estimate forward stroke volume, then divides that by the prosthetic jet measurements to derive valve area. In prosthetic aortic valves, this calculation must be interpreted with caution because errors in LVOT measurement, abnormal flow states, and frame geometry can affect the result. For that reason, the valve area is not interpreted in isolation; it is integrated with the gradient, DVI, and regurgitation severity. Here, the combination of severe stenosis and significant regurgitation explained the patient’s symptoms and confirmed true prosthetic failure rather than isolated flow artifact.

The coexistence of stenosis and regurgitation is especially important in a patient with layered transcatheter valves. Severe stenosis increases afterload, while regurgitation adds volume load, so the two lesions reinforce one another clinically. In this patient, the preserved ejection fraction did not exclude clinically important valve dysfunction; rather, it suggested that symptoms were driven primarily by the valve lesion rather than by systolic ventricular failure. The repeated rise in gradients after initial improvement also argued against a simple transient haemodynamic issue and made structural degeneration, recurrent leaflet restriction, or a combination of degeneration and mismatch more likely.

Because the patient had previously been treated for Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, excluding ongoing infection was mandatory before any new valve was implanted. Transoesophageal echocardiography was therefore used to look for vegetations, abscess, paravalvular leak, dehiscence, fistulous complications, or other signs of active prosthetic valve endocarditis. In parallel, repeated blood cultures were obtained, and the clinical picture was reassessed after completion of targeted antimicrobial therapy. The absence of persistent fever, negative repeat cultures, and the lack of echocardiographic or CT evidence of active endocarditis supported the conclusion that the infection had been eradicated rather than merely suppressed. This distinction is crucial: active prosthetic valve infection would generally shift management toward surgery and continued antimicrobial therapy, whereas resolved infection allows treatment decisions to focus on the residual mechanical valve problem.

Multislice CT was the other pivotal preprocedural tool because a third transcatheter valve inside two prior prostheses raises specific anatomic risks, especially coronary obstruction and inadequate residual orifice area. CT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame, confirming marked narrowing of the functional outflow channel. This is a severe degree of compromise from a haemodynamic standpoint, but not necessarily an absolute barrier to intervention if device delivery remains possible and the coronary anatomy is favourable. CT also allowed the team to assess coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between the previously implanted frames. Just as important, CT helped anticipate the position of the future neo-skirt, which can encroach on the coronary ostia and make later coronary access difficult. In this case, the anatomy suggested that a carefully planned high implantation strategy could preserve coronary flow without prophylactic coronary protection, but only because the imaging suggested adequate clearance and a favourable relationship between the valve frames and the coronaries.

Interpretation of the recurrent high gradients required a broad differential. Structural valve degeneration was likely, but it was probably not the only mechanism. The earlier episode of infective endocarditis may have caused leaflet injury or scarring, while the second transcatheter valve could also have been underexpanded or predisposed to patient-prosthesis mismatch because of the limited space inside the prior Lotus frame. In serial TAVI procedures, the distinction between true structural failure and haemodynamic crowding from layered stent frames is often blurred. That is why the team integrated all available data rather than relying on any single measurement. The severe gradient, small valve area, and regurgitation established that the prosthesis was failing functionally; CT then clarified whether a third intervention was technically feasible and how it should be planned.

Finally, surgical risk assessment and guideline context shaped the decision. Current guidance generally favours surgery for prosthetic valve endocarditis, particularly when infection is uncontrolled or when heart failure is driven by active infection. However, once infection has been treated and there is no residual microbiological or imaging evidence of active disease, management must be individualized. This patient had an extremely high operative risk, reflected by a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, along with COPD, diabetes, prior gastrointestinal bleeding, complex coronary artery disease, pacemaker dependence, and two deeply integrated transcatheter prostheses. A surgical explant would likely have required complex aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, making conventional surgery both technically hazardous and physiologically high risk. The heart-team discussion therefore linked the diagnostic findings to a pragmatic procedural choice: because infection had resolved and anatomy was judged suitable on CT, a third transcatheter intervention was considered the most reasonable option.

## Intervention: CT-Guided TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Planning and Execution

The third transcatheter intervention was planned as a carefully individualized redo procedure in a triple-valve anatomy, where the aims were not only to restore valve function but also to avoid coronary obstruction, preserve future coronary access, and obtain the largest possible residual effective orifice area. In this setting, the preprocedural CT study was central to decision-making. It was used to assess the geometry of the two existing transcatheter frames, the size of the remaining lumen, the coronary ostial relationship to the anticipated new leaflet plane, and the feasibility of a high implant strategy. Because the patient had completed antimicrobial therapy and had no clinical or imaging evidence of active endocarditis, the team treated the case as a structural valve failure problem rather than an active infection problem. The procedure was performed only after multidisciplinary heart-team review, with institutional approval for off-label use of the selected device and shared decision-making with the patient and family.

A self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve, size S, was chosen because its design was considered more favorable for a narrow, multiply stented aortic root. Supra-annular leaflet position can provide better hemodynamics in constrained anatomy by placing the functional valve plane higher and away from the smallest residual lumen. The relatively low frame height also helps when working within previously implanted transcatheter frames, where excessive bulk can worsen patient-prosthesis mismatch and complicate coronary access. These properties made the device attractive for a case in which the prior Evolut frame already imposed major spatial constraints and the CT showed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm.

The procedure was carried out under local anesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. This approach allowed diagnostic aortography and hemodynamic assessment while maintaining a minimally invasive strategy in a high-risk patient. Baseline aortography confirmed severe aortic regurgitation, consistent with the preprocedural echocardiographic assessment of failed prosthetic valve function. A Safari guidewire was positioned in the left ventricle through femoral access to provide stable support for delivery and retrieval maneuvers. Balloon predilation was then performed under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. In serial TAVI procedures, predilation serves not only to prepare the landing zone but also to test whether coronary flow remains safe under transient changes in leaflet and frame position; in this case, simultaneous aortography showed no coronary compromise.

The key technical decision was high implantation of the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets. This strategy was preplanned using CT and fluoroscopic landmarks to place the new prosthesis in a position that would pin the pre-existing leaflets outward against the stent frame rather than allowing them to fold inward and further narrow the orifice. By fixing the old leaflets more effectively and limiting the amount of new “neo-skirt” extending below the coronary ostia, the team aimed to preserve coronary perfusion and maximize the residual effective orifice area. Commissural alignment was also deliberately pursued, since the cumulative overlap of three valve frames can make future coronary reaccess extremely difficult. In patients with severe coronary disease, this issue is especially important because even if the immediate procedure is successful, later coronary interventions may become technically hazardous.

Although coronary obstruction is a major concern in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures, no coronary protection strategy or BASILICA was used here. That choice reflected the CT-based assessment that coronary clearance was acceptable, the planned high implantation strategy, and the observation during predilation that coronary flow remained unobstructed. In other words, the team judged that anatomy and implant technique reduced the predicted obstruction risk enough to proceed without prophylactic leaflet modification or coronary wiring. This is an important principle in serial redo TAVI: adjunctive protection is reserved for patients whose CT anatomy suggests truly high obstruction risk, whereas careful positioning may be sufficient when the anatomy is favorable enough.

After deployment of the Acurate neo2 valve, post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon was performed under rapid pacing to improve frame expansion and optimize leaflet opening within the prior prosthetic frames. In triple-stented anatomy, post-dilation can be particularly important because incomplete expansion may leave significant residual obstruction even when the valve appears well seated fluoroscopically. The final aortogram confirmed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and absence of both transvalvular and paravalvular regurgitation. The procedural endpoints therefore focused on safe implantation, stable valve position, and maintained coronary flow, while formal assessment of procedural success was made using Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 criteria, which provide a standardized framework for judging technical success, hemodynamic performance, and major procedure-related complications.

Even when the immediate implant appears technically successful, serial TAVI procedures carry important anticipated risks: coronary obstruction, vascular injury, embolization, residual stenosis or regurgitation, and increasingly difficult future coronary access. This case illustrates how CT-guided planning, deliberate implantation depth, commissural alignment, and careful intraprocedural hemodynamic testing can make a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach feasible in a patient who would otherwise have had extremely limited treatment options.

## Results, Hemodynamic Interpretation, and Lifetime Management Lessons

The immediate postprocedural echocardiogram showed a valve that was mechanically in place and functioning in a basic sense: the new leaflets were mobile, the valve opened, and there was no residual aortic regurgitation. However, the hemodynamics were still poor, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, a calculated aortic valve area of 0.9 cm², an indexed valve area of 0.4 cm²/m², and a Doppler velocity index of 0.24. In a patient with three overlapping transcatheter frames, these findings are best interpreted as severe prosthesis-patient mismatch rather than outright procedural failure. The valve was sealed and not leaking, but the effective opening was too small for the patient’s body size and flow requirements.

This distinction is important for trainees: a high gradient after valve-in-valve therapy does not automatically mean the procedure has failed. Gradients are influenced by valve expansion, frame constraints, loading conditions, stroke volume, and the residual internal diameter created by the previous prostheses. In nested transcatheter valves, especially with multiple layers, the residual orifice can remain markedly restricted even when leaflet motion appears acceptable. An indexed effective orifice area below 0.65 cm²/m² and a DVI below 0.25 support severe obstruction; in this case, those numbers fit the diagnosis of severe PPM immediately after the third implantation.

The later improvement in hemodynamics illustrates why serial assessment matters. Over the following month, the mean gradient fell from 67 to 27 mm Hg and the valve area increased to 1.1 cm², with no recurrent regurgitation. This improvement is consistent with the mechanisms described in the report: better frame expansion after post-dilation, improved leaflet opening, more favorable flow dynamics, and the effects of left ventricular remodeling as the circulation adapted to the new valve. In other words, an early postprocedural gradient can overestimate the final steady-state result in complex valve-in-valve settings, particularly when the valve is still settling within a constrained frame.

At 3.5 months, the patient remained asymptomatic, and by 1 year he was still clinically stable and active, without dyspnea, heart failure, regurgitation, or evidence of recurrent endocarditis. Left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved at 65%, and the mean gradient remained stable at 26 mm Hg with a valve area of 1.1 cm². Although the prosthesis did not normalize completely, the durable reduction in symptoms and the stable echocardiographic profile indicate a meaningful and sustained clinical benefit from the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy.

This case also shows how follow-up after triple transcatheter reintervention should be structured. Serial transthoracic echocardiography is needed to track gradients, valve area, regurgitation, Doppler velocity index, and ventricular remodeling over time rather than relying on the immediate postimplant result alone. Clinical surveillance should include symptom review for dyspnea, angina, syncope, and heart failure, along with infection surveillance because of the prior episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis. Given the patient’s extensive coronary disease and the triple-frame anatomy, follow-up should also preserve awareness of coronary access, since any future coronary intervention may be technically difficult.

The broader lesson is that transcatheter reintervention after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis can be considered when infection has cleared and surgery is prohibitive, but only after careful confirmation that there is no active endocarditis. In this case, repeated cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and CT supported eradication of infection, allowing the heart team to focus on anatomy, hemodynamic severity, and operative risk. This is a lifetime-management problem, not a single-procedure problem: the first valve choice, the orientation of commissures, and the preservation of coronary access all affect what can be done later.

The case also highlights how limited future rescue options become once a third prosthesis has been implanted. Surgical explantation would be technically hazardous, especially with deep frame incorporation into the aortic root. Techniques such as valve fracture or leaflet-laceration strategies may be considered in selected settings, but their role in triple-stented anatomy remains uncertain and is not well established. For that reason, the procedural plan for the third valve had to balance immediate hemodynamic improvement against the reality that future interventions may be extremely constrained.

For trainees, the main teaching points are straightforward. Reintervention strategy in structural valve disease must be driven by anatomy, imaging, and heart-team judgment, not by symptoms alone. CT-based planning is essential to assess coronary risk, frame interaction, and the feasibility of implantation depth and commissural alignment. Finally, this case underscores the principle of lifetime management in TAVI: as patients live longer after their first transcatheter valve, each subsequent procedure becomes more complex, and each decision should preserve as many future options as possible.
