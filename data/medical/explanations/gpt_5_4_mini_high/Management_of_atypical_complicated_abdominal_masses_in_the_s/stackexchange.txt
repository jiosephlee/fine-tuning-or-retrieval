Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a 21-week pregnancy with severe abdominal pain and dyspnoea from large abdominopelvic masses, what diagnostic modalities are considered safe and most useful, and what additional information can bimanual examination and non-contrast MRI provide beyond ultrasound?

In a symptomatic 21-week pregnancy, the safest and most useful diagnostic workup is a careful pelvic examination followed by ultrasonography with color Doppler, and, if the anatomy remains unclear, non-contrast MRI. These modalities are preferred because they avoid ionizing radiation; gadolinium should be avoided in pregnancy unless there is a compelling indication. In the case report, ultrasound was the key first test, and MRI without contrast was used to refine the diagnosis when the masses were large and their origin remained uncertain.

Bimanual examination adds bedside anatomic information that ultrasound may not fully capture in a very large gravid abdomen: it can show whether a mass is fixed or mobile, its consistency, and whether it is bulging into the posterior fornix or filling the lesser pelvis, as in this patient. Non-contrast MRI then adds the most useful detail for operative planning: it better defines whether the mass is ovarian versus uterine in origin, whether it is continuous with a leiomyoma or pedicle, the thickness of the wall, internal cystic degeneration versus solid components, the relationship to the uterine cavity and adjacent organs, and whether there is associated adenopathy. In this case, MRI helped show that the caudal lesion was in continuity with a posterior myoma and characterized the complex features of the larger mass, thereby guiding management and delivery planning.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When ultrasound in pregnancy shows two large unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation, what differential diagnoses should be prioritized, and which imaging or anatomic clues would favor cystic-degenerating leiomyoma over decidualised endometrioma or primary ovarian neoplasm?

In pregnancy, a large, unilocular cystic mass with smooth margins and peripheral vascularity should first raise concern for a benign pregnancy-related mimic of malignancy, especially **decidualized endometrioma** and **cystic-degenerating leiomyoma**, while still keeping a **primary ovarian neoplasm** in the differential.

The key distinction is **anatomic origin**. A **cystic-degenerating leiomyoma** is favored when imaging shows:
- **Continuity with the uterus** or with a known intramural/subserosal myoma
- A **pedicle, stalk, or broad uterine attachment**
- **Bridging or tumor-feeding vessels** arising from the uterus
- A mass that is **fixed to the posterior uterine wall/torus** rather than clearly ovarian
- On MRI, **thick walls**, **heterogeneous T1/T2 signal**, and **solid components** within a lesion that tracks with a uterine myoma

By contrast, a **decidualized endometrioma** is more likely when the lesion is clearly **ovarian in origin** and shows the classic endometrioma background, with **mural nodules** that may enhance in pregnancy. A **primary ovarian neoplasm** becomes more concerning if there is **no uterine continuity**, more complex solid-papillary architecture, ascites, adenopathy, or progressive malignant features.

In the case report, the strongest clue against an ovarian tumor was the **caudal mass’s continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma**, and the upper lesion was ultimately found at surgery to be a **pedunculated, highly vascularized uterine leiomyoma**. Mild CA-125 elevation did not distinguish the entities, because CA-125 is nonspecific in pregnancy.

So, in practice: **prioritize leiomyoma versus decidualized endometrioma first, and use uterine continuity/pedicle/feeding vessels and MRI correlation to favor cystic-degenerating leiomyoma over ovarian disease.**

### [Clinical Pearls] How should a mildly elevated CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL be interpreted in the second trimester of pregnancy, and why is a serial trend more informative than a single value when evaluating an adnexal or pelvic mass?

A CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester should be interpreted as a **mild, nonspecific elevation**, not as evidence of ovarian malignancy by itself. In pregnancy, CA-125 is an unreliable standalone tumor marker: it can rise physiologically, especially early in gestation, and it may also increase with **benign pelvic processes** such as endometriosis, leiomyoma, inflammation, or peritoneal irritation. In this case, the borderline elevation fit with a **complicated benign uterine mass** rather than cancer, and pathology ultimately showed **benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis near a twisted pedicle**, a plausible cause of marker elevation.

A **serial trend is more informative than a single value** because CA-125 varies with gestational age and with transient benign stimuli. One isolated result above 35 U/mL may simply reflect pregnancy-related fluctuation or local irritation; a **progressive rise over time**, especially when paired with suspicious imaging findings, is more concerning and helps with follow-up. Here, the increase from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over 2 weeks suggested persistence of the process, but it did not establish malignancy—the diagnosis still depended on the **clinical picture and ultrasound/MRI**, not the marker alone.

### [Pathophysiology] What pathophysiologic processes can make a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma appear as a mixed cystic-solid mass and cause pain, low-grade fever, and leakage at surgery?

A pedunculated leiomyoma can look “mixed cystic-solid” when it undergoes **degenerative change from vascular compromise**, most importantly **torsion of its stalk**. In this case report, the operative finding and pathology were classic: the mass was **twisted**, **highly vascular**, and the specimen showed **ischemic necrosis near the pedicle**.

The pathophysiology is as follows: torsion or rapid enlargement in pregnancy can impair venous outflow first, then arterial inflow, producing **ischemia, infarction, and necrosis**. Necrotic myoma tissue may then **liquefy**, creating **cystic-appearing spaces** within an otherwise solid smooth-muscle tumor. That is why imaging may show a **heterogeneous cystic-solid mass** rather than a uniformly solid leiomyoma.

These same changes explain the clinical symptoms. **Pain** results from acute ischemia, infarction, and stretching of the serosa and stalk. **Low-grade fever** can occur from the inflammatory response to necrotic tissue—classically seen with degenerating fibroids in pregnancy, including “red” degeneration. And **leakage at surgery** likely reflected escape of **serous, hemorrhagic, or necrotic fluid** from the degenerated, partially liquefied tumor.

So, in short: **torsion-related vascular compromise with ischemic/hemorrhagic degeneration and liquefaction** can make a pedunculated leiomyoma appear cystic-solid and produce pain, fever, and intraoperative leakage.

### [Morning Report] What specific clinical and radiographic features in this case justified operative intervention at 21 weeks and 6 days rather than continued expectant management?

Operative management at **21 weeks and 6 days** was justified because this was not an incidental, stable myoma; it was a **symptomatic, enlarging, and potentially complicated mass** with features that raised concern for imminent harm to the mother and pregnancy.

The **clinical triggers** were:

- **Severe acute abdominal pain** in both the left upper quadrant and pelvis, only partially relieved with analgesia  
- **Mild dyspnea**, consistent with **mechanical restrictive compression** from the masses and gravid uterus  
- **Fever**, which in this context raised concern for **torsion, ischemia, necrosis, or infection**  
- A markedly **enlarged, tender abdomen** and a **fixed, hard pelvic mass** on bimanual examination

The **radiographic triggers** were equally important:

- **Two very large masses**: one measuring **18 × 12 × 7 cm** extending from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus, and a second **9.5 × 8 × 8 cm** pelvic mass  
- **Peripheral vascularity**, **thick walls**, and **mixed cystic-solid components** on US/MRI, which are not reassuring in a symptomatic second-trimester mass  
- **Unclear cleavage plane** between the caudal mass and the posterior uterine myoma, making the diagnosis uncertain  
- **No identifiable stalk or feeding vessel** for the upper mass on imaging, leaving the origin ambiguous and sustaining concern for a potentially complicated adnexal or uterine lesion  
- A **slightly rising CA-125**, which is nonspecific in pregnancy, but in combination with the imaging and symptoms contributed to the concern for atypical pathology

In short, the decision for surgery was driven by the combination of **persistent/worsening symptoms, evidence of mass effect, suspected torsion/ischemia/infection, and diagnostic uncertainty with concerning imaging features**. The operative findings later validated that concern: the cranial lesion was a **twisted, highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma with leaking and ischemic necrosis**. This is exactly the kind of scenario in which expectant management becomes unsafe.

### [Management] What factors would make an open longitudinal laparotomy preferable to laparoscopy for management of a symptomatic mass in the second trimester of pregnancy?

An open longitudinal laparotomy is preferable to laparoscopy when the mass is **too large, too complex, or too poorly accessible for safe minimally invasive surgery** in the setting of a gravid uterus. In the case report, the decision for a xipho-pubic incision was driven by several factors that favor open exposure:

- **Giant size and mass effect**: the upper lesion was 18 cm and the pregnancy itself had already reduced intra-abdominal working space, making trocar placement and safe dissection difficult.
- **Multiple lesions**: there were two large masses, increasing operative complexity.
- **Critical location**: one mass extended into the left subphrenic space and the other was fixed in the pelvis/posterior uterus, where laparoscopic access and specimen control are harder.
- **Intramural or poorly separable origin / unclear anatomy**: imaging could not clearly define the cleavage plane, and the caudal mass appeared contiguous with the posterior uterine wall.
- **High vascularity and mixed solid-cystic features**: these raise concern for bleeding and make laparoscopic hemostasis more challenging.
- **Acute symptoms or complications**: worsening pain, dyspnea, and concern for torsion, rupture, infection, or necrosis justify a more definitive approach.
- **Need for diagnostic certainty**: when the origin is uncertain and there is any oncologic concern, open surgery allows better inspection and control.

By contrast, laparoscopy is generally better reserved for **smaller, more accessible, non-intramural masses**, especially earlier in the second trimester. In this case, the combination of **size, location, vascularity, symptoms, and uncertain anatomy** made open longitudinal laparotomy the safer and more controlled option.

### [Management] At laparotomy, why was the torsed cranial mass excised while the fixed caudal pelvic mass was intentionally left in situ?

The cranial mass was removed because it had become the **acute surgical lesion**: at laparotomy it was found to be a **huge pedunculated leiomyoma that was twisted, highly vascular, and leaking**, with ischemic change on pathology. In pregnancy, a **torsed pedunculated myoma** is an accepted indication for myomectomy because it can cause pain, necrosis, infection, and further maternal instability.

By contrast, the caudal pelvic mass was **fixed to the uterine torus/posterior wall**, not pedunculated, and did not present as the immediate source of torsion. Excision would have required a **more extensive myomectomy in a dangerous intramural/posterior location**, with a much higher risk of **hemorrhage, uterine injury, preterm labor, and pregnancy loss**. The operative goal was therefore to treat the emergency while **minimizing uterine manipulation and obstetric risk**. Consistent with that strategy, the caudal mass was left in situ and the pregnancy was carried forward to elective cesarean delivery later in gestation.

### [Management] During non-obstetric abdominal surgery in pregnancy, what intraoperative and perioperative measures should be taken to reduce maternal and fetal risk, including fetal assessment and obstetric precautions?

In non-obstetric abdominal surgery during pregnancy, the fetus is best protected by protecting the mother. That means careful preoperative multidisciplinary planning with obstetrics, anesthesia, and surgery; confirmation of gestational age, placental location, and fetal viability by ultrasound; and optimization of maternal problems such as anemia or infection before the operation. In the reported case, the team used ultrasound and non-contrast MRI to define anatomy, then proceeded only because the masses were symptomatic and potentially complicated.

Intraoperatively, the goals are to maintain uteroplacental perfusion and minimize uterine stimulation: avoid maternal hypoxia, hypotension, and aortocaval compression; use positioning that reduces diaphragmatic and vena caval compression; keep the procedure as short and blood-sparing as possible; and handle the uterus and adnexa as gently as possible. Fetal cardiac activity should be documented before incision and again after surgery, and if the fetus is viable, intermittent or continuous intraoperative monitoring may be used when technically feasible and when an emergency obstetric response is possible. Obstetric backup should be immediately available for preterm labor, fetal distress, or emergent delivery. Postoperatively, the patient needs close surveillance for contractions, bleeding, rupture of membranes, and fetal growth; tocolytic or progestational support may be considered when uterine irritability is a concern. In this case, the surgeons confirmed fetal cardiac activity during laparotomy, limited the procedure to the symptomatic pedunculated myoma to preserve the pregnancy, and then followed the pregnancy closely to a planned cesarean delivery because the residual pelvic mass would have obstructed labor.

### [Management] Why was elective caesarean delivery at 37 weeks favored over a trial of labor in this patient, and how do the size, number, and location of leiomyomas generally influence mode of delivery?

Elective caesarean delivery at 37 weeks was favored because the residual leiomyoma in this case was not a benign incidental finding; it was a large, fixed pelvic mass arising from the posterior uterine isthmus/torus and bulging into the posterior fornix. As the authors note, it would have been a “huge obstacle” to fetal head descent in the expulsive phase, creating a real risk of obstructed labor and failed vaginal delivery. In addition, she had already undergone laparotomy with uterine myomectomy, so a planned delivery avoided the added stress of labor on the uterine scar and reduced the chance of scar rupture. Scheduling the caesarean at 37 weeks balanced fetal maturity with avoidance of spontaneous labor.

In general, the likelihood of vaginal delivery depends on the leiomyoma’s **size, number, and location**. Small, solitary, subserosal, or fundal fibroids that do not distort the lower uterine segment often permit a trial of labor. By contrast, **large**, **multiple**, **intramural**, or **lower-segment/cervical/pelvic** leiomyomas are more likely to obstruct the birth canal, interfere with uterine contractility, or prevent fetal descent, making caesarean delivery more likely. In this report, the critical feature was the **posterior pelvic location and bulk** of the residual myoma, not simply its presence.
