Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] How should a mildly elevated CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL be interpreted in the second trimester of pregnancy, and why is a serial trend more informative than a single value when evaluating an adnexal or pelvic mass?

A CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester should be interpreted as a **mild, nonspecific elevation**, not as evidence of ovarian malignancy by itself. In pregnancy, CA-125 is an unreliable standalone tumor marker: it can rise physiologically, especially early in gestation, and it may also increase with **benign pelvic processes** such as endometriosis, leiomyoma, inflammation, or peritoneal irritation. In this case, the borderline elevation fit with a **complicated benign uterine mass** rather than cancer, and pathology ultimately showed **benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis near a twisted pedicle**, a plausible cause of marker elevation.

A **serial trend is more informative than a single value** because CA-125 varies with gestational age and with transient benign stimuli. One isolated result above 35 U/mL may simply reflect pregnancy-related fluctuation or local irritation; a **progressive rise over time**, especially when paired with suspicious imaging findings, is more concerning and helps with follow-up. Here, the increase from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over 2 weeks suggested persistence of the process, but it did not establish malignancy—the diagnosis still depended on the **clinical picture and ultrasound/MRI**, not the marker alone.
