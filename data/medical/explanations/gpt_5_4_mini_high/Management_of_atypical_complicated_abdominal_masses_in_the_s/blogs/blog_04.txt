Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Minimalist Pregnant Myomectomy: Resect the Problem, Leave the Risk

## Why this case matters

This is a great example of a principle that is easy to say and hard to execute in the OR: in pregnancy, you don’t have to remove everything you can see — you have to remove what is actively hurting the patient and threatening the pregnancy.

At 21+6 weeks, this patient had severe abdominal pain, dyspnoea, a giant upper abdominal mass, and a second fixed pelvic mass. Imaging was not definitive, the differential still included adnexal pathology, and CA-125 was only mildly elevated — enough to confuse the picture, not enough to diagnose malignancy. In the end, the team did something very pragmatic: they removed the twisted, leaking, highly vascular pedunculated myoma that looked like the acute problem, and they left the fixed pelvic mass in situ to avoid turning a difficult situation into a catastrophic one.

That is the story here: not “how to do a bigger surgery,” but how to do the smallest useful surgery.

## Treat the lesion, not the scan

Pregnancy distorts anatomy. The uterus expands, the bowel gets displaced, and even good ultrasound or MRI can leave you with a messy differential. In this case, the masses mimicked decidualized endometriomas on imaging, and the pelvic lesion seemed to be continuous with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma.

That matters because the pre-op question is not “Can I technically remove both masses?” It’s “What is the minimum intervention that relieves symptoms, addresses the complication, and preserves pregnancy?”

Here, the upper lesion was the one with the red flags:

- huge and vascular
- pedunculated
- twisted
- leaking
- associated with pain and low-grade fever
- pathology later showed ischemic necrosis near the pedicle

That is a surgical lesion. The lower pelvic mass, by contrast, was fixed, smooth, and not the acute driver of the patient’s deterioration. Removing it would likely have meant more uterine manipulation, more bleeding risk, more operative time, and more chance of preterm labor or uterine injury.

Minimalism wasn’t indecision. It was risk management.

## Why “stop after the source is controlled” is often the right move

The OR finding that really defines this case is the moment the team encountered the cranial mass and realized it was the dangerous one. They clamped the stalk, excised it, and closed the uterine root. Then they stopped.

That restraint is the key clinical pearl.

In non-obstetric surgery during pregnancy, the goal is not an anatomically perfect result. It is maternal stabilization with fetal preservation. Once the acute complication is controlled, every additional dissection step must justify itself. In this patient, the remaining pelvic myoma would have become a problem later — and it did — but not one that outweighed the immediate intraoperative risk of chasing it.

The follow-up confirms the logic: the residual myoma was large enough to obstruct the fetal head, so she ultimately underwent elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks. That is not a failure of the initial operation. That is staged problem-solving.

## Fetal monitoring: document viability, don’t pretend monitoring is magic

A recurring issue in non-obstetric surgery is what to do with fetal monitoring. The literature emphasizes checking and documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery when feasible. In this case, the team performed intermittent transvaginal ultrasound surveillance of fetal viability during the operation.

That’s a reasonable practical compromise.

A few points worth remembering:

- Maternal physiology drives fetal physiology.
- Continuous intraoperative monitoring is not always feasible or actionable, especially when gestational age is previable or when operative conditions make it impractical.
- The real goal is to avoid maternal hypoxia, hypotension, acidosis, and prolonged operative stress.
- Fetal checks are useful, but they are not a substitute for good anesthetic and surgical discipline.

If you’re going to operate on a pregnant patient, have a plan for fetal assessment before incision, during the case if possible, and after closure.

## Hemorrhage avoidance starts before the knife goes in

Even a “limited” myomectomy in pregnancy can create trouble with bleeding and anemia. This patient started with iron deficiency anemia, then worsened after surgery and required intravenous iron plus two transfusions. That should not be surprising — it’s a reminder to think ahead.

If you’re contemplating surgery on a vascular myoma in pregnancy, pre-op planning matters:

- correct iron deficiency early if time allows
- have blood products available
- anticipate that “just one pedunculated mass” can still bleed
- remember that pregnancy itself reduces tolerance for physiologic insult

The best hemorrhage strategy is not clever hemostasis after the bleeding starts. It’s choosing an operation that won’t create a hemorrhage you can’t easily control.

This case also shows why operative time matters. The surgery lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes — not trivial, but importantly, it was not allowed to expand into a prolonged attempt at definitive pelvic clearance. In pregnancy, time is not just money; it is uterine stimulation, anesthetic exposure, and physiologic drift.

## Progesterone and tocolysis: common, but the evidence is thin

This case used perioperative progesterone prophylaxis, which is not unusual in real-world practice. But we should be honest: the evidence base for routine progesterone or tocolytic therapy around non-obstetric surgery is limited.

That does not mean these interventions are wrong. It means they should be viewed as adjuncts, not as the main safety strategy.

If the operation is too aggressive, progesterone won’t rescue it. If the uterus is being repeatedly manipulated, tocolytics won’t erase that risk. The most effective “tocolysis” may simply be shorter surgery, less uterine handling, and a lower threshold to stop once the acute lesion has been dealt with.

Use pharmacology thoughtfully, but don’t confuse it with surgical judgment.

## The outcome validates the strategy

Pathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle — exactly the kind of finding that supports a targeted resection rather than a heroic one.

Postoperatively, the patient recovered, the pregnancy continued with close surveillance, fetal growth remained reassuring, and the residual pelvic myoma was ultimately managed expectantly until elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks. Mother and baby did well.

That’s the win here. Not “we removed all the pathology,” but “we protected the pregnancy, solved the urgent problem, and didn’t create a bigger one.”

## Take-home points

- In pregnancy, the best operation is often the smallest operation that safely solves the acute problem.
- A twisted, leaking pedunculated myoma is a different surgical beast than a fixed pelvic mass.
- If one lesion is clearly driving pain, fever, or mechanical compromise, treat that lesion and consider stopping.
- Pre-op anemia and limited transfusion reserve should push you toward a conservative operative plan.
- Document fetal viability before, during, and after surgery when feasible; maternal stability is fetal protection.
- Perioperative progesterone or tocolysis may be used, but the evidence is limited — don’t let drugs substitute for surgical restraint.
- Leaving a residual myoma in situ can be the right call if it reduces hemorrhage, shortens the case, and preserves the pregnancy.
