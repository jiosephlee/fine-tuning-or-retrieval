Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When to Operate on a Mass in Pregnancy: The Second-Trimester Decision Point

## The case that changes how you think about “wait and watch”

Pregnancy plus a giant abdominal mass is a setup for two bad instincts: operate too early and risk the pregnancy, or wait too long and let a mechanical problem become a surgical one. This case sits right in that uncomfortable middle.

A woman in her second trimester presented at 21+6 weeks with acute left upper quadrant pain, pelvic pain, and mild dyspnea. Imaging showed not one but two large cystic-appearing masses, and the initial differential leaned toward adnexal pathology, including decidualized endometriomas. In the end, the cranial lesion was a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma that had twisted, become highly vascular, and was likely leaking/necrosing. The lower pelvic mass was left in place to avoid needless obstetric harm. She later delivered by planned cesarean at 37 weeks with excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes.

That’s the teaching point: in pregnancy, a “mass” is not just an imaging finding. It’s a moving target, a mechanical issue, and sometimes a time-sensitive surgical problem.

## Start with symptoms, not just size

Most pregnancy-associated masses are managed conservatively. But “conservative” does not mean passive. It means you’re constantly asking whether the current plan still makes sense.

In this case, the red flags were not subtle:

- worsening abdominal pain
- dyspnea from mass effect
- concern for torsion, rupture, or infection
- uncertainty about the true origin of the lesions
- limited room for the uterus to grow as the pregnancy advanced

That combination matters more than a single size threshold. A large mass that is asymptomatic and stable is a very different problem from a large mass that is painful, vascular, and anatomically ambiguous.

The team tried temporizing measures first: analgesia and positioning helped a bit, but not enough to make the case go away. That’s the real-world sequence most of us face: stabilize, reassess, then decide whether you’re still buying time or just delaying trouble.

## Imaging in pregnancy: use the tools you can trust

This case is a good reminder that pregnancy narrows your diagnostic toolbox, but doesn’t empty it.

The workup relied on:

- careful physical examination, including bimanual assessment
- ultrasound with Doppler
- non-contrast MRI

That’s the right frame. Ultrasound is first-line, but large gravid anatomy can distort everything. MRI without contrast can be a major problem-solver when US is equivocal, especially for large, posterior, intramural, or anatomically confusing lesions. Gadolinium was appropriately avoided.

The interesting wrinkle here is that the imaging story did not neatly declare “fibroid” or “ovarian mass.” The upper lesion looked like a huge cystic adnexal mass; the lower lesion seemed to sit in continuity with a posterior uterine myoma. In other words, the scan gave enough information to escalate concern, but not enough to settle the diagnosis.

That’s common in pregnancy. Big lesions can erase the normal landmarks you rely on. A visible stalk, feeding vessel, or clear tissue plane may simply not be there on the images. When anatomy is compressed, absence of proof is not proof of absence.

## CA-125 is a clue, not a verdict

The CA-125 was mildly elevated and rising, which understandably added anxiety. But this is exactly where overinterpreting tumor markers gets people into trouble.

CA-125 can be elevated in benign gynecologic disease and can fluctuate during pregnancy. A mild elevation in the second trimester should not be treated as a cancer diagnosis. At most, it is one more piece of uncertainty in a larger puzzle.

In this case, the modest rise in CA-125 nudged the differential toward an adnexal process, but it did not override the overall picture. That’s the right hierarchy: markers support the story, they don’t drive it.

## Why the second trimester is often the least bad time

If surgery cannot be avoided, the second trimester is often the practical compromise.

Why? Because:

- organogenesis is complete
- the uterus is still smaller than it will be later
- the operative field is usually more manageable than in late pregnancy
- the baseline risk of pregnancy loss is generally lower than in the first trimester

That does not mean “second trimester = safe.” It means “second trimester = less bad,” which is often the best you can hope for in obstetric surgery.

In this case, the decision was not made just because the calendar said second trimester. It was made because the patient was symptomatic, the diagnosis remained uncertain, and the masses carried a credible risk of acute complications.

## The operative principle: do the minimum necessary

This is where the case gets really elegant.

The team performed a longitudinal laparotomy, confirmed fetal cardiac activity, obtained peritoneal fluid for culture/cytology, and addressed the dangerous lesion. The cranial mass turned out to be a twisted, highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma with ischemic change. It was excised from its uterine stalk and the base closed.

The lower pelvic mass was left in situ.

That is not indecision. That is surgical restraint.

When operating on masses in pregnancy, the goal is not to “clear everything out.” The goal is to remove the lesion that is driving symptoms or posing imminent risk, while minimizing uterine manipulation, bleeding, and obstetric harm. In other words: shortest safe operation, not most complete operation.

This distinction matters. A fixed pelvic mass that is not acutely threatening may be better left alone if removing it would substantially increase risk to the pregnancy. In this case, leaving the caudal mass preserved the pregnancy and likely avoided a much bigger operation.

## Don’t forget the delivery plan

The antepartum operation did not end the story. The residual pelvic myoma would have been a mechanical obstacle to vaginal delivery, so the team planned an elective cesarean at 37 weeks through the prior scar.

That is the kind of downstream thinking that matters: once you accept that a mass is going to remain, you have to plan around it. Delivery mode in these cases is individualized by size, number, and location of the lesions, not by dogma.

Postoperatively, the patient needed blood support and iron replacement, then had close obstetric follow-up with reassuring fetal growth. Pathology confirmed benign leiomyoma with ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, which is exactly the sort of result that makes the decision feel much less speculative in retrospect.

## Practical take-home points

- Symptomatic masses in pregnancy are not managed by size alone; pain, dyspnea, torsion, rupture, infection, and diagnostic uncertainty all matter.
- Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI are the key imaging tools when the anatomy is confusing.
- A mildly elevated CA-125 in pregnancy is not a cancer diagnosis.
- If surgery is unavoidable, the second trimester is often the least bad window.
- Operate on the lesion that is causing the problem; don’t automatically chase every mass.
- Delivery planning should be based on the residual anatomy, especially if a pelvic mass may obstruct labor.

The big lesson from this case is simple: sometimes the right answer is not “operate” or “don’t operate.” It’s “operate just enough.”
