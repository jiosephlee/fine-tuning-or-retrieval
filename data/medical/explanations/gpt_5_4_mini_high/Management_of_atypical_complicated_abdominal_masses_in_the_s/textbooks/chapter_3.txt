Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Reasoning: Distinguishing Leiomyoma From Adnexal Pathology

The central diagnostic challenge in this case was determining whether the two large cystic masses arose from the ovaries or from the uterus. In pregnancy, this distinction is often blurred because the enlarging uterus displaces pelvic anatomy, limits palpation, and can obscure the pedicle or point of origin on imaging. In this patient, the upper lesion occupied the left upper abdomen and the lower lesion filled the posterior pelvis, so both adnexal and uterine sources had to be considered. The bimanual examination was helpful because it identified a fixed, hard pelvic mass, which supported a true pelvic structural lesion rather than a transient gravid-uterine contour alone, but it still did not define the organ of origin.

On ultrasound, both masses appeared cystic with regular margins and peripheral vascularity, features that can be seen in benign lesions but are also concerning when the lesion is large, complex, and symptomatic. A key mimic in pregnancy is the decidualized endometrioma. Under the influence of high progesterone, the endometrial stroma within an endometrioma can undergo decidual change, creating vascular solid components or mural nodules that may resemble neoplasm. Because of this, decidualized endometriomas can look “atypical” and even malignant on ultrasound or MRI, especially when they are large or multiloculated, and they may be associated with elevated CA-125. In this case, the imaging appearance of a cystic lesion with vascularized components made an adnexal endometriotic lesion plausible, particularly for the cranial mass.

At the same time, the imaging also supported a uterine explanation. Cystic or degenerating leiomyomas can become markedly heterogeneous as they outgrow their blood supply, leading to ischemia, edema, liquefaction, and necrosis. These processes may produce a mass that looks partly solid and partly cystic, with thick walls and internal inhomogeneity on MRI. A pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma is especially deceptive because it can project away from the uterus and appear separate from it, closely simulating an ovarian mass. In this case, the caudal lesion appeared continuous with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma on both ultrasound and MRI, which strongly favored a uterine origin. The cranial mass was more difficult to classify because a distinct stalk or feeding vessel could not be identified, a common limitation when the gravid uterus and very large mass distort pelvic anatomy.

The mildly elevated and rising CA-125 added uncertainty but did not establish malignancy. CA-125 can rise physiologically in pregnancy, particularly in the first trimester, and benign gynecologic conditions such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, and leiomyoma can also increase it. A value just above the usual nonpregnant cutoff, especially without a convincing malignant pattern on imaging or associated adenopathy, should be interpreted cautiously. In this case, the trend supported continued concern but was not specific enough to distinguish endometrioma from leiomyoma or to diagnose cancer.

The patient’s symptoms also fit a complicated benign mass. Her dyspnoea was most consistent with mechanical restriction from the combined volume of the gravid uterus and abdominal masses, rather than primary cardiopulmonary disease, because oxygen saturation, ECG, and hemodynamics were normal. Her pain and low-grade fever could be explained by torsion, leakage, ischemia, or necrosis within a pedunculated leiomyoma, which was later confirmed intraoperatively and histologically. These complications are clinically important because they convert an otherwise expectantly managed mass into a potentially urgent surgical problem.

Ultimately, a definitive preoperative diagnosis remained difficult because each noninvasive clue pointed in a different direction: the imaging suggested both adnexal and uterine possibilities, CA-125 was mildly elevated, and pregnancy itself altered anatomy and tumor marker interpretation. The team therefore had to balance the possibility of a benign lesion against the need to exclude malignancy and prevent worsening complications such as torsion, rupture, infection, or progressive compression. This is the practical lesson of the case: in pregnancy, large complex pelvic masses often cannot be fully characterized before surgery, and management must be based on the total clinical picture rather than on a single imaging or laboratory finding.
