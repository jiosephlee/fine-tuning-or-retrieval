Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Adnexal Mass or Pedunculated Fibroid? The Pregnancy Imaging Trap

## Why this case matters

Pregnancy is a great way to make anatomy lie to you.

This patient, 21+6 weeks pregnant, presented with acute left upper quadrant and pelvic pain plus mild dyspnea. Imaging showed what looked like **two huge cystic abdominal masses**—one cranial mass stretching from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus, and one pelvic mass filling the pouch of Douglas. On ultrasound and MRI, the initial differential leaned toward **decidualized endometriomas or adnexal neoplasms**.

But at laparotomy, the “ovarian” lesion was actually a **huge pedunculated, cystic, twisted leiomyoma** with ischemic change and leakage. The pelvic mass was another leiomyoma, left in situ to avoid obstetric harm.

That’s the trap: in pregnancy, a fibroid can masquerade as an adnexal cystic mass, especially when the uterus is enlarged and the anatomy is distorted.

## The bedside still matters

Before the imaging rabbit hole, this case shows the value of a careful exam.

The abdomen was larger than expected for gestational age, with a tense, tender mass in the left upper quadrant. On bimanual exam, there was also a **fixed, hard, rounded 9 cm mass in the posterior pelvis** bulging into the posterior fornix. That second finding is important: a pelvic mass that feels fixed to the uterus should keep **leiomyoma** high on the list.

In pregnancy, the physical exam won’t always solve the problem, but it can keep you from anchoring too early on “adnexal mass” when the mass may actually be uterine.

## Ultrasound: helpful, but anatomy gets messy

Ultrasound remains the first-line imaging test in pregnancy, especially with color Doppler. In this case, both lesions were described as **large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization**. The cranial lesion had no obvious stalk or feeding vessel, which nudged the team toward an ovarian origin.

That’s a key limitation: a **giant pedunculated subserosal fibroid** can be hard to trace back to the uterus when the gravid uterus is huge and the lesion is high in the abdomen. Degeneration can also make a fibroid look cystic. When the internal architecture is altered, the classic “whorled fibroid” appearance may disappear.

Ultrasound clues that should raise suspicion for leiomyoma include:

- A mass that seems **contiguous with the uterus**
- A **posterior intramural/subserosal lesion** with cystic degeneration
- Peripheral vascularity without a clearly ovarian pattern
- A separate pelvic mass that feels fixed on exam

But if ultrasound can’t confidently define the origin, don’t force the diagnosis.

## Non-contrast MRI: the problem-solving study

MRI without contrast was the right next step here.

The scan showed:

- **Thick walls**
- **Inhomogeneous fluid content**
- **Focal solid hyperintense components**
- No adenopathy
- A caudal lesion whose cleavage plane with the uterus was unclear and seemed continuous with the known posterior myoma

MRI is especially helpful when the mass is **large, posterior, intramural, or multiple**, because it can better define the relationship to the uterus and the uterine cavity.

A few practical MRI lessons from this case:

- **Non-contrast MRI** is the preferred second-line study in pregnancy when ultrasound is indeterminate.
- MRI can help distinguish **uterine origin vs adnexal origin**, but it still may not be definitive in very large masses.
- If the gravid uterus and the lesion are both huge, **the stalk may remain hidden** even on MRI.
- Lack of adenopathy and a lesion that appears to arise from the uterus should keep fibroid high on the differential.

## CA-125 can mislead you

The mildly elevated CA-125 in this case added noise, not clarity.

CA-125 was **41.8 U/mL**, with a small upward trend. That number is barely above the standard cutoff and is not surprising in the second trimester, where CA-125 can be variably elevated for benign reasons. Endometriosis, pregnancy, inflammation, and fibroids can all confound interpretation.

So the pearl here is simple: **a mildly elevated CA-125 does not make an adnexal mass malignant in pregnancy**. It should be interpreted in context, and serial trends matter more than a single value.

## When do you operate?

Most fibroids in pregnancy are managed conservatively. Surgery raises the stakes: bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine irritability are real concerns.

This case crossed the threshold for intervention because of several converging issues:

- Worsening **pain**
- **Dyspnea** from mass effect/restrictive physiology
- Concern for **torsion/rupture/infection**
- Diagnostic uncertainty
- The possibility that the mass would enlarge further with advancing gestation

Intraoperatively, the cranial mass was indeed **twisted, highly vascularized, and leaking**, with pathological evidence of ischemic necrosis near the pedicle. So the decision to intervene was not just about diagnosis—it was about complications that were already unfolding.

## Surgical strategy: do the minimum needed to protect the pregnancy

A smart surgical plan in pregnancy is not “remove everything you can see.” It’s “do enough to solve the problem without creating a bigger obstetric problem.”

In this patient, the team removed the **pedunculated upper mass** and left the **caudal pelvic leiomyoma in situ** because it was fixed and would have increased obstetric risk if aggressively dissected. That restraint mattered.

That approach reflects the key principle in these rare cases:

- Remove the lesion if it is **pedunculated, torsed, symptomatic, or suspicious**
- Avoid enlarging the operation when a second lesion is **stable, uterine in origin, and not driving acute symptoms**
- Keep fetal and uterine preservation front and center

The team also documented fetal cardiac activity and followed the pregnancy closely afterward, which is exactly the kind of multidisciplinary management these cases require.

## Delivery planning is part of the diagnosis

The residual pelvic myoma was not just an incidental finding—it became a delivery issue. Its size and location would have obstructed descent in labor, so the patient ultimately underwent **elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks** with an excellent neonatal outcome.

That’s an important reminder: once you identify a large uterine mass in pregnancy, you’re not just asking “what is it?” You’re also asking:

- Will it grow?
- Will it obstruct the birth canal?
- Will it change the delivery plan?
- Is cesarean the safer route?

## Clinical pearls

- A **pedunculated subserosal fibroid** can look exactly like a large adnexal cyst, especially after cystic degeneration.
- A **bimanual exam** still matters; a fixed posterior pelvic mass should raise suspicion for uterine origin.
- **Color Doppler** helps, but large pregnancy-related masses can obscure the stalk and feeding vessel.
- **Non-contrast MRI** is the best problem-solving test when ultrasound cannot define the origin of a mass.
- A **mild CA-125 elevation** in pregnancy is nonspecific and should not drive a cancer diagnosis by itself.
- Operate for **symptoms, complications, or oncologic concern**—not just because a mass looks dramatic.
- In pregnancy surgery, aim to do the **smallest safe operation** and preserve the uterus and fetus whenever possible.

## Take-home points

- Huge cystic masses in pregnancy are not always ovarian—**fibroids can disguise themselves well**.
- If ultrasound is equivocal, **MRI without contrast** can clarify uterine vs adnexal origin.
- **CA-125 is a weak discriminator in pregnancy**; interpret it cautiously.
- **Pedunculated, twisted, or leaking fibroids** can justify surgery in the second trimester.
- Delivery planning should account for **mass size, number, and location**, because some myomas are mechanical obstacles to vaginal birth.



# CA-125 at 21 Weeks: When a Tumor Marker Creates More Heat Than Light

## The setup

This case is a reminder that a “slightly abnormal” tumor marker can be far more distracting than diagnostic.

A woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, came in with acute left upper quadrant pain, pelvic pain, and mild dyspnoea. Ultrasound and MRI showed two enormous cystic masses, one cranial and one pelvic. CA-125 was mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL and had crept up from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. That tiny bump nudged the differential toward ovarian pathology, especially decidualised endometrioma.

But the real story was different. At laparotomy, the cranial “mass” turned out to be a twisted, pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma with ischemic change and leakage. The pelvic lesion was another myoma, left in situ to avoid obstetric harm. Mother and baby did well.

That is the trap: CA-125 looked like signal. It was mostly noise.

## Why CA-125 is a slippery marker in pregnancy

CA-125 is one of those tests that feels reassuring because it has a number attached to it. The problem is that the number is heavily context-dependent.

In pregnancy, CA-125 is not a clean malignancy marker. It can be elevated in normal gestation, especially in the first and third trimesters, and it also rises in a long list of benign conditions: endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammation, menstruation, and even leiomyoma. In the second trimester, the median values are lower than in the first and third trimesters, but the overlap between “normal,” “benign,” and “concerning” is enormous.

So a CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL in a 21-week pregnant patient is not a cancer diagnosis. It is not even close.

The more useful question is: does the marker fit the story?

In this case, the answer was “maybe, but only weakly.” The masses were huge, vascular, mixed cystic-solid, and symptomatic. There was also mild fever and a slight inflammatory response, but the eventual pathology showed a twisted pedicle with ischemic necrosis — a very plausible reason for a borderline marker bump. In other words, CA-125 was reacting to irritation, not declaring malignancy.

## The real diagnostic problem: anatomy, not algebra

The temptation with tumor markers is to let them drive the differential. In pregnancy, that’s backwards.

The first diagnostic job is anatomical:
- Where is the mass?
- Is it ovarian or uterine?
- Is it cystic, solid, or mixed?
- Is there a stalk, continuity with the uterus, ascites, adenopathy, or concerning vascularity?

In this case, ultrasound and non-contrast MRI were essential because the gravid uterus and the size of the lesions made the anatomy hard to read. The upper mass mimicked a huge decidualised endometrioma, while the lower lesion seemed to arise from or adhere to a posterior uterine myoma. That is exactly where a marker like CA-125 can mislead: it can seem to “confirm” the wrong story.

It didn’t.

## How to use CA-125 without overreacting

CA-125 is most useful when it is treated as an adjunct, not an answer.

A single borderline value is rarely actionable by itself. A serial trend can be helpful, but only if it is interpreted alongside the clinical picture and imaging. In other words: a rising CA-125 should make you look harder, not panic harder.

In this case, the rise from 36 to 41.8 U/mL added concern, but it did not outweigh:
- the benign-leaning imaging features,
- the lack of adenopathy,
- the fact that the symptoms were mechanical and progressive,
- and the obstetric reality that the masses were now causing pain and restrictive dyspnoea.

That balance matters. The decision to operate was driven by symptoms, size, possible torsion/rupture/infection, and the need for a definitive diagnosis — not by the CA-125 alone.

## Practical bedside lessons from this case

This is a good framework when a pregnant patient has a mass and a borderline tumor marker.

First, ask whether the marker changes management or just increases anxiety. If the answer is “mostly anxiety,” slow down.

Second, use imaging to define the mass before you interpret the lab. Ultrasound with Doppler is the first pass. If anatomy is still unclear, non-contrast MRI is the next step, especially after the first trimester. In pregnancy, the goal is clarity without exposing the fetus to unnecessary risk.

Third, remember that serial marker trends are only meaningful when they are consistent with the rest of the picture. A small rise over two weeks is not the same as a rapidly escalating marker in a clearly suspicious lesion.

Finally, do not let a borderline CA-125 push you toward overtreatment. Surgery in pregnancy is sometimes necessary, but the threshold should be symptoms, complications, and oncologic concern — not a number that barely crosses a lab cutoff.

## The bottom line

This case ended well because the team treated CA-125 as one data point, not the diagnosis. They kept the focus on anatomy, symptoms, safe imaging, and obstetric preservation. The final pathology was benign leiomyoma, despite a marker that tried to send everyone down the wrong road.

That’s the lesson: in pregnancy, CA-125 can create heat. It rarely provides much light.

## Take-home points

- A mildly elevated CA-125 in pregnancy is **nonspecific** and should not be treated as evidence of malignancy by itself.
- In the second trimester, CA-125 is usually lower than in the first and third trimesters, but there is substantial overlap with benign disease.
- Use CA-125 as an **adjunct to imaging and clinical context**, not as a stand-alone decision tool.
- When a mass is large, symptomatic, or anatomically unclear, **ultrasound and non-contrast MRI** are more useful than tumor markers.
- A rising CA-125 should prompt **reassessment**, not reflexive escalation.
- In pregnant patients with complex masses, management should prioritize **maternal symptoms, fetal safety, and avoidance of overtreatment**.



# When to Operate on a Mass in Pregnancy: The Second-Trimester Decision Point

## The case that changes how you think about “wait and watch”

Pregnancy plus a giant abdominal mass is a setup for two bad instincts: operate too early and risk the pregnancy, or wait too long and let a mechanical problem become a surgical one. This case sits right in that uncomfortable middle.

A woman in her second trimester presented at 21+6 weeks with acute left upper quadrant pain, pelvic pain, and mild dyspnea. Imaging showed not one but two large cystic-appearing masses, and the initial differential leaned toward adnexal pathology, including decidualized endometriomas. In the end, the cranial lesion was a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma that had twisted, become highly vascular, and was likely leaking/necrosing. The lower pelvic mass was left in place to avoid needless obstetric harm. She later delivered by planned cesarean at 37 weeks with excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes.

That’s the teaching point: in pregnancy, a “mass” is not just an imaging finding. It’s a moving target, a mechanical issue, and sometimes a time-sensitive surgical problem.

## Start with symptoms, not just size

Most pregnancy-associated masses are managed conservatively. But “conservative” does not mean passive. It means you’re constantly asking whether the current plan still makes sense.

In this case, the red flags were not subtle:

- worsening abdominal pain
- dyspnea from mass effect
- concern for torsion, rupture, or infection
- uncertainty about the true origin of the lesions
- limited room for the uterus to grow as the pregnancy advanced

That combination matters more than a single size threshold. A large mass that is asymptomatic and stable is a very different problem from a large mass that is painful, vascular, and anatomically ambiguous.

The team tried temporizing measures first: analgesia and positioning helped a bit, but not enough to make the case go away. That’s the real-world sequence most of us face: stabilize, reassess, then decide whether you’re still buying time or just delaying trouble.

## Imaging in pregnancy: use the tools you can trust

This case is a good reminder that pregnancy narrows your diagnostic toolbox, but doesn’t empty it.

The workup relied on:

- careful physical examination, including bimanual assessment
- ultrasound with Doppler
- non-contrast MRI

That’s the right frame. Ultrasound is first-line, but large gravid anatomy can distort everything. MRI without contrast can be a major problem-solver when US is equivocal, especially for large, posterior, intramural, or anatomically confusing lesions. Gadolinium was appropriately avoided.

The interesting wrinkle here is that the imaging story did not neatly declare “fibroid” or “ovarian mass.” The upper lesion looked like a huge cystic adnexal mass; the lower lesion seemed to sit in continuity with a posterior uterine myoma. In other words, the scan gave enough information to escalate concern, but not enough to settle the diagnosis.

That’s common in pregnancy. Big lesions can erase the normal landmarks you rely on. A visible stalk, feeding vessel, or clear tissue plane may simply not be there on the images. When anatomy is compressed, absence of proof is not proof of absence.

## CA-125 is a clue, not a verdict

The CA-125 was mildly elevated and rising, which understandably added anxiety. But this is exactly where overinterpreting tumor markers gets people into trouble.

CA-125 can be elevated in benign gynecologic disease and can fluctuate during pregnancy. A mild elevation in the second trimester should not be treated as a cancer diagnosis. At most, it is one more piece of uncertainty in a larger puzzle.

In this case, the modest rise in CA-125 nudged the differential toward an adnexal process, but it did not override the overall picture. That’s the right hierarchy: markers support the story, they don’t drive it.

## Why the second trimester is often the least bad time

If surgery cannot be avoided, the second trimester is often the practical compromise.

Why? Because:

- organogenesis is complete
- the uterus is still smaller than it will be later
- the operative field is usually more manageable than in late pregnancy
- the baseline risk of pregnancy loss is generally lower than in the first trimester

That does not mean “second trimester = safe.” It means “second trimester = less bad,” which is often the best you can hope for in obstetric surgery.

In this case, the decision was not made just because the calendar said second trimester. It was made because the patient was symptomatic, the diagnosis remained uncertain, and the masses carried a credible risk of acute complications.

## The operative principle: do the minimum necessary

This is where the case gets really elegant.

The team performed a longitudinal laparotomy, confirmed fetal cardiac activity, obtained peritoneal fluid for culture/cytology, and addressed the dangerous lesion. The cranial mass turned out to be a twisted, highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma with ischemic change. It was excised from its uterine stalk and the base closed.

The lower pelvic mass was left in situ.

That is not indecision. That is surgical restraint.

When operating on masses in pregnancy, the goal is not to “clear everything out.” The goal is to remove the lesion that is driving symptoms or posing imminent risk, while minimizing uterine manipulation, bleeding, and obstetric harm. In other words: shortest safe operation, not most complete operation.

This distinction matters. A fixed pelvic mass that is not acutely threatening may be better left alone if removing it would substantially increase risk to the pregnancy. In this case, leaving the caudal mass preserved the pregnancy and likely avoided a much bigger operation.

## Don’t forget the delivery plan

The antepartum operation did not end the story. The residual pelvic myoma would have been a mechanical obstacle to vaginal delivery, so the team planned an elective cesarean at 37 weeks through the prior scar.

That is the kind of downstream thinking that matters: once you accept that a mass is going to remain, you have to plan around it. Delivery mode in these cases is individualized by size, number, and location of the lesions, not by dogma.

Postoperatively, the patient needed blood support and iron replacement, then had close obstetric follow-up with reassuring fetal growth. Pathology confirmed benign leiomyoma with ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, which is exactly the sort of result that makes the decision feel much less speculative in retrospect.

## Practical take-home points

- Symptomatic masses in pregnancy are not managed by size alone; pain, dyspnea, torsion, rupture, infection, and diagnostic uncertainty all matter.
- Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI are the key imaging tools when the anatomy is confusing.
- A mildly elevated CA-125 in pregnancy is not a cancer diagnosis.
- If surgery is unavoidable, the second trimester is often the least bad window.
- Operate on the lesion that is causing the problem; don’t automatically chase every mass.
- Delivery planning should be based on the residual anatomy, especially if a pelvic mass may obstruct labor.

The big lesson from this case is simple: sometimes the right answer is not “operate” or “don’t operate.” It’s “operate just enough.”



# The Minimalist Pregnant Myomectomy: Resect the Problem, Leave the Risk

## Why this case matters

This is a great example of a principle that is easy to say and hard to execute in the OR: in pregnancy, you don’t have to remove everything you can see — you have to remove what is actively hurting the patient and threatening the pregnancy.

At 21+6 weeks, this patient had severe abdominal pain, dyspnoea, a giant upper abdominal mass, and a second fixed pelvic mass. Imaging was not definitive, the differential still included adnexal pathology, and CA-125 was only mildly elevated — enough to confuse the picture, not enough to diagnose malignancy. In the end, the team did something very pragmatic: they removed the twisted, leaking, highly vascular pedunculated myoma that looked like the acute problem, and they left the fixed pelvic mass in situ to avoid turning a difficult situation into a catastrophic one.

That is the story here: not “how to do a bigger surgery,” but how to do the smallest useful surgery.

## Treat the lesion, not the scan

Pregnancy distorts anatomy. The uterus expands, the bowel gets displaced, and even good ultrasound or MRI can leave you with a messy differential. In this case, the masses mimicked decidualized endometriomas on imaging, and the pelvic lesion seemed to be continuous with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma.

That matters because the pre-op question is not “Can I technically remove both masses?” It’s “What is the minimum intervention that relieves symptoms, addresses the complication, and preserves pregnancy?”

Here, the upper lesion was the one with the red flags:

- huge and vascular
- pedunculated
- twisted
- leaking
- associated with pain and low-grade fever
- pathology later showed ischemic necrosis near the pedicle

That is a surgical lesion. The lower pelvic mass, by contrast, was fixed, smooth, and not the acute driver of the patient’s deterioration. Removing it would likely have meant more uterine manipulation, more bleeding risk, more operative time, and more chance of preterm labor or uterine injury.

Minimalism wasn’t indecision. It was risk management.

## Why “stop after the source is controlled” is often the right move

The OR finding that really defines this case is the moment the team encountered the cranial mass and realized it was the dangerous one. They clamped the stalk, excised it, and closed the uterine root. Then they stopped.

That restraint is the key clinical pearl.

In non-obstetric surgery during pregnancy, the goal is not an anatomically perfect result. It is maternal stabilization with fetal preservation. Once the acute complication is controlled, every additional dissection step must justify itself. In this patient, the remaining pelvic myoma would have become a problem later — and it did — but not one that outweighed the immediate intraoperative risk of chasing it.

The follow-up confirms the logic: the residual myoma was large enough to obstruct the fetal head, so she ultimately underwent elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks. That is not a failure of the initial operation. That is staged problem-solving.

## Fetal monitoring: document viability, don’t pretend monitoring is magic

A recurring issue in non-obstetric surgery is what to do with fetal monitoring. The literature emphasizes checking and documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery when feasible. In this case, the team performed intermittent transvaginal ultrasound surveillance of fetal viability during the operation.

That’s a reasonable practical compromise.

A few points worth remembering:

- Maternal physiology drives fetal physiology.
- Continuous intraoperative monitoring is not always feasible or actionable, especially when gestational age is previable or when operative conditions make it impractical.
- The real goal is to avoid maternal hypoxia, hypotension, acidosis, and prolonged operative stress.
- Fetal checks are useful, but they are not a substitute for good anesthetic and surgical discipline.

If you’re going to operate on a pregnant patient, have a plan for fetal assessment before incision, during the case if possible, and after closure.

## Hemorrhage avoidance starts before the knife goes in

Even a “limited” myomectomy in pregnancy can create trouble with bleeding and anemia. This patient started with iron deficiency anemia, then worsened after surgery and required intravenous iron plus two transfusions. That should not be surprising — it’s a reminder to think ahead.

If you’re contemplating surgery on a vascular myoma in pregnancy, pre-op planning matters:

- correct iron deficiency early if time allows
- have blood products available
- anticipate that “just one pedunculated mass” can still bleed
- remember that pregnancy itself reduces tolerance for physiologic insult

The best hemorrhage strategy is not clever hemostasis after the bleeding starts. It’s choosing an operation that won’t create a hemorrhage you can’t easily control.

This case also shows why operative time matters. The surgery lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes — not trivial, but importantly, it was not allowed to expand into a prolonged attempt at definitive pelvic clearance. In pregnancy, time is not just money; it is uterine stimulation, anesthetic exposure, and physiologic drift.

## Progesterone and tocolysis: common, but the evidence is thin

This case used perioperative progesterone prophylaxis, which is not unusual in real-world practice. But we should be honest: the evidence base for routine progesterone or tocolytic therapy around non-obstetric surgery is limited.

That does not mean these interventions are wrong. It means they should be viewed as adjuncts, not as the main safety strategy.

If the operation is too aggressive, progesterone won’t rescue it. If the uterus is being repeatedly manipulated, tocolytics won’t erase that risk. The most effective “tocolysis” may simply be shorter surgery, less uterine handling, and a lower threshold to stop once the acute lesion has been dealt with.

Use pharmacology thoughtfully, but don’t confuse it with surgical judgment.

## The outcome validates the strategy

Pathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle — exactly the kind of finding that supports a targeted resection rather than a heroic one.

Postoperatively, the patient recovered, the pregnancy continued with close surveillance, fetal growth remained reassuring, and the residual pelvic myoma was ultimately managed expectantly until elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks. Mother and baby did well.

That’s the win here. Not “we removed all the pathology,” but “we protected the pregnancy, solved the urgent problem, and didn’t create a bigger one.”

## Take-home points

- In pregnancy, the best operation is often the smallest operation that safely solves the acute problem.
- A twisted, leaking pedunculated myoma is a different surgical beast than a fixed pelvic mass.
- If one lesion is clearly driving pain, fever, or mechanical compromise, treat that lesion and consider stopping.
- Pre-op anemia and limited transfusion reserve should push you toward a conservative operative plan.
- Document fetal viability before, during, and after surgery when feasible; maternal stability is fetal protection.
- Perioperative progesterone or tocolysis may be used, but the evidence is limited — don’t let drugs substitute for surgical restraint.
- Leaving a residual myoma in situ can be the right call if it reduces hemorrhage, shortens the case, and preserves the pregnancy.



# Fibroids, Scar, and Outlet Obstruction: Planning Delivery After Antepartum Myomectomy

## The case that forces you to think about geometry, not labels

This was not a routine “fibroid in pregnancy” story. A woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, showed up with left upper quadrant pain, pelvic pain, and dyspnoea. Ultrasound found two large abdominal masses. One of them turned out, at laparotomy, to be a torsed, highly vascular pedunculated uterine leiomyoma that was leaking and likely driving some of the fever and pain. The other was left behind in the pelvis.

That residual mass is where the delivery lesson lives.

Fast forward: the pregnancy continued, fetal growth stayed reassuring, and the patient ultimately needed an elective cesarean because the remaining posterior pelvic myoma would have been a mechanical obstacle to vaginal birth. In other words, the antepartum operation solved the acute problem, but it also clarified that the pelvis itself was the real barrier.

## The pelvis does not care what the ultrasound report calls the mass

The first trap in cases like this is to get distracted by the label. “Fibroid,” “endometrioma,” “adnexal mass,” “decidualized lesion” — those are important diagnostically, but delivery planning is driven by anatomy.

In this case, the lower lesion was fixed in the lesser pelvis, bulging the posterior fornix on bimanual exam, and later interpreted as a large posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. That matters more than the fact that the tumor marker CA-125 was only mildly elevated. Pregnancy makes CA-125 noisy; the exam and imaging tell you whether a vaginal delivery is even mechanically plausible.

The key question is simple: can the fetal head descend past this mass?

Here, the answer was effectively no.

A fibroid sitting in the pouch of Douglas or low posterior pelvis is not just “present.” It can be an outlet obstruction. If it is fixed, low, and large enough to occupy the birth canal’s real estate, a trial of labor becomes a setup for failure or emergency cesarean.

## Why the antepartum myomectomy changed the conversation

The cranial mass was removed at 21 weeks because it was symptomatic, twisted, and threatening to declare itself as an acute complication. That was a pregnancy-preserving move. The team deliberately limited the operation and left the caudal mass in situ to minimize obstetric risk.

That’s an important point: not every myomectomy creates the same delivery problem. A pedunculated stalk excision is not the same as a deep reconstruction of the uterine wall. But once uterine surgery has happened during the pregnancy, you stop thinking in absolutes and start thinking in risk layers.

In this case, there were two separate reasons vaginal birth was unrealistic:

1. The residual fibroid sat low in the pelvis and would obstruct descent.
2. There had already been uterine surgery earlier in the same pregnancy.

The scar issue did not stand alone — the anatomy was already enough to push the team toward a planned cesarean. The prior surgery just made “let’s see what happens in labor” a much less attractive option.

## Why elective cesarean was the safer path

This is the part where dogma often takes over. People hear “fibroid” and either reflexively say “vaginal delivery is impossible” or, on the other side, “cesarean is overused.” Neither reflex is useful.

What made cesarean reasonable here was not fibroid diagnosis in the abstract. It was the combination of:

- a large residual posterior pelvic mass,
- fixed location in the lesser pelvis,
- likely obstruction of the fetal head during the expulsive phase,
- recent uterine surgery,
- and the desire to avoid an emergency in a pelvis already full of bad options.

The team waited until 37 weeks, when the fetus was mature enough and the pregnancy had remained stable. That is a very different risk profile than forcing labor into a pelvis that has already told you “no.”

If there is a practical pearl here, it’s this: when the fibroid is low, fixed, and functionally occupying the outlet, the delivery plan is usually made by anatomy, not by ideology.

## Postpartum regression can buy you time

One of the quiet wins in this case is what happened after delivery: the residual pelvic myoma regressed somewhat postpartum, the patient remained asymptomatic, breastfeeding went well, and she deferred further surgery.

That’s not failure — that’s good surgical restraint.

Fibroids can shrink after pregnancy, and if the patient is clinically well, it is perfectly reasonable to delay definitive surgery rather than chase every lesion immediately. In this case, the residual mass decreased to about 7.5 cm and the patient preferred to wait. That’s a legitimate plan when symptoms are absent and the acute obstetric issue has been resolved.

## Clinical pearls from the case

- A bimanual exam matters. A fixed mass bulging the posterior fornix is a mechanical warning sign, not an incidental finding.
- In pregnancy, fibroid management is about size, number, and location — especially relation to the lower pelvis and birth canal.
- Mild CA-125 elevation in pregnancy should not drive management by itself.
- Non-contrast MRI is useful when ultrasound leaves the origin of a mass unclear.
- Antepartum myomectomy may preserve pregnancy, but it also forces you to rethink delivery anatomy.
- If a residual fibroid makes vaginal delivery unrealistic, a planned cesarean is often safer than waiting for labor to prove the point.
- If symptoms resolve and the fibroid regresses postpartum, definitive surgery can often be deferred.

## Take-home points

- Fibroid delivery planning is an anatomy problem, not a dogma problem.
- A large posterior pelvic fibroid can create true outlet obstruction and make vaginal delivery unrealistic.
- Prior antepartum myomectomy changes the risk conversation, but the residual pelvic mass may be the decisive factor.
- Planned cesarean is often the safest option when labor is likely to fail or become emergent.
- Postpartum fibroid regression can justify waiting on definitive surgery if the patient is asymptomatic.
