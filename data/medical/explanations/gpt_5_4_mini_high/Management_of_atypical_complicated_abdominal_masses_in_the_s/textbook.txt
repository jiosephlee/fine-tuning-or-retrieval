Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Immediate Assessment of Abdominal Masses in Mid-Pregnancy

This patient presented at 21 weeks’ gestation with several features that immediately made the case high risk but not yet unstable: she was nulliparous, in her 40s, obese, and reported acute pain in both the left upper abdomen and the pelvis, together with mild dyspnoea. In a pregnant patient, this combination should prompt a rapid bedside assessment aimed first at maternal stability and second at obstetric safety. The key early question is whether the symptoms reflect a benign pregnancy-related problem, a complicated mass effect, or an acute surgical abdomen. In this case, the later work-up would show that the symptoms were due to very large abdominal masses, but the initial approach correctly began with stabilization and careful examination.

Her initial cardiopulmonary evaluation was reassuring. Oxygen saturation, blood pressure, heart rate, and ECG were normal, arguing against immediate hemodynamic compromise or primary cardiac instability as the cause of dyspnoea. This is an important first step in pregnancy because maternal instability rapidly threatens fetal well-being. Even when the final diagnosis is non-obstetric, fetal assessment remains a parallel priority, and the clinical team must preserve the pregnancy whenever possible while investigating the maternal symptoms.

The physical examination already suggested the presence of a large intra-abdominal process. The abdomen was more distended than expected for the gestational age, and there was a tender, taut-elastic mass occupying the left upper quadrant, measured clinically at about 18 cm. The absence of ascites was somewhat reassuring, but the size and tenderness of the mass raised concern for a complicated lesion rather than a simple incidental finding. The bimanual vaginal examination was particularly informative: it identified a fixed, hard, rounded mass in the posterior pelvis, partially bulging into the posterior fornix. In pregnant patients, this exam can still be very useful because it helps localize a pelvic mass relative to the uterus, cervix, and cul-de-sac, and it provides clues about mobility, fixation, and likely origin. Although the gravid uterus limits palpation, a careful bimanual exam may distinguish a posterior pelvic lesion from a mass arising higher in the abdomen.

Early management was appropriately supportive while the diagnosis was being clarified. Intravenous paracetamol and a semi-orthostatic position reduced her pain substantially and likely lessened diaphragmatic compression from the enlarged uterus and masses, improving the subjective dyspnoea without the need for oxygen or ventilatory support. This kind of conservative symptomatic treatment is appropriate when the patient is stable, because it can provide relief while avoiding unnecessary intervention before the anatomy is understood.

The inpatient laboratory findings required cautious interpretation. A low-grade fever prompted infection screening, which showed a negative SARS-CoV-2 test but a urinary infection with *Klebsiella pneumoniae*. Mildly elevated inflammatory markers and leukocytosis were present, but pregnancy itself can produce a degree of neutrophilia, so these results must be interpreted in context rather than assumed to indicate a major systemic inflammatory process. The iron-deficiency anaemia also mattered clinically, because reduced haemoglobin reserves can worsen tolerance of pain, infection, dyspnoea, and eventual surgery. Overall, the bedside picture was of a stable pregnant patient with significant mass effect, possible superimposed infection, and careful need for further imaging rather than immediate emergency intervention.

## Focused Diagnostic Workup for Complex Pelvic and Abdominal Masses During Pregnancy

The diagnostic approach to a complex pelvic or abdominal mass in pregnancy must answer two questions at the same time: what is the lesion, and is it causing, or at risk of causing, an obstetric or maternal complication. Because pregnancy limits the use of ionizing radiation and narrows the differential diagnosis, the workup relies on careful clinical examination, ultrasound as the first-line study, and non-contrast MRI when anatomy remains unclear or when the lesion has suspicious or mixed features. In this case, the gravid uterus and the very large size of the lesions made preoperative localization difficult, so imaging had to be interpreted together with the bimanual examination and the patient’s symptoms.

Ultrasound is the most important initial test because it is safe, widely available, and able to define size, morphology, wall characteristics, internal echoes, and the relationship of a lesion to the uterus and adnexa. Colour Doppler adds information about vascularity and can sometimes help identify a pedicle or feeding vessel, which is particularly useful when trying to distinguish a pedunculated or degenerating leiomyoma from an adnexal mass. Here, ultrasound showed two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation. The cranial lesion measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, while the caudal lesion measured about 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm and appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. These findings already suggested that at least part of the process was uterine in origin, although the size and displacement caused by pregnancy made this uncertain.

When ultrasound cannot confidently establish the origin of a mass, non-contrast MRI is the preferred second-line study in pregnancy. MRI provides a better overview of pelvic anatomy and improves tissue characterisation without exposing the fetus to radiation. Gadolinium is generally avoided because it crosses the placenta and its fetal safety is not well established, so the study is usually limited to non-contrast sequences. In this patient, MRI showed thick-walled lesions with inhomogeneous fluid content and focal solid-appearing components, and the cleavage plane of the caudal mass was unclear, suggesting continuity with the posterior uterine myoma seen on ultrasound. No adenopathy was identified. These findings supported a differential diagnosis of decidualised endometrioma versus cystic degeneration of leiomyoma, but they did not fully resolve the anatomical origin of the lesions.

Laboratory studies are useful mainly to detect complications and to support perioperative planning rather than to establish the diagnosis of the mass itself. This patient had iron deficiency anaemia, mild inflammatory marker elevation, and neutrophilia, which can be partly physiological in pregnancy but also warranted evaluation for infection. The low-grade fever led to infectious testing, including SARS-CoV-2 screening and urine culture; the latter grew Klebsiella pneumoniae, which was clinically relevant for treatment and may have contributed to the febrile episode. These results were important context, but they did not explain the masses.

Serum tumour markers must be interpreted cautiously in pregnancy. CA-125 is especially problematic because it can rise physiologically, particularly in the first trimester and again near term, and it may also be elevated in benign conditions such as endometriosis, leiomyoma, and pelvic inflammation. In this case, CA-125 was only mildly elevated and showed a slight upward trend, which increased diagnostic uncertainty but did not establish malignancy. The other markers were normal, which was somewhat reassuring, but no serum marker panel can reliably exclude cancer during pregnancy. Overall, this case illustrates a common limitation in pregnant patients with large masses: uterine enlargement, organ displacement, and mass effect reduce the accuracy of preoperative localisation, so diagnosis depends on combining clinical examination, ultrasound with Doppler, selective laboratory testing, and non-contrast MRI.

## Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Reasoning: Distinguishing Leiomyoma From Adnexal Pathology

The central diagnostic challenge in this case was determining whether the two large cystic masses arose from the ovaries or from the uterus. In pregnancy, this distinction is often blurred because the enlarging uterus displaces pelvic anatomy, limits palpation, and can obscure the pedicle or point of origin on imaging. In this patient, the upper lesion occupied the left upper abdomen and the lower lesion filled the posterior pelvis, so both adnexal and uterine sources had to be considered. The bimanual examination was helpful because it identified a fixed, hard pelvic mass, which supported a true pelvic structural lesion rather than a transient gravid-uterine contour alone, but it still did not define the organ of origin.

On ultrasound, both masses appeared cystic with regular margins and peripheral vascularity, features that can be seen in benign lesions but are also concerning when the lesion is large, complex, and symptomatic. A key mimic in pregnancy is the decidualized endometrioma. Under the influence of high progesterone, the endometrial stroma within an endometrioma can undergo decidual change, creating vascular solid components or mural nodules that may resemble neoplasm. Because of this, decidualized endometriomas can look “atypical” and even malignant on ultrasound or MRI, especially when they are large or multiloculated, and they may be associated with elevated CA-125. In this case, the imaging appearance of a cystic lesion with vascularized components made an adnexal endometriotic lesion plausible, particularly for the cranial mass.

At the same time, the imaging also supported a uterine explanation. Cystic or degenerating leiomyomas can become markedly heterogeneous as they outgrow their blood supply, leading to ischemia, edema, liquefaction, and necrosis. These processes may produce a mass that looks partly solid and partly cystic, with thick walls and internal inhomogeneity on MRI. A pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma is especially deceptive because it can project away from the uterus and appear separate from it, closely simulating an ovarian mass. In this case, the caudal lesion appeared continuous with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma on both ultrasound and MRI, which strongly favored a uterine origin. The cranial mass was more difficult to classify because a distinct stalk or feeding vessel could not be identified, a common limitation when the gravid uterus and very large mass distort pelvic anatomy.

The mildly elevated and rising CA-125 added uncertainty but did not establish malignancy. CA-125 can rise physiologically in pregnancy, particularly in the first trimester, and benign gynecologic conditions such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, and leiomyoma can also increase it. A value just above the usual nonpregnant cutoff, especially without a convincing malignant pattern on imaging or associated adenopathy, should be interpreted cautiously. In this case, the trend supported continued concern but was not specific enough to distinguish endometrioma from leiomyoma or to diagnose cancer.

The patient’s symptoms also fit a complicated benign mass. Her dyspnoea was most consistent with mechanical restriction from the combined volume of the gravid uterus and abdominal masses, rather than primary cardiopulmonary disease, because oxygen saturation, ECG, and hemodynamics were normal. Her pain and low-grade fever could be explained by torsion, leakage, ischemia, or necrosis within a pedunculated leiomyoma, which was later confirmed intraoperatively and histologically. These complications are clinically important because they convert an otherwise expectantly managed mass into a potentially urgent surgical problem.

Ultimately, a definitive preoperative diagnosis remained difficult because each noninvasive clue pointed in a different direction: the imaging suggested both adnexal and uterine possibilities, CA-125 was mildly elevated, and pregnancy itself altered anatomy and tumor marker interpretation. The team therefore had to balance the possibility of a benign lesion against the need to exclude malignancy and prevent worsening complications such as torsion, rupture, infection, or progressive compression. This is the practical lesson of the case: in pregnancy, large complex pelvic masses often cannot be fully characterized before surgery, and management must be based on the total clinical picture rather than on a single imaging or laboratory finding.

## Management Strategy in Pregnancy: When to Observe and When to Operate

The first principle in managing large abdominal or pelvic masses during pregnancy is to avoid surgery unless there is a clear reason to intervene. Many fibroids and some adnexal masses can be followed conservatively if the patient is asymptomatic, the lesion looks benign on ultrasound, and there is no sign of acute complication. In pregnancy, the diagnostic work-up is intentionally limited to modalities that are considered safe for the fetus, mainly careful clinical examination, ultrasound with Doppler, and non-contrast MRI when ultrasound is not enough. Serum markers such as CA-125 can add context, but they are not reliable enough in pregnancy to determine malignancy or to decide on surgery by themselves.

In this patient, observation was no longer sufficient because the masses were causing clinically important symptoms and there was genuine concern for complication. She had severe left upper quadrant and pelvic pain, mild dyspnoea from mass effect, an abdomen larger than expected for gestational age, and a fixed pelvic mass on bimanual examination. Imaging showed two very large, vascular, mixed cystic-solid lesions, and the upper lesion was particularly difficult to characterize. Conservative measures such as analgesia and positional changes improved pain only partially. The later appearance of low-grade fever increased concern for infection, torsion, or leakage from a twisted lesion. In other words, this was not a stable incidental mass; it was a symptomatic and potentially complicated lesion with ongoing mechanical and obstetric risk.

If surgery is necessary during pregnancy, the second trimester is usually the preferred time. By this point, organogenesis is complete, so the risk of teratogenic injury from surgery itself is lower than in the first trimester, while the uterus is still smaller than it will be later in gestation, making access and hemostasis easier. Waiting longer would have meant more uterine enlargement, more mass effect, and greater technical difficulty. In this case, the team also had to balance the desire for diagnostic certainty against the need to protect the pregnancy. Because the lesions were large, numerous, vascular, and possibly intramural, open laparotomy offered better exposure and control than laparoscopy. The case therefore illustrates that the route of surgery is not chosen by a single rule, but by gestational age, mass size and location, vascularity, and the surgeon’s ability to work safely and quickly.

Preoperative counselling and informed consent were central to the plan. The patient needed to understand that surgery in pregnancy carries maternal risks, including bleeding and infection, as well as obstetric risks such as pregnancy loss or preterm birth. The operative strategy was designed to minimize uterine manipulation and preserve the pregnancy. Fetal cardiac activity was checked intraoperatively, and the team used a multidisciplinary approach involving obstetrics, gynecology, and surgery. This kind of coordination is especially important when the diagnosis is uncertain and the lesion may be either adnexal or uterine in origin.

The operation changed the diagnosis in a decisive way. The cranial mass, initially thought on imaging to be possibly adnexal or a degenerated cystic lesion, proved to be a huge pedunculated leiomyoma that was twisted, highly vascular, and leaking. This finding explained the patient’s pain and likely contributed to the fever. Because the lesion had a pedicle and was the source of acute symptoms, the team performed a limited myomectomy, carefully clamping and excising only the dangerous cranial mass and then closing its uterine base. The caudal mass was fixed, smooth, and likely represented a deep intramural-subserosal leiomyoma; removing it would have required a larger and riskier uterine dissection. It was therefore left in situ to avoid excessive bleeding and to reduce obstetric risk. This is an important management lesson: in pregnancy, the goal is not necessarily complete removal of every lesion, but the safest intervention that relieves the immediate problem.

Peritoneal fluid was sampled because the atypical appearance and fever raised concern for alternative diagnoses, including infection or malignancy, and tissue diagnosis was needed once the patient was safely delivered through surgery. Perioperative cefazolin provided surgical prophylaxis, while antibiotics were also tailored to the concurrent urinary tract infection. Progesterone support was given after the operation as part of the effort to support the ongoing pregnancy. Postoperatively, her anemia worsened, which is not surprising after myomectomy in a patient who already had iron deficiency. She required intravenous iron and blood transfusion, decisions that were driven by the combination of low hemoglobin, pregnancy, and the need to restore maternal oxygen-carrying capacity.

Pathology ultimately confirmed benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, validating the decision to remove only the symptomatic pedunculated mass and to avoid more extensive surgery. The residual pelvic myoma later influenced delivery planning because it would have obstructed vaginal birth, so an elective caesarean was chosen at term. The broader lesson from this case is that careful observation is appropriate for many masses in pregnancy, but worsening symptoms, mass effect, torsion, infection, or diagnostic uncertainty justify intervention. Just as importantly, avoiding overtreatment can be as valuable as achieving a tissue diagnosis: the safest pregnancy-preserving operation is often the least extensive one that solves the immediate clinical problem.

## Outcome, Delivery Planning, and Clinical Lessons for Future Practice

The immediate postoperative course was mainly characterized by recovery from the laparotomy and correction of anemia rather than by surgical instability. After myomectomy, the patient’s hemoglobin fell further in the setting of persistent iron deficiency, so she required intravenous ferric carboxymaltose and two blood transfusions, with subsequent hematologic improvement. She was discharged 10 days later without operative complications. Histopathology was important for confirming the diagnosis: the resected lesion and the residual pelvic mass were benign uterine leiomyomas, and the cranial mass showed ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. This pathology matched the intraoperative impression of torsion and compromised blood flow, which likely explained the acute pain, low-grade fever, and atypical cystic appearance on imaging. Peritoneal fluid culture and cytology were negative, supporting a benign process.

Because the caudal mass was left in situ and occupied the lower pelvis, ongoing antenatal surveillance was essential. The team followed the pregnancy with serial obstetric assessments, including fetal growth and amniotic fluid monitoring, and the fetus maintained appropriate growth. This stepwise follow-up is typical after conservatively managed fibroids in pregnancy: once the acute complication is treated, the residual lesion and the pregnancy must both be reassessed over time. In this case, the residual leiomyoma later decreased slightly in size postpartum, consistent with the known tendency of fibroids to regress after delivery as the pregnancy-related hormonal and vascular environment resolves.

The residual caudal myoma strongly influenced delivery planning. Because it was large, fixed in the pelvis, and located in a position that would interfere with fetal head descent, vaginal birth was considered unsafe and likely mechanically impossible. For that reason, the team planned an elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks, before labor began, using the prior abdominal scar. This timing balanced fetal maturity against the risk of obstructed labor, bleeding, and technical difficulty from further uterine enlargement. The mother and newborn had an excellent outcome, and the cesarean proceeded uneventfully.

Postpartum, the patient remained asymptomatic, was breastfeeding well, and chose to defer further surgery because the residual myoma had partially regressed and she preferred observation after achieving a good maternal outcome. That shared decision-making is clinically appropriate when symptoms are absent and the patient understands the trade-off between surveillance and interval surgery. The broader lesson from this case is that atypical abdominal masses in pregnancy should be approached with a high index of suspicion for pedunculated or degenerating fibroids, especially when bimanual examination suggests uterine continuity. Careful use of ultrasound and non-contrast MRI helps distinguish uterine from adnexal disease, and surgery should be reserved for complications or oncologic concern. Finally, delivery mode should be individualized to the size, number, and location of the remaining myomas, because anatomy—not diagnosis alone—determines whether vaginal delivery is feasible and safe.
