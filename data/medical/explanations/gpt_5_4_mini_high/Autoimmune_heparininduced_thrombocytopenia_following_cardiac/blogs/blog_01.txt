Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# When the Platelets Fall Too Soon: Early HIT After Cardiac Surgery

## The trap: “It’s just post-bypass thrombocytopenia”
Cardiac surgery is one of the few settings where thrombocytopenia is almost expected, which is exactly why HIT gets missed.

This case is a good reminder that the *trend* matters more than the absolute platelet count. An elderly man underwent valve + aortic + CABG surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, had an uncomplicated operative course, and then his platelets steadily fell from 210 to 155 on POD1, then to 50 by POD5 and 26 by POD6. That would be easy to dismiss as “routine post-op thrombocytopenia” if not for two red flags:

1. the platelet count kept falling rather than stabilizing, and  
2. he developed a thrombotic complication — an acute left parietal infarct.

That combination should make HIT jump up the differential fast.

## What makes this case unusual
The surprising part is timing. Classic HIT after cardiac surgery usually shows up in the POD5–10 window. This patient’s platelet decline started earlier, before POD5.

That only makes sense once you learn about the pre-op heart catheterization: he had received a small dose of UFH about a week before surgery. That earlier exposure likely primed the immune response, so the larger intraoperative heparin dose during bypass and the postoperative dalteparin then “unmasked” the syndrome.

So the teaching point is simple: **early-onset HIT after cardiac surgery can happen if the patient had recent heparin exposure before the operation**. Don’t let the calendar fool you.

## When HIT is more than classic HIT
The immunoassay was strongly positive, which fit the clinical picture. But the functional assay was more interesting.

The serotonin-release assay showed:
- strong platelet activation at low heparin concentrations,
- no activation at very high heparin concentrations, which is typical for HIT,
- but also **strong serotonin release even in the absence of added heparin**.

That last feature points to **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** — a more aggressive form of HIT in which antibodies can activate platelets even without heparin present. Clinically, aHIT tends to look uglier than garden-variety HIT:
- more severe thrombocytopenia,
- more thrombosis,
- more persistent platelet activation,
- and sometimes consumptive coagulopathy with falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and even schistocytes.

That pattern showed up here. The platelet nadir reached 13 × 10⁹/L, fibrinogen fell during the course, and schistocytes appeared later while he was being treated.

One especially novel lab feature in this case was only **partial inhibition by the Fc receptor-blocking antibody IV.3**. In other words, the antibody behavior was so strong that the usual blockade wasn’t fully protective in vitro. That’s not something you’ll use at the bedside, but it reinforces how biologically potent these antibodies can be.

## Bedside diagnosis: don’t wait for the perfect lab result
The most important diagnostic move is to treat the patient, not the assay.

In this case:
- infection was not convincing,
- hemolysis was not convincing,
- transfusion-related causes were not relevant,
- and the timing plus thrombosis plus positive PF4/polyanion ELISA made HIT the leading diagnosis.

If your post-cardiac surgery patient has:
- an unexpected platelet fall that keeps progressing,
- a new thrombosis or neurologic event,
- and recent heparin exposure,

you should assume HIT until proven otherwise.

That means:
- **stop all heparin products immediately**
- send HIT testing
- start a **non-heparin anticoagulant** if suspicion is significant

## Treatment: anticoagulate the hypercoagulability, not the platelet count
This patient was initially given prophylactic fondaparinux when the platelet count began to fall. Once HIT became likely and the stroke was identified, heparin products were stopped and he was treated with **argatroban** plus **high-dose IVIG**.

That choice makes sense:
- **argatroban** is short-acting and titratable, which matters when there’s recent stroke and concern for bleeding
- **IVIG** can be helpful in aHIT by interfering with Fc-mediated platelet activation
- serial **fibrinogen and D-dimer** can give you a rough sense of whether the hypercoagulable process is settling down

In this case, the labs suggested ongoing consumptive activity despite treatment, so the team later switched to **therapeutic fondaparinux**. The platelet count eventually recovered, and the neurologic deficits resolved completely.

A few practical points:
- aHIT may not respond briskly the way you’d hope after IVIG
- persistent thrombocytopenia does not mean you’ve “missed something else” — it may reflect continued antibody activity
- if the patient is still prothrombotic, don’t be falsely reassured by a low platelet count; the clotting risk can remain high

## What to remember at the bedside
The big mistake is assuming that “early” means “not HIT.” In cardiac surgery patients, early thrombocytopenia is common — but **continued decline**, **new thrombosis**, or **recent pre-op heparin exposure** should push you toward HIT quickly.

This case is also a reminder that HIT is not a single phenotype. Autoimmune HIT can be severe, prolonged, and hard to interpret in the lab. If the clinical picture fits, start treatment first and sort out the serology second.

## Take-home points
- A platelet fall after CPB is common; a **progressive fall** is not something to shrug off.
- **HIT can present before POD5** if there was **recent preoperative heparin exposure**.
- New neurologic change or stroke in this setting should raise concern for HIT-associated thrombosis.
- **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** can show heparin-independent platelet activation and a more severe, persistent course.
- When suspicion is high: **stop all heparin and start a non-heparin anticoagulant promptly**.
- In aHIT, **IVIG may help**, but response can be slow or incomplete.
