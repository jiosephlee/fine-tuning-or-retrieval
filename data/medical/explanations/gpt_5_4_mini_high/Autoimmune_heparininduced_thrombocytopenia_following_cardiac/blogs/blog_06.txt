Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Postoperative Delirium or Occult Stroke? Arterial Thrombosis in HIT

## Why this case is a trap

Post-cardiac surgery delirium is common enough that it can become background noise. That’s exactly what makes this case uncomfortable: an elderly man looked like he had “just delirium,” but his platelets were falling and his brain was not right. CT then showed an acute left parietal infarct.

The diagnosis was not routine postoperative thrombocytopenia. It was autoimmune HIT, a severe HIT phenotype with heparin-independent platelet activation. In other words: this was HIT with arterial thrombosis, and the neurologic change was the clue.

## The timeline that should make you pause

This patient had a pre-op cardiac cath with a small dose of UFH, then a week later underwent major cardiac surgery with full-dose heparin on bypass. Postoperatively, he was started on dalteparin prophylaxis, but his platelet count kept sliding: 210 pre-op, 155 on POD1, 50 by POD5, then 26 on POD6.

That trajectory matters.

After cardiopulmonary bypass, platelets commonly dip early. What you do not want to ignore is a platelet count that keeps drifting down, especially when the patient is also developing neurologic changes or thrombosis. The case also shows why early-onset HIT after cardiac surgery should make you ask about prior heparin exposure. The cath lab dose likely primed the reaction.

## Don’t let “delirium” blind you to stroke

The tricky part here is that there were no dramatic focal findings. The patient just had worsening delirium. That is exactly how a postoperative stroke can hide in plain sight.

A parietal infarct may not announce itself with obvious hemiparesis. In a patient recovering from cardiac surgery, delirium, sedation, pain, sleep disruption, metabolic issues, and ICU noise can all blur the exam. But when mental status is worsening rather than improving, especially alongside thrombocytopenia, you need to widen the lens.

This case argues for a low threshold to image the brain when there is:
- worsening or unexplained delirium
- new inattention, aphasia, neglect, or subtle asymmetry
- a falling platelet count with concern for HIT
- any thrombotic clue, even if focal deficits are not obvious

The key mistake is anchoring on “postoperative delirium” and missing the infarct.

## HIT is not just a venous clot syndrome

We often teach HIT as DVT/PE risk, but it can absolutely present with arterial thrombosis. Stroke is part of the syndrome.

That matters because a platelet fall plus a neurologic event should move HIT high on the list, not low. In this case, the PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive, and the functional assay ultimately supported autoimmune HIT. The serum activated platelets even without heparin, which is a classic clue for aHIT. The patient’s course was also unusually severe, with profound thrombocytopenia and evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy.

This is the part worth remembering: if the brain is changing and the platelets are falling after heparin exposure, think HIT-related arterial thrombosis until proven otherwise.

## What to do at the bedside

The management principles are straightforward, even when the diagnosis is messy:

Stop all heparin products immediately.

Start a non-heparin anticoagulant if HIT is suspected and thrombosis is possible or confirmed. In this case, the team moved to argatroban because it has a short half-life, which is attractive when there is recent surgery and a new stroke. High-dose IVIG was added because this was autoimmune HIT with severe, persistent platelet activation.

One practical pearl: in severe aHIT, stopping heparin is necessary but may not be enough. These patients can keep clotting and can have ongoing platelet consumption despite initial therapy. If the clinical picture is worsening, treatment escalation is not optional.

This patient later transitioned to therapeutic fondaparinux and ultimately recovered his platelet count and neurologic function. But the important lesson is earlier: the worsening delirium was not benign, and imaging changed the whole trajectory.

## Take-home points

- Delirium after cardiac surgery is common, but worsening delirium is not something to wave away.
- A falling platelet count plus neurologic change should trigger consideration of HIT-related thrombosis.
- HIT can cause arterial events, including stroke, not just venous thromboembolism.
- Early-onset HIT after cardiac surgery can occur if there was recent pre-op heparin exposure.
- In suspected HIT with thrombosis, stop heparin and start non-heparin anticoagulation without waiting around for confirmatory testing.
- Autoimmune HIT can be unusually severe and may require IVIG in addition to anticoagulation.

The big takeaway: after cardiac surgery, “delirium” is a symptom, not a diagnosis. If the platelets are falling, keep looking.
