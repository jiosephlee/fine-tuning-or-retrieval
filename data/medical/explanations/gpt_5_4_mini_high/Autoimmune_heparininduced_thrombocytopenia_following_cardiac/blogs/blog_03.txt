Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Strong ELISA, Weird SRA: What to Do With Atypical HIT Laboratory Results

## The case that should make you pause

This is the kind of HIT case that can fool you if you let the lab stamp do the thinking.

An elderly man after complex cardiac surgery had a platelet count that fell steadily from 210 to 155 on postoperative day 1, then kept drifting down through POD5 to 50, and by POD6 to the mid-20s. He also developed worsening delirium, and brain imaging showed an acute left parietal infarct. No shock, no major bleeding, no transfusions, no clear infection, no hemolysis. The PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive. The SRA came back “indeterminate.”

But when the functional assay was unpacked, it was anything but reassuring: strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin, strong release at low-dose heparin, and suppression at very high heparin. That pattern is a big clue for autoimmune HIT, not a reason to wave the patient away.

## Why the timing mattered

After cardiac surgery, platelets commonly dip early from bypass-related consumption and then recover. That’s normal postoperative physiology. What makes HIT stand out is usually the second hit: a platelet fall around POD5–10, especially if there’s thrombosis.

This patient had a twist that explains the early onset. A week before surgery, he had a heart cath and received a small dose of UFH. That preoperative heparin exposure likely primed the immune response, so the postoperative platelet fall began earlier than the usual postcardiac surgery HIT window.

That’s a pearl worth remembering: a “too-early” HIT pattern after surgery often isn’t too early if the patient had heparin exposure days earlier.

## Reading the lab report like a clinician, not a robot

The PF4 ELISA is an immunoassay. It tells you antibodies are present. It does **not** tell you whether those antibodies are platelet-activating.

The SRA is the functional assay, and the pattern matters more than the label.

In classic HIT, you expect platelet activation at low heparin concentrations, with inhibition at very high heparin concentrations. That’s exactly what happened here at one level: serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with no release at 100 U/mL heparin. That part is very much HIT biology.

The unusual part was serotonin release even at **0 U/mL heparin**. That’s the autoimmune HIT clue. These antibodies can activate platelets in a heparin-independent fashion, so the assay is no longer behaving like textbook HIT.

Then came the extra wrinkle: the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 only partially blocked serotonin release. Usually, IV.3 should shut this down more completely. In this case, the lab called the result “indeterminate” because the profile didn’t match their usual pattern. But the biology still pointed toward aHIT.

That’s the key lesson: “indeterminate” is not the same as “negative,” and it definitely isn’t the same as “benign.”

## What autoimmune HIT changes at the bedside

Autoimmune HIT is the aggressive cousin of classic HIT. The antibodies have heparin-independent platelet-activating properties, which can mean:

- more profound thrombocytopenia,
- more persistent platelet activation,
- more consumptive/coagulopathic features,
- and a higher risk of thrombosis.

In this case, the patient had a thrombotic stroke and a nadir platelet count of 13 × 10⁹/L. That’s not routine postoperative thrombocytopenia. That’s a hypercoagulable immune process until proven otherwise.

The team did the right thing clinically: all heparin was stopped, anticoagulation was changed to argatroban, and high-dose IVIG was given. When hypercoagulability seemed to persist, they switched to fondaparinux, and the platelet count gradually recovered. Neurologically, he ultimately did well.

## Practical approach when the HIT workup doesn’t fit the textbook

If the patient’s story is convincing, don’t let a weird functional assay slow you down.

The bedside question is not “Is the SRA pretty?” It’s “Is this patient at high risk of ongoing HIT-mediated thrombosis right now?”

When the answer is yes, act first and clarify the lab later:

- stop all heparin exposure,
- start a non-heparin anticoagulant,
- look for thrombosis,
- and involve hematology/reference lab experts early.

In severe cases like this one, IVIG is worth thinking about, especially when thrombocytopenia is profound or the hypercoagulable picture seems to keep evolving despite anticoagulation.

Also: if the functional assay is odd, ask the reference lab for the raw pattern. The exact heparin concentrations and inhibition behavior matter. Sometimes the report summary hides the real signal.

## Take-home points

- A strong PF4 ELISA plus a compatible clinical picture should carry real weight, even if the SRA is reported as “indeterminate.”
- Serotonin release at **0 U/mL heparin** is a major red flag for autoimmune HIT.
- Suppression at **very high heparin** still supports HIT biology.
- Partial failure of Fc receptor blockade does **not** rule out HIT; it may reflect an unusually aggressive aHIT phenotype.
- In postcardiac surgery patients, always ask about heparin exposure in the prior 5–10 days, including “small” cath-lab doses.

The lab doesn’t have to look textbook for the disease to be real. In HIT, the platelet count, timing, thrombosis, and assay pattern all have to be read together.
