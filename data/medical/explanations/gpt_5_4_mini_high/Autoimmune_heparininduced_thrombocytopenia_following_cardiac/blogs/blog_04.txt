Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Argatroban, IVIG, and Then Fondaparinux: Anticoagulating aHIT After Fresh Surgery and Stroke

## Why this case is so interesting

This is not your usual post-cardiac-surgery thrombocytopenia story.

An elderly man had cardiac surgery with heavy intraoperative UFH exposure, then developed a platelet fall that began early, worsened rapidly, and was complicated by an acute parietal stroke. The lab picture eventually pointed to **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** — a more aggressive HIT phenotype where platelet activation can occur even without heparin present. That matters, because aHIT often behaves like a runaway thrombotic syndrome: profound thrombocytopenia, consumptive coagulopathy, and thrombosis can continue even when you think you’ve “done the right thing” by stopping heparin and starting an alternative anticoagulant.

This case also highlights a real-world management tension: the patient was **freshly postoperative, profoundly thrombocytopenic, and had an acute cerebral infarct**. So the team had to balance bleeding risk against an illness that was still actively prothrombotic.

## The clinical pattern that should make you stop and think

After cardiac surgery, platelet counts commonly drop early from hemodilution, CPB-related platelet consumption, and perioperative stress. That part is expected.

What is *not* expected is a platelet count that keeps falling after the early postoperative period, especially when it drops hard and is paired with new thrombosis. In this case:

- Pre-op platelets were normal.
- POD1 platelet count fell modestly, which could still fit routine post-CPB changes.
- Then the count kept dropping to 50 by POD5 and later to the 20s, with a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L.
- Delirium worsened, and brain imaging revealed an acute left parietal infarct.

That combination should push HIT high on the list, even before the serologies come back.

One subtle but important clue: the HIT-like fall began before the usual POD5–10 window for post-cardiac-surgery HIT. That seemed odd until a pre-op heparin exposure during diagnostic cath was uncovered. Prior heparin can “prime” the immune response and make the postoperative HIT timeline look earlier than expected.

## Classic HIT vs autoimmune HIT

Most of us were taught HIT as a heparin-dependent, antibody-mediated platelet activation disorder: heparin binds PF4, antibodies bind the complex, Fc receptors get cross-linked, and platelets activate.

**aHIT is different.** The antibodies can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. Clinically, that tends to mean:

- more severe thrombocytopenia
- more thrombosis
- consumptive coagulopathy features
- a more prolonged or refractory course

That’s exactly why aHIT is the entity to keep in mind when the patient looks “too sick for ordinary HIT.”

In this case, the PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive, but the serotonin release assay had an unusual profile:

- strong serotonin release at low-dose heparin
- **strong release even at 0 heparin**
- only partial inhibition with the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3

That’s a very aHIT-ish pattern, and the 0-heparin activation is the key clue that this was not just garden-variety HIT.

## Why argatroban first made sense

When the stroke was discovered and the platelet count had fallen further, the team stopped prophylactic fondaparinux and started **argatroban**.

That’s a reasonable initial move for a few reasons:

1. **It’s a short-acting, titratable anticoagulant.**  
   In a fresh postoperative patient with a recent stroke, the ability to rapidly turn off the anticoagulant matters.

2. **It avoids heparin exposure.**  
   In suspected HIT/aHIT, that is non-negotiable.

3. **It can be monitored with aPTT.**  
   That gives you at least some bedside feedback in a dynamic situation.

But here’s the catch: a therapeutic aPTT does **not** necessarily mean you have adequately controlled the hypercoagulability of aHIT.

That’s a crucial pearl from this case. The patient’s fibrinogen fell, d-dimer rose, and schistocytes appeared while on argatroban — all suggesting ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite apparently appropriate anticoagulation by lab monitoring.

So if the aPTT looks “fine” but the biology looks worse, don’t let the number lull you into complacency.

## Why IVIG was added

High-dose IVIG is often used in aHIT because the disorder seems to be driven by intensely platelet-activating antibodies that can persist despite standard anticoagulation. IVIG is used to help blunt the immune activation and interrupt the platelet Fc-receptor-mediated storm.

In this patient, IVIG was given because the thrombocytopenia was profound and the course looked like severe aHIT. The platelet response was only modest and slower than hoped, which itself is a reminder that aHIT can be stubborn.

If you’re used to thinking of IVIG as a “rapid fix,” this case is a good reality check. Sometimes the response is incomplete, especially in severe disease.

## Why they switched to fondaparinux despite argatroban

This is the most interesting management move in the case.

The team switched from argatroban to **therapeutic fondaparinux** because there were signs that the HIT-associated hypercoagulability was still not adequately controlled:

- fibrinogen was falling
- d-dimer was rising
- schistocytes appeared
- thrombocytopenia remained severe

So even though the aPTT was being managed on argatroban, the team judged that the overall thrombotic process was still active.

Why fondaparinux?

- It’s a practical once-daily subcutaneous option.
- It is commonly used off-label in HIT.
- It can be a reasonable long-run anticoagulant when ongoing parenteral titration is less appealing.
- In this case, the team also gave **antithrombin concentrate** first because fondaparinux is AT-dependent and the AT level was mildly low.

That’s a nice example of matching the anticoagulant to the biology of the illness, not just to the monitor.

## What to remember in the ED, ICU, or post-op floor

The big teaching point is that **HIT management is not just “stop heparin and start something else.”** In severe aHIT, you may need to treat a continuing immune-thrombotic process, not just replace one anticoagulant with another.

A few practical pearls:

- A postoperative platelet fall that **starts early and keeps falling** deserves a broader HIT workup, especially if there was any recent pre-op heparin exposure.
- In suspected aHIT, **thrombosis can occur with profound thrombocytopenia** — low platelets do not protect the patient from clotting.
- A therapeutic **aPTT on argatroban does not guarantee disease control** if fibrinogen is dropping, d-dimer is rising, or schistocytes are appearing.
- If the functional HIT assay shows **platelet activation at 0 heparin**, think aHIT.
- IVIG can be a useful adjunct when the phenotype is severe or refractory.
- Fondaparinux can be a reasonable next step when the patient remains prothrombotic and you want a simpler anticoagulant strategy, but this should be done thoughtfully and in context.

## Take-home points

- **Early postoperative thrombocytopenia that keeps worsening after cardiac surgery is not “routine until proven otherwise.”**
- **aHIT can present with heparin-independent platelet activation, severe thrombocytopenia, and thrombosis.**
- **A therapeutic aPTT on argatroban does not necessarily mean the HIT storm is under control.**
- **Falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and schistocytes are red flags for ongoing consumptive coagulopathy.**
- **IVIG and a switch to fondaparinux may be reasonable in severe/refractory aHIT, even after starting argatroban.**

## Bottom line

This case is a great reminder that severe HIT is sometimes an immune-thrombotic emergency rather than a simple anticoagulation problem. When the platelet count is crashing, the patient is thrombosing, and the labs suggest ongoing consumptive coagulopathy, you may need to think beyond “standard HIT” and into the world of **autoimmune HIT**.
