Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Heparin Use in Cardiac Surgery and the Biology of HIT vs Autoimmune HIT

Unfractionated heparin (UFH) remains the standard anticoagulant for cardiopulmonary bypass because it acts immediately, can be monitored reliably with the activated clotting time, is inexpensive, and is rapidly reversed with protamine at the end of surgery. Those features are particularly important during bypass, where blood is exposed to large artificial surfaces and clot formation must be prevented quickly and then “switched off” once the operation is complete. In contrast to many other anticoagulants, UFH is also not dependent on renal or hepatic clearance, which is useful in older cardiac surgery patients who may have variable organ function.

After cardiac surgery, some platelet count fall is expected even in uncomplicated cases. Cardiopulmonary bypass causes hemodilution, platelet activation, and platelet consumption, so the platelet count commonly drops during the first 1 to 3 postoperative days and then begins to recover. This early, mild thrombocytopenia is therefore not, by itself, abnormal. What is more concerning is a platelet count that continues to fall beyond the early postoperative period, or a second platelet decline after an initial recovery. In practice, the timing and pattern of the count change are often more informative than the absolute nadir alone.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a recognized complication after cardiac surgery, with a frequency that depends on the heparin regimen used and the population studied. It is seen more often when postoperative prophylaxis continues with heparin products, particularly unfractionated heparin, than when other anticoagulants are used, although HIT can still occur with low-molecular-weight heparin. The key point for the learner is that cardiac surgery patients are a special diagnostic setting: postoperative thrombocytopenia is common, but HIT must be considered because the syndrome is strongly linked to thrombosis rather than isolated laboratory abnormalities.

Classic HIT is an immune, prothrombotic disorder caused by IgG antibodies directed against complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) and heparin. PF4 is a positively charged protein released from platelet alpha granules; when it binds heparin, it forms antigenic multimolecular complexes. The antibodies then bind these complexes and their Fc portions cross-link FcγRIIa receptors on platelets, leading to platelet activation, release of more PF4, and generation of procoagulant platelet microparticles. Similar immune activation can also involve monocytes and endothelial cells, amplifying thrombin generation. This is why HIT is not simply a platelet-destructive process: it is a highly thrombotic state driven by immune-mediated coagulation activation.

That thrombin-generating biology explains why HIT is usually more dangerous for thrombosis than for bleeding. Platelets are consumed, so thrombocytopenia is present, but the dominant clinical problem is clot formation—venous thrombosis, arterial thrombosis, limb ischemia, stroke, or other ischemic events. Bleeding is uncommon unless the platelet count becomes extremely low or there are other reasons for hemorrhage. Thus, in a postoperative patient, a falling platelet count with new thrombosis should immediately raise concern for HIT, even if there is no bleeding.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) is a more severe variant within the HIT spectrum. In aHIT, the antibodies have heparin-independent platelet-activating properties, meaning they can activate platelets even when little or no heparin is present in vitro and can continue to drive thrombosis after heparin has been stopped. This explains why aHIT often behaves more aggressively than classic HIT: the platelet count may fall profoundly, hypercoagulability can persist, and thrombosis can progress despite appropriate discontinuation of heparin. Clinically, aHIT is often suggested by very severe thrombocytopenia, consumptive coagulopathy, thrombotic complications, or an unexpectedly persistent or worsening course after heparin withdrawal.

The timing of HIT also depends on recent heparin exposure. In a heparin-naive patient, the usual postoperative window for HIT is around day 5 to day 10, reflecting the time needed to generate detectable antibodies after exposure. However, if the patient has had heparin exposure in the preceding days or weeks, antibodies may already be present or may be rapidly reactivated, and HIT can appear earlier. This is especially relevant in cardiac surgery patients, who may receive heparin before the operation during catheterization or other procedures. In such patients, an early postoperative platelet fall cannot be dismissed as “too soon” for HIT without checking whether there was prior heparin exposure.

Taken together, these concepts set up the key clinical challenge in this case: distinguishing the expected early platelet decline after cardiopulmonary bypass from an immune heparin reaction, and recognizing when the severity of thrombocytopenia and thrombosis suggests not just classic HIT but the more aggressive autoimmune HIT phenotype.

## Case Timeline: From Preoperative Heparin Exposure to Early Postoperative Stroke

The patient was an elderly man with exertional dyspnea and longstanding atrial fibrillation treated with apixaban. A new systolic murmur led to further cardiac evaluation. Because he had not held apixaban before diagnostic catheterization, the procedure was done through a transradial approach with a reduced intraprocedural dose of unfractionated heparin. That detail later became important, because it established a heparin exposure before the operation and helped explain why subsequent heparin-induced thrombocytopenia could appear earlier than expected.

One week later, he underwent major cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass: aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic graft replacement, coronary artery bypass grafting, and left atrial appendage excision. As is standard in bypass surgery, he received a large intraoperative dose of unfractionated heparin. The operation was uncomplicated from a hemostatic standpoint: he came off bypass easily, had stable hemodynamics, no major bleeding, and no transfusion requirement. These features made an early postoperative platelet fall less likely to be explained by shock, hemorrhage, or massive transfusion.

As expected after cardiac surgery, the platelet count fell modestly on postoperative day 1, from 210 to 155 ×10⁹/L. A limited early decline after cardiopulmonary bypass is common and usually reflects hemodilution, platelet activation, and perioperative consumption. The clinical concern arose because the platelet count did not stabilize or begin to recover. Instead, postoperative dalteparin prophylaxis was started and continued on successive days, adding further heparin exposure after surgery. By postoperative day 5, the platelet count had fallen progressively to 50 ×10⁹/L. That pattern was more worrisome than routine post-bypass thrombocytopenia because the decline was persistent, substantial, and temporally linked to ongoing heparin exposure.

At the same time, the patient was developing postoperative delirium. Even without focal neurological findings, the worsening mental status together with the falling platelet count prompted brain imaging. This was a key decision point: in a postoperative cardiac patient with a rapidly declining platelet count, delirium should not be dismissed as nonspecific if there is any possibility of thrombotic complications. Computed tomography revealed an acute left parietal infarct, confirming an arterial thrombotic event rather than a bleeding complication.

The timing of the thrombocytopenia and stroke was especially informative. In postcardiac surgery patients, classic HIT usually begins between postoperative days 5 and 10, after an immune response has had time to develop. This patient’s apparent onset was unusually early, but the earlier catheterization with unfractionated heparin provided the missing piece: he likely had already been immunized by preoperative heparin exposure, so the major bypass-related heparin exposure and subsequent dalteparin prophylaxis encountered a primed immune system. The result was a coherent timeline of early progressive thrombocytopenia, postoperative delirium, and thrombotic stroke—features that, taken together, strongly supported HIT biology and later proved consistent with autoimmune HIT.

## Diagnostic Approach to Severe Thrombocytopenia After Cardiac Surgery

Severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery should be approached as a problem of pattern recognition. After cardiopulmonary bypass, many patients have an early platelet fall from hemodilution, platelet activation, and perioperative consumption, with the nadir often occurring in the first 2 to 3 postoperative days and then recovering. In contrast, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is suggested by a new or progressive platelet decline beginning around postoperative day 5 to 10, especially when it is accompanied by thrombosis. In this patient, the platelet count fell more than 50% from baseline and continued to worsen rather than recovering, while brain imaging revealed an acute ischemic stroke. That combination made a prothrombotic immune cause much more likely than simple postoperative thrombocytopenia.

Clinical probability tools such as the 4Ts score can be helpful, but they are less straightforward after cardiac surgery than in other settings. Timing is harder to interpret because nearly everyone has had heparin and most patients have some early postoperative thrombocytopenia from bypass itself. For that reason, the most useful clues are the overall trajectory of the platelet count, the presence of new thrombosis, and the lack of a better alternative explanation. In this case, the platelet fall was not a mild, self-limited postoperative decline; it was progressive and ultimately profound, and it was paired with stroke, making HIT the leading diagnosis even before confirmatory testing was complete.

The rest of the laboratory evaluation helped narrow the differential. The hemoglobin was reduced, but it was stable over serial measurements and consistent with expected postoperative anemia rather than active bleeding. The white blood cell count and differential were normal, cultures were negative, and the patient was hemodynamically stable, all of which argued against sepsis, cardiogenic shock, or another critical-illness state as the main driver of thrombocytopenia. Coagulation studies were also informative: the prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were normal, and fibrinogen was elevated rather than low. That pattern fits an early postoperative acute-phase response and argues against disseminated intravascular coagulation, in which one would usually expect consumption of fibrinogen and more global coagulation abnormalities. Later, if fibrinogen falls and d-dimer rises, that can reflect severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy, but the initial profile did not support DIC.

Peripheral blood smear review is an essential part of this workup. In this case, the smear showed true thrombocytopenia without platelet clumping, which excludes pseudothrombocytopenia from EDTA-dependent platelet aggregation. There were no schistocytes initially, making thrombotic microangiopathy such as postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura less likely at that time, and the normal haptoglobin supported the absence of significant hemolysis. The absence of red cell fragments does not absolutely exclude a microangiopathic process early on, but in context it reduced the likelihood of TTP/HUS as the primary diagnosis. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was also less convincing because the platelet decline began too early for the newly introduced postoperative medications, and post-transfusion purpura was implausible because no transfusions were given.

A key diagnostic step in postoperative HIT is to search carefully for every heparin exposure, including small procedural doses that may be overlooked. Here, the usual intraoperative heparin given for bypass did not explain the very early timing by itself, but review of the history uncovered heparin given during preoperative cardiac catheterization. That earlier exposure provided the temporal trigger for an anamnestic immune response and explained why thrombocytopenia could begin unusually early after surgery. This kind of history is critical, because missing a small prior heparin exposure can make HIT seem “too early” and lead clinicians away from the correct diagnosis.

Once the clinical picture strongly suggested HIT, treatment could not wait for complete laboratory clarification. HIT is a highly prothrombotic condition, and continued heparin exposure can worsen both thrombocytopenia and thrombosis. Therefore, stopping all heparin and switching to a non-heparin anticoagulant was appropriate as soon as suspicion became substantial, rather than waiting for confirmatory testing. In this case, the strong immunoassay result later supported that bedside judgment. The broader lesson is that after cardiac surgery, severe thrombocytopenia should be interpreted against the expected postoperative platelet pattern, but a progressive fall, especially with thrombosis and no better alternative explanation, should prompt immediate action for suspected HIT.

## HIT Laboratory Testing and the Serologic Recognition of Autoimmune HIT

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is diagnosed by combining the clinical picture with laboratory testing, because no single assay is sufficient on its own. In practice, testing falls into two broad categories. Immunoassays, such as the PF4/polyanion ELISA used in this case, detect antibodies that bind PF4-heparin complexes. These tests are sensitive, but they do not prove that the antibodies are actually capable of activating platelets. Functional assays, such as the serotonin-release assay (SRA), are more specific because they measure whether patient antibodies can trigger platelet activation in the presence of donor platelets. Thus, an immunoassay establishes that HIT antibodies are present, while a functional assay shows that those antibodies are pathogenic.

The patient’s PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive at 2.2 optical density units. Optical density helps estimate post-test probability: the higher the signal, the more likely the antibody is clinically relevant rather than an incidental postoperative seropositivity. This is especially important after cardiac surgery, where many patients develop detectable anti-PF4/heparin antibodies without true HIT. In this case, the strong ELISA result fit the clinical concern for HIT, but the diagnosis still required functional confirmation because postoperative thrombocytopenia can have multiple causes.

The SRA provides that functional confirmation by exposing radiolabeled donor platelets to the patient’s serum with different heparin concentrations. In classic HIT, a small amount of heparin promotes formation of PF4-heparin immune complexes, which cross-link platelet FcγRIIa receptors and cause serotonin release. At very high heparin concentrations, however, the complexes cannot form effectively, so platelet activation falls off again. That “bell-shaped” response—activation at therapeutic heparin concentrations and inhibition at high heparin concentration—is characteristic of typical HIT.

This patient’s SRA showed an important deviation from the classic pattern. His serum caused strong serotonin release not only in the presence of low-dose heparin, but also in the complete absence of heparin. Strong release at 0 U/mL heparin is the key serologic clue to autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a more aggressive form of HIT in which antibodies can activate platelets even without added heparin. Clinically, that helps explain why thrombocytopenia can be unusually severe and can persist despite stopping heparin.

At the same time, serotonin release disappeared at 100 U/mL heparin. That finding matters because it shows that the reaction still depends on PF4-heparin biology, rather than reflecting a completely unrelated platelet-activating antibody. In other words, the antibodies were heparin-independent in their ability to trigger platelets at low or absent heparin, but the system still retained the classic high-heparin “off switch” seen in HIT.

The McMaster laboratory also repeated the assay with monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks FcγRIIa on platelets. In ordinary HIT, IV.3 should markedly suppress serotonin release, because the pathogenic mechanism is Fc receptor-mediated platelet activation. In this case, however, IV.3 only partially inhibited the reaction. That atypical result led to an initial “indeterminate” interpretation, because the assay did not behave like a standard HIT sample with complete Fc receptor blockade.

The final interpretation depended on integrating the laboratory pattern with the clinical course. The patient had early postoperative thrombocytopenia after documented preoperative heparin exposure, a strong ELISA, thrombotic stroke, and persistent severe thrombocytopenia with consumptive features. Taken together, these findings were more consistent with a severe aHIT phenotype than with a false-positive functional assay. The assay was not dismissed; rather, it was understood as showing unusually strong, partially Fc receptor blockade-resistant platelet activation, which broadened the recognized serologic spectrum of aHIT.

Serial SRA testing was also informative. As the patient recovered, later samples showed a gradually more typical evolution toward heparin-dependent reactivity, and the last sample showed only minimal activation without heparin but strong activation with heparin present. This serial change supported the idea that the initial striking heparin-independent pattern reflected a severe but waning autoimmune response rather than an unrelated laboratory artifact.

The practical lesson is that unusual functional assay results should be reviewed with the reference laboratory when the clinical suspicion for HIT is high. In this case, the atypical SRA profile might have been misleading if interpreted in isolation. Careful correlation with the timing of thrombocytopenia, the thrombotic complication, and the strong ELISA allowed the diagnosis of autoimmune HIT to be recognized and treated appropriately.

## Management of Severe Autoimmune HIT, Monitoring of Hypercoagulability, and Clinical Lessons

Once autoimmune HIT (aHIT) was suspected, the first management priority was to stop further heparin exposure and replace it with a non-heparin anticoagulant. This is essential because HIT is primarily a prothrombotic disorder, not a bleeding disorder, even when the platelet count is very low. In this case, the postoperative prophylactic dalteparin was stopped, and therapeutic anticoagulation was started with argatroban. Argatroban was a reasonable initial choice because it is given intravenously, can be titrated closely, and has a short half-life, which is particularly useful in a patient who had just suffered an ischemic stroke and might need rapid dose adjustment if bleeding occurred. The drug was adjusted to a partial thromboplastin time target of 55–85 seconds, emphasizing the importance of careful laboratory monitoring in the immediate postoperative period.

Because aHIT is driven by HIT antibodies with heparin-independent platelet-activating properties, anticoagulation alone is sometimes not enough. High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was therefore added as rescue therapy. In aHIT, IVIG is used to blunt Fc receptor–mediated platelet activation and interrupt the immune process that sustains platelet consumption and thrombosis. The patient’s platelet count rose only modestly after IVIG, from 13 × 10^9/L to a transient peak of 54 × 10^9/L, which was less than would typically be expected if the disease were responding well. That muted response supported the impression that this was an unusually severe form of aHIT.

In severe HIT, the platelet count is not the only marker of disease activity. This case illustrates why clinicians should also follow indirect signs of ongoing thrombin generation and consumptive coagulopathy. Despite treatment, the patient developed falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and new schistocytes on the blood film. Together, these findings suggested that hypercoagulability was still active, even while the platelet count was slowly beginning to recover. Because of concern that argatroban was not adequately controlling the prothrombotic state, therapy was changed to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. Fondaparinux is an indirect factor Xa inhibitor that depends on antithrombin for activity, so the team also gave antithrombin concentrate when the antithrombin level was mildly low. This was a pragmatic step to avoid underdosing an antithrombin-dependent agent in a patient with severe consumptive illness.

The patient’s recovery was gradual but ultimately complete. Platelets normalized over weeks, reaching 155 × 10^9/L by postoperative day 62 and 202 × 10^9/L by day 87. Fondaparinux was continued until postoperative day 65, after which the patient returned to long-term apixaban for atrial fibrillation. His neurologic deficits resolved fully, underscoring that severe aHIT can still have a good outcome when recognized promptly and treated aggressively.

The main clinical lessons are practical. First, early postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery should not be dismissed, especially when there has been recent preoperative heparin exposure that can trigger an early HIT presentation. Second, postoperative delirium may be the only clue to stroke or other thrombosis in HIT, so worsening mental status warrants a low threshold for neuroimaging. Third, when HIT testing is atypical, expert laboratory interpretation matters: unusual serotonin-release assay patterns may still represent aHIT, and management should be guided by the overall clinical picture, not by a single screening result alone.
