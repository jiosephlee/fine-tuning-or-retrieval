Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# When the Platelets Fall Too Soon: Early HIT After Cardiac Surgery

## The trap: “It’s just post-bypass thrombocytopenia”
Cardiac surgery is one of the few settings where thrombocytopenia is almost expected, which is exactly why HIT gets missed.

This case is a good reminder that the *trend* matters more than the absolute platelet count. An elderly man underwent valve + aortic + CABG surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, had an uncomplicated operative course, and then his platelets steadily fell from 210 to 155 on POD1, then to 50 by POD5 and 26 by POD6. That would be easy to dismiss as “routine post-op thrombocytopenia” if not for two red flags:

1. the platelet count kept falling rather than stabilizing, and  
2. he developed a thrombotic complication — an acute left parietal infarct.

That combination should make HIT jump up the differential fast.

## What makes this case unusual
The surprising part is timing. Classic HIT after cardiac surgery usually shows up in the POD5–10 window. This patient’s platelet decline started earlier, before POD5.

That only makes sense once you learn about the pre-op heart catheterization: he had received a small dose of UFH about a week before surgery. That earlier exposure likely primed the immune response, so the larger intraoperative heparin dose during bypass and the postoperative dalteparin then “unmasked” the syndrome.

So the teaching point is simple: **early-onset HIT after cardiac surgery can happen if the patient had recent heparin exposure before the operation**. Don’t let the calendar fool you.

## When HIT is more than classic HIT
The immunoassay was strongly positive, which fit the clinical picture. But the functional assay was more interesting.

The serotonin-release assay showed:
- strong platelet activation at low heparin concentrations,
- no activation at very high heparin concentrations, which is typical for HIT,
- but also **strong serotonin release even in the absence of added heparin**.

That last feature points to **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** — a more aggressive form of HIT in which antibodies can activate platelets even without heparin present. Clinically, aHIT tends to look uglier than garden-variety HIT:
- more severe thrombocytopenia,
- more thrombosis,
- more persistent platelet activation,
- and sometimes consumptive coagulopathy with falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and even schistocytes.

That pattern showed up here. The platelet nadir reached 13 × 10⁹/L, fibrinogen fell during the course, and schistocytes appeared later while he was being treated.

One especially novel lab feature in this case was only **partial inhibition by the Fc receptor-blocking antibody IV.3**. In other words, the antibody behavior was so strong that the usual blockade wasn’t fully protective in vitro. That’s not something you’ll use at the bedside, but it reinforces how biologically potent these antibodies can be.

## Bedside diagnosis: don’t wait for the perfect lab result
The most important diagnostic move is to treat the patient, not the assay.

In this case:
- infection was not convincing,
- hemolysis was not convincing,
- transfusion-related causes were not relevant,
- and the timing plus thrombosis plus positive PF4/polyanion ELISA made HIT the leading diagnosis.

If your post-cardiac surgery patient has:
- an unexpected platelet fall that keeps progressing,
- a new thrombosis or neurologic event,
- and recent heparin exposure,

you should assume HIT until proven otherwise.

That means:
- **stop all heparin products immediately**
- send HIT testing
- start a **non-heparin anticoagulant** if suspicion is significant

## Treatment: anticoagulate the hypercoagulability, not the platelet count
This patient was initially given prophylactic fondaparinux when the platelet count began to fall. Once HIT became likely and the stroke was identified, heparin products were stopped and he was treated with **argatroban** plus **high-dose IVIG**.

That choice makes sense:
- **argatroban** is short-acting and titratable, which matters when there’s recent stroke and concern for bleeding
- **IVIG** can be helpful in aHIT by interfering with Fc-mediated platelet activation
- serial **fibrinogen and D-dimer** can give you a rough sense of whether the hypercoagulable process is settling down

In this case, the labs suggested ongoing consumptive activity despite treatment, so the team later switched to **therapeutic fondaparinux**. The platelet count eventually recovered, and the neurologic deficits resolved completely.

A few practical points:
- aHIT may not respond briskly the way you’d hope after IVIG
- persistent thrombocytopenia does not mean you’ve “missed something else” — it may reflect continued antibody activity
- if the patient is still prothrombotic, don’t be falsely reassured by a low platelet count; the clotting risk can remain high

## What to remember at the bedside
The big mistake is assuming that “early” means “not HIT.” In cardiac surgery patients, early thrombocytopenia is common — but **continued decline**, **new thrombosis**, or **recent pre-op heparin exposure** should push you toward HIT quickly.

This case is also a reminder that HIT is not a single phenotype. Autoimmune HIT can be severe, prolonged, and hard to interpret in the lab. If the clinical picture fits, start treatment first and sort out the serology second.

## Take-home points
- A platelet fall after CPB is common; a **progressive fall** is not something to shrug off.
- **HIT can present before POD5** if there was **recent preoperative heparin exposure**.
- New neurologic change or stroke in this setting should raise concern for HIT-associated thrombosis.
- **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** can show heparin-independent platelet activation and a more severe, persistent course.
- When suspicion is high: **stop all heparin and start a non-heparin anticoagulant promptly**.
- In aHIT, **IVIG may help**, but response can be slow or incomplete.



# Autoimmune HIT Is Not Just Severe HIT: Recognizing the Heparin-Independent Phenotype

## The twist in this case

Post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia is common. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is scary. But **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** is the version that should make you stop and rethink what “HIT” even means.

This patient didn’t just have a platelet fall after heparin exposure. He developed:

- **progressive thrombocytopenia starting by POD1–5**
- a **platelet nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L**
- **ischemic stroke**
- **persistent hypercoagulability despite heparin cessation**
- and a lab pattern showing **platelet activation even without heparin**

That last point is the key. This wasn’t routine HIT with heparin-dependent antibody activity. This was **autoimmune HIT**, a phenotype in which HIT antibodies can activate platelets **even in the absence of heparin**.

## Why this looked like more than routine post-op thrombocytopenia

After cardiopulmonary bypass, a platelet drop is expected. In this case, the immediate postoperative fall from **210 to 155 × 10⁹/L** on POD1 could easily be dismissed as standard postcardiac surgery physiology.

But then the count kept falling:

- POD5: **50 × 10⁹/L**
- POD6: **26 × 10⁹/L**
- nadir: **13 × 10⁹/L**

That trajectory is not the usual “post-CPB dip and recovery.” It’s the kind of persistent, worsening thrombocytopenia that should trigger HIT thinking, especially when thrombosis shows up.

And thrombosis did show up: brain imaging revealed an **acute left parietal infarct**.

## The clue that changes the whole diagnosis

The patient had heparin exposure twice:

1. **Preoperative cardiac cath**: 2000 U UFH
2. **Cardiac surgery with CPB**: 45,000 U UFH

He also received **dalteparin prophylaxis** postoperatively.

The early onset of thrombocytopenia can be confusing, because classic HIT after cardiac surgery usually declares itself around **POD5–10**. But **earlier-onset HIT can happen when there was recent preoperative heparin exposure**, which was the case here.

So the timeline matters:

- a routine postcardiac platelet fall? maybe
- early HIT? possible, especially with prior heparin exposure
- **aHIT?** think harder when thrombocytopenia is profound, thrombosis occurs, and the course keeps worsening despite stopping heparin

## What made this autoimmune HIT

The lab picture was classic for anti-PF4 disease, but the functional assay told the more interesting story.

The **PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive** at **2.2 OD**.

The **serotonin-release assay (SRA)** showed:

- strong serotonin release at **0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin**
- **no release at 100 U/mL heparin**  
  - that high-heparin inhibition pattern supports HIT biology
- but also **strong release at 0 heparin**
  - this is the big aHIT clue

In other words, the antibodies were not fully dependent on heparin to activate platelets. That’s what moves you from “HIT” to **autoimmune HIT**.

There was one more unusual wrinkle: inhibition with the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody **IV.3** was only partial, which is not the standard HIT pattern. The case ultimately settled into aHIT based on the clinical picture plus serial functional testing.

## Why aHIT matters clinically

This phenotype is not just a lab curiosity. It tends to behave badly.

Compared with routine HIT, aHIT is associated with:

- **more profound thrombocytopenia**
- **more persistent platelet activation**
- **ongoing thrombin generation / hypercoagulability**
- **thrombosis despite heparin discontinuation**
- slower platelet recovery

This patient showed exactly that pattern. While on argatroban, there was evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy:

- **falling fibrinogen**
- **rising D-dimer**
- later **schistocytes** on smear

That’s a helpful bedside pearl: in severe HIT/aHIT, the platelet count alone doesn’t tell the whole story. If fibrinogen is falling and D-dimer is climbing, the prothrombotic process may still be active.

## Treatment: not just “stop heparin”

Stopping heparin is necessary, but often not enough in aHIT.

In this case:

- **dalteparin was stopped**
- **argatroban** was started
- **high-dose IVIG** was given
- anticoagulation was later changed to **fondaparinux**
- **antithrombin concentrate** was given before fondaparinux because the antithrombin level was mildly low
- platelet recovery was slow but eventual

The patient’s platelet response to IVIG was only modest at first, which is another reminder that aHIT can be stubborn.

He eventually recovered:

- platelets normalized by **POD62**
- neuro deficits resolved completely
- he was later transitioned back to **apixaban**

## Practical bedside lessons

This case is a good reminder that “HIT” is not one single clinical entity. Some patients have a much more aggressive, heparin-independent form that behaves almost like a platelet-activating autoimmune syndrome.

A few clues should push you beyond routine HIT thinking:

- **platelets keep falling after heparin is stopped**
- **very low nadir platelet count**
- **new thrombosis, especially stroke**
- **laboratory evidence of ongoing consumption**
- **SRA positivity even at 0 heparin**
- **atypical or incomplete inhibition patterns in functional testing**

In a postcardiac surgery patient, early thrombocytopenia is easy to dismiss. But if the count keeps dropping, thrombosis appears, or the clinical course feels “too severe” for standard HIT, think aHIT.

## Take-home points

- **Autoimmune HIT is a distinct phenotype, not just “severe HIT.”**
- **Heparin-independent platelet activation** is the defining clue.
- In a postcardiac surgery patient, **platelet decline before POD5 can still be HIT** if there was recent pre-op heparin exposure.
- **Profound thrombocytopenia, stroke, falling fibrinogen, and rising D-dimer** should raise concern for ongoing HIT-associated hypercoagulability.
- A functional assay showing **serotonin release at 0 heparin** is a major red flag for **aHIT**.
- **Stopping heparin is necessary but may not be sufficient**; anticoagulation plus **IVIG** may be needed, and platelet recovery can be delayed.

# Strong ELISA, Weird SRA: What to Do With Atypical HIT Laboratory Results

## The case that should make you pause

This is the kind of HIT case that can fool you if you let the lab stamp do the thinking.

An elderly man after complex cardiac surgery had a platelet count that fell steadily from 210 to 155 on postoperative day 1, then kept drifting down through POD5 to 50, and by POD6 to the mid-20s. He also developed worsening delirium, and brain imaging showed an acute left parietal infarct. No shock, no major bleeding, no transfusions, no clear infection, no hemolysis. The PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive. The SRA came back “indeterminate.”

But when the functional assay was unpacked, it was anything but reassuring: strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin, strong release at low-dose heparin, and suppression at very high heparin. That pattern is a big clue for autoimmune HIT, not a reason to wave the patient away.

## Why the timing mattered

After cardiac surgery, platelets commonly dip early from bypass-related consumption and then recover. That’s normal postoperative physiology. What makes HIT stand out is usually the second hit: a platelet fall around POD5–10, especially if there’s thrombosis.

This patient had a twist that explains the early onset. A week before surgery, he had a heart cath and received a small dose of UFH. That preoperative heparin exposure likely primed the immune response, so the postoperative platelet fall began earlier than the usual postcardiac surgery HIT window.

That’s a pearl worth remembering: a “too-early” HIT pattern after surgery often isn’t too early if the patient had heparin exposure days earlier.

## Reading the lab report like a clinician, not a robot

The PF4 ELISA is an immunoassay. It tells you antibodies are present. It does **not** tell you whether those antibodies are platelet-activating.

The SRA is the functional assay, and the pattern matters more than the label.

In classic HIT, you expect platelet activation at low heparin concentrations, with inhibition at very high heparin concentrations. That’s exactly what happened here at one level: serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with no release at 100 U/mL heparin. That part is very much HIT biology.

The unusual part was serotonin release even at **0 U/mL heparin**. That’s the autoimmune HIT clue. These antibodies can activate platelets in a heparin-independent fashion, so the assay is no longer behaving like textbook HIT.

Then came the extra wrinkle: the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 only partially blocked serotonin release. Usually, IV.3 should shut this down more completely. In this case, the lab called the result “indeterminate” because the profile didn’t match their usual pattern. But the biology still pointed toward aHIT.

That’s the key lesson: “indeterminate” is not the same as “negative,” and it definitely isn’t the same as “benign.”

## What autoimmune HIT changes at the bedside

Autoimmune HIT is the aggressive cousin of classic HIT. The antibodies have heparin-independent platelet-activating properties, which can mean:

- more profound thrombocytopenia,
- more persistent platelet activation,
- more consumptive/coagulopathic features,
- and a higher risk of thrombosis.

In this case, the patient had a thrombotic stroke and a nadir platelet count of 13 × 10⁹/L. That’s not routine postoperative thrombocytopenia. That’s a hypercoagulable immune process until proven otherwise.

The team did the right thing clinically: all heparin was stopped, anticoagulation was changed to argatroban, and high-dose IVIG was given. When hypercoagulability seemed to persist, they switched to fondaparinux, and the platelet count gradually recovered. Neurologically, he ultimately did well.

## Practical approach when the HIT workup doesn’t fit the textbook

If the patient’s story is convincing, don’t let a weird functional assay slow you down.

The bedside question is not “Is the SRA pretty?” It’s “Is this patient at high risk of ongoing HIT-mediated thrombosis right now?”

When the answer is yes, act first and clarify the lab later:

- stop all heparin exposure,
- start a non-heparin anticoagulant,
- look for thrombosis,
- and involve hematology/reference lab experts early.

In severe cases like this one, IVIG is worth thinking about, especially when thrombocytopenia is profound or the hypercoagulable picture seems to keep evolving despite anticoagulation.

Also: if the functional assay is odd, ask the reference lab for the raw pattern. The exact heparin concentrations and inhibition behavior matter. Sometimes the report summary hides the real signal.

## Take-home points

- A strong PF4 ELISA plus a compatible clinical picture should carry real weight, even if the SRA is reported as “indeterminate.”
- Serotonin release at **0 U/mL heparin** is a major red flag for autoimmune HIT.
- Suppression at **very high heparin** still supports HIT biology.
- Partial failure of Fc receptor blockade does **not** rule out HIT; it may reflect an unusually aggressive aHIT phenotype.
- In postcardiac surgery patients, always ask about heparin exposure in the prior 5–10 days, including “small” cath-lab doses.

The lab doesn’t have to look textbook for the disease to be real. In HIT, the platelet count, timing, thrombosis, and assay pattern all have to be read together.



# Argatroban, IVIG, and Then Fondaparinux: Anticoagulating aHIT After Fresh Surgery and Stroke

## Why this case is so interesting

This is not your usual post-cardiac-surgery thrombocytopenia story.

An elderly man had cardiac surgery with heavy intraoperative UFH exposure, then developed a platelet fall that began early, worsened rapidly, and was complicated by an acute parietal stroke. The lab picture eventually pointed to **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** — a more aggressive HIT phenotype where platelet activation can occur even without heparin present. That matters, because aHIT often behaves like a runaway thrombotic syndrome: profound thrombocytopenia, consumptive coagulopathy, and thrombosis can continue even when you think you’ve “done the right thing” by stopping heparin and starting an alternative anticoagulant.

This case also highlights a real-world management tension: the patient was **freshly postoperative, profoundly thrombocytopenic, and had an acute cerebral infarct**. So the team had to balance bleeding risk against an illness that was still actively prothrombotic.

## The clinical pattern that should make you stop and think

After cardiac surgery, platelet counts commonly drop early from hemodilution, CPB-related platelet consumption, and perioperative stress. That part is expected.

What is *not* expected is a platelet count that keeps falling after the early postoperative period, especially when it drops hard and is paired with new thrombosis. In this case:

- Pre-op platelets were normal.
- POD1 platelet count fell modestly, which could still fit routine post-CPB changes.
- Then the count kept dropping to 50 by POD5 and later to the 20s, with a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L.
- Delirium worsened, and brain imaging revealed an acute left parietal infarct.

That combination should push HIT high on the list, even before the serologies come back.

One subtle but important clue: the HIT-like fall began before the usual POD5–10 window for post-cardiac-surgery HIT. That seemed odd until a pre-op heparin exposure during diagnostic cath was uncovered. Prior heparin can “prime” the immune response and make the postoperative HIT timeline look earlier than expected.

## Classic HIT vs autoimmune HIT

Most of us were taught HIT as a heparin-dependent, antibody-mediated platelet activation disorder: heparin binds PF4, antibodies bind the complex, Fc receptors get cross-linked, and platelets activate.

**aHIT is different.** The antibodies can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. Clinically, that tends to mean:

- more severe thrombocytopenia
- more thrombosis
- consumptive coagulopathy features
- a more prolonged or refractory course

That’s exactly why aHIT is the entity to keep in mind when the patient looks “too sick for ordinary HIT.”

In this case, the PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive, but the serotonin release assay had an unusual profile:

- strong serotonin release at low-dose heparin
- **strong release even at 0 heparin**
- only partial inhibition with the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3

That’s a very aHIT-ish pattern, and the 0-heparin activation is the key clue that this was not just garden-variety HIT.

## Why argatroban first made sense

When the stroke was discovered and the platelet count had fallen further, the team stopped prophylactic fondaparinux and started **argatroban**.

That’s a reasonable initial move for a few reasons:

1. **It’s a short-acting, titratable anticoagulant.**  
   In a fresh postoperative patient with a recent stroke, the ability to rapidly turn off the anticoagulant matters.

2. **It avoids heparin exposure.**  
   In suspected HIT/aHIT, that is non-negotiable.

3. **It can be monitored with aPTT.**  
   That gives you at least some bedside feedback in a dynamic situation.

But here’s the catch: a therapeutic aPTT does **not** necessarily mean you have adequately controlled the hypercoagulability of aHIT.

That’s a crucial pearl from this case. The patient’s fibrinogen fell, d-dimer rose, and schistocytes appeared while on argatroban — all suggesting ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite apparently appropriate anticoagulation by lab monitoring.

So if the aPTT looks “fine” but the biology looks worse, don’t let the number lull you into complacency.

## Why IVIG was added

High-dose IVIG is often used in aHIT because the disorder seems to be driven by intensely platelet-activating antibodies that can persist despite standard anticoagulation. IVIG is used to help blunt the immune activation and interrupt the platelet Fc-receptor-mediated storm.

In this patient, IVIG was given because the thrombocytopenia was profound and the course looked like severe aHIT. The platelet response was only modest and slower than hoped, which itself is a reminder that aHIT can be stubborn.

If you’re used to thinking of IVIG as a “rapid fix,” this case is a good reality check. Sometimes the response is incomplete, especially in severe disease.

## Why they switched to fondaparinux despite argatroban

This is the most interesting management move in the case.

The team switched from argatroban to **therapeutic fondaparinux** because there were signs that the HIT-associated hypercoagulability was still not adequately controlled:

- fibrinogen was falling
- d-dimer was rising
- schistocytes appeared
- thrombocytopenia remained severe

So even though the aPTT was being managed on argatroban, the team judged that the overall thrombotic process was still active.

Why fondaparinux?

- It’s a practical once-daily subcutaneous option.
- It is commonly used off-label in HIT.
- It can be a reasonable long-run anticoagulant when ongoing parenteral titration is less appealing.
- In this case, the team also gave **antithrombin concentrate** first because fondaparinux is AT-dependent and the AT level was mildly low.

That’s a nice example of matching the anticoagulant to the biology of the illness, not just to the monitor.

## What to remember in the ED, ICU, or post-op floor

The big teaching point is that **HIT management is not just “stop heparin and start something else.”** In severe aHIT, you may need to treat a continuing immune-thrombotic process, not just replace one anticoagulant with another.

A few practical pearls:

- A postoperative platelet fall that **starts early and keeps falling** deserves a broader HIT workup, especially if there was any recent pre-op heparin exposure.
- In suspected aHIT, **thrombosis can occur with profound thrombocytopenia** — low platelets do not protect the patient from clotting.
- A therapeutic **aPTT on argatroban does not guarantee disease control** if fibrinogen is dropping, d-dimer is rising, or schistocytes are appearing.
- If the functional HIT assay shows **platelet activation at 0 heparin**, think aHIT.
- IVIG can be a useful adjunct when the phenotype is severe or refractory.
- Fondaparinux can be a reasonable next step when the patient remains prothrombotic and you want a simpler anticoagulant strategy, but this should be done thoughtfully and in context.

## Take-home points

- **Early postoperative thrombocytopenia that keeps worsening after cardiac surgery is not “routine until proven otherwise.”**
- **aHIT can present with heparin-independent platelet activation, severe thrombocytopenia, and thrombosis.**
- **A therapeutic aPTT on argatroban does not necessarily mean the HIT storm is under control.**
- **Falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and schistocytes are red flags for ongoing consumptive coagulopathy.**
- **IVIG and a switch to fondaparinux may be reasonable in severe/refractory aHIT, even after starting argatroban.**

## Bottom line

This case is a great reminder that severe HIT is sometimes an immune-thrombotic emergency rather than a simple anticoagulation problem. When the platelet count is crashing, the patient is thrombosing, and the labs suggest ongoing consumptive coagulopathy, you may need to think beyond “standard HIT” and into the world of **autoimmune HIT**.



# Falling Fibrinogen in a Thrombotic Disorder: HIT-Associated Consumptive Coagulopathy

## The clue everyone wants to miss

After cardiac surgery, we’re trained to worry about bleeding, dilution, transfusion effects, and the usual postoperative platelet drift. But in this case, the ominous signal wasn’t bleeding — it was **a falling fibrinogen**.

An elderly man developed a progressive platelet fall after cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass. By postoperative day 5, his platelets were down to 50 × 10⁹/L, and by day 6 they had dropped further, with worsening delirium and brain imaging showing an acute parietal infarct. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive. Functional testing ultimately supported **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**, an especially aggressive form of HIT where platelet activation can become partly heparin-independent.

The twist in this case is what happened during treatment: despite starting non-heparin anticoagulation, he developed a **consumptive, DIC-like pattern** — falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and later schistocytes. That pattern is easy to misread as sepsis-associated DIC or TTP. In the right setting, though, it should make you think: **uncontrolled HIT-associated thrombin generation**.

## HIT is not always “just thrombocytopenia + clot”

Classic HIT is already a thrombotic disorder, not a bleeding disorder. Severe HIT, and especially autoimmune HIT, can push that biology further. Platelet activation drives thrombin generation; thrombin generation drives fibrin formation and consumption; consumption shows up as:

- falling platelet count
- rising D-dimer
- falling fibrinogen
- sometimes schistocytes

That makes the syndrome look DIC-like, but the engine is different. In HIT, the underlying problem is **ongoing immune-mediated platelet activation**, not primary consumptive coagulopathy from infection or malignancy.

After cardiac surgery, this is particularly treacherous because postoperative fibrinogen is usually elevated early. So if fibrinogen is **dropping over time** in a thrombocytopenic patient with suspected HIT, don’t reassure yourself that “the lab is still normal.” Trend matters.

## Why this case was especially important

This patient had several features that should raise the antenna for severe HIT/aHIT:

He had **early postoperative thrombocytopenia**, beginning before the usual day 5–10 window for HIT after cardiac surgery. That turned out to be explained by prior preoperative UFH exposure during cardiac catheterization.

He had **thrombosis**, in this case a cerebral infarct.

He had a **strongly positive immunoassay** and a functional assay with an unusual profile consistent with aHIT.

And then, during treatment, he showed **evidence of persistent hypercoagulability**: falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and later schistocytes. That is the part worth remembering at the bedside.

This is the key teaching point: in severe HIT, the platelet count is important, but it is not the whole story. If the coagulation system looks like it is being consumed, the disease may still be actively generating thrombin even after you’ve started treatment.

## Differentiating HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy from TTP or sepsis-DIC

The overlap is real. A patient with thrombocytopenia, neurologic change, schistocytes, and low fibrinogen can easily be labeled “DIC” or “TTP.” But the context matters.

In this case:

- There was no evidence of major bleeding.
- Hemoglobin was relatively stable in the expected postoperative range.
- Haptoglobin was elevated rather than low.
- Initial smear did **not** show schistocytes.
- PT and aPTT were normal early on, with fibrinogen initially elevated in the postoperative inflammatory state.
- Cultures were negative and there wasn’t a strong infectious picture.

That doesn’t rule out DIC in general, but it makes severe HIT a much better fit.

A useful mental model: **TTP is a platelet-vessel microthrombotic emergency; HIT is an antibody-driven thrombin explosion.** Severe HIT can borrow pieces of both pictures, including microangiopathic red cell fragments, but the treatment path is different.

## What to do when you see falling fibrinogen in suspected HIT

This is where serial labs can help. They are not a replacement for clinical judgment or HIT testing, but they can show you whether the patient is still in a prothrombotic state.

In this case, serial fibrinogen and D-dimer were followed as indirect markers of control. The fibrinogen fell to a nadir during the course, and D-dimer peaked around the second week — consistent with ongoing HIT-associated coagulopathy.

That should push you to think beyond “platelets are recovering slowly” and ask: **Is the antibody-mediated clotting still active? Is the current anticoagulant enough?**

The team used:
- **argatroban** for rapidly titratable anticoagulation
- **high-dose IVIG** to dampen Fc-mediated platelet activation
- later **fondaparinux** when there was concern about ongoing hypercoagulability despite argatroban
- and, because fondaparinux is AT-dependent, **antithrombin supplementation** was given when AT was mildly reduced

The patient eventually recovered neurologically and his platelets normalized.

## Practical bedside pearls

A falling fibrinogen in this setting should not automatically trigger a “bleeding” reflex. It may instead be the signature of **ongoing thrombin generation**.

A few bedside reminders:

- **Think HIT early after cardiac surgery** when thrombocytopenia is accompanied by thrombosis, especially if there was heparin exposure before the operation.
- **Don’t wait for classic timing** if there was recent prior heparin exposure; early-onset HIT can happen.
- **A low or falling fibrinogen is a bad sign in severe HIT/aHIT** and may reflect persistent consumptive coagulopathy.
- **Schistocytes do not equal TTP** in isolation; they can appear in severe HIT as part of a microangiopathic/consumptive picture.
- **Serial D-dimer and fibrinogen can be useful adjuncts** to judge whether the hypercoagulable state is improving.

## Take-home points

- Severe HIT, especially **autoimmune HIT**, can present with a **DIC-like consumptive coagulopathy**.
- In a thrombocytopenic post-heparin patient, **falling fibrinogen + rising D-dimer = ongoing thrombin generation until proven otherwise**.
- This pattern can mimic **TTP or sepsis-associated DIC**, but the management is different: stop heparin, use a non-heparin anticoagulant, and consider IVIG in aHIT.
- **Trends matter** more than single values in the postoperative period.
- If HIT looks “too severe” or “too persistent,” think **aHIT**.



# Postoperative Delirium or Occult Stroke? Arterial Thrombosis in HIT

## Why this case is a trap

Post-cardiac surgery delirium is common enough that it can become background noise. That’s exactly what makes this case uncomfortable: an elderly man looked like he had “just delirium,” but his platelets were falling and his brain was not right. CT then showed an acute left parietal infarct.

The diagnosis was not routine postoperative thrombocytopenia. It was autoimmune HIT, a severe HIT phenotype with heparin-independent platelet activation. In other words: this was HIT with arterial thrombosis, and the neurologic change was the clue.

## The timeline that should make you pause

This patient had a pre-op cardiac cath with a small dose of UFH, then a week later underwent major cardiac surgery with full-dose heparin on bypass. Postoperatively, he was started on dalteparin prophylaxis, but his platelet count kept sliding: 210 pre-op, 155 on POD1, 50 by POD5, then 26 on POD6.

That trajectory matters.

After cardiopulmonary bypass, platelets commonly dip early. What you do not want to ignore is a platelet count that keeps drifting down, especially when the patient is also developing neurologic changes or thrombosis. The case also shows why early-onset HIT after cardiac surgery should make you ask about prior heparin exposure. The cath lab dose likely primed the reaction.

## Don’t let “delirium” blind you to stroke

The tricky part here is that there were no dramatic focal findings. The patient just had worsening delirium. That is exactly how a postoperative stroke can hide in plain sight.

A parietal infarct may not announce itself with obvious hemiparesis. In a patient recovering from cardiac surgery, delirium, sedation, pain, sleep disruption, metabolic issues, and ICU noise can all blur the exam. But when mental status is worsening rather than improving, especially alongside thrombocytopenia, you need to widen the lens.

This case argues for a low threshold to image the brain when there is:
- worsening or unexplained delirium
- new inattention, aphasia, neglect, or subtle asymmetry
- a falling platelet count with concern for HIT
- any thrombotic clue, even if focal deficits are not obvious

The key mistake is anchoring on “postoperative delirium” and missing the infarct.

## HIT is not just a venous clot syndrome

We often teach HIT as DVT/PE risk, but it can absolutely present with arterial thrombosis. Stroke is part of the syndrome.

That matters because a platelet fall plus a neurologic event should move HIT high on the list, not low. In this case, the PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive, and the functional assay ultimately supported autoimmune HIT. The serum activated platelets even without heparin, which is a classic clue for aHIT. The patient’s course was also unusually severe, with profound thrombocytopenia and evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy.

This is the part worth remembering: if the brain is changing and the platelets are falling after heparin exposure, think HIT-related arterial thrombosis until proven otherwise.

## What to do at the bedside

The management principles are straightforward, even when the diagnosis is messy:

Stop all heparin products immediately.

Start a non-heparin anticoagulant if HIT is suspected and thrombosis is possible or confirmed. In this case, the team moved to argatroban because it has a short half-life, which is attractive when there is recent surgery and a new stroke. High-dose IVIG was added because this was autoimmune HIT with severe, persistent platelet activation.

One practical pearl: in severe aHIT, stopping heparin is necessary but may not be enough. These patients can keep clotting and can have ongoing platelet consumption despite initial therapy. If the clinical picture is worsening, treatment escalation is not optional.

This patient later transitioned to therapeutic fondaparinux and ultimately recovered his platelet count and neurologic function. But the important lesson is earlier: the worsening delirium was not benign, and imaging changed the whole trajectory.

## Take-home points

- Delirium after cardiac surgery is common, but worsening delirium is not something to wave away.
- A falling platelet count plus neurologic change should trigger consideration of HIT-related thrombosis.
- HIT can cause arterial events, including stroke, not just venous thromboembolism.
- Early-onset HIT after cardiac surgery can occur if there was recent pre-op heparin exposure.
- In suspected HIT with thrombosis, stop heparin and start non-heparin anticoagulation without waiting around for confirmatory testing.
- Autoimmune HIT can be unusually severe and may require IVIG in addition to anticoagulation.

The big takeaway: after cardiac surgery, “delirium” is a symptom, not a diagnosis. If the platelets are falling, keep looking.
