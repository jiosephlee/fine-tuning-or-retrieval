Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Pathophysiology] What clinical features distinguish autoimmune HIT from classic heparin-dependent HIT, particularly with respect to platelet nadir, persistence of thrombocytopenia, and tendency toward disseminated thrombosis or consumptive coagulopathy?

Autoimmune HIT is the more severe end of the HIT spectrum. In **classic heparin-dependent HIT**, the pathogenic IgG antibodies require **heparin to form the PF4-heparin immune complex** that activates platelets, so thrombocytopenia is usually **moderate**—most often a platelet fall of more than 50%, but the **nadir is commonly around 50 to 80 ×10⁹/L** and only rarely falls into the teens. Once heparin is stopped and an alternative anticoagulant is started, the platelet count typically begins to recover within several days.

By contrast, **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** is characterized by antibodies that can **activate platelets even without heparin**. Clinically, that means the thrombocytopenia is often **more profound and more persistent**, sometimes **worsening despite heparin discontinuation**, as in this case, where the platelet count fell to **13 ×10⁹/L** and recovery was slow. aHIT also has a much greater tendency toward **disseminated thrombosis and a DIC-like consumptive coagulopathy**—for example, falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytes—along with arterial or venous thrombosis such as the **parietal stroke** seen here.  

So the bedside clues favoring aHIT over classic HIT are: **very low platelet nadir, persistent or progressive thrombocytopenia after heparin is stopped, and severe hypercoagulability with diffuse thrombosis or consumptive coagulopathy.**
