Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a pregnant patient in the second trimester with severe abdominal pain, dyspnoea, and large abdominal masses, what bedside examination findings help distinguish a uterine mass from an adnexal mass before imaging?

Before imaging, the most useful bedside clue is whether the mass moves with the uterus on bimanual examination.

In this case, the pelvic exam showed a **fixed, rounded, hard mass in the lesser pelvis that partially bulged into the posterior fornix**, which favored a **uterine origin** rather than a freely mobile adnexal mass. A **mass that is continuous with the uterus, moves with the gravid uterus, or feels fixed to the uterine wall** suggests a leiomyoma. By contrast, an **adnexal mass is more likely to be separately palpable from the uterus, relatively mobile, and not clearly contiguous with the uterine contour**.

So, at the bedside, the key exam findings are:

- **Bimanual assessment of continuity with the uterus**
- **Mobility versus fixation**
- **Location relative to the posterior fornix and pelvic sidewalls**
- **Effect on the uterine contour**: a uterine mass often distorts or enlarges the uterus itself

In this report, the pelvic exam already hinted that at least one lesion was uterine, even before ultrasound and MRI confirmed a pedunculated leiomyoma and a posterior uterine myoma.

### [Management] What imaging strategy is preferred for evaluating complex abdominal or pelvic masses during pregnancy, and why are ultrasound with Doppler and non-contrast MRI favored over other modalities?

For a complex abdominal or pelvic mass during pregnancy, the preferred imaging strategy is **targeted ultrasound with color Doppler as the first-line study, followed by non-contrast MRI when ultrasound is indeterminate or the anatomy is obscured by the gravid uterus**.

In this case, ultrasound was the key initial test because it is **safe, readily available, repeatable, and excellent for defining whether a mass is cystic or solid, its size, relationship to the uterus and adnexa, and its vascular pattern**. Doppler adds important information about **peripheral or internal vascularity, pedicle vessels, and features that may suggest torsion, degeneration, or neoplastic concern**. That is exactly why the large masses in this patient were first characterized by ultrasound, which suggested complex cystic lesions but could not definitively establish their origin.

When ultrasound is limited by **large mass size, multiple lesions, posterior location, or distortion from the gravid uterus**, **non-contrast MRI** is preferred next because it provides **superior soft-tissue contrast and better anatomic delineation**. In the report, MRI helped assess **wall thickness, internal signal characteristics, continuity with the posterior uterine myoma, and absence of adenopathy**, all without exposing the fetus to ionizing radiation or gadolinium.

Other modalities are avoided whenever possible in pregnancy because they are less safe: **CT uses ionizing radiation**, and **gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally avoided** unless absolutely necessary because of fetal safety concerns. Thus, the practical rule is: **ultrasound with Doppler first, non-contrast MRI second, and avoid CT and contrast unless there is a compelling indication**.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What were the leading differential diagnoses for the two cystic masses in this case, and which sonographic and MRI features supported decidualised endometrioma versus cystic degenerating leiomyoma?

The leading differential diagnoses were **decidualised endometrioma versus cystic degenerating leiomyoma**. In practice, the **upper mass** was initially interpreted as an ovarian-type cystic lesion—most likely a **decidualised endometrioma**—whereas the **lower, pelvic mass** was favored to be a **posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration**.

The sonographic features that pushed the team toward **decidualised endometrioma** for the cranial lesion were its **large unilocular cystic appearance, regular margins, and peripheral vascularity**, together with the **slight CA-125 elevation** in the second trimester. Importantly, because of the gravid uterus and the mass size, **no definite stalk or feeding vessel** could be identified on ultrasound, which kept an ovarian origin in the differential.

By contrast, the **caudal mass** was more suggestive of **cystic degenerating leiomyoma** because it appeared **adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma** on ultrasound. MRI strengthened that impression: the lesion was seen in **continuity with the posterior uterine wall**, and the study showed **thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components**, all features compatible with a **degenerating leiomyoma** rather than a simple adnexal cyst. The absence of adenopathy also argued against an overt malignant process.

So, in this case, the key discriminators were not just “cystic versus solid,” but **anatomic continuity with the uterus on imaging** and the **MRI signal characteristics of degeneration**. Pathology ultimately confirmed that both lesions were **benign uterine leiomyomas**, with ischemic change near a twisted pedicle in the cranial mass.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should a mildly elevated CA-125 level in the second trimester be interpreted in a pregnant patient with adnexal-appearing masses, and what are the major limitations of this marker in pregnancy?

A mildly elevated CA-125 in the second trimester should be interpreted very cautiously and, by itself, should not be taken as evidence of malignancy. In this case, the value was only slightly above the conventional cutoff (41.8 U/mL) and rose from 36 U/mL over 2 weeks, but the patient ultimately had benign uterine leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis and torsion. That is exactly the kind of scenario in which CA-125 can be misleading: it may be modestly increased in pregnancy and in a variety of benign gynecologic conditions, including endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammation, menstruation, and myomas.

The major limitation of CA-125 in pregnancy is poor specificity. Physiologic pregnancy-related changes can elevate the marker, especially in the first trimester and again near term, while second-trimester values are generally lower; therefore, an isolated mildly abnormal result in midpregnancy has limited diagnostic value. In the case report, the “slight positivity” of CA-125 contributed to concern for an adnexal process, but imaging and operative findings showed a benign uterine origin. Thus, CA-125 should be used, at most, as a supportive trend marker—not as a stand-alone diagnostic test—when evaluating adnexal-appearing masses in pregnancy. Management should rely primarily on clinical status and safe imaging, particularly ultrasound and non-contrast MRI.

### [Pathophysiology] What pathophysiologic mechanisms can explain acute pain, low-grade fever, and imaging heterogeneity in a leiomyoma during pregnancy, and how did the final pathology support this process in this patient?

In this patient, the acute pain, low-grade fever, and heterogeneous imaging were most consistent with a **complicated leiomyoma undergoing torsion with ischemic degeneration**—what is often termed **red degeneration or infarction of a pedunculated myoma**.

The pathophysiology is straightforward: as the uterus enlarges in pregnancy, a pedunculated or otherwise vascular leiomyoma can **twist on its stalk**, compromising venous outflow first and then arterial inflow. That produces **ischemia, hemorrhagic infarction, and necrosis**, which are highly painful. The necrotic tissue can also provoke a **sterile inflammatory response**, explaining the low-grade fever and mild inflammatory marker elevation. In this case, the urinary infection may have contributed to fever, but the operative finding of a **twisted, leaking, highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma** strongly supports the myoma itself as the source of the acute syndrome.

The imaging heterogeneity likewise fits this process. On ultrasound and MRI, the lesion had **mixed solid-cystic components, inhomogeneous fluid content, and thick walls**, which are classic for **degenerative change within a leiomyoma**—including cystic degeneration, hemorrhage, and necrosis—rather than a simple uniform solid fibroid. This is why such lesions can mimic adnexal masses or endometriomas in pregnancy.

The final pathology confirmed the mechanism: it showed **benign uterine leiomyomas with areas of ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle**. That histology is the tissue-level correlate of torsion-related infarction and explains the patient’s pain, fever, and complex radiologic appearance.

### [Morning Report] What clinical factors in this case justified proceeding to surgery at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation rather than continued expectant management?

Proceeding to surgery at 21 weeks and 6 days was justified because this was not an asymptomatic mass that could safely be watched; it was a **symptomatic, complicated, and diagnostically uncertain lesion** with several red flags.

The decisive clinical factors were:

- **Worsening maternal symptoms despite conservative measures**: she had severe acute abdominal pain and dyspnea from mechanical compression, and although analgesia and positioning helped transiently, the symptoms were significant and persistent.
- **Risk of acute complication from the mass itself**: imaging showed **very large, vascularized masses with mixed solid-cystic features**, and at surgery the cranial lesion proved to be **pedunculated, twisted, and leaking with ischemic necrosis**—findings consistent with imminent or ongoing complication. The team was appropriately concerned about **torsion, rupture, infection, and necrosis**.
- **Need for a definitive diagnosis**: ultrasound and non-contrast MRI could not conclusively distinguish between adnexal pathology and a degenerating leiomyoma; the slightly elevated and rising **CA-125** added to the diagnostic uncertainty, even though it was not diagnostic of malignancy.
- **Progressive mass effect with anticipated worsening**: the lesions were so large that further uterine enlargement and fetal growth would likely increase compression, pain, and respiratory compromise, while also raising the risk of hypoxia and degeneration of the masses.
- **Time-sensitive obstetric planning**: delaying intervention would not necessarily improve safety, whereas the masses already threatened maternal status and later pregnancy would only make surgery more difficult.

So, the rationale was the classic pregnancy-surgery balance: **persistent maternal symptoms plus complication risk and uncertain pathology outweighed the risks of second-trimester laparotomy**. In this case, the operation was both diagnostic and therapeutic, and pathology confirmed the concern for **benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis near a twisted pedicle**.

### [Management] At laparotomy, why was the cranial mass resected while the caudal pelvic mass was left in situ, and what principles should guide the extent of surgery for fibroids during pregnancy?

The cranial mass was resected because, at laparotomy, it proved to be a **pedunculated, twisted, leaking, highly vascular leiomyoma**—in other words, a lesion with an **acute surgical indication**. Its pedicle made it technically amenable to safe excision, and leaving it in place would have risked ongoing torsion, necrosis, hemorrhage, infection, and continued maternal symptoms.

By contrast, the caudal pelvic mass was **fixed to the uterine torus/posterior lower segment**, appeared consistent with a **deep intramural-subserosal myoma**, and had **no clear stalk or safe cleavage plane**. Attempting to remove it would have required a much more extensive uterine dissection, with a substantially higher risk of **hemorrhage, uterine rupture, pregnancy loss, and preterm labor**. Since the goal in pregnancy is to relieve the dangerous lesion while **minimizing uterine trauma**, it was left in situ.

The general principle for fibroids in pregnancy is therefore **conservative surgery, not radical surgery**: intervene only when there is **persistent severe symptoms, torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, rupture/necrosis/infection, or concern for malignancy**, and then remove **only the lesion that is clearly responsible and safely resectable**. The operation should be **as limited and brief as possible**, with continued fetal assessment and a plan that preserves the pregnancy. Large, deep, posterior, intramural, or lower-segment fibroids are often better left until postpartum unless they create an unavoidable obstetric problem.

### [Management] What perioperative measures are important when non-obstetric abdominal surgery is performed during pregnancy, including fetal assessment, maternal monitoring, and postoperative obstetric follow-up?

When non-obstetric abdominal surgery is necessary in pregnancy, the perioperative goal is to protect both mother and fetus while minimizing uterine irritability and obstetric complications. In this case, the team did the key things correctly.

First, fetal assessment should be documented before, during when feasible, and after the operation. The case report specifically notes that fetal cardiac activity was confirmed at laparotomy, and the discussion emphasizes that fetal heart rate must be checked and documented pre-, intra-, and postoperatively. In practice, this means confirming viability before surgery, using intermittent fetal assessment intraoperatively if gestational age and logistics allow, and reassessing after the procedure.

Second, maternal monitoring must be meticulous, because maternal physiology is the best determinant of fetal well-being. Continuous attention to oxygenation, hemodynamics, pain control, and signs of preterm labor is essential. In the report, the patient underwent multidisciplinary assessment, had stable oxygen saturation and hemodynamics, and her dyspnea was recognized as mechanical compression rather than cardiopulmonary disease. Intraoperatively, the team aimed for a short, definitive procedure and used appropriate prophylaxis and supportive care, including antibiotics and progesterone, reflecting the principle of reducing uterine irritability and infectious risk while maintaining maternal stability. Blood loss and anemia should also be anticipated and corrected promptly, as was necessary here with transfusion and IV iron.

Third, postoperative obstetric follow-up is mandatory. After abdominal surgery in pregnancy, the patient should have close surveillance for contractions, bleeding, membrane rupture, fetal growth, and amniotic fluid volume. In this case, the patient was followed every 3 weeks, with documented fetal growth and amniotic fluid assessment, and ultimately delivered electively at 37 weeks because the residual pelvic myoma would have obstructed labor. This illustrates an important point: postoperative management does not end with wound healing; it includes ongoing obstetric planning, including the likely mode and timing of delivery based on the residual mass and uterine scar.

So the practical teaching point is: confirm fetal viability around the operation, monitor maternal oxygenation and hemodynamics carefully, minimize operative time and obstetric stress, and ensure structured postpartum obstetric follow-up with a delivery plan tailored to the residual pathology.

### [Morning Report] Why was elective caesarean delivery chosen in this patient, and how do the size, number, and anatomic location of leiomyomas influence mode of delivery planning in pregnancy?

Elective caesarean delivery was chosen because the residual pelvic leiomyoma was large, fixed, and located in the posterior lower uterus/torus, where it would likely have obstructed fetal head descent during labor. In this case, the operative team judged that the mass represented a mechanical barrier to the expulsive phase and that a planned cesarean at 37 weeks was the safest way to avoid labor dystocia, traumatic obstruction, and the need for an emergency intrapartum procedure through the prior laparotomy scar.

More generally, the mode of delivery in pregnancy with leiomyomas is determined not simply by the presence of myomas, but by their **size, number, and anatomic location**. Small, solitary, subserosal, or pedunculated fibroids that do not distort the lower uterine segment usually do not preclude vaginal delivery. In contrast, **large** fibroids, **multiple** fibroids, and especially those that are **intramural**, **posterior**, **isthmic**, **cervical**, or occupying the **lower uterine segment** are more likely to interfere with engagement and descent of the fetal head and therefore favor planned cesarean delivery. This case illustrates that point: the upper pedunculated myoma was removed, but the persistent caudal pelvic mass remained in a position that would have made vaginal birth unsafe or impossible.
