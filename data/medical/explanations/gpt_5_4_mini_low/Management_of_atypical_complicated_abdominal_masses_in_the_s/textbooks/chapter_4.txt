Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 4: ## Operative Findings, Surgical Management, and Perioperative Obstetric Care

At laparotomy, the operative findings clarified why preoperative imaging had been difficult to interpret. In pregnancy, the enlarged uterus and large mass effect can distort pelvic anatomy, so the surgical team first assessed the abdominal cavity, confirmed fetal cardiac activity, and then identified the true origin of the lesions. The cranial “adnexal” mass was not an ovarian neoplasm or endometrioma, but a huge pedunculated uterine leiomyoma with cystic degeneration that was twisted, highly vascular, and leaking. This intraoperative appearance explained the patient’s severe pain and the low-grade fever: torsion of a pedunculated myoma can compromise venous and lymphatic drainage, leading to ischaemia, necrosis, inflammatory leakage, and occasionally systemic signs of inflammation.

The operation was therefore not a blanket removal of all lesions, but a selective myomectomy guided by obstetric risk and technical feasibility. The pedunculated upper mass was the most acute problem because a twisted stalk can progress to infarction, rupture, or infection. It was removed by controlled dissection at its uterine implant, with the pedicle temporarily secured by clamps and the stump closed with absorbable suture to achieve haemostasis and restore the uterine wall. This is the key technical principle in pregnancy myomectomy: limit dissection, control bleeding quickly, and repair the uterus carefully to reduce postoperative bleeding and preserve the pregnancy.

By contrast, the caudal pelvic mass was left in place. Intraoperatively it appeared fixed, smooth, and consistent with a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma arising from the uterine torus rather than a freely resectable extrauterine lesion. Removing it would likely have required a larger uterine incision and a more extensive procedure, increasing the risks of haemorrhage, pregnancy loss, and later obstetric complications. In this context, the operative strategy was appropriately conservative: treat the lesion causing the acute complication, but avoid overintervention on a mass that could be managed expectantly with close follow-up.

Because there was still diagnostic uncertainty before histology, peritoneal fluid was collected for cytology and culture. This is a reasonable precaution when a mass has atypical imaging features or appears inflammatory or leaking, since it helps exclude occult malignancy or infection without materially increasing operative risk. The final pathology confirmed benign leiomyomas with areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, validating the decision to perform limited surgery rather than radical excision.

Maternal-fetal surveillance is a central part of surgery in pregnancy. Fetal heart activity should be documented before, during, and after the procedure, and the case report emphasizes this principle. Although continuous intraoperative fetal monitoring is not always feasible at 21 weeks’ gestation, repeated confirmation of viability helps guide obstetric decision-making and reassures the team that the fetus remains stable throughout maternal surgery. The use of perioperative progesterone in this case reflects an attempt to support uterine quiescence after surgery, even though such prophylaxis is not standardized across all settings. Antibiotic coverage was also appropriate because the patient had a documented urinary tract infection and was undergoing abdominal surgery; perioperative antibiotics help reduce maternal infectious risk, which is particularly important when there is concern for a contaminated or leaking mass.

Postoperatively, the major issue was blood loss and worsened anaemia. This is common after myomectomy in pregnancy, especially with large vascular leiomyomas. The patient required intravenous iron and transfusion to restore haemoglobin and oxygen-carrying capacity. After stabilization, she could be followed expectantly with serial obstetric assessments, including fetal growth and amniotic fluid surveillance. The later elective caesarean delivery was determined by the persistent pelvic myoma, which would have obstructed labour. Thus, the operative course illustrates the central management principle in complicated pregnancy-associated fibroid disease: intervene only when symptoms, torsion, bleeding, infection, or diagnostic uncertainty justify it, and when surgery is necessary, keep it as limited and obstetrically safe as possible.
