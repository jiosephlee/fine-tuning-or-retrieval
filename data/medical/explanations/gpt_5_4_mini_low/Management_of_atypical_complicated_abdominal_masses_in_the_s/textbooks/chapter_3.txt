Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning and Decision to Operate in the Second Trimester

Management of abdominal or pelvic masses during pregnancy begins with a fundamental question: can the patient be safely observed, or does the mass itself create enough maternal or fetal risk to justify surgery? In most pregnant patients, especially when the lesion is thought to be a uterine leiomyoma, conservative management is preferred. This is because many myomas remain stable or regress, and surgery carries important obstetric risks, including hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine irritability. Therefore, the default strategy is expectant follow-up with symptom control unless there is a clear clinical reason to intervene.

In this case, however, observation alone was not reassuring. The patient presented at 21 weeks with severe abdominal pain in two compartments—the upper left quadrant and the pelvis—together with dyspnoea. These symptoms were not trivial discomfort from pregnancy; they suggested a mechanical problem from very large intra-abdominal masses. The abdomen was larger than expected for gestational age, and bimanual examination confirmed a fixed pelvic mass. During admission, the worsening clinical picture, including low-grade fever, increased concern for a complication such as torsion, leakage, ischemia, or infection. At the same time, the masses were so large that further growth of the uterus and fetus could have increased pressure symptoms and made later surgery more difficult. Thus, the team was not dealing with an incidental lesion, but with symptomatic masses causing ongoing maternal compromise.

The diagnostic uncertainty also weighed heavily in favor of intervention. Ultrasound and MRI suggested two complex cystic lesions with regular margins, peripheral vascularity, and inhomogeneous internal content. On imaging, the upper lesion resembled a huge decidualised endometrioma, while the lower lesion seemed related to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration. In pregnancy, imaging interpretation can be difficult because the enlarged uterus distorts anatomy and can obscure the relationship between a mass and the ovary or uterine wall. When the origin of a lesion is uncertain, the clinical team must consider not only benign entities such as degenerating leiomyoma or endometrioma, but also the possibility of a neoplasm. In this case, the lack of a clearly identifiable stalk or feeding vessel for the upper mass made the diagnosis less secure on imaging alone.

Serum CA-125 added to the uncertainty but did not by itself establish malignancy. Mild elevation of CA-125 can occur in normal pregnancy and in benign gynecologic conditions, including endometriosis and leiomyoma. The modest rise to 41.8 U/mL, with a slight increase over 2 weeks, was therefore only a supportive clue rather than a diagnostic marker. In other words, CA-125 helped the team remain cautious, but it could not distinguish reliably between a decidualised endometrioma, a degenerating myoma, or a more concerning adnexal process. This is a common principle in obstetric imaging: tumor markers may contribute to the overall picture, but they rarely override clinical and radiologic assessment.

The decision to operate was made through multidisciplinary risk assessment. Obstetricians, gynecologists, surgeons, radiologists, and anesthesia personnel had to weigh maternal symptoms, fetal viability, and the technical feasibility of surgery. The second trimester is generally the preferred time for non-obstetric surgery when intervention is unavoidable. By this stage, organogenesis is complete, reducing teratogenic risk relative to the first trimester, and the uterus is not yet so enlarged that operative exposure becomes maximally difficult, as in the third trimester. The risk of spontaneous pregnancy loss is also lower than earlier in gestation. For this patient, 21 weeks represented a practical compromise: early enough to intervene before further enlargement and late enough that fetal development was more advanced.

The choice of laparotomy rather than laparoscopy reflected the anatomy and complexity of the case. Minimally invasive surgery may be appropriate for selected smaller, less vascular, and more accessible lesions, but this patient had huge, multiple, highly vascular masses with intramural and posterior pelvic involvement. Such features increase technical difficulty and the risk of uncontrolled bleeding, incomplete resection, or prolonged operative time. In addition, the mass location and the need to preserve pregnancy made a short, controlled procedure especially important. A laparotomic approach therefore offered better access and safer judgment intraoperatively, particularly when the exact nature of the masses could only be confirmed at exploration.

Preoperative counselling was central to the management plan. The patient had to understand that surgery in pregnancy carries real risks, including miscarriage, hemorrhage, preterm labor, uterine scar complications, and the possibility that not all lesions could or should be removed in one session. This was especially relevant here because the team anticipated that a complete excision of both masses might increase obstetric risk. The surgical plan was therefore intentionally conservative: remove the lesion most likely responsible for acute complication and defer the other mass if needed. That strategy reflected the core balance in complicated pregnancy-associated masses—treat the immediate threat, limit operative trauma, and preserve the pregnancy whenever possible.
