Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Bedside Assessment of Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

This case begins with a pregnant patient in the second trimester who developed acute, severe pain in two anatomically distinct regions: the left upper abdomen and the pelvis, accompanied by mild dyspnoea. In a patient at 21 weeks’ gestation, these symptoms require a broad differential diagnosis because abdominal pain and shortness of breath may reflect obstetric, gastrointestinal, urinary, respiratory, or gynecologic pathology. The initial task is not to “fit” the symptoms to pregnancy alone, but to identify whether the patient is unstable, whether there is a surgical abdomen, and whether the pregnancy itself is threatened.

The first bedside priority is maternal stabilization. In this case, the patient’s oxygen saturation, heart rate, blood pressure, and ECG were reassuring, and there was no immediate cardiopulmonary compromise. That matters because dyspnoea in pregnancy can be physiologic, but when it is accompanied by a large abdominal mass it may also signal mechanical compression of the diaphragm or, less commonly, a primary cardiopulmonary event. The absence of hypoxaemia or hemodynamic instability made an emergent respiratory or cardiovascular cause less likely and allowed a more focused obstetric and abdominal evaluation.

Physical examination is especially important when imaging options are limited by pregnancy. The abdomen was larger than expected for gestational age, and palpation identified a large tender mass in the left upper quadrant. There were no clinical signs of ascites. This is an important bedside clue: in pregnancy, a uterus enlarged by gestation can mask or distort the location of a coexisting mass, so recognizing asymmetry, focal tenderness, or an unexpectedly enlarged abdomen can be the first indication that the patient has more than routine gravid enlargement. The combination of pain and a palpable, tense mass also raises concern for complications such as torsion, degeneration, rupture, hemorrhage, or infection.

A bimanual vaginal examination added crucial anatomic information. It showed a fixed, hard mass in the lesser pelvis, partially bulging into the posterior fornix. In pregnant patients, this exam can help determine whether a lesion is truly adnexal, uterine, or fixed within the pelvis, and whether it is likely to interfere with labor later in pregnancy. Here, the pelvic mass could be appreciated independently of the gravid uterus, suggesting a large lesion low in the pelvis rather than only uterine enlargement.

Supportive care was appropriate while the diagnostic workup proceeded. Analgesia with intravenous paracetamol and a semi-orthostatic position improved symptoms. The positional maneuver was reasonable because elevating the upper body may reduce diaphragmatic compression from very large intra-abdominal masses and the gravid uterus, easing dyspnoea without exposing the fetus to unnecessary intervention. Importantly, symptom improvement did not eliminate concern, because large masses can remain clinically significant even when pain is partially controlled.

During admission, additional findings complicated the picture but did not fully explain the presentation. The patient developed a low-grade fever, had a urinary tract infection with *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, and showed iron deficiency anaemia. Mild leukocytosis and neutrophilia can be seen in normal pregnancy, so these laboratory changes must be interpreted cautiously rather than assumed to indicate a severe inflammatory process. In this case, the urinary infection could have contributed to fever and discomfort, but it did not account for the large abdominal and pelvic masses. The anaemia also mattered clinically because it increased the importance of careful planning if surgery became necessary.

Several features increased concern and pushed the team toward further evaluation: worsening pain, dyspnoea likely caused by mass effect, fever, and the presence of two large palpable lesions in a pregnant patient whose abdomen was already enlarged by the uterus. Obesity added another layer of difficulty by making both palpation and interpretation of abdominal contour less reliable. Together, these factors illustrate why bedside assessment in pregnancy must be systematic: confirm maternal stability, characterize symptoms carefully, perform focused abdominal and pelvic examination, and identify red flags that suggest torsion, rupture, infection, or malignancy rather than benign gestational discomfort alone.

## Imaging Strategy and Differential Diagnosis of Complex Adnexal-Appearing Masses During Pregnancy

When a pregnant patient presents with a large pelvic or abdominal mass, the diagnostic goal is to localize the lesion, characterize its likely tissue of origin, and identify features suggesting complication or malignancy while avoiding fetal harm. In practice, this means relying first on clinical examination and ultrasound, then using non-contrast MRI when sonographic findings are indeterminate or anatomy is distorted by the gravid uterus. In this case, the masses were symptomatic and unusually large, so imaging had to answer two key questions: were these ovarian/adnexal lesions or uterine in origin, and was there any evidence of acute complication that would justify surgery?

Ultrasound remains the first-line study in pregnancy because it is safe, available, and often sufficient to identify lesion size, morphology, and relationship to the uterus and adnexa. Colour Doppler adds important information about vascularity and can help determine whether a mass has a feeding pedicle or internal vascular pattern consistent with a uterine leiomyoma. Its main limitation in advanced pregnancy is that the enlarged uterus and large lesion size can obscure anatomy, making it difficult to identify the ovary, stalk, or cleavage planes. In this patient, ultrasound showed two very large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation. The upper lesion occupied the left subphrenic region and extended toward the uterine fundus, whereas the lower lesion appeared to be related to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. This pattern already suggested that at least one lesion might be uterine rather than ovarian, but the anatomy was still not definitive.

Because ultrasound could not confidently establish the origin of the masses, non-contrast MRI was appropriate. MRI is especially useful in pregnancy when ultrasound is equivocal, particularly for large, multiple, posterior, intramural, or deeply situated lesions. It provides better tissue contrast and can show whether a lesion is contiguous with the uterus, whether a stalk is present, and whether there are features suggesting hemorrhage, degeneration, or solid tumor components. Gadolinium is generally avoided in pregnancy because it crosses the placenta and is reserved only for exceptional circumstances where the result would materially change management. In this case, the MRI was performed without contrast, which is the standard safe approach when further characterization is needed.

The MRI findings were still not entirely straightforward. The lesions had thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and focal solid-appearing components, with unclear cleavage planes from surrounding structures. The absence of adenopathy was reassuring against an obvious advanced malignant process, but it did not by itself establish benignity. Imaging features such as thickened walls and heterogeneous contents can be seen in cystic degeneration of leiomyomas, endometriomas with decidual change, hemorrhagic cysts, and some neoplasms. The key interpretive challenge in this case was that large uterine leiomyomas, especially when pedunculated or posterior, can masquerade as adnexal masses because their uterine attachment may be hard to visualize when the uterus is enlarged and displaced by pregnancy.

This diagnostic ambiguity is why decidualised endometrioma entered the differential diagnosis. During pregnancy, endometriomas can undergo decidualisation under progesterone influence, becoming more vascular and developing mural nodularity or solid-appearing components that may resemble neoplasm. This can make an endometrioma appear more complex than usual on both ultrasound and MRI. In the present case, the slight CA-125 elevation further pushed the team to consider an ovarian endometriotic lesion, since endometriosis and related entities can raise CA-125 and cause complex cystic appearances. However, the imaging also suggested continuity of the caudal lesion with a posterior uterine myoma, which favored a myomatous origin. Later surgery confirmed that the upper mass was a pedunculated, torsed leiomyoma and the lower lesion represented a cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

Cystic degeneration and torsion are important complications of leiomyomas in pregnancy because they can change the imaging appearance dramatically and explain acute symptoms. Degeneration may produce a predominantly cystic lesion with heterogeneous signal and thickened walls, while torsion of a pedunculated myoma can produce pain, ischemic change, and sometimes low-grade fever. These features overlap with adnexal pathology, so the clinical context matters. In this patient, worsening pain, a large vascular lesion, and mild fever supported the possibility of a complicated leiomyoma rather than a simple benign cyst.

Tumour markers can be tempting to interpret as clues to malignancy, but in pregnancy they must be used cautiously. CA-125 is particularly limited because it can rise physiologically in early pregnancy and may remain mildly elevated in benign conditions, including endometriosis, leiomyomas, and pregnancy itself. A single isolated value is not diagnostic. In this case, CA-125 was only mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL and other markers, including CA 19-9, CA 15-3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, were normal. That pattern did not prove cancer and did not rule it out either. The value of the marker lay mainly in contributing to the broader differential diagnosis, especially when the imaging appearance was equivocal. A small upward trend over two weeks was noted, but this was not specific enough to override the overall radiologic and clinical picture.

The differential diagnosis for this patient’s masses therefore had to remain broad until surgery. The leading considerations were ovarian endometrioma with decidual change, adnexal neoplasm, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma, and cystically degenerating intramural-subserosal myoma. Other cystic pelvic masses were less likely but still part of the initial framework. The main lesson from this case is that complex adnexal-appearing masses in pregnancy should be approached systematically: start with ultrasound and Doppler, use MRI without contrast when anatomy is unclear, interpret tumour markers conservatively, and remember that degenerating or pedunculated leiomyomas can closely mimic ovarian pathology.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Decision to Operate in the Second Trimester

Management of abdominal or pelvic masses during pregnancy begins with a fundamental question: can the patient be safely observed, or does the mass itself create enough maternal or fetal risk to justify surgery? In most pregnant patients, especially when the lesion is thought to be a uterine leiomyoma, conservative management is preferred. This is because many myomas remain stable or regress, and surgery carries important obstetric risks, including hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine irritability. Therefore, the default strategy is expectant follow-up with symptom control unless there is a clear clinical reason to intervene.

In this case, however, observation alone was not reassuring. The patient presented at 21 weeks with severe abdominal pain in two compartments—the upper left quadrant and the pelvis—together with dyspnoea. These symptoms were not trivial discomfort from pregnancy; they suggested a mechanical problem from very large intra-abdominal masses. The abdomen was larger than expected for gestational age, and bimanual examination confirmed a fixed pelvic mass. During admission, the worsening clinical picture, including low-grade fever, increased concern for a complication such as torsion, leakage, ischemia, or infection. At the same time, the masses were so large that further growth of the uterus and fetus could have increased pressure symptoms and made later surgery more difficult. Thus, the team was not dealing with an incidental lesion, but with symptomatic masses causing ongoing maternal compromise.

The diagnostic uncertainty also weighed heavily in favor of intervention. Ultrasound and MRI suggested two complex cystic lesions with regular margins, peripheral vascularity, and inhomogeneous internal content. On imaging, the upper lesion resembled a huge decidualised endometrioma, while the lower lesion seemed related to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration. In pregnancy, imaging interpretation can be difficult because the enlarged uterus distorts anatomy and can obscure the relationship between a mass and the ovary or uterine wall. When the origin of a lesion is uncertain, the clinical team must consider not only benign entities such as degenerating leiomyoma or endometrioma, but also the possibility of a neoplasm. In this case, the lack of a clearly identifiable stalk or feeding vessel for the upper mass made the diagnosis less secure on imaging alone.

Serum CA-125 added to the uncertainty but did not by itself establish malignancy. Mild elevation of CA-125 can occur in normal pregnancy and in benign gynecologic conditions, including endometriosis and leiomyoma. The modest rise to 41.8 U/mL, with a slight increase over 2 weeks, was therefore only a supportive clue rather than a diagnostic marker. In other words, CA-125 helped the team remain cautious, but it could not distinguish reliably between a decidualised endometrioma, a degenerating myoma, or a more concerning adnexal process. This is a common principle in obstetric imaging: tumor markers may contribute to the overall picture, but they rarely override clinical and radiologic assessment.

The decision to operate was made through multidisciplinary risk assessment. Obstetricians, gynecologists, surgeons, radiologists, and anesthesia personnel had to weigh maternal symptoms, fetal viability, and the technical feasibility of surgery. The second trimester is generally the preferred time for non-obstetric surgery when intervention is unavoidable. By this stage, organogenesis is complete, reducing teratogenic risk relative to the first trimester, and the uterus is not yet so enlarged that operative exposure becomes maximally difficult, as in the third trimester. The risk of spontaneous pregnancy loss is also lower than earlier in gestation. For this patient, 21 weeks represented a practical compromise: early enough to intervene before further enlargement and late enough that fetal development was more advanced.

The choice of laparotomy rather than laparoscopy reflected the anatomy and complexity of the case. Minimally invasive surgery may be appropriate for selected smaller, less vascular, and more accessible lesions, but this patient had huge, multiple, highly vascular masses with intramural and posterior pelvic involvement. Such features increase technical difficulty and the risk of uncontrolled bleeding, incomplete resection, or prolonged operative time. In addition, the mass location and the need to preserve pregnancy made a short, controlled procedure especially important. A laparotomic approach therefore offered better access and safer judgment intraoperatively, particularly when the exact nature of the masses could only be confirmed at exploration.

Preoperative counselling was central to the management plan. The patient had to understand that surgery in pregnancy carries real risks, including miscarriage, hemorrhage, preterm labor, uterine scar complications, and the possibility that not all lesions could or should be removed in one session. This was especially relevant here because the team anticipated that a complete excision of both masses might increase obstetric risk. The surgical plan was therefore intentionally conservative: remove the lesion most likely responsible for acute complication and defer the other mass if needed. That strategy reflected the core balance in complicated pregnancy-associated masses—treat the immediate threat, limit operative trauma, and preserve the pregnancy whenever possible.

## Operative Findings, Surgical Management, and Perioperative Obstetric Care

At laparotomy, the operative findings clarified why preoperative imaging had been difficult to interpret. In pregnancy, the enlarged uterus and large mass effect can distort pelvic anatomy, so the surgical team first assessed the abdominal cavity, confirmed fetal cardiac activity, and then identified the true origin of the lesions. The cranial “adnexal” mass was not an ovarian neoplasm or endometrioma, but a huge pedunculated uterine leiomyoma with cystic degeneration that was twisted, highly vascular, and leaking. This intraoperative appearance explained the patient’s severe pain and the low-grade fever: torsion of a pedunculated myoma can compromise venous and lymphatic drainage, leading to ischaemia, necrosis, inflammatory leakage, and occasionally systemic signs of inflammation.

The operation was therefore not a blanket removal of all lesions, but a selective myomectomy guided by obstetric risk and technical feasibility. The pedunculated upper mass was the most acute problem because a twisted stalk can progress to infarction, rupture, or infection. It was removed by controlled dissection at its uterine implant, with the pedicle temporarily secured by clamps and the stump closed with absorbable suture to achieve haemostasis and restore the uterine wall. This is the key technical principle in pregnancy myomectomy: limit dissection, control bleeding quickly, and repair the uterus carefully to reduce postoperative bleeding and preserve the pregnancy.

By contrast, the caudal pelvic mass was left in place. Intraoperatively it appeared fixed, smooth, and consistent with a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma arising from the uterine torus rather than a freely resectable extrauterine lesion. Removing it would likely have required a larger uterine incision and a more extensive procedure, increasing the risks of haemorrhage, pregnancy loss, and later obstetric complications. In this context, the operative strategy was appropriately conservative: treat the lesion causing the acute complication, but avoid overintervention on a mass that could be managed expectantly with close follow-up.

Because there was still diagnostic uncertainty before histology, peritoneal fluid was collected for cytology and culture. This is a reasonable precaution when a mass has atypical imaging features or appears inflammatory or leaking, since it helps exclude occult malignancy or infection without materially increasing operative risk. The final pathology confirmed benign leiomyomas with areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, validating the decision to perform limited surgery rather than radical excision.

Maternal-fetal surveillance is a central part of surgery in pregnancy. Fetal heart activity should be documented before, during, and after the procedure, and the case report emphasizes this principle. Although continuous intraoperative fetal monitoring is not always feasible at 21 weeks’ gestation, repeated confirmation of viability helps guide obstetric decision-making and reassures the team that the fetus remains stable throughout maternal surgery. The use of perioperative progesterone in this case reflects an attempt to support uterine quiescence after surgery, even though such prophylaxis is not standardized across all settings. Antibiotic coverage was also appropriate because the patient had a documented urinary tract infection and was undergoing abdominal surgery; perioperative antibiotics help reduce maternal infectious risk, which is particularly important when there is concern for a contaminated or leaking mass.

Postoperatively, the major issue was blood loss and worsened anaemia. This is common after myomectomy in pregnancy, especially with large vascular leiomyomas. The patient required intravenous iron and transfusion to restore haemoglobin and oxygen-carrying capacity. After stabilization, she could be followed expectantly with serial obstetric assessments, including fetal growth and amniotic fluid surveillance. The later elective caesarean delivery was determined by the persistent pelvic myoma, which would have obstructed labour. Thus, the operative course illustrates the central management principle in complicated pregnancy-associated fibroid disease: intervene only when symptoms, torsion, bleeding, infection, or diagnostic uncertainty justify it, and when surgery is necessary, keep it as limited and obstetrically safe as possible.

## Pregnancy Outcome, Delivery Planning, and Clinical Lessons from Atypical Leiomyomas

Histopathology confirmed the intraoperative impression that these were benign uterine leiomyomas rather than adnexal malignancy. The cranial mass showed areas of ischaemic necrosis near a twisted pedicle, which is a classic consequence of torsion and vascular compromise in a pedunculated fibroid. This finding is clinically important because it explains the acute pain and may also account for the low-grade fever that developed preoperatively. In pregnancy, degeneration or torsion of a fibroid can produce symptoms that mimic infection or an acute surgical abdomen, so pathology is often what definitively resolves the diagnostic uncertainty.

After surgery, obstetric follow-up focused on maternal recovery and fetal well-being. Serial assessments were used to monitor fetal growth, amniotic fluid volume, and ongoing symptoms, while also keeping track of the residual pelvic mass. This is the appropriate surveillance strategy after conservative myomectomy in pregnancy: the immediate goal is to confirm that the pregnancy remains viable and that no postoperative complication, such as bleeding, preterm labor, or recurrent pain, is developing. In this case, the pregnancy remained stable, fetal growth was reassuring, and the maternal symptoms improved substantially after removal of the pedunculated lesion.

The postoperative course also illustrates the usual behavior of leiomyomas across pregnancy and the puerperium. Fibroids do not always enlarge steadily; instead, they may show a non-linear course with variable growth, degeneration, or even relative stability during gestation. Larger lesions, particularly those with limited vascular reserve, may undergo ischemic or cystic degeneration, as occurred here. After delivery, leiomyomas often regress partially as estrogen and progesterone levels fall and the uterus involutes. That natural postpartum shrinkage was seen in this patient, whose remaining pelvic myoma decreased in size from about 9 cm to 7.5 cm. This regression supported the decision to defer additional surgery, since she was asymptomatic and breastfeeding, and there was no immediate oncologic concern.

Delivery planning depended on the residual lower pelvic mass and its relationship to the birth canal. Even though the upper pedunculated fibroid had been removed, the caudal leiomyoma remained in the pelvis and would likely have obstructed fetal head descent during labor. When a fibroid is large, low-lying, or fixed in the pelvis, vaginal delivery may be mechanically impossible or unsafe. In such cases, the mode of delivery should be individualized based on the number, size, and exact location of the masses, rather than on gestational age alone. Here, an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks was chosen because the fetus had reached term, the pregnancy had remained stable, and the residual pelvic myoma represented a clear obstructive risk.

The prior uterine surgery also influenced delivery planning. Because a myomectomy had been performed on the uterine body during pregnancy, labor was not ideal, especially in the presence of a persistent lower uterine lesion and a surgical scar that could complicate uterine contractility or raise rupture concerns. The team therefore selected a planned caesarean on the same surgical scar to control timing, minimize the risk of emergency delivery, and avoid labor against an obstructed pelvis. The neonatal outcome was excellent, supporting the effectiveness of this strategy.

This case highlights several practical lessons. First, atypical leiomyomas in pregnancy can resemble adnexal tumors or decidualized endometriomas on ultrasound and MRI, so diagnosis requires integration of pelvic examination, imaging, and clinical context. Second, serum tumor markers, including CA-125, are unreliable in isolation during pregnancy and should not drive management without supportive imaging or pathology. Third, conservative management is preferred whenever possible, but surgery is justified when there is torsion, necrosis, infection, persistent severe symptoms, or a significant diagnostic dilemma. Finally, delivery mode must be tailored to the anatomic consequences of the residual mass and any uterine surgery, with the priority of protecting both mother and fetus while avoiding overtreatment.

A practical approach to similar cases is to begin with careful clinical examination and obstetric assessment, then use ultrasound with Doppler as the first-line imaging test and non-contrast MRI when anatomy remains unclear. Multidisciplinary review is essential when the mass is large, symptomatic, or atypical. If there is convincing evidence of complications such as torsion, necrosis, or compression symptoms, limited surgery should be performed with the shortest safe operative time and ongoing fetal monitoring. Afterward, close obstetric surveillance should continue until delivery, and mode of birth should be planned according to the residual mass, uterine scar, and pelvic anatomy. This case is a good example of how individualized management can preserve pregnancy, avoid unnecessary radical surgery, and still achieve excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes.
