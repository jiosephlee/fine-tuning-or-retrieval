Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Operate or Observe? The Management Tension of Symptomatic Pelvic Masses in Pregnancy

## The case that forces the question

Pregnancy can turn a “mystery mass” into a management headache very quickly. In this case, a woman in her 40s at 21 weeks’ gestation presented with abdominal pain, dyspnea, and a surprisingly enlarged abdomen. Imaging showed two very large cystic-appearing masses, one crowding the left upper abdomen and the other filling the pelvis. The differential included decidualized endometriomas and cystic-degenerating leiomyomas. Tumor markers were mostly unrevealing, except for a mildly elevated and rising CA-125.

The real tension here wasn’t just diagnosis. It was the classic pregnant-surgery dilemma: do you watch and wait, or do you operate before the mass declares itself with torsion, rupture, infection, or worsening maternal compromise?

This case is useful because it shows that “conservative by default” is not the same as “never operate.” Sometimes the right move is still surgery—but only after you’ve thought hard about what problem you are actually trying to solve.

## Why pelvic masses in pregnancy are so hard

During pregnancy, diagnostic and therapeutic options are limited. You want enough information to avoid missing malignancy or a surgical emergency, but you also want to minimize fetal risk. In practice, that usually means:

- careful bimanual exam
- ultrasound with Doppler
- non-contrast MRI when anatomy is unclear

This case illustrates a common trap: large leiomyomas can mimic adnexal masses, and degenerating fibroids can look cystic enough to resemble endometriomas or even neoplasm. Once the uterus is enlarged and the anatomy is distorted, identifying a stalk or seeing a clear tissue plane may be impossible.

CA-125 also adds noise rather than clarity. Mild elevation in pregnancy is not rare, and a value just above the usual nonpregnant cutoff should not be overcalled as malignancy in isolation. Serial trend matters more than a single number, and even then it must be interpreted in context.

## When observation is reasonable

For most fibroids discovered in pregnancy, conservative management is preferred. That’s because surgery itself carries real obstetric risk:

- hemorrhage
- pregnancy loss
- preterm labor
- uterine rupture
- need for cesarean delivery later on

If the patient is stable, pain is controlled, fetal status is reassuring, and imaging does not raise major oncologic concern, observation is usually the default.

This is especially true when the mass is:

- not causing compression symptoms
- not growing rapidly
- not suspicious for torsion or infection
- not clearly obstructing future delivery

In other words: if it’s ugly but quiet, you can often watch it.

## What pushes you toward intervention

This case had several features that moved the team away from observation:

### 1) Persistent symptoms
The patient had significant abdominal pain and dyspnea. Those symptoms improved somewhat with positioning and acetaminophen, but they were not trivial. Mechanical restriction from a large mass can become progressively less tolerable as the pregnancy advances.

### 2) Mass effect
The size and location mattered. One lesion occupied the subphrenic region; the other was fixed in the pelvis. That creates two problems:

- maternal compression symptoms now
- potential obstruction of uterine enlargement and later delivery

### 3) Diagnostic uncertainty
Imaging suggested benign disease, but not with enough certainty to ignore the possibility of a complicated or atypical lesion. When you cannot confidently characterize a giant mass, you have to ask whether the cost of waiting exceeds the cost of operating.

### 4) Risk of acute complications
Large pedunculated or degenerating myomas can twist, infarct, leak, rupture, or get infected. In this patient, the cranial mass was in fact twisted and leaking at surgery, with ischemic necrosis on pathology. That’s the kind of pathology that retrospectively validates the concern.

### 5) Future obstetric obstruction
The caudal mass was left in situ because removing it would have carried more obstetric risk. But it was still large enough to pose a delivery problem. That sort of anatomy forces individualized planning for the mode of delivery.

## The decision-making principle: intervene for physiology, not just for size

A big take-home from this case is that size alone is not the whole story. Plenty of large fibroids can be observed if they are asymptomatic and clearly benign-appearing. Conversely, a smaller lesion can warrant surgery if it is torsed, infected, bleeding, or causing severe pain.

The practical threshold for intervention in pregnancy is usually driven by a combination of:

- worsening pain despite conservative measures
- dyspnea or restrictive physiology
- concern for torsion or necrosis
- possible rupture or infection
- uncertainty about diagnosis, including malignancy
- impending obstruction of uterine growth or delivery

That “combined indication” approach is much more useful than asking, “How big is it?”

## Why MRI mattered here

Ultrasound was the first-line test, but it wasn’t enough to settle the question. Non-contrast MRI helped refine the anatomy, characterize wall thickness and internal signal, and assess continuity with the uterus. In pregnancy, that’s often the best next step when ultrasound leaves ambiguity.

A few practical points:

- non-contrast MRI is useful when ultrasound is limited by body habitus, gestational size, or complex anatomy
- gadolinium is generally avoided unless there is a compelling indication
- MRI can help distinguish uterine from adnexal origin, which directly affects management

Even with MRI, though, this case shows that sometimes the “answer” only appears at laparotomy.

## Why the team chose surgery

By 21+6 weeks, the patient had:

- persistent pain
- dyspnea from mass effect
- uncertain diagnosis
- concern for future complications
- a lesion that appeared pedunculated and highly vascularized

That’s a reasonable justification for operative management, especially if the goal is to remove the highest-risk lesion while minimizing fetal and uterine disruption.

At surgery, the cranial lesion was a pedunculated leiomyoma with twisting and leakage, and it was excised. The pelvic mass was left in place to reduce obstetric risk. That selective strategy is important: surgery in pregnancy is not always “all or nothing.” Sometimes the safest approach is to address the immediate problem and defer the rest.

## The surgical tradeoff in pregnancy

Myomectomy during pregnancy is not routine, but it can be appropriate in selected cases. The key is to keep the operation as limited, efficient, and pregnancy-preserving as possible.

Potential benefits:

- relief of maternal symptoms
- prevention of torsion, rupture, or infection
- diagnostic certainty
- avoidance of later, more difficult surgery

Potential harms:

- bleeding
- uterine irritation
- miscarriage or preterm labor
- technical difficulty from enlarged uterus and distorted anatomy

This is why the decision has to be multidisciplinary. Obstetrics, gynecology, surgery, anesthesia, and sometimes radiology all have a role in deciding whether the maternal benefit justifies the fetal risk.

## Obstetric follow-up still matters after successful surgery

The case didn’t end in the operating room. Postoperative management included anemia correction, infection treatment, progesterone support, and close fetal surveillance. The pregnancy continued to term, and delivery was by elective cesarean because the residual pelvic myoma would have obstructed vaginal delivery.

That sequence is a nice reminder that once you decide to intervene, the work is not just “remove mass and move on.” You still have to think through:

- preterm labor risk
- fetal monitoring
- maternal anemia and infection
- mode of delivery
- timing of delivery relative to the residual lesion

## Clinical pearls

- A symptomatic pelvic mass in pregnancy is not automatically “observe until after delivery.”
- Worsening pain, dyspnea, diagnostic uncertainty, and risk of torsion/rupture/infection are all legitimate reasons to consider surgery.
- Mild CA-125 elevation in pregnancy is nonspecific and should not drive management alone.
- Ultrasound is first-line, but non-contrast MRI can be decisive when anatomy is unclear.
- When operating in pregnancy, aim for the smallest effective procedure that addresses the immediate risk.
- Delivery planning should account for residual mass size, location, and the likelihood of mechanical obstruction.

## Bottom line

This case sits right at the intersection of maternal symptoms, fetal safety, and diagnostic ambiguity. The lesson is not that surgery is always better—or that conservative care is always safer. The lesson is that large symptomatic pelvic masses in pregnancy demand a tailored, multidisciplinary risk assessment.

If the mass is causing physiology problems, may be complicated, or remains diagnostically uncertain, observation can become the riskier option. In those cases, selective surgery with pregnancy-preserving intent may be the most conservative choice of all.

## Take-home points

- In pregnancy, “watchful waiting” is appropriate only when the mass is stable, benign-appearing, and not causing maternal or obstetric compromise.
- Pain, dyspnea, torsion, rupture, infection, and space-occupying effects are strong arguments for intervention.
- Mild CA-125 elevation is not enough to label a lesion malignant in pregnancy.
- Non-contrast MRI is a valuable adjunct when ultrasound cannot confidently define origin or behavior.
- If surgery is needed, focus on the lesion most likely to cause immediate harm and preserve the pregnancy whenever possible.
