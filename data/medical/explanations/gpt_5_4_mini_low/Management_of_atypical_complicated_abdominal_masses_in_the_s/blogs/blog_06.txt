Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Fibroid That Determines the Delivery: Planning Birth Around Residual Pelvic Obstruction

## Why this case is worth your attention

Pregnancy and fibroids usually co-exist uneasily, but this case takes that tension to the next level: a patient in the second trimester presented with pain, dyspnea, and two large abdominal masses that turned out not to be ovarian tumors, but uterine leiomyomas with atypical cystic degeneration. One of them was removed. The other was intentionally left behind.

That decision matters, because the remaining caudal myoma was not just an incidental finding. It sat in the pelvis, fixed and bulky enough to create a likely mechanical barrier to vaginal delivery. In the end, it became the lesion that “decided” the mode of birth: elective cesarean at 37 weeks, with good maternal and neonatal outcomes.

This is a useful case for thinking about a bigger obstetric principle: in pregnant patients with fibroids, delivery planning is not just about gestational age or symptoms. It’s about anatomy.

## When a fibroid becomes a delivery problem

Most fibroids in pregnancy are managed conservatively. That’s the default for a reason: surgery carries real risks, including bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. But not all fibroids behave like quiet bystanders.

In this case, the residual pelvic mass was:
- Large
- Fixed in the lesser pelvis
- Located posteriorly, near the uterine torus / lower pelvic compartment
- Persistent after the first surgery

That combination is exactly what raises concern for obstructed labor. Even if the pregnancy continues uneventfully, a mass in the lower pelvis can interfere with descent and engagement of the fetal head. In practical terms, a fibroid like this can make vaginal birth unrealistic or unsafe.

The key is not simply “fibroid present” versus “fibroid absent.” It’s:
- Size
- Number
- Location
- Relationship to the cervix, lower uterine segment, and birth canal
- Whether the lesion distorts the pelvis enough to block the presenting part

## Anatomy beats optimism

A common temptation is to hope the pelvis will “make room” as pregnancy progresses. Sometimes it does. Fibroids can also regress postpartum, which is why definitive treatment is often deferred until after delivery. But regression after birth does not help you if the mass is already occupying the pelvis during labor.

That’s the crucial teaching point here: postpartum shrinkage may eventually simplify management, but it does not erase intrapartum obstruction.

This patient’s caudal myoma was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk during the first operation, which was reasonable because the more urgent problem was the symptomatic cranial mass. But once that residual pelvic fibroid was identified as a likely barrier to fetal head progression, the delivery plan had to change. The team appropriately planned an elective cesarean rather than forcing a trial of labor against pelvic anatomy.

## Imaging isn’t just about diagnosis — it’s about birth planning

This case also reinforces how useful ultrasound and non-contrast MRI are in pregnancy when the anatomy is unclear.

The upper lesion initially looked like a huge decidualized endometrioma, and the lower lesion seemed like an adnexal mass adherent to a posterior uterine myoma. MRI helped refine the differential and demonstrate the continuity between the caudal mass and uterine tissue. That matters, because once you recognize the lesion as a fibroid rather than an ovarian mass, the entire risk conversation changes.

For delivery planning, imaging should answer:
- Is the mass in the lower uterine segment or true pelvis?
- Does it distort the cervix or block the pelvic inlet/outlet?
- Is it pedunculated, intramural, subserosal, or fixed?
- Is there more than one lesion?
- What is the likely relationship to the fetal presenting part?

If the answer suggests persistent outlet obstruction, then cesarean should be planned early rather than discovered late in labor.

## Why leaving the caudal mass in place made sense

The operative team removed the cranial pedunculated fibroid because it was twisted, vascular, and likely responsible for symptoms and fever. The caudal mass was different: fixed, pelvic, and not the immediate source of trouble. Attempting to remove both during pregnancy would likely have increased bleeding and obstetric risk.

That is an important surgical principle in pregnancy: don’t chase every lesion. Remove what is dangerous or clearly driving symptoms, and avoid turning a manageable situation into a major obstetric emergency.

In this patient, the staged approach worked:
- First surgery addressed the acute symptomatic lesion
- Pregnancy continued with close follow-up
- Delivery was then planned around the remaining pelvic obstruction

That’s individualized care, not undertreatment.

## Timing of delivery: why 37 weeks was a smart finish line

Once the residual fibroid was recognized as a likely mechanical obstacle, the question became not whether to deliver vaginally, but when and how to deliver surgically.

Elective cesarean at 37 weeks balanced several competing issues:
- Avoid spontaneous labor and an obstructed vaginal delivery
- Avoid ongoing fibroid-related growth or degeneration
- Reduce the chance of urgent, unplanned surgery
- Allow fetal maturity to remain acceptable

This is the practical art of obstetric planning: if the pelvis is blocked, don’t wait for labor to tell you that the block is real.

## Postpartum regression: real, but not a reason to gamble

Fibroids often shrink after delivery, and this patient’s residual myoma did decrease somewhat postpartum. That’s clinically relevant, because postpartum regression can justify delayed definitive treatment if the patient is asymptomatic and wants to avoid another surgery.

But regression should not be used to justify a risky vaginal delivery in the presence of clear obstruction.

A useful way to think about it:
- Postpartum regression informs future management
- It does not change the immediate anatomy of labor

In this case, that meant:
- No rush to re-operate postpartum because symptoms improved and the mass regressed somewhat
- But still no attempt at vaginal birth, because the mass likely would have blocked descent

## Practical pearls for the bedside

When you see fibroids in pregnancy, don’t just ask “is it benign?” Ask, “Will this mass let the baby out?”

A few bedside principles stand out from this case:
- A fixed pelvic mass in pregnancy is not an incidental finding until proven otherwise.
- Lower uterine segment and posterior pelvic fibroids deserve special attention because they can obstruct labor.
- Ultrasound is first-line; non-contrast MRI is helpful when anatomy is unclear.
- Conservative management is usually preferred, but symptomatic, torsed, infected, or obstructive lesions may require surgery.
- If a residual fibroid is likely to block the pelvis, plan cesarean rather than waiting for labor to fail.

## Take-home points

- Fibroid management in pregnancy is not just about symptom control — it’s about anatomy and delivery feasibility.
- A residual lower pelvic or lower uterine segment myoma can be a true mechanical barrier to vaginal birth.
- Postpartum fibroid regression is real, but it should not override intrapartum obstruction risk.
- Ultrasound plus non-contrast MRI can help distinguish ovarian masses from fibroids and guide birth planning.
- In selected cases, a staged strategy — remove the dangerous lesion, leave the rest, and plan cesarean later — is the safest path for mother and fetus.
