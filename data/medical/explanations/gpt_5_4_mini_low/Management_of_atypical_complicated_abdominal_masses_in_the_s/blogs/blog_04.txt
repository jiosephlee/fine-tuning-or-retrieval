Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Pregnant, Dyspneic, and Full of Fibroids: Mass Effect as a Real Maternal Complication

## The part that should make you pause

A pregnant patient with dyspnea is supposed to trigger your usual dangerous differential: PE, pneumonia, asthma, cardiomyopathy, anemia, pulmonary edema, maybe even anxiety if you’re feeling unlucky. What’s easy to miss is that pregnancy itself can also become the victim of **mechanical physiology** — especially when the abdomen is packed with large masses.

This case is a great reminder that **mass effect can be a true maternal complication**, not just an incidental imaging finding. Here, a woman in her second trimester had severe left upper quadrant pain, pelvic pain, and mild dyspnea. The punchline? The dyspnea was not cardiopulmonary disease. It was **restrictive physiology from huge abdominal/pelvic masses**.

And those masses turned out not to be ovarian tumors, but **atypical leiomyomas** with cystic degeneration and torsion-related ischemic change.

## Why this case is clinically interesting

Pregnancy limits your diagnostic and therapeutic options. You can’t just reflexively send everyone to contrast CT or rush to surgery because the anatomy is weird and the stakes are high. In this patient, the team had to answer several questions at once:

- Is the dyspnea coming from a dangerous cardiopulmonary process?
- Are these adnexal masses, degenerating fibroids, or something malignant?
- Is conservative management still safe?
- At what point does symptom burden and mass effect justify surgery during pregnancy?

That combination — **symptoms, uncertainty, and restricted imaging** — is what makes this case worth learning from.

## Initial assessment: don’t anchor on pregnancy

She had normal oxygen saturation, normal ECG, and no hemodynamic instability. That matters. Dyspnea in pregnancy is common, but that doesn’t mean it should be dismissed. A structured assessment still applies:

- Vital signs and oxygenation
- Cardiopulmonary exam
- ECG
- Targeted evaluation for infection, anemia, thromboembolism, and heart failure when indicated

In this case, the exam was reassuring from a cardiac and pulmonary standpoint. The abdomen, however, was not. She had an enlarged abdomen for gestational age, a large tender upper abdominal mass, and a fixed pelvic mass on bimanual exam.

That bimanual exam is worth emphasizing. In pregnancy, it can still be a useful clue when imaging is equivocal. The pelvic mass was not just “seen on imaging” — it was **palpable, fixed, and clinically meaningful**.

## Dyspnea from mass effect: how it happens

The patient’s shortness of breath was attributed to **restrictive lung mechanics from compression** by the gravid uterus plus very large abdominal lesions. That’s a real physiologic problem:

- Reduced diaphragmatic excursion
- Decreased functional residual capacity
- Increased work of breathing
- Worse symptoms in supine position
- More pronounced discomfort with large upper abdominal masses

Her symptoms improved somewhat with semi-upright positioning and analgesia, which also supports a mechanical component.

A useful clinical pearl: if dyspnea improves with **positioning**, and the cardiopulmonary workup is unrevealing, think about **mass effect** rather than defaulting to an intrinsic lung or heart problem.

## The imaging trap: not every adnexal mass is an adnexal mass

Ultrasound showed two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity. The initial differential included decidualized endometriomas, adnexal neoplasm, and cystic degenerating leiomyomas.

This is where pregnancy-specific imaging nuance matters. Uterine fibroids can masquerade as adnexal masses, especially when they are:

- Pedunculated
- Large
- Degenerating/cystic
- Posterior or subserosal
- Distorted by the gravid uterus

The team appropriately escalated to **non-contrast MRI**, which is a key pregnancy-safe tool when ultrasound cannot define the anatomy. MRI suggested thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid, and a continuity with posterior uterine myoma for the caudal lesion. Even then, the diagnosis was not fully certain.

That uncertainty is the point: in pregnancy, the question is often not “what is the exact pathology?” but “is this likely benign enough and stable enough to watch?”

## CA-125: helpful? maybe. decisive? no.

CA-125 was mildly elevated and trending up. That understandably nudged the differential toward endometrioma or ovarian pathology, but this is exactly where clinicians can get fooled.

Important reminder:

- CA-125 can rise physiologically in pregnancy
- Mild elevations are nonspecific
- Trend matters more than a single isolated value
- It is **not** a stand-alone cancer test in pregnancy

In this case, the lab data did not solve the problem. It only added to the uncertainty.

## When does mass effect become an indication for intervention?

Usually, fibroids in pregnancy are managed conservatively. Surgery is avoided because of the risks of:

- Bleeding
- Pregnancy loss
- Preterm labor
- Uterine rupture

But conservative management has limits. This patient had several reasons to move toward surgery:

- Worsening pain
- Restrictive dyspnea
- Very large, vascularized masses
- Mixed solid/cystic features
- Concern for torsion, rupture, infection, or necrosis
- Concern that the masses would obstruct future uterine enlargement
- Uncertain diagnosis despite ultrasound and MRI

That’s the key FOAM take-home: **mass effect alone can be a legitimate maternal indication for procedural intervention** when symptoms are significant and conservative management is no longer acceptable.

## The operative decision: do the least necessary thing

At laparotomy, the cranial mass was found to be a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma that had twisted and was leaking, with ischemic necrosis near the pedicle — a very plausible explanation for her pain and fever.

The team removed that lesion and left the caudal mass in situ because it was fixed in the pelvis and would have increased obstetric risk if aggressively resected.

That is a very good surgical principle in pregnancy: **don’t chase completeness if the cost is obstetric harm**. Remove the lesion that is causing acute danger, and preserve the pregnancy when feasible.

The team also monitored fetal viability intraoperatively and documented fetal heart activity. That’s consistent with the broader principle that fetal monitoring should be planned around the procedure, not improvised after the fact.

## Outcome matters here

This case had an excellent ending:

- Pain and symptoms improved
- The patient was treated for anemia and infection
- Fetal growth remained appropriate on follow-up
- The residual pelvic myoma was left alone until delivery
- She ultimately underwent elective cesarean delivery because the remaining mass would have obstructed vaginal birth
- Mother and baby did well

This is a good example of staged obstetric surgical thinking:
1. Treat the acute symptomatic lesion
2. Stabilize and follow closely
3. Deliver when term is reached and anatomy demands it
4. Defer more definitive surgery until postpartum if symptoms allow

## Practical pearls from the case

- **Dyspnea in pregnancy is not always cardiopulmonary.** If vitals and exam are reassuring, look for mechanical causes.
- **Always examine the abdomen and pelvis carefully.** Bimanual exam can add important information.
- **Ultrasound first, MRI second.** Non-contrast MRI is a useful problem-solving tool in pregnancy.
- **CA-125 is not definitive in pregnancy.** Mild elevation may mislead more than it helps.
- **Fibroids can mimic adnexal masses.** Especially when large, degenerating, pedunculated, or posterior.
- **Surgery in pregnancy is reserved for symptoms, complications, or concerning features.** When you operate, aim for the shortest, safest, most pregnancy-preserving procedure.
- **Mass effect can justify intervention.** Severe pain and restrictive dyspnea are not trivial symptoms.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that a pregnant patient with dyspnea and abdominal pain may have a problem that is not pulmonary at all — it may be **space-occupying disease causing real mechanical compromise**. When the mass is large, symptomatic, and diagnostically uncertain, conservative management may no longer be the safest choice.

In other words: sometimes the lungs are fine. It’s the abdomen that’s doing the damage.

## Take-home points

- **Consider mass effect** in pregnant patients with dyspnea, especially when cardiopulmonary evaluation is unrevealing.
- **Non-contrast MRI** is a valuable adjunct when ultrasound cannot clarify the origin of a mass.
- **Mild CA-125 elevation is nonspecific in pregnancy** and should not drive management alone.
- **Operate for symptoms or complications, not just imaging.**
- **In pregnancy, the goal is not perfect resection — it’s the safest possible balance between maternal symptom control and fetal preservation.**
