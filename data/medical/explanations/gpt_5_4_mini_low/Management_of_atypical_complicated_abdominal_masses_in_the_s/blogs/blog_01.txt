Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When a “Huge Adnexal Mass” Is Actually a Fibroid: Diagnostic Traps in Pregnancy

## The case that fools your eyes

A pregnant patient shows up with pain, dyspnea, and what looks like a giant adnexal mass on ultrasound. The reflex is to think: ovarian tumor, endometrioma, maybe something malignant.

But sometimes the “adnexal” mass is actually a uterine leiomyoma wearing a very convincing disguise.

That is the trap in this case. A woman in her second trimester presented with upper abdominal pain and pelvic pain, plus mild dyspnea. Imaging suggested two large cystic masses: one high in the left upper abdomen and another deep in the pelvis. On ultrasound and MRI, the lesions looked like unilocular adnexal cysts with vascularized walls and some solid-appearing components. CA-125 was mildly elevated. The differential leaned toward decidualized endometrioma.

At surgery, the truth was more surprising: the cranial “cystic adnexal mass” was actually a huge pedunculated, vascular, twisted, leaking leiomyoma arising from the uterus. The pelvic lesion was also myomatous in origin.

This is exactly the kind of case that reminds us how pregnancy can distort anatomy, blur imaging landmarks, and make benign pathology look much more ominous than it is.

## Why fibroids are such convincing imposters in pregnancy

Leiomyomas can masquerade as adnexal masses for several reasons:

- The gravid uterus displaces pelvic organs and stretches anatomy out of shape.
- Pedunculated fibroids can lose their obvious connection to the uterus.
- Posterior or intramural-subserosal fibroids may be difficult to trace on ultrasound.
- Degeneration can create cystic, heterogeneous, or complex-appearing lesions.
- Torsion or ischemic change can add pain, edema, and inflammatory features that make the mass look “aggressive.”

In this case, the upper lesion occupied the left subphrenic space and extended to the uterine fundus, while the lower lesion filled the pouch of Douglas. That anatomy alone should make you cautious about assuming an ovarian origin. Large uterine masses can migrate upward and outward as the uterus enlarges, and the usual landmarks become unreliable.

## Bedside clues matter more than people think

Imaging matters, but this case also highlights the value of a careful exam. The bimanual examination found a fixed, hard, rounded mass in the lesser pelvis partially bulging into the posterior fornix. That is not the classic story of a simple ovarian cyst.

A few practical bedside clues should raise suspicion for uterine origin:

- A mass that feels fixed to the uterus or pelvis rather than freely mobile
- Posterior fornix bulging
- Abdominal enlargement out of proportion to gestational age
- Mechanical symptoms such as dyspnea from mass effect
- Pain suggesting torsion, degeneration, or pressure-related complications

A good pelvic exam won’t “rule in” fibroid origin by itself, but it can keep you from anchoring too early on an ovarian diagnosis.

## Ultrasound: first-line, but not always definitive

Ultrasound is the first test for a reason: it’s safe, accessible, and usually enough to identify many pregnancy-related mass lesions. But in a uterus full of distortion, ultrasound can be misleading.

In this case, the lesions were described as:

- Large
- Unilocular cystic
- Regular margins
- Peripheral vascularization

Those features can fit an adnexal lesion, especially a decidualized endometrioma. But they can also fit a degenerating fibroid, particularly when the lesion is large and the uterine attachment is obscured.

What should you look for on ultrasound?

- A stalk or continuity with the uterus
- Relationship to the myometrium
- Doppler flow patterns
- Solid components suggesting fibroid degeneration
- Posterior location and distortion of the uterine contour

One key limitation in this case was the inability to identify a clear feeding vessel or connective stalk for the upper mass. That made the lesion look ovarian. But absence of an obvious stalk does not exclude uterine origin when the uterus is enormous and anatomy is displaced.

## MRI without contrast: the problem-solver

When ultrasound is indeterminate, non-contrast MRI is the next best step in pregnancy. It offers better soft tissue definition and can help determine whether a lesion is arising from the ovary, uterus, or somewhere else entirely.

Here, MRI showed:

- Thick walls
- Inhomogeneous fluid content
- Focal hyperintense solid components
- No adenopathy
- The caudal lesion seeming continuous with a posterior myoma

That is exactly the kind of result that should keep fibroid degeneration on the table.

The practical MRI lesson is simple: if you can trace continuity with the uterus, think fibroid until proven otherwise. If you can’t, don’t assume ovarian origin too quickly—pregnancy can erase the very anatomic clues you are looking for.

## CA-125: useful data point, dangerous anchor

The mildly elevated CA-125 in this patient nudged the team toward an adnexal diagnosis. That’s understandable, but CA-125 is a classic pregnancy trap.

A few reminders:

- CA-125 can rise physiologically in pregnancy, especially early.
- It can also be elevated with benign gynecologic disease.
- A single value is rarely diagnostic.
- Mild elevation is not enough to call malignancy.

In this case, CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL, slightly above the standard nonpregnant cutoff. That is not the kind of elevation that should override the anatomy, exam, and clinical context. Serial trend may help, but isolated CA-125 values can easily lead you astray.

## When to operate

Most fibroids in pregnancy are managed conservatively. Surgery carries real risks: bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine injury. So why operate here?

Because management is about symptoms, complications, and uncertainty—not just the diagnosis label.

This patient had several reasons that pushed the team toward surgery:

- Worsening pain
- Dyspnea from mass effect
- Concern for torsion, rupture, infection, or necrosis
- Need for a definitive diagnosis
- Large size and likely future mechanical obstruction as the uterus grew

At laparotomy, the cranial mass was twisted, vascular, and leaking, with ischemic necrosis near the pedicle on pathology. That validated the decision to intervene.

The key point: if a fibroid is symptomatic, pedunculated, torsed, or suspicious for complication, myomectomy in pregnancy can be appropriate in selected cases. The goal is not maximal surgery; it is the smallest safe intervention that addresses the problem and preserves pregnancy.

## Why they removed one mass and left the other

This is a very FOAM-worthy surgical principle: not all pathology discovered in the OR should be chased to completion.

In this case, the surgical team removed the dangerous upper pedunculated fibroid and left the pelvic mass in situ because it appeared fixed and its removal would have added obstetric risk. That restraint mattered.

Sometimes the best surgery in pregnancy is selective surgery:

- Remove the lesion causing the acute issue
- Avoid prolonged dissection
- Minimize blood loss
- Preserve uterine integrity
- Prioritize fetal viability and maternal stability

That approach is especially sensible when the residual lesion is not acutely dangerous and can be addressed later, if needed, after delivery.

## Delivery planning is part of the treatment plan

The story didn’t end with surgery. The residual pelvic myoma was still large enough to obstruct the birth canal, so the team planned an elective cesarean section at 37 weeks.

That is an important reminder: the management of fibroids in pregnancy is not just “operate or don’t operate.” It also affects how and when delivery happens.

Think ahead about:

- Whether the mass will obstruct labor
- Whether the lesion is near the lower uterine segment or cervix
- Whether vaginal delivery is feasible
- Whether cesarean should be planned to avoid emergent complications

In this case, delivery planning was individualized based on the size and position of the remaining myoma.

## Practical take-home points

- A huge “adnexal” mass in pregnancy may be a fibroid, especially if it is posterior, pedunculated, or degenerating.
- Always pair imaging with a careful bimanual exam; anatomy is distorted in pregnancy, but the exam still gives useful clues.
- Ultrasound is first-line, but non-contrast MRI is often the tie-breaker when the origin of the lesion is unclear.
- Mild CA-125 elevation is a weak signal in pregnancy and should not dominate your differential.
- Operate for symptoms, complications, or significant diagnostic uncertainty—not simply because a mass looks scary.
- If surgery is needed, keep it targeted and as short as possible.
- Delivery planning should account for residual mass size, location, and risk of obstructing labor.

## Bottom line

Pregnancy can make a uterine fibroid look like an ovarian tumor, and a complex cystic lesion can be a pedunculated, torsed, degenerating leiomyoma in disguise. The trick is to resist premature closure: examine the patient, trace the anatomy, use ultrasound wisely, escalate to non-contrast MRI when needed, and keep the differential broad until the uterus has had its say.
