Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] How should a mildly elevated CA-125 level in the second trimester be interpreted in a pregnant patient with adnexal-appearing masses, and what are the major limitations of this marker in pregnancy?

A mildly elevated CA-125 in the second trimester should be interpreted very cautiously and, by itself, should not be taken as evidence of malignancy. In this case, the value was only slightly above the conventional cutoff (41.8 U/mL) and rose from 36 U/mL over 2 weeks, but the patient ultimately had benign uterine leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis and torsion. That is exactly the kind of scenario in which CA-125 can be misleading: it may be modestly increased in pregnancy and in a variety of benign gynecologic conditions, including endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammation, menstruation, and myomas.

The major limitation of CA-125 in pregnancy is poor specificity. Physiologic pregnancy-related changes can elevate the marker, especially in the first trimester and again near term, while second-trimester values are generally lower; therefore, an isolated mildly abnormal result in midpregnancy has limited diagnostic value. In the case report, the “slight positivity” of CA-125 contributed to concern for an adnexal process, but imaging and operative findings showed a benign uterine origin. Thus, CA-125 should be used, at most, as a supportive trend marker—not as a stand-alone diagnostic test—when evaluating adnexal-appearing masses in pregnancy. Management should rely primarily on clinical status and safe imaging, particularly ultrasound and non-contrast MRI.
