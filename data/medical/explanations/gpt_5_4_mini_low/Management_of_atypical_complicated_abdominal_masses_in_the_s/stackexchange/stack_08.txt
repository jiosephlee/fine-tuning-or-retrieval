Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] What perioperative measures are important when non-obstetric abdominal surgery is performed during pregnancy, including fetal assessment, maternal monitoring, and postoperative obstetric follow-up?

When non-obstetric abdominal surgery is necessary in pregnancy, the perioperative goal is to protect both mother and fetus while minimizing uterine irritability and obstetric complications. In this case, the team did the key things correctly.

First, fetal assessment should be documented before, during when feasible, and after the operation. The case report specifically notes that fetal cardiac activity was confirmed at laparotomy, and the discussion emphasizes that fetal heart rate must be checked and documented pre-, intra-, and postoperatively. In practice, this means confirming viability before surgery, using intermittent fetal assessment intraoperatively if gestational age and logistics allow, and reassessing after the procedure.

Second, maternal monitoring must be meticulous, because maternal physiology is the best determinant of fetal well-being. Continuous attention to oxygenation, hemodynamics, pain control, and signs of preterm labor is essential. In the report, the patient underwent multidisciplinary assessment, had stable oxygen saturation and hemodynamics, and her dyspnea was recognized as mechanical compression rather than cardiopulmonary disease. Intraoperatively, the team aimed for a short, definitive procedure and used appropriate prophylaxis and supportive care, including antibiotics and progesterone, reflecting the principle of reducing uterine irritability and infectious risk while maintaining maternal stability. Blood loss and anemia should also be anticipated and corrected promptly, as was necessary here with transfusion and IV iron.

Third, postoperative obstetric follow-up is mandatory. After abdominal surgery in pregnancy, the patient should have close surveillance for contractions, bleeding, membrane rupture, fetal growth, and amniotic fluid volume. In this case, the patient was followed every 3 weeks, with documented fetal growth and amniotic fluid assessment, and ultimately delivered electively at 37 weeks because the residual pelvic myoma would have obstructed labor. This illustrates an important point: postoperative management does not end with wound healing; it includes ongoing obstetric planning, including the likely mode and timing of delivery based on the residual mass and uterine scar.

So the practical teaching point is: confirm fetal viability around the operation, monitor maternal oxygenation and hemodynamics carefully, minimize operative time and obstetric stress, and ensure structured postpartum obstetric follow-up with a delivery plan tailored to the residual pathology.
