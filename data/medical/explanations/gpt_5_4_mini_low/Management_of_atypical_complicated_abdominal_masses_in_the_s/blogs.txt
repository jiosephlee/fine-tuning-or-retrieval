Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When a “Huge Adnexal Mass” Is Actually a Fibroid: Diagnostic Traps in Pregnancy

## The case that fools your eyes

A pregnant patient shows up with pain, dyspnea, and what looks like a giant adnexal mass on ultrasound. The reflex is to think: ovarian tumor, endometrioma, maybe something malignant.

But sometimes the “adnexal” mass is actually a uterine leiomyoma wearing a very convincing disguise.

That is the trap in this case. A woman in her second trimester presented with upper abdominal pain and pelvic pain, plus mild dyspnea. Imaging suggested two large cystic masses: one high in the left upper abdomen and another deep in the pelvis. On ultrasound and MRI, the lesions looked like unilocular adnexal cysts with vascularized walls and some solid-appearing components. CA-125 was mildly elevated. The differential leaned toward decidualized endometrioma.

At surgery, the truth was more surprising: the cranial “cystic adnexal mass” was actually a huge pedunculated, vascular, twisted, leaking leiomyoma arising from the uterus. The pelvic lesion was also myomatous in origin.

This is exactly the kind of case that reminds us how pregnancy can distort anatomy, blur imaging landmarks, and make benign pathology look much more ominous than it is.

## Why fibroids are such convincing imposters in pregnancy

Leiomyomas can masquerade as adnexal masses for several reasons:

- The gravid uterus displaces pelvic organs and stretches anatomy out of shape.
- Pedunculated fibroids can lose their obvious connection to the uterus.
- Posterior or intramural-subserosal fibroids may be difficult to trace on ultrasound.
- Degeneration can create cystic, heterogeneous, or complex-appearing lesions.
- Torsion or ischemic change can add pain, edema, and inflammatory features that make the mass look “aggressive.”

In this case, the upper lesion occupied the left subphrenic space and extended to the uterine fundus, while the lower lesion filled the pouch of Douglas. That anatomy alone should make you cautious about assuming an ovarian origin. Large uterine masses can migrate upward and outward as the uterus enlarges, and the usual landmarks become unreliable.

## Bedside clues matter more than people think

Imaging matters, but this case also highlights the value of a careful exam. The bimanual examination found a fixed, hard, rounded mass in the lesser pelvis partially bulging into the posterior fornix. That is not the classic story of a simple ovarian cyst.

A few practical bedside clues should raise suspicion for uterine origin:

- A mass that feels fixed to the uterus or pelvis rather than freely mobile
- Posterior fornix bulging
- Abdominal enlargement out of proportion to gestational age
- Mechanical symptoms such as dyspnea from mass effect
- Pain suggesting torsion, degeneration, or pressure-related complications

A good pelvic exam won’t “rule in” fibroid origin by itself, but it can keep you from anchoring too early on an ovarian diagnosis.

## Ultrasound: first-line, but not always definitive

Ultrasound is the first test for a reason: it’s safe, accessible, and usually enough to identify many pregnancy-related mass lesions. But in a uterus full of distortion, ultrasound can be misleading.

In this case, the lesions were described as:

- Large
- Unilocular cystic
- Regular margins
- Peripheral vascularization

Those features can fit an adnexal lesion, especially a decidualized endometrioma. But they can also fit a degenerating fibroid, particularly when the lesion is large and the uterine attachment is obscured.

What should you look for on ultrasound?

- A stalk or continuity with the uterus
- Relationship to the myometrium
- Doppler flow patterns
- Solid components suggesting fibroid degeneration
- Posterior location and distortion of the uterine contour

One key limitation in this case was the inability to identify a clear feeding vessel or connective stalk for the upper mass. That made the lesion look ovarian. But absence of an obvious stalk does not exclude uterine origin when the uterus is enormous and anatomy is displaced.

## MRI without contrast: the problem-solver

When ultrasound is indeterminate, non-contrast MRI is the next best step in pregnancy. It offers better soft tissue definition and can help determine whether a lesion is arising from the ovary, uterus, or somewhere else entirely.

Here, MRI showed:

- Thick walls
- Inhomogeneous fluid content
- Focal hyperintense solid components
- No adenopathy
- The caudal lesion seeming continuous with a posterior myoma

That is exactly the kind of result that should keep fibroid degeneration on the table.

The practical MRI lesson is simple: if you can trace continuity with the uterus, think fibroid until proven otherwise. If you can’t, don’t assume ovarian origin too quickly—pregnancy can erase the very anatomic clues you are looking for.

## CA-125: useful data point, dangerous anchor

The mildly elevated CA-125 in this patient nudged the team toward an adnexal diagnosis. That’s understandable, but CA-125 is a classic pregnancy trap.

A few reminders:

- CA-125 can rise physiologically in pregnancy, especially early.
- It can also be elevated with benign gynecologic disease.
- A single value is rarely diagnostic.
- Mild elevation is not enough to call malignancy.

In this case, CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL, slightly above the standard nonpregnant cutoff. That is not the kind of elevation that should override the anatomy, exam, and clinical context. Serial trend may help, but isolated CA-125 values can easily lead you astray.

## When to operate

Most fibroids in pregnancy are managed conservatively. Surgery carries real risks: bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine injury. So why operate here?

Because management is about symptoms, complications, and uncertainty—not just the diagnosis label.

This patient had several reasons that pushed the team toward surgery:

- Worsening pain
- Dyspnea from mass effect
- Concern for torsion, rupture, infection, or necrosis
- Need for a definitive diagnosis
- Large size and likely future mechanical obstruction as the uterus grew

At laparotomy, the cranial mass was twisted, vascular, and leaking, with ischemic necrosis near the pedicle on pathology. That validated the decision to intervene.

The key point: if a fibroid is symptomatic, pedunculated, torsed, or suspicious for complication, myomectomy in pregnancy can be appropriate in selected cases. The goal is not maximal surgery; it is the smallest safe intervention that addresses the problem and preserves pregnancy.

## Why they removed one mass and left the other

This is a very FOAM-worthy surgical principle: not all pathology discovered in the OR should be chased to completion.

In this case, the surgical team removed the dangerous upper pedunculated fibroid and left the pelvic mass in situ because it appeared fixed and its removal would have added obstetric risk. That restraint mattered.

Sometimes the best surgery in pregnancy is selective surgery:

- Remove the lesion causing the acute issue
- Avoid prolonged dissection
- Minimize blood loss
- Preserve uterine integrity
- Prioritize fetal viability and maternal stability

That approach is especially sensible when the residual lesion is not acutely dangerous and can be addressed later, if needed, after delivery.

## Delivery planning is part of the treatment plan

The story didn’t end with surgery. The residual pelvic myoma was still large enough to obstruct the birth canal, so the team planned an elective cesarean section at 37 weeks.

That is an important reminder: the management of fibroids in pregnancy is not just “operate or don’t operate.” It also affects how and when delivery happens.

Think ahead about:

- Whether the mass will obstruct labor
- Whether the lesion is near the lower uterine segment or cervix
- Whether vaginal delivery is feasible
- Whether cesarean should be planned to avoid emergent complications

In this case, delivery planning was individualized based on the size and position of the remaining myoma.

## Practical take-home points

- A huge “adnexal” mass in pregnancy may be a fibroid, especially if it is posterior, pedunculated, or degenerating.
- Always pair imaging with a careful bimanual exam; anatomy is distorted in pregnancy, but the exam still gives useful clues.
- Ultrasound is first-line, but non-contrast MRI is often the tie-breaker when the origin of the lesion is unclear.
- Mild CA-125 elevation is a weak signal in pregnancy and should not dominate your differential.
- Operate for symptoms, complications, or significant diagnostic uncertainty—not simply because a mass looks scary.
- If surgery is needed, keep it targeted and as short as possible.
- Delivery planning should account for residual mass size, location, and risk of obstructing labor.

## Bottom line

Pregnancy can make a uterine fibroid look like an ovarian tumor, and a complex cystic lesion can be a pedunculated, torsed, degenerating leiomyoma in disguise. The trick is to resist premature closure: examine the patient, trace the anatomy, use ultrasound wisely, escalate to non-contrast MRI when needed, and keep the differential broad until the uterus has had its say.



# CA-125 in the Second Trimester: Signal, Noise, or Red Herring?

## The part of this case that should make you pause

A pregnant patient in the second trimester shows up with abdominal pain, dyspnea, and two large abdominopelvic masses. Ultrasound and MRI are equivocal. And then the CA-125 comes back mildly elevated and rising: 36 to 41.8 U/mL.

That’s the trap.

In nonpregnant patients, clinicians can get uncomfortably anchored to tumor markers. In pregnancy, that instinct becomes even more dangerous. CA-125 can be a useful data point, but it is rarely decisive on its own. In this case, the borderline elevation added diagnostic uncertainty, but it did not define the diagnosis—and it certainly should not be used in isolation to justify major intervention.

This case is a nice reminder that in pregnancy, CA-125 is usually a clue, not a verdict.

## Why CA-125 is such a slippery marker in pregnancy

CA-125 is best known as an ovarian cancer marker, but it is notoriously nonspecific. In pregnancy, interpretation gets even messier because physiologic variation is expected.

A few useful principles:

- CA-125 can rise in normal pregnancy, especially in the first trimester.
- Levels usually normalize later, but mild elevations can still occur.
- A single value is hard to interpret.
- Small upward trends are not synonymous with malignancy.

In this report, the CA-125 was only mildly elevated above the standard cutoff and showed a slight increase over two weeks. That is enough to keep malignancy on the differential, but not enough to “diagnose cancer until proven otherwise.”

Other benign causes of elevation matter too, including:

- endometriosis
- adenomyosis
- inflammation/infection
- fibroids
- pregnancy itself

That last point is the critical one: pregnancy is not a clean baseline.

## What CA-125 can and cannot do here

In this case, the marker created a diagnostic bias toward decidualized endometrioma, especially because the upper lesion looked cystic and adnexal on imaging. But the imaging and operative findings ultimately showed pedunculated and intramural-subserosal uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration and torsion/ischemia.

That’s the lesson: CA-125 may nudge your pretest probability, but it should never outweigh the actual clinical picture.

Think of CA-125 during pregnancy as having three limitations:

1. **Poor specificity**
   A mild elevation does not distinguish benign from malignant disease.

2. **Weak positive predictive value**
   Especially in younger pregnant patients, the test is not a reliable cancer screen.

3. **Context dependence**
   Gestational age, symptoms, imaging, and trend matter more than one number.

## Better questions than “Is CA-125 elevated?”

When a pregnant patient has a complex mass, ask:

- What does the physical exam show?
- Is the patient symptomatic or unstable?
- Is there a torsion, rupture, infection, or bleeding concern?
- Does ultrasound suggest uterine, ovarian, or extra-gynecologic origin?
- Does MRI clarify anatomy without contrast?
- Is there a clinically meaningful trend in the marker, or just noise?

In other words, let CA-125 sit in the broader diagnostic frame instead of letting it become the frame.

## Imaging and exam still do the heavy lifting

This case also highlights something easy to forget: bimanual examination and careful imaging are not “old-school extras” in pregnancy—they are essential.

The team used:

- bimanual exam
- ultrasound with Doppler
- non-contrast MRI

That was the right sequence. MRI helped define the relationship between the masses and the uterus without resorting to contrast, which would have added risk without necessarily solving the problem.

The key point is that the mildly abnormal CA-125 did not drive the workup by itself. It was only one piece of a multidisciplinary evaluation.

## When a marker should change your management

A borderline CA-125 in pregnancy should not automatically push you toward aggressive surgery. Management should change when the **whole picture** changes:

- worsening pain
- dyspnea from mass effect
- concern for torsion, rupture, infection, or necrosis
- uncertain anatomy with possible oncologic pathology
- inability to safely continue pregnancy without intervention

That’s exactly what happened here. The surgery was not performed because CA-125 was 41.8. It was performed because the patient had large symptomatic masses, increasing concern for complications, and the anatomy remained uncertain.

That distinction matters.

## Practical take-home: avoid marker anchoring

A borderline CA-125 can be a red herring if you let it override the rest of the case. This patient’s excellent outcome came from multidisciplinary thinking, not from reacting to a number.

The right approach is:

- use CA-125 as a supplementary clue
- interpret it in gestational context
- follow trends, not isolated values
- prioritize exam, imaging, and symptoms
- reserve surgery for complications, oncologic concern, or significant mechanical effects

## Bottom line

This case is a strong example of why tumor markers in pregnancy need humility. CA-125 can rise physiologically, fluctuate mildly, and mislead clinicians into overcalling malignancy. In a pregnant patient with an abdominal mass, the marker should inform your differential—not hijack it.

## Take-home points

- Mild CA-125 elevation in pregnancy is **nonspecific** and often physiologic.
- A single abnormal value is far less useful than the **clinical context and imaging**.
- Borderline CA-125 should **not** drive overly aggressive management by itself.
- In symptomatic pregnant patients with abdominal masses, prioritize **exam, ultrasound, and non-contrast MRI**.
- Surgery should be guided by **symptoms, complications, and oncologic suspicion**, not by tumor markers alone.



# Operate or Observe? The Management Tension of Symptomatic Pelvic Masses in Pregnancy

## The case that forces the question

Pregnancy can turn a “mystery mass” into a management headache very quickly. In this case, a woman in her 40s at 21 weeks’ gestation presented with abdominal pain, dyspnea, and a surprisingly enlarged abdomen. Imaging showed two very large cystic-appearing masses, one crowding the left upper abdomen and the other filling the pelvis. The differential included decidualized endometriomas and cystic-degenerating leiomyomas. Tumor markers were mostly unrevealing, except for a mildly elevated and rising CA-125.

The real tension here wasn’t just diagnosis. It was the classic pregnant-surgery dilemma: do you watch and wait, or do you operate before the mass declares itself with torsion, rupture, infection, or worsening maternal compromise?

This case is useful because it shows that “conservative by default” is not the same as “never operate.” Sometimes the right move is still surgery—but only after you’ve thought hard about what problem you are actually trying to solve.

## Why pelvic masses in pregnancy are so hard

During pregnancy, diagnostic and therapeutic options are limited. You want enough information to avoid missing malignancy or a surgical emergency, but you also want to minimize fetal risk. In practice, that usually means:

- careful bimanual exam
- ultrasound with Doppler
- non-contrast MRI when anatomy is unclear

This case illustrates a common trap: large leiomyomas can mimic adnexal masses, and degenerating fibroids can look cystic enough to resemble endometriomas or even neoplasm. Once the uterus is enlarged and the anatomy is distorted, identifying a stalk or seeing a clear tissue plane may be impossible.

CA-125 also adds noise rather than clarity. Mild elevation in pregnancy is not rare, and a value just above the usual nonpregnant cutoff should not be overcalled as malignancy in isolation. Serial trend matters more than a single number, and even then it must be interpreted in context.

## When observation is reasonable

For most fibroids discovered in pregnancy, conservative management is preferred. That’s because surgery itself carries real obstetric risk:

- hemorrhage
- pregnancy loss
- preterm labor
- uterine rupture
- need for cesarean delivery later on

If the patient is stable, pain is controlled, fetal status is reassuring, and imaging does not raise major oncologic concern, observation is usually the default.

This is especially true when the mass is:

- not causing compression symptoms
- not growing rapidly
- not suspicious for torsion or infection
- not clearly obstructing future delivery

In other words: if it’s ugly but quiet, you can often watch it.

## What pushes you toward intervention

This case had several features that moved the team away from observation:

### 1) Persistent symptoms
The patient had significant abdominal pain and dyspnea. Those symptoms improved somewhat with positioning and acetaminophen, but they were not trivial. Mechanical restriction from a large mass can become progressively less tolerable as the pregnancy advances.

### 2) Mass effect
The size and location mattered. One lesion occupied the subphrenic region; the other was fixed in the pelvis. That creates two problems:

- maternal compression symptoms now
- potential obstruction of uterine enlargement and later delivery

### 3) Diagnostic uncertainty
Imaging suggested benign disease, but not with enough certainty to ignore the possibility of a complicated or atypical lesion. When you cannot confidently characterize a giant mass, you have to ask whether the cost of waiting exceeds the cost of operating.

### 4) Risk of acute complications
Large pedunculated or degenerating myomas can twist, infarct, leak, rupture, or get infected. In this patient, the cranial mass was in fact twisted and leaking at surgery, with ischemic necrosis on pathology. That’s the kind of pathology that retrospectively validates the concern.

### 5) Future obstetric obstruction
The caudal mass was left in situ because removing it would have carried more obstetric risk. But it was still large enough to pose a delivery problem. That sort of anatomy forces individualized planning for the mode of delivery.

## The decision-making principle: intervene for physiology, not just for size

A big take-home from this case is that size alone is not the whole story. Plenty of large fibroids can be observed if they are asymptomatic and clearly benign-appearing. Conversely, a smaller lesion can warrant surgery if it is torsed, infected, bleeding, or causing severe pain.

The practical threshold for intervention in pregnancy is usually driven by a combination of:

- worsening pain despite conservative measures
- dyspnea or restrictive physiology
- concern for torsion or necrosis
- possible rupture or infection
- uncertainty about diagnosis, including malignancy
- impending obstruction of uterine growth or delivery

That “combined indication” approach is much more useful than asking, “How big is it?”

## Why MRI mattered here

Ultrasound was the first-line test, but it wasn’t enough to settle the question. Non-contrast MRI helped refine the anatomy, characterize wall thickness and internal signal, and assess continuity with the uterus. In pregnancy, that’s often the best next step when ultrasound leaves ambiguity.

A few practical points:

- non-contrast MRI is useful when ultrasound is limited by body habitus, gestational size, or complex anatomy
- gadolinium is generally avoided unless there is a compelling indication
- MRI can help distinguish uterine from adnexal origin, which directly affects management

Even with MRI, though, this case shows that sometimes the “answer” only appears at laparotomy.

## Why the team chose surgery

By 21+6 weeks, the patient had:

- persistent pain
- dyspnea from mass effect
- uncertain diagnosis
- concern for future complications
- a lesion that appeared pedunculated and highly vascularized

That’s a reasonable justification for operative management, especially if the goal is to remove the highest-risk lesion while minimizing fetal and uterine disruption.

At surgery, the cranial lesion was a pedunculated leiomyoma with twisting and leakage, and it was excised. The pelvic mass was left in place to reduce obstetric risk. That selective strategy is important: surgery in pregnancy is not always “all or nothing.” Sometimes the safest approach is to address the immediate problem and defer the rest.

## The surgical tradeoff in pregnancy

Myomectomy during pregnancy is not routine, but it can be appropriate in selected cases. The key is to keep the operation as limited, efficient, and pregnancy-preserving as possible.

Potential benefits:

- relief of maternal symptoms
- prevention of torsion, rupture, or infection
- diagnostic certainty
- avoidance of later, more difficult surgery

Potential harms:

- bleeding
- uterine irritation
- miscarriage or preterm labor
- technical difficulty from enlarged uterus and distorted anatomy

This is why the decision has to be multidisciplinary. Obstetrics, gynecology, surgery, anesthesia, and sometimes radiology all have a role in deciding whether the maternal benefit justifies the fetal risk.

## Obstetric follow-up still matters after successful surgery

The case didn’t end in the operating room. Postoperative management included anemia correction, infection treatment, progesterone support, and close fetal surveillance. The pregnancy continued to term, and delivery was by elective cesarean because the residual pelvic myoma would have obstructed vaginal delivery.

That sequence is a nice reminder that once you decide to intervene, the work is not just “remove mass and move on.” You still have to think through:

- preterm labor risk
- fetal monitoring
- maternal anemia and infection
- mode of delivery
- timing of delivery relative to the residual lesion

## Clinical pearls

- A symptomatic pelvic mass in pregnancy is not automatically “observe until after delivery.”
- Worsening pain, dyspnea, diagnostic uncertainty, and risk of torsion/rupture/infection are all legitimate reasons to consider surgery.
- Mild CA-125 elevation in pregnancy is nonspecific and should not drive management alone.
- Ultrasound is first-line, but non-contrast MRI can be decisive when anatomy is unclear.
- When operating in pregnancy, aim for the smallest effective procedure that addresses the immediate risk.
- Delivery planning should account for residual mass size, location, and the likelihood of mechanical obstruction.

## Bottom line

This case sits right at the intersection of maternal symptoms, fetal safety, and diagnostic ambiguity. The lesson is not that surgery is always better—or that conservative care is always safer. The lesson is that large symptomatic pelvic masses in pregnancy demand a tailored, multidisciplinary risk assessment.

If the mass is causing physiology problems, may be complicated, or remains diagnostically uncertain, observation can become the riskier option. In those cases, selective surgery with pregnancy-preserving intent may be the most conservative choice of all.

## Take-home points

- In pregnancy, “watchful waiting” is appropriate only when the mass is stable, benign-appearing, and not causing maternal or obstetric compromise.
- Pain, dyspnea, torsion, rupture, infection, and space-occupying effects are strong arguments for intervention.
- Mild CA-125 elevation is not enough to label a lesion malignant in pregnancy.
- Non-contrast MRI is a valuable adjunct when ultrasound cannot confidently define origin or behavior.
- If surgery is needed, focus on the lesion most likely to cause immediate harm and preserve the pregnancy whenever possible.



# Pregnant, Dyspneic, and Full of Fibroids: Mass Effect as a Real Maternal Complication

## The part that should make you pause

A pregnant patient with dyspnea is supposed to trigger your usual dangerous differential: PE, pneumonia, asthma, cardiomyopathy, anemia, pulmonary edema, maybe even anxiety if you’re feeling unlucky. What’s easy to miss is that pregnancy itself can also become the victim of **mechanical physiology** — especially when the abdomen is packed with large masses.

This case is a great reminder that **mass effect can be a true maternal complication**, not just an incidental imaging finding. Here, a woman in her second trimester had severe left upper quadrant pain, pelvic pain, and mild dyspnea. The punchline? The dyspnea was not cardiopulmonary disease. It was **restrictive physiology from huge abdominal/pelvic masses**.

And those masses turned out not to be ovarian tumors, but **atypical leiomyomas** with cystic degeneration and torsion-related ischemic change.

## Why this case is clinically interesting

Pregnancy limits your diagnostic and therapeutic options. You can’t just reflexively send everyone to contrast CT or rush to surgery because the anatomy is weird and the stakes are high. In this patient, the team had to answer several questions at once:

- Is the dyspnea coming from a dangerous cardiopulmonary process?
- Are these adnexal masses, degenerating fibroids, or something malignant?
- Is conservative management still safe?
- At what point does symptom burden and mass effect justify surgery during pregnancy?

That combination — **symptoms, uncertainty, and restricted imaging** — is what makes this case worth learning from.

## Initial assessment: don’t anchor on pregnancy

She had normal oxygen saturation, normal ECG, and no hemodynamic instability. That matters. Dyspnea in pregnancy is common, but that doesn’t mean it should be dismissed. A structured assessment still applies:

- Vital signs and oxygenation
- Cardiopulmonary exam
- ECG
- Targeted evaluation for infection, anemia, thromboembolism, and heart failure when indicated

In this case, the exam was reassuring from a cardiac and pulmonary standpoint. The abdomen, however, was not. She had an enlarged abdomen for gestational age, a large tender upper abdominal mass, and a fixed pelvic mass on bimanual exam.

That bimanual exam is worth emphasizing. In pregnancy, it can still be a useful clue when imaging is equivocal. The pelvic mass was not just “seen on imaging” — it was **palpable, fixed, and clinically meaningful**.

## Dyspnea from mass effect: how it happens

The patient’s shortness of breath was attributed to **restrictive lung mechanics from compression** by the gravid uterus plus very large abdominal lesions. That’s a real physiologic problem:

- Reduced diaphragmatic excursion
- Decreased functional residual capacity
- Increased work of breathing
- Worse symptoms in supine position
- More pronounced discomfort with large upper abdominal masses

Her symptoms improved somewhat with semi-upright positioning and analgesia, which also supports a mechanical component.

A useful clinical pearl: if dyspnea improves with **positioning**, and the cardiopulmonary workup is unrevealing, think about **mass effect** rather than defaulting to an intrinsic lung or heart problem.

## The imaging trap: not every adnexal mass is an adnexal mass

Ultrasound showed two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity. The initial differential included decidualized endometriomas, adnexal neoplasm, and cystic degenerating leiomyomas.

This is where pregnancy-specific imaging nuance matters. Uterine fibroids can masquerade as adnexal masses, especially when they are:

- Pedunculated
- Large
- Degenerating/cystic
- Posterior or subserosal
- Distorted by the gravid uterus

The team appropriately escalated to **non-contrast MRI**, which is a key pregnancy-safe tool when ultrasound cannot define the anatomy. MRI suggested thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid, and a continuity with posterior uterine myoma for the caudal lesion. Even then, the diagnosis was not fully certain.

That uncertainty is the point: in pregnancy, the question is often not “what is the exact pathology?” but “is this likely benign enough and stable enough to watch?”

## CA-125: helpful? maybe. decisive? no.

CA-125 was mildly elevated and trending up. That understandably nudged the differential toward endometrioma or ovarian pathology, but this is exactly where clinicians can get fooled.

Important reminder:

- CA-125 can rise physiologically in pregnancy
- Mild elevations are nonspecific
- Trend matters more than a single isolated value
- It is **not** a stand-alone cancer test in pregnancy

In this case, the lab data did not solve the problem. It only added to the uncertainty.

## When does mass effect become an indication for intervention?

Usually, fibroids in pregnancy are managed conservatively. Surgery is avoided because of the risks of:

- Bleeding
- Pregnancy loss
- Preterm labor
- Uterine rupture

But conservative management has limits. This patient had several reasons to move toward surgery:

- Worsening pain
- Restrictive dyspnea
- Very large, vascularized masses
- Mixed solid/cystic features
- Concern for torsion, rupture, infection, or necrosis
- Concern that the masses would obstruct future uterine enlargement
- Uncertain diagnosis despite ultrasound and MRI

That’s the key FOAM take-home: **mass effect alone can be a legitimate maternal indication for procedural intervention** when symptoms are significant and conservative management is no longer acceptable.

## The operative decision: do the least necessary thing

At laparotomy, the cranial mass was found to be a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma that had twisted and was leaking, with ischemic necrosis near the pedicle — a very plausible explanation for her pain and fever.

The team removed that lesion and left the caudal mass in situ because it was fixed in the pelvis and would have increased obstetric risk if aggressively resected.

That is a very good surgical principle in pregnancy: **don’t chase completeness if the cost is obstetric harm**. Remove the lesion that is causing acute danger, and preserve the pregnancy when feasible.

The team also monitored fetal viability intraoperatively and documented fetal heart activity. That’s consistent with the broader principle that fetal monitoring should be planned around the procedure, not improvised after the fact.

## Outcome matters here

This case had an excellent ending:

- Pain and symptoms improved
- The patient was treated for anemia and infection
- Fetal growth remained appropriate on follow-up
- The residual pelvic myoma was left alone until delivery
- She ultimately underwent elective cesarean delivery because the remaining mass would have obstructed vaginal birth
- Mother and baby did well

This is a good example of staged obstetric surgical thinking:
1. Treat the acute symptomatic lesion
2. Stabilize and follow closely
3. Deliver when term is reached and anatomy demands it
4. Defer more definitive surgery until postpartum if symptoms allow

## Practical pearls from the case

- **Dyspnea in pregnancy is not always cardiopulmonary.** If vitals and exam are reassuring, look for mechanical causes.
- **Always examine the abdomen and pelvis carefully.** Bimanual exam can add important information.
- **Ultrasound first, MRI second.** Non-contrast MRI is a useful problem-solving tool in pregnancy.
- **CA-125 is not definitive in pregnancy.** Mild elevation may mislead more than it helps.
- **Fibroids can mimic adnexal masses.** Especially when large, degenerating, pedunculated, or posterior.
- **Surgery in pregnancy is reserved for symptoms, complications, or concerning features.** When you operate, aim for the shortest, safest, most pregnancy-preserving procedure.
- **Mass effect can justify intervention.** Severe pain and restrictive dyspnea are not trivial symptoms.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that a pregnant patient with dyspnea and abdominal pain may have a problem that is not pulmonary at all — it may be **space-occupying disease causing real mechanical compromise**. When the mass is large, symptomatic, and diagnostically uncertain, conservative management may no longer be the safest choice.

In other words: sometimes the lungs are fine. It’s the abdomen that’s doing the damage.

## Take-home points

- **Consider mass effect** in pregnant patients with dyspnea, especially when cardiopulmonary evaluation is unrevealing.
- **Non-contrast MRI** is a valuable adjunct when ultrasound cannot clarify the origin of a mass.
- **Mild CA-125 elevation is nonspecific in pregnancy** and should not drive management alone.
- **Operate for symptoms or complications, not just imaging.**
- **In pregnancy, the goal is not perfect resection — it’s the safest possible balance between maternal symptom control and fetal preservation.**



# Myomectomy During Pregnancy: How to Do Less, Safely

## When the right operation is the smaller operation

Pregnancy plus a huge abdominal mass is one of those scenarios that tempts everyone toward “fix everything while you’re there.” But this case is a nice reminder that in obstetric surgery, maximalism can be the enemy of good outcomes.

A woman in her second trimester presented with pain, dyspnea, and two large abdominal masses that looked, on ultrasound and MRI, a lot like complex adnexal disease. The imaging uncertainty, the worsening symptoms, and a mildly rising CA-125 created just enough concern to justify surgery. What made the case elegant was the intraoperative decision-making: the surgeons removed the torsed, leaking, highly vascular pedunculated fibroid, then intentionally left the fixed pelvic mass in situ.

That is the teaching point.

The safest pregnancy-preserving surgery is often the shortest, most targeted one.

## The pre-op problem: pregnancy makes mass interpretation messy

Pregnancy distorts anatomy, limits imaging choices, and makes benign disease look malignant. In this case:

- The upper mass was large, cystic, vascular, and symptomatic.
- The lower mass appeared fixed in the pelvis and was associated with a posterior uterine myoma.
- Ultrasound suggested decidualized endometriomas.
- MRI without contrast helped refine the anatomy but still left uncertainty.
- CA-125 was mildly elevated, which is not surprising in pregnancy and is not, by itself, a cancer signal.

This is where clinical restraint starts before the OR. You do not need a perfect diagnosis to justify surgery, but you do need a clear reason to operate. Here, the reasons were strong:

- worsening pain
- dyspnea from mass effect
- concern for torsion, rupture, infection, or necrosis
- need for a definitive diagnosis
- increasing difficulty accommodating a growing uterus around the masses

## Intraoperative principle: treat the dangerous lesion, not the whole scan

At laparotomy, the “cranial mass” turned out to be a huge pedunculated leiomyoma that was twisted, highly vascular, and leaking, with ischemic necrosis near the pedicle. That is a classic surgical problem: a symptomatic, mechanically dangerous lesion with a clear target.

So they did the right thing:
they removed the torsed pedunculated myoma, controlled the stalk, and minimized manipulation.

Then they stopped.

The caudal mass was fixed, apparently arising from the uterine torus/posterior uterus, and would have required a much larger dissection to remove. That second resection would have increased:

- bleeding risk
- uterine irritation
- operative time
- risk of pregnancy loss or preterm labor
- chance of scar-related complications later

Leaving it in place was not “incomplete surgery.” It was pregnancy-sparing surgery.

## Surgical restraint is not indecision

There’s a reflex in surgery to equate completeness with quality. Pregnancy is one of the few settings where “less” can be clearly better.

When deciding whether to remove a fibroid during pregnancy, the useful questions are:

- Is this lesion causing the complication?
- Is it torsed, ruptured, infected, or rapidly worsening?
- Can it be removed quickly and safely?
- Will removing additional masses materially improve maternal or fetal outcome?
- Or will it just add bleeding, uterine manipulation, and obstetric risk?

In this case, the upper lesion had a pedicle and clear complication potential. The lower lesion did not. That difference mattered.

## Minimize uterine manipulation and bleeding

The technical goals in pregnant myomectomy are simple to state and hard to execute:

- keep the operation short
- limit uterine handling
- preserve hemostasis
- avoid unnecessary dissection
- don’t chase asymptomatic disease

This case illustrates several practical themes:

### 1) Target the pedicle
A pedunculated fibroid with torsion is the most surgically “worth it” fibroid in pregnancy. It is a discrete problem with a discrete solution.

### 2) Avoid broad uterine dissection
The lower mass was fixed. Removing it would have required more uterine manipulation and likely a much larger defect. If the mass is not the source of acute compromise, deferring it is often the safer move.

### 3) Control the stalk cleanly
They used a double clamp, excised the mass, and closed the root with absorbable suture. The principle here is straightforward: secure hemostasis quickly and move on.

### 4) Accept that residual disease may be managed later
The remaining pelvic myoma was followed through pregnancy and ultimately influenced delivery planning. That is a better tradeoff than forcing a high-risk second resection.

## Don’t forget fetal assessment

One useful point from the case: fetal viability was checked intraoperatively and documented before, during, and after surgery.

That matters because pregnancy surgery is not just maternal surgery with an incidental fetus. Fetal status can change with:

- maternal hypoperfusion
- uterine irritation
- anesthesia
- bleeding
- prolonged operative time

You don’t need heroic fetal interventions in every case, but you do need a plan for surveillance and documentation. In this case, serial transvaginal ultrasound monitoring supported fetal viability during the procedure, and the pregnancy continued uneventfully afterward.

## Why the CA-125 did not drive the knife

CA-125 was mildly elevated and rising, which understandably contributed to concern. But this marker is notoriously nonspecific in pregnancy and benign gynecologic disease. The broader lesson is to avoid over-weighting a small lab abnormality when the imaging and clinical picture point toward a mechanically symptomatic fibroid process.

In other words: a mildly abnormal tumor marker should not force an oncologic operation if the anatomy and behavior are more consistent with benign disease.

## Delivery planning is part of the surgery story

The residual caudal mass remained large enough to obstruct vaginal delivery, so the team planned elective cesarean once the fetus reached term. That’s another good lesson: the operative decision in the second trimester affects the delivery plan months later.

You do not just ask, “Can I remove this mass now?”
You also ask, “What will this leave behind for the remainder of pregnancy and for delivery?”

## Take-home points

- In pregnancy, the best operation is often the one that solves the immediate problem and stops there.
- Pedunculated, torsed, leaking fibroids are reasonable targets for urgent myomectomy.
- Fixed posterior or intramural masses are often better left alone if removal would substantially increase uterine manipulation, bleeding, or obstetric risk.
- Mild CA-125 elevation in pregnancy should not override anatomy and clinical context.
- Use ultrasound and non-contrast MRI to map the lesion, but let the intraoperative situation guide how far you go.
- Always document fetal viability before, during, and after surgery when feasible.
- Pregnancy-preserving surgery should be short, targeted, and hemostatic.

## Bottom line

This case is a clean example of surgical restraint done well. The team removed the lesion that was twisted, leaking, and causing symptoms, while deliberately leaving the fixed pelvic mass alone. That choice likely preserved the pregnancy, minimized bleeding, and avoided unnecessary uterine trauma.

In obstetric surgery, “less” is not a compromise when it is the safer, smarter, more anatomically honest operation.



# The Fibroid That Determines the Delivery: Planning Birth Around Residual Pelvic Obstruction

## Why this case is worth your attention

Pregnancy and fibroids usually co-exist uneasily, but this case takes that tension to the next level: a patient in the second trimester presented with pain, dyspnea, and two large abdominal masses that turned out not to be ovarian tumors, but uterine leiomyomas with atypical cystic degeneration. One of them was removed. The other was intentionally left behind.

That decision matters, because the remaining caudal myoma was not just an incidental finding. It sat in the pelvis, fixed and bulky enough to create a likely mechanical barrier to vaginal delivery. In the end, it became the lesion that “decided” the mode of birth: elective cesarean at 37 weeks, with good maternal and neonatal outcomes.

This is a useful case for thinking about a bigger obstetric principle: in pregnant patients with fibroids, delivery planning is not just about gestational age or symptoms. It’s about anatomy.

## When a fibroid becomes a delivery problem

Most fibroids in pregnancy are managed conservatively. That’s the default for a reason: surgery carries real risks, including bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. But not all fibroids behave like quiet bystanders.

In this case, the residual pelvic mass was:
- Large
- Fixed in the lesser pelvis
- Located posteriorly, near the uterine torus / lower pelvic compartment
- Persistent after the first surgery

That combination is exactly what raises concern for obstructed labor. Even if the pregnancy continues uneventfully, a mass in the lower pelvis can interfere with descent and engagement of the fetal head. In practical terms, a fibroid like this can make vaginal birth unrealistic or unsafe.

The key is not simply “fibroid present” versus “fibroid absent.” It’s:
- Size
- Number
- Location
- Relationship to the cervix, lower uterine segment, and birth canal
- Whether the lesion distorts the pelvis enough to block the presenting part

## Anatomy beats optimism

A common temptation is to hope the pelvis will “make room” as pregnancy progresses. Sometimes it does. Fibroids can also regress postpartum, which is why definitive treatment is often deferred until after delivery. But regression after birth does not help you if the mass is already occupying the pelvis during labor.

That’s the crucial teaching point here: postpartum shrinkage may eventually simplify management, but it does not erase intrapartum obstruction.

This patient’s caudal myoma was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk during the first operation, which was reasonable because the more urgent problem was the symptomatic cranial mass. But once that residual pelvic fibroid was identified as a likely barrier to fetal head progression, the delivery plan had to change. The team appropriately planned an elective cesarean rather than forcing a trial of labor against pelvic anatomy.

## Imaging isn’t just about diagnosis — it’s about birth planning

This case also reinforces how useful ultrasound and non-contrast MRI are in pregnancy when the anatomy is unclear.

The upper lesion initially looked like a huge decidualized endometrioma, and the lower lesion seemed like an adnexal mass adherent to a posterior uterine myoma. MRI helped refine the differential and demonstrate the continuity between the caudal mass and uterine tissue. That matters, because once you recognize the lesion as a fibroid rather than an ovarian mass, the entire risk conversation changes.

For delivery planning, imaging should answer:
- Is the mass in the lower uterine segment or true pelvis?
- Does it distort the cervix or block the pelvic inlet/outlet?
- Is it pedunculated, intramural, subserosal, or fixed?
- Is there more than one lesion?
- What is the likely relationship to the fetal presenting part?

If the answer suggests persistent outlet obstruction, then cesarean should be planned early rather than discovered late in labor.

## Why leaving the caudal mass in place made sense

The operative team removed the cranial pedunculated fibroid because it was twisted, vascular, and likely responsible for symptoms and fever. The caudal mass was different: fixed, pelvic, and not the immediate source of trouble. Attempting to remove both during pregnancy would likely have increased bleeding and obstetric risk.

That is an important surgical principle in pregnancy: don’t chase every lesion. Remove what is dangerous or clearly driving symptoms, and avoid turning a manageable situation into a major obstetric emergency.

In this patient, the staged approach worked:
- First surgery addressed the acute symptomatic lesion
- Pregnancy continued with close follow-up
- Delivery was then planned around the remaining pelvic obstruction

That’s individualized care, not undertreatment.

## Timing of delivery: why 37 weeks was a smart finish line

Once the residual fibroid was recognized as a likely mechanical obstacle, the question became not whether to deliver vaginally, but when and how to deliver surgically.

Elective cesarean at 37 weeks balanced several competing issues:
- Avoid spontaneous labor and an obstructed vaginal delivery
- Avoid ongoing fibroid-related growth or degeneration
- Reduce the chance of urgent, unplanned surgery
- Allow fetal maturity to remain acceptable

This is the practical art of obstetric planning: if the pelvis is blocked, don’t wait for labor to tell you that the block is real.

## Postpartum regression: real, but not a reason to gamble

Fibroids often shrink after delivery, and this patient’s residual myoma did decrease somewhat postpartum. That’s clinically relevant, because postpartum regression can justify delayed definitive treatment if the patient is asymptomatic and wants to avoid another surgery.

But regression should not be used to justify a risky vaginal delivery in the presence of clear obstruction.

A useful way to think about it:
- Postpartum regression informs future management
- It does not change the immediate anatomy of labor

In this case, that meant:
- No rush to re-operate postpartum because symptoms improved and the mass regressed somewhat
- But still no attempt at vaginal birth, because the mass likely would have blocked descent

## Practical pearls for the bedside

When you see fibroids in pregnancy, don’t just ask “is it benign?” Ask, “Will this mass let the baby out?”

A few bedside principles stand out from this case:
- A fixed pelvic mass in pregnancy is not an incidental finding until proven otherwise.
- Lower uterine segment and posterior pelvic fibroids deserve special attention because they can obstruct labor.
- Ultrasound is first-line; non-contrast MRI is helpful when anatomy is unclear.
- Conservative management is usually preferred, but symptomatic, torsed, infected, or obstructive lesions may require surgery.
- If a residual fibroid is likely to block the pelvis, plan cesarean rather than waiting for labor to fail.

## Take-home points

- Fibroid management in pregnancy is not just about symptom control — it’s about anatomy and delivery feasibility.
- A residual lower pelvic or lower uterine segment myoma can be a true mechanical barrier to vaginal birth.
- Postpartum fibroid regression is real, but it should not override intrapartum obstruction risk.
- Ultrasound plus non-contrast MRI can help distinguish ovarian masses from fibroids and guide birth planning.
- In selected cases, a staged strategy — remove the dangerous lesion, leave the rest, and plan cesarean later — is the safest path for mother and fetus.
