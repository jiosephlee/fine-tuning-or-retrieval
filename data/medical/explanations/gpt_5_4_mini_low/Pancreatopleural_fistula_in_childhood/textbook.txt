Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Recognizing the Clinical Pattern: Recurrent Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion with a Hidden Abdominal Source

This child’s first presentation was dominated by respiratory complaints: right-sided chest pain, progressive dyspnea, tachypnea, and mild hypoxemia. On examination, the classic bedside signs of a large pleural effusion were present on the right side—reduced chest movement, stony dullness to percussion, decreased breath sounds, and diminished vocal fremitus. These findings point to fluid occupying the pleural space and compressing the underlying lung. In a child with respiratory distress, such a presentation naturally prompts urgent thoracic evaluation, which is why the initial clinical focus in this case was on stabilization and drainage rather than immediately on the pancreas.

The child was appropriately triaged to the PICU, given supplemental oxygen, and monitored closely. Thoracocentesis produced immediate symptomatic benefit: the tachypnea improved, oxygen saturation normalized, and chest pain eased after removal of a large volume of pleural fluid. This response confirms that the effusion itself was the proximate cause of the respiratory compromise. However, the fluid was not ordinary straw-colored effusion; it was repeatedly dark red, or blood-stained. In children, a hemorrhagic pleural effusion should broaden the differential diagnosis beyond infection alone and raise suspicion for conditions such as trauma, malignancy, pulmonary embolic phenomena, tuberculosis, esophageal injury, and, more rarely, pancreatic disease. The repeated appearance of dark red fluid was therefore an important warning sign that the underlying process had not yet been identified.

A key teaching point in this case is that recurrent large unilateral pleural effusions should prompt clinicians to step back and reconsider the diagnosis rather than simply repeating drainage. The child improved transiently after aspiration, but the effusion recurred within days, and then again after another short interval. Such recurrence suggests an ongoing source of fluid production or leakage rather than a self-limited pleural process. In pancreatopleural fistula, pancreatic secretions escape from the pancreatic duct or a pancreatic collection and track into the thorax, causing persistent or recurrent effusion. Because the thoracic manifestations dominate, the true abdominal source is often missed at first. This is the “pulmonary mask” of pancreatopleural fistula: the child appears to have a primary chest problem, but the pathology lies in the pancreas.

The history in this case contained several clues that became important only after repeated questioning. The delayed disclosure of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle was a major turning point, because pancreatic injury is a recognized cause of duct disruption and fistula formation. The family history of tuberculosis contact and the mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA initially made pleural tuberculosis seem plausible, especially in a child with recurrent unilateral effusion. That possibility explains why empirical antitubercular treatment was started early, but the overall picture was not typical: there was no chronic cough, fever, weight loss, or other systemic evidence of tuberculosis. The child’s obesity and highly processed diet are not direct causes of this fistula, but they are relevant to the broader clinical context because they can distract attention from trauma history and may influence how seriously abdominal symptoms are initially interpreted. Most importantly, this case shows the value of repeated history-taking. Children may not report trauma clearly the first time, and a detail such as a bicycle handle injury can remain hidden unless the clinician revisits the history after the pattern of recurrent effusion becomes apparent.

Together, the recurrent unilateral hemorrhagic pleural effusion, relief after drainage, and eventual recognition of abdominal trauma created the clinical pattern that should raise suspicion for pancreatopleural fistula. The lesson is that in a child with repeated pleural effusions, especially when the fluid is blood-stained and the course is atypical for common pulmonary causes, the diagnostic frame must shift from chest to abdomen.

## Core Pathophysiology and Pediatric Background of Pancreatopleural Fistula

Pancreatopleural fistula is an internal pancreatic fistula in which pancreatic secretions escape from the ductal system or a pancreatic collection and track into the pleural space. It is a rare complication in children because the underlying pancreatic diseases that lead to major duct disruption are themselves uncommon in pediatrics. Unlike adults, where chronic alcoholic pancreatitis dominates, pediatric cases usually arise from pancreatitis related to genetic predisposition, ductal anomalies, or trauma. This rarity is one reason the diagnosis is often delayed: recurrent pleural effusion usually triggers a chest-focused evaluation before the pancreas is considered.

Understanding the anatomy explains how a pancreatic problem can present as a thoracic one. The pancreas is largely retroperitoneal, and its posterior aspect lies close to the retroperitoneal tissues and the diaphragm. When the pancreatic duct is disrupted, pancreatic juice may leak posteriorly into the retroperitoneum rather than anteriorly into the peritoneal cavity. From there, the fluid follows the path of least resistance upward and can pass through natural openings in the diaphragm, especially the oesophageal hiatus and less commonly the aortic hiatus, to reach the mediastinum and pleural cavity. In some cases, the leak begins as a pancreatic pseudocyst or a localized collection that later ruptures or erodes into adjacent structures, creating a persistent fistulous tract.

The mechanism in children is often initiated by pancreatitis or trauma. Blunt abdominal trauma, including handlebar injuries, can injure the pancreatic parenchyma or duct and produce delayed ductal leakage. Because the pancreas is fixed against the spine, compression injuries are especially relevant in children and may be missed if the abdominal pain is mild or transient. Once a duct disruption occurs, pancreatic enzymes continue to leak, causing inflammation, tissue breakdown, and formation of a tract toward the thorax. This explains why pleural effusions can recur quickly after aspiration unless the pancreatic source is controlled.

Pleural manifestations may dominate because the leak is effectively a “one-way drain” from the pancreas to the chest. The pleural cavity can accommodate large volumes of fluid, so the child may present with dyspnea, chest pain, tachypnea, or hypoxemia, while abdominal findings remain minimal. Abdominal pain, vomiting, or tenderness may be absent, particularly if the pancreatic injury is subacute or if the child does not volunteer the trauma history. In this case, the recurrent right-sided effusion and respiratory distress were the main symptoms, which is typical of pancreatopleural fistula and explains why the initial workup focused on thoracic causes.

The pleural fluid in this condition is usually large volume, recurrent, and rich in pancreatic enzymes. Amylase and lipase leak directly into the pleural space, making the effusion “amylase-rich.” A very high pleural fluid amylase level strongly supports pancreatopleural fistula when combined with the right clinical setting. The effusion is often hemorrhagic or dark colored because of inflammatory injury to surrounding tissues and associated blood contamination. Reaccumulation after thoracocentesis is common because aspiration removes the fluid but does not stop the ongoing pancreatic leak.

In children, the etiologic spectrum includes acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, hereditary pancreatitis, ductal abnormalities, and trauma. Recurrent or chronic pancreatitis should raise concern for an underlying genetic cause, particularly when there is no obvious acquired trigger. Important genes include PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC, which are associated with hereditary or recurrent pancreatitis and may predispose to ductal injury and pseudocyst formation. PRSS1 mutations are especially important in hereditary pancreatitis, while SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC variants can act as susceptibility factors or disease modifiers. In a child with recurrent pancreatitis or unexplained pancreatic duct disruption, these genetic causes become part of the etiologic framework.

For the case in question, blunt abdominal trauma was the likely non-hereditary trigger that led to pancreatic duct disruption and fistula formation. The child’s recurrent pleural effusions reflect the physiology of persistent pancreatic leakage into the thorax rather than a primary pulmonary disorder. This is the key concept: pancreatopleural fistula is a pancreatic disease with respiratory presentation. Recognizing that relationship is essential to avoid repeated pleural drainage alone and to direct attention toward the underlying pancreatic source.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning: From Pleural Effusion to Pancreatic Fistula

This case began as a common respiratory problem: a child with dyspnea, chest pain, hypoxemia, and a unilateral pleural effusion. The first diagnostic steps were therefore appropriately thoracic. Chest radiography confirmed a right pleural effusion, and bedside ultrasound showed a large fluid collection, helping quantify the effusion and support urgent drainage. In a child with respiratory distress, these bedside studies are valuable because they rapidly establish the presence of pleural fluid and guide thoracocentesis, which can both relieve symptoms and provide diagnostic material.

Repeated thoracocentesis was initially useful for stabilization, but the recurrence of a large effusion after temporary improvement signaled that the underlying cause had not been addressed. This is an important clinical point: when a pleural effusion repeatedly reaccumulates, clinicians must move beyond symptomatic drainage and search for the source of fluid production. In this case, each aspirate was dark red, which broadened the differential and raised concern for hemorrhagic or inflammatory causes rather than a simple transudate.

Pleural fluid analysis is central in hemorrhagic effusions because it helps separate infection, malignancy, tuberculosis, pancreatic disease, and other less common causes. The child’s pleural fluid had only a mildly elevated ADA, which can suggest tuberculosis in the right clinical setting, but ADA is not specific and should never be interpreted in isolation. Cytology did not show malignant cells, and microbiologic testing did not support tuberculosis. These negative or nonspecific results illustrate a common pitfall: a single borderline abnormality can distract clinicians from a more unusual diagnosis if it is not integrated with the overall picture.

The key biochemical clue was the markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase and lipase. Very high pleural fluid amylase is strongly suggestive of a pancreatic source, especially when levels are in the thousands and the effusion is recurrent. Serum amylase and lipase may also be elevated, but they are supportive rather than definitive because they can reflect pancreatic inflammation without proving a fistulous communication to the chest. In pleural effusions with amylase elevation, the main competing considerations are pancreatic disease, esophageal perforation, and some malignancies; in this case, the enzyme profile, fluid appearance, and imaging favored a pancreatic origin.

Cross-sectional imaging was decisive. CT of the chest demonstrated right hydropneumothorax, patchy consolidation, and, most importantly, an elongated multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the pleural region through the retroesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. This pattern suggested that the thoracic process was actually the end result of a retroperitoneal pancreatic leak tracking upward. CT is often the first advanced imaging study because it is widely available and can identify associated findings such as pseudocysts, collections, ductal dilation, or complications in the chest and abdomen. However, CT may not always show the fistulous tract itself clearly.

MRCP then provided the clearest noninvasive confirmation. It demonstrated pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreas extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a smaller communication to the left pleural space. It also showed continuity with the pancreatic duct and narrowing proximal to the communication. This ductal mapping is exactly where MRCP excels: it can show the pancreatic ductal anatomy and fistulous extensions without instrumentation. In suspected pancreatopleural fistula, MRCP is often the best noninvasive test to confirm the diagnosis and guide next steps.

ERCP remains the gold standard when endoscopic treatment is being considered, because it can directly define ductal anatomy and allow stent placement. In this case, MRCP was sufficient to establish the diagnosis, so ERCP was not required. That sequence is typical: CT may raise suspicion, MRCP confirms the fistulous pathway noninvasively, and ERCP is reserved mainly for therapeutic planning or when intervention is needed.

The diagnostic reasoning in this child ultimately depended on putting several clues together. Recurrent large unilateral effusions, dark red pleural fluid, a history of blunt abdominal trauma, very high pleural fluid pancreatic enzymes, and imaging showing a collection tracking from the pancreas to the pleura all pointed to pancreatopleural fistula. The earlier TB-oriented workup was understandable because the effusion was recurrent and hemorrhagic with a mildly elevated ADA, but the absence of constitutional symptoms, the negative microbiology, and the striking enzyme elevation made a pancreatic source far more likely. In children, this case is a useful reminder that respiratory symptoms may be the presenting mask of abdominal pancreatic injury, and that pleural fluid amylase typing can be helpful when the source is uncertain, because pancreatic amylase supports PPF while salivary-type amylase raises alternative diagnoses such as esophageal perforation or other nonpancreatic causes.

## Differential Diagnosis of Amylase-Rich or Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion in Children

A child with a recurrent, blood-stained pleural effusion immediately raises concern for a thoracic disease, but this case illustrates why the differential must remain broad. The key bedside clue is that pleural fluid was repeatedly dark red and re-accumulated quickly after aspiration. In any child with recurrent pleural fluid, clinicians should think not only about infection and malignancy, but also about unusual causes such as esophageal perforation, diaphragmatic defects, abdominal trauma, and pancreatic disease. In this case, the eventual diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula was delayed because the presentation initially looked entirely “chest-based.”

Tuberculous pleural effusion was a reasonable early consideration because the child had a history of contact with pulmonary tuberculosis and the pleural fluid ADA was mildly elevated. However, ADA is not specific for tuberculosis; mild elevations can occur in other inflammatory effusions. A negative GeneXpert for Mycobacterium tuberculosis argued against TB in this setting, and the clinical picture was also not typical: there was no fever, chronic cough, weight loss, or other constitutional symptoms. In a child with a blood-stained recurrent effusion, a contact history should prompt testing, but it should not override the rest of the data when the overall syndrome does not fit tuberculosis.

Empyema and parapneumonic effusion were also less likely. These usually present with fever, a toxic appearance, raised inflammatory markers, and pleural fluid that is purulent or neutrophil-rich. This child did not have fever, and the pleural fluid description did not suggest frank pus. Although chest radiography showed lung opacity, that can occur from compressive collapse or adjacent inflammation and does not by itself establish bacterial pleural infection. The repeated recurrence after thoracentesis without an infectious clinical syndrome should prompt reconsideration of a noninfectious source.

Malignancy is another important cause of pleural effusion in children, especially leukemia, lymphoma, lung tumors, or metastatic disease. Hemorrhagic pleural fluid can be seen with malignancy, and amylase-rich pleural effusions may occasionally occur in tumors as well. In this case, malignancy became less likely because the child had no weight loss, no persistent fever, and no abnormal blood picture or peripheral smear findings. Pleural fluid cytology also did not show malignant cells. While cytology cannot exclude all malignancy, the combination of normal hematologic assessment and the later pancreatic imaging made a neoplastic cause very unlikely.

Boerhaave syndrome, or esophageal perforation, is a classic cause of amylase-rich pleural fluid and must be considered when pleural effusion is accompanied by chest pain and respiratory distress. The pleural fluid amylase in esophageal perforation typically reflects salivary-type amylase from swallowed contents. However, this child had no history of forceful vomiting or acute mediastinal catastrophe, and the clinical course was subacute and recurrent rather than fulminant. The eventual imaging showed a tract from the pancreas into the pleural space, which is not compatible with primary esophageal rupture.

Structural thoracoabdominal causes were also considered. An acquired diaphragmatic hernia can mimic pleural disease, but imaging would usually show abdominal viscera within the chest or a visible diaphragmatic defect. That was not seen here. Similarly, hepatic hydrothorax is usually associated with chronic liver disease and right-sided pleural effusion due to transdiaphragmatic movement of ascitic fluid. Normal liver function tests, normal liver imaging, and absence of chronic liver disease made this diagnosis implausible. Renal and retroperitoneal causes can produce pleural collections, but normal renal imaging and renal function tests helped exclude these possibilities.

The pleural fluid chemistry was the pivotal clue. Markedly elevated amylase and lipase strongly suggested a pancreatic source. In practice, it is useful to remember that not all amylase-rich effusions are pancreatic: salivary-type amylase is more typical of esophageal rupture, malignancy, or some other thoracic processes, whereas pancreatic-type amylase points toward pancreatic leakage or fistulation. In this case, the very high pleural fluid amylase and lipase, together with the abdominal trauma history and MRCP findings, fit pancreatopleural fistula far better than any primary chest pathology.

Several red flags should push clinicians toward pancreatopleural fistula when faced with a “pulmonary” presentation. These include recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, rapid reaccumulation after drainage, blood-stained fluid, elevated pleural fluid amylase, a history of pancreatitis or trauma, and imaging that shows a retroperitoneal or mediastinal tract. The key lesson from this case is that respiratory symptoms can be the presenting feature of a pancreatic injury, so repeated pleural effusions without a clear pulmonary explanation should trigger a search for an abdominal source.

This case also highlights how cognitive bias can delay diagnosis. Anchoring on tuberculosis was understandable because of the contact history and mildly elevated ADA, but it diverted attention from the more important clues: recurrent hemorrhagic effusion, abdominal trauma, and the striking imaging pattern. In rare pediatric presentations, repeated bedside history-taking and a willingness to revisit the differential are essential. When the clinical course does not fit the initial diagnosis, the safest approach is to step back and ask whether a common explanation is being overused to fit an uncommon disease.

## Management Strategy, Outcome, and Clinical Lessons for Practice

Management of pancreatopleural fistula has two immediate goals: stabilize the child’s breathing and reduce further pancreatic leakage so the fistulous tract can close. In this case, the child initially required oxygen support because of hypoxemia and respiratory distress from a large recurrent pleural effusion. Therapeutic thoracocentesis provided rapid symptom relief by decompressing the pleural space, and when the effusion recurred repeatedly, an intercostal drain was placed to allow continuous drainage. Chest tube placement is appropriate when effusions are large, symptomatic, recurrent, or causing respiratory compromise, especially when repeated aspirations are no longer sufficient. In a child with persistent hydropneumothorax or a large ongoing collection, drainage also helps monitor the volume and character of pleural output while the underlying pancreatic source is treated.

Once pancreatopleural fistula was recognized, the team shifted to a conservative “pancreatic rest” strategy. The child was kept nil per oral and started on total parenteral nutrition to minimize pancreatic stimulation from enteral feeding. This approach is intended to reduce exocrine pancreatic secretion, lower pressure in the ductal system, and give the fistulous tract a chance to seal. Octreotide, a somatostatin analogue, was added because it suppresses pancreatic exocrine secretion and gastrointestinal hormone release, further decreasing pancreatic fluid output. Although pediatric dosing is not standardized, it is generally used in weight-based regimens, as in this report, with close monitoring for response and adverse effects. Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy was also given as supportive care, mainly to aid digestion while the child was not feeding normally and to reduce pancreatic stimulation during recovery.

Response to treatment is followed clinically and objectively. In this child, the key indicators were a steady fall in chest tube output, improvement in respiratory rate and oxygenation, and eventual cessation of drainage. Imaging can be repeated if the clinical course is unclear or if output remains high, but in a favorable response, decreasing pleural drainage and improved respiratory status are usually enough to guide ongoing management. Biochemical markers such as pleural fluid amylase and lipase are most useful for diagnosis rather than serial monitoring, but they help confirm that the effusion is pancreatic in origin. In practice, chest tube removal is considered once drainage has markedly decreased or stopped, the lung has re-expanded adequately, and the child is clinically stable without recurrent respiratory symptoms. In this case, the drain output progressively decreased over about 10 days, after which the tube was removed safely.

An important part of management is stopping empiric therapies once the diagnosis is clarified. This child was initially started on antitubercular treatment because of recurrent hemorrhagic effusion, mildly elevated ADA, and a history of tuberculosis contact. Once MRCP and pleural fluid amylase supported pancreatopleural fistula, tuberculosis was no longer the leading diagnosis, and antitubercular therapy was discontinued. This is an important clinical principle: treatment should be reassessed promptly when new evidence identifies a more accurate cause, so that children are not exposed to unnecessary medications and delays in definitive care are avoided.

If conservative therapy does not succeed, escalation to endoscopic or surgical treatment is considered. ERCP with transpapillary stenting is typically the next step when there is persistent fistula output, a demonstrable ductal leak, or ductal obstruction that can be bridged endoscopically. Stenting reduces intraductal pressure and diverts pancreatic juice into the duodenum, which can facilitate fistula closure. Surgery is reserved for cases with failed conservative and endoscopic treatment, inaccessible duct anatomy, severe duct obstruction, or a distal fistula that is not amenable to stenting. Operations may include distal pancreatectomy, pancreaticojejunostomy, or cystojejunostomy depending on anatomy and the underlying pancreatic lesion.

The outcome in this child was favorable with conservative management alone. After drainage, bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide, and supportive care, pleural output ceased over 10 days, the chest tube was removed, and the child was discharged without recurrence during follow-up. This course is consistent with the fact that some pediatric fistulas, particularly those identified early and associated with a potentially reversible traumatic insult, may close without invasive pancreatic intervention.

Long-term follow-up is still important because pancreatopleural fistula may reflect an underlying tendency to recurrent pancreatitis or ductal abnormality, especially if symptoms recur. Follow-up should include surveillance for recurrent pleural effusion, recurrent abdominal pain, or pancreatitis. In children with repeated episodes, clinicians should revisit the history for additional trauma or pancreatitis triggers, counsel the family on injury prevention, and consider genetic evaluation for hereditary pancreatitis if the pattern suggests recurrent or chronic pancreatic disease. The major lesson from this case is that recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion in a child should prompt repeated history-taking and early consideration of an abdominal source, particularly when pleural fluid amylase is markedly elevated. MRCP is especially useful early in unexplained recurrent effusions because it can demonstrate the fistulous tract and guide appropriate management before unnecessary thoracic or anti-infective treatments are prolonged.
