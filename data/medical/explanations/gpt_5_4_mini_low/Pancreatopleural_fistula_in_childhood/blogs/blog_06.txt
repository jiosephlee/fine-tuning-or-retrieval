Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# TB, ADA, and Anchoring Bias: Lessons from a Recurrent Effusion That Wasn’t Tuberculosis

## The setup: a “TB-looking” effusion that kept coming back

Recurrent pleural effusion in a child, in a TB-endemic setting, with a family contact and a mildly elevated pleural ADA? That’s a recipe for anchoring bias.

This case is a great reminder that when a diagnosis feels familiar, it can also become dangerously sticky. A late-childhood boy presented with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain, and respiratory distress. The pleural fluid was repeatedly dark red. A history of Koch’s contact and a slightly elevated ADA pushed the team toward empiric antitubercular therapy.

But the effusion recurred again. And again.

The eventual diagnosis was not tuberculosis at all, but a pancreatopleural fistula after blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar injury.

That’s the teaching pearl: **when the same effusion keeps returning, the first diagnosis deserves a second look.**

## Why this case matters

This case sits at the intersection of three common cognitive traps:

1. **TB prevalence bias**  
   In high-prevalence settings, it is easy to overcall TB when the pleural fluid is exudative, ADA is mildly elevated, and there is a contact history.

2. **Premature closure**  
   Once TB became the working diagnosis, the team had a reason to stop searching—until the patient re-presented.

3. **Availability bias from “classic” pleural effusion workups**  
   Recurrent unilateral effusion naturally triggers a thoracic differential. But the pathology here was pancreatic, not pulmonary.

## ADA: useful, but easy to misuse

Pleural ADA is often used as a surrogate marker for tuberculous pleuritis, but it is not TB-specific.

In this case, the ADA was only mildly elevated, yet that was enough to reinforce the TB hypothesis. That’s the problem: **a test that supports TB is not the same as a test that proves TB**.

A few practical reminders:

- ADA is best interpreted in context, not in isolation.
- Mild elevation should not override a discordant clinical picture.
- Hemorrhagic or dark red pleural fluid should widen the differential, not narrow it.
- If the effusion is recurrent and the story doesn’t fit neatly, pause before committing to a chronic infectious diagnosis.

In this child, the pleural fluid was dark red on repeated taps—an important clue that should keep the differential broad.

## Recurrent hemorrhagic effusion: think beyond the chest

The key pivot came from careful re-history. Only after repeated admissions did the child disclose blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle about a month earlier.

That one detail unlocked the diagnosis.

CT chest showed right hydropneumothorax and a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the paraesophageal region toward the tail of the pancreas. MRCP then demonstrated pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection extending from the distal pancreas into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow communication to the left pleural cavity as well.

Pleural fluid amylase was massively elevated at 6118 U/L, with similarly high lipase. At that point, the diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula was no longer subtle.

## Pancreatopleural fistula: the “pulmonary mask” diagnosis

PPF is rare in children, but when it happens, it loves to masquerade as a thoracic process.

The mechanism is straightforward enough: pancreatic duct disruption or a pseudocyst leaks pancreatic fluid into the retroperitoneum, then tracks through natural pathways like the esophageal or aortic hiatus into the pleural cavity.

Clinically, that can present as:

- recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions
- dyspnea or respiratory distress
- chest pain
- blood-stained pleural fluid

Abdominal symptoms may be minimal or absent, which is why the diagnosis is so often delayed.

This case is a classic example of a disease with a **pulmonary mask**.

## How to avoid the TB trap in recurrent effusion

The point is not that TB should be ignored. It absolutely belongs in the differential in the right setting. The point is that TB should not become a reflex diagnosis when the data are incomplete or discordant.

A practical approach:

### 1) Reassess the story each time the patient returns
If a patient keeps re-presenting with the same effusion, ask:

- What diagnosis best explains the recurrence?
- What finding doesn’t fit?
- Did we anchor on a convenient explanation too early?

### 2) Let the fluid characteristics guide you
Dark red or hemorrhagic pleural fluid should prompt broader thinking, including:

- trauma-related processes
- malignancy
- esophageal perforation
- pancreatic disease
- pulmonary embolic processes in older patients

### 3) Use ADA as one data point, not the final answer
A mildly elevated ADA should not end the workup when:

- the fluid is frankly hemorrhagic
- imaging is atypical
- there is a trauma history
- the effusion recurs despite treatment

### 4) Escalate to anatomy when physiology is unexplained
When recurrent thoracic fluid collections don’t behave like infection, get imaging that can identify a source.

In this case, CT and then MRCP were decisive.

### 5) Search for “hidden” abdominal history
Children may underreport trauma, especially if the injury seems minor, embarrassing, or forgotten. A gentle second history can matter more than the first one.

## Diagnostic pearls from this case

The workup ultimately hinged on a few high-yield findings:

- **Recurrent pleural effusion despite aspiration**
- **Dark red pleural fluid**
- **Recent blunt abdominal trauma**
- **Very high pleural fluid amylase and lipase**
- **CT/MRCP evidence of pancreatic-to-pleural communication**

Pleural fluid amylase is a major clue here. In the right context, markedly elevated amylase should push PPF high on the list.

## Management: drain the chest, rest the pancreas

Once the diagnosis was confirmed, the team stopped antitubercular therapy and treated conservatively:

- right intercostal drain for pleural drainage
- nil per oral
- total parenteral nutrition
- octreotide infusion
- pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy

Over 10 days, the drainage decreased and stopped, the chest tube came out, and the child was discharged symptom-free.

That’s another useful point: **in pediatric PPF, conservative therapy can work, but only after the diagnosis is actually made.**

## Take-home points

- A recurrent pleural effusion is not a diagnosis; it’s a prompt to keep thinking.
- Mild pleural ADA elevation does not equal tuberculosis.
- Hemorrhagic or dark red pleural fluid should broaden the differential, especially if the effusion keeps returning.
- Always re-check for trauma, abdominal pain, or subtle pancreatic clues in “thoracic” disease.
- In suspected PPF, pleural fluid amylase/lipase and MRCP are key diagnostic tools.
- Anchoring bias is especially dangerous in high-TB settings, where empiric treatment can feel reassuring before it is justified.

## Final thought

This child did have a good outcome—but only after the diagnosis shifted from a familiar infection to an uncommon fistula.

That’s the real lesson: **when a patient keeps coming back with the same problem, the diagnosis may be the thing that’s stuck, not the disease.**
