Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion in a Child: Don’t Forget to Ask About Trauma

## Why this case matters

A child with recurrent, blood-stained pleural effusion is already a diagnostic headache. Add a mildly elevated pleural ADA, a TB contact history, and respiratory distress, and it becomes very easy to anchor on tuberculosis or another primary thoracic process.

But the surprising turn in this case was simple and powerful: the problem was not in the chest at all. It was pancreatic. And the key that unlocked the diagnosis was a repeated history, eventually revealing blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle about a month earlier.

That’s the teaching point here: when the clinical course doesn’t fit, go back and ask again.

## The presentation that misleads

This boy presented with right-sided chest pain and progressive dyspnea. Exam and imaging showed a large right pleural effusion. Thoracocentesis gave temporary relief, but the effusion recurred twice within days.

A few features should make you pause:

- The pleural fluid was dark red, not just serous or purulent.
- The effusion recurred quickly after drainage.
- There was no convincing infectious syndrome.
- Initial workup pointed away from a straightforward pulmonary cause.

It is very easy in a child like this to keep working up the thorax and miss the abdomen entirely.

## The clue hidden in the history

Only after repeated history-taking did the child disclose blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier.

That single detail completely reframed the differential.

Children often underreport trauma, especially if:

- the injury seemed minor at the time,
- symptoms were delayed,
- they fear getting in trouble,
- or they simply don’t connect a remote abdominal hit with current chest symptoms.

If the story doesn’t make sense, ask again. Then ask a third time, in a different way.

## When the pancreas presents like a lung problem

This case was ultimately diagnosed as a pancreatopleural fistula, a rare complication of pancreatic duct disruption that allows pancreatic fluid to track into the pleural space.

The mechanism is worth remembering:

- pancreatic fluid escapes from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst,
- tracks through the retroperitoneum,
- passes via the esophageal or aortic hiatus,
- and reaches the pleural cavity.

In children, this may occur after blunt pancreatic trauma, though hereditary or chronic pancreatitis can also be involved.

What makes it tricky is that the presenting complaint is often respiratory:
dyspnea, chest pain, pleural effusion, even hydropneumothorax.

So the child comes to you looking like a pulmonary patient, but the disease is upstream in the abdomen.

## How the diagnosis was made

In this case, the path to diagnosis was a combination of imaging and pleural fluid chemistry.

CT showed:

- right-sided hydropneumothorax,
- patchy right lung consolidation,
- and a multiloculated collection tracking from the paraesophageal region toward the pancreatic tail.

MRCP then confirmed the fistulous communication between the pancreatic duct/collection and the pleural cavity.

The pleural fluid was the biochemical giveaway:

- amylase 6118 U/L
- lipase 7211 U/L

That’s the kind of pleural fluid that should make you think “pancreatic source” immediately.

### Clinical pearl
Pleural fluid amylase is not specific for PPF by itself, but a very high amylase in recurrent pleural effusion should trigger a search for:

- pancreatic disease,
- esophageal perforation,
- malignancy,
- and tuberculosis only if the rest of the picture supports it.

## Why the first diagnosis was so tempting

This child had a positive TB contact history and a mildly elevated ADA, so antitubercular therapy was started empirically.

That’s understandable, but the course didn’t fit. Recurrent large hemorrhagic effusions, rapid reaccumulation after drainage, and then the delayed trauma history all pointed elsewhere.

This is the danger of partial fits:
a small amount of supporting data can seduce us into a diagnosis that doesn’t explain the whole story.

## Management: give the pancreas a chance to rest

Once PPF was identified, the team stopped ATT and moved to conservative pancreatic rest:

- nil per oral,
- total parenteral nutrition,
- octreotide infusion,
- pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy,
- and pleural drainage via intercostal drain.

The output gradually decreased over about 10 days, and the chest tube was removed.

That’s a good reminder that even rare fistulas can close with conservative therapy if the patient stabilizes and the pancreatic leak settles.

## Take-home lessons

- Recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion in a child should not be assumed to be pulmonary.
- Repeated history-taking is not redundancy; it is often the diagnostic test.
- Ask specifically about abdominal trauma, even if it seems remote or “minor.”
- A very high pleural fluid amylase should prompt evaluation for pancreatic pathology.
- MRCP is extremely helpful when a pancreaticopleural fistula is suspected.
- When the diagnosis is unclear, avoid anchoring on TB, infection, or malignancy too early.

## Bottom line

A bicycle handle to the abdomen can show up weeks later as respiratory distress.

If a child has recurrent pleural effusions, especially blood-stained ones, and the story is not quite adding up, go back to the bedside and re-interrogate the history. The chest may be where the child lands, but the problem may be in the pancreas.
