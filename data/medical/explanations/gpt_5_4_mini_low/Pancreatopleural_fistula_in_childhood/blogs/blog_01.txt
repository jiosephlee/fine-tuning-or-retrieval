Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# The Pulmonary Mask of Pancreatitis: When Recurrent Pleural Effusions Aren’t a Chest Problem

## Why this case matters

A child comes in with dyspnea, chest pain, and a large pleural effusion. The chest X-ray is abnormal, the pleural fluid is blood-stained, and the immediate temptation is to stay focused on the thorax: pneumonia, TB, malignancy, empyema, maybe trauma. But in this case, the lung was not the primary problem at all.

The real pathology was a **pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)** — an internal leak from the pancreatic duct system into the pleural space. That’s a rare diagnosis in adults, and even rarer in children. Yet when it happens, it often presents as a respiratory problem first, which is exactly why it gets missed.

This case is a good reminder that **recurrent, large, rapidly reaccumulating pleural effusions should trigger a broader differential**, especially when the fluid is dark red and the workup isn’t fitting a primary pulmonary process.

## The clinical trap: when the chest is just the symptom, not the source

This child presented with:

- Progressive dyspnea and right-sided chest pain
- Hypoxemia
- Large right pleural effusion on imaging
- Reaccumulation after thoracentesis
- Dark red pleural fluid

The early workup understandably leaned toward a pleural or pulmonary process. TB was considered because of contact history and a mildly elevated ADA. Empiric antitubercular therapy was started. But the effusion kept coming back.

That pattern should make you pause.

A pleural effusion that:

- **recurs quickly**
- **is large-volume**
- **is blood-stained or dark red**
- **doesn’t behave like infection**
- **doesn’t make sense as malignancy or TB**

…should make you think outside the chest.

## What is a pancreatopleural fistula?

A PPF is an abnormal communication between the pancreatic ductal system and the pleural cavity. Pancreatic secretions track through the retroperitoneum, often via the **esophageal hiatus** or **aortic hiatus**, and then enter the pleural space.

In children, the most common underlying triggers are:

- **Pancreatic trauma**
- **Acute pancreatitis**
- **Chronic pancreatitis**, often hereditary

In this case, the key clue was the delayed history of **blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle** one month earlier.

That’s a classic diagnostic theme in pediatric medicine: the eventual diagnosis lives in a history detail that was easy to miss the first time.

## What should make you think of PPF?

The biggest clues are clinical pattern recognition.

Consider PPF when you see:

- **Recurrent massive pleural effusion**
- **Blood-stained or dark pleural fluid**
- **Rapid reaccumulation after drainage**
- **Respiratory symptoms out of proportion to pulmonary findings**
- **History of pancreatitis or abdominal trauma**
- **Amylase-rich pleural fluid**

The pleural fluid amylase in this case was markedly elevated, and that is one of the most important diagnostic pivots. In the right context, **pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is highly suggestive of PPF**.

## Why this gets missed

PPF is a classic example of diagnostic anchoring.

Once a patient presents with pleural effusion, the evaluation often stays in the chest:

- chest X-ray
- chest ultrasound
- thoracentesis
- antibiotics
- TB testing
- malignancy evaluation

That makes sense initially. But if the effusion keeps returning, you have to ask whether the pleural space is the destination rather than the origin.

This case also illustrates how a **TB-contact history and mildly elevated ADA** can distract from the true diagnosis. Not every lymphocyte-leaning, recurrent pleural effusion is TB, and not every bloody pleural effusion is cancer.

## Imaging: CT helps, but MRCP shows the road map

Once the abdominal trauma history emerged, CT and MRCP became the key studies.

### CT
CT showed:

- Right patchy consolidation
- Right hydropneumothorax
- A multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas

CT is useful because it can show:
- pseudocysts
- ductal abnormalities
- collections
- indirect evidence of a fistula

But CT may still miss the actual fistulous tract.

### MRCP
MRCP confirmed:

- pancreatitis
- distal pancreatic collection
- extension through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity
- a narrow communication with the left pleural cavity

This is the imaging study that really ties the anatomy together. In suspected PPF, MRCP is often the best noninvasive test to map the duct and the fistula.

## Differential diagnosis: what else causes amylase-rich pleural fluid?

An elevated pleural fluid amylase is a useful clue, but it is not specific.

The differential includes:

- **Pancreatopleural fistula**
- **Esophageal perforation**
- **Malignancy**
- **Tuberculosis** and other inflammatory causes

In this case, the team considered and worked through several alternatives:
- TB was less likely with negative GeneXpert and lack of supportive clinical features
- Empyema was unlikely without fever or purulent neutrophilic fluid
- Malignancy was less likely with normal blood picture, normal smear, and negative cytology
- Boerhaave’s syndrome was not supported by the history
- Diaphragmatic hernia and hepatobiliary/renal causes were not supported on imaging and labs

The key point is not that every alternate diagnosis must be excluded before you think of PPF. It’s that **recurrent unexplained pleural effusion should prompt you to actively look for a pancreatic source**.

## Management: drain the chest, rest the pancreas

Treatment in this case was conservative and worked.

The child received:

- Oxygen support
- Repeated thoracocentesis
- Right intercostal drainage
- Nil per oral
- Total parenteral nutrition
- Octreotide infusion
- Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy

After about 10 days, drainage decreased and stopped, and the chest tube was removed.

That’s the practical management principle: **control the pleural space problem while reducing pancreatic secretion and allowing the fistula to close**.

## When conservative management may be enough — and when it isn’t

There are no robust pediatric guidelines because PPF is so rare, but the usual stepwise approach is:

1. **Conservative therapy first**
   - bowel rest
   - TPN
   - octreotide
   - chest drainage when needed

2. **Endoscopic therapy**
   - ERCP with pancreatic duct stenting if conservative treatment fails or ductal anatomy suggests benefit

3. **Surgery**
   - for refractory cases, duct obstruction, or anatomy not amenable to endoscopic treatment

The literature suggests conservative therapy alone has a relatively low success rate in children, so persistence of drainage or recurrence should push you toward ductal imaging and possible endoscopic management.

## Clinical pearls

- A **recurrent, large pleural effusion in a child is not automatically a lung disease**.
- **Dark red or blood-stained pleural fluid** should widen the differential quickly.
- **Pleural fluid amylase is a major clue**; if it is markedly elevated, think pancreatic source.
- **Ask again about trauma** when the first history doesn’t explain the physiology.
- **MRCP is excellent for defining the fistula anatomy** and can prevent prolonged diagnostic wandering.
- **Chest drainage alone won’t fix the problem** if the pancreas keeps leaking.

## Take-home points

- **Pancreatopleural fistula is a rare but important extrathoracic cause of recurrent pleural effusion, especially when the effusion is large, blood-stained, and rapidly recurrent.**
- **In a child with repeated thoracenteses and no clear pulmonary diagnosis, check pleural fluid amylase and broaden the history to include abdominal trauma or pancreatitis.**
- **CT can suggest the diagnosis, but MRCP is often the study that maps the fistula.**
- **Initial management is usually conservative: drain the chest, rest the pancreas, suppress secretion, and provide nutrition support.**
- **If the effusion keeps coming back, think ductal anatomy and consider ERCP or surgery.**

## Final thought

This is one of those cases that reminds us why medicine rewards pattern recognition plus curiosity. The child didn’t have a primary chest disease masquerading as abdominal symptoms — he had the reverse: a pancreatic injury masquerading as respiratory distress.

That’s the “pulmonary mask” of pancreatitis. And once you’ve seen it, you’re less likely to miss the next recurrent pleural effusion that’s trying to tell you the problem is downstairs.
