Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# CT, MRCP, or ERCP? Imaging Strategy for Suspected Pancreatopleural Fistula

## The “pleural effusion” that wasn’t really a chest problem

Recurrent, large, blood-stained pleural effusions in a child should make you pause. Especially when the effusion keeps coming back after thoracentesis, the chest tube doesn’t solve the problem, and the usual pulmonary explanations start to feel forced.

That was the trap in this case.

A boy in late childhood came in with right-sided pleural effusion, chest pain, and respiratory distress. He had a history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle a month earlier, but that detail only emerged after repeated history-taking. His pleural fluid was dark red each time. CT then showed something much more revealing than a simple effusion: a multiloculated collection tracking from the paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas. MRCP confirmed pancreatitis with a tract into the right pleural cavity and even a smaller communication to the left. Pleural fluid amylase and lipase were strikingly elevated.

This is the key teaching point: in pancreatopleural fistula, the chest is often the messenger, not the source.

## Why pancreatopleural fistula gets missed

PPF is rare in children, and that rarity matters because we naturally look first for the common thoracic causes of pleural effusion:
- parapneumonic effusion/empyema
- tuberculosis
- malignancy
- trauma
- esophageal perforation

But in PPF, the pathology begins in the pancreas, and the pleural effusion is just where the pancreatic secretions end up. That means repeated chest procedures may temporarily relieve symptoms without addressing the actual disease.

In this case, the recurrent hemorrhagic effusion and mild ADA elevation led to empiric antitubercular treatment before the pancreatic source was recognized. That’s a very understandable detour, and exactly why imaging strategy matters.

## CT: the fast, practical first look

CT is often the first “big” imaging study when the diagnosis is unclear and the child is sick enough to need answers quickly. In this case, CT was the turning point because it showed more than fluid in the chest.

CT can help you identify:
- pleural effusion or hydropneumothorax
- lung consolidation or compressive atelectasis
- retroperitoneal or peripancreatic collections
- extension of fluid along fascial planes
- indirect evidence of a pancreatic source

In this patient, CT demonstrated a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space through the paraesophageal region toward the tail of the pancreas. That pattern is the kind of clue that should make you think beyond the lungs.

### CT strengths
- Rapid and widely available
- Good for unstable patients
- Excellent for thoracic complications
- Good at identifying pseudocysts, collections, ductal dilation, calcifications, trauma-related anatomy

### CT limitations
- The actual fistulous tract is often not directly seen
- Radiation exposure matters, especially in children
- It may suggest the diagnosis without fully defining ductal anatomy

So CT is a great “something is connected here” test, but it is usually not the best test to map the full pancreatic story.

## MRCP: the best noninvasive roadmap

If CT raises suspicion for a pancreatic source, MRCP is often the most useful next study.

Why? Because MRCP is noninvasive and can show the ductal anatomy and fistulous tract without instrumenting the biliary/pancreatic system. In this case, MRCP elegantly confirmed:
- pancreatitis
- a tubular cystic collection in the distal body/tail
- extension through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity
- a narrow communication with the left pleural cavity
- connection with the distal pancreatic duct

That is exactly the kind of anatomic map you want before deciding whether conservative therapy, endoscopic intervention, or surgery is most appropriate.

### MRCP strengths
- Noninvasive
- Best for ductal anatomy and fistula delineation
- No radiation
- Particularly helpful in children
- Can support diagnosis when pleural fluid amylase is high but the source remains uncertain

### MRCP limitations
- Less available in some centers
- May require more time and patient cooperation
- Not therapeutic
- Can be limited by motion or inability to tolerate MRI

In a stable child with recurrent unexplained pleural effusion and elevated pleural fluid amylase, MRCP is often the best next step after CT, or even the first dedicated pancreatic study if the clinical suspicion is high enough.

## ERCP: the diagnostic test that can also treat

ERCP remains the gold standard when you need both ductal definition and a possible intervention.

It can:
- demonstrate duct disruption or ductal stenosis
- localize the leak
- permit transpapillary stenting
- reduce intraductal pressure and promote fistula closure

That’s the big appeal of ERCP: it’s not just a look, it’s a treatment platform.

But it also comes with tradeoffs:
- invasive
- requires expertise
- carries procedure-related risks
- may be technically difficult in children or when the duct is severely disrupted

So ERCP is usually most attractive when:
- MRCP has already suggested a ductal leak
- conservative management is failing
- there is a target for stenting
- you are moving toward endoscopic therapy

## A practical stepwise imaging approach

For recurrent unexplained pleural effusion, especially if it is blood-stained or amylase-rich, think in layers:

### Step 1: Confirm the effusion isn’t just “a chest problem”
- Chest X-ray
- Bedside ultrasound
- Thoracentesis with fluid analysis

If the pleural fluid is dark, recurrent, or unexpectedly exudative, send:
- amylase
- lipase
- cytology
- culture
- ADA when TB is in the differential

### Step 2: Look for pancreatic or tracking pathology
- Contrast-enhanced CT if the child is unstable, the diagnosis is broad, or thoracic complications need rapid assessment

CT is especially helpful when you need to see:
- hydropneumothorax
- collections
- retroperitoneal extension
- trauma-associated changes

### Step 3: Define the duct and fistula
- MRCP to map ductal anatomy and fistulous tracts noninvasively

This is often the most informative test once a pancreatic source is suspected.

### Step 4: Use ERCP if you are considering intervention
- diagnostic confirmation
- transpapillary stenting
- ductal decompression

## Clinical pearls from this case

A few things should stick with you:

- Recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusion, especially if blood-stained, should prompt a search for an abdominal source.
- Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L strongly supports pancreatopleural fistula in the right context.
- Dark red pleural fluid is not specific, but it should push you to widen the differential.
- CT often gives the first clue by showing tracking collections or hydropneumothorax.
- MRCP is the best noninvasive study to define the pancreatic duct and fistulous anatomy.
- ERCP is most useful when you are ready to treat as well as diagnose.
- In children, blunt abdominal trauma may be the hidden trigger, and the history may be delayed or initially withheld.

## Don’t let the chest distract you from the pancreas

This case is a great reminder that recurrent pleural effusion is not always a pulmonary disease. In pancreatopleural fistula, the chest is the downstream consequence of a pancreatic leak. Imaging should follow that logic.

A simple way to think about it:
- **CT** finds the problem and the surrounding damage
- **MRCP** maps the fistula noninvasively
- **ERCP** can close the loop by treating the duct

If you see a recurrent, unexplained, amylase-rich pleural effusion, especially after abdominal trauma or in a child with pancreatitis risk factors, don’t stop at the chest. Look for the leak.

## Take-home points

- Recurrent pleural effusion with high pleural fluid amylase should trigger concern for pancreatopleural fistula.
- CT is a strong first-line cross-sectional study for identifying collections, hydropneumothorax, and thoracoabdominal tracking.
- MRCP is the best noninvasive test for defining pancreatic duct anatomy and fistulous communication.
- ERCP is both diagnostic and therapeutic, but is usually best reserved when endoscopic intervention is being considered.
- In children, abdominal trauma may be the key clue, even when the presentation looks purely respiratory.
