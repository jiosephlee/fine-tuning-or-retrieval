Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning: From Pleural Effusion to Pancreatic Fistula

This case began as a common respiratory problem: a child with dyspnea, chest pain, hypoxemia, and a unilateral pleural effusion. The first diagnostic steps were therefore appropriately thoracic. Chest radiography confirmed a right pleural effusion, and bedside ultrasound showed a large fluid collection, helping quantify the effusion and support urgent drainage. In a child with respiratory distress, these bedside studies are valuable because they rapidly establish the presence of pleural fluid and guide thoracocentesis, which can both relieve symptoms and provide diagnostic material.

Repeated thoracocentesis was initially useful for stabilization, but the recurrence of a large effusion after temporary improvement signaled that the underlying cause had not been addressed. This is an important clinical point: when a pleural effusion repeatedly reaccumulates, clinicians must move beyond symptomatic drainage and search for the source of fluid production. In this case, each aspirate was dark red, which broadened the differential and raised concern for hemorrhagic or inflammatory causes rather than a simple transudate.

Pleural fluid analysis is central in hemorrhagic effusions because it helps separate infection, malignancy, tuberculosis, pancreatic disease, and other less common causes. The child’s pleural fluid had only a mildly elevated ADA, which can suggest tuberculosis in the right clinical setting, but ADA is not specific and should never be interpreted in isolation. Cytology did not show malignant cells, and microbiologic testing did not support tuberculosis. These negative or nonspecific results illustrate a common pitfall: a single borderline abnormality can distract clinicians from a more unusual diagnosis if it is not integrated with the overall picture.

The key biochemical clue was the markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase and lipase. Very high pleural fluid amylase is strongly suggestive of a pancreatic source, especially when levels are in the thousands and the effusion is recurrent. Serum amylase and lipase may also be elevated, but they are supportive rather than definitive because they can reflect pancreatic inflammation without proving a fistulous communication to the chest. In pleural effusions with amylase elevation, the main competing considerations are pancreatic disease, esophageal perforation, and some malignancies; in this case, the enzyme profile, fluid appearance, and imaging favored a pancreatic origin.

Cross-sectional imaging was decisive. CT of the chest demonstrated right hydropneumothorax, patchy consolidation, and, most importantly, an elongated multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the pleural region through the retroesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. This pattern suggested that the thoracic process was actually the end result of a retroperitoneal pancreatic leak tracking upward. CT is often the first advanced imaging study because it is widely available and can identify associated findings such as pseudocysts, collections, ductal dilation, or complications in the chest and abdomen. However, CT may not always show the fistulous tract itself clearly.

MRCP then provided the clearest noninvasive confirmation. It demonstrated pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreas extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a smaller communication to the left pleural space. It also showed continuity with the pancreatic duct and narrowing proximal to the communication. This ductal mapping is exactly where MRCP excels: it can show the pancreatic ductal anatomy and fistulous extensions without instrumentation. In suspected pancreatopleural fistula, MRCP is often the best noninvasive test to confirm the diagnosis and guide next steps.

ERCP remains the gold standard when endoscopic treatment is being considered, because it can directly define ductal anatomy and allow stent placement. In this case, MRCP was sufficient to establish the diagnosis, so ERCP was not required. That sequence is typical: CT may raise suspicion, MRCP confirms the fistulous pathway noninvasively, and ERCP is reserved mainly for therapeutic planning or when intervention is needed.

The diagnostic reasoning in this child ultimately depended on putting several clues together. Recurrent large unilateral effusions, dark red pleural fluid, a history of blunt abdominal trauma, very high pleural fluid pancreatic enzymes, and imaging showing a collection tracking from the pancreas to the pleura all pointed to pancreatopleural fistula. The earlier TB-oriented workup was understandable because the effusion was recurrent and hemorrhagic with a mildly elevated ADA, but the absence of constitutional symptoms, the negative microbiology, and the striking enzyme elevation made a pancreatic source far more likely. In children, this case is a useful reminder that respiratory symptoms may be the presenting mask of abdominal pancreatic injury, and that pleural fluid amylase typing can be helpful when the source is uncertain, because pancreatic amylase supports PPF while salivary-type amylase raises alternative diagnoses such as esophageal perforation or other nonpancreatic causes.
