Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 4: ## Differential Diagnosis of Amylase-Rich or Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion in Children

A child with a recurrent, blood-stained pleural effusion immediately raises concern for a thoracic disease, but this case illustrates why the differential must remain broad. The key bedside clue is that pleural fluid was repeatedly dark red and re-accumulated quickly after aspiration. In any child with recurrent pleural fluid, clinicians should think not only about infection and malignancy, but also about unusual causes such as esophageal perforation, diaphragmatic defects, abdominal trauma, and pancreatic disease. In this case, the eventual diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula was delayed because the presentation initially looked entirely “chest-based.”

Tuberculous pleural effusion was a reasonable early consideration because the child had a history of contact with pulmonary tuberculosis and the pleural fluid ADA was mildly elevated. However, ADA is not specific for tuberculosis; mild elevations can occur in other inflammatory effusions. A negative GeneXpert for Mycobacterium tuberculosis argued against TB in this setting, and the clinical picture was also not typical: there was no fever, chronic cough, weight loss, or other constitutional symptoms. In a child with a blood-stained recurrent effusion, a contact history should prompt testing, but it should not override the rest of the data when the overall syndrome does not fit tuberculosis.

Empyema and parapneumonic effusion were also less likely. These usually present with fever, a toxic appearance, raised inflammatory markers, and pleural fluid that is purulent or neutrophil-rich. This child did not have fever, and the pleural fluid description did not suggest frank pus. Although chest radiography showed lung opacity, that can occur from compressive collapse or adjacent inflammation and does not by itself establish bacterial pleural infection. The repeated recurrence after thoracentesis without an infectious clinical syndrome should prompt reconsideration of a noninfectious source.

Malignancy is another important cause of pleural effusion in children, especially leukemia, lymphoma, lung tumors, or metastatic disease. Hemorrhagic pleural fluid can be seen with malignancy, and amylase-rich pleural effusions may occasionally occur in tumors as well. In this case, malignancy became less likely because the child had no weight loss, no persistent fever, and no abnormal blood picture or peripheral smear findings. Pleural fluid cytology also did not show malignant cells. While cytology cannot exclude all malignancy, the combination of normal hematologic assessment and the later pancreatic imaging made a neoplastic cause very unlikely.

Boerhaave syndrome, or esophageal perforation, is a classic cause of amylase-rich pleural fluid and must be considered when pleural effusion is accompanied by chest pain and respiratory distress. The pleural fluid amylase in esophageal perforation typically reflects salivary-type amylase from swallowed contents. However, this child had no history of forceful vomiting or acute mediastinal catastrophe, and the clinical course was subacute and recurrent rather than fulminant. The eventual imaging showed a tract from the pancreas into the pleural space, which is not compatible with primary esophageal rupture.

Structural thoracoabdominal causes were also considered. An acquired diaphragmatic hernia can mimic pleural disease, but imaging would usually show abdominal viscera within the chest or a visible diaphragmatic defect. That was not seen here. Similarly, hepatic hydrothorax is usually associated with chronic liver disease and right-sided pleural effusion due to transdiaphragmatic movement of ascitic fluid. Normal liver function tests, normal liver imaging, and absence of chronic liver disease made this diagnosis implausible. Renal and retroperitoneal causes can produce pleural collections, but normal renal imaging and renal function tests helped exclude these possibilities.

The pleural fluid chemistry was the pivotal clue. Markedly elevated amylase and lipase strongly suggested a pancreatic source. In practice, it is useful to remember that not all amylase-rich effusions are pancreatic: salivary-type amylase is more typical of esophageal rupture, malignancy, or some other thoracic processes, whereas pancreatic-type amylase points toward pancreatic leakage or fistulation. In this case, the very high pleural fluid amylase and lipase, together with the abdominal trauma history and MRCP findings, fit pancreatopleural fistula far better than any primary chest pathology.

Several red flags should push clinicians toward pancreatopleural fistula when faced with a “pulmonary” presentation. These include recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, rapid reaccumulation after drainage, blood-stained fluid, elevated pleural fluid amylase, a history of pancreatitis or trauma, and imaging that shows a retroperitoneal or mediastinal tract. The key lesson from this case is that respiratory symptoms can be the presenting feature of a pancreatic injury, so repeated pleural effusions without a clear pulmonary explanation should trigger a search for an abdominal source.

This case also highlights how cognitive bias can delay diagnosis. Anchoring on tuberculosis was understandable because of the contact history and mildly elevated ADA, but it diverted attention from the more important clues: recurrent hemorrhagic effusion, abdominal trauma, and the striking imaging pattern. In rare pediatric presentations, repeated bedside history-taking and a willingness to revisit the differential are essential. When the clinical course does not fit the initial diagnosis, the safest approach is to step back and ask whether a common explanation is being overused to fit an uncommon disease.
