Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Clinical Context and Index Presentation of a Failing Multilayer Aortic Bioprosthesis

This case begins with a symptomatic elderly man who re-presented with progressive exertional limitation, including dyspnoea, chest discomfort described as a spastic sensation in the left chest during walking, dizziness, and fatigue. In a patient with prior aortic valve intervention, this symptom pattern is highly suggestive of recurrent haemodynamically significant prosthetic valve dysfunction. The clinical question at presentation was not simply whether the valve was failing, but why it was failing: structural degeneration, endocarditis-related damage, or a combination of both. That distinction matters because it shapes both the urgency and the type of reintervention.

His comorbidity burden made him a high-risk procedural candidate from the outset. He had hypertension, extensive multivessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions, complete atrioventricular block requiring pacemaker implantation, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. These conditions increased the risk of open surgery, complicated antithrombotic management, and added to the likelihood of adverse peri-procedural outcomes. In particular, prior coronary disease and repeated PCI are important in redo aortic valve planning because coronary access may be needed in the future and may already be technically challenging. COPD and diabetes further increase operative risk, while prior bleeding complicates long-term antiplatelet decisions.

The patient’s aortic valve history illustrates the concept of lifetime management of aortic stenosis. In 2014, he underwent transcatheter aortic valve implantation with a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe native aortic stenosis. This was already a high-risk treatment strategy, chosen instead of surgical valve replacement because of his clinical profile. Over time, however, transcatheter bioprostheses can fail through structural valve deterioration, thrombosis, or endocarditis. By late 2021 and early 2022, the first prosthesis had developed severe stenosis, with worsening symptoms, rising transvalvular gradients, and a decline in left ventricular ejection fraction. This pattern is typical of pressure overload from valve obstruction: as afterload increases, the ventricle initially compensates but eventually decompensates, leading to reduced systolic performance and exertional symptoms.

Because surgical explantation would have been very high risk, the patient underwent a second transcatheter procedure in 2022, a TAV-in-TAV with implantation of a 23 mm Evolut valve inside the original Lotus prosthesis. This repeat intervention produced an early haemodynamic benefit, reflected by improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction from 42% to 54%. That recovery is clinically important because it suggests that a significant portion of the pre-procedure ventricular dysfunction was afterload-related and therefore reversible once the obstruction was relieved. Shortly after this second intervention, however, the patient experienced a vascular access complication: a femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm requiring open surgical repair. This is a reminder that redo transcatheter procedures may avoid sternotomy but are not free of procedural morbidity, especially in patients with diseased peripheral vessels and multiple prior catheter-based interventions.

The course became more complex in September 2022, when the patient was hospitalized with fever and worsening heart failure symptoms. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis, and prosthetic valve endocarditis was diagnosed. In the setting of a transcatheter prosthesis, infective endocarditis can cause leaflet destruction, reduced mobility, paravalvular complications, and accelerated valve dysfunction. In this case, infection was treated with a full targeted antimicrobial course, and subsequent evaluation suggested resolution of active infection. Nevertheless, the episode likely contributed to additional injury and scarring of the prosthetic apparatus, creating a substrate for recurrent stenosis and regurgitation later on. This distinction between active infection and residual structural damage is crucial: once endocarditis has been adequately treated and no ongoing infection is present, decisions revert to the broader problem of prosthetic failure and anatomical feasibility for reintervention.

By 2024, the patient had developed severe recurrent prosthetic dysfunction in a now triple-layered aortic valve complex. Echocardiography showed severe restenosis with moderate-to-severe intraprosthetic regurgitation, while the mean gradient remained markedly elevated. The presence of two overlapping transcatheter valves created a particularly challenging anatomy, because each added frame can reduce the effective orifice area, complicate leaflet motion, and make coronary access more difficult. In other words, the patient no longer had a single failing valve but a multilayered transcatheter construct with combined obstructive and regurgitant failure. This is the setting in which third-time transcatheter intervention, or TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, becomes relevant.

The clinical importance of the case lies in its representation of a growing problem in contemporary valve care. As TAVI is used in younger and lower-risk patients, and as initial recipients live longer, clinicians will increasingly confront the challenge of treating failed transcatheter prostheses rather than native valves. Each reintervention must account for prior frame geometry, coronary obstruction risk, residual valve area, and future access. This case is therefore a practical example of lifetime structural heart disease management: initial device choice affects the feasibility of later redo procedures, and early planning must anticipate the possibility of serial valve failure.

## Core Background Medicine: Structural Valve Failure, Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis, and the Challenges of Serial Redo TAVI

This case sits at the intersection of two increasingly common realities in modern structural heart disease: more patients are living long enough after transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) to develop valve failure, and a small but important subset will later have infective endocarditis involving their prosthesis. TAVI was originally reserved for patients at very high surgical risk, but it is now used in broader populations. As survival improves, clinicians are seeing not only the first failure of a transcatheter valve, but also failure of a redo transcatheter valve, creating a “lifetime management” problem rather than a single-procedure solution. In this patient, the sequence of initial TAVI, TAV-in-TAV, and then TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV illustrates how repeated interventions progressively narrow the functional outflow tract and increase technical complexity.

Transcatheter bioprosthetic valves can fail through several overlapping mechanisms. Structural valve degeneration refers to intrinsic deterioration of the valve tissue over time, including leaflet thickening, fibrosis, calcification, tearing, and reduced mobility. In practice, this may present as rising transvalvular gradients from progressive stenosis, worsening regurgitation from incomplete leaflet coaptation, or a mixed lesion with both. Mechanical restriction can also develop when prior valve frames alter leaflet motion or when the frame itself limits expansion of a newly implanted valve. In this case, the abnormal valve function was mixed: the patient had severe restenosis with elevated gradients and intraprosthetic regurgitation, indicating both obstructive and incompetent prosthetic dysfunction.

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) adds another layer of complexity. Even after the infection is microbiologically cured, endocarditis may have already damaged the valve leaflets, caused fibrosis, altered leaflet motion, or accelerated structural degeneration. Thus, “cure” of infection does not necessarily mean restoration of valve function. Clinically, this is why it is important to distinguish active infection from healed endocarditis. Active PVE is suggested by persistent fever, positive blood cultures, vegetations, abscess, paravalvular complications, or ongoing inflammatory findings. Once infection has resolved and imaging is negative, management of the remaining prosthetic dysfunction is guided mainly by haemodynamics and anatomy rather than by the prior infection itself. In this case, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and CT were used to confirm the absence of active endocarditis before the third TAVI was undertaken.

The echocardiographic findings in the case—high mean gradients, reduced aortic valve area, and intraprosthetic regurgitation—represent severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. The mean gradient reflects the pressure load across the valve; as the effective orifice area becomes smaller, the velocity and gradient rise. The aortic valve area is usually estimated by the continuity equation, which compares flow in the left ventricular outflow tract with flow through the valve. When gradients rise and the calculated valve area falls, the prosthesis is functionally stenotic. When regurgitation is also present, the valve is mixed-dysfunction, which can worsen symptoms and complicate interpretation because a valve may be both narrowed and leaky. In serial prosthesis failure, the haemodynamic burden can become substantial even when the valve leaflets appear to open.

Prosthesis-patient mismatch is a key issue in redo and serial TAVI. It occurs when the effective orifice area of the implanted valve is too small relative to the patient’s body size, resulting in higher-than-expected gradients despite technically successful implantation. This is especially likely when one valve is implanted inside another, because each additional frame and leaflet layer reduces the available lumen. Severe mismatch can limit symptomatic improvement, contribute to persistent left ventricular pressure overload, and make the immediate post-procedural gradient remain high even when the valve is well positioned. In this patient, the initial post-third-TAVI gradient was still markedly elevated, consistent with severe mismatch in a triple-stented anatomy, although it improved over time.

Coronary obstruction is one of the most feared complications of valve-in-valve and valve-in-transcatheter-valve procedures. When a new valve is deployed inside a prior bioprosthesis, displaced leaflets or the outer frame can cover the coronary ostia or block coronary inflow. The risk depends on the anatomy of the root and the relationship between the valve and the coronaries. Important CT measurements include coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, and valve-to-coronary distance. A low coronary ostium, small sinus of Valsalva, or short valve-to-coronary distance increases risk. In addition, the “neo-skirt” created by the new valve frame and leaflets can sit near the coronary origins and create an additional barrier to blood flow or future catheter access. This is why preprocedural CT planning is central in redo procedures and especially in serial TAVI.

Commissural alignment is another practical concept that matters more as the number of transcatheter layers increases. The commissures are the points where valve leaflets meet; if the new valve’s commissures are aligned favorably relative to the coronaries, future coronary engagement may remain possible and coronary flow may be less compromised. Poor alignment can make later coronary angiography or intervention difficult because catheters must pass through overlapping frames at awkward angles. In this case, commissural alignment was deliberately considered during device deployment to preserve coronary access, reflecting a forward-looking strategy rather than simply aiming for immediate haemodynamic success.

The problem of overlapping stent frames is not just one of access; it also affects valve expansion, leaflet motion, and the effective orifice area. When multiple frames are nested, the inner valve may not expand fully, and the pre-existing frame may constrain the new prosthesis in a noncircular or undersized configuration. This can worsen gradients and predispose to patient-prosthesis mismatch. Overlapping frames also make any future intervention more difficult, because each additional valve further reduces the available workspace and increases the chance of coronary compromise. That is why serial transcatheter reintervention requires careful anticipation of not only the current procedure, but also any possible next step.

Guidelines still treat surgery as the standard treatment for prosthetic valve endocarditis, particularly when infection is uncontrolled, there is heart failure, abscess, or destructive valve disease. However, the guidelines also emphasize individualized decision-making. In patients whose infection has been adequately treated but who remain poor surgical candidates because of frailty, comorbidity, prior complex interventions, or hostile anatomy, a transcatheter approach may be considered after multidisciplinary review. This case exemplifies that principle: once infection was resolved and surgery was deemed prohibitively risky, CT-based planning allowed a third transcatheter valve to be used as a pragmatic solution to severe prosthetic dysfunction. The key lesson is that in modern structural heart disease, the question is no longer simply whether a valve can be implanted, but how each intervention affects haemodynamics, coronary safety, and the feasibility of any future reintervention.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning: From Recurrent Symptoms to Procedural Feasibility

The diagnostic workup in this case began with a familiar clinical problem in patients with prior transcatheter aortic valve implantation: recurrence of exertional dyspnoea, fatigue, dizziness, and reduced exercise tolerance raised concern for prosthetic valve dysfunction. In June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated clear evidence of severe bioprosthetic valve dysfunction. The mean transvalvular gradient was 49 mm Hg, the aortic valve area (AVA) was only 0.5 cm², and there was moderate-to-severe intraprosthetic regurgitation. These findings are important because prosthetic valve failure is not defined by a single parameter; rather, the echocardiographic pattern must be interpreted as a whole. A markedly elevated mean gradient suggests significant obstruction to forward flow, while a reduced AVA confirms a severely narrowed effective orifice. The Doppler velocity index (DVI), which compares left ventricular outflow tract flow to transprosthetic flow, further helps distinguish true obstruction from measurement error or low-flow states; in this patient, the later postprocedural DVI of 0.24 was consistent with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch. The presence of both high gradients and meaningful regurgitation indicates mixed prosthetic valve disease, which can produce severe symptoms even when left ventricular ejection fraction remains preserved.

The AVA was calculated using the continuity equation, which is the standard Doppler method for estimating valve area in stenotic lesions. This approach relies on the principle that flow proximal to the valve equals flow through the valve, allowing AVA to be derived from left ventricular outflow tract dimensions and velocity-time integrals. In prosthetic valves, the continuity equation remains useful, although interpretation must consider valve type, flow conditions, and the possibility of patient-prosthesis mismatch. Here, the measured AVA of 0.5 cm² and the associated gradient supported severe prosthetic stenosis rather than isolated regurgitation or nonspecific hemodynamic variation.

A major diagnostic challenge was determining whether the patient’s worsening valve function reflected recurrent structural degeneration alone or active prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In September 2022, the combination of persistent fever, worsening heart failure symptoms, severe restenosis on echocardiography, and positive blood cultures for Enterococcus faecalis established the diagnosis of PVE. In prosthetic valves, infection may present subtly, and fever plus positive cultures in the context of new or worsening valve dysfunction should prompt immediate evaluation. The organism in this case was clinically relevant because enterococcal endocarditis commonly affects elderly patients with multiple comorbidities and can involve prosthetic material. At that time, the patient received a full targeted antimicrobial course, which is essential because durable control of infection is required before any further valve intervention can be considered.

Before proceeding with the third transcatheter intervention, the team had to demonstrate that the infection had resolved. This is a critical step because active endocarditis generally makes elective transcatheter reintervention inappropriate. The reassessment included clinical improvement, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and multislice computed tomography (MSCT). Repeated negative cultures helped confirm microbiologic cure, while TEE was used to exclude vegetations, abscesses, or paravalvular complications that might suggest persistent infection. TEE is particularly valuable in prosthetic valves because acoustic shadowing on TTE can obscure small vegetations or periannular extension. In this patient, TEE showed no evidence of active endocarditis, and CT similarly did not identify inflammatory or structural complications attributable to ongoing infection. In other words, prior endocarditis remained part of the history, but it was no longer the active driver of treatment decisions once infection had been adequately treated and excluded.

CT imaging was then central to determining whether a third transcatheter valve could be implanted safely and effectively. In triple-layer valve anatomy, CT is not simply confirmatory imaging; it is the planning tool that defines feasibility. MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the existing Evolut prosthesis, indicating severe restriction of the functional orifice. Beyond measuring lumen size, CT was used to evaluate the interaction of the two prior transcatheter frames, the anticipated landing zone for a third valve, and the risk of coronary obstruction. Important parameters included coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and valve-to-coronary distance. The team also considered the anticipated neo-skirt, meaning the new obstructive column created by overlapping valve frames and leaflets after deployment. If this neo-skirt is too close to the coronary ostia, coronary flow can be compromised during or after implantation, and future coronary access may become difficult or impossible. The CT findings suggested that a high implantation strategy could preserve coronary flow, and this allowed the operators to proceed without coronary protection.

Operative risk assessment strongly favored a transcatheter strategy over surgery. The patient’s EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, reflecting very high predicted surgical risk. This was compounded by multiple comorbidities, including severe coronary artery disease with several prior percutaneous coronary interventions, pacemaker dependence after complete atrioventricular block, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. In addition, he had already undergone two prior TAVI procedures, with extensive stent incorporation into the aortic root. Surgical explantation in such a setting would likely require complex aortic root and ascending aortic replacement, making the operation technically hazardous and potentially prohibitive. Conservative therapy alone was not appropriate because the patient had severe symptomatic prosthetic dysfunction and progressive hemodynamic compromise. Thus, the realistic options were surgery, continued medical management, or a third transcatheter intervention, and the combination of infection resolution, CT feasibility, and prohibitive surgical risk made the transcatheter option the preferred strategy.

The key reasoning step was the conclusion that treated prior endocarditis was not, by itself, a contraindication to reintervention. Once the patient had completed antimicrobial therapy, remained clinically stable, had negative blood cultures, and showed no evidence of active infection on TEE and CT, the management question shifted from infection control to structural valve failure. In that context, the heart team judged that the risk of leaving the patient with severe mixed prosthetic dysfunction was greater than the risk of a carefully planned transcatheter redo procedure. This case therefore illustrates how decision-making in complex structural heart disease depends on integrating symptoms, echocardiography, microbiology, advanced imaging, and surgical risk assessment into a single procedural feasibility analysis.

## Procedural Strategy for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV: Valve Selection, Imaging Guidance, and Technical Execution

The procedural plan in this case was built around a heart-team discussion and shared decision-making, which are essential when treating a patient with three potential competing hazards: recurrent severe prosthetic valve dysfunction, prior treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, and very high surgical risk. Although conventional surgery is generally the preferred approach for active prosthetic valve endocarditis, this patient no longer had evidence of active infection, had already completed targeted antimicrobial therapy, and had prohibitive operative risk because of advanced age, multiple comorbidities, complex coronary disease, prior pacemaker implantation, and two deeply incorporated transcatheter valve frames. In that setting, a third transcatheter intervention was judged to be the most realistic strategy for restoring valve function while avoiding the morbidity of redo root or ascending aortic surgery.

Valve choice was driven by anatomy and by the need to maximize residual valve area inside a very constrained, multilayered outflow tract. The Acurate neo2 was selected because its supra-annular leaflet position and relatively favorable hemodynamic profile can be advantageous in narrow annular or valve-in-valve settings, where the main threat is severe prosthesis-patient mismatch from a progressively smaller effective orifice area. In a triple-frame construct, even small differences in frame height, skirt position, and leaflet location can influence both flow and coronary access. The team therefore sought a prosthesis that could be implanted with a controlled, high position and that would permit deliberate commissural orientation.

Preprocedural CT was central to the strategy. Multislice CT allowed assessment of the internal diameter of the existing Evolut valve, the spatial relationship between the prior valve frames and the coronary ostia, and the anticipated “neo-skirt” position after implantation. This information was used to estimate the risk of coronary obstruction and to plan a landing zone that would keep the new valve high enough to preserve coronary flow but still sufficiently anchored for stability. CT modeling also informed leaflet alignment and implant depth, which are especially important in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures because misalignment can worsen coronary reaccess and reduce the already limited effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia with left radial and left femoral arterial access. Local anaesthesia is often preferred in complex transcatheter aortic interventions when feasible because it avoids the hemodynamic effects and recovery burden of general anaesthesia, while still allowing close neurological and clinical monitoring. The chosen access sites provided both procedural control and the ability to perform continuous angiographic assessment. Intra-procedural aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed the baseline severe aortic regurgitation and provided a fluoroscopic reference for valve deployment.

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle to provide stable support across the aortic valve complex. This type of wire is commonly used in TAVI because it offers a combination of stiffness and flexibility that helps maintain position during ballooning and valve delivery. Balloon pre-dilation was then performed under rapid ventricular pacing. In a multilayer prosthetic construct, pre-dilation can help open the stenotic or restricted pathway, facilitate device crossing, and provide an early check of coronary safety and annular compliance. Rapid pacing reduces cardiac output and motion, allowing more controlled balloon inflation and decreasing the chance of balloon-induced displacement. Importantly, simultaneous aortography after pre-dilation showed preserved coronary flow, which confirmed that the planned valve trajectory remained acceptable and that coronary protection was not required.

The third valve was implanted high, at the level of the Evolut leaflets, rather than deeply in the outflow tract. This was a deliberate strategy. High implantation helped pin the prior leaflets against the frame and limited inward leaflet folding, thereby preserving the maximal possible effective orifice area. In practical terms, this approach also reduced the size of the neo-skirt below the coronary ostia, which matters because a bulky new skirt in a triple-stented anatomy can create functional coronary obstruction even when the ostia are not directly covered. The implant position was guided by the CT-based plan and fluoroscopic landmarks, with the goal of reproducing the intended depth and avoiding both embolization and excessive ventricular extension.

Commissural alignment was another important part of the technique. When transcatheter valves are implanted in complex prior frames, the orientation of commissural posts can determine whether future coronary engagement remains possible. The team attempted to align the new prosthesis with the existing leaflet and frame geometry to preserve access to the coronary arteries. Although perfect alignment may be difficult in layered constructs, it is a worthwhile objective because it can reduce the complexity of any future coronary intervention.

After deployment, post-dilation was performed with a balloon under rapid pacing to improve expansion of the new valve and to optimize flow through the stacked frames. The purpose here was not intentional frame fracture, which was not performed, but rather to maximize radial expansion and reduce residual underexpansion that could contribute to high gradients. In this case, the combination of careful positioning and post-dilation was aimed at improving leaflet mobility and central flow while maintaining coronary patency. Final aortography confirmed correct valve position, preserved coronary perfusion, and absence of both residual aortic regurgitation and major procedural complications.

This case illustrates the technical pitfalls unique to TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures. The main hazards are coronary obstruction, limited future coronary access, valve underexpansion, and severe patient-prosthesis mismatch from progressive reduction in effective orifice area. These risks were mitigated by CT-guided planning, choice of a supra-annular prosthesis, high implantation, attention to commissural alignment, careful pre-dilation and post-dilation, and real-time angiographic confirmation of coronary flow. The result was a technically successful third transcatheter valve implantation in a highly constrained anatomy.

## Outcomes, Interpretation of Post-Procedural Haemodynamics, and Clinical Lessons for Lifetime Valve Management

The immediate result of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation was judged using Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) concepts, which focus on procedural success, residual valve dysfunction, and complications. In this case, the new valve was deployed in the intended position, there was no residual aortic regurgitation, and coronary perfusion was preserved, which all support technical success. However, the early transthoracic echocardiogram also showed a very high mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg, a calculated aortic valve area of 0.9 cm², and a low Doppler velocity index of 0.24. These findings indicated severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM), meaning that although the valve was functioning and not leaking, the effective opening was still too small for the patient’s body size and flow requirements. In complex valve-in-valve or valve-in-valve-in-valve procedures, this is a key point: the absence of regurgitation does not necessarily mean optimal haemodynamics.

Early post-implant gradients after repeat TAVI can be difficult to interpret. Immediately after implantation, flow conditions may be affected by incomplete frame expansion, interaction between multiple valve frames, residual leaflet restriction, and transient changes in loading conditions. In this case, the unexpectedly high gradient did not represent procedural failure, but rather a haemodynamic limitation that improved over time. Several mechanisms likely contributed to that improvement. High-pressure post-dilation probably enhanced frame expansion, the self-expanding prosthesis may have continued to settle and open more fully after the procedure, and the altered geometry of the prior valve layers may have gradually permitted better leaflet motion and more central flow. High implantation strategy and commissural alignment may also have helped reduce functional obstruction and preserve flow through the valve.

This was reflected at the 1-month follow-up, when echocardiography showed a lower mean gradient of 27 mm Hg and a larger valve area of 1.1 cm². The left ventricle also showed reverse remodelling, with a reduction in chamber volumes, suggesting that the improved valve function translated into better haemodynamic loading conditions. Importantly, there was no residual regurgitation. The valve remained in the moderately stenotic range, but the overall trend was clearly favorable, showing that the early high gradient had not prevented meaningful functional improvement.

Clinically, the patient’s course after the procedure was reassuring. At intermediate follow-up, he was asymptomatic, had good exercise tolerance, and showed no evidence of recurrent heart failure or infection. This is particularly important in a patient with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis, because any new decline in functional status, fever, or inflammatory syndrome would require urgent reassessment for recurrent infection or prosthetic dysfunction. In such patients, surveillance cannot rely on symptoms alone; it must also include serial echocardiography and a low threshold for repeat blood cultures and imaging if infection is suspected.

At 1 year, the outcome remained stable. The patient was still clinically well, the mean gradient remained around 26 mm Hg, valve area was preserved at 1.1 cm², and left ventricular ejection fraction was 65%. There was no recurrent endocarditis, no significant regurgitation, and no valve-related complication reported. For a patient with triple transcatheter valve layers, severe comorbidity, and prior infective endocarditis, this represents a meaningful durable benefit.

The case also highlights the broader challenge of lifetime management of structural valve disease. As patients live longer after the first TAVI, some will require repeat interventions, and the complexity of each subsequent procedure increases. Serial transcatheter valves create a layered construct that can limit residual orifice area, complicate commissural orientation, and make future coronary access more difficult. This means that planning should not focus only on the immediate procedure, but also on what may be needed years later. CT-guided assessment is central to this approach because it helps define coronary height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary relationships, and the interaction between valve frames.

If the current prosthesis were to fail in the future, options would be limited. Surgical explantation of a triple-stented aortic root would be technically difficult and very high risk. Catheter-based bailout concepts such as valve fracture or leaflet modification techniques, including BASILICA-type strategies, may theoretically be relevant, but their performance in triple-layer constructs remains uncertain. The practical lesson is that future rescue options may be constrained, so the choice made at the current intervention has implications for later years as well.

Overall, this case teaches several important lessons. First, procedural success after complex redo TAVI includes not only correct deployment but also careful haemodynamic interpretation. Second, an early high gradient does not always indicate a failed intervention, because haemodynamics may improve after post-dilation and with time. Third, patients who are likely to outlive their first valve intervention need a lifetime strategy from the outset, with CT-based planning, attention to coronary access, and anticipation of potential future reinterventions. In short, this case shows that even very complex TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV anatomy can be managed successfully when treatment is guided by imaging, procedural planning, and long-term surveillance.
