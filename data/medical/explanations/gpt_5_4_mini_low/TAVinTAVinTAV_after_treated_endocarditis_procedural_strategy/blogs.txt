# Triple-Deck TAVI: When a Third Transcatheter Valve Is the Least Bad Option

## Why this case grabs your attention

Most of us are used to thinking in binaries: native valve or prosthetic valve, surgery or transcatheter, first-time TAVI or redo TAVI. This case blows past those neat categories.

Here was a man in his late 70s with:
- a prior surgical-risk profile that had already pushed him into transcatheter therapy,
- two previous transcatheter aortic valves,
- treated prosthetic valve endocarditis,
- severe recurrent prosthetic dysfunction,
- and anatomy that made open surgery look less like a solution and more like a hazard.

The team ultimately performed a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** using CT-guided planning and a deliberately staged procedural strategy. At 1 year, he remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function.

That’s the interesting part: this wasn’t “can we do it?” so much as “what is the least bad option left?”

## The clinical setup

The patient had extensive cardiovascular disease:
- hypertension
- three-vessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior PCI procedures
- complete AV block with pacemaker
- type 2 diabetes
- COPD
- recurrent GI bleeding

He first received a **27 mm Lotus valve in 2014** for severe aortic stenosis. By 2022, that prosthesis had failed with severe stenosis, rising gradients, and reduced EF, so he underwent **TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut**.

That second procedure initially helped:
- EF improved from **42% to 54%**

But then came the complication that changes the whole conversation: **Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis** in September 2022, treated with a full 6-week IV ampicillin-based regimen and follow-up oral therapy per ID guidance. At the time of the third procedure, the infection had been considered resolved:
- repeat blood cultures negative
- TEE without active endocarditis
- CT without evidence of ongoing infection

By 2024, he had again deteriorated:
- severe stenosis
- moderate-to-severe regurgitation
- mean gradient **49 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.5 cm²**
- EF recovered to **66%**

In June 2024, the question was no longer whether the valve was bad. It clearly was. The question was how to fix it without killing the patient in the process.

## Why redo surgery was not the easy answer

On paper, prior prosthetic valve endocarditis and failed bioprosthesis might push you toward surgery. In real life, this patient had a long list of reasons surgery was unattractive:

- **EuroSCORE II 27.36%**
- COPD
- diabetes
- prior GI bleeding
- extensive coronary disease
- prior pacemaker
- two prior transcatheter frames deeply integrated into the aortic root

The authors note that surgical explantation would likely have required **total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement**. That is not a “redo AVR” problem anymore; that is major reconstructive surgery in a frail, comorbid patient.

This is the heart-team lesson: sometimes “standard” and “feasible” are not the same thing.

## What imaging had to answer before anyone touched the valve

For a third transcatheter valve, the preprocedure CT is not just a formality. It is the case.

The team used multislice CT to assess:
- coronary ostial height
- sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- valve-to-coronary distance
- interaction between the two existing valve frames
- the relationship of the anticipated neo-skirt to the coronaries
- feasibility of a high implant to preserve coronary flow and future access

The CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis**, confirming severe restriction and helping guide the strategy.

A few practical pearls here:
- In layered TAVI, you’re no longer planning around one valve frame; you’re planning around **multiple metal cages and their combined effect on coronary access and effective orifice area**.
- CT is being used not just to “see anatomy,” but to predict what the new valve will do to the geometry of the old ones.
- The goal is not simply to implant a valve; it is to avoid creating an unworkable coronary situation or catastrophic patient-prosthesis mismatch.

## The procedural strategy: high implant, careful alignment, and no coronary protection

The team proceeded with **local anesthesia**, **left radial and left femoral arterial access**, and a **self-expanding Acurate neo2 (size S)**.

A few procedural details matter:

- Aortography confirmed severe regurgitation.
- A Safari wire was placed into the LV.
- Balloon pre-dilation was performed with a **22/40 mm balloon** under rapid pacing at **180 bpm**.
- Simultaneous aortography confirmed preserved coronary flow.
- The Acurate neo2 was deployed with deliberate alignment to the **Evolut leaflets**, based on preprocedural CT modeling.
- Post-dilation with a **20/40 mm balloon** was performed under rapid pacing.
- Final aortography showed correct position, preserved coronary perfusion, and no residual regurgitation.

The key strategic idea was **high implantation** at the level of the Evolut leaflets. The authors explain this as a way to:
- fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame,
- reduce further inward folding of leaflets,
- limit the formation of a neo-skirt below the coronaries,
- preserve coronary flow,
- and maintain future access.

That’s elegant, and it’s also a reminder that in complex redo TAVI, the implant depth is not a technical footnote — it’s the strategy.

## Why choose the Acurate neo2?

The authors selected the **Acurate neo2** for a few practical reasons:
- supra-annular leaflet position
- relatively low frame height
- favorable hemodynamics in narrow anatomies
- support for commissural alignment

In a triple-stented anatomy, every millimeter matters. The goal was to maximize the effective orifice area while minimizing the coronary and access penalties of another frame.

## The post-procedure hemodynamics: not perfect, but acceptable

Immediately after implantation, the valve looked good on echo in terms of motion and regurgitation:
- leaflets mobile
- complete opening
- **no aortic regurgitation**

But the hemodynamics initially showed severe **patient-prosthesis mismatch**:
- mean gradient **67 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.9 cm²**
- indexed AVA **0.4 cm²/m²**
- DVI **0.24**

That’s an important reminder: technical success and hemodynamic optimization are not always the same thing right away.

By 1 month:
- mean gradient improved to **27 mm Hg**
- AVA increased to **1.1 cm²**
- LV remodeling was seen
- no residual regurgitation

At 1 year:
- mean gradient **26 mm Hg**
- AVA **1.1 cm²**
- EF **65%**
- no recurrent endocarditis
- no valve-related complications
- patient asymptomatic and active

So while the immediate postimplant gradient was concerning, the valve performance settled into a durable, clinically useful result.

## What probably happened physiologically?

The authors suggest the gradient improvement likely reflected a combination of:
- effective commissural alignment
- post-dilation
- partial expansion of the prior Evolut frame
- improved leaflet motion and central flow dynamics

No intentional frame fracture was done. But high-pressure balloon post-dilation may have contributed to further radial expansion of the Evolut.

That matters because the physiology of layered transcatheter valves is dynamic. You’re not just placing a new valve; you’re also altering the mechanics of the old ones.

## The endocarditis question: when can you reintervene?

This case is especially interesting because of the prior **prosthetic valve endocarditis**.

Current guideline thinking still generally favors surgery for PVE, especially if there is heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this case sits in the gray zone that comes up in real practice:
- infection treated
- no evidence of active endocarditis
- blood cultures negative
- TEE negative for ongoing infection
- CT without active infectious features
- surgical risk prohibitive

In other words, the infection history mattered, but it did not automatically dictate the procedure once it was resolved. The decision then became one of:
- anatomy,
- procedural feasibility,
- expected benefit,
- and patient-centered risk tolerance.

## The bigger lesson: lifetime management starts at valve #1

This case is really about longitudinal thinking.

Each transcatheter reintervention compounds the complexity:
- more frame layering
- more coronary access issues
- higher risk of residual stenosis/PPM
- more difficult future bailout options

That means planning for “what happens if this valve fails?” should begin early, not after the third intervention is already needed.

## Take-home points

- A third transcatheter aortic valve can be a reasonable off-label option when **surgery is prohibitive** and **infection has been treated and excluded as active**.
- In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, **CT planning is everything**: coronary height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, frame interaction, and implant depth all matter.
- **High implantation** may help preserve coronary flow and improve the effective orifice area in layered valve anatomy.
- Immediate postprocedure gradients may be misleading; **follow-up hemodynamics can improve** as the system settles.
- In patients with prior PVE, the decision is not “endocarditis equals no TAVI”; it’s whether there is **active infection** and whether the anatomy and risk profile make surgery genuinely viable.
- Lifetime structural heart planning matters. Every valve you implant changes the options for the next one.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that structural heart disease often lives in the land of imperfect choices. A third transcatheter valve after treated endocarditis is not routine, and it should not be treated casually. But in a patient with prohibitive surgical risk, no active infection, and CT-confirmed feasibility, it may be the least bad option — and, as shown here, it can provide durable symptomatic and hemodynamic benefit at 1 year.



# CT Before the Third Valve: Planning for Coronary Obstruction, Neo-Skirt, and Future Access

## Why this case matters

Most of us are getting used to the idea that transcatheter aortic valves are not always “one and done.” But a **third** transcatheter aortic valve in the same patient? That’s the kind of anatomy that forces you to slow down and think hard about imaging, coronary safety, and what future bailout options might look like.

This case is a good reminder that in serial TAVI reintervention, the procedure is not really decided in the cath lab. It is decided on **CT**.

The patient had:
- a prior **27 mm Lotus** valve,
- then a **23 mm Evolut** valve-in-valve,
- then severe recurrent dysfunction after treated **Enterococcus faecalis** prosthetic valve endocarditis,
- and ultimately a **third transcatheter valve**: an **Acurate neo2**.

The key question wasn’t just “can we place another valve?” It was:

- Can we do it **without coronary obstruction**?
- Can we preserve **coronary re-access** for the future?
- Can we avoid creating a geometry that leaves the patient with a critically small effective orifice area?

That’s where preprocedural CT did the heavy lifting.

## The CT questions you must answer before a third valve

In a standard redo TAVI, CT is already important. In **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**, it becomes the entire strategy.

The imaging team in this case used MSCT to assess:

- **Coronary ostial height**
- **Sinus of Valsalva dimensions**
- **Valve-to-coronary distance**
- **Interaction between the existing frames**
- **The anticipated neo-skirt position**
- **Feasibility of a high implantation strategy**
- **Whether coronary protection would be needed**

The scan showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis**, confirming severe narrowing and helping define the landing zone for the third valve.

That’s a crucial point: CT wasn’t just describing anatomy. It was used to decide whether the intervention was **technically feasible** and how to make it **safer and more durable**.

## Coronary obstruction: the fear that drives the whole plan

Coronary obstruction is the nightmare complication in redo transcatheter valve procedures, especially when multiple frames stack up in the aortic root. The risk comes from a combination of:

- displaced native or bioprosthetic leaflets,
- a constrained root,
- low coronary height,
- small sinuses,
- and the creation of a bulky **neo-skirt** from the new valve frame and leaflets.

In this case, the team specifically reviewed:
- coronary ostial height,
- sinus geometry,
- valve-to-coronary distance,
- and how the new valve would interact with the old frames.

That allowed them to proceed **without coronary protection**, but only because imaging suggested the risk was acceptable and a strategy had been chosen to preserve coronary flow.

That is the nuance: **“No coronary protection” should never mean “no coronary planning.”**

## Neo-skirt matters more than people think

When you place one transcatheter valve inside another, you don’t just reduce the outflow area. You also create a **neo-skirt**—the functional barrier formed by overlapping valve structures and displaced leaflets.

Why does that matter?

Because the neo-skirt can:
- cover the coronary ostia,
- make future coronary access difficult,
- and worsen the effective orifice area.

This case was planned with that in mind. The team aimed for a **high implantation** strategy, guided by CT, to minimize how much new structure sat below the coronaries and to preserve flow.

That’s a useful principle in complex redo anatomy:

> The best CT plan is not only about avoiding acute coronary obstruction. It’s also about avoiding a future coronary access problem.

## Frame interaction: the anatomy is now mechanical, not just valvular

After two transcatheter valves, the aortic root is no longer a simple valve space. It becomes a **multi-frame mechanical environment**.

CT was used here to understand:
- how the prior frames were positioned,
- how much lumen remained,
- and how the third prosthesis might be deployed relative to the Evolut leaflets.

The operators deliberately aligned the new **Acurate neo2** with the **Evolut leaflets**, aiming to optimize effective orifice area and reduce patient-prosthesis mismatch.

That is a very practical CT-driven concept:
- if the frame geometry is constrained,
- you need a plan for where the leaflets will end up,
- and how much residual opening you can realistically expect.

## Why high implantation was the winning move

In this case, the third valve was implanted **high**, at the level of the Evolut leaflets.

Why do that?

Because high implantation:
- helps fix the pre-existing leaflets against the frame,
- prevents further inward folding that would further narrow the orifice,
- minimizes the neo-skirt below the coronary ostia,
- and supports future coronary access.

That strategy was not guessed at the table. It was based on **preprocedural CT modeling** and fluoroscopic landmarks.

This is one of the best examples of why imaging planning matters in lifetime valve management: the implant is not just treating today’s stenosis. It is shaping the anatomy the patient will have to live with after this procedure.

## Why the team did not use coronary protection

Given the anatomy, one might ask why they did not wire or protect the coronaries prophylactically.

The answer is in the CT-guided risk assessment. The imaging suggested:
- acceptable coronary clearance,
- a feasible valve position,
- and a deployment strategy that would preserve coronary flow.

The operators still watched coronary flow carefully during:
- balloon predilation,
- rapid pacing,
- and final deployment.

Aortography confirmed that coronary flow remained unobstructed.

So the lesson here is not “skip coronary protection.” It’s:

> Use CT to decide when coronary protection is truly needed, and when a well-planned deployment can safely avoid it.

## What happened hemodynamically after deployment

The immediate post-procedure echo showed something that often happens in very complex valve-in-valve anatomy: **good leaflet motion and no regurgitation, but a high residual gradient**.

At first:
- mean gradient was **67 mm Hg**
- AVA was **0.9 cm²**
- DVI was **0.24**
- consistent with **severe patient-prosthesis mismatch**

But then the gradient improved substantially over time:
- at 1 month: mean gradient **27 mm Hg**, AVA **1.1 cm²**
- at 1 year: mean gradient **26 mm Hg**, AVA **1.1 cm²**, EF **65%**

So the CT-guided strategy did what it was supposed to do:
- preserve coronary flow,
- allow a technically successful third implant,
- and provide durable clinical benefit at 1 year.

## Practical CT pearls from this case

A few points worth carrying into practice:

- **CT is not optional** before serial transcatheter reintervention.
- In triple-valve anatomy, CT should assess not just size, but **coronary access geometry**.
- Look specifically at:
  - coronary ostial height,
  - sinus dimensions,
  - valve-to-coronary distance,
  - frame interaction,
  - and expected neo-skirt position.
- A **high implantation strategy** can help preserve coronary flow and future access.
- The choice to avoid coronary protection should be **imaging-driven**, not convenience-driven.
- In patients with prior endocarditis that has been treated and is no longer active, the decision is still dominated by:
  - anatomy,
  - hemodynamics,
  - and surgical risk.

## Take-home points

- **Before a third valve, CT is the procedure plan.**
- **Coronary obstruction risk is only part of the story; future coronary access matters too.**
- **Neo-skirt and frame stacking can be as important as ostial height.**
- **High implantation, when anatomically feasible, can improve both flow and re-access.**
- **In complex redo anatomy, coronary protection is a decision—not a reflex.**

In the end, this case shows that a third TAVI can be done successfully even after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, but only when the team treats CT as a roadmap rather than a formality.



# After Endocarditis, Can You Go Back In? TAVI Reintervention Once Prosthetic Valve Infection Is Treated

## Why this case matters

Here’s the uncomfortable question: if a transcatheter aortic valve has already failed, and then later gets infected, can you ever safely go back in with another valve once the infection is treated?

This case says: sometimes, yes — but only after you’ve done the hard work of proving the infection is really over.

A man in his late 70s had a remarkable valve history: a 27 mm Lotus TAVI in 2014, a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut in 2022, then prosthetic valve endocarditis due to *Enterococcus faecalis*, followed by progressive restenosis and regurgitation. In 2024, after clinical recovery from infection and CT/TEE confirmation of no active endocarditis, he underwent a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation — a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV — with an Acurate neo2. At 1 year, he remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function.

That’s the headline. The real lesson is bigger: once endocarditis is treated, the reintervention question shifts away from microbiology and toward anatomy, hemodynamics, and procedural feasibility.

## The dilemma: guidelines versus reality

The guideline instinct is straightforward: prosthetic valve endocarditis is a surgical disease, especially when there’s heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or destructive complications. That’s still the default for active PVE.

But this case lives in the gray zone that many clinicians actually face:

- the infection was treated
- follow-up blood cultures were negative
- transesophageal echo showed no active endocarditis
- multislice CT showed no paravalvular complications
- the patient remained high surgical risk
- the valve was now failing again, with severe stenosis and regurgitation

At that point, the management problem is no longer “infected or not infected?” It becomes: can you safely and effectively reintervene transcatheterly in a multiply layered aortic root?

## What counts as “infection resolved” enough to proceed?

There is no single magic test that proves endocarditis is cured. In practice, you’re looking for a converging set of reassuring findings.

In this case, the team had:

- completion of targeted antimicrobial therapy for *E. faecalis* PVE
- no ongoing suppressive antibiotics
- repeat blood cultures without persistent bacteremia
- TEE without vegetations or paravalvular complications
- MSCT without evidence of active endocarditis
- clinical improvement and no ongoing sepsis physiology

That’s not perfect certainty — medicine rarely gives that — but it is enough to move the question from “treat infection” to “treat residual valve failure.”

Clinical pearl: in post-PVE valve dysfunction, you do not need a sterilized spreadsheet. You need a multidisciplinary conviction that active infection is no longer present.

## Anatomy becomes the enemy

Once infection is off the table, the next problem is the hardware.

This patient had:

- a first transcatheter valve: Lotus
- a second transcatheter valve: Evolut
- severe narrowing of the residual lumen on CT: minimum lumen diameter 13.7 mm
- complex stent-frame overlap
- known coronary artery disease, making future coronary access a real concern

That combination raises the usual TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV alarms:

- coronary obstruction risk
- reduced effective orifice area
- severe prosthesis-patient mismatch
- difficult commissural alignment
- uncertain future coronary reaccess

This is where CT planning is not optional. It is the procedure.

## What CT was doing here

Preprocedural CT was used to answer the questions that matter most:

- Where are the coronary ostia?
- How tall are the sinuses?
- What is the valve-to-coronary distance?
- How will the new frame interact with the old frames?
- Can a high implant preserve coronary flow and maximize residual orifice area?

In this case, CT supported the decision to proceed without coronary protection. That’s not casual optimism — that’s anatomy-based risk assessment.

Clinical pearl: in complex redo TAVI, CT is not just for sizing. It is your map for coronary risk, implantation depth, and future access.

## The procedural strategy: high, aligned, and deliberate

The procedure itself was very much a strategy-driven intervention.

The team used:

- local anesthesia
- left radial and left femoral arterial access
- balloon pre-dilation under rapid pacing
- implantation of a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve
- post-dilation to optimize expansion

The key technical idea was high implantation, aligned to the Evolut leaflets. Why does that matter?

Because in a triple-stented aortic root, you are trying to do several things at once:

1. prevent further inward collapse of the effective orifice
2. avoid creating an even worse neo-skirt below the coronaries
3. preserve coronary flow
4. maintain future coronary access
5. keep hemodynamics acceptable despite the layered frames

This is not just “putting a valve in a valve in a valve.” It’s architecture.

## Why the Acurate neo2?

The authors chose the Acurate neo2 for reasons that make sense in this anatomy:

- supra-annular leaflet position
- relatively low frame height
- favorable hemodynamics in narrow spaces
- support for commissural alignment

In a tight, layered annulus, those features matter. The goal was not simply to deploy a device, but to maximize effective orifice area and reduce the chance of catastrophic patient-prosthesis mismatch.

That said, even with a technically successful implant, the immediate post-procedure gradient was still high. That is a reminder that in layered valves, “successful deployment” and “good hemodynamics” are not always the same thing.

## The gradient story: don’t panic too early

Immediately after implantation, the patient had:

- no aortic regurgitation
- mean gradient of 67 mm Hg
- AVA 0.9 cm²
- DVI 0.24

That looks terrible on paper. But at 1 month, the gradient improved to 27 mm Hg, AVA increased to 1.1 cm², and LV remodeling was seen. By 1 year, the gradient was 26 mm Hg with EF 65% and no regurgitation.

What happened?

The authors suggest a combination of:

- optimized commissural alignment
- effective post-dilation
- partial expansion of the prior Evolut frame
- improved leaflet motion and central flow dynamics

Clinical pearl: in complex transcatheter redo procedures, the immediate post-implant gradient may not be the final gradient. Follow-up imaging matters.

## What this case teaches about lifetime valve management

This case is a warning and a preview.

It shows how transcatheter aortic valve therapy is increasingly a lifetime disease-management strategy, not a one-and-done intervention. Every redo adds layers of complexity:

- frame upon frame
- coronary access becomes harder
- residual orifice gets smaller
- future options narrow
- imaging becomes more critical

The earlier the lifetime plan is considered, the better. That includes the first TAVI, not just the third.

## Take-home points

- Treated prosthetic valve endocarditis does not automatically exclude later transcatheter reintervention.
- Before reintervention, you need convincing evidence that infection is inactive: clinical improvement, negative cultures, TEE, and CT findings all matter.
- Once infection is controlled, the decision shifts from microbiology to anatomy, hemodynamics, and surgical risk.
- In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, CT planning is essential for coronary risk assessment, implantation depth, and future coronary access.
- High implantation and commissural alignment can be key tools to preserve valve area and coronary flow in layered prostheses.
- Immediate post-procedure gradients may overstate the final hemodynamic result; serial follow-up is crucial.
- Lifetime planning should start with the first valve, because each redo gets harder.

## Bottom line

The classic reflex is to say prosthetic valve endocarditis means surgery. That’s true when infection is active or uncontrolled. But once infection has been treated and there’s no evidence of ongoing disease, the question changes.

In this patient, a third TAVI after treated PVE was feasible because the team did the hard part first: they proved the infection was over, then used CT-guided planning to solve the anatomy problem.

That’s the real lesson here — after endocarditis, you can sometimes go back in, but only if you’ve earned the right to do so.



# Beating Prosthesis-Patient Mismatch in a Valve-in-Valve-in-Valve Procedure

## Why this case is worth your attention

Layering one transcatheter aortic valve inside another is already a geometric compromise. Layering **three** of them is where anatomy starts to fight back.

This case is a great reminder that in repeat transcatheter valve procedures, the problem is not just “can we get a valve in?” but “can we get a valve in without creating a tiny, high-gradient, patient-prosthesis-mismatched cul-de-sac?” In this patient, the team had to balance three competing goals:

1. restore valve function after severe degeneration,
2. avoid coronary obstruction and preserve future access, and
3. minimize the hemodynamic penalty of multiple stacked frames.

The result was a carefully planned **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** using CT-guided strategy, high implantation, and post-dilation — with a striking lesson: an immediately high gradient does not always represent the final hemodynamic story.

## The clinical setup: why this wasn’t a routine redo-TAVI

The patient was a man in his late 70s with extensive cardiovascular disease, including:

- prior multivessel PCI
- pacemaker dependence for complete AV block
- hypertension, diabetes, and COPD
- recurrent GI bleeding
- prior transcatheter aortic valve procedures
- prior treated prosthetic valve endocarditis

He had first received a **27 mm Lotus valve** in 2014. By 2022, that prosthesis had developed severe stenosis with worsening symptoms and a mean gradient up to **83 mm Hg** and EF down to **42%**. He then underwent a **TAV-in-TAV** with a **23 mm Evolut** valve.

That helped initially, but then things got more complicated. Within months he developed **Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis**, was treated with a full course of antibiotics, and later returned with recurrent severe prosthetic dysfunction: severe stenosis plus regurgitation, but with no evidence of active infection on blood cultures, TEE, or CT.

By the time of the third intervention, the anatomy was essentially a rigid, layered valve cage with a severely narrowed effective orifice.

## The hemodynamic problem: prosthesis-patient mismatch at the third layer

This is the key concept in the case.

Each additional transcatheter valve reduces the available internal diameter. In a triple-stack configuration, the issue is not just the current valve leaflets — it’s the cumulative effect of:

- the prior valve frames
- constrained leaflet opening
- reduced inflow and outflow area
- altered flow vectors
- potential leaflet pinning or inward folding
- limited room for expansion

That combination creates a perfect setup for **severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM)**, especially if the new valve is implanted too low or if the frame remains underexpanded.

In this case, preprocedural MSCT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm** in the Evolut prosthesis, underscoring just how tight the functional orifice had become.

## Why CT planning mattered so much

When you are dealing with multiple overlapping frames, CT is not a luxury — it is the roadmap.

The team used MSCT to assess:

- coronary ostial height
- sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- valve-to-coronary distance
- frame interaction between the prior prostheses
- feasibility of a high implant strategy
- anticipated relationship between the neo-skirt and coronary ostia

This matters because the third valve needs to solve one problem without creating another. In particular, a new valve can worsen coronary access or even obstruct the coronaries if the neo-skirt and stacked frames encroach too much on the sinuses.

Here, CT suggested the intervention could be done **without coronary protection**, but only because the anatomy and planned implant position were carefully analyzed in advance.

## Why a supra-annular self-expanding valve was chosen

The third prosthesis was an **Acurate neo2**, size S.

That choice is very on-theme for this case. In narrow, layered valve anatomies, a valve with **supra-annular leaflet positioning** is attractive because it can offer a larger effective orifice area than some alternative designs. In plain terms: if you’re working in a tight cylinder, you want as much of the valve’s functional opening as possible to sit above the most constricted part of the cage.

The Acurate neo2 was also selected for:

- relatively low frame height
- favorable hemodynamics in small/narrow anatomies
- support for commissural alignment
- the ability to pursue a high implant strategy

This wasn’t about elegance for its own sake. It was about preserving flow through a very constrained system.

## The procedural strategy: high implantation at the prior leaflet plane

One of the most important technical decisions was **where** to implant the third valve.

The team intentionally aligned the new valve with the **Evolut leaflets**, placing it high enough to maximize the effective orifice area and reduce further encroachment into the left ventricular outflow tract. The rationale was clever:

- high implantation helps avoid burying the valve too deep in the already narrowed zone
- it can reduce inward leaflet folding of the prior prosthesis
- it helps preserve coronary flow by limiting neo-skirt extension below the ostia
- it may improve future coronary access

This is the kind of planning that separates “successful deployment” from “useful hemodynamics.”

## Ballooning wasn’t optional here

Before and after valve implantation, the team performed balloon intervention:

- **pre-dilation** with a 22/40 mm balloon
- **post-dilation** with a 20/40 mm balloon

In a triple-valve anatomy, post-dilation is not just about cosmetic expansion. It may improve:

- frame expansion
- leaflet mobility
- central flow dynamics
- final valve geometry
- functional orifice area

Importantly, there was no reported intentional frame fracture here. But the authors note that **high-pressure post-dilation likely contributed to partial expansion of the prior Evolut frame**, which may have improved the final hemodynamics.

That matters because stacked valves are not simply passive scaffolds. If you can coax the prior frame into a slightly larger radial profile, you may gain meaningful forward flow.

## The surprising part: the immediate gradient looked bad

This is where the case gets especially interesting.

Immediately after the third valve was implanted, TTE showed:

- no aortic regurgitation
- good leaflet motion
- but a **mean gradient of 67 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.9 cm²**
- indexed AVA **0.4 cm²/m²**
- DVI **0.24**

By conventional standards, that looks terrible. Severe PPM, full stop.

But the story didn’t end there.

At **1 month**, the gradient had improved to **27 mm Hg**, AVA increased to **1.1 cm²**, and there was remodeling of the LV with smaller chamber volumes. At **1 year**, he remained asymptomatic with:

- mean gradient **26 mm Hg**
- AVA **1.1 cm²**
- EF **65%**
- no regurgitation
- no recurrent endocarditis

So what happened?

The likely explanation is a mix of:

- further frame expansion over time
- improved leaflet motion after post-dilation
- better flow dynamics after the device settled
- optimized commissural alignment and implant position
- reduced mechanical restriction of the prior prosthesis

The lesson is not that high gradients are fine. They are not. The lesson is that in layered transcatheter anatomy, the **immediate post-implant echo may not be the final physiologic endpoint**. Serial reassessment matters.

## What this case teaches about PPM in valve-in-valve-in-valve

This case is a reminder that PPM is not just a sizing issue — it’s an architectural issue.

When you layer valves, the effective orifice area is constrained by:

- internal stent geometry
- valve-to-valve interaction
- implant depth
- leaflet suspension level
- residual frame underexpansion
- coronary and sinus constraints that limit where you can place the valve

The practical response is to think ahead:

- choose a valve design that maximizes supra-annular flow when possible
- plan the implant height deliberately
- use CT to model the landing zone and coronary risk
- consider post-dilation to optimize expansion
- reassess hemodynamics over time rather than relying only on the immediate gradient

## A note on the endocarditis history

This patient had treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, but the third TAVI was only pursued after:

- completion of antimicrobial therapy
- negative blood cultures
- TEE without active infection
- CT without evidence of active endocarditis

That distinction matters. The team did not treat “active infection” with a transcatheter valve. They treated **structural failure after resolved infection** in a patient with prohibitive surgical risk.

## Key take-home points

- **Triple transcatheter valve layering can produce profound PPM**, even when the new valve is technically well deployed.
- **Preprocedural CT is essential** for coronary risk assessment, frame interaction analysis, and implant planning.
- **Supra-annular self-expanding valves** can be attractive in tight valve-in-valve-in-valve anatomy because they may preserve a larger functional orifice.
- **High implantation at the level of the prior leaflet plane** can help maximize effective orifice area and preserve coronary access.
- **Post-dilation may meaningfully improve final valve function** by optimizing frame expansion and leaflet mechanics.
- **An immediately high post-TAVI gradient may improve over time**, so early echo should be interpreted in the context of valve settling and serial follow-up.
- In patients with **resolved prosthetic valve endocarditis**, the decision for reintervention should be driven by infection status, anatomy, and procedural feasibility — not the prior infection alone.

## Bottom line

This case shows that even in a near-hopeless valve cylinder, thoughtful planning can still win the day. The third transcatheter valve was not just “placed” — it was **engineered** to work within a hostile anatomy.

For the structural heart team, the big message is simple: in valve-in-valve-in-valve procedures, success depends less on simply deploying another valve and more on **how much functional geometry you can recover** from the space you have left.



# Commissural Alignment Is No Longer Optional: Lifetime Planning Starts with the First TAVI

## A third valve in a triple-stented aorta — and a reminder that today’s implant affects tomorrow’s options

This case is remarkable not just because a third transcatheter aortic valve was successfully implanted, but because it forces us to think differently about TAVI. The question is no longer simply, “Can we treat the stenotic valve now?” It is increasingly, “What will this valve frame do to the patient’s coronary access, redo options, and future lifetime management?”

Here, a man in his late 70s had already lived through two prior transcatheter aortic valve implantations, treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, and then progressive degeneration of the second transcatheter valve. A third transcatheter valve was ultimately implanted successfully after careful CT-guided planning. At 1 year, he remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function. That outcome is impressive. But the deeper lesson is even more important: as TAVI expands and patients live longer after their first intervention, procedural strategy must be designed with future reintervention in mind.

## Why this case matters

For years, TAVI was often framed as a “one-and-done” solution for severe aortic stenosis in high-risk patients. That era is over.

We now see:
- younger and lower-risk patients receiving TAVI
- longer survival after the index procedure
- more structural valve degeneration over time
- more TAV-in-TAV and now TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios
- more concern about coronary obstruction and future coronary access

This case captures that evolution perfectly. The patient’s valve journey started with a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014, then progressed to a 23 mm Evolut TAV-in-TAV in 2022, and finally to an Acurate neo2 in 2024 after treated enterococcal prosthetic valve endocarditis and recurrent severe prosthetic dysfunction.

The key point: the third procedure was feasible only because the team treated this as a lifetime structural heart problem, not a single-procedure problem.

## The anatomy of future failure

Once you stack transcatheter valves, the technical issues multiply fast.

The main hazards are familiar, but they become more consequential with each layer:

- **Coronary obstruction**
- **Reduced effective orifice area**
- **Severe prosthesis-patient mismatch**
- **Coronary re-access difficulty**
- **Complex frame interactions**
- **Unpredictable leaflet and skirt behavior**

In this patient, CT showed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm inside the Evolut prosthesis. That is a tiny functional space to work with, and it helps explain why repeat intervention required meticulous planning.

The team evaluated:
- coronary ostial height
- sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- valve-to-coronary relationship
- interaction between the existing valve frames
- anticipated neo-skirt position
- feasibility of a high implantation strategy

That’s the real lesson: CT is not just for “can I do it?” It is for “how do I do it without burning future bridges?”

## Commissural alignment: no longer a nice extra

Commissural alignment used to feel like an advanced nuance. In complex redo TAVI, it is becoming a core procedural goal.

Why? Because misalignment can:
- obstruct coronary access
- make future PCI more difficult or impossible
- worsen frame overlap in redo settings
- reduce the ability to optimize leaflet opening and flow
- complicate any later TAV-in-TAV or surgical bailout

In this case, the third valve was deliberately aligned with the Evolut leaflets based on CT modeling. The intent was to maximize effective orifice area and preserve coronary access. That is exactly the mindset we need to adopt earlier in the disease course, not only when patients are already on their second or third device.

If you place the first valve carelessly, you may be making tomorrow’s coronary angiogram significantly harder.

## High implantation and supra-annular design: why they mattered here

The procedural strategy was not random. The team used a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve and intentionally implanted it high, at the level of the Evolut leaflets.

That strategy served several purposes:
- it helped fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame
- it reduced further inward leaflet folding
- it limited neo-skirt burden below the coronaries
- it supported future coronary flow and access
- it improved the final hemodynamic result in a very narrow anatomy

The choice of a supra-annular valve with relatively low frame height was also logical in a triple-stented aorta. In narrow, stacked anatomies, every millimeter matters.

After balloon pre-dilation and post-dilation, the final angiogram showed preserved coronary perfusion and no regurgitation. The immediate echo, however, still showed severe prosthesis-patient mismatch with a high gradient — a reminder that “technical success” and “perfect hemodynamics” are not always the same thing in layered-valve anatomy.

Even so, gradients improved over time, with the mean gradient falling to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and remaining stable at 1 year. The patient stayed asymptomatic and active.

## What about the prior endocarditis?

This is an important nuance.

Guidelines still favor surgery for prosthetic valve endocarditis, especially when infection is uncontrolled or heart failure is present. But this patient had completed targeted therapy for Enterococcus faecalis, had no clinical or imaging evidence of active infection, and had negative repeat evaluation with TEE and MSCT.

So the decision to reintervene was not “TAVI in active endocarditis.” It was TAVI after infection resolution, in a patient with:
- prohibitive surgical risk
- severe prosthetic dysfunction
- complex prior valve frames
- very limited surgical options
- favorable CT feasibility for another transcatheter approach

That distinction matters. This case should not be overgeneralized as support for transcatheter treatment of active PVE. It is better understood as a carefully selected redo strategy after infection was treated and excluded.

## The broader lifetime-management lesson

This is the part that should change practice.

When you implant the first TAVI, you are not just treating today’s stenosis. You are setting the geometry for:
- future valve degeneration
- possible redo TAVI
- coronary angiography and PCI
- potential future surgical explant
- rescue strategies if the patient develops endocarditis or thrombosis

That means the first procedure should be planned with:
- valve choice
- frame height
- coronary clearance
- commissural position
- potential future coronary access
- likely redo trajectory

This is especially relevant for:
- younger patients
- patients with known CAD
- those likely to need future PCI
- patients expected to outlive their first valve
- anyone in whom lifetime valve strategy is foreseeable from day one

The central idea is simple: **the first TAVI should not create an avoidable future problem.**

## Practical pearls from this case

A few take-home principles stand out:

- **CT-guided planning is not optional in complex redo TAVI.**  
  It is essential for assessing coronary risk, frame interaction, and implantation strategy.

- **Commissural alignment should be considered at the index procedure.**  
  It may preserve coronary access and reduce future procedural complexity.

- **Valve choice should reflect lifetime anatomy, not just immediate hemodynamics.**  
  Frame height, skirt design, and supra-annular performance all matter.

- **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is possible, but only in selected patients.**  
  You need favorable anatomy, no active infection, and a realistic pathway to safe coronary flow.

- **Prior endocarditis does not automatically rule out redo transcatheter intervention.**  
  But active infection must be excluded carefully and repeatedly.

- **Future coronary access is a real clinical endpoint.**  
  Not an abstract technical concern.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that structural heart interventions now live in a lifetime-management world. As TAVI expands and patients survive longer, the consequences of valve choice, commissural orientation, and frame geometry become more important with every subsequent procedure.

Commissural alignment is no longer a “nice-to-have.” In many patients, it is a prerequisite for preserving future options.

## Take-home points

- TAVI planning must increasingly be framed as **lifetime valve management**, not single-procedure treatment.
- **Commissural alignment matters now** because it affects future coronary access and redo feasibility.
- **CT-based planning** is critical for TAV-in-TAV and especially TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.
- In carefully selected patients with treated endocarditis and prohibitive surgical risk, **a third transcatheter valve can be feasible**.
- The first valve you implant can make the next intervention easier — or much harder.



# The Echo Trap After Complex Redo TAVI: Interpreting Early High Gradients Without Panicking

## The surprising part of this case

A patient undergoes a technically successful third transcatheter aortic valve implantation — no regurgitation, good leaflet motion, coronary flow preserved — and then the immediate post-procedure echo shows a **mean gradient of 67 mm Hg**.

That is the kind of result that can make everyone in the room tense up. Did we fail? Is the valve too small? Is there severe patient-prosthesis mismatch? Should we be thinking about urgent reintervention?

In this case, the answer was: **not so fast**.

By 1 month, the mean gradient fell to **27 mm Hg**, the valve area improved, and by 1 year the patient remained asymptomatic with a stable mean gradient of **26 mm Hg** and preserved EF. In other words, the immediate post-op echo looked alarming, but the longer arc of follow-up told a very different story.

This is the key lesson: in complex redo TAVI, the first postprocedural echo can be an **echo trap** if interpreted in isolation.

## Why immediate gradients can be misleading

After transcatheter valve-in-valve procedures — especially when you are stacking valves inside valves inside valves — the hemodynamics do not always declare themselves fully on day 0. Several things can make the immediate gradient look worse than it will later:

- **Incomplete frame expansion early on**
- **Residual constraint from prior valve frames**
- **Altered flow dynamics in a narrow, layered prosthetic tunnel**
- **Suboptimal leaflet opening until the new frame settles or expands further**
- **Transient hemodynamic conditions after the procedure**
- **Measurement variability on early transthoracic echocardiography**

In this case, the patient had a triple transcatheter construct and severe pre-existing narrowing. Preprocedural CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm** in the Evolut prosthesis. That is a very tight landing zone. Even after a technically successful implant, the early Doppler gradient may reflect the geometry of the whole system, not simply the new valve leaflet function.

Importantly, the immediate post-procedure echo showed:

- Mobile leaflets
- No aortic regurgitation
- DVI 0.24
- AVA 0.9 cm²

That pattern is not the same as a catastrophic procedural failure. It is more consistent with a valve that is functioning, but within a constrained anatomy that may continue to evolve after implantation and post-dilation.

## Residual obstruction vs evolving expansion

This is the practical question: when you see a high gradient after redo TAVI, is it fixed obstruction or a dynamic situation that may improve?

### Clues favoring fixed residual obstruction
Think more carefully about a structural problem if you see:

- Persistent severe symptoms
- No improvement in leaflet motion
- Significant transvalvular or paravalvular regurgitation
- Evidence of malposition or underexpansion that is clearly not physiologic
- Correlation with CT showing severe crowding or malalignment
- Coronary compromise or other procedural complication

### Clues favoring evolving improvement
Be more patient when you have:

- Good leaflet mobility on echo
- No regurgitation
- A technically well-positioned valve
- High-risk layered anatomy where flow dynamics are expected to be unusual
- Planned post-dilation that may continue to optimize frame expansion
- Hemodynamic improvement over serial studies

That’s exactly what happened here. The team performed **post-dilation**, and the valve was intentionally implanted with attention to **high positioning and commissural alignment** to preserve coronary flow and optimize the effective orifice area. The 1-month echo then showed a meaningful drop in gradient from **67 to 27 mm Hg**, with stable findings thereafter.

## Why CT mattered so much

This case is a reminder that in redo TAVI, **echo answers the question “how is it working?” but CT helps answer “why is it working this way?”**

Preprocedural MSCT was used to evaluate:

- Coronary ostial height
- Sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- Valve-to-coronary distance
- Interaction between the multiple valve frames
- The anticipated relationship between the neo-skirt and coronary ostia
- Feasibility of a high implant strategy

That imaging guided a very deliberate plan: implant the third valve in a way that would **maximize the effective orifice area**, avoid coronary obstruction, and preserve future coronary access as much as possible.

In other words, the high early gradient was not interpreted in a vacuum. It was interpreted in the context of a highly constrained anatomy that had already been carefully mapped out.

## The role of serial echocardiography

This case argues strongly for **trend-based follow-up**, not knee-jerk reaction to a single early study.

A reasonable follow-up framework after complex redo TAVI is:

- **Immediate post-procedure TTE**  
  Useful for confirming valve position, regurgitation, gross leaflet motion, and complications. But gradients can be noisy here.

- **Early repeat TTE at about 1 month**  
  Often much more informative for the “true” early hemodynamic result.

- **Ongoing clinical and echocardiographic follow-up**  
  Needed to confirm stability, especially in layered prostheses where late degeneration or recurrent obstruction may emerge.

In this case, the 1-month study was the pivot point. It showed that the scary early number was not the final story. The patient also remained asymptomatic with good exercise tolerance, which matters. Hemodynamics and symptoms should be interpreted together.

## A few clinical pearls from this case

The most important pearl is simple: **a high immediate gradient after complex TAVI does not automatically equal procedural failure**.

Other take-home points:

- In layered TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV anatomy, the valve system behaves differently from a first-time implant.
- Immediate post-op gradients may improve as the frame fully expands and flow dynamics stabilize.
- Serial echo is often more useful than a single early measurement.
- CT-based planning is essential for understanding coronary risk, frame interaction, and achievable effective orifice area.
- Symptoms matter. A patient who is clinically improving and has a well-seated valve with no regurgitation deserves cautious observation, not reflexive reintervention.
- High implantation and post-dilation can be purposeful strategies to preserve coronary access and improve hemodynamics in complex redo cases.

## Why this matters beyond one patient

As TAVI patients live longer, more of us will encounter these “what do we do with this gradient?” moments. The challenge is not only technical — it’s interpretive. We need to get comfortable with the idea that after complex transcatheter reintervention, the immediate echo may represent a **starting point**, not a final verdict.

This case shows that with careful CT planning, thoughtful valve choice, targeted implantation strategy, and structured follow-up, even a third transcatheter valve after treated endocarditis can have a durable and clinically meaningful result.

## Bottom line

The main trap here is overreacting to the first Doppler number.

In complex redo TAVI, especially TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, a very high immediate gradient can improve substantially over time. If the valve is well seated, leaflets are mobile, regurgitation is absent, coronary flow is preserved, and the patient is clinically improving, serial echocardiography may be the safest and smartest path.

## Key take-home points

- **Don’t judge complex redo TAVI by the immediate gradient alone.**
- **High early gradients may reflect constrained anatomy and evolving valve expansion.**
- **CT planning is critical for assessing coronary risk and achievable hemodynamics.**
- **Repeat echo at 1 month can be far more informative than day 0.**
- **Clinical stability should temper the urge for premature reintervention.**
