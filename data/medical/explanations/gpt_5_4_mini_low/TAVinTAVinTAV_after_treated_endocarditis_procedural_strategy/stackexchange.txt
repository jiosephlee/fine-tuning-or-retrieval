Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient with prior TAVI who presents with dyspnoea, dizziness, and fatigue, what echocardiographic findings in this case established severe prosthetic aortic valve dysfunction before the third intervention?

Before the third intervention, transthoracic echocardiography showed **severe prosthetic aortic valve dysfunction** by demonstrating:

- **Markedly elevated transvalvular obstruction**: a **mean gradient of 49 mm Hg**
- **Severely reduced valve opening area**: **aortic valve area 0.5 cm²** by the continuity equation
- **Significant regurgitation**: **grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation**
- Visualization of **two overlapping transcatheter bioprosthetic valves**, confirming the complex redo anatomy

Taken together, these findings established **combined severe prosthetic stenosis and regurgitation**, consistent with failure of the prior transcatheter aortic valve and prompting the third TAVI.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the major causes of failure of a transcatheter aortic bioprosthesis, and how did this patient’s prior Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis likely contribute to later restenosis and regurgitation?

The major causes of failure of a transcatheter aortic bioprosthesis are the same broad mechanisms that affect surgical bioprostheses, but they are often amplified by the constrained geometry of the transcatheter frame. The two principal categories are:

1. **Structural valve deterioration** — progressive leaflet fibrosis, calcification, tearing, and stiffening, leading to restenosis, regurgitation, or both.  
2. **Nonstructural dysfunction** — patient–prosthesis mismatch, malexpansion, frame deformation, pannus, or leaflet immobility from thrombus or external constraint.  
3. **Infection** — prosthetic valve endocarditis can directly damage leaflets, create vegetations, perforation, abscess, or paravalvular leakage.  
4. **Thrombosis or pannus-related obstruction** — less common, but it can present with rising gradients and restricted leaflet motion.  
5. **Endocarditis-related sequelae and healing after infection** — even when active infection resolves, the valve may be left with scarred, thickened, mechanically restricted leaflets.

In this patient, the later **restenosis and regurgitation were likely multifactorial**, but his prior **Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis** probably played an important role. The infection occurred after the TAV-in-TAV and was followed by recurrent prosthetic dysfunction. Even though blood cultures cleared and subsequent TEE/MSCT showed no active infection, endocarditis can leave behind **permanent leaflet injury**: inflammatory destruction, fibrosis, and retraction or stiffness of the transcatheter leaflets. That kind of postinfectious remodeling can cause **both obstruction and incompetence**—that is, progressive restenosis from restricted leaflet opening and intraprosthetic regurgitation from poor coaptation or leaflet damage.

So, in this case, the later valve failure was not simply recurrent infection. Rather, the episode of **E. faecalis PVE likely accelerated leaflet injury and mechanical restriction in a valve already prone to degeneration**, contributing to the combination of severe gradient elevation and moderate-to-severe regurgitation that ultimately required a third transcatheter intervention.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with recurrent dysfunction after two prior transcatheter aortic valves, what differential diagnosis should be considered for elevated transvalvular gradients and new regurgitation, and which features in this case argued against active infective endocarditis at the time of the third procedure?

In a patient with recurrent dysfunction after two prior transcatheter aortic valves, the differential diagnosis for **elevated transvalvular gradients with new regurgitation** should be broad. The major considerations are **structural valve deterioration** of one or more prostheses, **patient–prosthesis mismatch** from progressively smaller effective orifice area in a layered valve stack, **leaflet thrombosis**, **prosthesis malexpansion or underexpansion** related to frame interaction, **pannus or fibrotic ingrowth**, and **prosthetic valve endocarditis** with leaflet destruction or paravalvular complications. In a triple-valve anatomy, these mechanisms can overlap, so imaging and clinical context are essential.

In this case, several features argued **against active infective endocarditis at the time of the third procedure**. He had **completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy** for prior *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis, had **no ongoing suppressive antibiotics**, and on reassessment had **no clinical evidence of infection**. Importantly, **repeat blood cultures were negative**, and both **transesophageal echocardiography and multislice CT showed no vegetations, abscess, or paravalvular complications**. Thus, the recurrent stenosis and regurgitation were interpreted as **post-infectious structural dysfunction and hemodynamic failure of the prostheses**, not ongoing infection, which is why the team proceeded with reintervention rather than treating presumed active endocarditis.

### [Morning Report] Why is preprocedural CT especially important in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV planning, and which specific anatomic measurements and relationships were used in this case to assess feasibility and the risk of coronary obstruction?

Preprocedural CT is especially important in **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** because the usual geometric constraints are amplified by the presence of **three overlapping valve frames**. With each added prosthesis, the risks of **coronary obstruction, impaired future coronary access, and severe patient–prosthesis mismatch** increase, while fluoroscopy and echocardiography alone cannot reliably define the three-dimensional relationships that determine feasibility. In this setting, CT is the key modality for deciding whether a third valve can be implanted safely, where it should be deployed, and whether coronary protection is needed.

In this case, CT was used to assess several specific anatomic factors:

- **Coronary ostial height**
- **Sinus of Valsalva dimensions**
- **Valve-to-coronary distance**
- **Interaction between the frames of the previously implanted transcatheter valves**
- The anticipated relationship between the **neo-skirt** of the new valve and the **coronary ostia**
- Feasibility of **high implantation** to preserve coronary flow and future coronary access

CT also demonstrated the **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis**, confirming severe narrowing of the functional orifice and helping guide valve choice and implantation strategy.

So, the practical lesson from this case is that in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV planning, CT is not just confirmatory imaging—it is the study that determines whether the procedure is anatomically possible and whether the coronary arteries can be protected by careful valve selection and high deployment.

### [Management] What factors made this patient a poor candidate for surgical explantation and redo valve surgery, despite guideline preference for surgery in many cases of prosthetic valve endocarditis?

Although surgery is generally preferred for prosthetic valve endocarditis when there is active infection, heart failure, or uncontrolled complications, this patient had several features that made surgical explantation and redo valve surgery a poor option.

First, the endocarditis had been treated and was no longer active. He completed a full antibiotic course, and repeat blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and CT showed no vegetations, abscess, paravalvular leak, or other signs of ongoing infection. So the main indication for surgery was no longer infection control, but structural valve dysfunction.

Second, his operative risk was prohibitive. He was in his late 70s with a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, and he had major comorbidities: COPD, type 2 diabetes, extensive three-vessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior PCIs, a pacemaker, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. These factors substantially increased perioperative mortality and bleeding risk.

Third, the anatomy made surgery especially hazardous. He had two previously implanted transcatheter valves with deep fixation and extensive stent integration into the aortic root/ascending aorta. Explantation would likely have required complex root and ascending aortic replacement rather than simple valve surgery, with major technical difficulty and high risk of injury to the surrounding structures.

So, despite the general guideline preference for surgery in prosthetic valve endocarditis, this was a patient in whom infection had resolved and the combination of prohibitive surgical risk, frailty/comorbidity burden, and hostile redo anatomy made a transcatheter strategy the more reasonable choice.

### [Management] Why was a self-expanding Acurate neo2 chosen as the third prosthesis in this case, and what design features made it attractive in the setting of a narrow, triple-stented aortic valve anatomy?

The Acurate neo2 was chosen as the third prosthesis because the team needed a valve that could deliver the best possible hemodynamics in a severely constrained, triple-stented aortic root while also preserving coronary flow and future coronary access. In this patient, CT showed a very narrow residual lumen within the Evolut frame, so any additional transcatheter valve risked worsening patient-prosthesis mismatch and creating an even more obstructive neo-skirt. The Acurate neo2 was attractive because it could be implanted high and aligned with the prior Evolut leaflets, helping to pin the old leaflets outward against the frame, preserve the effective orifice area, and avoid further compromise of the coronary ostia.

Its design features were particularly useful in this anatomy: it is a self-expanding valve with supra-annular leaflet position, which favors a larger functional orifice in a small annulus or constrained landing zone; it has a relatively low frame height, which helps limit interaction with the coronaries; and it supports commissural alignment, an important advantage when future coronary reaccess may be needed. In short, the device was selected not just for deliverability, but because its geometry offered the best chance of acceptable gradients, coronary preservation, and workable lifetime valve management in a very narrow triple-valve configuration.

### [Pathophysiology] What was the rationale for high implantation of the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets, and how can this strategy improve effective orifice area while helping preserve coronary flow and future coronary access?

The rationale for **high implantation at the level of the Evolut leaflets** was to use the third valve to “pin” the already-implanted Evolut leaflets outward against its stent frame rather than allowing them to fold inward and further narrow the central channel. In a triple-valve stack, the main hemodynamic problem is progressive loss of luminal area from overlapping frames and inward leaflet displacement, which worsens **patient-prosthesis mismatch** and increases transvalvular gradients. By seating the Acurate neo2 high, at the level of the Evolut leaflets, the operators aimed to **maximize the residual effective orifice area** and improve forward flow.

This strategy also had an anatomic advantage: a higher implant position helps keep the **neo-skirt** and bulky leaflet material away from the coronary ostia. That lowers the risk of coronary obstruction and, importantly in this patient with extensive coronary disease, helps preserve **future coronary access** by avoiding a deeper, more obstructive valve position. In short, high implantation was chosen to optimize hemodynamics while minimizing the coronary penalty of a third transcatheter frame.

### [Morning Report] Immediately after the third TAVI, the mean gradient remained high at 67 mm Hg with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² and DVI of 0.24. How would you interpret these findings, and what explains the subsequent improvement in haemodynamics over the following month?

Immediately after the third TAVI, those Doppler findings should be interpreted as **severe residual prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) / functional prosthetic stenosis**, not as recurrent infection or an obviously failed implant. A mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m², and DVI of 0.24 indicate that, despite apparently good leaflet motion and no regurgitation, the **effective orifice was still critically small** in this triple-layered valve anatomy. In this case, the most likely mechanism was **marked constraint of the new valve within two prior transcatheter frames**, with incomplete immediate expansion of the prosthesis and a very limited residual lumen.

The subsequent improvement over the following month is explained by a combination of factors described in the report: **continued functional expansion of the self-expanding valve and the pre-existing Evolut frame after deployment, benefit from post-dilation, and optimization of leaflet geometry/commissural alignment**, which together increased the effective orifice area and lowered the gradient. In other words, the early postprocedural study reflected a temporarily “tight” triple-valve configuration, whereas the 1-month echocardiogram captured the **settled hemodynamic result after further frame expansion and remodeling**, with the mean gradient falling from 67 to 27 mm Hg and AVA increasing accordingly.
