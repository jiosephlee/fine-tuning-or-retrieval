Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 5: ## Outcomes, Interpretation of Post-Procedural Haemodynamics, and Clinical Lessons for Lifetime Valve Management

The immediate result of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation was judged using Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) concepts, which focus on procedural success, residual valve dysfunction, and complications. In this case, the new valve was deployed in the intended position, there was no residual aortic regurgitation, and coronary perfusion was preserved, which all support technical success. However, the early transthoracic echocardiogram also showed a very high mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg, a calculated aortic valve area of 0.9 cm², and a low Doppler velocity index of 0.24. These findings indicated severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM), meaning that although the valve was functioning and not leaking, the effective opening was still too small for the patient’s body size and flow requirements. In complex valve-in-valve or valve-in-valve-in-valve procedures, this is a key point: the absence of regurgitation does not necessarily mean optimal haemodynamics.

Early post-implant gradients after repeat TAVI can be difficult to interpret. Immediately after implantation, flow conditions may be affected by incomplete frame expansion, interaction between multiple valve frames, residual leaflet restriction, and transient changes in loading conditions. In this case, the unexpectedly high gradient did not represent procedural failure, but rather a haemodynamic limitation that improved over time. Several mechanisms likely contributed to that improvement. High-pressure post-dilation probably enhanced frame expansion, the self-expanding prosthesis may have continued to settle and open more fully after the procedure, and the altered geometry of the prior valve layers may have gradually permitted better leaflet motion and more central flow. High implantation strategy and commissural alignment may also have helped reduce functional obstruction and preserve flow through the valve.

This was reflected at the 1-month follow-up, when echocardiography showed a lower mean gradient of 27 mm Hg and a larger valve area of 1.1 cm². The left ventricle also showed reverse remodelling, with a reduction in chamber volumes, suggesting that the improved valve function translated into better haemodynamic loading conditions. Importantly, there was no residual regurgitation. The valve remained in the moderately stenotic range, but the overall trend was clearly favorable, showing that the early high gradient had not prevented meaningful functional improvement.

Clinically, the patient’s course after the procedure was reassuring. At intermediate follow-up, he was asymptomatic, had good exercise tolerance, and showed no evidence of recurrent heart failure or infection. This is particularly important in a patient with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis, because any new decline in functional status, fever, or inflammatory syndrome would require urgent reassessment for recurrent infection or prosthetic dysfunction. In such patients, surveillance cannot rely on symptoms alone; it must also include serial echocardiography and a low threshold for repeat blood cultures and imaging if infection is suspected.

At 1 year, the outcome remained stable. The patient was still clinically well, the mean gradient remained around 26 mm Hg, valve area was preserved at 1.1 cm², and left ventricular ejection fraction was 65%. There was no recurrent endocarditis, no significant regurgitation, and no valve-related complication reported. For a patient with triple transcatheter valve layers, severe comorbidity, and prior infective endocarditis, this represents a meaningful durable benefit.

The case also highlights the broader challenge of lifetime management of structural valve disease. As patients live longer after the first TAVI, some will require repeat interventions, and the complexity of each subsequent procedure increases. Serial transcatheter valves create a layered construct that can limit residual orifice area, complicate commissural orientation, and make future coronary access more difficult. This means that planning should not focus only on the immediate procedure, but also on what may be needed years later. CT-guided assessment is central to this approach because it helps define coronary height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary relationships, and the interaction between valve frames.

If the current prosthesis were to fail in the future, options would be limited. Surgical explantation of a triple-stented aortic root would be technically difficult and very high risk. Catheter-based bailout concepts such as valve fracture or leaflet modification techniques, including BASILICA-type strategies, may theoretically be relevant, but their performance in triple-layer constructs remains uncertain. The practical lesson is that future rescue options may be constrained, so the choice made at the current intervention has implications for later years as well.

Overall, this case teaches several important lessons. First, procedural success after complex redo TAVI includes not only correct deployment but also careful haemodynamic interpretation. Second, an early high gradient does not always indicate a failed intervention, because haemodynamics may improve after post-dilation and with time. Third, patients who are likely to outlive their first valve intervention need a lifetime strategy from the outset, with CT-based planning, attention to coronary access, and anticipation of potential future reinterventions. In short, this case shows that even very complex TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV anatomy can be managed successfully when treatment is guided by imaging, procedural planning, and long-term surveillance.
