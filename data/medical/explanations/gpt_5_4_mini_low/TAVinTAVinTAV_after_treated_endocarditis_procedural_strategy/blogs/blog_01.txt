Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Triple-Deck TAVI: When a Third Transcatheter Valve Is the Least Bad Option

## Why this case grabs your attention

Most of us are used to thinking in binaries: native valve or prosthetic valve, surgery or transcatheter, first-time TAVI or redo TAVI. This case blows past those neat categories.

Here was a man in his late 70s with:
- a prior surgical-risk profile that had already pushed him into transcatheter therapy,
- two previous transcatheter aortic valves,
- treated prosthetic valve endocarditis,
- severe recurrent prosthetic dysfunction,
- and anatomy that made open surgery look less like a solution and more like a hazard.

The team ultimately performed a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** using CT-guided planning and a deliberately staged procedural strategy. At 1 year, he remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function.

That’s the interesting part: this wasn’t “can we do it?” so much as “what is the least bad option left?”

## The clinical setup

The patient had extensive cardiovascular disease:
- hypertension
- three-vessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior PCI procedures
- complete AV block with pacemaker
- type 2 diabetes
- COPD
- recurrent GI bleeding

He first received a **27 mm Lotus valve in 2014** for severe aortic stenosis. By 2022, that prosthesis had failed with severe stenosis, rising gradients, and reduced EF, so he underwent **TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut**.

That second procedure initially helped:
- EF improved from **42% to 54%**

But then came the complication that changes the whole conversation: **Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis** in September 2022, treated with a full 6-week IV ampicillin-based regimen and follow-up oral therapy per ID guidance. At the time of the third procedure, the infection had been considered resolved:
- repeat blood cultures negative
- TEE without active endocarditis
- CT without evidence of ongoing infection

By 2024, he had again deteriorated:
- severe stenosis
- moderate-to-severe regurgitation
- mean gradient **49 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.5 cm²**
- EF recovered to **66%**

In June 2024, the question was no longer whether the valve was bad. It clearly was. The question was how to fix it without killing the patient in the process.

## Why redo surgery was not the easy answer

On paper, prior prosthetic valve endocarditis and failed bioprosthesis might push you toward surgery. In real life, this patient had a long list of reasons surgery was unattractive:

- **EuroSCORE II 27.36%**
- COPD
- diabetes
- prior GI bleeding
- extensive coronary disease
- prior pacemaker
- two prior transcatheter frames deeply integrated into the aortic root

The authors note that surgical explantation would likely have required **total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement**. That is not a “redo AVR” problem anymore; that is major reconstructive surgery in a frail, comorbid patient.

This is the heart-team lesson: sometimes “standard” and “feasible” are not the same thing.

## What imaging had to answer before anyone touched the valve

For a third transcatheter valve, the preprocedure CT is not just a formality. It is the case.

The team used multislice CT to assess:
- coronary ostial height
- sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- valve-to-coronary distance
- interaction between the two existing valve frames
- the relationship of the anticipated neo-skirt to the coronaries
- feasibility of a high implant to preserve coronary flow and future access

The CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis**, confirming severe restriction and helping guide the strategy.

A few practical pearls here:
- In layered TAVI, you’re no longer planning around one valve frame; you’re planning around **multiple metal cages and their combined effect on coronary access and effective orifice area**.
- CT is being used not just to “see anatomy,” but to predict what the new valve will do to the geometry of the old ones.
- The goal is not simply to implant a valve; it is to avoid creating an unworkable coronary situation or catastrophic patient-prosthesis mismatch.

## The procedural strategy: high implant, careful alignment, and no coronary protection

The team proceeded with **local anesthesia**, **left radial and left femoral arterial access**, and a **self-expanding Acurate neo2 (size S)**.

A few procedural details matter:

- Aortography confirmed severe regurgitation.
- A Safari wire was placed into the LV.
- Balloon pre-dilation was performed with a **22/40 mm balloon** under rapid pacing at **180 bpm**.
- Simultaneous aortography confirmed preserved coronary flow.
- The Acurate neo2 was deployed with deliberate alignment to the **Evolut leaflets**, based on preprocedural CT modeling.
- Post-dilation with a **20/40 mm balloon** was performed under rapid pacing.
- Final aortography showed correct position, preserved coronary perfusion, and no residual regurgitation.

The key strategic idea was **high implantation** at the level of the Evolut leaflets. The authors explain this as a way to:
- fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame,
- reduce further inward folding of leaflets,
- limit the formation of a neo-skirt below the coronaries,
- preserve coronary flow,
- and maintain future access.

That’s elegant, and it’s also a reminder that in complex redo TAVI, the implant depth is not a technical footnote — it’s the strategy.

## Why choose the Acurate neo2?

The authors selected the **Acurate neo2** for a few practical reasons:
- supra-annular leaflet position
- relatively low frame height
- favorable hemodynamics in narrow anatomies
- support for commissural alignment

In a triple-stented anatomy, every millimeter matters. The goal was to maximize the effective orifice area while minimizing the coronary and access penalties of another frame.

## The post-procedure hemodynamics: not perfect, but acceptable

Immediately after implantation, the valve looked good on echo in terms of motion and regurgitation:
- leaflets mobile
- complete opening
- **no aortic regurgitation**

But the hemodynamics initially showed severe **patient-prosthesis mismatch**:
- mean gradient **67 mm Hg**
- AVA **0.9 cm²**
- indexed AVA **0.4 cm²/m²**
- DVI **0.24**

That’s an important reminder: technical success and hemodynamic optimization are not always the same thing right away.

By 1 month:
- mean gradient improved to **27 mm Hg**
- AVA increased to **1.1 cm²**
- LV remodeling was seen
- no residual regurgitation

At 1 year:
- mean gradient **26 mm Hg**
- AVA **1.1 cm²**
- EF **65%**
- no recurrent endocarditis
- no valve-related complications
- patient asymptomatic and active

So while the immediate postimplant gradient was concerning, the valve performance settled into a durable, clinically useful result.

## What probably happened physiologically?

The authors suggest the gradient improvement likely reflected a combination of:
- effective commissural alignment
- post-dilation
- partial expansion of the prior Evolut frame
- improved leaflet motion and central flow dynamics

No intentional frame fracture was done. But high-pressure balloon post-dilation may have contributed to further radial expansion of the Evolut.

That matters because the physiology of layered transcatheter valves is dynamic. You’re not just placing a new valve; you’re also altering the mechanics of the old ones.

## The endocarditis question: when can you reintervene?

This case is especially interesting because of the prior **prosthetic valve endocarditis**.

Current guideline thinking still generally favors surgery for PVE, especially if there is heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this case sits in the gray zone that comes up in real practice:
- infection treated
- no evidence of active endocarditis
- blood cultures negative
- TEE negative for ongoing infection
- CT without active infectious features
- surgical risk prohibitive

In other words, the infection history mattered, but it did not automatically dictate the procedure once it was resolved. The decision then became one of:
- anatomy,
- procedural feasibility,
- expected benefit,
- and patient-centered risk tolerance.

## The bigger lesson: lifetime management starts at valve #1

This case is really about longitudinal thinking.

Each transcatheter reintervention compounds the complexity:
- more frame layering
- more coronary access issues
- higher risk of residual stenosis/PPM
- more difficult future bailout options

That means planning for “what happens if this valve fails?” should begin early, not after the third intervention is already needed.

## Take-home points

- A third transcatheter aortic valve can be a reasonable off-label option when **surgery is prohibitive** and **infection has been treated and excluded as active**.
- In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, **CT planning is everything**: coronary height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, frame interaction, and implant depth all matter.
- **High implantation** may help preserve coronary flow and improve the effective orifice area in layered valve anatomy.
- Immediate postprocedure gradients may be misleading; **follow-up hemodynamics can improve** as the system settles.
- In patients with prior PVE, the decision is not “endocarditis equals no TAVI”; it’s whether there is **active infection** and whether the anatomy and risk profile make surgery genuinely viable.
- Lifetime structural heart planning matters. Every valve you implant changes the options for the next one.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that structural heart disease often lives in the land of imperfect choices. A third transcatheter valve after treated endocarditis is not routine, and it should not be treated casually. But in a patient with prohibitive surgical risk, no active infection, and CT-confirmed feasibility, it may be the least bad option — and, as shown here, it can provide durable symptomatic and hemodynamic benefit at 1 year.
