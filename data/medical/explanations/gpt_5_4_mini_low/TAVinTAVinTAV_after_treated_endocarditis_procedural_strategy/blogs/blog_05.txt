Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Commissural Alignment Is No Longer Optional: Lifetime Planning Starts with the First TAVI

## A third valve in a triple-stented aorta — and a reminder that today’s implant affects tomorrow’s options

This case is remarkable not just because a third transcatheter aortic valve was successfully implanted, but because it forces us to think differently about TAVI. The question is no longer simply, “Can we treat the stenotic valve now?” It is increasingly, “What will this valve frame do to the patient’s coronary access, redo options, and future lifetime management?”

Here, a man in his late 70s had already lived through two prior transcatheter aortic valve implantations, treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, and then progressive degeneration of the second transcatheter valve. A third transcatheter valve was ultimately implanted successfully after careful CT-guided planning. At 1 year, he remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function. That outcome is impressive. But the deeper lesson is even more important: as TAVI expands and patients live longer after their first intervention, procedural strategy must be designed with future reintervention in mind.

## Why this case matters

For years, TAVI was often framed as a “one-and-done” solution for severe aortic stenosis in high-risk patients. That era is over.

We now see:
- younger and lower-risk patients receiving TAVI
- longer survival after the index procedure
- more structural valve degeneration over time
- more TAV-in-TAV and now TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios
- more concern about coronary obstruction and future coronary access

This case captures that evolution perfectly. The patient’s valve journey started with a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014, then progressed to a 23 mm Evolut TAV-in-TAV in 2022, and finally to an Acurate neo2 in 2024 after treated enterococcal prosthetic valve endocarditis and recurrent severe prosthetic dysfunction.

The key point: the third procedure was feasible only because the team treated this as a lifetime structural heart problem, not a single-procedure problem.

## The anatomy of future failure

Once you stack transcatheter valves, the technical issues multiply fast.

The main hazards are familiar, but they become more consequential with each layer:

- **Coronary obstruction**
- **Reduced effective orifice area**
- **Severe prosthesis-patient mismatch**
- **Coronary re-access difficulty**
- **Complex frame interactions**
- **Unpredictable leaflet and skirt behavior**

In this patient, CT showed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm inside the Evolut prosthesis. That is a tiny functional space to work with, and it helps explain why repeat intervention required meticulous planning.

The team evaluated:
- coronary ostial height
- sinus of Valsalva dimensions
- valve-to-coronary relationship
- interaction between the existing valve frames
- anticipated neo-skirt position
- feasibility of a high implantation strategy

That’s the real lesson: CT is not just for “can I do it?” It is for “how do I do it without burning future bridges?”

## Commissural alignment: no longer a nice extra

Commissural alignment used to feel like an advanced nuance. In complex redo TAVI, it is becoming a core procedural goal.

Why? Because misalignment can:
- obstruct coronary access
- make future PCI more difficult or impossible
- worsen frame overlap in redo settings
- reduce the ability to optimize leaflet opening and flow
- complicate any later TAV-in-TAV or surgical bailout

In this case, the third valve was deliberately aligned with the Evolut leaflets based on CT modeling. The intent was to maximize effective orifice area and preserve coronary access. That is exactly the mindset we need to adopt earlier in the disease course, not only when patients are already on their second or third device.

If you place the first valve carelessly, you may be making tomorrow’s coronary angiogram significantly harder.

## High implantation and supra-annular design: why they mattered here

The procedural strategy was not random. The team used a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve and intentionally implanted it high, at the level of the Evolut leaflets.

That strategy served several purposes:
- it helped fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame
- it reduced further inward leaflet folding
- it limited neo-skirt burden below the coronaries
- it supported future coronary flow and access
- it improved the final hemodynamic result in a very narrow anatomy

The choice of a supra-annular valve with relatively low frame height was also logical in a triple-stented aorta. In narrow, stacked anatomies, every millimeter matters.

After balloon pre-dilation and post-dilation, the final angiogram showed preserved coronary perfusion and no regurgitation. The immediate echo, however, still showed severe prosthesis-patient mismatch with a high gradient — a reminder that “technical success” and “perfect hemodynamics” are not always the same thing in layered-valve anatomy.

Even so, gradients improved over time, with the mean gradient falling to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and remaining stable at 1 year. The patient stayed asymptomatic and active.

## What about the prior endocarditis?

This is an important nuance.

Guidelines still favor surgery for prosthetic valve endocarditis, especially when infection is uncontrolled or heart failure is present. But this patient had completed targeted therapy for Enterococcus faecalis, had no clinical or imaging evidence of active infection, and had negative repeat evaluation with TEE and MSCT.

So the decision to reintervene was not “TAVI in active endocarditis.” It was TAVI after infection resolution, in a patient with:
- prohibitive surgical risk
- severe prosthetic dysfunction
- complex prior valve frames
- very limited surgical options
- favorable CT feasibility for another transcatheter approach

That distinction matters. This case should not be overgeneralized as support for transcatheter treatment of active PVE. It is better understood as a carefully selected redo strategy after infection was treated and excluded.

## The broader lifetime-management lesson

This is the part that should change practice.

When you implant the first TAVI, you are not just treating today’s stenosis. You are setting the geometry for:
- future valve degeneration
- possible redo TAVI
- coronary angiography and PCI
- potential future surgical explant
- rescue strategies if the patient develops endocarditis or thrombosis

That means the first procedure should be planned with:
- valve choice
- frame height
- coronary clearance
- commissural position
- potential future coronary access
- likely redo trajectory

This is especially relevant for:
- younger patients
- patients with known CAD
- those likely to need future PCI
- patients expected to outlive their first valve
- anyone in whom lifetime valve strategy is foreseeable from day one

The central idea is simple: **the first TAVI should not create an avoidable future problem.**

## Practical pearls from this case

A few take-home principles stand out:

- **CT-guided planning is not optional in complex redo TAVI.**  
  It is essential for assessing coronary risk, frame interaction, and implantation strategy.

- **Commissural alignment should be considered at the index procedure.**  
  It may preserve coronary access and reduce future procedural complexity.

- **Valve choice should reflect lifetime anatomy, not just immediate hemodynamics.**  
  Frame height, skirt design, and supra-annular performance all matter.

- **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is possible, but only in selected patients.**  
  You need favorable anatomy, no active infection, and a realistic pathway to safe coronary flow.

- **Prior endocarditis does not automatically rule out redo transcatheter intervention.**  
  But active infection must be excluded carefully and repeatedly.

- **Future coronary access is a real clinical endpoint.**  
  Not an abstract technical concern.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that structural heart interventions now live in a lifetime-management world. As TAVI expands and patients survive longer, the consequences of valve choice, commissural orientation, and frame geometry become more important with every subsequent procedure.

Commissural alignment is no longer a “nice-to-have.” In many patients, it is a prerequisite for preserving future options.

## Take-home points

- TAVI planning must increasingly be framed as **lifetime valve management**, not single-procedure treatment.
- **Commissural alignment matters now** because it affects future coronary access and redo feasibility.
- **CT-based planning** is critical for TAV-in-TAV and especially TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.
- In carefully selected patients with treated endocarditis and prohibitive surgical risk, **a third transcatheter valve can be feasible**.
- The first valve you implant can make the next intervention easier — or much harder.
