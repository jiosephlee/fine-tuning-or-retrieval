Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# CT Before the Third Valve: Planning for Coronary Obstruction, Neo-Skirt, and Future Access

## Why this case matters

Most of us are getting used to the idea that transcatheter aortic valves are not always “one and done.” But a **third** transcatheter aortic valve in the same patient? That’s the kind of anatomy that forces you to slow down and think hard about imaging, coronary safety, and what future bailout options might look like.

This case is a good reminder that in serial TAVI reintervention, the procedure is not really decided in the cath lab. It is decided on **CT**.

The patient had:
- a prior **27 mm Lotus** valve,
- then a **23 mm Evolut** valve-in-valve,
- then severe recurrent dysfunction after treated **Enterococcus faecalis** prosthetic valve endocarditis,
- and ultimately a **third transcatheter valve**: an **Acurate neo2**.

The key question wasn’t just “can we place another valve?” It was:

- Can we do it **without coronary obstruction**?
- Can we preserve **coronary re-access** for the future?
- Can we avoid creating a geometry that leaves the patient with a critically small effective orifice area?

That’s where preprocedural CT did the heavy lifting.

## The CT questions you must answer before a third valve

In a standard redo TAVI, CT is already important. In **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**, it becomes the entire strategy.

The imaging team in this case used MSCT to assess:

- **Coronary ostial height**
- **Sinus of Valsalva dimensions**
- **Valve-to-coronary distance**
- **Interaction between the existing frames**
- **The anticipated neo-skirt position**
- **Feasibility of a high implantation strategy**
- **Whether coronary protection would be needed**

The scan showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis**, confirming severe narrowing and helping define the landing zone for the third valve.

That’s a crucial point: CT wasn’t just describing anatomy. It was used to decide whether the intervention was **technically feasible** and how to make it **safer and more durable**.

## Coronary obstruction: the fear that drives the whole plan

Coronary obstruction is the nightmare complication in redo transcatheter valve procedures, especially when multiple frames stack up in the aortic root. The risk comes from a combination of:

- displaced native or bioprosthetic leaflets,
- a constrained root,
- low coronary height,
- small sinuses,
- and the creation of a bulky **neo-skirt** from the new valve frame and leaflets.

In this case, the team specifically reviewed:
- coronary ostial height,
- sinus geometry,
- valve-to-coronary distance,
- and how the new valve would interact with the old frames.

That allowed them to proceed **without coronary protection**, but only because imaging suggested the risk was acceptable and a strategy had been chosen to preserve coronary flow.

That is the nuance: **“No coronary protection” should never mean “no coronary planning.”**

## Neo-skirt matters more than people think

When you place one transcatheter valve inside another, you don’t just reduce the outflow area. You also create a **neo-skirt**—the functional barrier formed by overlapping valve structures and displaced leaflets.

Why does that matter?

Because the neo-skirt can:
- cover the coronary ostia,
- make future coronary access difficult,
- and worsen the effective orifice area.

This case was planned with that in mind. The team aimed for a **high implantation** strategy, guided by CT, to minimize how much new structure sat below the coronaries and to preserve flow.

That’s a useful principle in complex redo anatomy:

> The best CT plan is not only about avoiding acute coronary obstruction. It’s also about avoiding a future coronary access problem.

## Frame interaction: the anatomy is now mechanical, not just valvular

After two transcatheter valves, the aortic root is no longer a simple valve space. It becomes a **multi-frame mechanical environment**.

CT was used here to understand:
- how the prior frames were positioned,
- how much lumen remained,
- and how the third prosthesis might be deployed relative to the Evolut leaflets.

The operators deliberately aligned the new **Acurate neo2** with the **Evolut leaflets**, aiming to optimize effective orifice area and reduce patient-prosthesis mismatch.

That is a very practical CT-driven concept:
- if the frame geometry is constrained,
- you need a plan for where the leaflets will end up,
- and how much residual opening you can realistically expect.

## Why high implantation was the winning move

In this case, the third valve was implanted **high**, at the level of the Evolut leaflets.

Why do that?

Because high implantation:
- helps fix the pre-existing leaflets against the frame,
- prevents further inward folding that would further narrow the orifice,
- minimizes the neo-skirt below the coronary ostia,
- and supports future coronary access.

That strategy was not guessed at the table. It was based on **preprocedural CT modeling** and fluoroscopic landmarks.

This is one of the best examples of why imaging planning matters in lifetime valve management: the implant is not just treating today’s stenosis. It is shaping the anatomy the patient will have to live with after this procedure.

## Why the team did not use coronary protection

Given the anatomy, one might ask why they did not wire or protect the coronaries prophylactically.

The answer is in the CT-guided risk assessment. The imaging suggested:
- acceptable coronary clearance,
- a feasible valve position,
- and a deployment strategy that would preserve coronary flow.

The operators still watched coronary flow carefully during:
- balloon predilation,
- rapid pacing,
- and final deployment.

Aortography confirmed that coronary flow remained unobstructed.

So the lesson here is not “skip coronary protection.” It’s:

> Use CT to decide when coronary protection is truly needed, and when a well-planned deployment can safely avoid it.

## What happened hemodynamically after deployment

The immediate post-procedure echo showed something that often happens in very complex valve-in-valve anatomy: **good leaflet motion and no regurgitation, but a high residual gradient**.

At first:
- mean gradient was **67 mm Hg**
- AVA was **0.9 cm²**
- DVI was **0.24**
- consistent with **severe patient-prosthesis mismatch**

But then the gradient improved substantially over time:
- at 1 month: mean gradient **27 mm Hg**, AVA **1.1 cm²**
- at 1 year: mean gradient **26 mm Hg**, AVA **1.1 cm²**, EF **65%**

So the CT-guided strategy did what it was supposed to do:
- preserve coronary flow,
- allow a technically successful third implant,
- and provide durable clinical benefit at 1 year.

## Practical CT pearls from this case

A few points worth carrying into practice:

- **CT is not optional** before serial transcatheter reintervention.
- In triple-valve anatomy, CT should assess not just size, but **coronary access geometry**.
- Look specifically at:
  - coronary ostial height,
  - sinus dimensions,
  - valve-to-coronary distance,
  - frame interaction,
  - and expected neo-skirt position.
- A **high implantation strategy** can help preserve coronary flow and future access.
- The choice to avoid coronary protection should be **imaging-driven**, not convenience-driven.
- In patients with prior endocarditis that has been treated and is no longer active, the decision is still dominated by:
  - anatomy,
  - hemodynamics,
  - and surgical risk.

## Take-home points

- **Before a third valve, CT is the procedure plan.**
- **Coronary obstruction risk is only part of the story; future coronary access matters too.**
- **Neo-skirt and frame stacking can be as important as ostial height.**
- **High implantation, when anatomically feasible, can improve both flow and re-access.**
- **In complex redo anatomy, coronary protection is a decision—not a reflex.**

In the end, this case shows that a third TAVI can be done successfully even after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, but only when the team treats CT as a roadmap rather than a formality.
