Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When DIC Isn’t the Whole Story: Spotting TMA After Sepsis Begins to Improve

## The twist in this case

This case starts where a lot of ICU stories start: a previously healthy woman, a dog bite, rapidly progressive sepsis, DIC, AKI, and multiorgan dysfunction. Nothing about the first few hours screams “zebra.” In fact, the early picture fit sepsis so well that DIC seemed like the obvious unifying diagnosis.

But the real teaching point is what happened next.

As the infection markers improved and the coagulopathy settled, the patient developed a new pattern: persistent thrombocytopaenia, falling haemoglobin without bleeding, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes on smear, and worsening kidney injury that had already progressed to dialysis dependence. That’s the moment to stop thinking about “just sepsis labs” and start asking whether there is a second process layered on top: thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

In this case, that second process was complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection.

## Why this is easy to miss

Sepsis can absolutely cause thrombocytopaenia, AKI, lactic acidosis, and even DIC. That overlap is what makes TMA so slippery in the early phase. If a patient is already critically ill, it is tempting to attribute every abnormality to one diagnosis and keep moving.

The problem is that DIC and TMA are not interchangeable, and the treatment implications are very different.

DIC is a consumptive coagulopathy driven by systemic inflammation and coagulation activation. TMA, by contrast, is a microvascular platelet-rich thrombotic process that causes:

- microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA)
- thrombocytopaenia
- organ injury, especially kidney injury

They can coexist, but when the infection begins to improve and the haemolysis becomes more obvious, you need to re-open the differential.

## The pivot point: when to suspect a superimposed TMA

The key clinical shift here was not at presentation. It was later, when the expected sepsis trajectory and the laboratory trajectory stopped matching.

Red flags for TMA in a recovering septic patient include:

- haemoglobin falling without bleeding
- persistent or worsening thrombocytopaenia despite improvement in sepsis
- new schistocytes on peripheral smear
- undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH
- AKI that is out of proportion, persistent, or worsening
- ongoing organ ischemia/cyanosis despite resolution of shock

In this patient, the initial picture was interpreted as sepsis-associated DIC, which was reasonable. But once the inflammatory profile improved and haemostasis normalized, the continued thrombocytopaenia and abrupt isolated haemolysis were hard to explain by DIC alone.

That is the diagnostic hinge.

## DIC vs TMA: how to keep them straight

Clinically, both syndromes can present with thrombocytopaenia and organ dysfunction. The lab pattern helps.

DIC tends to feature:

- prolonged coagulation tests
- low fibrinogen
- elevated D-dimer
- thrombocytopaenia
- bleeding tendency

TMA tends to feature:

- MAHA with schistocytes
- thrombocytopaenia
- organ ischemia, especially renal
- relatively less prominent coagulation abnormalities

This patient initially had DIC-like features in the setting of sepsis. Later, the coagulation abnormalities improved, but the haemolysis declared itself. That evolution should raise suspicion that you are no longer dealing with a pure consumptive coagulopathy.

## The differential diagnosis of TMA: don’t anchor too early

Once you decide “this is TMA,” the next step is not to leap straight to treatment for CM-HUS. First, exclude the emergencies that change management immediately.

### 1) TTP
This is the must-not-miss diagnosis.

- Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, usually <10%, supports TTP.
- In this case, ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, making TTP unlikely.
- The PLASMIC score was 4/7, which was low probability.

This matters because plasma exchange is lifesaving in TTP. If you suspect it, don’t wait.

### 2) STEC-HUS
Another key consideration, especially if diarrhoeal prodrome is present.

- Here, stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative.
- There was no enteric prodrome.

### 3) CM-HUS
Once TTP and STEC-HUS are off the table, CM-HUS becomes much more likely, particularly when there is:

- persistent MAHA
- thrombocytopaenia
- kidney injury
- biopsy evidence of TMA
- a trigger such as infection

Normal C3/C4 do not exclude it. That’s an important pearl.

## Why the dog bite matters

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is an unusual but very real zoonotic pathogen. It lives in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats, and human infection can follow bites, scratches, or even close contact.

Most clinicians associate it with fulminant sepsis, DIC, and multiorgan failure. This case adds another layer: it can also be the trigger for complement-mediated TMA.

That makes biological sense. Complement is part of host defence, but in susceptible settings, infectious activation can overshoot and damage the endothelium, tipping the patient into TMA.

## Renal biopsy: the diagnosis got real

The renal biopsy in this case was valuable because it showed the tissue-level story:

- severe acute tubular necrosis
- ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi
- scattered crescent formation
- findings consistent with TMA

This is the kind of biopsy that helps convert suspicion into confidence. When the clinical picture is noisy, pathology can tell you whether there is an active microangiopathic process beyond septic AKI.

## Treatment: why plasma exchange was only a bridge

This is where the management becomes nuanced.

Plasma exchange was started while the diagnostic workup was pending. That is appropriate when TTP is still in play, and it can provide temporary stabilisation in other TMAs. But it does not fix the underlying complement dysregulation in CM-HUS.

Eculizumab did.

In this patient:

- haemolysis did not respond to corticosteroids
- plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy
- eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative
- haematologic remission followed
- kidney function partially recovered

That sequence is important. If you see transient improvement after plasma exchange but the platelet count and haemolysis don’t truly normalize, keep looking.

## The infection-treatment tension

One of the hardest parts of this case is the timing of complement blockade during a recent bacteraemia. That concern is real: terminal complement is important for defence against encapsulated organisms.

The team mitigated risk by:

- treating the infection aggressively with antibiotics
- confirming culture clearance before eculizumab
- vaccinating against meningococcal disease
- giving antibiotic prophylaxis

That’s the practical balance: don’t withhold definitive CM-HUS therapy indefinitely, but don’t ignore the infectious risk either.

## Practical bedside lessons

The major teaching point is not “dog bites can cause rare complement disease,” though they can. The bigger lesson is diagnostic discipline in septic patients whose labs stop behaving like sepsis.

When the infection is improving but the blood count is not, think beyond DIC.

### Take-home points

- Sepsis can explain a lot, but persistent thrombocytopaenia plus MAHA is not “just critical illness” until proven otherwise.
- Reassess when the trajectory changes: improving inflammatory markers with worsening haemolysis is a classic pivot point.
- DIC and TMA can overlap, but schistocytes, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, and ongoing kidney injury should prompt a TMA workup.
- In adults with TMA, urgently exclude TTP with ADAMTS13 testing; don’t wait to act if TTP is strongly suspected.
- Normal C3/C4 do not rule out CM-HUS.
- Plasma exchange may bridge while the diagnosis is being clarified, but eculizumab is the disease-specific therapy for CM-HUS.
- Unusual infections, including *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, can act as triggers for complement-mediated TMA.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that sepsis can be the opening act, not the whole play. In a patient with initially convincing DIC, the diagnosis should be reconsidered when the infection improves but thrombocytopaenia, haemolysis, and renal injury persist.

That is the moment to ask: is this still DIC, or has TMA joined the party?

In the ICU and on the ward, that question can be the difference between supportive care and a disease-specific treatment path that saves kidney function—and sometimes the patient.
