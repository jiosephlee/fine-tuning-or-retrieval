Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Eculizumab During or After Infection? Managing Complement Blockade in Infection-Triggered HUS

## The uncomfortable part of this case

This case sits right in the clinical danger zone: a previously healthy woman develops fulminant sepsis after a dog bite, cultures grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, and the initial picture screams DIC. Then, just as the infection starts to come under control, the story shifts—sudden hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, worsening kidney failure, schistocytes, and biopsy-proven thrombotic microangiopathy.

That’s the moment the management gets tricky.

Because if this is complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), delaying eculizumab risks ongoing endothelial injury, dialysis dependence, and incomplete renal recovery. But if active infection is still in play, complement blockade can feel uncomfortably like disabling one of the host’s core defenses against encapsulated bacteria.

This case is a good reminder that in infection-triggered TMA, the real question is often not “Should we treat?” but “How do we treat without making a bad infection worse?”

## First: don’t miss the evolving phenotype

Early in the course, this patient looked like severe sepsis with DIC:

- fever
- pancytopenia
- coagulopathy
- lactic acidosis
- AKI
- mottled extremities
- later, diffuse bleeding and acral ischemic changes

That is a very reasonable initial frame. In fact, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a classic “sick dog bite” pathogen that can cause exactly this kind of fulminant systemic illness.

But the pivot came later, when the infection markers improved and the hematologic picture worsened in a way that didn’t fit simple recovery:

- isolated hemoglobin drop
- persistent thrombocytopenia
- undetectable haptoglobin
- rising LDH
- schistocytes on smear
- dialysis-dependent AKI

That combination should trigger a TMA workup, even if the patient is already labeled as “sepsis/DIC.”

## Why this becomes a complement problem

The differential in adult TMA is always the same short list:

- TTP
- STEC-HUS
- complement-mediated HUS
- secondary TMA from severe illness, drugs, pregnancy, autoimmune disease, malignancy, etc.

In this case:

- ADAMTS13 was 43.5%, which makes TTP very unlikely
- stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin, arguing against STEC-HUS
- kidney biopsy showed TMA
- the clinical course improved after complement blockade

That’s enough to support CM-HUS, even with normal C3/C4. Normal complement levels do **not** rule out CM-HUS.

The key clinical clue is timing: the hemolysis emerged as sepsis began to improve, which is exactly when an infection can act as the trigger for complement dysregulation rather than being the sole cause of the hematologic injury.

## The therapeutic tension: infection vs complement blockade

Eculizumab is the disease-specific therapy for CM-HUS. It blocks C5 and shuts down terminal complement activation, which can rapidly stop the microangiopathic process.

But terminal complement is also important for defense against encapsulated organisms. That’s why the main concern with eculizumab is not theoretical—it’s real infectious risk, especially meningococcal disease.

So how do you manage a patient with suspected CM-HUS while infection is still unfolding?

## Plasma exchange as a bridge, not a destination

This is where plasma exchange often enters the story.

In TTP, PEX is lifesaving and should not be delayed. In CM-HUS, it’s not definitive therapy, but it can serve as a bridge while you sort out the diagnosis and prepare for eculizumab.

In this case, plasma exchange was started while:

- ADAMTS13 was pending
- the diagnosis was still evolving
- the team was preparing for complement blockade

That’s a reasonable move when TTP remains on the table or when the patient is deteriorating and you need temporizing therapy.

But the important pearl: in CM-HUS, PEX may transiently stabilize the situation without truly fixing the problem. Don’t mistake short-lived improvement for resolution.

## When to start eculizumab

There isn’t a universal “day X” rule, but the principle is straightforward:

- start eculizumab when CM-HUS is sufficiently likely
- do not wait for full genetic confirmation
- do not delay solely because complement levels are normal
- in infection-triggered cases, balance the urgency of renal/hematologic rescue against the state of bacteremia

In this case, eculizumab was started after blood cultures had become negative, which is a sensible compromise when feasible. That reduces the risk of giving terminal complement blockade during uncontrolled bacteremia.

Still, the clinical reality is that some patients will not afford you that luxury. If the TMA is rapidly progressive, waiting too long may mean irreversible kidney injury. In that setting, the risk-benefit balance may still favor earlier treatment, but it should be a deliberate decision with infectious disease, nephrology, and hematology input.

## Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis: the logistics matter

If you are going to give eculizumab, you have to think ahead about encapsulated organisms.

In this case:

- meningococcal vaccines were given first
- eculizumab induction followed
- because vaccination had not been completed 2 weeks before complement blockade, prophylactic penicillin was used

That sequence matters.

Practical points:

- give meningococcal vaccination as early as possible
- if you cannot wait 2 weeks before starting eculizumab, use antibiotic prophylaxis
- counsel the patient about fever and urgent evaluation
- monitor closely for breakthrough infection

This is especially relevant in urgent inpatients, where treatment decisions are made faster than vaccine schedules can work.

## A few bedside lessons from this case

This case also reinforces a classic ICU/nephrology pitfall: sepsis and TMA can overlap. Early DIC can obscure evolving CM-HUS, and a “sepsis explains everything” mindset can delay the diagnosis.

Red flags that should prompt a TMA reassessment:

- hemolysis that appears or worsens as sepsis improves
- persistent thrombocytopenia after coagulation parameters normalize
- schistocytes on smear
- worsening AKI out of proportion to the rest of the sepsis picture
- dialysis dependence without another explanation

Also worth remembering: a positive DAT does not end the workup. In this patient, the DAT was likely a false positive related to penicillin exposure or transfusion. If the smear shows schistocytes and the hemolysis is mechanically patterned, keep looking for TMA.

## Take-home points

- Infection-triggered CM-HUS can emerge after the initial septic/DIC phase starts to improve.
- Normal C3/C4 do **not** exclude CM-HUS.
- ADAMTS13 is crucial to exclude TTP; don’t wait passively if the patient is worsening.
- Plasma exchange is a bridge when diagnosis is unclear, but it is not definitive therapy for CM-HUS.
- Eculizumab is the key disease-modifying treatment, and delaying it risks incomplete renal recovery.
- When possible, start eculizumab after bacteremia is controlled; if not possible, make the risk-benefit decision explicitly.
- Meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are not optional details—they’re part of the treatment plan.

## Bottom line

Infection-triggered HUS is one of those conditions where the “right” treatment can seem dangerous because it targets the very system helping the patient fight infection. This case shows the balancing act: treat the infection aggressively, use plasma exchange as a bridge when the diagnosis is uncertain, and move to complement blockade as soon as CM-HUS is likely enough to justify it.

The cost of waiting too long can be a kidney that never comes back.
