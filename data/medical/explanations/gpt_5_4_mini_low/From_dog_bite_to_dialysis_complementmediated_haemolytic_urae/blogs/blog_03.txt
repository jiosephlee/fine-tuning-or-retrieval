Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# MAHA in the ICU: TTP, STEC-HUS, CM-HUS, or Something Else?

## The case that keeps you honest

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s was bitten on the lip by her dog, didn’t start the prescribed antibiotic, and then returned four days later with sepsis, DIC, AKI, and a scary-looking peripheral circulation. Blood cultures eventually grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. So far, so bad—but not rare enough to be shocking.

What makes this case memorable is what happened next. After the initial septic phase began to settle, she developed isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, dialysis-dependent AKI, and a kidney biopsy consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). That’s the pivot point: when “just sepsis/DIC” stops explaining the picture, you have to start actively separating TTP from STEC-HUS and complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

This is the ICU trap: MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI all look similar at the bedside, but the treatment paths diverge fast.

## First principles: what counts as TMA?

Think of TMA as a syndrome, not a diagnosis. The classic bedside triad is:

- Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)
- Thrombocytopenia
- End-organ injury, especially kidney and brain

In practice, the signal comes from a cluster:

- Falling hemoglobin without bleeding
- Rising LDH
- Undetectable or low haptoglobin
- Schistocytes on smear
- Thrombocytopenia
- AKI out of proportion to other causes

In a sick ICU patient, sepsis, DIC, drug effects, marrow suppression, and mechanical hemolysis can muddy the waters. The job is not to prove TMA immediately; it’s to recognize when the pattern has escaped “routine sepsis.”

## The first fork in the road: TTP until proven otherwise

Among adult TMAs, TTP is the one you cannot afford to miss. Why? Because it kills quickly and responds dramatically to plasma exchange.

The key diagnostic concept is ADAMTS13 deficiency:

- **ADAMTS13 activity <10% strongly supports TTP**
- Values above that make TTP much less likely

While awaiting ADAMTS13, bedside triage scores like **PLASMIC** can help estimate pretest probability. In this case, the PLASMIC score was 4/7, which is low. That doesn’t rule out TTP on its own, but it makes it less likely.

Pragmatically:
- If you have **MAHA + thrombocytopenia** and TTP is plausible, **don’t wait for ADAMTS13** to treat
- Start plasma exchange if the clinical picture supports it
- Send ADAMTS13 early, before plasma products if possible, but treatment should not be delayed when suspicion is high

In this patient, ADAMTS13 activity returned at **43.5%**, which effectively argued against TTP.

## Don’t get distracted by DIC

This case is a good reminder that DIC and TMA can coexist or mimic each other early on.

Sepsis can cause:
- Thrombocytopenia
- Coagulopathy
- AKI
- Hemolysis-like lab abnormalities

But classic DIC usually gives you:
- Prolonged coagulation parameters
- Low fibrinogen
- High D-dimer
- A clinical context of severe infection/shock

This patient initially looked exactly like septic DIC, which was a reasonable first interpretation. The problem is that later she developed a more specific hemolytic picture:

- Hemoglobin dropped abruptly
- Haptoglobin became undetectable
- LDH rose
- Schistocytes appeared
- Thrombocytopenia persisted

That evolution should trigger a second look.

## Smear review still matters

The peripheral smear is one of the highest-yield tests in the TMA workup.

Schistocytes support MAHA, and in the right context they point toward TMA rather than isolated marrow suppression or anemia of critical illness.

This patient’s smear eventually showed **15%–20% schistocytes**, which is hard to ignore.

A few practical points:
- Get the smear reviewed early
- Don’t over-rely on a single “normal” early hemolysis panel
- Hemolysis can evolve over time, especially if the initial syndrome is dominated by sepsis

## DAT-positive does not automatically mean autoimmune hemolysis

This case had a useful diagnostic pitfall: the direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs) was initially positive for IgG, which raised concern for autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

But the overall story didn’t fit:
- No bleeding
- Persistent thrombocytopenia
- Schistocytes
- Rising LDH
- Undetectable haptoglobin
- No response to steroids

Repeat DAT was negative, and the initial positivity was felt to be false positive, likely related to penicillin exposure or transfusion.

Take-home: a positive DAT does **not** exclude TMA, and it should not anchor you into autoimmune hemolysis if the rest of the picture screams MAHA.

## STEC-HUS: yes, adults can get it

Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*–associated HUS is classically pediatric, but adults can absolutely get it. The clue is usually a diarrheal prodrome, though not always dramatic.

The key test is **Shiga toxin testing**, often by stool PCR.

In this case:
- Stool PCR was negative
- There was no enteric prodrome
- The course fit an infection-triggered TMA better than STEC-HUS

So if you’re sorting adult TMA in real time:
- Ask about diarrheal illness early
- Send stool Shiga toxin/PCR promptly
- But don’t let a negative GI story prematurely close the differential

## When to think CM-HUS

Once TTP and STEC-HUS are less likely, **CM-HUS** moves up the list.

This is the complement-mediated, non-STEC, non-TTP TMA syndrome historically called “atypical HUS.” It’s often triggered by a second hit:
- Infection
- Pregnancy
- Autoimmune disease
- Drugs

This case is especially interesting because the trigger was **Capnocytophaga canimorsus**, a rare dog-associated pathogen. The infection dominated early, and only later did the complement-mediated TMA declare itself.

A few pearls:
- **Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS**
- CM-HUS is a **clinicopathologic diagnosis of exclusion**
- Complement genetics may be normal
- A trigger does not have to be “classic” to matter

In this patient, the key clue was timing: hemolysis emerged **after** the septic picture started improving, which is a pattern that should make you think of a superimposed complement-mediated process rather than pure infection-related consumptive coagulopathy.

## Kidney biopsy: not first-line, but very helpful when the dust settles

In unstable ICU patients, kidney biopsy is usually not the first move. But once the platelet count recovers enough to make it feasible, biopsy can be very helpful when the diagnosis remains uncertain.

This patient’s biopsy showed:
- Severe acute tubular necrosis
- Ischemic glomeruli
- Fibrin thrombi
- Findings consistent with TMA

Biopsy doesn’t replace the clinical diagnosis, but it can:
- Support TMA when labs are equivocal
- Help distinguish glomerular disease from tubular injury
- Strengthen the case for complement blockade when the diagnosis is uncertain

## A practical ICU approach to MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI

When the triad shows up, I find it useful to think in this order:

1. **Confirm the syndrome**
   - CBC, smear, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, retic count
   - Renal function and urinalysis
   - Coagulation studies for DIC

2. **Rule out TTP early**
   - Send ADAMTS13
   - Use PLASMIC as a triage tool, not a crutch
   - If suspicion is high, start plasma exchange immediately

3. **Look for STEC-HUS**
   - Stool Shiga toxin/PCR
   - GI prodrome
   - Exposure history

4. **Reassess the sepsis/DIC story**
   - Is coagulopathy improving but MAHA worsening?
   - Is thrombocytopenia persisting after infection control?

5. **Consider CM-HUS**
   - Especially if TTP/STEC are unlikely
   - Particularly if renal failure is prominent
   - Consider complement testing, recognizing that normal C3/C4 does not rule it out

6. **Use biopsy selectively**
   - When safe and when it will change management

## Plasma exchange: bridge, not cure, in CM-HUS

This is one of the most important management distinctions.

- In **TTP**, plasma exchange is the definitive acute therapy
- In **CM-HUS**, plasma exchange may transiently stabilize but does not fix the underlying complement dysregulation

In this case, PEX was used as bridging therapy while the diagnosis was being clarified and before eculizumab could be started. That’s a very reasonable move when the distinction between TTP and CM-HUS is still unresolved.

## Eculizumab: powerful, but don’t forget the infection risk

Eculizumab changed the game for CM-HUS, but it comes with a major caveat: terminal complement blockade increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms.

That matters here because the patient had:
- Recent bacteremia
- A dog-bite-associated pathogen
- A still-recent active infectious process

In this case, eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative, with:
- Meningococcal vaccination
- Antibiotic prophylaxis
- Close monitoring

That’s the balancing act: you want to treat the TMA early enough to preserve kidneys, but not so recklessly that you worsen an active infection.

## What this case teaches

This patient’s course is a reminder that ICU TMA is often a moving target. Early on, the story looked like septic DIC from *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Later, the persistence of thrombocytopenia plus a new hemolytic pattern forced a broader differential, and the diagnosis ultimately became CM-HUS.

The big mistake would have been to stop at “sepsis explains everything.”

## Take-home points

- In adults with **MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI**, think **TMA** and move fast.
- **TTP is the emergency diagnosis**: send ADAMTS13, use PLASMIC to triage, and treat empirically if suspicion is high.
- **Normal C3/C4 do not rule out CM-HUS**.
- **Negative Shiga toxin testing** and lack of diarrheal prodrome make STEC-HUS less likely, but not impossible without proper testing.
- A **positive DAT does not exclude TMA**; don’t let it anchor you.
- **Peripheral smear review is essential**—schistocytes matter.
- **Kidney biopsy** can be very helpful once it’s safe, especially when the diagnosis is still unclear.
- In **CM-HUS**, plasma exchange is a bridge; **eculizumab** is the disease-modifying therapy.
- When infection is the trigger, management requires a careful balance between **complement blockade** and **infection control**.

## Bottom line

Not every ICU patient with thrombocytopenia and renal failure has DIC. If the hemolysis evolves, the smear turns positive, and the kidneys keep failing even as sepsis improves, you need to widen the lens.

That’s where the real diagnostic art lives: separating TTP from STEC-HUS and CM-HUS quickly enough to give the right therapy before the kidneys are gone.
