Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# From Anuria to Partial Recovery: What Renal Biopsy and Clinical Course Teach Us About Prognosis in CM-HUS

## A dog bite, septic shock, and then the real kidney story

This case is fascinating because the kidney injury didn’t just “happen during sepsis” and then fade away. It evolved. What began as fulminant *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis with DIC and AKI later declared itself as a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and ultimately complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS).

That sequence matters.

The nephrology lesson here is that in CM-HUS, the hematology can improve first while the kidneys lag badly behind—or never fully recover. In this patient, the transition from initial oliguria to complete anuria, dialysis dependence, and biopsy-proven renal TMA marked a severe renal phenotype. Even with appropriate treatment, she only achieved partial renal recovery.

So the big question is: what does this case teach us about prognosis?

## Why the kidney course is the part to watch

At presentation, the renal injury could plausibly be explained by septic AKI, hypoperfusion, and DIC. But the subsequent course was more ominous:

- progression to **complete anuria**
- need for **continuous renal replacement therapy**
- persistent **dialysis-dependent AKI**
- later **renal biopsy showing severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli, and fibrin thrombi**
- eventual **return of urine output only after PEX and before/around eculizumab**
- **incomplete recovery** at follow-up, with creatinine still elevated

That pattern strongly suggests there was more than one kidney insult:

1. **Sepsis-associated ATN**
2. **Microvascular injury from TMA**
3. Possibly superimposed ischemic damage from the systemic inflammatory/coagulopathic state

Biopsy helped separate these pieces. The finding of **acute tubular necrosis plus glomerular fibrin thrombi** is important because it shows both tubular injury and active microangiopathy. In practical terms, that’s a bad prognostic combination.

## What does anuria mean in CM-HUS?

Complete anuria is not just a severity marker—it often reflects extensive renal ischemia and microvascular injury.

In CM-HUS, anuria usually means one or more of the following:

- widespread endothelial injury with capillary occlusion
- severe glomerular ischemia
- major tubular necrosis
- delayed recognition, allowing ongoing complement-mediated damage

In this case, the fact that she became **completely anuric after ICU transfer** is a warning sign. Even though she later recovered some diuresis, the kidney had likely already sustained substantial structural injury.

A useful bedside pearl: **return of urine output is encouraging, but it does not guarantee full renal recovery**. In TMA, urine output may come back before GFR meaningfully improves.

## Renal biopsy: why it changes the conversation

Biopsy isn’t always needed to diagnose CM-HUS, but when the clinical picture is muddy—as it was here—it can be very helpful.

This biopsy showed:

- **severe acute tubular necrosis**
- **ischemic glomeruli**
- **fibrin thrombi**
- findings consistent with **TMA**

That combination supports a microvascular process, not just “post-sepsis AKI.”

Why does that matter prognostically?

- **ATN alone** can recover substantially, even after dialysis
- **TMA with fibrin thrombi** implies ongoing vascular injury and a greater risk of permanent nephron loss
- **ischemic glomeruli** suggest that filtration units were directly compromised, not merely “shut down” from hemodynamics

In other words, the biopsy identified a kidney that had been hit from multiple angles.

## What predicts renal recovery in CM-HUS?

The literature and clinical experience point to a few themes:

### 1) Time to complement blockade matters
The earlier eculizumab is started, the better the chance of renal recovery. Once irreversible ischemic injury and fibrosis set in, hematologic remission may outpace kidney recovery.

### 2) Severity of initial kidney injury matters
Features associated with worse renal outcomes include:

- dialysis requirement
- anuria
- severe AKI at presentation
- delayed diagnosis
- biopsy evidence of extensive TMA or cortical ischemia

This patient had several of these.

### 3) Hematologic remission does not equal renal remission
This is a crucial teaching point. Platelets can normalize, hemolysis can stop, and the patient can still be left with chronic kidney disease.

That’s exactly why nephrology follow-up matters even after the “CRASH” of the acute illness is over.

### 4) Genetic predisposition is not required for severe disease
This patient had no pathogenic complement variants identified. That does **not** mean the disease was mild or less real. Infection-triggered CM-HUS can be severe even without a detectable inherited mutation.

## Normal complement tests do not rescue you

Another practical pearl from this case: **normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS**.

That matters because clinicians often anchor on normal complement levels and move away from the diagnosis. But CM-HUS is a functional disease of dysregulated complement activation, and routine complement levels may be normal.

So if you have:

- MAHA
- thrombocytopenia
- AKI
- schistocytes
- TTP excluded
- STEC excluded
- biopsy consistent with TMA

then CM-HUS stays on the table whether C3/C4 are normal or not.

## Dialysis dependence: reversible or not?

This case sits in the gray zone that matters most in nephrology: dialysis dependence does not automatically mean ESRD, but it is a major prognostic flag.

In CM-HUS, some patients recover enough kidney function to come off dialysis if:

- the diagnosis is made early
- complement blockade is started promptly
- the biopsy does not show irreversible cortical loss
- urine output returns

But prolonged anuria and delayed recognition reduce the odds of full recovery.

In this patient, the return of spontaneous diuresis after PEX and around the time of complement blockade was encouraging, but the final outcome—**partial recovery only**—fits the severity of the initial injury.

## Where does plasma exchange fit?

PEX was used here as a bridge while the team was sorting out TTP vs CM-HUS. That makes sense. In a patient with MAHA and thrombocytopenia, you cannot wait on every send-out before acting.

But once ADAMTS13 was not severely deficient and the picture pointed to CM-HUS, PEX was not the definitive therapy. It may have provided transient stabilization, but the durable response came after **eculizumab**.

For kidneys, that distinction is everything: transient hematologic improvement is not the same as stopping complement-mediated endothelial injury.

## How long should eculizumab be continued if genetics are negative?

This is one of the hardest real-world decisions.

If a patient has:

- no pathogenic complement variants identified
- a clear infectious trigger
- significant initial renal injury
- only partial recovery

then stopping eculizumab is not straightforward.

The challenge is that “genetically negative” does not equal “low risk.” Testing may be incomplete, variants of uncertain significance may be missed or not yet interpreted, and the clinical phenotype can still be severe.

In practice, duration is individualized based on:

- severity of the initial episode
- renal recovery trajectory
- recurrence risk
- trigger control
- availability of close follow-up
- patient preference and infection risk counseling

In this case, continuing maintenance eculizumab for a period after induction is reasonable given the severity of the kidney injury and incomplete recovery. If renal function stabilizes and there is no recurrence, reassessment for discontinuation makes sense. But if a pathogenic complement abnormality had been found, the argument for longer-term treatment would be stronger.

## The infectious-risk tradeoff

There’s a deep irony here: complement blockade can be kidney-saving, but complement is also part of host defense.

That means eculizumab in the setting of a recent bloodstream infection requires careful planning:

- ensure cultures have cleared if possible
- vaccinate against meningococcal disease
- use antimicrobial prophylaxis when vaccination is not fully established
- monitor closely for recurrent infection

This patient received vaccines and prophylactic antibiotics, which is exactly the kind of safety net you want when using terminal complement blockade.

## Take-home points

- **Anuria in CM-HUS is a bad prognostic sign** and often reflects severe microvascular and tubular injury.
- **Dialysis dependence does not rule out recovery**, but the likelihood of full recovery drops as kidney injury becomes more severe and prolonged.
- **Renal biopsy can clarify prognosis** by showing whether the dominant lesion is ATN alone or ATN plus active TMA/ischemia.
- **Hematologic remission can occur before renal recovery**; do not use platelet recovery as a surrogate for kidney prognosis.
- **Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS**.
- **Eculizumab is most effective when started early**, but even delayed therapy can still produce hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.
- **Negative genetic testing does not eliminate relapse risk**, so duration of complement blockade should be individualized.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that in CM-HUS, the kidney often tells the truth before the blood counts do—and sometimes keeps telling it long after the hematologic storm has passed. Complete anuria, dialysis dependence, and biopsy-proven TMA point to a severe renal insult with a real risk of incomplete recovery.

The good news is that recovery is still possible, even after dialysis. The less good news is that the kidney may recover only partially, especially when the diagnosis is delayed and the biopsy already shows both tubular necrosis and microvascular thrombosis.

In other words: when the urine stops, the prognosis gets serious fast.
