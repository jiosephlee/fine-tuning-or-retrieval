Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Dog Bite, Pancytopenia, Shock: Why *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* Belongs on the Early Differential

## The weird part of this case

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s got a dog bite on the lip, didn’t start the prescribed penicillin, and 4 days later showed up very sick: fever >40°C, hypoglycaemia, mottled extremities, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury, and lactic acidosis. That’s not “simple cellulitis after a dog bite.” That’s an ICU problem until proven otherwise.

The organism was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a slow-growing, fastidious gram-negative rod from the oral flora of dogs and cats. It’s a classic reminder that the wound can look minor while the physiology is screaming sepsis.

## Why this bug should be on your radar

Most dog bites do not turn into fulminant multiorgan failure. But when *Capnocytophaga* does invade, it can move fast and hit hard:

- septic shock
- DIC/coagulopathy
- AKI and dialysis-requiring renal failure
- leukopaenia or pancytopenia
- microvascular thrombosis and skin ischemia
- occasionally thrombotic microangiopathy

And crucially: this can happen even in immunocompetent adults. Asplenia, alcoholism, cirrhosis, and immunosuppression raise the risk, but their absence does **not** make the diagnosis go away.

## The emergency medicine lesson: trust the physiology, not the bite mark

The initial temptation is to anchor on the bite and underestimate the illness. Don’t.

In this case, the early syndrome looked like severe sepsis with DIC:
- fever
- pancytopenia
- coagulopathy
- elevated lactate
- AKI
- mottled, cool extremities

That’s enough to justify immediate broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and ICU-level monitoring. The patient was started on piperacillin/tazobactam, which is very reasonable empiric coverage for a severely ill patient after dog exposure.

### Empiric antibiotic takeaway
For a patient who is systemically ill after a dog bite, think beyond “bite prophylaxis” and treat for invasive zoonotic infection.

Practical points:
- start antibiotics early if there is any concern for systemic illness
- piperacillin/tazobactam is a strong empiric choice in severe presentations
- *Capnocytophaga* is generally susceptible to beta-lactams, but do not wait for culture confirmation before treating
- if the patient is in shock or has organ dysfunction, this is not outpatient bite management

## Why the diagnosis is often delayed

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a frustrating organism:
- slow-growing
- fastidious
- can take days to confirm on blood cultures
- sometimes requires molecular methods for faster detection

In this case, blood cultures were positive 7 days later. That lag matters because the clinical picture evolves while everyone is still trying to name the bug.

If your patient is crashing after dog exposure, do not wait for culture proof to act.

## The ICU trap: sepsis, DIC, or something more?

This case gets especially interesting because the early course was dominated by sepsis and DIC, but then the picture changed.

After initial improvement in inflammation and coagulation, she developed:
- sudden isolated hemoglobin drop
- persistent thrombocytopenia
- undetectable haptoglobin
- rising LDH
- schistocytes on smear
- worsening kidney injury

That’s the moment to widen the differential beyond “resolving sepsis.”

The key diagnostic pivot is recognizing **thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)** when the classic triad appears:
- microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia
- thrombocytopenia
- organ injury, especially kidney injury

In this patient, the later haemolysis was not just DIC marching on. It was a superimposed TMA, ultimately diagnosed as complement-mediated HUS.

## DIC and TMA can coexist — don’t force a single label too early

One of the hardest parts of this case is that the patient really did look like DIC early on. And that was a reasonable interpretation.

But DIC and TMA can blur together clinically:
- both can present with thrombocytopenia
- both can cause organ dysfunction
- both may show ischemic skin findings
- both may occur in sepsis

The clue that should make you pause is **persistent or new haemolysis with schistocytes**, especially when coagulation parameters are improving but renal failure and thrombocytopenia are not.

## The hemolysis workup that matters

Once MAHA/TMA enters the differential, the priorities are to distinguish:
- TTP
- STEC-HUS
- complement-mediated HUS

In this case:
- PLASMIC score was 4/7, so TTP was less likely
- ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP
- stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin, arguing against STEC-HUS
- kidney biopsy showed TMA

That made complement-mediated HUS the best fit.

### Diagnostic pearl
Normal C3/C4 do **not** exclude complement-mediated HUS. Don’t let “normal complement levels” falsely reassure you.

## Why this matters in the ED and ICU

The management implications are huge.

If you think this is:
- **TTP** → urgent plasma exchange is lifesaving
- **STEC-HUS** → supportive care
- **CM-HUS** → complement blockade with eculizumab is the disease-specific therapy

In the real world, you often have to start something before the answer is in. In this case, plasma exchange was used as a bridge while awaiting ADAMTS13 and other studies, then eculizumab was started once the diagnosis settled and blood cultures had cleared.

## The complement blockade dilemma

Eculizumab is highly effective for CM-HUS, but it raises an important infectious risk question in a patient with recent bacteremia: terminal complement is important for defense against encapsulated organisms.

That’s a real concern here, especially because *Capnocytophaga* itself is a complement-sensitive pathogen. The team waited until blood cultures were negative, gave meningococcal vaccination, and used antibiotic prophylaxis.

This is a good example of balancing two competing hazards:
- uncontrolled complement-mediated endothelial injury
- risk of worsening infection under complement blockade

## Clinical pearls from this case

- A dog bite plus shock, DIC, AKI, or pancytopenia is an ICU problem, not a routine wound visit.
- *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* can cause fulminant sepsis even in immunocompetent adults.
- Blood cultures may take days to turn positive because the organism is fastidious and slow growing.
- If the patient improves from sepsis but then develops schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia, and haemolysis, think TMA.
- Normal complement levels do not rule out complement-mediated HUS.
- ADAMTS13 is the key test to help separate TTP from other TMAs.
- Plasma exchange can be a bridge, but eculizumab is the disease-specific treatment for CM-HUS.
- In severe post–dog bite sepsis, treat early with broad IV antibiotics and do not wait for microbiologic confirmation.

## Bottom line

Dog bites are common; *Capnocytophaga* sepsis is not. But when it happens, it can look like catastrophic multisystem disease with DIC, renal failure, and later TMA. The takeaway is simple: if a patient looks far sicker than the wound suggests, widen the lens early.

That means:
- think *Capnocytophaga* after dog exposure
- start broad empiric antibiotics promptly
- monitor closely for DIC, AKI, and evolving haemolysis
- keep TMA on the table if the picture changes

This case is a reminder that the “bite” may be the smallest part of the story.
