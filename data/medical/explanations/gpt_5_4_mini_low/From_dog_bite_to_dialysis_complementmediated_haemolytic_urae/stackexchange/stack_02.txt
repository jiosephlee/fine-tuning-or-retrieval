Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Morning Report] What clinical and laboratory features in this case supported an initial diagnosis of disseminated intravascular coagulation from severe sepsis rather than primary thrombotic microangiopathy at presentation?

At presentation, the picture fit **severe sepsis with DIC** much better than a primary thrombotic microangiopathy.

The key clues were the **clinical context** and the **pattern of laboratory abnormalities**. She had a clear septic trigger — a recent **dog bite**, high fever, lactic acidosis, mottled extremities, hypoglycemia, and rapidly progressive multiorgan dysfunction. Her initial bloodwork showed **marked inflammation, pancytopenia, AKI, liver injury, and coagulopathy**, which is the classic sepsis-associated DIC phenotype. In addition, she developed skin bleeding and ischemic acral changes, findings that can occur in DIC from widespread microvascular thrombosis and consumptive coagulopathy.

By contrast, the usual early laboratory signature of a primary TMA such as HUS or TTP is **microangiopathic hemolysis** — schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, and thrombocytopenia, often with **relatively preserved coagulation studies**. In this case, that pattern was not present initially: **haptoglobin was normal**, reticulocytosis was not appropriate, and the dominant abnormalities were **coagulopathy and pancytopenia** in the setting of bacteremia. That is why the initial working diagnosis of **DIC secondary to severe sepsis** was reasonable.

Only later, after the septic coagulopathy had begun to improve, did she develop the more specific TMA features — **persistent thrombocytopenia, falling hemoglobin without bleeding, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and schistocytes** — which then shifted the diagnosis toward superimposed CM-HUS.
