Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In an adult who develops microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury after sepsis, what are the major thrombotic microangiopathy syndromes that must be distinguished, and which key tests help separate TTP, STEC-HUS and complement-mediated HUS?

In an adult who develops **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury after sepsis**, the thrombotic microangiopathies that must be distinguished are:

1. **Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)**
2. **Shiga toxin–producing E. coli–associated HUS (STEC-HUS)**
3. **Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS, formerly “atypical HUS”)**

The first diagnostic priority is to **exclude TTP**, because it is a hematologic emergency requiring urgent plasma exchange. The key test is **ADAMTS13 activity**:  
- **Severe deficiency, typically <10%**, strongly supports TTP.  
- In the case report, ADAMTS13 activity was **43.5%**, which essentially ruled out TTP.

Next, **STEC-HUS** is separated by evidence of Shiga toxin exposure/infection:  
- **Stool PCR for Shiga toxin** and/or stool culture are the key tests.  
- In this case, **stool PCR was negative**, arguing against STEC-HUS.

If ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and Shiga toxin testing is negative, the remaining major diagnosis is **CM-HUS**, which is a **clinicopathologic diagnosis of exclusion**. Helpful supportive studies include:  
- **Complement studies**: C3/C4 may be obtained, but **normal values do not exclude CM-HUS**, as shown in this case.  
- **Alternative pathway functional testing** such as **AH50** may show reduced activity, though results can be affected by treatment.  
- **Kidney biopsy**, when safe, can confirm a **TMA pattern** with fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomerular injury, as in this patient.

So, in practice: **ADAMTS13 activity distinguishes TTP; Shiga toxin PCR distinguishes STEC-HUS; and normal ADAMTS13 plus negative Shiga toxin testing, with biopsy-proven TMA, supports CM-HUS even if C3/C4 are normal.**

### [Morning Report] What clinical and laboratory features in this case supported an initial diagnosis of disseminated intravascular coagulation from severe sepsis rather than primary thrombotic microangiopathy at presentation?

At presentation, the picture fit **severe sepsis with DIC** much better than a primary thrombotic microangiopathy.

The key clues were the **clinical context** and the **pattern of laboratory abnormalities**. She had a clear septic trigger — a recent **dog bite**, high fever, lactic acidosis, mottled extremities, hypoglycemia, and rapidly progressive multiorgan dysfunction. Her initial bloodwork showed **marked inflammation, pancytopenia, AKI, liver injury, and coagulopathy**, which is the classic sepsis-associated DIC phenotype. In addition, she developed skin bleeding and ischemic acral changes, findings that can occur in DIC from widespread microvascular thrombosis and consumptive coagulopathy.

By contrast, the usual early laboratory signature of a primary TMA such as HUS or TTP is **microangiopathic hemolysis** — schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, and thrombocytopenia, often with **relatively preserved coagulation studies**. In this case, that pattern was not present initially: **haptoglobin was normal**, reticulocytosis was not appropriate, and the dominant abnormalities were **coagulopathy and pancytopenia** in the setting of bacteremia. That is why the initial working diagnosis of **DIC secondary to severe sepsis** was reasonable.

Only later, after the septic coagulopathy had begun to improve, did she develop the more specific TMA features — **persistent thrombocytopenia, falling hemoglobin without bleeding, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and schistocytes** — which then shifted the diagnosis toward superimposed CM-HUS.

### [Pathophysiology] Why does a normal C3 and C4 level not exclude complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome, and what does that imply for the diagnostic approach when suspicion remains high?

Normal C3 and C4 levels do **not** exclude complement-mediated HUS because these are **insensitive screening tests** for alternative-pathway dysregulation. In CM-HUS, the problem is often **uncontrolled complement activation on the endothelial surface**, not necessarily a large enough fall in circulating C3 or C4 to cross below the reference range. In addition, complement levels may be **normal between flares**, and standard assays do not reliably capture functional or localized complement consumption. That is exactly why, in this case, the patient’s normal C3/C4 and normal “standard” complement tests did not rule out CM-HUS, despite the later biopsy-proven thrombotic microangiopathy and response to eculizumab.

The implication is that **CM-HUS remains a clinical diagnosis of exclusion**. If suspicion is high—particularly in a patient with **MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, schistocytes, and a biopsy showing TMA**—you should not wait for low complement levels to act. Instead, urgently exclude **TTP** with ADAMTS13 testing and **STEC-HUS** with stool testing, send **extended complement studies and genetic testing** if available, and consider **early complement blockade** once infection is controlled and other causes have been reasonably excluded. Normal C3/C4 should never reassure you out of the diagnosis when the phenotype fits.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should the ADAMTS13 activity result of 43.5% be interpreted in this patient, and why is the threshold of less than 10% clinically important?

An ADAMTS13 activity of **43.5% is not consistent with thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)**. In this patient, it argues strongly **against severe ADAMTS13 deficiency as the cause of her thrombotic microangiopathy**, and therefore away from TTP as the primary diagnosis. That is clinically important because her presentation later evolved into **MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury**, which can be seen in both TTP and complement-mediated HUS, but the treatment and urgency differ.

The threshold of **<10%** matters because **severe ADAMTS13 deficiency is the diagnostic hallmark of TTP**. When ADAMTS13 activity is below 10%, platelet-rich microthrombi form because von Willebrand factor multimers are not adequately cleaved. This is the setting in which **urgent plasma exchange is lifesaving**, often started empirically before the result returns. By contrast, an activity of 43.5% is much more compatible with **secondary TMA or CM-HUS**, as in this case, where the kidney biopsy and clinical course supported complement-mediated disease rather than TTP.

So, in this patient, the ADAMTS13 result was used to **exclude TTP and redirect management toward complement-mediated HUS**, with plasma exchange only as a bridge while eculizumab was arranged.

### [Differential Diagnosis] The patient initially had a positive direct antiglobulin test but was later found to have 15%–20% schistocytes on smear. How do you reconcile these findings, and what clues argue for microangiopathic haemolysis rather than autoimmune haemolytic anaemia?

The apparent contradiction is resolved by recognizing that a **positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) does not prove autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA)** in this setting. In this case, the DAT was likely a **false-positive or incidental finding**, plausibly related to **penicillin exposure and/or recent transfusions**, and it was later negated on repeat testing. By contrast, the peripheral smear showing **15%–20% schistocytes** is a much stronger morphologic clue to **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)** from a thrombotic microangiopathy.

Several features favor MAHA over AIHA:

- **Schistocytes on smear**: fragmented red cells are the hallmark of mechanical injury in the microvasculature; a burden as high as 15%–20% is highly suggestive of TMA.  
- **Persistent thrombocytopenia**: MAHA in TMA classically accompanies platelet consumption, whereas isolated AIHA does not usually cause marked thrombocytopenia.  
- **Severe kidney injury with anuria and dialysis dependence**: renal involvement is central to TMA/HUS and is not a typical dominant feature of AIHA.  
- **Failure to respond to corticosteroids**: the hemoglobin drop did not improve with prednisone, arguing against immune-mediated hemolysis.  
- **Biopsy confirmation of TMA**: fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomerular injury supported a microangiopathic process.  
- **Hemolysis emerging as sepsis improved**: this temporal pattern is classic for a superimposed complement-mediated TMA rather than primary AIHA.

So, the key teaching point is that in a septic patient, a positive DAT can mislead, but **the combination of schistocytosis, thrombocytopenia, AKI, and steroid nonresponse should redirect the diagnosis from AIHA to MAHA/TMA**.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed pathophysiologic link between Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection and complement-mediated HUS, and how can infection act as a trigger even in a patient without an identified pathogenic complement gene variant?

The proposed link is that **Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection can activate the alternative complement pathway**, and in a susceptible host this can tip the balance into **uncontrolled terminal complement activation on the endothelium**, producing a thrombotic microangiopathy and thus **complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)**. In other words, the infection is not merely a bystander; it can serve as the **triggering inflammatory stimulus** that amplifies complement activation, leading to endothelial injury, platelet consumption, microvascular thrombosis, MAHA, and kidney failure.

Crucially, **an identified pathogenic complement gene variant is not required for the diagnosis or for the disease to occur**. CM-HUS has **incomplete penetrance**: many patients have no detectable mutation, and even when mutations are present, clinical disease often requires a **“second hit”** such as infection, pregnancy, or another inflammatory stressor. In this case, the dog-bite-associated **C. canimorsus sepsis** likely provided that trigger. The patient’s later development of schistocytic hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia, after the initial septic/DIC phase was improving, is consistent with infection unmasking a complement-driven TMA. Normal C3/C4 levels do not exclude this process, and the absence of a pathogenic complement variant does not rule out infection-triggered CM-HUS.

So the key teaching point is: **infection can precipitate CM-HUS by activating complement in a host with functionally vulnerable, but not necessarily genetically proven, complement regulation**.

### [Management] In a patient like this, why might plasma exchange be started before the diagnosis is fully clarified, and why is eculizumab considered the definitive therapy once complement-mediated HUS becomes the leading diagnosis?

Plasma exchange is often started **before the diagnosis is fully clarified** because, in an adult with **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury**, the most time-critical alternative diagnosis is **thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)**. TTP can be rapidly fatal and is treated immediately with plasma exchange while ADAMTS13 testing is pending. In this case, the patient’s initial picture was clouded by severe sepsis, DIC, pancytopenia, and evolving renal failure; only later did the more classic TMA pattern emerge with schistocytes, falling haptoglobin, rising LDH, and persistent thrombocytopenia. In that setting, PEX is a reasonable **bridging therapy** because it may temporarily remove circulating autoantibodies or inflammatory mediators and buys time while the team excludes TTP and other causes.

Once **complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)** becomes the leading diagnosis, **eculizumab is the definitive therapy** because the disease is driven by **uncontrolled terminal complement activation**, not by a problem that plasma exchange can correct. PEX does not fix the underlying complement dysregulation; at best, it provides transient improvement. By contrast, eculizumab blocks **C5**, preventing formation of the membrane attack complex and halting the endothelial injury that drives TMA. That is why, in this case, the durable hematologic response and renal recovery followed complement blockade rather than plasma exchange alone. The case also illustrates an important practical point: because eculizumab increases susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria, it is ideally started when infection is controlled, with meningococcal vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis in place.

### [Morning Report] What renal biopsy findings in this case supported thrombotic microangiopathy, and how did the biopsy help distinguish ongoing TMA from isolated acute tubular necrosis due to septic shock?

The biopsy supported thrombotic microangiopathy by showing **fibrin thrombi within ischemic glomeruli**, along with **scattered crescent formation**. Those findings indicate **microvascular endothelial injury and intraglomerular thrombosis**, which are the histologic hallmarks of TMA. In this case, immunofluorescence showed **no immune-complex deposition**, and electron microscopy showed **no primary glomerulopathy**, making an immune-mediated nephritis or another primary glomerular disease unlikely.

At the same time, the biopsy also showed **severe acute tubular necrosis**, which is entirely compatible with the patient’s preceding septic shock and ischemic kidney injury. The key point is that **ATN explains tubular damage, but it does not explain fibrin thrombi in glomeruli**. Thus, the biopsy distinguished **concurrent ischemic ATN from ongoing TMA**: the tubular injury reflected shock, whereas the **fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomerular lesions proved active microangiopathic renal injury** from complement-mediated HUS.

### [Management] What infection-prevention measures are required before and after starting eculizumab, and why are these especially important in someone recovering from Capnocytophaga bacteraemia?

Before starting eculizumab, the key infection-prevention step is **meningococcal protection**: patients should receive **both MenACWY and MenB vaccination ideally at least 2 weeks before the first dose**. If eculizumab must be started urgently, as in this case, then vaccination should be given **as soon as possible**, and the patient should receive **concurrent antimicrobial prophylaxis**—commonly **penicillin or amoxicillin** if tolerated—because vaccine-induced protection is not immediate.

After eculizumab is started, this protection must be **maintained throughout treatment**: patients need to **complete the vaccine series and boosters on schedule**, and many centers continue **antibiotic prophylaxis for the duration of complement blockade**, with close counseling to seek urgent care for fever or meningitic symptoms.

These measures are especially important after **Capnocytophaga bacteraemia** because the patient is recovering from an infection in which **complement-mediated host defense is central**. Eculizumab blocks terminal complement activity, which markedly increases susceptibility to **encapsulated bacteria**, classically **Neisseria**, but also raises concern for **recurrent or uncontrolled Capnocytophaga infection**. In other words, this patient has just demonstrated a complement-sensitive zoonotic infection, and eculizumab removes one of her major remaining defenses. That is why the case appropriately paired **vaccination, penicillin prophylaxis, and careful timing of complement blockade after blood cultures cleared**.
