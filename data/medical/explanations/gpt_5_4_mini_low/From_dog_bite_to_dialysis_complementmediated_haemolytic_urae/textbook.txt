Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Capnocytophaga canimorsus Infection After Dog Bite: Clinical Context and Early Disease Mechanisms

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an uncommon but important zoonotic pathogen carried in the normal oral flora of dogs and, less commonly, cats. Human infection typically follows a bite, scratch, or close salivary exposure, and the organism is particularly relevant after dog bites because transmission can occur even when the skin injury seems minor. In this case, the patient’s upper-lip bite provided the portal of entry, and the absence of timely antibiotic prophylaxis likely increased the risk of invasive infection. Although many post-bite wounds do not become infected, this case illustrates why clinicians must treat dog bites as potential exposures to aggressive gram-negative pathogens, not simply local skin injuries.

Microbiologically, C. canimorsus is a slow-growing, fastidious, encapsulated gram-negative rod. These features matter clinically because the organism may be difficult to recover in routine cultures and may not be immediately recognized by the laboratory. Its capsule and other immune-evasion mechanisms help it survive in the bloodstream, contributing to the wide spectrum of disease, from localized soft tissue infection to fulminant bacteraemia, septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation, multiorgan failure, and, more rarely, thrombotic microangiopathy. The present case began as a severe invasive infection and later evolved into a thrombotic microangiopathy, but early on the dominant process was sepsis with systemic coagulopathy.

Host defence against C. canimorsus depends heavily on innate immune mechanisms, particularly complement. Complement promotes opsonization and bacterial killing, especially for encapsulated organisms. This is clinically relevant because even immunocompetent patients can develop severe infection when bacterial inoculum is high, host-pathogen interactions favor immune evasion, or early recognition and treatment are delayed. Importantly, C. canimorsus infection does not require a classic immunocompromising condition such as asplenia or alcoholism, although these are well-known risk factors. The case therefore underscores that severe disease can occur in an otherwise healthy adult, and that “immunocompetent” does not mean “low risk” after a dog bite.

The patient’s initial symptoms fit a rapidly progressive sepsis syndrome: high fever, malaise, poor intake, and then peripheral numbness and discoloration suggesting evolving hypoperfusion. By the time she arrived for hospital care, she had mottled, cool extremities, metabolic acidosis, elevated lactate, hypoglycaemia, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy. This pattern is compatible with severe sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation. In sepsis, hypoglycaemia can occur when hepatic glucose production is impaired and peripheral consumption is high, particularly in severe systemic inflammation. Lactic acidosis reflects tissue hypoperfusion and altered cellular metabolism. Coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia arise from systemic activation of coagulation, consumption of platelets and clotting factors, endothelial injury, and impaired hepatic synthesis. Together, these abnormalities can produce a picture of multiorgan dysfunction before a focal source is fully established.

At this early stage, the main differential diagnosis was appropriately broad. Severe sepsis with DIC was the leading explanation because it unified the fever, hypotension risk, thrombocytopenia, hypofibrinogenemia, organ injury, and skin changes. Necrotising soft tissue infection had to be considered because mottled or ischemic extremities after an animal bite can signal deep tissue infection, but clinical assessment and imaging did not support this. Acute haematologic disease, such as leukaemia, and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis were also considered because pancytopenia can be profound in both sepsis and primary marrow disorders. At this point, thrombotic microangiopathy was not yet the most likely diagnosis, because the patient’s initial laboratory pattern was dominated by sepsis physiology and consumptive coagulopathy rather than isolated microangiopathic haemolysis.

The diagnosis of C. canimorsus can be challenging because blood cultures may become positive only after several days, and routine microbiology can miss the organism if suspicion is low. In this case, the pathogen was suspected early because of the dog-bite exposure and the severity of the presentation, and broad-spectrum antibiotics with activity against dog-associated flora were started promptly. Ultimately, blood cultures confirmed the diagnosis. In contemporary practice, molecular methods such as rapid sequencing-based diagnostics can shorten the time to identification, which is important because delay in recognizing C. canimorsus can allow the infection to progress to shock, DIC, renal failure, and other life-threatening complications. This case shows why early empiric coverage after a high-risk dog bite is clinically important and why invasive zoonotic infection must remain on the differential even in previously healthy adults.

## Initial Stabilisation and ICU Course: Managing Septic Shock, DIC, and Dialysis-Dependent Acute Kidney Injury

At presentation, the key clinical problem was not yet a primary haematologic disorder but fulminant sepsis after a dog bite, with early multiorgan dysfunction. The combination of high fever, mottled extremities, lactic acidosis, hypoglycaemia, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy strongly suggested severe systemic infection with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). In a patient who had recently sustained a dog bite, this pattern should immediately raise suspicion for zoonotic pathogens, especially *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, even before cultures are positive. The initial bedside task is to stabilise the patient while simultaneously treating the most likely source and excluding immediately dangerous alternatives.

Empiric antimicrobial therapy was therefore started with broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam. This was a rational choice because it provides coverage for many dog-associated organisms and common skin and soft tissue pathogens while awaiting definitive microbiology. *C. canimorsus* is often slow to grow in culture, so treatment cannot wait for organism identification. In parallel, the mottled and discoloured extremities required assessment for surgical emergencies such as necrotising fasciitis or compartment syndrome. Clinical examination and imaging did not support a necrotising soft tissue infection, allowing management to remain focused on medical sepsis care rather than urgent debridement.

The initial blood tests reflected severe systemic illness. Pancytopenia in this setting can result from sepsis-associated marrow suppression, consumption, cytokine-mediated haemophagocytic physiology, or DIC-related consumption, and it should not be assumed to represent a primary haematologic malignancy without further evidence. Coagulopathy with thrombocytopenia and low fibrinogen is characteristic of DIC, in which widespread coagulation activation consumes platelets and clotting factors. Liver injury, metabolic acidosis, and elevated lactate are markers of poor perfusion and organ hypoxia. The urinalysis showing protein and blood suggested renal involvement early, but preserved urine output at that stage meant kidney injury had not yet become fully oliguric.

Because the patient rapidly evolved into more severe organ dysfunction, she required ICU-level monitoring. The transition from sepsis to septic cardiomyopathy and fluid overload is clinically important: aggressive resuscitation may be necessary early, but once myocardial depression and renal failure develop, further fluid administration can worsen oxygen delivery and tissue oedema. Despite initial attempts with fluids and loop diuretics, she became completely anuric, indicating severe acute tubular injury in the context of shock and systemic inflammation. Continuous renal replacement therapy was appropriate because it provides gentler volume and solute control than intermittent haemodialysis in an unstable septic patient, particularly when fluid overload and cardiac dysfunction are present.

Her bleeding and skin findings in the ICU were consistent with DIC-related microvascular thrombosis. Ecchymoses and distal cyanotic or ischaemic changes, especially when pulses remain present, point to small-vessel occlusion rather than large-artery obstruction. This is an important bedside distinction: the extremity is perfused at the macrovascular level, but the microcirculation is compromised by thrombin generation and fibrin deposition. The presence of distal ischaemic lesions therefore fits severe consumptive coagulopathy in sepsis and does not by itself prove thrombotic microangiopathy, which only became clearer later in the course.

Supportive management of DIC in this case included transfusion of platelets and fibrinogen-containing products when clinically indicated. The goal in sepsis-associated DIC is not to “normalise” coagulation tests, but to treat bleeding risk and replace consumed components when there is active bleeding, procedures, or severe deficiencies. As the underlying infection came under control, the inflammatory profile improved, coagulation parameters normalised, and platelet counts began to recover, all of which argued that the early coagulopathy was indeed driven by sepsis. Once *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia was confirmed, antibiotics were narrowed from broad empiric therapy to ampicillin and then oral penicillin-class therapy, reflecting organism-directed treatment and clinical stabilisation.

This early course matters because it shows how severe sepsis can mask evolving secondary processes. The initial syndrome was dominated by infection, DIC, and shock-related organ dysfunction, and the later diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy was only considered after the patient’s sepsis began to improve but renal failure and thrombocytopenia persisted. Recognising the transition from acute septic multiorgan dysfunction to a new haemolytic process is essential, because it changes both the diagnostic workup and the treatment strategy.

## When the Course Changes: Recognising Superimposed Thrombotic Microangiopathy

In this case, the diagnostic turning point came after the patient’s initial septic illness began to improve. Early on, her pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, and multiorgan dysfunction were plausibly explained by severe sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). However, the later appearance of a new, more specific pattern—an abrupt fall in haemoglobin without overt bleeding, persistent thrombocytopaenia despite falling inflammatory markers, and worsening kidney failure—should prompt clinicians to think beyond DIC and consider a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

A sudden haemoglobin drop in a recovering septic patient is especially important when there is no haemodynamic instability, no obvious source of blood loss, and no large external bleeding. In this patient, the haemoglobin fell rapidly over several days while platelets stayed low and renal dysfunction persisted. The laboratory pattern supported active haemolysis: LDH rose, haptoglobin became undetectable, and transfusion requirements increased. These findings are classic clues that red cells are being mechanically destroyed in the microvasculature rather than lost through bleeding. In TMA, the endothelium is injured and platelet-rich thrombi form in small vessels, leading to red cell fragmentation and organ ischemia.

The peripheral blood smear was crucial. The presence of schistocytes—fragmented red cells—provided direct morphologic evidence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). MAHA is not a final diagnosis; it is a syndrome that tells the clinician that red cells are being sheared while passing through narrowed or obstructed small vessels. Once schistocytes appear in the setting of thrombocytopaenia and organ injury, a TMA must be considered, even if another explanation such as sepsis-related DIC has already been identified. In this case, the smear helped shift the diagnostic framework from generalized consumptive coagulopathy to a more specific endothelial-injury syndrome.

This distinction mattered because DIC no longer fully explained the evolving picture. During the initial septic phase, coagulopathy and thrombocytopaenia were expected. But as infection control improved, coagulation parameters normalised and inflammatory markers recovered, yet haemolysis and thrombocytopaenia persisted. That dissociation is a key teaching point: if the “DIC-like” laboratory abnormalities improve but MAHA continues, the clinician should look for a second process. Persistent thrombocytopaenia and ongoing hemolysis after apparent sepsis recovery are not typical of resolving DIC alone.

The positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) initially introduced another diagnostic possibility: autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. Because DAT positivity can occur in true immune haemolysis, starting corticosteroids was a reasonable early step while more data were gathered. In this case, however, the lack of response to high-dose prednisolone, together with the eventual smear findings and repeat negative DAT, argued strongly against primary autoimmune haemolysis. A false-positive DAT can occur after penicillin exposure or following transfusion, both of which were relevant here. This is an important reminder that a positive DAT should be interpreted in the full clinical context; it does not exclude TMA, and it should not distract from the broader haemolytic workup when the overall pattern is inconsistent with immune haemolysis.

The kidney findings were also highly informative. TMA commonly injures the renal microvasculature, causing acute kidney injury that may progress to oliguria or anuria and require dialysis. In this patient, renal failure worsened after the initial septic phase, and she became completely anuric despite resuscitation, diuretics, and supportive care. Dialysis dependence in the context of MAHA and thrombocytopaenia strongly reinforces the suspicion for TMA, particularly when kidney dysfunction persists after hemodynamic stabilisation. Renal biopsy later confirmed this suspicion by showing fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomerular injury consistent with TMA.

Timing was another major clue. The haemolysis did not peak at the height of sepsis; it emerged later, after the infectious and coagulopathic picture had begun to settle. That sequence is more suggestive of an infection-triggered secondary TMA than of sepsis alone. In practice, this delayed evolution is exactly why TMA can be missed in critically ill patients: the early phase looks like DIC, but the later phase reveals a different biology. Recognising that transition is essential, because the management pathway changes substantially once TMA is suspected.

Overall, the case teaches that in a septic patient, a new pattern of isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopaenia, and renal failure should not be attributed automatically to ongoing infection or DIC. Schistocytes, rising LDH, low haptoglobin, and dialysis-dependent AKI should prompt urgent evaluation for TMA. In this setting, MAHA is the syndrome that signals the need for a targeted diagnostic workup, which in this case ultimately led to the diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome.

## Diagnostic Reasoning in Adult TMA: Distinguishing DIC, TTP, STEC-HUS, and Complement-Mediated HUS

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a clinicopathological syndrome defined by the combination of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and organ injury, most often involving the kidneys and sometimes the brain. In practice, TMA is not a single disease but a shared final pathway of endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis. The clinical challenge in adults is to determine which TMA subtype is present, because the urgent treatments differ substantially. In this case, the patient’s evolving haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury eventually pointed to TMA, but the early septic phase initially made disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) the more plausible explanation.

DIC and TMA overlap because both can present with thrombocytopenia, haemolysis, organ dysfunction, and abnormal coagulation studies. However, DIC is primarily a consumptive coagulopathy driven by systemic coagulation activation, so prolonged clotting times, low fibrinogen, and markedly elevated D-dimer are common. TMA, by contrast, is characterised by platelet-rich microthrombi in the microcirculation and usually produces MAHA with schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, and organ injury without the same degree of global coagulation factor consumption. In real patients, the two can coexist, especially in severe sepsis, and that was part of the diagnostic difficulty here. Early on, the patient’s fever, shock physiology, pancytopenia, hypofibrinogenaemia, and multiorgan dysfunction fit sepsis-associated DIC. Later, however, when coagulation parameters improved but haemolysis persisted and kidney failure worsened, the picture no longer fit DIC alone.

The most urgent TMA diagnosis to exclude in adults is thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), because it can be rapidly fatal but responds dramatically to plasma exchange. The PLASMIC score is a bedside tool that estimates the likelihood of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency while results are pending. Although helpful for triage, it does not confirm the diagnosis. Definitive TTP is defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, typically activity below 10%. In this patient, the PLASMIC score was low and the ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, which effectively ruled out TTP. That value is far above the diagnostic threshold, making classic immune-mediated TTP very unlikely and supporting the decision to look for other causes of TMA.

Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) is another important cause of TMA, classically preceded by diarrhoeal illness, especially bloody diarrhoea, after a gastrointestinal exposure. Stool PCR for Shiga toxin is a useful microbiological test when the history suggests STEC infection. In this case, there was no compatible enteric prodrome, and stool PCR was negative, which argued strongly against STEC-HUS. That exclusion matters because STEC-HUS is managed supportively, whereas complement-mediated HUS may require complement blockade.

Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), historically called atypical HUS, is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion. It is caused by dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, which injures endothelial cells and drives thrombotic microangiopathy. Importantly, normal serum C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS. These levels are often normal, or only transiently abnormal, because routine complement measurements are insensitive to the functional dysregulation that underlies the disease. For that reason, normal C3/C4 should not reassure clinicians when the phenotype strongly suggests complement-mediated TMA.

Functional complement assays can be helpful, but they must be interpreted cautiously. In this case, pathway testing was performed after plasma exchange and after the first dose of eculizumab, both of which can alter circulating complement activity and confound interpretation. The markedly reduced alternative pathway activity was therefore not proof of an inherited complement defect; it was more likely influenced by treatment timing. This illustrates a common pitfall: complement studies are most informative when obtained before plasma products or complement blockade, but in real life treatment often cannot wait.

Kidney biopsy provided key diagnostic support. The biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation, all of which are compatible with renal TMA. In TMA, the biopsy may show endothelial swelling, fibrin-rich thrombi in glomerular capillaries or arterioles, mesangiolysis, and downstream ischaemic injury. Acute tubular necrosis can coexist because renal hypoperfusion and microvascular occlusion both damage the tubules. Crescents are not specific for TMA, but in the setting of vascular thrombotic injury they can reflect severe glomerular insult. The biopsy helped distinguish a microangiopathic process from isolated acute tubular necrosis due to sepsis alone.

Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy are also important in evaluating renal injury because they can identify immune complex deposition or primary glomerular diseases that may mimic or coexist with TMA. In this case, immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition and electron microscopy did not suggest a primary glomerulopathy, which strengthened the diagnosis of isolated TMA rather than immune complex-mediated nephritis or another renal disease causing secondary vascular injury.

The workup for complement-mediated TMA also includes evaluation for genetic or acquired dysregulation of complement regulation. Pathogenic variants may involve complement factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein CD46, and some patients have anti-factor H antibodies. These abnormalities reduce control of the alternative pathway and create a predisposition to endothelial injury. However, the absence of an identifiable mutation does not exclude CM-HUS. Penetrance is incomplete, and many patients only manifest disease after an external trigger. This “trigger-plus-susceptibility” model is central to understanding the case: an infectious insult may precipitate complement activation and TMA even when no pathogenic variant is found.

The final diagnosis rested on synthesis rather than any single test. The patient had clear evidence of severe infection after a dog bite, with Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia and an early DIC-like phase. Later, as sepsis improved, she developed persistent thrombocytopenia, sudden isolated haemolysis, schistocytosis on peripheral smear, and dialysis-dependent renal failure. TTP was excluded by a non-severely reduced ADAMTS13 level, STEC-HUS by negative stool PCR and absent diarrhoeal prodrome, and renal biopsy confirmed a thrombotic microangiopathy. Normal C3/C4 did not rule out CM-HUS, and the clinical response to plasma exchange followed by eculizumab supported complement-mediated disease. Together, these findings justified the diagnosis of C. canimorsus-triggered CM-HUS, likely arising from infection-triggered complement dysregulation rather than a demonstrable inherited complement defect.

## Treatment Strategy, Outcome, and Clinical Lessons from Infection-Triggered Complement-Mediated HUS

Once thrombotic microangiopathy became a serious possibility, management had to proceed before every diagnostic question was resolved. In adults with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopaenia, and acute kidney injury, the first priority is to exclude thrombotic thrombocytopaenic purpura (TTP), because that condition is rapidly fatal without urgent plasma exchange. At the same time, supportive care must continue: treatment of the underlying infection, dialysis if needed, blood product support when clinically indicated, and close monitoring for evolving haemolysis or organ dysfunction. In this patient, the clinical picture was initially confounded by sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation, so the later appearance of isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopaenia, schistocytes, and worsening renal injury prompted reconsideration of the diagnosis.

Plasma exchange was started as a bridge while TTP was being excluded and while definitive complement-directed therapy was being arranged. This is a common pragmatic step in severe TMA when the subtype is not yet established. Plasma exchange is lifesaving in TTP because it removes anti-ADAMTS13 autoantibodies and replaces deficient ADAMTS13, but in complement-mediated HUS it does not correct the underlying driver of disease. Its benefit in complement-mediated HUS is therefore temporary and indirect, mainly through removal of circulating inflammatory mediators or autoantibodies. In this case, the transient return of urine output after plasma exchange likely reflected short-lived stabilisation rather than true disease control.

Eculizumab is the key disease-specific treatment for complement-mediated HUS. It is a monoclonal antibody against complement component C5, preventing formation of the terminal complement complex and thereby limiting ongoing endothelial injury, platelet activation, and microvascular thrombosis. Because complement dysregulation is central to the pathophysiology of CM-HUS, C5 inhibition is the standard therapy and offers the best chance of haematologic remission and renal recovery. The urgency of treatment is important: the longer the delay to complement blockade, the greater the risk of incomplete renal recovery and chronic kidney disease.

Starting eculizumab in the setting of recent bacteraemia requires careful balancing. On the one hand, active or recent infection can trigger CM-HUS and ongoing complement activation may be worsening renal injury; on the other hand, terminal complement is important for defence against encapsulated bacteria, so complement blockade increases susceptibility to serious infection. In this patient, eculizumab was begun after blood cultures had become negative, which reduced the risk of uncontrolled infection while still allowing prompt treatment of the TMA. This timing illustrates the practical challenge in infection-triggered CM-HUS: clinicians must control the infection, but also avoid waiting so long that the kidney injury becomes irreversible.

Because terminal complement blockade markedly increases the risk of meningococcal disease, vaccination is required before treatment whenever possible. This patient received meningococcal vaccines covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W, as well as serogroup B. Ideally, vaccination should be completed at least 2 weeks before the first dose of eculizumab. When treatment cannot be delayed, antimicrobial prophylaxis is used. In this case, penicillin prophylaxis was given because therapy had to begin before the recommended vaccine-to-treatment interval had elapsed. This is an important safety step whenever urgent complement inhibition is necessary.

Response to treatment was assessed clinically and biochemically. The most useful markers were platelet recovery, falling markers of haemolysis such as LDH, restoration of haptoglobin, rising haemoglobin without ongoing bleeding, return of urine output, and eventual improvement in dialysis dependence and creatinine. In this patient, platelets began to recover and spontaneous diuresis returned after plasma exchange, but the sustained improvement followed eculizumab, supporting the diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS rather than a transient sepsis-related TMA alone. She achieved haematologic remission and partial renal recovery, although kidney function did not return to baseline.

Longer-term management depends partly on whether a pathogenic complement variant is identified. In patients with a clear inherited or autoimmune complement abnormality, prolonged therapy is often favored because relapse risk is higher. In this case, extended complement genetic testing did not identify a pathogenic variant, so the team planned reassessment for eventual discontinuation if renal function stabilises. Even without a detected mutation, continued treatment may still be justified after a severe presentation, especially when the trigger was an infection and the initial renal injury was profound. The decision is individualized and depends on the severity of the episode, recovery trajectory, and perceived relapse risk.

This case reinforces several practical lessons. First, new TMA after sepsis should not be attributed automatically to DIC or infection alone; a second process such as CM-HUS may emerge as sepsis improves. Second, normal C3 and C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS, because complement consumption may be absent or subtle on routine testing. Third, a changing clinical trajectory matters: the reappearance of isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopaenia, and biopsy-proven TMA should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis even after apparent recovery from sepsis. Finally, rare infections such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus can act as triggers for complement-mediated TMA, so unusual infectious exposures should be part of the diagnostic reasoning in adults with haemolysis, thrombocytopaenia, and AKI.
