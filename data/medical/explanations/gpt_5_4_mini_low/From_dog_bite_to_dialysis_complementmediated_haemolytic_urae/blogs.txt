Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When DIC Isn’t the Whole Story: Spotting TMA After Sepsis Begins to Improve

## The twist in this case

This case starts where a lot of ICU stories start: a previously healthy woman, a dog bite, rapidly progressive sepsis, DIC, AKI, and multiorgan dysfunction. Nothing about the first few hours screams “zebra.” In fact, the early picture fit sepsis so well that DIC seemed like the obvious unifying diagnosis.

But the real teaching point is what happened next.

As the infection markers improved and the coagulopathy settled, the patient developed a new pattern: persistent thrombocytopaenia, falling haemoglobin without bleeding, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes on smear, and worsening kidney injury that had already progressed to dialysis dependence. That’s the moment to stop thinking about “just sepsis labs” and start asking whether there is a second process layered on top: thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

In this case, that second process was complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection.

## Why this is easy to miss

Sepsis can absolutely cause thrombocytopaenia, AKI, lactic acidosis, and even DIC. That overlap is what makes TMA so slippery in the early phase. If a patient is already critically ill, it is tempting to attribute every abnormality to one diagnosis and keep moving.

The problem is that DIC and TMA are not interchangeable, and the treatment implications are very different.

DIC is a consumptive coagulopathy driven by systemic inflammation and coagulation activation. TMA, by contrast, is a microvascular platelet-rich thrombotic process that causes:

- microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA)
- thrombocytopaenia
- organ injury, especially kidney injury

They can coexist, but when the infection begins to improve and the haemolysis becomes more obvious, you need to re-open the differential.

## The pivot point: when to suspect a superimposed TMA

The key clinical shift here was not at presentation. It was later, when the expected sepsis trajectory and the laboratory trajectory stopped matching.

Red flags for TMA in a recovering septic patient include:

- haemoglobin falling without bleeding
- persistent or worsening thrombocytopaenia despite improvement in sepsis
- new schistocytes on peripheral smear
- undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH
- AKI that is out of proportion, persistent, or worsening
- ongoing organ ischemia/cyanosis despite resolution of shock

In this patient, the initial picture was interpreted as sepsis-associated DIC, which was reasonable. But once the inflammatory profile improved and haemostasis normalized, the continued thrombocytopaenia and abrupt isolated haemolysis were hard to explain by DIC alone.

That is the diagnostic hinge.

## DIC vs TMA: how to keep them straight

Clinically, both syndromes can present with thrombocytopaenia and organ dysfunction. The lab pattern helps.

DIC tends to feature:

- prolonged coagulation tests
- low fibrinogen
- elevated D-dimer
- thrombocytopaenia
- bleeding tendency

TMA tends to feature:

- MAHA with schistocytes
- thrombocytopaenia
- organ ischemia, especially renal
- relatively less prominent coagulation abnormalities

This patient initially had DIC-like features in the setting of sepsis. Later, the coagulation abnormalities improved, but the haemolysis declared itself. That evolution should raise suspicion that you are no longer dealing with a pure consumptive coagulopathy.

## The differential diagnosis of TMA: don’t anchor too early

Once you decide “this is TMA,” the next step is not to leap straight to treatment for CM-HUS. First, exclude the emergencies that change management immediately.

### 1) TTP
This is the must-not-miss diagnosis.

- Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, usually <10%, supports TTP.
- In this case, ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, making TTP unlikely.
- The PLASMIC score was 4/7, which was low probability.

This matters because plasma exchange is lifesaving in TTP. If you suspect it, don’t wait.

### 2) STEC-HUS
Another key consideration, especially if diarrhoeal prodrome is present.

- Here, stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative.
- There was no enteric prodrome.

### 3) CM-HUS
Once TTP and STEC-HUS are off the table, CM-HUS becomes much more likely, particularly when there is:

- persistent MAHA
- thrombocytopaenia
- kidney injury
- biopsy evidence of TMA
- a trigger such as infection

Normal C3/C4 do not exclude it. That’s an important pearl.

## Why the dog bite matters

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is an unusual but very real zoonotic pathogen. It lives in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats, and human infection can follow bites, scratches, or even close contact.

Most clinicians associate it with fulminant sepsis, DIC, and multiorgan failure. This case adds another layer: it can also be the trigger for complement-mediated TMA.

That makes biological sense. Complement is part of host defence, but in susceptible settings, infectious activation can overshoot and damage the endothelium, tipping the patient into TMA.

## Renal biopsy: the diagnosis got real

The renal biopsy in this case was valuable because it showed the tissue-level story:

- severe acute tubular necrosis
- ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi
- scattered crescent formation
- findings consistent with TMA

This is the kind of biopsy that helps convert suspicion into confidence. When the clinical picture is noisy, pathology can tell you whether there is an active microangiopathic process beyond septic AKI.

## Treatment: why plasma exchange was only a bridge

This is where the management becomes nuanced.

Plasma exchange was started while the diagnostic workup was pending. That is appropriate when TTP is still in play, and it can provide temporary stabilisation in other TMAs. But it does not fix the underlying complement dysregulation in CM-HUS.

Eculizumab did.

In this patient:

- haemolysis did not respond to corticosteroids
- plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy
- eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative
- haematologic remission followed
- kidney function partially recovered

That sequence is important. If you see transient improvement after plasma exchange but the platelet count and haemolysis don’t truly normalize, keep looking.

## The infection-treatment tension

One of the hardest parts of this case is the timing of complement blockade during a recent bacteraemia. That concern is real: terminal complement is important for defence against encapsulated organisms.

The team mitigated risk by:

- treating the infection aggressively with antibiotics
- confirming culture clearance before eculizumab
- vaccinating against meningococcal disease
- giving antibiotic prophylaxis

That’s the practical balance: don’t withhold definitive CM-HUS therapy indefinitely, but don’t ignore the infectious risk either.

## Practical bedside lessons

The major teaching point is not “dog bites can cause rare complement disease,” though they can. The bigger lesson is diagnostic discipline in septic patients whose labs stop behaving like sepsis.

When the infection is improving but the blood count is not, think beyond DIC.

### Take-home points

- Sepsis can explain a lot, but persistent thrombocytopaenia plus MAHA is not “just critical illness” until proven otherwise.
- Reassess when the trajectory changes: improving inflammatory markers with worsening haemolysis is a classic pivot point.
- DIC and TMA can overlap, but schistocytes, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, and ongoing kidney injury should prompt a TMA workup.
- In adults with TMA, urgently exclude TTP with ADAMTS13 testing; don’t wait to act if TTP is strongly suspected.
- Normal C3/C4 do not rule out CM-HUS.
- Plasma exchange may bridge while the diagnosis is being clarified, but eculizumab is the disease-specific therapy for CM-HUS.
- Unusual infections, including *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, can act as triggers for complement-mediated TMA.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that sepsis can be the opening act, not the whole play. In a patient with initially convincing DIC, the diagnosis should be reconsidered when the infection improves but thrombocytopaenia, haemolysis, and renal injury persist.

That is the moment to ask: is this still DIC, or has TMA joined the party?

In the ICU and on the ward, that question can be the difference between supportive care and a disease-specific treatment path that saves kidney function—and sometimes the patient.



# Dog Bite, Pancytopenia, Shock: Why *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* Belongs on the Early Differential

## The weird part of this case

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s got a dog bite on the lip, didn’t start the prescribed penicillin, and 4 days later showed up very sick: fever >40°C, hypoglycaemia, mottled extremities, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury, and lactic acidosis. That’s not “simple cellulitis after a dog bite.” That’s an ICU problem until proven otherwise.

The organism was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a slow-growing, fastidious gram-negative rod from the oral flora of dogs and cats. It’s a classic reminder that the wound can look minor while the physiology is screaming sepsis.

## Why this bug should be on your radar

Most dog bites do not turn into fulminant multiorgan failure. But when *Capnocytophaga* does invade, it can move fast and hit hard:

- septic shock
- DIC/coagulopathy
- AKI and dialysis-requiring renal failure
- leukopaenia or pancytopenia
- microvascular thrombosis and skin ischemia
- occasionally thrombotic microangiopathy

And crucially: this can happen even in immunocompetent adults. Asplenia, alcoholism, cirrhosis, and immunosuppression raise the risk, but their absence does **not** make the diagnosis go away.

## The emergency medicine lesson: trust the physiology, not the bite mark

The initial temptation is to anchor on the bite and underestimate the illness. Don’t.

In this case, the early syndrome looked like severe sepsis with DIC:
- fever
- pancytopenia
- coagulopathy
- elevated lactate
- AKI
- mottled, cool extremities

That’s enough to justify immediate broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and ICU-level monitoring. The patient was started on piperacillin/tazobactam, which is very reasonable empiric coverage for a severely ill patient after dog exposure.

### Empiric antibiotic takeaway
For a patient who is systemically ill after a dog bite, think beyond “bite prophylaxis” and treat for invasive zoonotic infection.

Practical points:
- start antibiotics early if there is any concern for systemic illness
- piperacillin/tazobactam is a strong empiric choice in severe presentations
- *Capnocytophaga* is generally susceptible to beta-lactams, but do not wait for culture confirmation before treating
- if the patient is in shock or has organ dysfunction, this is not outpatient bite management

## Why the diagnosis is often delayed

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a frustrating organism:
- slow-growing
- fastidious
- can take days to confirm on blood cultures
- sometimes requires molecular methods for faster detection

In this case, blood cultures were positive 7 days later. That lag matters because the clinical picture evolves while everyone is still trying to name the bug.

If your patient is crashing after dog exposure, do not wait for culture proof to act.

## The ICU trap: sepsis, DIC, or something more?

This case gets especially interesting because the early course was dominated by sepsis and DIC, but then the picture changed.

After initial improvement in inflammation and coagulation, she developed:
- sudden isolated hemoglobin drop
- persistent thrombocytopenia
- undetectable haptoglobin
- rising LDH
- schistocytes on smear
- worsening kidney injury

That’s the moment to widen the differential beyond “resolving sepsis.”

The key diagnostic pivot is recognizing **thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)** when the classic triad appears:
- microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia
- thrombocytopenia
- organ injury, especially kidney injury

In this patient, the later haemolysis was not just DIC marching on. It was a superimposed TMA, ultimately diagnosed as complement-mediated HUS.

## DIC and TMA can coexist — don’t force a single label too early

One of the hardest parts of this case is that the patient really did look like DIC early on. And that was a reasonable interpretation.

But DIC and TMA can blur together clinically:
- both can present with thrombocytopenia
- both can cause organ dysfunction
- both may show ischemic skin findings
- both may occur in sepsis

The clue that should make you pause is **persistent or new haemolysis with schistocytes**, especially when coagulation parameters are improving but renal failure and thrombocytopenia are not.

## The hemolysis workup that matters

Once MAHA/TMA enters the differential, the priorities are to distinguish:
- TTP
- STEC-HUS
- complement-mediated HUS

In this case:
- PLASMIC score was 4/7, so TTP was less likely
- ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP
- stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin, arguing against STEC-HUS
- kidney biopsy showed TMA

That made complement-mediated HUS the best fit.

### Diagnostic pearl
Normal C3/C4 do **not** exclude complement-mediated HUS. Don’t let “normal complement levels” falsely reassure you.

## Why this matters in the ED and ICU

The management implications are huge.

If you think this is:
- **TTP** → urgent plasma exchange is lifesaving
- **STEC-HUS** → supportive care
- **CM-HUS** → complement blockade with eculizumab is the disease-specific therapy

In the real world, you often have to start something before the answer is in. In this case, plasma exchange was used as a bridge while awaiting ADAMTS13 and other studies, then eculizumab was started once the diagnosis settled and blood cultures had cleared.

## The complement blockade dilemma

Eculizumab is highly effective for CM-HUS, but it raises an important infectious risk question in a patient with recent bacteremia: terminal complement is important for defense against encapsulated organisms.

That’s a real concern here, especially because *Capnocytophaga* itself is a complement-sensitive pathogen. The team waited until blood cultures were negative, gave meningococcal vaccination, and used antibiotic prophylaxis.

This is a good example of balancing two competing hazards:
- uncontrolled complement-mediated endothelial injury
- risk of worsening infection under complement blockade

## Clinical pearls from this case

- A dog bite plus shock, DIC, AKI, or pancytopenia is an ICU problem, not a routine wound visit.
- *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* can cause fulminant sepsis even in immunocompetent adults.
- Blood cultures may take days to turn positive because the organism is fastidious and slow growing.
- If the patient improves from sepsis but then develops schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia, and haemolysis, think TMA.
- Normal complement levels do not rule out complement-mediated HUS.
- ADAMTS13 is the key test to help separate TTP from other TMAs.
- Plasma exchange can be a bridge, but eculizumab is the disease-specific treatment for CM-HUS.
- In severe post–dog bite sepsis, treat early with broad IV antibiotics and do not wait for microbiologic confirmation.

## Bottom line

Dog bites are common; *Capnocytophaga* sepsis is not. But when it happens, it can look like catastrophic multisystem disease with DIC, renal failure, and later TMA. The takeaway is simple: if a patient looks far sicker than the wound suggests, widen the lens early.

That means:
- think *Capnocytophaga* after dog exposure
- start broad empiric antibiotics promptly
- monitor closely for DIC, AKI, and evolving haemolysis
- keep TMA on the table if the picture changes

This case is a reminder that the “bite” may be the smallest part of the story.



# MAHA in the ICU: TTP, STEC-HUS, CM-HUS, or Something Else?

## The case that keeps you honest

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s was bitten on the lip by her dog, didn’t start the prescribed antibiotic, and then returned four days later with sepsis, DIC, AKI, and a scary-looking peripheral circulation. Blood cultures eventually grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. So far, so bad—but not rare enough to be shocking.

What makes this case memorable is what happened next. After the initial septic phase began to settle, she developed isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, dialysis-dependent AKI, and a kidney biopsy consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). That’s the pivot point: when “just sepsis/DIC” stops explaining the picture, you have to start actively separating TTP from STEC-HUS and complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

This is the ICU trap: MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI all look similar at the bedside, but the treatment paths diverge fast.

## First principles: what counts as TMA?

Think of TMA as a syndrome, not a diagnosis. The classic bedside triad is:

- Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)
- Thrombocytopenia
- End-organ injury, especially kidney and brain

In practice, the signal comes from a cluster:

- Falling hemoglobin without bleeding
- Rising LDH
- Undetectable or low haptoglobin
- Schistocytes on smear
- Thrombocytopenia
- AKI out of proportion to other causes

In a sick ICU patient, sepsis, DIC, drug effects, marrow suppression, and mechanical hemolysis can muddy the waters. The job is not to prove TMA immediately; it’s to recognize when the pattern has escaped “routine sepsis.”

## The first fork in the road: TTP until proven otherwise

Among adult TMAs, TTP is the one you cannot afford to miss. Why? Because it kills quickly and responds dramatically to plasma exchange.

The key diagnostic concept is ADAMTS13 deficiency:

- **ADAMTS13 activity <10% strongly supports TTP**
- Values above that make TTP much less likely

While awaiting ADAMTS13, bedside triage scores like **PLASMIC** can help estimate pretest probability. In this case, the PLASMIC score was 4/7, which is low. That doesn’t rule out TTP on its own, but it makes it less likely.

Pragmatically:
- If you have **MAHA + thrombocytopenia** and TTP is plausible, **don’t wait for ADAMTS13** to treat
- Start plasma exchange if the clinical picture supports it
- Send ADAMTS13 early, before plasma products if possible, but treatment should not be delayed when suspicion is high

In this patient, ADAMTS13 activity returned at **43.5%**, which effectively argued against TTP.

## Don’t get distracted by DIC

This case is a good reminder that DIC and TMA can coexist or mimic each other early on.

Sepsis can cause:
- Thrombocytopenia
- Coagulopathy
- AKI
- Hemolysis-like lab abnormalities

But classic DIC usually gives you:
- Prolonged coagulation parameters
- Low fibrinogen
- High D-dimer
- A clinical context of severe infection/shock

This patient initially looked exactly like septic DIC, which was a reasonable first interpretation. The problem is that later she developed a more specific hemolytic picture:

- Hemoglobin dropped abruptly
- Haptoglobin became undetectable
- LDH rose
- Schistocytes appeared
- Thrombocytopenia persisted

That evolution should trigger a second look.

## Smear review still matters

The peripheral smear is one of the highest-yield tests in the TMA workup.

Schistocytes support MAHA, and in the right context they point toward TMA rather than isolated marrow suppression or anemia of critical illness.

This patient’s smear eventually showed **15%–20% schistocytes**, which is hard to ignore.

A few practical points:
- Get the smear reviewed early
- Don’t over-rely on a single “normal” early hemolysis panel
- Hemolysis can evolve over time, especially if the initial syndrome is dominated by sepsis

## DAT-positive does not automatically mean autoimmune hemolysis

This case had a useful diagnostic pitfall: the direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs) was initially positive for IgG, which raised concern for autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

But the overall story didn’t fit:
- No bleeding
- Persistent thrombocytopenia
- Schistocytes
- Rising LDH
- Undetectable haptoglobin
- No response to steroids

Repeat DAT was negative, and the initial positivity was felt to be false positive, likely related to penicillin exposure or transfusion.

Take-home: a positive DAT does **not** exclude TMA, and it should not anchor you into autoimmune hemolysis if the rest of the picture screams MAHA.

## STEC-HUS: yes, adults can get it

Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*–associated HUS is classically pediatric, but adults can absolutely get it. The clue is usually a diarrheal prodrome, though not always dramatic.

The key test is **Shiga toxin testing**, often by stool PCR.

In this case:
- Stool PCR was negative
- There was no enteric prodrome
- The course fit an infection-triggered TMA better than STEC-HUS

So if you’re sorting adult TMA in real time:
- Ask about diarrheal illness early
- Send stool Shiga toxin/PCR promptly
- But don’t let a negative GI story prematurely close the differential

## When to think CM-HUS

Once TTP and STEC-HUS are less likely, **CM-HUS** moves up the list.

This is the complement-mediated, non-STEC, non-TTP TMA syndrome historically called “atypical HUS.” It’s often triggered by a second hit:
- Infection
- Pregnancy
- Autoimmune disease
- Drugs

This case is especially interesting because the trigger was **Capnocytophaga canimorsus**, a rare dog-associated pathogen. The infection dominated early, and only later did the complement-mediated TMA declare itself.

A few pearls:
- **Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS**
- CM-HUS is a **clinicopathologic diagnosis of exclusion**
- Complement genetics may be normal
- A trigger does not have to be “classic” to matter

In this patient, the key clue was timing: hemolysis emerged **after** the septic picture started improving, which is a pattern that should make you think of a superimposed complement-mediated process rather than pure infection-related consumptive coagulopathy.

## Kidney biopsy: not first-line, but very helpful when the dust settles

In unstable ICU patients, kidney biopsy is usually not the first move. But once the platelet count recovers enough to make it feasible, biopsy can be very helpful when the diagnosis remains uncertain.

This patient’s biopsy showed:
- Severe acute tubular necrosis
- Ischemic glomeruli
- Fibrin thrombi
- Findings consistent with TMA

Biopsy doesn’t replace the clinical diagnosis, but it can:
- Support TMA when labs are equivocal
- Help distinguish glomerular disease from tubular injury
- Strengthen the case for complement blockade when the diagnosis is uncertain

## A practical ICU approach to MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI

When the triad shows up, I find it useful to think in this order:

1. **Confirm the syndrome**
   - CBC, smear, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, retic count
   - Renal function and urinalysis
   - Coagulation studies for DIC

2. **Rule out TTP early**
   - Send ADAMTS13
   - Use PLASMIC as a triage tool, not a crutch
   - If suspicion is high, start plasma exchange immediately

3. **Look for STEC-HUS**
   - Stool Shiga toxin/PCR
   - GI prodrome
   - Exposure history

4. **Reassess the sepsis/DIC story**
   - Is coagulopathy improving but MAHA worsening?
   - Is thrombocytopenia persisting after infection control?

5. **Consider CM-HUS**
   - Especially if TTP/STEC are unlikely
   - Particularly if renal failure is prominent
   - Consider complement testing, recognizing that normal C3/C4 does not rule it out

6. **Use biopsy selectively**
   - When safe and when it will change management

## Plasma exchange: bridge, not cure, in CM-HUS

This is one of the most important management distinctions.

- In **TTP**, plasma exchange is the definitive acute therapy
- In **CM-HUS**, plasma exchange may transiently stabilize but does not fix the underlying complement dysregulation

In this case, PEX was used as bridging therapy while the diagnosis was being clarified and before eculizumab could be started. That’s a very reasonable move when the distinction between TTP and CM-HUS is still unresolved.

## Eculizumab: powerful, but don’t forget the infection risk

Eculizumab changed the game for CM-HUS, but it comes with a major caveat: terminal complement blockade increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms.

That matters here because the patient had:
- Recent bacteremia
- A dog-bite-associated pathogen
- A still-recent active infectious process

In this case, eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative, with:
- Meningococcal vaccination
- Antibiotic prophylaxis
- Close monitoring

That’s the balancing act: you want to treat the TMA early enough to preserve kidneys, but not so recklessly that you worsen an active infection.

## What this case teaches

This patient’s course is a reminder that ICU TMA is often a moving target. Early on, the story looked like septic DIC from *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Later, the persistence of thrombocytopenia plus a new hemolytic pattern forced a broader differential, and the diagnosis ultimately became CM-HUS.

The big mistake would have been to stop at “sepsis explains everything.”

## Take-home points

- In adults with **MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI**, think **TMA** and move fast.
- **TTP is the emergency diagnosis**: send ADAMTS13, use PLASMIC to triage, and treat empirically if suspicion is high.
- **Normal C3/C4 do not rule out CM-HUS**.
- **Negative Shiga toxin testing** and lack of diarrheal prodrome make STEC-HUS less likely, but not impossible without proper testing.
- A **positive DAT does not exclude TMA**; don’t let it anchor you.
- **Peripheral smear review is essential**—schistocytes matter.
- **Kidney biopsy** can be very helpful once it’s safe, especially when the diagnosis is still unclear.
- In **CM-HUS**, plasma exchange is a bridge; **eculizumab** is the disease-modifying therapy.
- When infection is the trigger, management requires a careful balance between **complement blockade** and **infection control**.

## Bottom line

Not every ICU patient with thrombocytopenia and renal failure has DIC. If the hemolysis evolves, the smear turns positive, and the kidneys keep failing even as sepsis improves, you need to widen the lens.

That’s where the real diagnostic art lives: separating TTP from STEC-HUS and CM-HUS quickly enough to give the right therapy before the kidneys are gone.



# Normal C3, Normal C4, Still aHUS: The Complement Testing Trap

## The case that should make you nervous about “normal complement”

Here’s the trap: a patient develops thrombotic microangiopathy after sepsis, the routine complement panel comes back normal, and everyone breathes a little easier. But in complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), that reassurance can be false.

This case is a great reminder that **normal C3 and C4 do not rule out CM-HUS**. In fact, this patient had a classic clinical evolution that should keep aHUS on the table: infection-triggered shock, DIC-like early labs, then later **persistent thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, dialysis-requiring AKI, schistocytes, and kidney biopsy evidence of TMA**. The complement studies looked “not impressive,” but the disease was very real.

## Why this is so easy to miss

Early on, the picture looked like severe sepsis with DIC from *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia after a dog bite. That makes sense. It also makes TMA harder to see.

In this case, the first phase was dominated by:
- Fever, lactic acidosis, pancytopenia
- Coagulopathy consistent with DIC
- Progressive AKI, then anuria
- Skin mottling and distal ischemic changes

Later, after the infection was improving, the lab pattern shifted:
- Hemoglobin dropped abruptly
- Haptoglobin became undetectable
- LDH rose
- Platelets stayed low
- Smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes

That transition matters. When hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persist or worsen after the septic physiology starts settling, you should think beyond “just DIC.”

## The complement testing trap

Routine complement testing usually means **C3 and C4**. Those are useful in some immune-complex diseases, but they are **not sensitive screening tests for CM-HUS**.

Why not?

Because CM-HUS is a disorder of **complement regulation**, not simply a state of low circulating complement levels. A patient can have:
- Normal C3
- Normal C4
- Even normal functional complement assays, depending on timing and treatment

That does **not** exclude complement-mediated TMA.

This is exactly why CM-HUS remains a **clinical diagnosis of exclusion**. The diagnosis is built from the whole picture:
- MAHA
- Thrombocytopenia
- AKI
- Exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS
- Supportive biopsy findings when available
- Clinical response to complement blockade

## Don’t let treatment contaminate the labs

Another key point in this case: **the timing of testing mattered**.

Extended complement testing was obtained after:
- Multiple sessions of plasma exchange
- One induction dose of eculizumab

That creates a major interpretation problem. Plasma exchange can remove complement components and circulating factors. Eculizumab changes terminal complement activity. So if your extended complement workup looks “abnormal” or “blunted” after treatment, you may be seeing the **effect of therapy**, not the patient’s baseline biology.

In this patient, the reduced alternative pathway activity was thought to be most consistent with treatment effects rather than a primary intrinsic depletion state.

## Practical approach to suspected CM-HUS

When the presentation fits TMA, don’t wait for complement studies to decide whether the syndrome exists. First, rule out the urgent look-alikes:

### 1) Exclude TTP
- Send ADAMTS13 urgently
- Use a clinical score such as PLASMIC to guide urgency
- Severe deficiency (<10%) supports TTP

In this case, ADAMTS13 was 43.5%, which made TTP unlikely.

### 2) Exclude STEC-HUS
- Stool PCR for Shiga toxin-producing organisms
- Look for diarrheal prodrome, though absence doesn’t fully exclude it

This patient’s stool PCR was negative.

### 3) Look for evidence of TMA
- Schistocytes
- Falling haptoglobin
- Rising LDH
- Thrombocytopenia
- AKI out of proportion to hemodynamics
- Biopsy if needed and feasible

### 4) Treat if the clinical picture is convincing
If CM-HUS is likely, **don’t be falsely reassured by normal C3/C4**. Complement blockade with eculizumab is the disease-specific therapy. Plasma exchange may serve as a bridge when TTP is still in the differential, but it is not definitive CM-HUS therapy.

## What this case teaches about infection-triggered CM-HUS

This patient had no known pathogenic complement variant identified, which makes the case even more instructive. It supports the idea that a major infection can act as the trigger for complement-mediated TMA even in someone without an obvious inherited complement defect.

The likely sequence:
1. Dog bite inoculates *C. canimorsus*
2. Severe sepsis and DIC-like physiology develop
3. As the infection evolves, endothelial injury and complement activation tip the patient into TMA
4. CM-HUS declares itself only after the initial septic phase starts to improve

That delayed hematologic shift is a huge clue.

## Pearls for the bedside

- **Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS.**
- CM-HUS is a **clinical diagnosis of exclusion**, not a lab-panel diagnosis.
- In suspected TMA, **rule out TTP and STEC-HUS first**.
- **Timing matters**: plasma exchange and eculizumab can distort extended complement testing.
- A patient whose “sepsis” is improving but who develops **new schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening AKI** deserves reconsideration for TMA.
- Kidney biopsy, when safe, can be very helpful in confirming TMA.

## Take-home points

- **Do not use normal C3/C4 to rule out CM-HUS.**
- **Complement studies can be misleading after plasma exchange or eculizumab.**
- **If the clinical syndrome fits TMA, act on the syndrome—not the screening complement panel.**
- **Persistent hemolysis and thrombocytopenia after sepsis improvement should trigger a second look for CM-HUS.**
- **Early recognition matters because time-to-eculizumab strongly affects renal recovery.**



# Eculizumab During or After Infection? Managing Complement Blockade in Infection-Triggered HUS

## The uncomfortable part of this case

This case sits right in the clinical danger zone: a previously healthy woman develops fulminant sepsis after a dog bite, cultures grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, and the initial picture screams DIC. Then, just as the infection starts to come under control, the story shifts—sudden hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, worsening kidney failure, schistocytes, and biopsy-proven thrombotic microangiopathy.

That’s the moment the management gets tricky.

Because if this is complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), delaying eculizumab risks ongoing endothelial injury, dialysis dependence, and incomplete renal recovery. But if active infection is still in play, complement blockade can feel uncomfortably like disabling one of the host’s core defenses against encapsulated bacteria.

This case is a good reminder that in infection-triggered TMA, the real question is often not “Should we treat?” but “How do we treat without making a bad infection worse?”

## First: don’t miss the evolving phenotype

Early in the course, this patient looked like severe sepsis with DIC:

- fever
- pancytopenia
- coagulopathy
- lactic acidosis
- AKI
- mottled extremities
- later, diffuse bleeding and acral ischemic changes

That is a very reasonable initial frame. In fact, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a classic “sick dog bite” pathogen that can cause exactly this kind of fulminant systemic illness.

But the pivot came later, when the infection markers improved and the hematologic picture worsened in a way that didn’t fit simple recovery:

- isolated hemoglobin drop
- persistent thrombocytopenia
- undetectable haptoglobin
- rising LDH
- schistocytes on smear
- dialysis-dependent AKI

That combination should trigger a TMA workup, even if the patient is already labeled as “sepsis/DIC.”

## Why this becomes a complement problem

The differential in adult TMA is always the same short list:

- TTP
- STEC-HUS
- complement-mediated HUS
- secondary TMA from severe illness, drugs, pregnancy, autoimmune disease, malignancy, etc.

In this case:

- ADAMTS13 was 43.5%, which makes TTP very unlikely
- stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin, arguing against STEC-HUS
- kidney biopsy showed TMA
- the clinical course improved after complement blockade

That’s enough to support CM-HUS, even with normal C3/C4. Normal complement levels do **not** rule out CM-HUS.

The key clinical clue is timing: the hemolysis emerged as sepsis began to improve, which is exactly when an infection can act as the trigger for complement dysregulation rather than being the sole cause of the hematologic injury.

## The therapeutic tension: infection vs complement blockade

Eculizumab is the disease-specific therapy for CM-HUS. It blocks C5 and shuts down terminal complement activation, which can rapidly stop the microangiopathic process.

But terminal complement is also important for defense against encapsulated organisms. That’s why the main concern with eculizumab is not theoretical—it’s real infectious risk, especially meningococcal disease.

So how do you manage a patient with suspected CM-HUS while infection is still unfolding?

## Plasma exchange as a bridge, not a destination

This is where plasma exchange often enters the story.

In TTP, PEX is lifesaving and should not be delayed. In CM-HUS, it’s not definitive therapy, but it can serve as a bridge while you sort out the diagnosis and prepare for eculizumab.

In this case, plasma exchange was started while:

- ADAMTS13 was pending
- the diagnosis was still evolving
- the team was preparing for complement blockade

That’s a reasonable move when TTP remains on the table or when the patient is deteriorating and you need temporizing therapy.

But the important pearl: in CM-HUS, PEX may transiently stabilize the situation without truly fixing the problem. Don’t mistake short-lived improvement for resolution.

## When to start eculizumab

There isn’t a universal “day X” rule, but the principle is straightforward:

- start eculizumab when CM-HUS is sufficiently likely
- do not wait for full genetic confirmation
- do not delay solely because complement levels are normal
- in infection-triggered cases, balance the urgency of renal/hematologic rescue against the state of bacteremia

In this case, eculizumab was started after blood cultures had become negative, which is a sensible compromise when feasible. That reduces the risk of giving terminal complement blockade during uncontrolled bacteremia.

Still, the clinical reality is that some patients will not afford you that luxury. If the TMA is rapidly progressive, waiting too long may mean irreversible kidney injury. In that setting, the risk-benefit balance may still favor earlier treatment, but it should be a deliberate decision with infectious disease, nephrology, and hematology input.

## Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis: the logistics matter

If you are going to give eculizumab, you have to think ahead about encapsulated organisms.

In this case:

- meningococcal vaccines were given first
- eculizumab induction followed
- because vaccination had not been completed 2 weeks before complement blockade, prophylactic penicillin was used

That sequence matters.

Practical points:

- give meningococcal vaccination as early as possible
- if you cannot wait 2 weeks before starting eculizumab, use antibiotic prophylaxis
- counsel the patient about fever and urgent evaluation
- monitor closely for breakthrough infection

This is especially relevant in urgent inpatients, where treatment decisions are made faster than vaccine schedules can work.

## A few bedside lessons from this case

This case also reinforces a classic ICU/nephrology pitfall: sepsis and TMA can overlap. Early DIC can obscure evolving CM-HUS, and a “sepsis explains everything” mindset can delay the diagnosis.

Red flags that should prompt a TMA reassessment:

- hemolysis that appears or worsens as sepsis improves
- persistent thrombocytopenia after coagulation parameters normalize
- schistocytes on smear
- worsening AKI out of proportion to the rest of the sepsis picture
- dialysis dependence without another explanation

Also worth remembering: a positive DAT does not end the workup. In this patient, the DAT was likely a false positive related to penicillin exposure or transfusion. If the smear shows schistocytes and the hemolysis is mechanically patterned, keep looking for TMA.

## Take-home points

- Infection-triggered CM-HUS can emerge after the initial septic/DIC phase starts to improve.
- Normal C3/C4 do **not** exclude CM-HUS.
- ADAMTS13 is crucial to exclude TTP; don’t wait passively if the patient is worsening.
- Plasma exchange is a bridge when diagnosis is unclear, but it is not definitive therapy for CM-HUS.
- Eculizumab is the key disease-modifying treatment, and delaying it risks incomplete renal recovery.
- When possible, start eculizumab after bacteremia is controlled; if not possible, make the risk-benefit decision explicitly.
- Meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are not optional details—they’re part of the treatment plan.

## Bottom line

Infection-triggered HUS is one of those conditions where the “right” treatment can seem dangerous because it targets the very system helping the patient fight infection. This case shows the balancing act: treat the infection aggressively, use plasma exchange as a bridge when the diagnosis is uncertain, and move to complement blockade as soon as CM-HUS is likely enough to justify it.

The cost of waiting too long can be a kidney that never comes back.



# From Anuria to Partial Recovery: What Renal Biopsy and Clinical Course Teach Us About Prognosis in CM-HUS

## A dog bite, septic shock, and then the real kidney story

This case is fascinating because the kidney injury didn’t just “happen during sepsis” and then fade away. It evolved. What began as fulminant *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis with DIC and AKI later declared itself as a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and ultimately complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS).

That sequence matters.

The nephrology lesson here is that in CM-HUS, the hematology can improve first while the kidneys lag badly behind—or never fully recover. In this patient, the transition from initial oliguria to complete anuria, dialysis dependence, and biopsy-proven renal TMA marked a severe renal phenotype. Even with appropriate treatment, she only achieved partial renal recovery.

So the big question is: what does this case teach us about prognosis?

## Why the kidney course is the part to watch

At presentation, the renal injury could plausibly be explained by septic AKI, hypoperfusion, and DIC. But the subsequent course was more ominous:

- progression to **complete anuria**
- need for **continuous renal replacement therapy**
- persistent **dialysis-dependent AKI**
- later **renal biopsy showing severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli, and fibrin thrombi**
- eventual **return of urine output only after PEX and before/around eculizumab**
- **incomplete recovery** at follow-up, with creatinine still elevated

That pattern strongly suggests there was more than one kidney insult:

1. **Sepsis-associated ATN**
2. **Microvascular injury from TMA**
3. Possibly superimposed ischemic damage from the systemic inflammatory/coagulopathic state

Biopsy helped separate these pieces. The finding of **acute tubular necrosis plus glomerular fibrin thrombi** is important because it shows both tubular injury and active microangiopathy. In practical terms, that’s a bad prognostic combination.

## What does anuria mean in CM-HUS?

Complete anuria is not just a severity marker—it often reflects extensive renal ischemia and microvascular injury.

In CM-HUS, anuria usually means one or more of the following:

- widespread endothelial injury with capillary occlusion
- severe glomerular ischemia
- major tubular necrosis
- delayed recognition, allowing ongoing complement-mediated damage

In this case, the fact that she became **completely anuric after ICU transfer** is a warning sign. Even though she later recovered some diuresis, the kidney had likely already sustained substantial structural injury.

A useful bedside pearl: **return of urine output is encouraging, but it does not guarantee full renal recovery**. In TMA, urine output may come back before GFR meaningfully improves.

## Renal biopsy: why it changes the conversation

Biopsy isn’t always needed to diagnose CM-HUS, but when the clinical picture is muddy—as it was here—it can be very helpful.

This biopsy showed:

- **severe acute tubular necrosis**
- **ischemic glomeruli**
- **fibrin thrombi**
- findings consistent with **TMA**

That combination supports a microvascular process, not just “post-sepsis AKI.”

Why does that matter prognostically?

- **ATN alone** can recover substantially, even after dialysis
- **TMA with fibrin thrombi** implies ongoing vascular injury and a greater risk of permanent nephron loss
- **ischemic glomeruli** suggest that filtration units were directly compromised, not merely “shut down” from hemodynamics

In other words, the biopsy identified a kidney that had been hit from multiple angles.

## What predicts renal recovery in CM-HUS?

The literature and clinical experience point to a few themes:

### 1) Time to complement blockade matters
The earlier eculizumab is started, the better the chance of renal recovery. Once irreversible ischemic injury and fibrosis set in, hematologic remission may outpace kidney recovery.

### 2) Severity of initial kidney injury matters
Features associated with worse renal outcomes include:

- dialysis requirement
- anuria
- severe AKI at presentation
- delayed diagnosis
- biopsy evidence of extensive TMA or cortical ischemia

This patient had several of these.

### 3) Hematologic remission does not equal renal remission
This is a crucial teaching point. Platelets can normalize, hemolysis can stop, and the patient can still be left with chronic kidney disease.

That’s exactly why nephrology follow-up matters even after the “CRASH” of the acute illness is over.

### 4) Genetic predisposition is not required for severe disease
This patient had no pathogenic complement variants identified. That does **not** mean the disease was mild or less real. Infection-triggered CM-HUS can be severe even without a detectable inherited mutation.

## Normal complement tests do not rescue you

Another practical pearl from this case: **normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS**.

That matters because clinicians often anchor on normal complement levels and move away from the diagnosis. But CM-HUS is a functional disease of dysregulated complement activation, and routine complement levels may be normal.

So if you have:

- MAHA
- thrombocytopenia
- AKI
- schistocytes
- TTP excluded
- STEC excluded
- biopsy consistent with TMA

then CM-HUS stays on the table whether C3/C4 are normal or not.

## Dialysis dependence: reversible or not?

This case sits in the gray zone that matters most in nephrology: dialysis dependence does not automatically mean ESRD, but it is a major prognostic flag.

In CM-HUS, some patients recover enough kidney function to come off dialysis if:

- the diagnosis is made early
- complement blockade is started promptly
- the biopsy does not show irreversible cortical loss
- urine output returns

But prolonged anuria and delayed recognition reduce the odds of full recovery.

In this patient, the return of spontaneous diuresis after PEX and around the time of complement blockade was encouraging, but the final outcome—**partial recovery only**—fits the severity of the initial injury.

## Where does plasma exchange fit?

PEX was used here as a bridge while the team was sorting out TTP vs CM-HUS. That makes sense. In a patient with MAHA and thrombocytopenia, you cannot wait on every send-out before acting.

But once ADAMTS13 was not severely deficient and the picture pointed to CM-HUS, PEX was not the definitive therapy. It may have provided transient stabilization, but the durable response came after **eculizumab**.

For kidneys, that distinction is everything: transient hematologic improvement is not the same as stopping complement-mediated endothelial injury.

## How long should eculizumab be continued if genetics are negative?

This is one of the hardest real-world decisions.

If a patient has:

- no pathogenic complement variants identified
- a clear infectious trigger
- significant initial renal injury
- only partial recovery

then stopping eculizumab is not straightforward.

The challenge is that “genetically negative” does not equal “low risk.” Testing may be incomplete, variants of uncertain significance may be missed or not yet interpreted, and the clinical phenotype can still be severe.

In practice, duration is individualized based on:

- severity of the initial episode
- renal recovery trajectory
- recurrence risk
- trigger control
- availability of close follow-up
- patient preference and infection risk counseling

In this case, continuing maintenance eculizumab for a period after induction is reasonable given the severity of the kidney injury and incomplete recovery. If renal function stabilizes and there is no recurrence, reassessment for discontinuation makes sense. But if a pathogenic complement abnormality had been found, the argument for longer-term treatment would be stronger.

## The infectious-risk tradeoff

There’s a deep irony here: complement blockade can be kidney-saving, but complement is also part of host defense.

That means eculizumab in the setting of a recent bloodstream infection requires careful planning:

- ensure cultures have cleared if possible
- vaccinate against meningococcal disease
- use antimicrobial prophylaxis when vaccination is not fully established
- monitor closely for recurrent infection

This patient received vaccines and prophylactic antibiotics, which is exactly the kind of safety net you want when using terminal complement blockade.

## Take-home points

- **Anuria in CM-HUS is a bad prognostic sign** and often reflects severe microvascular and tubular injury.
- **Dialysis dependence does not rule out recovery**, but the likelihood of full recovery drops as kidney injury becomes more severe and prolonged.
- **Renal biopsy can clarify prognosis** by showing whether the dominant lesion is ATN alone or ATN plus active TMA/ischemia.
- **Hematologic remission can occur before renal recovery**; do not use platelet recovery as a surrogate for kidney prognosis.
- **Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS**.
- **Eculizumab is most effective when started early**, but even delayed therapy can still produce hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.
- **Negative genetic testing does not eliminate relapse risk**, so duration of complement blockade should be individualized.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that in CM-HUS, the kidney often tells the truth before the blood counts do—and sometimes keeps telling it long after the hematologic storm has passed. Complete anuria, dialysis dependence, and biopsy-proven TMA point to a severe renal insult with a real risk of incomplete recovery.

The good news is that recovery is still possible, even after dialysis. The less good news is that the kidney may recover only partially, especially when the diagnosis is delayed and the biopsy already shows both tubular necrosis and microvascular thrombosis.

In other words: when the urine stops, the prognosis gets serious fast.
