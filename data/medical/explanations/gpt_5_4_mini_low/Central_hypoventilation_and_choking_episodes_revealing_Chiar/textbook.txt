Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Chiari Malformation Type I as a Cause of Bulbar and Respiratory Dysfunction

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is defined radiologically by descent of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. In practice, the imaging threshold is only the starting point: a patient with tonsillar descent may be asymptomatic, while another with similar anatomy may have substantial neurologic symptoms. This distinction is important in understanding the present case, because the patient’s longstanding choking episodes, nocturnal desaturation, and chronic occipital pain were ultimately more informative than the imaging measurement alone.

Epidemiologically, CMI is seen relatively often on modern imaging, but symptomatic disease is uncommon. This gap between imaging prevalence and clinical prevalence reflects the fact that many cases are incidental or only mildly crowd the craniocervical junction. The current view is that CMI lies on a spectrum, ranging from a benign anatomical variant to a clinically significant Chiari syndrome. In symptomatic patients, the malformation is usually interpreted as a functional disorder of the posterior fossa and cervicomedullary junction rather than a purely static structural abnormality.

The central pathophysiologic concept is posterior fossa crowding, or a volumetric mismatch between the size of the posterior fossa and its contents. When the bony space is relatively small, the cerebellar tonsils can herniate downward through the foramen magnum, narrowing the subarachnoid spaces around the medulla and upper cervical cord. This region is clinically important because it contains the lower brainstem, respiratory control centers, and the lower cranial nerves that mediate swallowing and pharyngeal function. Crowding at this level can also disturb cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, which may contribute to symptoms through pressure dissociation, traction, and impaired pulsatile CSF dynamics.

The classic adult presentation is suboccipital headache, often worsened by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Myelopathic symptoms and syringomyelia may also occur when CSF flow obstruction extends along the cervical spinal cord. Lower cranial nerve involvement is especially relevant in cases like this one, because dysfunction of the glossopharyngeal, vagal, and hypoglossal pathways can produce dysphagia, choking, palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, and cricopharyngeal dysfunction. When these manifestations are present, patients may first be evaluated by gastroenterology or otolaryngology, as happened here, before a neurologic cause is recognized.

Respiratory involvement is an increasingly recognized manifestation of CMI. Compression or distortion of the medulla can impair the networks that regulate breathing during sleep and, in some patients, during wakefulness as well. This can lead to sleep-disordered breathing, central apnoeas, intermittent hypoxaemia, and a phenotype resembling central hypoventilation. In the case report, the patient’s repeated desaturation without a clear pulmonary explanation, and her predominantly central events on sleep testing, fit this pattern. Importantly, these respiratory abnormalities may occur even when lung imaging, pulmonary function testing, and basic arterial blood gas values are normal, because the problem lies in central control rather than in the lungs themselves.

The distinction between congenital central hypoventilation syndrome and acquired central hypoventilation due to brainstem compression is essential. Congenital disease is usually related to PHOX2B mutations and presents early in life, whereas acquired central hypoventilation arises from structural or inflammatory injury to the medulla or its connections. In CMI, the mechanism is acquired and anatomically localized: crowding at the foramen magnum interferes with automatic respiratory drive and lower cranial nerve function. This is why CMI can present with a combination of choking, sleep-related desaturation, and minimal subjective dyspnoea, yet remain difficult to detect unless clinicians consider a structural brainstem process.

## Clinical Presentation and Problem Representation of the Case

This patient’s presentation is best understood by first reconstructing the symptoms as they evolved over time, because no single complaint fully explained the illness at the outset. For 7 years, she experienced recurrent choking episodes during meals and during sleep. Importantly, these episodes affected both solids and liquids, which argues against a simple mechanical obstruction such as a fixed esophageal stricture and instead raises concern for impaired oropharyngeal coordination or lower cranial nerve dysfunction. The nocturnal episodes were especially striking: she would awaken several times each week with coughing and gasping, suggesting intermittent failure of ventilatory control during sleep rather than isolated swallowing difficulty.

Her history also included 14 years of chronic occipital and vertex headache beginning after a minor motor vehicle accident. The pain was not a classic Chiari headache in one respect—it was not clearly worsened by coughing or straining—but it had several concerning features. It was pressure-like, localized to the posterior head and vertex, and associated with marked focal allodynia, including discomfort from the weight of her hair. This scalp sensitivity and relief after cutting her hair short suggested a neuropathic or cervicogenic component rather than a routine primary headache syndrome. Because the headache began after trauma and was repeatedly labeled as nummular headache, the possibility of a structural posterior fossa disorder was not initially pursued.

The functional impact was substantial and should be part of the clinical picture. She avoided restaurants and social gatherings because of fear of choking in public, and the persistent symptoms caused withdrawal and emotional distress. Even her attempt to reduce head pain by keeping her hair short created cultural and personal distress. These details matter because they show that the syndrome was not only chronic but also disabling, and they help explain why the patient repeatedly sought care even when prior tests were unrevealing.

The most unusual clue came from her home pulse oximetry during the COVID-19 pandemic. She repeatedly recorded oxygen saturations in the 70%–80% range without feeling markedly short of breath. That combination—severe desaturation with little or no dyspnea—is a red flag for impaired automatic respiratory control, because patients with ordinary pulmonary disease usually experience distress out of proportion to the degree of hypoxemia. The airport episode made this even more compelling: she became dizzy and nauseated, was found to have an oxygen saturation of 78%, and improved rapidly with supplemental oxygen. This was a physiologic event, not just anxiety, and it pointed toward episodic hypoventilation or a central control problem rather than intrinsic lung disease.

The diagnostic delay is understandable in hindsight. She had undergone multiple flexible laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies, all normal, which understandably shifted attention toward otolaryngologic or gastrointestinal explanations. However, persistent choking with normal ENT and GI evaluations should prompt reconsideration of a neurologic cause, especially when symptoms involve both solids and liquids and are accompanied by unexplained hypoxia or chronic posterior head pain. In this case, the failure to find an anatomic lesion in the aerodigestive tract was not reassuring; it was a clue that the problem might lie higher, at the level of the brainstem or craniocervical junction.

A useful problem representation for this case is: a middle-aged woman with long-standing posterior head pain after minor trauma, chronic dysphagia/choking affecting solids and liquids, nocturnal gasping episodes, and intermittent severe oxygen desaturation without perceived dyspnea, ultimately suggesting a structural brainstem disorder causing bulbar dysfunction and impaired automatic breathing. That synthesis is what should trigger neurologic reconsideration. In retrospect, the symptom cluster was not random: the chronic posterior fossa-type headache, bulbar symptoms, and apparent failure of automatic ventilation all fit a single unifying explanation at the craniocervical junction rather than separate ENT, GI, or pulmonary diseases.

## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Diagnosis

This patient’s workup illustrates a common diagnostic challenge: recurrent choking and intermittent desaturation can be misattributed to reflux, anxiety, or lung disease unless the clinician steps back and asks whether the pattern is actually neurogenic. Her symptoms were long-standing, episodic, and involved both daytime meals and nocturnal events. Because she initially presented with choking, the first evaluations appropriately focused on the upper aerodigestive tract: flexible laryngoscopy, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, and reflux-directed assessment. These tests are useful for excluding mucosal, structural, or obvious laryngeal causes of dysphagia, but normal results do not rule out a swallowing disorder of neurologic origin. In this case, repeatedly normal endoscopic studies argued against an obstructing lesion, severe laryngopharyngeal reflux, or a clear structural esophageal problem.

A key clinical clue was that she had difficulty with both solids and liquids equally. Mechanical esophageal obstruction classically produces progressive dysphagia for solids first, with liquids affected later as narrowing becomes severe. In contrast, simultaneous difficulty with solids and liquids suggests impaired coordination or weakness of the swallowing mechanism, which is more consistent with neurogenic dysphagia. That pattern should prompt consideration of bulbar dysfunction, neuromuscular junction disease, or central nervous system pathology rather than an isolated gastrointestinal cause.

Because she also reported unexplained oxygen desaturation, pulmonary disease had to be considered. Chest imaging and pulmonary function testing were normal, which made intrinsic lung disease, major airflow obstruction, and restrictive pulmonary pathology unlikely explanations for her hypoxemia. Her spirometry and respiratory muscle measures were essentially preserved, arguing against a primary ventilatory limitation. A normal daytime arterial blood gas similarly did not support chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure. However, a normal arterial blood gas does not exclude intermittent or sleep-related central hypoventilation. Patients with episodic brainstem dysfunction may breathe adequately while awake and still develop marked nocturnal or episodic desaturation when automatic respiratory drive is impaired.

The laboratory evaluation was directed at common mimics. Normal thyroid studies, vitamin B12, metabolic testing, inflammatory markers, and neuromuscular junction antibodies helped exclude reversible metabolic, inflammatory, and deficiency-related causes of bulbar or respiratory symptoms. Myasthenia gravis was a particularly important consideration because it can present with choking, fatigable dysphagia, and fluctuating respiratory complaints. In this patient, however, the clinical pattern was not typical: there was no fatigability, no clear diurnal worsening, and no exam findings suggesting generalized weakness. Negative acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibodies further lowered the likelihood of myasthenia gravis, although clinicians should remember that seronegativity does not absolutely exclude it when the phenotype is strongly suggestive.

The turning point in the case was re-review of an old head CT obtained after her motor vehicle accident. The original report had been normal, but careful reassessment showed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. This is a valuable diagnostic lesson: when symptoms remain unexplained, prior imaging should be revisited rather than accepted at face value, especially if the clinical picture evolves over time. Historical imaging can reveal subtle abnormalities that were overlooked initially or were not considered relevant at the time.

MRI then confirmed Chiari malformation type I, with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent and crowding at the foramen magnum. In the appropriate clinical context, this degree of tonsillar descent is significant because it supports compression at the craniocervical junction, where lower cranial nerve nuclei and respiratory control pathways are located. The imaging findings helped unify her chronic occipital pain, choking episodes, and episodic hypoxemia under a single structural diagnosis.

Sleep evaluation added another important layer. The sleep study showed only mild overall apnea-hypopnea index, but most events were central rather than obstructive. In selected patients, event type can matter more than the absolute AHI because predominant central events point toward impaired central respiratory control rather than upper airway obstruction. This is particularly relevant in Chiari malformation, where brainstem compression can disrupt automatic ventilation. The study used a type III portable device, which is less definitive than full polysomnography because it lacks EEG monitoring. Without EEG, total sleep time cannot be measured accurately, so respiratory events are often divided by recording time rather than true sleep time, which can underestimate severity. Type III testing can be helpful for screening, but if the clinical suspicion for central hypoventilation is high, full polysomnography with EEG, capnography, and ideally video monitoring is preferred to define sleep state, event timing, carbon dioxide retention, and the relationship of events to arousals.

The differential diagnosis in this case therefore narrowed in a stepwise fashion. Reflux and laryngopharyngeal reflux became less likely after repeated normal endoscopy and lack of a structural explanation. Mechanical esophageal disease was not supported by the equal difficulty with solids and liquids. Myasthenia gravis was unlikely given the clinical pattern and negative antibodies. Primary pulmonary disease was excluded by normal chest imaging, pulmonary function testing, and daytime blood gas values. Anxiety may have complicated the patient’s experience and was repeatedly invoked during prior evaluations, but it could not account for objective desaturation to the 70%–80% range. The remaining unifying explanation was acquired central hypoventilation from structural brainstem pathology related to Chiari malformation type I. This case shows why clinicians should maintain a broad differential and avoid prematurely labeling unexplained respiratory or bulbar symptoms as functional when objective abnormalities are present.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Linking Chiari I to Choking and Central Hypoventilation

This case required a unifying diagnosis because the patient’s symptoms did not fit neatly into a single gastrointestinal, otolaryngologic, pulmonary, or psychiatric disorder. Over many years she underwent repeated laryngoscopic, endoscopic, and pulmonary evaluations, yet these studies remained unrevealing. That pattern should prompt clinicians to step back and ask whether several apparently unrelated complaints are actually manifestations of one lesion in a strategic neuroanatomic location. Here, the combination of choking on both solids and liquids, nocturnal gasping, unexplained hypoxemia, and chronic occipital/vertex pain pointed away from isolated organ disease and toward dysfunction at the craniocervical junction.

The symptom pattern was localizing. Recurrent choking with a normal structural swallowing workup suggests neurogenic oropharyngeal dysphagia rather than esophageal obstruction or reflux alone. In Chiari I malformation, the cerebellar tonsils descend through the foramen magnum and can crowd the medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei. That anatomy can impair the glossopharyngeal, vagal, and hypoglossal pathways that coordinate swallowing and airway protection, producing coughing, choking, and aspiration-like episodes even when the larynx and esophagus look normal on direct examination. In the same region lie the respiratory centers that generate automatic breathing, so medullary compression can also disrupt ventilatory control.

A key physiologic concept in this case is the difference between voluntary and automatic breathing. Voluntary breathing is consciously driven through cortical pathways and can remain intact even when the brainstem is compromised. Automatic breathing, especially during sleep or relaxed wakefulness, depends on medullary respiratory centers and their integration of chemosensory input. Chiari-related crowding can selectively damage this automatic drive, creating a phenotype of central hypoventilation: the patient can appear to breathe normally when awake and attentive, yet fail to maintain ventilation when asleep or distracted. That is why central sleep apnea and episodic desaturation are such important clues in symptomatic Chiari I.

The severe oxygen desaturations without obvious dyspnea were particularly informative. In ordinary cardiopulmonary disease, a fall in oxygen saturation is usually accompanied by visible respiratory distress and a strong sensation of air hunger. In this patient, however, saturations dropped into the 70%–80% range while she remained relatively unaware and did not appear distressed. This mismatch suggests impaired brainstem respiratory control and possibly impaired interoceptive signaling, meaning that the usual conscious warning of breathlessness was blunted. The airport episode captured this physiology well: during a period of distraction and reduced attentional control, she became symptomatic and profoundly desaturated, then improved rapidly with supplemental oxygen. The episode strongly supported failure of automatic respiratory regulation rather than anxiety alone.

The choking episodes also fit lower cranial nerve dysfunction. When the medulla and adjacent nerve roots are compressed, swallowing coordination may become unreliable, causing cough, throat closure sensations, and choking with both liquids and solids. Equal difficulty with multiple consistencies is less suggestive of a fixed mechanical obstruction and more consistent with a neurologic swallowing disorder. The normal endoscopies and laryngoscopies therefore became supportive, not contradictory, because they excluded mucosal or structural lesions while leaving a neurogenic problem on the table.

Imaging provided the structural confirmation needed to unify the clinical picture. Re-review of the old head CT showed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction, and MRI later demonstrated 11 mm of tonsillar descent. Together, these findings established the anatomic substrate for medullary and lower cranial nerve compression. The historical CT was especially important because it showed that the abnormality predated the recognition of the patient’s symptoms and had been missed initially. In a patient with this symptom complex, such imaging findings make Chiari I a compelling explanation.

The role of trauma remained uncertain. Her symptoms began after a motor vehicle accident, but without pre-trauma imaging it is impossible to know whether the accident caused tonsillar descent, unmasked a previously asymptomatic Chiari malformation, or simply led to the first imaging study in an already present abnormality. Clinically, this distinction matters less than recognizing that the temporal relationship to trauma should not prevent consideration of Chiari I, especially when later symptoms are anatomically coherent.

Even though the available sleep study was only a type III study, central hypoventilation remained a strong clinical hypothesis. Type III testing lacks EEG, so total sleep time cannot be measured precisely, and the apnea-hypopnea index may be falsely low because events are divided by recording time rather than true sleep time. That limitation helps explain why the recorded AHI was only mildly abnormal despite dramatic desaturation events. The predominance of central events, the structural MRI findings, and the observed episodes of unexplained hypoxemia were more persuasive than the absolute AHI value alone. In other words, the sleep study did not rule out clinically important central hypoventilation; it simply could not characterize it definitively.

Her headache history added another layer of localization. Chiari I classically causes occipital headache, often worsened by Valsalva, but headache phenotypes can vary. In this patient, the pain was chronic, occipital/vertex in location, and associated with marked scalp allodynia, including sensitivity to the weight of her hair. Those features are not specific for Chiari I, and alternative explanations such as occipital neuralgia or a separate scalp-pain syndrome remain possible. Still, in the context of brainstem crowding, the headache history was not random; it was another clue that the craniocervical junction could be clinically relevant.

This case also illustrates several common diagnostic pitfalls. First, repeated specialty-by-specialty evaluations can fragment the clinical picture and obscure a unifying neurologic cause. Second, subtle imaging abnormalities may be missed on initial review when the study was ordered for another indication. Third, unexplained symptoms are too easily attributed to anxiety, especially when examinations are normal and the patient appears stable between episodes. The key lesson is that recurrent choking, sleep-related desaturation, and occipital pain should prompt consideration of brainstem pathology, and Chiari I should remain on the differential even when the presentation is atypical.

## Management, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons

Management of symptomatic Chiari malformation type I is driven less by the millimetres of tonsillar descent and more by the clinical syndrome it produces. Patients with isolated incidental tonsillar ectopia can often be observed, but referral to neurosurgery is important when there are symptoms suggesting brainstem, lower cranial nerve, or spinal cord involvement. In this case, the combination of chronic occipital pain, progressive choking with both solids and liquids, and unexplained desaturation with central-predominant sleep-related events made the malformation clinically significant rather than incidental. The rationale for posterior fossa decompression is to relieve crowding at the cervicomedullary junction and restore cerebrospinal fluid flow around the foramen magnum, thereby reducing compression-related dysfunction. When successful, surgery may improve headache, dysphagia, and respiratory abnormalities, although the degree of benefit is variable and recovery is not guaranteed, especially when symptoms have been present for years.

Because the patient chose to defer surgery while considering her options, conservative management focused on symptom control and safety. She adopted dietary adjustments to reduce choking risk and used practical strategies for her headaches, including keeping her hair short, which reduced scalp allodynia. Given the history of severe oxygen desaturations, she was also counseled to remain vigilant for worsening respiratory symptoms and to seek urgent care if episodes became more frequent or prolonged. This kind of interim management is reasonable when the patient is stable and still weighing operative treatment, but it does not replace definitive neurosurgical assessment.

A structured surveillance plan was put in place, reflecting the possibility that symptoms could progress over time. She was scheduled for regular clinical review, overnight oximetry to monitor hypoxic events, and interval MRI to assess for progression at the craniocervical junction. Clear escalation criteria are essential in such patients: worsening dysphagia, new neurological deficits, increasing frequency or severity of desaturation, or radiographic progression would strengthen the case for surgery. Because her presentation spans family medicine, neurology, pulmonology, sleep medicine, otolaryngology, gastroenterology, and neurosurgery, multidisciplinary coordination is central to care.

This case illustrates several practical diagnostic lessons. First, persistent choking or dysphagia with normal endoscopy and laryngoscopy should raise concern for a neurogenic cause, especially when both solids and liquids are affected equally. Second, unexplained sleep-related hypoventilation or central-predominant apnoeic events should prompt evaluation for structural brain abnormalities at the craniocervical junction. Third, reviewing prior imaging can be decisive; in this patient, an old CT scan that had been read as normal contained the clue that ultimately redirected the diagnosis. More broadly, the case shows that Chiari malformation type I can masquerade as an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, so structural causes should remain on the differential in patients with otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.
