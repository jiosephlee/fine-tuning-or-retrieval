Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Mild AHI, Serious Problem: Reading Sleep Studies in Suspected Central Breathing Disorders

## The trap: “The sleep study was only mildly abnormal”

This case is a great reminder that a “mild” apnea-hypopnea index can be dangerously misleading when the clinical story is pointing somewhere more serious.

A woman in her early 40s had years of choking episodes, nocturnal gasping, and unexplained oxygen desaturations into the 70s–80s. Yet her type III sleep study showed only mild sleep apnea, with an AHI of 6.0. If you stopped there, you might be tempted to downplay the problem.

But the key clue is that most of the events were central, not obstructive, and the study itself had important limitations. In the end, she was found to have Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm of tonsillar descent and brainstem crowding at the foramen magnum — a structural explanation for central breathing dysfunction.

## Why this case matters

This is the kind of patient who can get lost between specialties:

- ENT scopes are normal
- Endoscopy is normal
- Pulmonary testing is normal
- Symptoms get blamed on anxiety
- Sleep study sounds “mild”

Meanwhile, the patient is having recurrent oxygen desaturation and symptoms suggesting impaired automatic breathing. That mismatch between symptoms and testing is the red flag.

The lesson: in suspected central breathing disorders, **the number alone does not tell the whole story**.

## What a type III sleep study can miss

Type III studies are useful in the right setting, but they are not equivalent to full polysomnography.

They generally measure:

- Airflow
- Respiratory effort
- Oxygen saturation
- Heart rate

What they do **not** include is EEG-based sleep staging. That matters a lot.

Without EEG, the study cannot accurately determine:

- Total sleep time
- Sleep onset latency
- Arousals
- Sleep architecture

Instead, respiratory event indices are often calculated using **total recording time**, not true sleep time. If the patient spent a significant part of the night awake, the denominator is inflated and the AHI can look artificially low.

So a type III study can **underestimate severity**, especially when:

- Sleep is fragmented
- There are intermittent events
- The patient has central apnea or hypoventilation
- Symptoms occur mainly in specific sleep stages or during sleep-wake transitions

## Why central disorders are easy to underestimate

Obstructive sleep apnea often announces itself with repetitive obstruction, snoring, and classic event patterns. Central disorders can be more subtle.

In central hypoventilation or central sleep apnea, the issue is not upper airway collapse — it’s impaired respiratory drive. That means:

- Events may be fewer in number but physiologically more important
- Desaturation burden may be disproportionate to the AHI
- The patient may not feel dyspneic, even during significant hypoxemia
- Symptoms may be most obvious during sleep or during distracted wakefulness

In this case, the patient had documented severe desaturation without respiratory distress, which is exactly the kind of pattern that should make you skeptical of a reassuring “mild AHI.”

## Don’t let a low AHI overrule the bedside story

A low AHI is not a trump card.

If the history suggests central breathing dysfunction, you need to ask whether the study actually answered the question you care about. In this case, the clinically important issue was not simply “does she have sleep apnea?” but rather:

- Is she having central apneas?
- Is she hypoventilating?
- Is her respiratory drive impaired?
- Is there a structural brainstem cause?

Those questions are better answered with **formal polysomnography**, ideally including:

- EEG
- Full respiratory channels
- Capnography or end-tidal CO₂ monitoring
- Video recording, when available

## When to push for full polysomnography

Think harder about a formal PSG with EEG and CO₂ monitoring when you see any of the following:

- Unexplained nocturnal desaturation
- Symptoms out of proportion to a mild or equivocal home study
- Suspected central apneas
- Concern for hypoventilation rather than simple apnea
- Neurologic symptoms suggesting brainstem disease
- Dysphagia, choking, or other bulbar symptoms
- Preserved daytime oxygenation but abnormal sleep-related events
- Recurrent episodes of hypoxemia without obvious pulmonary disease

This patient had several of those features: choking with solids and liquids, nocturnal gasping, and unexplained oxygen desaturation. In retrospect, the sleep study was a clue — but not the whole answer.

## The bigger diagnostic pearl: think brainstem

Chiari malformation type I can present with more than headache.

This case ties together three symptoms that should raise suspicion for structural craniocervical disease:

- Chronic occipital headaches
- Dysphagia or choking
- Central respiratory abnormalities

The respiratory phenotype can resemble acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, sometimes described as an Ondine’s curse-like picture. The mechanism is brainstem compression and disruption of automatic breathing pathways.

That’s why a “mild” AHI should not reassure you if the patient’s physiology says otherwise.

## Practical take-home points

- **A low AHI can be falsely reassuring** in patients with suspected central breathing disorders.
- **Type III sleep studies may underestimate severity** because they usually lack EEG and therefore may use total recording time instead of true sleep time.
- **Central events and desaturation burden matter**, not just the AHI number.
- **Formal polysomnography with EEG and capnography** is preferred when central apnea or hypoventilation is suspected.
- **Unexplained choking, dysphagia, occipital headache, and nocturnal desaturation** should prompt consideration of a structural neurologic cause such as Chiari malformation.
- **Read the story, not just the index.**

## Final thought

Sleep studies are powerful, but only if you know what kind of study you’re looking at and what clinical question it can actually answer. In this case, the “mild” AHI was the beginning of the workup, not the end of it.

When the symptoms scream central dysfunction, don’t let a limited sleep study talk you out of a serious diagnosis.
