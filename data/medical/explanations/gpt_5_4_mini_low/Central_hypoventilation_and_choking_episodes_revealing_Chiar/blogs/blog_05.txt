Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Chiari I Is Common on MRI—But Who Actually Has Chiari Syndrome?

## The MRI finding is easy. The patient is the hard part.

Chiari I malformation sits in that awkward space between “real disease” and “incidental anatomy.” We see tonsils below the foramen magnum, we measure the millimeters, and then we have to answer the much more important question: is this actually causing the patient’s symptoms?

That distinction mattered a lot in this case. A woman in her early 40s had years of choking episodes, nocturnal gasping, unexplained desaturations into the 70s–80s, and chronic occipital/vertex headaches. Multiple ENT and GI evaluations were normal. The clue came from a re-review of a 14-year-old head CT: a crowded foramen magnum that had been missed the first time. MRI later confirmed Chiari I malformation with 11 mm of tonsillar descent.

This is the core teaching point: **millimeters alone don’t define Chiari syndrome**. The imaging finding is the starting point, not the diagnosis.

## Chiari I: anatomy, variant, or syndrome?

Chiari I is classically defined by cerebellar tonsillar descent at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. But that threshold is just a radiographic convention. It does not reliably tell you who is symptomatic, who is incidental, or who needs surgery.

That matters because Chiari I is not rare on imaging, while symptomatic Chiari syndrome is much less common. In other words, plenty of patients have tonsillar ectopia; far fewer have clinically meaningful brainstem or craniocervical junction dysfunction.

So what makes the diagnosis clinically meaningful?

Think in terms of **Chiari syndrome**, not Chiari measurement:

- Valsalva-provoked occipital headache
- Bulbar symptoms: dysphagia, choking, aspiration, dysarthria
- Lower cranial nerve dysfunction
- Long tract signs, ataxia, syrinx-related symptoms
- Sleep-disordered breathing or central hypoventilation
- Brainstem compression physiology

This patient had several red flags that fit the syndrome far better than a benign incidental MRI finding.

## When “choking” isn’t ENT, and “hypoxia” isn’t pulmonary

One of the most interesting things in this case is how convincingly the presentation mimicked more common problems.

She had recurrent choking with both solids and liquids, and multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies were normal. That should make you pause. Mechanical esophageal disease usually doesn’t affect solids and liquids equally, and repeated normal structural evaluations should push you toward neurogenic dysphagia.

She also had profound desaturations without obvious distress. Pulmonary workup was unrevealing, and her ABG in clinic was normal. That kind of story should raise suspicion for intermittent central hypoventilation or sleep-related breathing disorder, especially when paired with brainstem symptoms.

The pattern here is classic for a structural neurologic problem masquerading as GI, ENT, anxiety, or “something functional.”

## The headaches were another clue — even if they weren’t textbook

She had chronic occipital and vertex pain starting after a motor vehicle accident. It was not the stereotypical Chiari cough headache; it was constant, pressure-like, and later associated with marked scalp allodynia. She even found that cutting her hair short reduced pain because hair weight triggered discomfort.

That’s not the classic board-style description, but it’s still clinically relevant. Chiari patients can have a range of head pain phenotypes, and associated occipital neuralgia or scalp allodynia may be part of the picture. The key lesson is not to over-anchor on the “typical” cough headache phenotype as a prerequisite for considering Chiari syndrome.

If the posterior fossa anatomy is crowded and the patient has bulbar or respiratory symptoms, the headache phenotype may be messy and still real.

## Why respiratory symptoms are the big deal

Respiratory involvement is where Chiari moves from interesting anatomy to potentially dangerous disease.

This patient had:

- nocturnal choking 3–4 times weekly
- documented oxygen desaturations to 70%–80%
- central events on sleep study
- no pulmonary explanation

That constellation should make you think about brainstem-mediated respiratory dysfunction. Chiari can impair automatic respiratory control by compressing medullary centers at the craniocervical junction. Patients may preserve voluntary breathing while losing automatic drive, especially during sleep.

That’s why some patients with central hypoventilation seem oddly comfortable despite significant hypoxemia. They may not feel the expected dyspnea because the afferent pathways that generate the conscious sensation of air hunger may also be disrupted.

This case is a good reminder that **lack of distress does not equal lack of danger**.

## A note on sleep testing: a “mild” AHI can be misleading

Her type III sleep study showed a mild AHI, but 72% of events were central. That result should be interpreted cautiously because type III studies do not include EEG. Without EEG, sleep time is estimated from recording time, which can underestimate the true severity of sleep-disordered breathing.

So if the story screams central hypoventilation but the home sleep study looks “mild,” don’t stop there. Full polysomnography with EEG, capnography, and ideally video is much more informative.

In this case, the mismatch between symptoms and sleep-study severity is not reassuring; it’s a clue that the test may be undercalling the physiology.

## Imaging matters — but so does re-reading old imaging

A particularly useful pearl from this case: the diagnosis was hiding in an old CT that had been reported as normal. On re-review, there was a crowded foramen magnum and loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction.

That’s a practical lesson for emergency and general clinicians alike: if the clinical picture is evolving and unexplained, **old imaging is not inert**. Re-reviewing prior studies with the new question in mind can be high yield.

MRI then confirmed 11 mm tonsillar descent, which strengthened the diagnosis, but again: the symptom complex is what made the imaging meaningful.

## Management: conservative monitoring vs decompression

The decision point in Chiari I is not “Do they have tonsillar descent?” It’s “Is there evidence of clinically significant Chiari syndrome?”

Posterior fossa decompression is generally considered when patients have:

- significant or progressive neurologic symptoms
- brainstem/bulbar dysfunction
- syringomyelia
- sleep-disordered breathing or hypoventilation attributable to Chiari
- disabling headache clearly linked to Chiari physiology

This patient had several features that would make surgery a very reasonable discussion: choking, nocturnal respiratory events, central apneas, and anatomic brainstem crowding. She chose conservative management for now, with close follow-up and planned surveillance.

That’s also appropriate when symptoms are stable, the patient is hesitant, or the diagnostic picture is still evolving. But conservative care should not mean dismissal. It should mean **structured monitoring with a low threshold to escalate**.

## How to think about Chiari on your next shift

When you see tonsillar ectopia on MRI, resist the urge to stop at the radiology number. Ask whether the patient has evidence of **Chiari syndrome**.

A practical bedside framework:

1. **Is the anatomy compatible?**  
   Crowded foramen magnum, tonsillar descent, loss of CSF space, syrinx, or other craniocervical junction abnormalities.

2. **Is there a symptom pattern that fits?**  
   Occipital headache, bulbar dysfunction, choking, dysphagia, sleep-disordered breathing, ataxia, long tract signs.

3. **Could this be incidental?**  
   If the patient has no compatible symptoms, it may be an incidental MRI finding.

4. **Is there brainstem physiology at stake?**  
   Central apneas, unexplained hypoxemia, aspiration, or impaired automatic ventilation should raise the stakes.

5. **Does the patient need neurosurgical evaluation?**  
   If symptoms suggest clinically meaningful compression, don’t let the “only 5 mm” mindset delay referral.

## Take-home points

- **Chiari I on MRI is not the same thing as Chiari syndrome.**
- **Millimeters of tonsillar descent do not define severity.** Symptoms and physiology do.
- Recurrent choking with normal ENT/GI workup should prompt consideration of **neurogenic dysphagia**.
- Unexplained nocturnal desaturation or central apneas should raise concern for **brainstem-related hypoventilation**.
- A mild or type III sleep study can underestimate clinically important disease.
- If Chiari is causing **bulbar or respiratory dysfunction**, neurosurgical evaluation is warranted even if the imaging finding seemed “incidental” at first.

## Bottom line

The modern challenge with Chiari I is not finding it — it’s deciding whether it matters. This case is a great reminder that the patient, not the millimeters, determines the diagnosis. When tonsillar ectopia is paired with choking, dysphagia, central apneas, or unexplained hypoxia, you should stop thinking “incidental MRI finding” and start thinking “brainstem disease until proven otherwise.”
