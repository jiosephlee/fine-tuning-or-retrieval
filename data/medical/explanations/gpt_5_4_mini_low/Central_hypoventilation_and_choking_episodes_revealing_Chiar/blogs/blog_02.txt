Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# The Patient Who Desaturated Without Distress: Structural Causes of Acquired Central Hypoventilation

## Why this case matters

A woman in her early 40s spent years being told her choking episodes, nocturnal gasping, and intermittent severe desaturations were “probably anxiety” or a GI/ENT problem. Her oxygen saturations would drop into the 70s–80s, yet she often looked surprisingly calm and did not feel dyspneic. The unifying diagnosis turned out to be Chiari malformation type I with brainstem compression.

That’s the teaching point: when hypoxemia is real, recurrent, and unexplained — especially when it happens during sleep or with minimal subjective distress — don’t stop at the lungs. Sometimes the problem is not oxygen exchange. It’s respiratory control.

## The physiologic setup: breathing has two control systems

Most of us think of breathing as one reflex. It isn’t.

There are at least two clinically important components:

- **Voluntary breathing**: cortical control, used for speech, breath-holding, intentional deep breaths
- **Automatic breathing**: brainstem-driven respiratory rhythm generation, especially important during sleep and when attention is elsewhere

In this case, the patient could still breathe voluntarily, but her automatic drive appeared impaired. That’s why she could be profoundly desaturated without the expected air hunger or visible distress.

This pattern is the essence of **acquired central hypoventilation**, sometimes described as an acquired “Ondine’s curse.” Unlike congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, this is not usually a genetic PHOX2B problem — it’s a structural or acquired brainstem problem until proven otherwise.

## How Chiari I fits in

Chiari malformation type I is defined by downward tonsillar descent through the foramen magnum. In this patient, MRI showed **11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent** with crowding at the craniocervical junction.

Why does this matter?

The medulla and lower brainstem house respiratory control centers and pathways involved in swallowing and airway protection. Compression at the foramen magnum can produce a mixed syndrome:

- **central sleep-disordered breathing**
- **bulbar symptoms** like choking, dysphagia, or aspiration
- **occipital headaches**
- sometimes **minimal dyspnea despite major gas-exchange abnormalities**

That last feature is what makes these cases so dangerous. The patient may not “look like” someone in respiratory failure.

## Red flags that should make you think central, not pulmonary

This case had several clues that the problem was neurologic rather than primary lung disease.

### 1) Desaturation with little subjective distress
Repeated home oximetry readings in the 70s–80s, plus an airport episode with SpO₂ 78%, yet no major respiratory complaint. That mismatch should raise suspicion for impaired respiratory perception or drive.

### 2) Nocturnal choking/gasping
Three to four nocturnal episodes per week is not a benign symptom. Recurrent sleep-related events should trigger consideration of sleep-disordered breathing, including central events.

### 3) Choking with solids and liquids equally
Equal difficulty with liquids and solids points away from a simple mechanical obstruction and toward a neurogenic swallowing problem.

### 4) Normal ENT and GI workup
Multiple normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies don’t rule out serious disease — they just push the differential higher up the neuraxis.

### 5) Chronic occipital headache
Chiari syndrome classically causes occipital headache, often Valsalva-provoked, though this patient’s headache was atypical and initially misclassified.

### 6) A history of prior head trauma with an old “normal” CT
The old CT, when re-reviewed, showed a crowded foramen magnum. Sometimes the answer is already in the chart; it just wasn’t recognized the first time.

## Why the patient may not feel short of breath

Dyspnea is not just a number on a pulse oximeter. It’s a conscious sensation generated when afferent respiratory signals are processed by the brain — especially cortical and insular regions.

If the brainstem pathways are disrupted, a patient may have:

- hypoxemia without air hunger
- hypercapnia with little complaint
- sleep-related or episodic respiratory failure that is underappreciated until monitored

That’s why “she didn’t look distressed” is not reassuring in suspected central hypoventilation. In some cases, absence of distress is part of the disease.

## Sleep testing: don’t be fooled by a low AHI if the study is limited

This patient’s type III sleep study showed mild sleep apnea by AHI, but **72% of events were central**. That fits the clinical story better than the raw AHI might suggest.

Important limitation: a type III study lacks EEG, so it can’t determine actual sleep time. That means the AHI may be underestimated because events are divided by recording time rather than true sleep time.

Clinical takeaway: if the story screams central hypoventilation, a “mild” home or limited sleep study should not close the case.

## Differential diagnosis in real life

This patient’s workup reasonably included GI, ENT, pulmonary, and neuromuscular causes. That’s appropriate. The key is knowing when those avenues have been exhausted.

Consider:

- **GERD/laryngopharyngeal reflux** when choking is meal-related, but don’t anchor there if studies are negative
- **Myasthenia gravis** when bulbar symptoms are present, but look for fatigability and antibody support
- **Primary lung disease** when desaturation occurs, but normal imaging, spirometry, and blood gas should push you back to the CNS
- **Congenital central hypoventilation** in the right phenotype, but adults with new symptoms need evaluation for acquired causes
- **Structural brainstem pathology**, including Chiari I, when respiratory symptoms are unexplained and accompanied by headache or dysphagia

## The big lesson: central hypoventilation is a localization problem

The most useful way to think about this case is not “mysterious hypoxia,” but **localizing respiratory dysfunction to the brainstem**.

When to search for central neurologic pathology:

- recurrent unexplained nocturnal desaturation
- central events on sleep testing
- choking/dysphagia with normal ENT/GI evaluation
- hypercapnia or hypoxemia out of proportion to pulmonary findings
- preserved voluntary breathing but impaired automatic breathing
- occipital headaches or other posterior fossa symptoms
- a history of trauma or prior imaging that may have been underread

If those boxes start getting checked, MRI of the brain and craniocervical junction is not optional — it’s the test that can reveal the diagnosis.

## Practical take-home points

- **Severe hypoxemia without distress is a red flag, not reassurance.**
- **Think central hypoventilation when desaturation is recurrent, nocturnal, or episodic and the pulmonary workup is unrevealing.**
- **Equal dysphagia for solids and liquids suggests a neurogenic process.**
- **Chiari I can present with respiratory symptoms before the classic neurologic diagnosis is obvious.**
- **A limited sleep study can underestimate severity; central events matter even when the AHI looks “mild.”**
- **Review old imaging when the story doesn’t fit — the clue may already be there.**

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that some patients “forget to breathe” because their brainstem can’t reliably generate automatic respiration, not because they are anxious or noncompliant. Chiari malformation type I can produce exactly that picture: choking, nocturnal gasping, and profound desaturation with surprisingly little dyspnea.

If the lungs look normal and the oximeter keeps telling a scary story, ask a neurologic question.
