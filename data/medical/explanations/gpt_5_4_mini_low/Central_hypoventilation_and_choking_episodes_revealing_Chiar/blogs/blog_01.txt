Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# When Choking Isn’t GI: Neurogenic Dysphagia as a Clue to Chiari I

## The case that breaks the “it’s probably reflux” reflex

A woman in her early 40s spent years being worked up for recurrent choking. Solids. Liquids. Daytime meals. Nocturnal episodes that woke her gasping. Three flexible laryngoscopies. Two upper endoscopies. All normal.

That’s the part that should make you sit up.

When a patient chokes on both solids and liquids equally, and the ENT/GI evaluation is unrevealing, you need to stop thinking only about the esophagus and start thinking about the brainstem. In this case, the unifying diagnosis was Chiari malformation type I, with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent and a crowded foramen magnum—ultimately explaining the choking episodes, chronic occipital/vertex headaches, and intermittent unexplained desaturation.

## Why this matters in the ED, clinic, and everywhere else

Recurrent dysphagia gets bucketed into “mechanical” versus “motility” far too quickly. But a third bucket exists: **neurogenic/bulbar dysfunction**. If the swallowing problem affects solids and liquids from the start, that’s a red flag against a simple obstructing lesion.

This patient’s story is a reminder that “normal laryngoscopy” and “normal endoscopy” do not equal “nothing is wrong.” They just mean the lesion is probably not in the places those tests can see.

Chiari I is usually introduced as a headache diagnosis, but it can also present with:

- dysphagia or choking
- sleep-disordered breathing
- central hypoventilation
- lower cranial nerve dysfunction
- occult brainstem compression symptoms

In other words: it can masquerade as a GI complaint.

## Pattern recognition: what the symptom mix is telling you

The key clinical clue here was the **equal difficulty with solids and liquids**. That pattern should immediately broaden the differential beyond fixed obstruction.

### Think mechanical obstruction when:
- solids are harder than liquids, especially early on
- symptoms progress from solids → liquids
- there’s food impaction, weight loss, or structural abnormality on endoscopy

### Think neurogenic/bulbar dysfunction when:
- solids and liquids are both problematic from the start
- choking/coughing is prominent
- symptoms are intermittent and unexplained by endoscopy
- there are associated neurologic symptoms: headache, cranial nerve findings, imbalance, dysarthria, sleep-related breathing issues

That last point is where Chiari I starts to matter.

## Chiari I: not just an incidental MRI finding

Chiari I is defined radiographically by cerebellar tonsillar descent of at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. But the diagnosis is really clinical-radiographic: the anatomy matters most when it explains symptoms.

In this case, the symptom cluster was hard to ignore:

- 7 years of choking episodes
- nocturnal choking several times weekly
- chronic occipital/vertex headaches for 14 years
- unexplained desaturation to the 70s–80s without respiratory distress
- normal chest imaging and pulmonary testing
- normal endoscopy/laryngoscopy
- MRI confirming 11 mm tonsillar descent

That combination is classic for “the problem is higher up.”

## Why Chiari can cause choking

The medulla is where the hardware for swallowing and automatic breathing lives. Chiari I can compress the brainstem and distort lower cranial nerve function, leading to:

- impaired pharyngeal coordination
- cricopharyngeal dysfunction
- aspiration or choking
- sleep-related central apneas
- central hypoventilation

This is what makes Chiari I such a dangerous mimic. Patients may not look “neurologic” at first glance. They may present to GI, ENT, pulmonology, or sleep medicine long before anyone thinks about the posterior fossa.

## The respiratory clue: “I’m not short of breath, but my oxygen is low”

One of the more striking features in this case was profound desaturation without dyspnea or distress. That matters.

In structural brainstem disease, patients can lose automatic respiratory drive while preserving voluntary breathing. The result is a pattern that resembles acquired central hypoventilation—sometimes called Ondine’s curse. The patient may breathe fine when awake and focused, but the automatic control that should run in the background becomes unreliable, especially during sleep or distraction.

That’s why the airport episode was so revealing: dizziness, nausea, SpO₂ 78%, then quick improvement with oxygen. The physiology was there; the perception wasn’t.

Take that as a reminder that **absence of air hunger does not exclude dangerous hypoventilation**.

## The headache may be another clue, even if it’s atypical

This patient had longstanding occipital and vertex pain, with scalp allodynia so prominent that even hair contact was uncomfortable. It wasn’t classic Valsalva-provoked Chiari headache, which is part of why the diagnosis was missed.

Still, in the setting of choking and hypoxemia, chronic posterior head pain should raise suspicion for posterior fossa pathology. Not every Chiari headache looks textbook. Real patients rarely read the chapter first.

## Diagnostic approach: what to do when the workup keeps coming back normal

When a patient has recurrent choking and both ENT and GI testing are unrevealing, the next move is not to repeat the same tests forever. It’s to ask whether the swallow problem is neurogenic.

A practical approach:

1. **Clarify the pattern**
   - solids vs liquids
   - intermittent vs progressive
   - coughing/choking vs “food sticking”
   - nocturnal symptoms
   - associated neurologic symptoms

2. **Look for lower cranial nerve or brainstem hints**
   - dysarthria
   - nasal regurgitation
   - voice change
   - tongue weakness/atrophy
   - gait imbalance
   - occipital headaches
   - sleep-disordered breathing

3. **Use swallowing studies appropriately**
   - a modified barium swallow can help identify neurogenic oropharyngeal dysfunction
   - normal endoscopy doesn’t exclude impaired swallow coordination

4. **Think about imaging**
   - MRI brain and craniocervical junction is the key study if symptoms suggest posterior fossa disease
   - old imaging deserves a second look, especially if the patient’s symptoms predate the “normal” read

5. **If respiratory symptoms are present, evaluate sleep breathing**
   - central events should raise suspicion for brainstem pathology
   - polysomnography is often helpful in Chiari patients with suspected sleep-disordered breathing

## A note on the “normal” sleep study

This case also highlights a common trap: a mildly abnormal sleep study does not necessarily mean the problem is mild.

The patient had a type III study, not a full EEG-based polysomnogram. That matters because type III studies can underestimate event burden by using recording time rather than true sleep time. In other words, the test may have been less sensitive than the patient’s symptoms.

When the clinical picture screams “central hypoventilation” but the screening sleep study looks modest, don’t let the number bulldoze the bedside story.

## What should trigger a neurological workup?

Consider neurology/neurosurgery or MRI craniocervical junction when dysphagia/choking is accompanied by any of the following:

- occipital or posterior headaches
- symptoms affecting both solids and liquids equally
- nocturnal choking or witnessed breathing pauses
- unexplained oxygen desaturation
- normal laryngoscopy and endoscopy
- other bulbar symptoms
- signs of brainstem dysfunction

## Take-home points

- **Choking on both solids and liquids is a neurogenic red flag**, especially when endoscopy and laryngoscopy are normal.
- **Equal difficulty across consistencies points away from mechanical obstruction** and toward bulbar or brainstem dysfunction.
- **Chiari I can present as dysphagia, choking, sleep apnea, or central hypoventilation**, not just headache.
- **Normal GI/ENT tests do not rule out posterior fossa pathology**.
- **If the symptoms are unexplained, image the craniocervical junction** and think neurologically.
- **Don’t dismiss silent hypoxemia**—patients with brainstem compression may not feel dyspneic despite severe desaturation.

## Bottom line

If the swallow study is “normal,” the scope is normal, and the patient is still choking on both food and water, the diagnosis may not be in the esophagus at all. Chiari I is a useful reminder that persistent ENT/GI-negative dysphagia deserves a neurological lens—especially when headaches, nocturnal choking, or unexplained hypoxemia are part of the story.
