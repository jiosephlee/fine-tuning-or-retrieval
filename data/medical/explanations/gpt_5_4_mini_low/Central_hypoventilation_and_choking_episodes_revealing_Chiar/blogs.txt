Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# When Choking Isn’t GI: Neurogenic Dysphagia as a Clue to Chiari I

## The case that breaks the “it’s probably reflux” reflex

A woman in her early 40s spent years being worked up for recurrent choking. Solids. Liquids. Daytime meals. Nocturnal episodes that woke her gasping. Three flexible laryngoscopies. Two upper endoscopies. All normal.

That’s the part that should make you sit up.

When a patient chokes on both solids and liquids equally, and the ENT/GI evaluation is unrevealing, you need to stop thinking only about the esophagus and start thinking about the brainstem. In this case, the unifying diagnosis was Chiari malformation type I, with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent and a crowded foramen magnum—ultimately explaining the choking episodes, chronic occipital/vertex headaches, and intermittent unexplained desaturation.

## Why this matters in the ED, clinic, and everywhere else

Recurrent dysphagia gets bucketed into “mechanical” versus “motility” far too quickly. But a third bucket exists: **neurogenic/bulbar dysfunction**. If the swallowing problem affects solids and liquids from the start, that’s a red flag against a simple obstructing lesion.

This patient’s story is a reminder that “normal laryngoscopy” and “normal endoscopy” do not equal “nothing is wrong.” They just mean the lesion is probably not in the places those tests can see.

Chiari I is usually introduced as a headache diagnosis, but it can also present with:

- dysphagia or choking
- sleep-disordered breathing
- central hypoventilation
- lower cranial nerve dysfunction
- occult brainstem compression symptoms

In other words: it can masquerade as a GI complaint.

## Pattern recognition: what the symptom mix is telling you

The key clinical clue here was the **equal difficulty with solids and liquids**. That pattern should immediately broaden the differential beyond fixed obstruction.

### Think mechanical obstruction when:
- solids are harder than liquids, especially early on
- symptoms progress from solids → liquids
- there’s food impaction, weight loss, or structural abnormality on endoscopy

### Think neurogenic/bulbar dysfunction when:
- solids and liquids are both problematic from the start
- choking/coughing is prominent
- symptoms are intermittent and unexplained by endoscopy
- there are associated neurologic symptoms: headache, cranial nerve findings, imbalance, dysarthria, sleep-related breathing issues

That last point is where Chiari I starts to matter.

## Chiari I: not just an incidental MRI finding

Chiari I is defined radiographically by cerebellar tonsillar descent of at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. But the diagnosis is really clinical-radiographic: the anatomy matters most when it explains symptoms.

In this case, the symptom cluster was hard to ignore:

- 7 years of choking episodes
- nocturnal choking several times weekly
- chronic occipital/vertex headaches for 14 years
- unexplained desaturation to the 70s–80s without respiratory distress
- normal chest imaging and pulmonary testing
- normal endoscopy/laryngoscopy
- MRI confirming 11 mm tonsillar descent

That combination is classic for “the problem is higher up.”

## Why Chiari can cause choking

The medulla is where the hardware for swallowing and automatic breathing lives. Chiari I can compress the brainstem and distort lower cranial nerve function, leading to:

- impaired pharyngeal coordination
- cricopharyngeal dysfunction
- aspiration or choking
- sleep-related central apneas
- central hypoventilation

This is what makes Chiari I such a dangerous mimic. Patients may not look “neurologic” at first glance. They may present to GI, ENT, pulmonology, or sleep medicine long before anyone thinks about the posterior fossa.

## The respiratory clue: “I’m not short of breath, but my oxygen is low”

One of the more striking features in this case was profound desaturation without dyspnea or distress. That matters.

In structural brainstem disease, patients can lose automatic respiratory drive while preserving voluntary breathing. The result is a pattern that resembles acquired central hypoventilation—sometimes called Ondine’s curse. The patient may breathe fine when awake and focused, but the automatic control that should run in the background becomes unreliable, especially during sleep or distraction.

That’s why the airport episode was so revealing: dizziness, nausea, SpO₂ 78%, then quick improvement with oxygen. The physiology was there; the perception wasn’t.

Take that as a reminder that **absence of air hunger does not exclude dangerous hypoventilation**.

## The headache may be another clue, even if it’s atypical

This patient had longstanding occipital and vertex pain, with scalp allodynia so prominent that even hair contact was uncomfortable. It wasn’t classic Valsalva-provoked Chiari headache, which is part of why the diagnosis was missed.

Still, in the setting of choking and hypoxemia, chronic posterior head pain should raise suspicion for posterior fossa pathology. Not every Chiari headache looks textbook. Real patients rarely read the chapter first.

## Diagnostic approach: what to do when the workup keeps coming back normal

When a patient has recurrent choking and both ENT and GI testing are unrevealing, the next move is not to repeat the same tests forever. It’s to ask whether the swallow problem is neurogenic.

A practical approach:

1. **Clarify the pattern**
   - solids vs liquids
   - intermittent vs progressive
   - coughing/choking vs “food sticking”
   - nocturnal symptoms
   - associated neurologic symptoms

2. **Look for lower cranial nerve or brainstem hints**
   - dysarthria
   - nasal regurgitation
   - voice change
   - tongue weakness/atrophy
   - gait imbalance
   - occipital headaches
   - sleep-disordered breathing

3. **Use swallowing studies appropriately**
   - a modified barium swallow can help identify neurogenic oropharyngeal dysfunction
   - normal endoscopy doesn’t exclude impaired swallow coordination

4. **Think about imaging**
   - MRI brain and craniocervical junction is the key study if symptoms suggest posterior fossa disease
   - old imaging deserves a second look, especially if the patient’s symptoms predate the “normal” read

5. **If respiratory symptoms are present, evaluate sleep breathing**
   - central events should raise suspicion for brainstem pathology
   - polysomnography is often helpful in Chiari patients with suspected sleep-disordered breathing

## A note on the “normal” sleep study

This case also highlights a common trap: a mildly abnormal sleep study does not necessarily mean the problem is mild.

The patient had a type III study, not a full EEG-based polysomnogram. That matters because type III studies can underestimate event burden by using recording time rather than true sleep time. In other words, the test may have been less sensitive than the patient’s symptoms.

When the clinical picture screams “central hypoventilation” but the screening sleep study looks modest, don’t let the number bulldoze the bedside story.

## What should trigger a neurological workup?

Consider neurology/neurosurgery or MRI craniocervical junction when dysphagia/choking is accompanied by any of the following:

- occipital or posterior headaches
- symptoms affecting both solids and liquids equally
- nocturnal choking or witnessed breathing pauses
- unexplained oxygen desaturation
- normal laryngoscopy and endoscopy
- other bulbar symptoms
- signs of brainstem dysfunction

## Take-home points

- **Choking on both solids and liquids is a neurogenic red flag**, especially when endoscopy and laryngoscopy are normal.
- **Equal difficulty across consistencies points away from mechanical obstruction** and toward bulbar or brainstem dysfunction.
- **Chiari I can present as dysphagia, choking, sleep apnea, or central hypoventilation**, not just headache.
- **Normal GI/ENT tests do not rule out posterior fossa pathology**.
- **If the symptoms are unexplained, image the craniocervical junction** and think neurologically.
- **Don’t dismiss silent hypoxemia**—patients with brainstem compression may not feel dyspneic despite severe desaturation.

## Bottom line

If the swallow study is “normal,” the scope is normal, and the patient is still choking on both food and water, the diagnosis may not be in the esophagus at all. Chiari I is a useful reminder that persistent ENT/GI-negative dysphagia deserves a neurological lens—especially when headaches, nocturnal choking, or unexplained hypoxemia are part of the story.



# The Patient Who Desaturated Without Distress: Structural Causes of Acquired Central Hypoventilation

## Why this case matters

A woman in her early 40s spent years being told her choking episodes, nocturnal gasping, and intermittent severe desaturations were “probably anxiety” or a GI/ENT problem. Her oxygen saturations would drop into the 70s–80s, yet she often looked surprisingly calm and did not feel dyspneic. The unifying diagnosis turned out to be Chiari malformation type I with brainstem compression.

That’s the teaching point: when hypoxemia is real, recurrent, and unexplained — especially when it happens during sleep or with minimal subjective distress — don’t stop at the lungs. Sometimes the problem is not oxygen exchange. It’s respiratory control.

## The physiologic setup: breathing has two control systems

Most of us think of breathing as one reflex. It isn’t.

There are at least two clinically important components:

- **Voluntary breathing**: cortical control, used for speech, breath-holding, intentional deep breaths
- **Automatic breathing**: brainstem-driven respiratory rhythm generation, especially important during sleep and when attention is elsewhere

In this case, the patient could still breathe voluntarily, but her automatic drive appeared impaired. That’s why she could be profoundly desaturated without the expected air hunger or visible distress.

This pattern is the essence of **acquired central hypoventilation**, sometimes described as an acquired “Ondine’s curse.” Unlike congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, this is not usually a genetic PHOX2B problem — it’s a structural or acquired brainstem problem until proven otherwise.

## How Chiari I fits in

Chiari malformation type I is defined by downward tonsillar descent through the foramen magnum. In this patient, MRI showed **11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent** with crowding at the craniocervical junction.

Why does this matter?

The medulla and lower brainstem house respiratory control centers and pathways involved in swallowing and airway protection. Compression at the foramen magnum can produce a mixed syndrome:

- **central sleep-disordered breathing**
- **bulbar symptoms** like choking, dysphagia, or aspiration
- **occipital headaches**
- sometimes **minimal dyspnea despite major gas-exchange abnormalities**

That last feature is what makes these cases so dangerous. The patient may not “look like” someone in respiratory failure.

## Red flags that should make you think central, not pulmonary

This case had several clues that the problem was neurologic rather than primary lung disease.

### 1) Desaturation with little subjective distress
Repeated home oximetry readings in the 70s–80s, plus an airport episode with SpO₂ 78%, yet no major respiratory complaint. That mismatch should raise suspicion for impaired respiratory perception or drive.

### 2) Nocturnal choking/gasping
Three to four nocturnal episodes per week is not a benign symptom. Recurrent sleep-related events should trigger consideration of sleep-disordered breathing, including central events.

### 3) Choking with solids and liquids equally
Equal difficulty with liquids and solids points away from a simple mechanical obstruction and toward a neurogenic swallowing problem.

### 4) Normal ENT and GI workup
Multiple normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies don’t rule out serious disease — they just push the differential higher up the neuraxis.

### 5) Chronic occipital headache
Chiari syndrome classically causes occipital headache, often Valsalva-provoked, though this patient’s headache was atypical and initially misclassified.

### 6) A history of prior head trauma with an old “normal” CT
The old CT, when re-reviewed, showed a crowded foramen magnum. Sometimes the answer is already in the chart; it just wasn’t recognized the first time.

## Why the patient may not feel short of breath

Dyspnea is not just a number on a pulse oximeter. It’s a conscious sensation generated when afferent respiratory signals are processed by the brain — especially cortical and insular regions.

If the brainstem pathways are disrupted, a patient may have:

- hypoxemia without air hunger
- hypercapnia with little complaint
- sleep-related or episodic respiratory failure that is underappreciated until monitored

That’s why “she didn’t look distressed” is not reassuring in suspected central hypoventilation. In some cases, absence of distress is part of the disease.

## Sleep testing: don’t be fooled by a low AHI if the study is limited

This patient’s type III sleep study showed mild sleep apnea by AHI, but **72% of events were central**. That fits the clinical story better than the raw AHI might suggest.

Important limitation: a type III study lacks EEG, so it can’t determine actual sleep time. That means the AHI may be underestimated because events are divided by recording time rather than true sleep time.

Clinical takeaway: if the story screams central hypoventilation, a “mild” home or limited sleep study should not close the case.

## Differential diagnosis in real life

This patient’s workup reasonably included GI, ENT, pulmonary, and neuromuscular causes. That’s appropriate. The key is knowing when those avenues have been exhausted.

Consider:

- **GERD/laryngopharyngeal reflux** when choking is meal-related, but don’t anchor there if studies are negative
- **Myasthenia gravis** when bulbar symptoms are present, but look for fatigability and antibody support
- **Primary lung disease** when desaturation occurs, but normal imaging, spirometry, and blood gas should push you back to the CNS
- **Congenital central hypoventilation** in the right phenotype, but adults with new symptoms need evaluation for acquired causes
- **Structural brainstem pathology**, including Chiari I, when respiratory symptoms are unexplained and accompanied by headache or dysphagia

## The big lesson: central hypoventilation is a localization problem

The most useful way to think about this case is not “mysterious hypoxia,” but **localizing respiratory dysfunction to the brainstem**.

When to search for central neurologic pathology:

- recurrent unexplained nocturnal desaturation
- central events on sleep testing
- choking/dysphagia with normal ENT/GI evaluation
- hypercapnia or hypoxemia out of proportion to pulmonary findings
- preserved voluntary breathing but impaired automatic breathing
- occipital headaches or other posterior fossa symptoms
- a history of trauma or prior imaging that may have been underread

If those boxes start getting checked, MRI of the brain and craniocervical junction is not optional — it’s the test that can reveal the diagnosis.

## Practical take-home points

- **Severe hypoxemia without distress is a red flag, not reassurance.**
- **Think central hypoventilation when desaturation is recurrent, nocturnal, or episodic and the pulmonary workup is unrevealing.**
- **Equal dysphagia for solids and liquids suggests a neurogenic process.**
- **Chiari I can present with respiratory symptoms before the classic neurologic diagnosis is obvious.**
- **A limited sleep study can underestimate severity; central events matter even when the AHI looks “mild.”**
- **Review old imaging when the story doesn’t fit — the clue may already be there.**

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that some patients “forget to breathe” because their brainstem can’t reliably generate automatic respiration, not because they are anxious or noncompliant. Chiari malformation type I can produce exactly that picture: choking, nocturnal gasping, and profound desaturation with surprisingly little dyspnea.

If the lungs look normal and the oximeter keeps telling a scary story, ask a neurologic question.



# Mild AHI, Serious Problem: Reading Sleep Studies in Suspected Central Breathing Disorders

## The trap: “The sleep study was only mildly abnormal”

This case is a great reminder that a “mild” apnea-hypopnea index can be dangerously misleading when the clinical story is pointing somewhere more serious.

A woman in her early 40s had years of choking episodes, nocturnal gasping, and unexplained oxygen desaturations into the 70s–80s. Yet her type III sleep study showed only mild sleep apnea, with an AHI of 6.0. If you stopped there, you might be tempted to downplay the problem.

But the key clue is that most of the events were central, not obstructive, and the study itself had important limitations. In the end, she was found to have Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm of tonsillar descent and brainstem crowding at the foramen magnum — a structural explanation for central breathing dysfunction.

## Why this case matters

This is the kind of patient who can get lost between specialties:

- ENT scopes are normal
- Endoscopy is normal
- Pulmonary testing is normal
- Symptoms get blamed on anxiety
- Sleep study sounds “mild”

Meanwhile, the patient is having recurrent oxygen desaturation and symptoms suggesting impaired automatic breathing. That mismatch between symptoms and testing is the red flag.

The lesson: in suspected central breathing disorders, **the number alone does not tell the whole story**.

## What a type III sleep study can miss

Type III studies are useful in the right setting, but they are not equivalent to full polysomnography.

They generally measure:

- Airflow
- Respiratory effort
- Oxygen saturation
- Heart rate

What they do **not** include is EEG-based sleep staging. That matters a lot.

Without EEG, the study cannot accurately determine:

- Total sleep time
- Sleep onset latency
- Arousals
- Sleep architecture

Instead, respiratory event indices are often calculated using **total recording time**, not true sleep time. If the patient spent a significant part of the night awake, the denominator is inflated and the AHI can look artificially low.

So a type III study can **underestimate severity**, especially when:

- Sleep is fragmented
- There are intermittent events
- The patient has central apnea or hypoventilation
- Symptoms occur mainly in specific sleep stages or during sleep-wake transitions

## Why central disorders are easy to underestimate

Obstructive sleep apnea often announces itself with repetitive obstruction, snoring, and classic event patterns. Central disorders can be more subtle.

In central hypoventilation or central sleep apnea, the issue is not upper airway collapse — it’s impaired respiratory drive. That means:

- Events may be fewer in number but physiologically more important
- Desaturation burden may be disproportionate to the AHI
- The patient may not feel dyspneic, even during significant hypoxemia
- Symptoms may be most obvious during sleep or during distracted wakefulness

In this case, the patient had documented severe desaturation without respiratory distress, which is exactly the kind of pattern that should make you skeptical of a reassuring “mild AHI.”

## Don’t let a low AHI overrule the bedside story

A low AHI is not a trump card.

If the history suggests central breathing dysfunction, you need to ask whether the study actually answered the question you care about. In this case, the clinically important issue was not simply “does she have sleep apnea?” but rather:

- Is she having central apneas?
- Is she hypoventilating?
- Is her respiratory drive impaired?
- Is there a structural brainstem cause?

Those questions are better answered with **formal polysomnography**, ideally including:

- EEG
- Full respiratory channels
- Capnography or end-tidal CO₂ monitoring
- Video recording, when available

## When to push for full polysomnography

Think harder about a formal PSG with EEG and CO₂ monitoring when you see any of the following:

- Unexplained nocturnal desaturation
- Symptoms out of proportion to a mild or equivocal home study
- Suspected central apneas
- Concern for hypoventilation rather than simple apnea
- Neurologic symptoms suggesting brainstem disease
- Dysphagia, choking, or other bulbar symptoms
- Preserved daytime oxygenation but abnormal sleep-related events
- Recurrent episodes of hypoxemia without obvious pulmonary disease

This patient had several of those features: choking with solids and liquids, nocturnal gasping, and unexplained oxygen desaturation. In retrospect, the sleep study was a clue — but not the whole answer.

## The bigger diagnostic pearl: think brainstem

Chiari malformation type I can present with more than headache.

This case ties together three symptoms that should raise suspicion for structural craniocervical disease:

- Chronic occipital headaches
- Dysphagia or choking
- Central respiratory abnormalities

The respiratory phenotype can resemble acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, sometimes described as an Ondine’s curse-like picture. The mechanism is brainstem compression and disruption of automatic breathing pathways.

That’s why a “mild” AHI should not reassure you if the patient’s physiology says otherwise.

## Practical take-home points

- **A low AHI can be falsely reassuring** in patients with suspected central breathing disorders.
- **Type III sleep studies may underestimate severity** because they usually lack EEG and therefore may use total recording time instead of true sleep time.
- **Central events and desaturation burden matter**, not just the AHI number.
- **Formal polysomnography with EEG and capnography** is preferred when central apnea or hypoventilation is suspected.
- **Unexplained choking, dysphagia, occipital headache, and nocturnal desaturation** should prompt consideration of a structural neurologic cause such as Chiari malformation.
- **Read the story, not just the index.**

## Final thought

Sleep studies are powerful, but only if you know what kind of study you’re looking at and what clinical question it can actually answer. In this case, the “mild” AHI was the beginning of the workup, not the end of it.

When the symptoms scream central dysfunction, don’t let a limited sleep study talk you out of a serious diagnosis.



# The Missed Foramen Magnum: Why Old Imaging Deserves a Second Look

## Why this case matters

A woman in her early 40s spent 14 years collecting diagnoses that never quite fit: “nummular headache,” anxiety, reflux, unexplained choking, intermittent hypoxia. The unifying answer was hiding in plain sight on an old trauma CT — a crowded foramen magnum that had been read as normal.

That’s the uncomfortable lesson here: sometimes the diagnostic clue is not a new symptom, but an old image we failed to truly interrogate.

This case is a reminder that Chiari malformation type I can masquerade as a lot of things, including GI disease, ENT disease, pulmonary disease, and even functional symptoms. It also raises a practical question for clinicians: when should we personally go back and look at prior imaging instead of relying only on the old report?

## The patient’s story, in brief

Her symptoms began after a minor motor vehicle accident with brief loss of consciousness. Soon after, she developed persistent occipital and vertex headaches. Over time, she also had recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids, including nocturnal choking several times per week.

She underwent multiple flexible laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies, all normal. During the COVID era she began checking her own oxygen saturation and found repeated asymptomatic desaturations into the 70s–80s. One episode at an airport documented an oxygen saturation of 78%, with rapid improvement after supplemental oxygen.

A historical CT head from 14 years earlier was re-reviewed and showed a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. MRI confirmed Chiari I with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent. Sleep testing showed mild sleep apnea, predominantly central events.

The key move was not a new test. It was a second look at an old one.

## Why old imaging gets missed

There are at least three reasons prior imaging can fail us:

First, the original study may not have been ordered for the right question. A trauma CT is great for blood, fracture, and mass effect. It is not optimized to diagnose Chiari. But that doesn’t mean the craniocervical junction should be ignored.

Second, subtle findings are easy to overlook when the radiology question is narrow or the patient is young and otherwise stable. If the report says “no acute intracranial abnormality,” clinicians often stop reading. That can be a mistake when the symptom pattern evolves over years.

Third, we are prone to anchoring. Once a patient has a working label — reflux, globus, anxiety, headache disorder — future symptoms tend to get filtered through that frame. The old image becomes background noise instead of a clue.

## When to personally revisit old imaging

You do not need to independently re-read every CT. But there are situations where it’s worth opening the images yourself, or asking radiology to re-review them with a specific question in mind:

- Symptoms are progressive and remain unexplained after routine workup
- Multiple specialists have evaluated a patient without a unifying diagnosis
- There are brainstem, bulbar, or cerebellar clues:
  - choking or dysphagia
  - voice changes
  - unexplained cough or choking at night
  - occipital headaches
  - sleep-disordered breathing, especially central events
- The symptom cluster spans several organ systems without a clear peripheral explanation
- There is a prior head or neck CT, especially if the original complaint was trauma

In this case, the old CT became highly relevant because the symptom constellation pointed back toward the craniocervical junction.

## What to look for on CT before MRI

MRI remains the diagnostic study of choice for Chiari I, but CT can absolutely offer clues.

Subtle CT features that should make you pause include:

- Crowding at the foramen magnum
- Loss of visible CSF space at the craniocervical junction
- Low-lying cerebellar tonsils, if visible on the available cuts
- Tonsillar tissue appearing to extend to or below the opisthion
- A “tight” posterior fossa impression, especially when paired with symptoms

The important point is not to overcall Chiari from CT alone. It’s to recognize when the anatomy looks crowded enough that MRI is warranted, especially if the symptoms fit.

## The symptom pattern that should raise suspicion

Chiari I is famous for occipital headache, especially with Valsalva, but this patient did not have that textbook presentation. Her headaches were constant, pressure-like, and localized to the vertex with marked cutaneous allodynia. Her most disabling symptoms were choking episodes and nocturnal desaturation.

That’s the trap: Chiari does not always present in the neat pattern we memorized.

Clues that should broaden your differential include:

- choking with both solids and liquids
- recurrent nocturnal choking
- unexplained central apneas or desaturations
- persistent occipital or posterior head pain
- lower cranial nerve-type symptoms
- normal ENT and GI workup despite persistent complaints

When symptoms span swallowing, breathing, and headache, think brainstem until proven otherwise.

## The respiratory lesson: central hypoventilation can be sneaky

This case is especially instructive because the patient had profound desaturation without much subjective dyspnea. That’s a classic “wait, what?” moment.

Her study showed mostly central events, and the authors appropriately emphasize that type III sleep testing can underestimate severity because it lacks EEG and uses recording time rather than true sleep time. Even so, the overall picture strongly suggested acquired central hypoventilation from brainstem compression.

That matters clinically because structural causes of central hypoventilation are easy to miss if you assume every nocturnal breathing issue is OSA, reflux, panic, or asthma.

## Diagnostic unity is the real prize

One of the best things re-reviewing the prior CT did was unify the case:

- headaches after the accident
- progressive choking
- nocturnal respiratory events
- asymptomatic hypoxemia
- normal pulmonary and GI evaluation

Instead of five separate “small” problems, the patient likely had one structural problem with multiple downstream manifestations.

That is what makes second-look imaging so powerful. It can collapse years of diagnostic fragmentation into one coherent pathophysiologic story.

## Practical take-home approach

When you are faced with unexplained symptoms that seem to cross organ systems, ask:

1. Is there prior imaging that already visualized the relevant anatomy?
2. Did the original scan come from a context that could have distracted from subtle structural findings?
3. Do the symptoms point to brainstem, bulbar, or craniocervical pathology?
4. Would a re-review by radiology, with a specific clinical question, change management?
5. Is MRI needed now because CT has already hinted at crowding?

A deliberate second look can be the difference between years of symptom chasing and a real diagnosis.

## Bottom line

- Chiari I can present with choking, nocturnal respiratory events, and unexplained hypoxia, not just Valsalva headache.
- Old CT scans deserve a second look when symptoms suggest craniocervical pathology.
- On CT, crowding at the foramen magnum and loss of CSF space should prompt MRI.
- Re-reviewing historical imaging can unify years of seemingly unrelated symptoms.
- If the story spans headache, dysphagia, and breathing abnormalities, think structural brainstem disease early.

## Final thought

This case is not just about a missed Chiari. It’s about diagnostic humility. We are often taught to look forward for the next test, but sometimes the most important test is the one already done — if we are willing to look at it again.



# Chiari I Is Common on MRI—But Who Actually Has Chiari Syndrome?

## The MRI finding is easy. The patient is the hard part.

Chiari I malformation sits in that awkward space between “real disease” and “incidental anatomy.” We see tonsils below the foramen magnum, we measure the millimeters, and then we have to answer the much more important question: is this actually causing the patient’s symptoms?

That distinction mattered a lot in this case. A woman in her early 40s had years of choking episodes, nocturnal gasping, unexplained desaturations into the 70s–80s, and chronic occipital/vertex headaches. Multiple ENT and GI evaluations were normal. The clue came from a re-review of a 14-year-old head CT: a crowded foramen magnum that had been missed the first time. MRI later confirmed Chiari I malformation with 11 mm of tonsillar descent.

This is the core teaching point: **millimeters alone don’t define Chiari syndrome**. The imaging finding is the starting point, not the diagnosis.

## Chiari I: anatomy, variant, or syndrome?

Chiari I is classically defined by cerebellar tonsillar descent at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. But that threshold is just a radiographic convention. It does not reliably tell you who is symptomatic, who is incidental, or who needs surgery.

That matters because Chiari I is not rare on imaging, while symptomatic Chiari syndrome is much less common. In other words, plenty of patients have tonsillar ectopia; far fewer have clinically meaningful brainstem or craniocervical junction dysfunction.

So what makes the diagnosis clinically meaningful?

Think in terms of **Chiari syndrome**, not Chiari measurement:

- Valsalva-provoked occipital headache
- Bulbar symptoms: dysphagia, choking, aspiration, dysarthria
- Lower cranial nerve dysfunction
- Long tract signs, ataxia, syrinx-related symptoms
- Sleep-disordered breathing or central hypoventilation
- Brainstem compression physiology

This patient had several red flags that fit the syndrome far better than a benign incidental MRI finding.

## When “choking” isn’t ENT, and “hypoxia” isn’t pulmonary

One of the most interesting things in this case is how convincingly the presentation mimicked more common problems.

She had recurrent choking with both solids and liquids, and multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies were normal. That should make you pause. Mechanical esophageal disease usually doesn’t affect solids and liquids equally, and repeated normal structural evaluations should push you toward neurogenic dysphagia.

She also had profound desaturations without obvious distress. Pulmonary workup was unrevealing, and her ABG in clinic was normal. That kind of story should raise suspicion for intermittent central hypoventilation or sleep-related breathing disorder, especially when paired with brainstem symptoms.

The pattern here is classic for a structural neurologic problem masquerading as GI, ENT, anxiety, or “something functional.”

## The headaches were another clue — even if they weren’t textbook

She had chronic occipital and vertex pain starting after a motor vehicle accident. It was not the stereotypical Chiari cough headache; it was constant, pressure-like, and later associated with marked scalp allodynia. She even found that cutting her hair short reduced pain because hair weight triggered discomfort.

That’s not the classic board-style description, but it’s still clinically relevant. Chiari patients can have a range of head pain phenotypes, and associated occipital neuralgia or scalp allodynia may be part of the picture. The key lesson is not to over-anchor on the “typical” cough headache phenotype as a prerequisite for considering Chiari syndrome.

If the posterior fossa anatomy is crowded and the patient has bulbar or respiratory symptoms, the headache phenotype may be messy and still real.

## Why respiratory symptoms are the big deal

Respiratory involvement is where Chiari moves from interesting anatomy to potentially dangerous disease.

This patient had:

- nocturnal choking 3–4 times weekly
- documented oxygen desaturations to 70%–80%
- central events on sleep study
- no pulmonary explanation

That constellation should make you think about brainstem-mediated respiratory dysfunction. Chiari can impair automatic respiratory control by compressing medullary centers at the craniocervical junction. Patients may preserve voluntary breathing while losing automatic drive, especially during sleep.

That’s why some patients with central hypoventilation seem oddly comfortable despite significant hypoxemia. They may not feel the expected dyspnea because the afferent pathways that generate the conscious sensation of air hunger may also be disrupted.

This case is a good reminder that **lack of distress does not equal lack of danger**.

## A note on sleep testing: a “mild” AHI can be misleading

Her type III sleep study showed a mild AHI, but 72% of events were central. That result should be interpreted cautiously because type III studies do not include EEG. Without EEG, sleep time is estimated from recording time, which can underestimate the true severity of sleep-disordered breathing.

So if the story screams central hypoventilation but the home sleep study looks “mild,” don’t stop there. Full polysomnography with EEG, capnography, and ideally video is much more informative.

In this case, the mismatch between symptoms and sleep-study severity is not reassuring; it’s a clue that the test may be undercalling the physiology.

## Imaging matters — but so does re-reading old imaging

A particularly useful pearl from this case: the diagnosis was hiding in an old CT that had been reported as normal. On re-review, there was a crowded foramen magnum and loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction.

That’s a practical lesson for emergency and general clinicians alike: if the clinical picture is evolving and unexplained, **old imaging is not inert**. Re-reviewing prior studies with the new question in mind can be high yield.

MRI then confirmed 11 mm tonsillar descent, which strengthened the diagnosis, but again: the symptom complex is what made the imaging meaningful.

## Management: conservative monitoring vs decompression

The decision point in Chiari I is not “Do they have tonsillar descent?” It’s “Is there evidence of clinically significant Chiari syndrome?”

Posterior fossa decompression is generally considered when patients have:

- significant or progressive neurologic symptoms
- brainstem/bulbar dysfunction
- syringomyelia
- sleep-disordered breathing or hypoventilation attributable to Chiari
- disabling headache clearly linked to Chiari physiology

This patient had several features that would make surgery a very reasonable discussion: choking, nocturnal respiratory events, central apneas, and anatomic brainstem crowding. She chose conservative management for now, with close follow-up and planned surveillance.

That’s also appropriate when symptoms are stable, the patient is hesitant, or the diagnostic picture is still evolving. But conservative care should not mean dismissal. It should mean **structured monitoring with a low threshold to escalate**.

## How to think about Chiari on your next shift

When you see tonsillar ectopia on MRI, resist the urge to stop at the radiology number. Ask whether the patient has evidence of **Chiari syndrome**.

A practical bedside framework:

1. **Is the anatomy compatible?**  
   Crowded foramen magnum, tonsillar descent, loss of CSF space, syrinx, or other craniocervical junction abnormalities.

2. **Is there a symptom pattern that fits?**  
   Occipital headache, bulbar dysfunction, choking, dysphagia, sleep-disordered breathing, ataxia, long tract signs.

3. **Could this be incidental?**  
   If the patient has no compatible symptoms, it may be an incidental MRI finding.

4. **Is there brainstem physiology at stake?**  
   Central apneas, unexplained hypoxemia, aspiration, or impaired automatic ventilation should raise the stakes.

5. **Does the patient need neurosurgical evaluation?**  
   If symptoms suggest clinically meaningful compression, don’t let the “only 5 mm” mindset delay referral.

## Take-home points

- **Chiari I on MRI is not the same thing as Chiari syndrome.**
- **Millimeters of tonsillar descent do not define severity.** Symptoms and physiology do.
- Recurrent choking with normal ENT/GI workup should prompt consideration of **neurogenic dysphagia**.
- Unexplained nocturnal desaturation or central apneas should raise concern for **brainstem-related hypoventilation**.
- A mild or type III sleep study can underestimate clinically important disease.
- If Chiari is causing **bulbar or respiratory dysfunction**, neurosurgical evaluation is warranted even if the imaging finding seemed “incidental” at first.

## Bottom line

The modern challenge with Chiari I is not finding it — it’s deciding whether it matters. This case is a great reminder that the patient, not the millimeters, determines the diagnosis. When tonsillar ectopia is paired with choking, dysphagia, central apneas, or unexplained hypoxia, you should stop thinking “incidental MRI finding” and start thinking “brainstem disease until proven otherwise.”



# Occipital Headache, Scalp Allodynia, and the Haircut Clue: Atypical Pain Phenotypes in Chiari I

## The part of this case that should make you pause

Chiari I malformation is usually taught as a “Valsalva occipital headache” diagnosis. Fair enough. But this case is a reminder that the pain phenotype can be much messier than the textbook version.

This woman had 14 years of chronic occipital/vertex pain, marked scalp allodynia, and a bizarre-but-important clue: cutting her hair short reduced her pain. She was labeled with a primary headache disorder, received local scalp injections, and still carried the wrong diagnosis for years. Only later, when her headache was viewed alongside choking episodes and unexplained desaturation, did the broader brainstem story come into focus.

The lesson is not that Chiari causes every odd scalp pain syndrome. It doesn’t. The lesson is that unusual pain patterns, especially when they coexist with bulbar or respiratory symptoms, should widen your differential beyond primary headache.

## Chiari pain is often described too narrowly

When people think of Chiari I pain, they think of occipital headache worsened by cough, strain, or Valsalva. That pattern is common, and in this case report the classic Valsalva trigger was notably absent. Instead, the patient had constant pressure-like occipital/vertex pain and a coin-sized area of scalp sensitivity at the vertex.

That matters, because Chiari-related pain can present in several ways:

- occipital or suboccipital pain
- neck pain
- headaches provoked by cough or strain
- scalp dysesthesia or cutaneous allodynia
- less classically, pain that gets mislabeled as a primary headache syndrome

The “textbook” label can create cognitive anchoring. If the pain doesn’t look classic, it is easy to stop considering Chiari at all. But the posterior fossa doesn’t read the textbook.

## Why the scalp allodynia matters

This patient’s most distinctive pain feature was allodynia at the vertex: even the weight of her hair was uncomfortable. She discovered that cutting her hair short reduced symptoms. That’s an unusual history, but clinically it is gold.

Scalp allodynia should immediately raise your suspicion for a neuropathic or sensitization process. In the context of Chiari, possible explanations include:

- occipital neuralgia related to cranial/cervical nerve irritation
- a Chiari-associated pain syndrome with referred scalp sensitivity
- a separate primary headache disorder coexisting with Chiari
- another entity entirely, such as a burning scalp/vertex pain syndrome

The case report is appropriately cautious here. There is not strong evidence that scalp allodynia is a specific Chiari signature. But when the symptom is unusual, persistent, and paired with other hindbrain/brainstem features, it should not be waved away as “just a headache.”

## Nummular headache, occipital neuralgia, burning vertex syndrome: don’t get trapped by the label

The patient was diagnosed with nummular headache because her pain was localized to a small area of the scalp. That is understandable. But localized scalp pain is a pattern, not a diagnosis.

A few reminders:

### Nummular headache
- classically a small, fixed, coin-shaped area of pain
- often chronic and localized
- usually considered a primary headache disorder
- should remain a diagnosis of exclusion when other neurologic symptoms are present

### Occipital neuralgia
- neuropathic pain in the distribution of the occipital nerves
- can include dysesthesia and scalp tenderness/allodynia
- may be associated with painful hair contact
- can overlap symptomatically with cervicogenic or posterior fossa pathology

### Burning vertex syndrome
- a newer, less well-defined pain phenotype
- vertex pain and cutaneous allodynia
- doesn’t fit neatly into standard primary headache categories
- still a clinical description more than a mature disease entity

The practical point: if the pain label feels convenient but the overall syndrome feels off, keep looking.

## The haircut clue is more than a quirky story

It is tempting to dismiss the haircut detail as anecdotal. Don’t.

Hair-related pain clues often point to cutaneous allodynia, scalp hypersensitivity, or nerve irritation. In this case, hair weight itself triggered discomfort, and shortening the hair reduced symptoms. That is a real, functionally meaningful clue that the scalp nerves were sensitized.

Could that happen in primary headache disorders? Absolutely. But the clue becomes more concerning when it exists alongside:

- chronic occipital symptoms
- dysphagia or choking
- unexplained desaturation
- brainstem-type complaints
- a history suggesting posterior fossa pathology

In other words: the haircut clue doesn’t diagnose Chiari. It helps you notice that this is not a routine tension headache story.

## When pain is the appetizer and brainstem symptoms are the entree

The reason this case is so compelling is that the headache was not isolated. It was part of a broader syndrome:

- recurrent choking with solids and liquids
- nocturnal choking episodes
- severe oxygen desaturation without obvious distress
- normal ENT and GI workup
- MRI showing Chiari I with 11 mm tonsillar descent

That combination should make you think beyond primary headache. Bulbar dysfunction plus odd scalp pain is a much more interesting signal than scalp pain alone.

For emergency and general clinicians, the key is to connect seemingly separate complaints. Chronic headache, dysphagia, choking, and unexplained hypoxia may all be downstream of one structural problem at the craniocervical junction.

## Clinical pearls

A few practical takeaways from this case:

- Not all Chiari headaches are classic cough headaches.
- Scalp allodynia and localized vertex pain should prompt a neurologic differential, not automatic labeling as a primary headache disorder.
- Hair sensitivity or relief with haircut is a real clue to cutaneous allodynia.
- If “headache” is accompanied by dysphagia, choking, or unexplained respiratory events, think brainstem.
- A normal ENT or GI workup does not exclude a neurologic cause of choking.
- Re-review of prior imaging can be diagnostic years later, especially when the original study was read as “normal.”

## What to do at the bedside

If you see a patient with odd scalp pain and a story that doesn’t quite fit, ask a few focused questions:

- Is the pain fixed in one location or migratory?
- Is there pain from combing, touching, or moving the hair?
- Any cough/strain component?
- Any choking, dysphagia, hoarseness, sleep-related symptoms, or unexplained hypoxia?
- Any prior trauma or historical imaging that deserves a second look?

If the answer to “pain plus brainstem symptoms” is yes, think MRI of the craniocervical junction.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that Chiari I can masquerade as a primary headache disorder, especially when the pain is atypical and scalp-localized. Vertex pain, cutaneous allodynia, and even a haircut helping the symptoms may sound odd, but they are still neurologically meaningful clues.

The real teaching point is simple: unusual scalp pain should not be isolated from the rest of the neurologic story. When it travels with choking, bulbar symptoms, or unexplained hypoxia, broaden the differential and look for a structural lesion at the foramen magnum.

## Take-home points

- Chiari I can present with non-classic pain phenotypes, including vertex pain and scalp allodynia.
- Localized scalp pain should not be automatically labeled as nummular headache without considering secondary causes.
- Hair sensitivity or relief with shorter hair suggests cutaneous allodynia and should prompt deeper neurologic thinking.
- Pairing atypical headache with choking, dysphagia, or respiratory symptoms should trigger evaluation of the craniocervical junction.
- Re-review of old imaging can reveal missed Chiari findings and shorten a very long diagnostic delay.
