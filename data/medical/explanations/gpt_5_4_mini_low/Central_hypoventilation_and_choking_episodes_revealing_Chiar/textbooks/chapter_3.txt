Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Diagnosis

This patient’s workup illustrates a common diagnostic challenge: recurrent choking and intermittent desaturation can be misattributed to reflux, anxiety, or lung disease unless the clinician steps back and asks whether the pattern is actually neurogenic. Her symptoms were long-standing, episodic, and involved both daytime meals and nocturnal events. Because she initially presented with choking, the first evaluations appropriately focused on the upper aerodigestive tract: flexible laryngoscopy, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, and reflux-directed assessment. These tests are useful for excluding mucosal, structural, or obvious laryngeal causes of dysphagia, but normal results do not rule out a swallowing disorder of neurologic origin. In this case, repeatedly normal endoscopic studies argued against an obstructing lesion, severe laryngopharyngeal reflux, or a clear structural esophageal problem.

A key clinical clue was that she had difficulty with both solids and liquids equally. Mechanical esophageal obstruction classically produces progressive dysphagia for solids first, with liquids affected later as narrowing becomes severe. In contrast, simultaneous difficulty with solids and liquids suggests impaired coordination or weakness of the swallowing mechanism, which is more consistent with neurogenic dysphagia. That pattern should prompt consideration of bulbar dysfunction, neuromuscular junction disease, or central nervous system pathology rather than an isolated gastrointestinal cause.

Because she also reported unexplained oxygen desaturation, pulmonary disease had to be considered. Chest imaging and pulmonary function testing were normal, which made intrinsic lung disease, major airflow obstruction, and restrictive pulmonary pathology unlikely explanations for her hypoxemia. Her spirometry and respiratory muscle measures were essentially preserved, arguing against a primary ventilatory limitation. A normal daytime arterial blood gas similarly did not support chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure. However, a normal arterial blood gas does not exclude intermittent or sleep-related central hypoventilation. Patients with episodic brainstem dysfunction may breathe adequately while awake and still develop marked nocturnal or episodic desaturation when automatic respiratory drive is impaired.

The laboratory evaluation was directed at common mimics. Normal thyroid studies, vitamin B12, metabolic testing, inflammatory markers, and neuromuscular junction antibodies helped exclude reversible metabolic, inflammatory, and deficiency-related causes of bulbar or respiratory symptoms. Myasthenia gravis was a particularly important consideration because it can present with choking, fatigable dysphagia, and fluctuating respiratory complaints. In this patient, however, the clinical pattern was not typical: there was no fatigability, no clear diurnal worsening, and no exam findings suggesting generalized weakness. Negative acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibodies further lowered the likelihood of myasthenia gravis, although clinicians should remember that seronegativity does not absolutely exclude it when the phenotype is strongly suggestive.

The turning point in the case was re-review of an old head CT obtained after her motor vehicle accident. The original report had been normal, but careful reassessment showed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. This is a valuable diagnostic lesson: when symptoms remain unexplained, prior imaging should be revisited rather than accepted at face value, especially if the clinical picture evolves over time. Historical imaging can reveal subtle abnormalities that were overlooked initially or were not considered relevant at the time.

MRI then confirmed Chiari malformation type I, with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent and crowding at the foramen magnum. In the appropriate clinical context, this degree of tonsillar descent is significant because it supports compression at the craniocervical junction, where lower cranial nerve nuclei and respiratory control pathways are located. The imaging findings helped unify her chronic occipital pain, choking episodes, and episodic hypoxemia under a single structural diagnosis.

Sleep evaluation added another important layer. The sleep study showed only mild overall apnea-hypopnea index, but most events were central rather than obstructive. In selected patients, event type can matter more than the absolute AHI because predominant central events point toward impaired central respiratory control rather than upper airway obstruction. This is particularly relevant in Chiari malformation, where brainstem compression can disrupt automatic ventilation. The study used a type III portable device, which is less definitive than full polysomnography because it lacks EEG monitoring. Without EEG, total sleep time cannot be measured accurately, so respiratory events are often divided by recording time rather than true sleep time, which can underestimate severity. Type III testing can be helpful for screening, but if the clinical suspicion for central hypoventilation is high, full polysomnography with EEG, capnography, and ideally video monitoring is preferred to define sleep state, event timing, carbon dioxide retention, and the relationship of events to arousals.

The differential diagnosis in this case therefore narrowed in a stepwise fashion. Reflux and laryngopharyngeal reflux became less likely after repeated normal endoscopy and lack of a structural explanation. Mechanical esophageal disease was not supported by the equal difficulty with solids and liquids. Myasthenia gravis was unlikely given the clinical pattern and negative antibodies. Primary pulmonary disease was excluded by normal chest imaging, pulmonary function testing, and daytime blood gas values. Anxiety may have complicated the patient’s experience and was repeatedly invoked during prior evaluations, but it could not account for objective desaturation to the 70%–80% range. The remaining unifying explanation was acquired central hypoventilation from structural brainstem pathology related to Chiari malformation type I. This case shows why clinicians should maintain a broad differential and avoid prematurely labeling unexplained respiratory or bulbar symptoms as functional when objective abnormalities are present.
