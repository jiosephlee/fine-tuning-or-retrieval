Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 1: ## Chiari Malformation Type I as a Cause of Bulbar and Respiratory Dysfunction

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is defined radiologically by descent of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. In practice, the imaging threshold is only the starting point: a patient with tonsillar descent may be asymptomatic, while another with similar anatomy may have substantial neurologic symptoms. This distinction is important in understanding the present case, because the patient’s longstanding choking episodes, nocturnal desaturation, and chronic occipital pain were ultimately more informative than the imaging measurement alone.

Epidemiologically, CMI is seen relatively often on modern imaging, but symptomatic disease is uncommon. This gap between imaging prevalence and clinical prevalence reflects the fact that many cases are incidental or only mildly crowd the craniocervical junction. The current view is that CMI lies on a spectrum, ranging from a benign anatomical variant to a clinically significant Chiari syndrome. In symptomatic patients, the malformation is usually interpreted as a functional disorder of the posterior fossa and cervicomedullary junction rather than a purely static structural abnormality.

The central pathophysiologic concept is posterior fossa crowding, or a volumetric mismatch between the size of the posterior fossa and its contents. When the bony space is relatively small, the cerebellar tonsils can herniate downward through the foramen magnum, narrowing the subarachnoid spaces around the medulla and upper cervical cord. This region is clinically important because it contains the lower brainstem, respiratory control centers, and the lower cranial nerves that mediate swallowing and pharyngeal function. Crowding at this level can also disturb cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, which may contribute to symptoms through pressure dissociation, traction, and impaired pulsatile CSF dynamics.

The classic adult presentation is suboccipital headache, often worsened by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Myelopathic symptoms and syringomyelia may also occur when CSF flow obstruction extends along the cervical spinal cord. Lower cranial nerve involvement is especially relevant in cases like this one, because dysfunction of the glossopharyngeal, vagal, and hypoglossal pathways can produce dysphagia, choking, palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, and cricopharyngeal dysfunction. When these manifestations are present, patients may first be evaluated by gastroenterology or otolaryngology, as happened here, before a neurologic cause is recognized.

Respiratory involvement is an increasingly recognized manifestation of CMI. Compression or distortion of the medulla can impair the networks that regulate breathing during sleep and, in some patients, during wakefulness as well. This can lead to sleep-disordered breathing, central apnoeas, intermittent hypoxaemia, and a phenotype resembling central hypoventilation. In the case report, the patient’s repeated desaturation without a clear pulmonary explanation, and her predominantly central events on sleep testing, fit this pattern. Importantly, these respiratory abnormalities may occur even when lung imaging, pulmonary function testing, and basic arterial blood gas values are normal, because the problem lies in central control rather than in the lungs themselves.

The distinction between congenital central hypoventilation syndrome and acquired central hypoventilation due to brainstem compression is essential. Congenital disease is usually related to PHOX2B mutations and presents early in life, whereas acquired central hypoventilation arises from structural or inflammatory injury to the medulla or its connections. In CMI, the mechanism is acquired and anatomically localized: crowding at the foramen magnum interferes with automatic respiratory drive and lower cranial nerve function. This is why CMI can present with a combination of choking, sleep-related desaturation, and minimal subjective dyspnoea, yet remain difficult to detect unless clinicians consider a structural brainstem process.
