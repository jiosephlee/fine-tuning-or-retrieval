Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 5: ## Management, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons

Management of symptomatic Chiari malformation type I is driven less by the millimetres of tonsillar descent and more by the clinical syndrome it produces. Patients with isolated incidental tonsillar ectopia can often be observed, but referral to neurosurgery is important when there are symptoms suggesting brainstem, lower cranial nerve, or spinal cord involvement. In this case, the combination of chronic occipital pain, progressive choking with both solids and liquids, and unexplained desaturation with central-predominant sleep-related events made the malformation clinically significant rather than incidental. The rationale for posterior fossa decompression is to relieve crowding at the cervicomedullary junction and restore cerebrospinal fluid flow around the foramen magnum, thereby reducing compression-related dysfunction. When successful, surgery may improve headache, dysphagia, and respiratory abnormalities, although the degree of benefit is variable and recovery is not guaranteed, especially when symptoms have been present for years.

Because the patient chose to defer surgery while considering her options, conservative management focused on symptom control and safety. She adopted dietary adjustments to reduce choking risk and used practical strategies for her headaches, including keeping her hair short, which reduced scalp allodynia. Given the history of severe oxygen desaturations, she was also counseled to remain vigilant for worsening respiratory symptoms and to seek urgent care if episodes became more frequent or prolonged. This kind of interim management is reasonable when the patient is stable and still weighing operative treatment, but it does not replace definitive neurosurgical assessment.

A structured surveillance plan was put in place, reflecting the possibility that symptoms could progress over time. She was scheduled for regular clinical review, overnight oximetry to monitor hypoxic events, and interval MRI to assess for progression at the craniocervical junction. Clear escalation criteria are essential in such patients: worsening dysphagia, new neurological deficits, increasing frequency or severity of desaturation, or radiographic progression would strengthen the case for surgery. Because her presentation spans family medicine, neurology, pulmonology, sleep medicine, otolaryngology, gastroenterology, and neurosurgery, multidisciplinary coordination is central to care.

This case illustrates several practical diagnostic lessons. First, persistent choking or dysphagia with normal endoscopy and laryngoscopy should raise concern for a neurogenic cause, especially when both solids and liquids are affected equally. Second, unexplained sleep-related hypoventilation or central-predominant apnoeic events should prompt evaluation for structural brain abnormalities at the craniocervical junction. Third, reviewing prior imaging can be decisive; in this patient, an old CT scan that had been read as normal contained the clue that ultimately redirected the diagnosis. More broadly, the case shows that Chiari malformation type I can masquerade as an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, so structural causes should remain on the differential in patients with otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.
