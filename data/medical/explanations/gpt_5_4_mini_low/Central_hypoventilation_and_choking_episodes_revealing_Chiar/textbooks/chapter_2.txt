Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Presentation and Problem Representation of the Case

This patient’s presentation is best understood by first reconstructing the symptoms as they evolved over time, because no single complaint fully explained the illness at the outset. For 7 years, she experienced recurrent choking episodes during meals and during sleep. Importantly, these episodes affected both solids and liquids, which argues against a simple mechanical obstruction such as a fixed esophageal stricture and instead raises concern for impaired oropharyngeal coordination or lower cranial nerve dysfunction. The nocturnal episodes were especially striking: she would awaken several times each week with coughing and gasping, suggesting intermittent failure of ventilatory control during sleep rather than isolated swallowing difficulty.

Her history also included 14 years of chronic occipital and vertex headache beginning after a minor motor vehicle accident. The pain was not a classic Chiari headache in one respect—it was not clearly worsened by coughing or straining—but it had several concerning features. It was pressure-like, localized to the posterior head and vertex, and associated with marked focal allodynia, including discomfort from the weight of her hair. This scalp sensitivity and relief after cutting her hair short suggested a neuropathic or cervicogenic component rather than a routine primary headache syndrome. Because the headache began after trauma and was repeatedly labeled as nummular headache, the possibility of a structural posterior fossa disorder was not initially pursued.

The functional impact was substantial and should be part of the clinical picture. She avoided restaurants and social gatherings because of fear of choking in public, and the persistent symptoms caused withdrawal and emotional distress. Even her attempt to reduce head pain by keeping her hair short created cultural and personal distress. These details matter because they show that the syndrome was not only chronic but also disabling, and they help explain why the patient repeatedly sought care even when prior tests were unrevealing.

The most unusual clue came from her home pulse oximetry during the COVID-19 pandemic. She repeatedly recorded oxygen saturations in the 70%–80% range without feeling markedly short of breath. That combination—severe desaturation with little or no dyspnea—is a red flag for impaired automatic respiratory control, because patients with ordinary pulmonary disease usually experience distress out of proportion to the degree of hypoxemia. The airport episode made this even more compelling: she became dizzy and nauseated, was found to have an oxygen saturation of 78%, and improved rapidly with supplemental oxygen. This was a physiologic event, not just anxiety, and it pointed toward episodic hypoventilation or a central control problem rather than intrinsic lung disease.

The diagnostic delay is understandable in hindsight. She had undergone multiple flexible laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies, all normal, which understandably shifted attention toward otolaryngologic or gastrointestinal explanations. However, persistent choking with normal ENT and GI evaluations should prompt reconsideration of a neurologic cause, especially when symptoms involve both solids and liquids and are accompanied by unexplained hypoxia or chronic posterior head pain. In this case, the failure to find an anatomic lesion in the aerodigestive tract was not reassuring; it was a clue that the problem might lie higher, at the level of the brainstem or craniocervical junction.

A useful problem representation for this case is: a middle-aged woman with long-standing posterior head pain after minor trauma, chronic dysphagia/choking affecting solids and liquids, nocturnal gasping episodes, and intermittent severe oxygen desaturation without perceived dyspnea, ultimately suggesting a structural brainstem disorder causing bulbar dysfunction and impaired automatic breathing. That synthesis is what should trigger neurologic reconsideration. In retrospect, the symptom cluster was not random: the chronic posterior fossa-type headache, bulbar symptoms, and apparent failure of automatic ventilation all fit a single unifying explanation at the craniocervical junction rather than separate ENT, GI, or pulmonary diseases.
