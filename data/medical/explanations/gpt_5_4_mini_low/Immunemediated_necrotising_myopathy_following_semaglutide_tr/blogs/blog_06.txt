Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# The Semaglutide Era and Rare Harm Signals: How Clinicians Should Think About Pharmacovigilance

## Why this case matters

Semaglutide and other GLP-1 receptor agonists have quickly gone from “newish diabetes drugs” to mainstream therapy for obesity, diabetes, and metabolic disease. That scale matters. When a medication is prescribed to millions of people, rare adverse events stop being academic curiosities and become bedside problems.

This case is interesting because the patient’s course did **not** fit simple drug intolerance or straightforward toxic rhabdomyolysis. She had a dramatic GI reaction after her first semaglutide dose, but then—rather than improving—she developed a progressive, severe proximal myopathy over months, with dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK that rose from mildly elevated to nearly 4,000 U/L. Muscle biopsy supported **immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)**, and she improved with corticosteroids, IVIG, and stopping semaglutide.

That combination raises a bigger question: when a patient gets worse after starting a GLP-1 receptor agonist, are we always dealing with “expected” side effects, deconditioning, or sarcopenia? Or are we occasionally seeing an underrecognized autoimmune muscle syndrome?

## The bedside problem: distinguishing toxicity from autoimmunity

In day-to-day practice, muscle symptoms in a patient on a weight-loss medication can be easy to dismiss. Fatigue, weakness, reduced intake, vomiting, and generalized deconditioning are common enough in this population that clinicians can be falsely reassured.

But the timeline in this case should make you stop and think. A toxic or metabolic myopathy usually declares itself early and improves after drug withdrawal. This patient instead had:

- Progressive proximal weakness
- Bulbar symptoms: dysphagia and dysphonia
- Marked CK rise over time
- MRI evidence of muscle edema
- Biopsy with necrotic and regenerating fibers plus macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis
- Clinical response to immunosuppression

That is a different beast.

## Why IMNM is the key syndrome to recognize

IMNM is a distinct inflammatory myopathy characterized by aggressive proximal weakness, elevated CK, and muscle fiber necrosis with relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation. It is often associated with anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies, but not always—seronegative disease is real and creates diagnostic uncertainty.

A practical point: **CK elevation plus weakness is not enough to call something rhabdomyolysis**. Rhabdo is generally a toxic or physical injury pattern. IMNM is an immune disease that can masquerade as rhabdo early, especially if you focus only on the lab value and not the trajectory.

Clues favoring IMNM over direct drug toxicity include:

- Progressive weakness rather than rapid recovery
- Persistent or rising CK after the drug is stopped
- Bulbar involvement or severe proximal weakness
- MRI edema in a myositis pattern
- Muscle biopsy with necrosis/regeneration and macrophage infiltration
- Response to steroids, IVIG, or other immunotherapy

In this case, the patient’s CK would not be expected to climb 12-fold over 4 months from a one-time toxic exposure alone. That prolonged progression is what makes the autoimmune explanation compelling.

## Pharmacovigilance starts at the bedside

Single case reports are not proof of causality, but they are often the first signal that something rare is happening. That is exactly why they matter.

When assessing a possible drug-related immune toxicity, clinicians should think in terms of **plausibility, timing, exclusion of alternatives, and response to withdrawal/treatment** rather than demanding perfect proof. In this report, the authors applied WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre logic and judged the relationship as **probable**, not definite. That is the right level of humility.

A few practical principles:

- A temporal relationship is necessary, but not sufficient.
- If there is a plausible alternative explanation, don’t overcall causality.
- Improvement after stopping the drug supports the association, but immunosuppression can confound that interpretation.
- Rechallenge is usually the cleanest test scientifically, but often impossible or unsafe clinically.
- With severe immune-mediated toxicity, rechallenge is frequently **unethical**.

That last point is important. In this case, asking a patient to re-expose themselves to a medication after severe weakness, CK elevation, and biopsy-proven myopathy would be hard to justify. Pharmacovigilance has to work without perfect experiments.

## What this means for clinicians using GLP-1 receptor agonists

GLP-1 drugs are not “bad drugs.” Their benefits are real and often transformative. But the speed and scale of their adoption means we need a low threshold to recognize uncommon harm signals.

This case should push us to ask:

- Could some “medication intolerance” syndromes actually be early immune toxicities?
- Are we missing inflammatory myopathies because the initial symptoms are nonspecific?
- Are we attributing weakness to weight loss or deconditioning when the patient is actually developing a treatable autoimmune disease?

The literature already suggests GLP-1 receptor agonists can be associated with reduced skeletal muscle mass, and there are emerging reports of severe muscle injury with related agents. But muscle wasting, rhabdomyolysis, and IMNM are not the same diagnosis. The distinction matters because the treatment pathways differ dramatically.

## A pragmatic approach to the patient with weakness on a GLP-1 receptor agonist

If a patient on semaglutide or another GLP-1 agent presents with weakness, especially if it is progressive or accompanied by bulbar symptoms, don’t stop at “it’s probably the medication.”

Consider:

- CK and basic metabolic panel
- AST/ALT in context of possible muscle injury
- Focused neuro exam: proximal strength, neck flexors, bulbar function
- Urinalysis for myoglobin if rhabdo is suspected
- Review for statins, supplements, recent exertion, infection, or endocrine causes
- Autoimmune work-up if weakness is persistent or disproportionate
- MRI and muscle biopsy when the diagnosis remains unclear

If the clinical pattern suggests inflammatory myopathy, early rheumatology/neurology involvement is appropriate. And if the patient is significantly weak or has dysphagia, don’t wait for every serology before starting treatment.

## Take-home points

- **Not all muscle symptoms on GLP-1 therapy are benign intolerance or sarcopenia.**
- **Progressive weakness, dysphagia, and rising CK should trigger evaluation for inflammatory myopathy, including IMNM.**
- **Rising CK over weeks to months after stopping the drug argues against simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.**
- **Muscle biopsy can be decisive when antibodies are negative.**
- **Rechallenge is often not ethical after severe immune-mediated toxicity.**
- **Clinicians should report suspected rare adverse events to pharmacovigilance systems; single cases can become important safety signals.**

## Final thought

The semaglutide era is going to generate more “rare event” questions, not fewer. Most will not represent true causality. But some will. The challenge for bedside clinicians is to remain skeptical without becoming dismissive—to recognize that when a common drug meets an uncommon syndrome, the first clue may be a patient who doesn’t follow the expected recovery curve.

That is where pharmacovigilance stops being abstract and becomes clinical medicine.
