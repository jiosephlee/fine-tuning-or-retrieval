Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# AST, ALT, and CK: Don’t Let “Liver Disease” Hide a Myopathy

## The trap

A woman in her 60s had years of mildly elevated AST and ALT, obesity, and biopsy-proven steatotic liver disease. Reasonable conclusion, right? “It’s the NAFLD.”

Then she developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK in the thousands. Muscle biopsy showed necrotizing myopathy. Her earlier “liver enzymes” had been the warning sign we all too easily file away as hepatic.

That’s the teaching point: AST and ALT are not liver-specific. When muscle is injured, they can rise too — and in a patient with known fatty liver disease, it’s easy to anchor on the liver and miss a myopathy until the patient is already profoundly weak.

## Why this matters

AST and ALT are commonly called “liver enzymes,” but that shorthand is misleading. They’re aminotransferases found in hepatocytes, yes — but also in skeletal muscle. If muscle injury is the real driver, the liver workup can be unrevealing while the patient’s weakness progresses.

This is especially tricky when NAFLD is already on the chart. A plausible hepatic explanation can become a diagnostic dead end.

In this case, the patient had biopsy-proven steatohepatitis years before semaglutide exposure, so her baseline transaminase elevation was real. But later, when she became weak and her CK rose dramatically, the aminotransferases were no longer just a liver story. The key is not choosing liver versus muscle too early. Often, you need to think “both until proven otherwise.”

## How muscle elevates AST and ALT

Skeletal muscle injury can cause aminotransferase elevation from:

- **Direct release from damaged myocytes**
- **Inflammatory myopathy**
- **Rhabdomyolysis**
- **Severe exertional injury**
- **Drug-induced myotoxicity**

AST tends to rise more prominently than ALT in muscle injury, but either can be elevated. The degree of transaminase elevation does not reliably distinguish liver from muscle.

The bigger clue is the **pattern**:
- Aminotransferases elevated out of proportion to other liver markers
- **CK elevated**
- Symptoms of muscle disease: weakness, myalgia, dysphagia, dark urine, exercise intolerance

## A practical way to separate liver from muscle

When AST/ALT are abnormal, don’t stop at “fatty liver.” Ask: is there evidence of muscle injury?

A useful bedside framework:

### 1) Look for muscle symptoms
Weakness matters. So do dysphagia, dysphonia, and difficulty standing from a chair or climbing stairs. These are not liver symptoms.

### 2) Check CK early
If AST/ALT are elevated and the history is even vaguely compatible with muscle disease, get a CK. It’s cheap, fast, and often decisive.

### 3) Review the liver panel, not just AST/ALT
If bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, and INR are normal, isolated aminotransferase elevation is less convincing for intrinsic liver injury.

### 4) Trend the numbers together
If CK rises and falls with AST/ALT, muscle is likely contributing. In this case, the patient’s AST/ALT were elevated in the setting of high CK, and her transaminases remained mildly abnormal even after CK normalized — supporting a background hepatic source plus superimposed muscle injury.

### 5) Don’t let known NAFLD close the case
NAFLD is common, and common things do happen commonly. But it can also become a diagnostic distraction. A liver biopsy showing steatosis does not exclude concurrent myositis.

## What this case adds

This patient’s course is unusual for another reason: the myopathy appeared after a single semaglutide dose, and the authors consider immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy probably associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure. Whether or not that association proves causal, the diagnostic lesson is broader.

The transaminases were a clue, but only when interpreted in context:
- longstanding mild AST/ALT elevation from steatotic liver disease
- later profound weakness
- marked CK elevation
- muscle biopsy consistent with necrotizing myopathy

That’s the pivot point: once muscle symptoms appeared, the elevated aminotransferases could no longer be safely attributed to the liver alone.

## Clinical pearls

- **AST and ALT are not liver-specific.**
- **In any patient with elevated aminotransferases plus weakness, check CK early.**
- **Normal bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase make isolated hepatocellular disease less certain.**
- **NAFLD can coexist with myopathy and obscure the diagnosis.**
- **A “liver” explanation should not delay evaluation for inflammatory myopathy when the clinical story fits.**

## Take-home points

- Elevated AST/ALT do not equal liver disease.
- Muscle injury can produce transaminase elevations that mimic hepatic injury.
- In patients with known NAFLD, don’t anchor on steatosis if new weakness appears.
- CK is the key test that helps unmask occult myopathy.
- If the story includes proximal weakness, dysphagia, or dysphonia, think beyond the liver.
