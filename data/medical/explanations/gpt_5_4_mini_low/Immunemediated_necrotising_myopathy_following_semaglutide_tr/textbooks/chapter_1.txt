Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Early Course: From Semaglutide Exposure to Progressive Weakness

This patient was a woman in her 60s who had been functionally independent before the onset of illness. Her medical history included obesity, hepatosteatosis/non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote history of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. The pre-existing liver disease is relevant because she had already been evaluated for chronic transaminase elevation and had biopsy-proven steatohepatitis before any neuromuscular symptoms appeared. In that setting, semaglutide was started for weight loss and metabolic benefit, a common contemporary indication for glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in patients with obesity and fatty liver disease.

The first clinical event after semaglutide exposure was an acute intolerance-like syndrome. Within hours of her first and only 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose, she developed nausea and vomiting that progressed to poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury, and severe metabolic acidosis requiring hospitalization. She improved with supportive care and hydration, suggesting that this initial episode was dominated by gastrointestinal toxicity and hypovolaemia rather than a primary muscle disorder. Although creatine kinase was checked during that admission and was mildly elevated at 337 U/L, this degree of elevation is nonspecific and far below what would usually be expected in established rhabdomyolysis. Mild CK elevation can occur with many stress states, dehydration, or limited muscle injury, so at that stage the laboratory value alone did not establish a necrotising myopathy.

The more important clue was the subsequent evolution over the next four months. Rather than recovering fully after the acute drug reaction, she developed steadily progressive proximal weakness. By the time she re-presented, she could no longer stand or sit upright without assistance, and she had developed dysphagia to both liquids and solids as well as dysphonia. These are red-flag neuromuscular symptoms that are difficult to explain by deconditioning alone. Deconditioning typically causes generalized weakness and reduced endurance, but it does not usually produce a marked proximal pattern with bulbar involvement and a substantial CK rise. Dysphagia and dysphonia in particular raise concern for an inflammatory myopathy, a necrotising autoimmune process, or another disorder affecting skeletal muscle rather than simple fatigue after hospitalization.

The medication timeline was central to the diagnostic reasoning. The weakness began after semaglutide exposure and progressed despite the drug having been discontinued after the single dose, which argued against a transient adverse effect and suggested an ongoing pathologic process. A careful exposure history also helped narrow the differential. She denied prior statin use, which is important because statins are the classic medication linked to immune-mediated necrotising myopathy through anti-HMGCR autoimmunity. She also denied use of red yeast rice, mushrooms, or other supplements sometimes associated with myotoxicity, as well as alcohol or illicit drugs. There was no family history of myopathy and no history of excessive exertion that might have explained the CK rise or weakness.

Taken together, the early course moved the case away from isolated semaglutide intolerance or postoperative deconditioning and toward a suspected drug-associated myopathy. The combination of persistent and progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms, and rising CK months after a single exposure created concern for an inflammatory or necrotising muscle process, setting the stage for the later laboratory, imaging, and biopsy work-up.
