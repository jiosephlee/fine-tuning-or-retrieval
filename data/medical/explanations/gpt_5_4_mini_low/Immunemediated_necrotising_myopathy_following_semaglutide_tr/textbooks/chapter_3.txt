Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup: Laboratory, Imaging, and Biopsy Evidence

The diagnostic evaluation in this case was built around three tasks: confirming that the patient had an active myopathic process, determining whether it fit immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), and excluding other common mimics such as toxic rhabdomyolysis, endocrine myopathy, systemic connective tissue disease, malignancy-associated myositis, or primary liver disease. Because IMNM can present with progressive proximal weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), but may also be seronegative, the interpretation of each test depended on the overall pattern rather than any single abnormality.

The CK trend was especially informative. Her admission CK at the tertiary center was already elevated at 1237 U/L, and the outside-hospital peak of 3992 U/L showed that muscle injury had increased substantially over time. This degree of elevation is compatible with inflammatory myopathy and is much more than would be expected from simple deconditioning. The fact that CK rose progressively over four months, rather than falling quickly after the initial semaglutide exposure, argued against a brief toxic or ischemic rhabdomyolysis and supported an ongoing immune process. In IMNM, CK is often markedly elevated and may remain high until immunosuppression is started and the offending trigger is removed.

Her AST and ALT were also elevated, which can be confusing in a patient with both muscle disease and known fatty liver. Aminotransferases are not liver-specific: AST, and to a lesser extent ALT, can rise from skeletal muscle injury. In inflammatory myopathies, transaminase elevations may therefore mirror CK. In this case, however, the interpretation was complicated by prior biopsy-proven nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). That chronic hepatic diagnosis made it important not to over-attribute the enzyme abnormalities to muscle alone. The repeat liver biopsy was therefore helpful because it again showed steatosis without significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis, supporting chronic hepatosteatosis as the primary hepatic explanation while leaving muscle injury as the likely contributor to the acute enzyme rise.

The basic screening studies helped exclude common alternative explanations and assess severity. Thyroid-stimulating hormone was normal, making hypothyroid myopathy unlikely. Renal function was normal at the time of the myopathy workup, which suggested that the CK elevation was not causing clinically significant pigment nephropathy. Blood counts and complement levels were unremarkable, which lowered concern for a systemic lupus-like or vasculitic process. ESR and C-reactive protein were mildly elevated, consistent with inflammation but not specific for a particular autoimmune syndrome. The modest inflammatory marker elevation fits with IMNM, which can have variable systemic inflammatory findings and often presents more with weakness and CK elevation than with overt constitutional symptoms.

A broad autoantibody evaluation was performed because the differential diagnosis of inflammatory myopathy includes connective tissue disease overlap syndromes and several myositis subtypes. Her serologies for ANA, SSA, Sm, RNP, dsDNA, ANCA, and antimitochondrial antibody were negative, and the myositis panel was negative for the two antibodies most strongly associated with IMNM, anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP, as well as other myositis-specific antibodies. Negative HMGCR and SRP testing did not exclude IMNM. A substantial minority of patients with IMNM are seronegative, and in such patients the diagnosis relies more heavily on the clinicopathologic pattern. In practical terms, a negative myositis panel reduces the likelihood of a defined autoantibody-associated subtype, but it does not rule out immune-mediated disease.

Her isolated low-titer SS-B positivity required cautious interpretation. An isolated SS-B result with negative ANA and SSA is often nonspecific and is not, by itself, sufficient to diagnose Sjögren syndrome or another connective tissue disease. In this case, the absence of sicca symptoms, rash, arthritis, cytopenias, hypocomplementemia, or other organ involvement made a systemic connective tissue disease less likely. Thus, the low-titer SS-B did not outweigh the broader negative autoimmune workup or the muscle-biopsy findings.

Imaging supported the suspicion of an inflammatory myopathy and helped assess for extra-muscular disease. MRI of the thighs showed symmetric muscle edema, particularly in the proximal quadriceps, which is a typical pattern in active inflammatory myopathy. Muscle edema on MRI reflects increased water content from inflammation and injury and is useful because it can identify affected muscle for biopsy and distinguish active disease from chronic fatty replacement or simple atrophy. The bilateral, symmetric distribution favored a systemic myopathic process rather than focal injury or denervation.

Because the patient had an elevated troponin and markedly elevated BNP, cardiac involvement also had to be considered. Troponin elevation in a myositis patient may reflect myocardial inflammation, skeletal muscle cross-reactivity depending on the assay, or demand ischemia in a severely ill patient. Echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, while cardiac MRI demonstrated myocardial edema with mild regional hypokinesis but no definite scar or infarction. This pattern was concerning for possible early myocarditis or stress-related myocardial dysfunction, but it was not definitive for a severe primary cardiac process. The cardiac workup was important because inflammatory myopathies can involve the heart, and because identifying myocardial inflammation would strengthen the impression of a systemic immune-mediated syndrome.

Additional imaging was used to exclude associated systemic conditions that can accompany inflammatory myopathy. High-resolution CT of the chest showed no interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy, which lowered concern for antisynthetase syndrome or an occult thoracic malignancy. Abdominal MRI again showed steatotic hepatomegaly without lymphadenopathy. This was relevant because malignancy-associated myositis is an important consideration in new-onset inflammatory myopathy, particularly in older adults. Although imaging does not eliminate all cancer risk, the absence of suspicious adenopathy or other masses made a paraneoplastic explanation less likely in this case.

It showed necrotic and regenerating fibers, myophagocytosis with CD68-positive macrophages, membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, and only sparse CD3-positive lymphocytes. This pattern is classic for IMNM: prominent myofiber necrosis and regeneration with relatively little lymphocytic inflammation. The presence of macrophage-mediated cleanup of necrotic fibers and complement deposition supports an immune-mediated process rather than simple toxic injury. By contrast, many other inflammatory myopathies show denser lymphocytic infiltrates or different tissue patterns, and toxic rhabdomyolysis typically lacks this characteristic immune signature.

Biopsy was especially pivotal because the patient was seronegative. When the expected autoantibodies are absent, the diagnosis of IMNM depends on clinicopathologic correlation: progressive proximal weakness, substantial CK elevation, supportive MRI edema, exclusion of endocrine and systemic mimics, and a biopsy showing necrotising myopathy with minimal lymphocytic inflammation. In this case, the biopsy supplied the pathologic proof that allowed clinicians to move beyond a nonspecific “myopathy” label and treat as IMNM.
