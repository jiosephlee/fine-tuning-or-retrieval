Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 4: ## Differential Diagnosis and Causal Reasoning: Why Semaglutide-Associated IMNM Was Favored

The key diagnostic problem in this case was to explain a subacute syndrome of progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms, and rising creatine kinase (CK) after a single semaglutide exposure. In a patient with difficulty rising or sitting upright, dysphagia, dysphonia, and CK elevation, the differential is broad and includes toxic or inflammatory myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, endocrine myopathy, inherited muscle disease, and less commonly neuromuscular junction disorders or motor neuron disease. The CK level and the distribution of weakness made a primary muscle process most likely. Bulbar involvement raised concern for a severe myopathy rather than a purely deconditioning-related problem, and the absence of sensory loss or focal neurologic deficits argued against a central nervous system cause.

A major distinction was between rhabdomyolysis and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Rhabdomyolysis is usually an acute toxic or metabolic muscle injury, often with abrupt CK elevation, myalgias, pigmenturia, and improvement after stopping the trigger and giving supportive care. That pattern fit poorly here. Although the patient had an early CK elevation during her initial semaglutide-associated illness, it was modest and did not explain the much larger rise to nearly 4000 U/L four months later. More importantly, her weakness worsened progressively while she was no longer taking semaglutide, which is not the usual time course for simple toxic rhabdomyolysis. In contrast, IMNM typically causes progressive proximal weakness, marked CK elevation, and persistent disease activity unless the autoimmune process is treated. The muscle biopsy, showing necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition, supported an inflammatory necrotising process rather than a transient toxic injury.

The clinicians also had to distinguish a drug-induced toxic myopathy from a drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy. This distinction matters because toxic myopathy generally improves after withdrawal of the drug alone, whereas autoimmune necrotising myopathy often requires immunosuppression even after the offending agent is stopped. In this case, the temporal relationship suggested semaglutide as a possible trigger, but the delayed progression and the clear response to corticosteroids plus IVIG pointed toward an immune-mediated process. Her rapid biochemical improvement after immunotherapy, together with sustained recovery after semaglutide avoidance, was more compatible with a drug-triggered IMNM than with direct myotoxicity.

Several other inflammatory myopathies were considered less likely. Polymyositis is now an uncommon and less specific diagnosis, and it usually lacks the necrotising pathology and autoantibody associations seen in this case. Dermatomyositis was unlikely because she had no rash, no classic skin findings, and no biopsy pattern typical of perifascicular injury. Overlap myositis was also less favored because she lacked clinical features of a connective tissue disease such as arthritis, Raynaud phenomenon, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, interstitial lung disease, or systemic sclerosis features, and her serologic work-up was broadly negative. Inclusion body myositis is generally a more chronic, slowly progressive disorder in older adults, often with distal weakness and poor response to immunotherapy; that phenotype did not match this patient’s relatively rapid decline and treatment responsiveness. Endocrine myopathy from hypothyroidism was excluded by a normal TSH, and inherited myopathy was unlikely given the late onset, previously normal function, and absence of a relevant family history.

The transaminase elevation also required interpretation. AST and ALT can rise in muscle disease, so elevated liver enzymes may falsely suggest hepatic injury. In this patient, however, the long-standing transaminitis predated the myopathy by years and was already explained by biopsy-proven nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). The repeat liver biopsy again showed steatosis with only mild progression of NAFLD, and the enzymes remained borderline abnormal even after CK normalized. This made occult myositis a less likely explanation for the chronic transaminitis and supported liver disease as the more plausible source.

Cardiac abnormalities added complexity but did not overturn the overall diagnosis. Mild troponin and BNP elevation, along with cardiac MRI showing myocardial oedema without definite scar, raised possibilities such as myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or nonspecific inflammatory involvement. These findings were not definitive enough to establish a separate primary cardiac disease, and they later resolved. In a patient with systemic muscle inflammation and severe physiologic stress, transient cardiac involvement can occur, but the heart findings were interpreted cautiously and were not the main driver of the diagnosis.

The strongest argument for semaglutide-associated IMNM was the totality of evidence rather than any single test. The timing was suggestive, because symptoms began after semaglutide exposure. Alternative explanations were progressively less convincing as the case evolved. The biopsy showed necrotising myopathy with inflammatory features. The patient improved with withdrawal of semaglutide, high-dose corticosteroids, and a single course of IVIG, and then remained well off immunosuppression. This pattern fits a probable drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy. At the same time, the authors correctly acknowledged uncertainty: there was no pre-semaglutide CK, so a smouldering pre-existing IMNM cannot be fully excluded. That limitation is important because causality in adverse drug reactions is often inferred, not proven.

The report applies the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality framework, which weighs temporal association, alternative causes, response to drug withdrawal, and rechallenge. Here, the relationship was judged “probable” because the timing was reasonable, other causes were not compelling, the patient improved after stopping the drug and receiving treatment, and rechallenge would have been unsafe. Rechallenge is generally unethical when a medication is suspected of causing a severe immune-mediated reaction, especially one that caused disabling weakness and required hospitalization. Thus, the case supports semaglutide as a likely trigger of IMNM, while appropriately stopping short of claiming definitive proof.
