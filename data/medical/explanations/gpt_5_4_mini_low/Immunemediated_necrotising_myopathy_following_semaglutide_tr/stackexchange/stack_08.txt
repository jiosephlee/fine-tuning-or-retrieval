Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient had elevated troponin and proBNP levels with cardiac MRI abnormalities; how would you interpret possible cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathy, and how should such findings affect the workup and monitoring plan?

In inflammatory myopathy, a mild elevation of troponin and proBNP should be taken seriously as a possible signal of cardiac involvement, but it is not by itself diagnostic of myocarditis. In this case, the biomarker abnormalities, together with the cardiac MRI showing focal myocardial edema and mild regional hypokinesis without late gadolinium enhancement, suggested myocardial inflammation or an alternative stress-related cardiomyopathy rather than established scar, infarction, or definitive fulminant myocarditis. The fact that the abnormalities later normalized supports a transient inflammatory or stress-mediated process.

The practical point is that cardiac involvement can be clinically silent in myositis, especially in immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, so even modest biomarker elevation warrants structured evaluation rather than dismissal. A reasonable workup includes ECG, transthoracic echocardiography, serial troponin and natriuretic peptide measurements, and cardiac MRI when biomarkers are abnormal or symptoms, arrhythmia, or echo changes are present. In a patient like this, the prior atrial fibrillation further strengthens the need for cardiac surveillance.

Management should be driven by severity and trajectory. If MRI suggests active inflammation, or if there are arrhythmias, heart failure symptoms, or worsening biomarkers, involve cardiology early, monitor rhythm, and follow ventricular function over time. If the findings are mild and the patient improves with treatment of the myopathy, repeat biomarkers and repeat echo or cardiac MRI can confirm resolution, as occurred here. The key is to treat these findings as potentially real cardiac involvement until proven otherwise, while recognizing that transient edema without scar may resolve with control of the underlying inflammatory myopathy.
