Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] When evaluating elevated AST and ALT in a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy, how do you distinguish muscle-derived transaminase elevation from underlying liver disease, and how was that distinction made here?

When AST and ALT are elevated in suspected inflammatory myopathy, the key point is that these aminotransferases are not liver-specific: both are present in skeletal muscle, and muscle injury can raise them, sometimes substantially. The distinction from liver disease is made by looking for a pattern that supports hepatocellular injury versus one that tracks with muscle disease.

A muscle source is suggested when AST and ALT rise in parallel with CK, aldolase, and clinical myositis; bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, and INR are normal; and the transaminases improve as the myopathy improves. A liver source is more likely when there are cholestatic abnormalities, impaired synthetic function, imaging or serologic evidence of liver disease, or persistent transaminase elevation despite normalization of muscle enzymes.

In this case, the distinction was made by the longitudinal pattern and by liver pathology. She had mild AST/ALT elevation years before the myopathy, and at that time she already had hepatosteatosis on ultrasound and, importantly, a liver biopsy showing steatohepatitis consistent with NAFLD. During the later episode of IMNM, her AST and ALT were elevated in the setting of marked CK elevation, but her liver biopsy again showed steatosis without significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis, and her transaminases remained borderline elevated even after CK normalized. That persistence after muscle recovery strongly supported a chronic hepatic source rather than transaminases released from muscle necrosis alone.
