Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a creatine kinase level in the thousands, what clinical features should raise suspicion for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy rather than simple drug-induced rhabdomyolysis?

What should make you think of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, rather than a straightforward toxic rhabdomyolysis, is the **pattern and persistence of the weakness**.

In this case, the key clues were:

- **Progressive, symmetric proximal muscle weakness** rather than isolated acute myalgias or transient weakness
- **Bulbar involvement** — specifically **dysphagia and dysphonia**, which are red flags for an inflammatory myopathy
- **A subacute course over weeks to months**, with continued decline **after the drug had already been stopped**, instead of the rapid onset-and-recovery pattern expected with toxic rhabdomyolysis
- **Creatine kinase remaining elevated and even rising over time** — here, from a modest elevation initially to nearly 4,000 U/L four months later
- **Lack of a classic rhabdomyolysis picture**, such as severe myalgias, cola-colored urine, or rapid CK normalization with hydration
- **Objective evidence of myositis**, including MRI muscle edema and, most importantly, biopsy showing **necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and only sparse lymphocytic inflammation**, which is characteristic of IMNM

By contrast, simple drug-induced rhabdomyolysis usually presents as an **acute toxic injury**: CK peaks early, the inciting agent is withdrawn, hydration is given, and the CK generally falls quickly over days to a few weeks. In this case, the persistent progression off semaglutide and the bulbar/proximal weakness pointed away from rhabdomyolysis and toward an autoimmune necrotising myopathy that required immunosuppression.

### [Morning Report] How does the time course of this patient’s illness help distinguish semaglutide-associated toxic muscle injury from a possible semaglutide-triggered autoimmune necrotising myopathy?

The **time course strongly favors an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy rather than a direct toxic myopathy/rhabdomyolysis**.

In a **toxic semaglutide-associated muscle injury**, one would expect the muscle injury to occur **during active exposure** and then begin to **improve quickly after the drug is stopped**, with CK falling over **days to a few weeks** and without the need for immunosuppression. That is the pattern described in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, including the tirzepatide case referenced in the report.

By contrast, this patient had only **a modest CK elevation at the time of the initial semaglutide-related hospitalization**, when her symptoms were dominated by gastrointestinal toxicity, hypovolemia, and AKI. The key clue is that she then **continued to deteriorate over the next 4 months while off semaglutide**, developing **progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a 12-fold rise in CK**. That delayed, progressive course is much more typical of **autoimmune IMNM**, in which the inflammatory muscle injury continues **after the inciting exposure has ended**.

Her subsequent course also supports an immune process: she had **necrotising myopathy on biopsy** and improved only after **pulse steroids plus IVIG**, with sustained recovery and normalization of CK. So the temporal pattern is not “drug exposure → acute muscle injury → rapid resolution,” but rather “brief exposure → delayed progressive myopathy persisting off drug → response to immunosuppression,” which is the clinical behavior of a **triggered autoimmune necrotising myopathy**.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the two autoantibodies classically associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and how should a negative HMGCR and SRP panel influence your diagnostic reasoning in this case?

The two autoantibodies classically associated with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy are **anti-HMGCR** and **anti-SRP**.

A **negative HMGCR and SRP panel does not rule out IMNM**. In fact, a substantial minority of patients are **seronegative**, so the diagnosis remains **clinicopathologic** rather than serology-only. In this case, the key features supporting IMNM were the **subacute progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, marked CK elevation, MRI muscle edema, and muscle biopsy showing necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and sparse lymphocytic inflammation**. The negative antibody panel should therefore lower confidence in the *classic seropositive subtypes*, but it should **not derail the diagnosis** when the phenotype and biopsy are strongly supportive, as in this patient.

In other words: **negative HMGCR/SRP makes the case atypical, not incompatible with IMNM**.

### [Pathophysiology] What muscle-biopsy findings are characteristic of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and which specific histopathologic features in this case supported that diagnosis?

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy is characterized on muscle biopsy by **prominent myofiber necrosis and regeneration with relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation**. The key pathologic pattern is **necrotic and regenerating fibers out of proportion to inflammatory infiltrate**, often with **macrophage-predominant cleanup of dead fibers** rather than a dense T-cell infiltrate, and sometimes **membrane attack complex (C5b-9) deposition on muscle fibers**.

In this case, the biopsy supported IMNM because it showed:

- **Dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers**
- **Myophagocytosis**
- **CD68-positive macrophage infiltrates**
- **Acid phosphatase positivity**, consistent with active fiber breakdown/repair
- **Membrane attack complex staining on necrotizing and non-necrotizing myofibers**
- Only **rare CD3-positive T cells** and limited **perivascular inflammatory cells**

Taken together, the biopsy showed a **necrotizing myopathy with minimal lymphocytic inflammation**, which is the classic histopathologic signature of IMNM.

### [Morning Report] When evaluating elevated AST and ALT in a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy, how do you distinguish muscle-derived transaminase elevation from underlying liver disease, and how was that distinction made here?

When AST and ALT are elevated in suspected inflammatory myopathy, the key point is that these aminotransferases are not liver-specific: both are present in skeletal muscle, and muscle injury can raise them, sometimes substantially. The distinction from liver disease is made by looking for a pattern that supports hepatocellular injury versus one that tracks with muscle disease.

A muscle source is suggested when AST and ALT rise in parallel with CK, aldolase, and clinical myositis; bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, and INR are normal; and the transaminases improve as the myopathy improves. A liver source is more likely when there are cholestatic abnormalities, impaired synthetic function, imaging or serologic evidence of liver disease, or persistent transaminase elevation despite normalization of muscle enzymes.

In this case, the distinction was made by the longitudinal pattern and by liver pathology. She had mild AST/ALT elevation years before the myopathy, and at that time she already had hepatosteatosis on ultrasound and, importantly, a liver biopsy showing steatohepatitis consistent with NAFLD. During the later episode of IMNM, her AST and ALT were elevated in the setting of marked CK elevation, but her liver biopsy again showed steatosis without significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis, and her transaminases remained borderline elevated even after CK normalized. That persistence after muscle recovery strongly supported a chronic hepatic source rather than transaminases released from muscle necrosis alone.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What is the differential diagnosis for seronegative necrotising myopathy in this patient, and what historical, laboratory, imaging, and biopsy data argued against connective tissue disease, hereditary myopathy, or statin-associated disease?

The differential diagnosis of a **seronegative necrotizing myopathy** in this patient included:

1. **Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)**, including seronegative IMNM  
2. **Connective tissue disease–associated myositis**  
3. **Hereditary myopathy** presenting with necrosis and elevated CK  
4. **Drug-induced myopathy**, especially **statin-associated** IMNM or toxic rhabdomyolysis  
5. Less likely, **endocrine/metabolic or inflammatory mimics** such as hypothyroid myopathy, though her thyroid studies were normal

In this case, several features argued **against connective tissue disease**. Clinically, she had **no rash, synovitis, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, interstitial lung disease, lymphadenopathy, or other systemic features** of a defined rheumatic disease. Serologically, her evaluation was essentially unrevealing: **ANA was negative**, as were **anti-SSA, anti-Smith, anti-U1 RNP, dsDNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies**, with **normal complements** and no cytopenias. The isolated low-titer **SS-B positivity** was not accompanied by the usual serologic or clinical pattern of Sjögren’s or lupus. Her chest CT was negative for ILD, and the overall picture did not support an overlap connective tissue disease.

A **hereditary myopathy** was less likely because she had been **fully independent and in good health until this subacute illness**, with **no family history of myopathy**, no earlier lifelong weakness, and no history suggesting a chronic inherited muscular dystrophy or metabolic myopathy. The abrupt onset in her 60s, the marked CK rise, and the biopsy pattern were not typical of a primary genetic myopathy.

A **statin-associated process** was also unlikely. She had **no prior statin exposure**, and she denied other recognized triggers such as **red yeast rice or mushroom supplements**. Importantly, the muscle disease did not behave like a transient toxic myopathy: her CK **rose progressively over 4 months** and weakness worsened **despite being off semaglutide**, which argues against an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis alone. In contrast, her biopsy showed a true **necrotizing autoimmune pattern**: **scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition, and only rare lymphocytic infiltrates**—findings consistent with **IMNM**, not simple toxic injury.

So, the strongest working diagnosis was **seronegative IMNM**, with semaglutide considered a possible trigger after other major causes—connective tissue disease, hereditary myopathy, and statin-associated disease—were not supported by the history, serologies, imaging, or biopsy.

### [Management] Why was this patient treated with pulse methylprednisolone followed by high-dose prednisone and IVIG, and what clinical features justified escalation beyond corticosteroids alone?

She was treated aggressively because this was not a mild, self-limited myositis; it was a severe immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy with bulbar involvement. In IMNM, prompt high-dose corticosteroids are used to rapidly suppress muscle inflammation and halt ongoing necrosis, so the initial 5-day pulse of methylprednisolone followed by prednisone 1 mg/kg daily was appropriate first-line therapy.

IVIG was added because several features signaled a high-risk, refractory presentation in which corticosteroids alone might be inadequate. She had:

- **Progressive, profound proximal weakness** to the point that she could no longer stand or sit independently  
- **Bulbar symptoms** — dysphagia and dysphonia — which indicate more severe disease and raise concern for aspiration and respiratory compromise  
- **Marked CK elevation** approaching 4000 U/L with continued clinical decline over months, consistent with active muscle injury  
- **Biopsy-proven IMNM**, showing necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition  
- **Possible cardiac involvement** on imaging and biomarker elevation, which further supported a systemic inflammatory process

In practice, IMNM often requires **early combination immunotherapy** rather than steroids alone, especially when weakness is severe or function-threatening. IVIG is particularly useful because it can produce a faster clinical response in severe necrotizing myopathy and can be steroid-sparing.

So, the escalation reflected the combination of **bulbar involvement, severe proximal weakness, ongoing CK elevation, and biopsy-confirmed necrotizing autoimmune myopathy**, all of which made corticosteroids alone insufficient.

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient had elevated troponin and proBNP levels with cardiac MRI abnormalities; how would you interpret possible cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathy, and how should such findings affect the workup and monitoring plan?

In inflammatory myopathy, a mild elevation of troponin and proBNP should be taken seriously as a possible signal of cardiac involvement, but it is not by itself diagnostic of myocarditis. In this case, the biomarker abnormalities, together with the cardiac MRI showing focal myocardial edema and mild regional hypokinesis without late gadolinium enhancement, suggested myocardial inflammation or an alternative stress-related cardiomyopathy rather than established scar, infarction, or definitive fulminant myocarditis. The fact that the abnormalities later normalized supports a transient inflammatory or stress-mediated process.

The practical point is that cardiac involvement can be clinically silent in myositis, especially in immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, so even modest biomarker elevation warrants structured evaluation rather than dismissal. A reasonable workup includes ECG, transthoracic echocardiography, serial troponin and natriuretic peptide measurements, and cardiac MRI when biomarkers are abnormal or symptoms, arrhythmia, or echo changes are present. In a patient like this, the prior atrial fibrillation further strengthens the need for cardiac surveillance.

Management should be driven by severity and trajectory. If MRI suggests active inflammation, or if there are arrhythmias, heart failure symptoms, or worsening biomarkers, involve cardiology early, monitor rhythm, and follow ventricular function over time. If the findings are mild and the patient improves with treatment of the myopathy, repeat biomarkers and repeat echo or cardiac MRI can confirm resolution, as occurred here. The key is to treat these findings as potentially real cardiac involvement until proven otherwise, while recognizing that transient edema without scar may resolve with control of the underlying inflammatory myopathy.

### [Management] What aspects of this patient’s recovery suggest a drug-triggered immune-mediated necrotising myopathy rather than idiopathic IMNM, and how did that influence the duration of immunosuppression?

Several features of her recovery favor a **drug-triggered immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy** rather than idiopathic IMNM.

First, the illness had a **clear temporal relationship** to semaglutide exposure: her weakness and CK rise began after a **single dose**, then progressed over months despite stopping the drug. Second, once she received **withdrawal of the suspected trigger plus a brief course of immunosuppression**—pulse methylprednisolone, short-course prednisone, and one inpatient course of IVIG—she had a **sustained and relatively rapid response**: CK normalized, strength improved steadily, and she returned to baseline function. Third, she remained well **off immunosuppression for many months** without relapse. That pattern is less typical of idiopathic IMNM, which often requires **prolonged, combination immunosuppression** and frequently relapses when steroids are tapered.

That recovery pattern influenced management directly: because she improved after stopping semaglutide and after a short induction regimen, her prednisone was **weaned rapidly over about 2 months**, she did **not** receive maintenance IVIG, and she was ultimately able to remain **off immunosuppression** for 7–8 months while continuing to avoid GLP-1 receptor agonists. In other words, the sustained remission after trigger removal supported a **limited course of treatment** rather than the longer-term immunosuppression usually needed for idiopathic IMNM.

### [Clinical Pearls] Using a pharmacovigilance framework such as the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria, what elements support a ‘probable’ causal association between semaglutide and this patient’s myopathy, and what key limitations prevent a definitive conclusion?

Using the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre framework, this case meets **“probable”** rather than “certain” causality because several elements fit a drug-related adverse event, but the evidence is not definitive.

The features supporting a **probable causal association** are:

1. **Reasonable temporal relationship**: her symptoms began after semaglutide exposure, with a striking initial intolerance after the first dose and then progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and CK rise over the ensuing months.
2. **Plausible clinical syndrome**: the biopsy showed **necrotizing myopathy with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex staining**, consistent with IMNM rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.
3. **Lack of a better alternative explanation**: she had no statin exposure, no clear toxin or supplement trigger, negative connective-tissue-disease serologies, and no clear evidence of another myositis subtype or active malignancy in the reported evaluation.
4. **Clinically reasonable response after withdrawal**: semaglutide was not continued, and she improved with immunosuppression and IVIG, with normalization of CK and recovery of baseline function while avoiding GLP-1 receptor agonists.
5. **Positive dechallenge in the broad sense**: the course was compatible with removal of the suspected agent, although improvement was also confounded by treatment of the myopathy itself.

The **key limitations** preventing a definitive conclusion are equally important:

- **No pre-semaglutide CK or muscle evaluation**, so a smoldering autoimmune myopathy before exposure cannot be excluded.
- **Single-case observation**: there is no rechallenge, no dose-response relationship, and no comparator, so causality cannot be proven.
- **Rechallenge would be unethical** given the severity of the presentation.
- **Potential confounding by treatment**: recovery occurred after corticosteroids and IVIG, so improvement cannot be attributed to semaglutide withdrawal alone.
- **Seronegative IMNM remains possible**: negative HMGCR/SRP and other myositis antibodies do not rule out IMNM, and a subset of cases are seronegative.
- **Other contributors cannot be fully excluded**, including the acute systemic illness, deconditioning, and the possibility of an unrelated autoimmune process.

So, in pharmacovigilance terms, this is a **reasonable, temporally associated, dechallenge-supported signal**, but not proof of causation.
