Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Clinical Presentation and Early Course: From Semaglutide Exposure to Progressive Weakness

This patient was a woman in her 60s who had been functionally independent before the onset of illness. Her medical history included obesity, hepatosteatosis/non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote history of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. The pre-existing liver disease is relevant because she had already been evaluated for chronic transaminase elevation and had biopsy-proven steatohepatitis before any neuromuscular symptoms appeared. In that setting, semaglutide was started for weight loss and metabolic benefit, a common contemporary indication for glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in patients with obesity and fatty liver disease.

The first clinical event after semaglutide exposure was an acute intolerance-like syndrome. Within hours of her first and only 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose, she developed nausea and vomiting that progressed to poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury, and severe metabolic acidosis requiring hospitalization. She improved with supportive care and hydration, suggesting that this initial episode was dominated by gastrointestinal toxicity and hypovolaemia rather than a primary muscle disorder. Although creatine kinase was checked during that admission and was mildly elevated at 337 U/L, this degree of elevation is nonspecific and far below what would usually be expected in established rhabdomyolysis. Mild CK elevation can occur with many stress states, dehydration, or limited muscle injury, so at that stage the laboratory value alone did not establish a necrotising myopathy.

The more important clue was the subsequent evolution over the next four months. Rather than recovering fully after the acute drug reaction, she developed steadily progressive proximal weakness. By the time she re-presented, she could no longer stand or sit upright without assistance, and she had developed dysphagia to both liquids and solids as well as dysphonia. These are red-flag neuromuscular symptoms that are difficult to explain by deconditioning alone. Deconditioning typically causes generalized weakness and reduced endurance, but it does not usually produce a marked proximal pattern with bulbar involvement and a substantial CK rise. Dysphagia and dysphonia in particular raise concern for an inflammatory myopathy, a necrotising autoimmune process, or another disorder affecting skeletal muscle rather than simple fatigue after hospitalization.

The medication timeline was central to the diagnostic reasoning. The weakness began after semaglutide exposure and progressed despite the drug having been discontinued after the single dose, which argued against a transient adverse effect and suggested an ongoing pathologic process. A careful exposure history also helped narrow the differential. She denied prior statin use, which is important because statins are the classic medication linked to immune-mediated necrotising myopathy through anti-HMGCR autoimmunity. She also denied use of red yeast rice, mushrooms, or other supplements sometimes associated with myotoxicity, as well as alcohol or illicit drugs. There was no family history of myopathy and no history of excessive exertion that might have explained the CK rise or weakness.

Taken together, the early course moved the case away from isolated semaglutide intolerance or postoperative deconditioning and toward a suspected drug-associated myopathy. The combination of persistent and progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms, and rising CK months after a single exposure created concern for an inflammatory or necrotising muscle process, setting the stage for the later laboratory, imaging, and biopsy work-up.

## Essential Background: Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy and Drug-Associated Muscle Injury

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct inflammatory myopathy defined by rapidly progressive muscle weakness, very high creatine kinase (CK) levels, and muscle biopsy showing prominent myofibre necrosis and regeneration with relatively little lymphocytic inflammation. Clinically, patients usually present with severe symmetric proximal weakness affecting the hips and shoulders, and in more advanced cases they may develop dysphagia, dysphonia, or respiratory involvement. The course is often aggressive, with functional decline over weeks to months rather than years. Because serum CK can be markedly elevated, IMNM is frequently mistaken at first for toxic muscle injury or rhabdomyolysis, especially when symptoms follow a medication exposure.

The pathology of IMNM helps separate it from other muscle disorders. Muscle biopsy typically shows scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage-predominant myophagocytosis, and only sparse lymphocytic infiltrates. This pattern reflects immune-mediated muscle fibre destruction rather than a purely inflammatory lymphocytic myositis. In practice, the biopsy findings are especially helpful in seronegative patients or in cases where a medication trigger is suspected. The presence of complement deposition on injured fibres may further support an immune process, although biopsy interpretation must be integrated with the clinical picture.

Two autoantibodies are most strongly associated with IMNM: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP). Anti-HMGCR myopathy is classically linked to statin exposure, and anti-SRP disease often presents with severe weakness and high CK. Importantly, however, a substantial minority of patients with IMNM are seronegative. Seronegativity does not exclude the diagnosis; instead, it increases diagnostic uncertainty and raises the possibility that additional autoantigens or triggers have not yet been identified. In a patient with a convincing clinical phenotype and compatible biopsy, a negative myositis panel does not rule out IMNM.

The association between statins and anti-HMGCR myopathy shaped the modern understanding of drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy. Early cases were initially labeled as toxic statin myopathy or polymyositis because the muscle symptoms and CK elevation persisted even after statin discontinuation. Over time, the key clue was that unlike ordinary toxic myopathy, the weakness and CK elevation did not promptly resolve when the drug was stopped; instead, many patients improved only after corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), or other immunosuppressive therapy. This pattern supported the concept that the medication had acted as an immune trigger rather than simply causing direct muscle toxicity. That distinction is central when evaluating a possible drug-related myopathy in this case report.

This case also requires differentiation of IMNM from direct toxic myopathy and rhabdomyolysis. Toxic myopathy typically improves after withdrawal of the offending agent, and CK usually falls relatively quickly as muscle injury stops. Rhabdomyolysis presents with acute muscle breakdown, often with very high CK, myalgias, weakness, dark urine, and sometimes acute kidney injury due to myoglobin-induced renal injury. The CK trajectory is usually abrupt and then improves over days once hydration and removal of the cause occur. By contrast, IMNM tends to persist or progress after the exposure has ended, with ongoing proximal weakness and sustained CK elevation over weeks to months. A response to immunosuppression, rather than simple supportive care, further supports an autoimmune process. In this case, the delayed and progressive neuromuscular decline after the initial drug exposure, along with biopsy findings and improvement with corticosteroids and IVIG, fit IMNM better than self-limited rhabdomyolysis.

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are widely used for type 2 diabetes and obesity. Their common adverse effects are gastrointestinal, especially nausea, vomiting, and reduced appetite. More recently, concern has emerged about loss of lean body mass and possible muscle injury in some patients receiving these agents. The biologic basis for a muscle-related adverse effect is not yet clear, and most patients do not develop clinically significant myopathy. Nonetheless, because these drugs alter metabolic signaling and can produce rapid changes in body composition, they are clinically relevant when unexplained muscle symptoms or CK elevation arise after exposure.

A single dose can still plausibly act as an immune trigger if it initiates a cascade in a predisposed host. Autoimmune diseases often do not require ongoing exposure to progress once immune activation has begun. In suspected drug-triggered IMNM, the temporal relationship is important, but so is the pattern of disease after withdrawal. Persistence of weakness and CK elevation after the medication has been stopped suggests that the initial exposure may have triggered an autoimmune process that then became self-sustaining. That concept is what makes the semaglutide association in this case biologically plausible even though the patient received only one dose.

For clinicians, the main lesson is that unusual medication associations should not be dismissed simply because they are rare or previously unreported. When a patient develops progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and marked CK elevation after a drug exposure, the differential should include immune-mediated myopathy, not just toxic injury. Recognizing IMNM early matters because treatment differs from rhabdomyolysis: supportive care alone may be insufficient, and timely immunosuppression can be needed to halt progression and restore function.

## Diagnostic Workup: Laboratory, Imaging, and Biopsy Evidence

The diagnostic evaluation in this case was built around three tasks: confirming that the patient had an active myopathic process, determining whether it fit immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), and excluding other common mimics such as toxic rhabdomyolysis, endocrine myopathy, systemic connective tissue disease, malignancy-associated myositis, or primary liver disease. Because IMNM can present with progressive proximal weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), but may also be seronegative, the interpretation of each test depended on the overall pattern rather than any single abnormality.

The CK trend was especially informative. Her admission CK at the tertiary center was already elevated at 1237 U/L, and the outside-hospital peak of 3992 U/L showed that muscle injury had increased substantially over time. This degree of elevation is compatible with inflammatory myopathy and is much more than would be expected from simple deconditioning. The fact that CK rose progressively over four months, rather than falling quickly after the initial semaglutide exposure, argued against a brief toxic or ischemic rhabdomyolysis and supported an ongoing immune process. In IMNM, CK is often markedly elevated and may remain high until immunosuppression is started and the offending trigger is removed.

Her AST and ALT were also elevated, which can be confusing in a patient with both muscle disease and known fatty liver. Aminotransferases are not liver-specific: AST, and to a lesser extent ALT, can rise from skeletal muscle injury. In inflammatory myopathies, transaminase elevations may therefore mirror CK. In this case, however, the interpretation was complicated by prior biopsy-proven nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). That chronic hepatic diagnosis made it important not to over-attribute the enzyme abnormalities to muscle alone. The repeat liver biopsy was therefore helpful because it again showed steatosis without significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis, supporting chronic hepatosteatosis as the primary hepatic explanation while leaving muscle injury as the likely contributor to the acute enzyme rise.

The basic screening studies helped exclude common alternative explanations and assess severity. Thyroid-stimulating hormone was normal, making hypothyroid myopathy unlikely. Renal function was normal at the time of the myopathy workup, which suggested that the CK elevation was not causing clinically significant pigment nephropathy. Blood counts and complement levels were unremarkable, which lowered concern for a systemic lupus-like or vasculitic process. ESR and C-reactive protein were mildly elevated, consistent with inflammation but not specific for a particular autoimmune syndrome. The modest inflammatory marker elevation fits with IMNM, which can have variable systemic inflammatory findings and often presents more with weakness and CK elevation than with overt constitutional symptoms.

A broad autoantibody evaluation was performed because the differential diagnosis of inflammatory myopathy includes connective tissue disease overlap syndromes and several myositis subtypes. Her serologies for ANA, SSA, Sm, RNP, dsDNA, ANCA, and antimitochondrial antibody were negative, and the myositis panel was negative for the two antibodies most strongly associated with IMNM, anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP, as well as other myositis-specific antibodies. Negative HMGCR and SRP testing did not exclude IMNM. A substantial minority of patients with IMNM are seronegative, and in such patients the diagnosis relies more heavily on the clinicopathologic pattern. In practical terms, a negative myositis panel reduces the likelihood of a defined autoantibody-associated subtype, but it does not rule out immune-mediated disease.

Her isolated low-titer SS-B positivity required cautious interpretation. An isolated SS-B result with negative ANA and SSA is often nonspecific and is not, by itself, sufficient to diagnose Sjögren syndrome or another connective tissue disease. In this case, the absence of sicca symptoms, rash, arthritis, cytopenias, hypocomplementemia, or other organ involvement made a systemic connective tissue disease less likely. Thus, the low-titer SS-B did not outweigh the broader negative autoimmune workup or the muscle-biopsy findings.

Imaging supported the suspicion of an inflammatory myopathy and helped assess for extra-muscular disease. MRI of the thighs showed symmetric muscle edema, particularly in the proximal quadriceps, which is a typical pattern in active inflammatory myopathy. Muscle edema on MRI reflects increased water content from inflammation and injury and is useful because it can identify affected muscle for biopsy and distinguish active disease from chronic fatty replacement or simple atrophy. The bilateral, symmetric distribution favored a systemic myopathic process rather than focal injury or denervation.

Because the patient had an elevated troponin and markedly elevated BNP, cardiac involvement also had to be considered. Troponin elevation in a myositis patient may reflect myocardial inflammation, skeletal muscle cross-reactivity depending on the assay, or demand ischemia in a severely ill patient. Echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, while cardiac MRI demonstrated myocardial edema with mild regional hypokinesis but no definite scar or infarction. This pattern was concerning for possible early myocarditis or stress-related myocardial dysfunction, but it was not definitive for a severe primary cardiac process. The cardiac workup was important because inflammatory myopathies can involve the heart, and because identifying myocardial inflammation would strengthen the impression of a systemic immune-mediated syndrome.

Additional imaging was used to exclude associated systemic conditions that can accompany inflammatory myopathy. High-resolution CT of the chest showed no interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy, which lowered concern for antisynthetase syndrome or an occult thoracic malignancy. Abdominal MRI again showed steatotic hepatomegaly without lymphadenopathy. This was relevant because malignancy-associated myositis is an important consideration in new-onset inflammatory myopathy, particularly in older adults. Although imaging does not eliminate all cancer risk, the absence of suspicious adenopathy or other masses made a paraneoplastic explanation less likely in this case.

The muscle biopsy was the निर्णative test. It showed necrotic and regenerating fibers, myophagocytosis with CD68-positive macrophages, membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, and only sparse CD3-positive lymphocytes. This pattern is classic for IMNM: prominent myofiber necrosis and regeneration with relatively little lymphocytic inflammation. The presence of macrophage-mediated cleanup of necrotic fibers and complement deposition supports an immune-mediated process rather than simple toxic injury. By contrast, many other inflammatory myopathies show denser lymphocytic infiltrates or different tissue patterns, and toxic rhabdomyolysis typically lacks this characteristic immune signature.

Biopsy was especially pivotal because the patient was seronegative. When the expected autoantibodies are absent, the diagnosis of IMNM depends on clinicopathologic correlation: progressive proximal weakness, substantial CK elevation, supportive MRI edema, exclusion of endocrine and systemic mimics, and a biopsy showing necrotising myopathy with minimal lymphocytic inflammation. In this case, the biopsy supplied the pathologic proof that allowed clinicians to move beyond a nonspecific “myopathy” label and treat as IMNM.

## Differential Diagnosis and Causal Reasoning: Why Semaglutide-Associated IMNM Was Favored

The key diagnostic problem in this case was to explain a subacute syndrome of progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms, and rising creatine kinase (CK) after a single semaglutide exposure. In a patient with difficulty rising or sitting upright, dysphagia, dysphonia, and CK elevation, the differential is broad and includes toxic or inflammatory myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, endocrine myopathy, inherited muscle disease, and less commonly neuromuscular junction disorders or motor neuron disease. The CK level and the distribution of weakness made a primary muscle process most likely. Bulbar involvement raised concern for a severe myopathy rather than a purely deconditioning-related problem, and the absence of sensory loss or focal neurologic deficits argued against a central nervous system cause.

A major distinction was between rhabdomyolysis and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Rhabdomyolysis is usually an acute toxic or metabolic muscle injury, often with abrupt CK elevation, myalgias, pigmenturia, and improvement after stopping the trigger and giving supportive care. That pattern fit poorly here. Although the patient had an early CK elevation during her initial semaglutide-associated illness, it was modest and did not explain the much larger rise to nearly 4000 U/L four months later. More importantly, her weakness worsened progressively while she was no longer taking semaglutide, which is not the usual time course for simple toxic rhabdomyolysis. In contrast, IMNM typically causes progressive proximal weakness, marked CK elevation, and persistent disease activity unless the autoimmune process is treated. The muscle biopsy, showing necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition, supported an inflammatory necrotising process rather than a transient toxic injury.

The clinicians also had to distinguish a drug-induced toxic myopathy from a drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy. This distinction matters because toxic myopathy generally improves after withdrawal of the drug alone, whereas autoimmune necrotising myopathy often requires immunosuppression even after the offending agent is stopped. In this case, the temporal relationship suggested semaglutide as a possible trigger, but the delayed progression and the clear response to corticosteroids plus IVIG pointed toward an immune-mediated process. Her rapid biochemical improvement after immunotherapy, together with sustained recovery after semaglutide avoidance, was more compatible with a drug-triggered IMNM than with direct myotoxicity.

Several other inflammatory myopathies were considered less likely. Polymyositis is now an uncommon and less specific diagnosis, and it usually lacks the necrotising pathology and autoantibody associations seen in this case. Dermatomyositis was unlikely because she had no rash, no classic skin findings, and no biopsy pattern typical of perifascicular injury. Overlap myositis was also less favored because she lacked clinical features of a connective tissue disease such as arthritis, Raynaud phenomenon, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, interstitial lung disease, or systemic sclerosis features, and her serologic work-up was broadly negative. Inclusion body myositis is generally a more chronic, slowly progressive disorder in older adults, often with distal weakness and poor response to immunotherapy; that phenotype did not match this patient’s relatively rapid decline and treatment responsiveness. Endocrine myopathy from hypothyroidism was excluded by a normal TSH, and inherited myopathy was unlikely given the late onset, previously normal function, and absence of a relevant family history.

The transaminase elevation also required interpretation. AST and ALT can rise in muscle disease, so elevated liver enzymes may falsely suggest hepatic injury. In this patient, however, the long-standing transaminitis predated the myopathy by years and was already explained by biopsy-proven nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). The repeat liver biopsy again showed steatosis with only mild progression of NAFLD, and the enzymes remained borderline abnormal even after CK normalized. This made occult myositis a less likely explanation for the chronic transaminitis and supported liver disease as the more plausible source.

Cardiac abnormalities added complexity but did not overturn the overall diagnosis. Mild troponin and BNP elevation, along with cardiac MRI showing myocardial oedema without definite scar, raised possibilities such as myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or nonspecific inflammatory involvement. These findings were not definitive enough to establish a separate primary cardiac disease, and they later resolved. In a patient with systemic muscle inflammation and severe physiologic stress, transient cardiac involvement can occur, but the heart findings were interpreted cautiously and were not the main driver of the diagnosis.

The strongest argument for semaglutide-associated IMNM was the totality of evidence rather than any single test. The timing was suggestive, because symptoms began after semaglutide exposure. Alternative explanations were progressively less convincing as the case evolved. The biopsy showed necrotising myopathy with inflammatory features. The patient improved with withdrawal of semaglutide, high-dose corticosteroids, and a single course of IVIG, and then remained well off immunosuppression. This pattern fits a probable drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy. At the same time, the authors correctly acknowledged uncertainty: there was no pre-semaglutide CK, so a smouldering pre-existing IMNM cannot be fully excluded. That limitation is important because causality in adverse drug reactions is often inferred, not proven.

The report applies the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality framework, which weighs temporal association, alternative causes, response to drug withdrawal, and rechallenge. Here, the relationship was judged “probable” because the timing was reasonable, other causes were not compelling, the patient improved after stopping the drug and receiving treatment, and rechallenge would have been unsafe. Rechallenge is generally unethical when a medication is suspected of causing a severe immune-mediated reaction, especially one that caused disabling weakness and required hospitalization. Thus, the case supports semaglutide as a likely trigger of IMNM, while appropriately stopping short of claiming definitive proof.

## Management, Outcome, and Clinical Lessons for Practice

This patient’s presentation required urgent treatment because she had several features of severe myopathy: rapidly progressive proximal weakness, inability to sit or stand without assistance, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK). In immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), these findings raise concern for ongoing immune injury to skeletal muscle and, when swallowing is involved, for aspiration risk and nutritional compromise. In practice, severe functional decline and bulbar symptoms justify prompt immunosuppression even before every diagnostic result is finalized, especially when the clinical picture and muscle biopsy strongly support IMNM.

She was treated first with pulse-dose intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by high-dose oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily. This approach is typical for severe inflammatory myopathy because corticosteroids rapidly suppress immune-mediated muscle injury. However, steroid monotherapy is often insufficient in IMNM, particularly in patients with profound weakness or active progression. For that reason, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added at a total dose of 2 g/kg over 4 days. IVIG is commonly used as an early adjunct in severe IMNM because it can produce a faster clinical response than waiting for longer-term steroid effect alone, and it may be especially useful when there is concern for bulbar involvement or when a more aggressive initial response is needed.

Treatment response was monitored using several complementary markers: CK trajectory, bedside strength, swallowing function, and overall mobility. Her CK fell markedly over about 2 weeks, and her functional status improved in parallel. This is an important principle in inflammatory myopathy: biochemical improvement should be interpreted alongside clinical recovery, because CK alone does not fully reflect disability, and strength may lag behind laboratory improvement. In her case, the combination of falling CK, improving proximal strength, and eventual recovery of swallowing ability supported true therapeutic response rather than a transient fluctuation.

The patient’s course also illustrates the need to balance immunosuppressive efficacy against infection risk and comorbidity burden. After discharge, she was readmitted with septic shock from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, a complication that is highly relevant in someone recently treated with high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG and still recovering from severe illness. Her CK remained normal during that admission, indicating that the infection did not coincide with renewed muscle injury. This episode underscores the importance of close follow-up, vigilance for infectious complications, and a low threshold to reassess patients who deteriorate while receiving immunosuppression.

Because she improved quickly and maintained normal CK levels, prednisone was tapered relatively rapidly over about 2 months. That decision was supported by her sustained clinical recovery and the desire to minimize steroid toxicity, especially in a patient with obesity, prior deconditioning, and infection risk. She did not receive ongoing IVIG or additional steroid-sparing agents such as methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, or rituximab. In a typical chronic idiopathic IMNM course, these agents are often needed to maintain remission, but in this case they were deferred because she had already regained function, remained biochemically stable off semaglutide, and had responded to a short course of treatment. Her improvement suggested that continued aggressive long-term immunosuppression was not necessary.

The outcome was favorable: CK normalized, she returned to her baseline functional status, and she was able to discontinue immunosuppression. Her cardiac imaging abnormalities also resolved on follow-up, arguing that the earlier myocardial findings were reversible and possibly related to a transient inflammatory or stress-related process rather than permanent myocardial injury. The overall pattern—single apparent medication exposure, delayed onset of progressive muscle weakness, biopsy-confirmed necrotising myopathy, response to short-course immunosuppression, and sustained recovery after withdrawal of the suspected trigger—favored a drug-triggered, self-limited autoimmune process rather than classic chronic idiopathic IMNM.

Because the temporal association with semaglutide was strong and no alternative cause was identified, she was counseled to avoid semaglutide permanently and, more broadly, to avoid GLP-1 receptor agonists unless future evidence clarifies the safety of rechallenge within the class. This counseling is important even though causality cannot be proven from a single case. When a severe adverse event is plausibly medication-related, the practical standard is to avoid the suspected agent and document the reaction clearly.

The broader lesson from this report is a pharmacovigilance one. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become more widely used, clinicians should remain alert to rare but serious adverse events that may not appear during preapproval trials. New cases of unexplained weakness after starting semaglutide or another GLP-1 agent should prompt evaluation for CK elevation, neuromuscular weakness, bulbar symptoms, and, when indicated, referral for neurology/rheumatology assessment and muscle biopsy. Importantly, persistent weakness or rising CK after drug discontinuation should not be dismissed as simple “toxic myopathy,” because an autoimmune myopathy may require immunosuppression.

For clinicians, the practical take-home points are straightforward. Severe weakness with dysphagia and CK elevation after a medication exposure should be treated as potentially urgent. In suspected IMNM, early steroids and consideration of IVIG are appropriate when disease is severe. Response should be tracked with both labs and function, and immunosuppression should be tapered only after sustained improvement. Finally, rare suspected adverse drug reactions should be reported, because individual cases may provide the first signal of a new drug-associated autoimmune syndrome.
