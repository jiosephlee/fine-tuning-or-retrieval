# When “Deconditioning” Isn’t: Progressive Proximal Weakness After GLP-1 Agonist Exposure

## The case that should make us pause

This patient’s story is a great reminder that “she’s just deconditioned” can be a dangerously sticky diagnosis.

A woman in her 60s received a single semaglutide dose, developed severe gastrointestinal intolerance with AKI and acidosis, and was discharged after a short hospitalization with a CK of only 337 U/L. Reasonable enough to think she was recovering from an acute medication reaction plus critical illness deconditioning.

But then the plot shifted: over the next 4 months she developed progressive proximal weakness, then bulbar symptoms — dysphagia and dysphonia — and eventually could no longer stand or sit upright without assistance. At that point, this was no longer a benign post-hospital recovery story. Her CK had climbed to nearly 4,000 U/L, and muscle biopsy ultimately showed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

That diagnostic pivot is the lesson here.

## Why this is clinically important

We are good at attributing weakness after hospitalization to deconditioning, especially when there has been an ICU stay, AKI, poor intake, or a prolonged recovery. And often that’s true.

But deconditioning does not usually give you:

- Progressive proximal weakness over weeks to months
- Dysphagia or dysphonia
- A rising CK trend, especially into the thousands
- Biopsy evidence of necrotising myopathy
- A response to immunosuppression

Those are inflammatory myopathy red flags, not just “slow bounce-back” features.

## The diagnostic trap: anchoring after an initial medication reaction

The semaglutide exposure matters here because it can create a perfectly plausible early narrative:

- nausea/vomiting
- volume depletion
- AKI
- metabolic derangements
- mild CK elevation

That opening act can make later weakness seem like a downstream consequence of acute illness rather than a separate neuromuscular process. But the timeline matters. In this case, the patient worsened after the acute GI illness resolved, not during the acute dehydration phase.

That should make us ask: is this a persistent toxic syndrome, or a new immune-mediated disease unmasked around the same time?

This case report argues for the latter.

## Red flags that should trigger a rethink

When a patient is said to be “deconditioned,” look hard for these clues that the story doesn’t fit:

- **Loss of independent posture or transfers**
  - Not just “weak,” but unable to sit or stand without support
- **Bulbar involvement**
  - Dysphagia, dysphonia, aspiration concern
- **Objective muscle injury**
  - CK trending upward, especially persistent elevation
- **Proximal-predominant weakness**
  - Hip and shoulder girdle weakness out of proportion to generalized fatigue
- **Persistence despite resolution of the acute illness**
  - Weakness continues after hydration, renal recovery, and GI symptoms improve

In this case, the combination of progressive proximal weakness plus dysphagia/dysphonia plus marked CK elevation should push inflammatory myopathy high on the list.

## What made this IMNM and not rhabdomyolysis?

That distinction matters.

With rhabdomyolysis, you usually expect:

- a more acute insult
- a marked CK spike
- improvement after stopping the trigger and providing supportive care

Here, the course was different. The CK rose over 4 months after a single semaglutide dose, and the weakness continued to progress long after the GI toxicity resolved. That pattern is much more compatible with an autoimmune myopathy than a direct toxic muscle injury.

Muscle biopsy showed:

- necrotic and regenerating fibers
- myophagocytosis
- macrophage-rich inflammatory response
- membrane attack complex deposition

That is classic territory for IMNM.

## Why this case is especially interesting

GLP-1 receptor agonists are everywhere now, and semaglutide in particular has become a familiar part of outpatient metabolic care. Most clinicians think about GI side effects, gallbladder disease, or weight loss complications. Myositis is not where most of us start.

This report is notable because it describes what may be the first case of IMNM probably associated with a GLP-1 receptor agonist. Causality is not proven, but the temporal relationship, lack of other clear triggers, persistent decline off drug, biopsy findings, and improvement with immunosuppression make the association hard to ignore.

There are a few important caveats:

- She had no prior CK baseline, so a smoldering autoimmune process before semaglutide cannot be fully excluded.
- Her myositis serologies were negative, including HMGCR and SRP.
- She had a low-titer isolated SS-B positivity, but no convincing connective tissue disease phenotype.
- Cardiac findings were equivocal and later normalized.

So this is not a slam dunk mechanistic proof. It is, however, a strong pharmacovigilance signal.

## Practical bedside takeaways

The real value of this case is not just in the rare diagnosis — it’s in the diagnostic discipline it demands.

If a patient recently exposed to a GLP-1 agonist re-presents with “weakness,” don’t stop at deconditioning if the story is evolving. Recheck the basics, and think neuromuscular when the phenotype fits.

A few high-yield moves:

- Reassess **pattern of weakness**
  - proximal > distal?
  - neck flexors?
  - bulbar symptoms?
- Trend **CK**
  - a single mildly elevated CK early on does not exclude later inflammatory myopathy
- Look for **transaminase elevation** that may actually be muscle-derived
- Don’t be reassured by a benign initial explanation if the patient is **getting worse**
- Escalate to MRI, EMG, serologies, and biopsy when suspicion remains high

## Take-home points

- **Deconditioning is a diagnosis of pattern and trajectory, not just a label applied after hospitalization.**
- **Bulbar symptoms and loss of independent sitting/standing are major red flags for inflammatory myopathy.**
- **A rising CK over weeks to months is not consistent with simple post-hospital weakness.**
- **IMNM can be seronegative, so negative HMGCR/SRP testing does not rule it out.**
- **In a patient worsening after semaglutide exposure, don’t assume all subsequent weakness is part of the initial medication intolerance.**
- **When the story doesn’t fit, revisit the diagnosis early — because the treatment path changes completely.**

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder to keep a wide differential when weakness progresses after an apparent medication reaction. Semaglutide may have been the trigger, the coincidence, or the unmasking event — but the clinical lesson is broader: if weakness evolves into proximal failure with dysphagia, dysphonia, and rising CK, stop calling it deconditioning and start thinking inflammatory myopathy.



# Rhabdo or IMNM? Sorting Out CK Elevation After a New Metabolic Drug

## Why this case matters

A patient takes one dose of semaglutide, gets hit hard with GI symptoms and acute kidney injury, and then months later shows up with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK in the thousands.

That’s the kind of timeline that should make you stop and ask: is this just toxic rhabdomyolysis, or have we uncovered an autoimmune myopathy?

This case is interesting because the answer matters. Acute drug-induced rhabdomyolysis usually improves when you stop the offending agent and support the kidneys. Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, on the other hand, can keep advancing long after the drug is gone and often needs immunosuppression to turn the corner. In this report, the patient’s delayed, progressive course, biopsy findings, and response to steroids plus IVIG fit much better with IMNM than with direct muscle toxicity.

## The clinical pattern: tempo is the clue

The patient’s first semaglutide-related presentation sounded very much like medication intolerance with systemic consequences: nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis within 2 days of the first and only dose. Her CK at that point was only 337 U/L.

That initial episode does not scream rhabdomyolysis. Then comes the key twist: after discharge, she doesn’t rapidly recover. Instead, she gradually worsens over 4 months with progressive weakness and bulbar symptoms, eventually becoming unable to sit or stand without help and developing dysphagia to both liquids and solids. By then, CK had risen to 3992 U/L.

That delayed, progressive decline is the opposite of what you’d expect from a straightforward toxic myopathy.

### Think about tempo

- **Acute toxic rhabdomyolysis**
  - Usually abrupt
  - CK spikes early and often dramatically
  - Improves after drug withdrawal and hydration
  - Weakness is often less persistent than the lab abnormality

- **IMNM**
  - Often subacute or progressive over weeks to months
  - Proximal weakness is prominent
  - CK remains elevated or rises over time
  - Does not reliably improve with drug cessation alone
  - Often needs immunosuppression

## CK kinetics: the trajectory tells a story

CK is not just a number; it’s a timeline.

In classic rhabdomyolysis, CK rises sharply and then falls fairly quickly once the insult stops. In this case, the CK did the opposite of what you’d want to see if semaglutide had simply caused a direct toxic muscle injury:

- CK 337 U/L during the initial GI/AKI admission
- CK 3992 U/L four months later
- Marked CK improvement only after steroids and IVIG

That delayed 12-fold increase over months argues against a one-and-done toxic event. It suggests an ongoing immune process.

A useful bedside pearl: **if CK stays elevated or climbs after the suspected drug has been stopped, don’t anchor on rhabdo.** Keep IMNM and other inflammatory myopathies on the table.

## What makes IMNM different?

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy is a distinct inflammatory myopathy characterized by:

- Severe proximal muscle weakness
- High CK
- Necrotic and regenerating myofibres on biopsy
- Relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation compared with other inflammatory myopathies

Commonly associated autoantibodies include:

- Anti-HMGCR
- Anti-SRP

But importantly, a substantial subset of patients are seronegative, which makes diagnosis harder and reminds us that not every autoimmune myopathy announces itself with a positive panel.

In this case, the myositis panel was negative, including HMGCR and SRP, but the muscle biopsy still showed the pattern expected in IMNM.

## The biopsy matters more than the antibody panel

The muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis showed:

- Dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres
- Myophagocytosis
- Macrophage staining with CD68
- Membrane attack complex positivity on necrotic and non-necrotic fibres
- Rare CD3-positive cells and perivascular inflammatory cells

That is not the picture of simple toxic myopathy alone. It supports an inflammatory necrotising process, and in the clinical context, IMNM becomes the leading diagnosis.

This is the lesson: **when the antibody panel is negative but the story is right, biopsy can still clinch the diagnosis.**

## Why this is not just rhabdomyolysis

The authors make a strong case that this looks more like IMNM than direct drug toxicity.

A few reasons:

### 1. The course was too slow for toxic rhabdo
Direct muscle toxicity usually declares itself early and improves after stopping the agent. Here, weakness progressed over 4 months.

### 2. The CK trajectory was wrong for a toxic hit
A CK of 337 U/L at the initial admission is modest. The later rise to nearly 4000 U/L months later suggests a continuing process rather than a single acute injury.

### 3. The weakness was clinically convincing
This was not just a lab abnormality. She developed marked proximal weakness plus dysphagia and dysphonia.

### 4. Biopsy supported necrotising autoimmune myopathy
The pathology was consistent with IMNM.

### 5. She improved with immunosuppression
She received pulse methylprednisolone followed by prednisone and IVIG, with improvement in CK and function. She later came off immunosuppression and returned to baseline.

That combination — persistent decline off drug, biopsy-confirmed necrotising myopathy, and response to immunotherapy — is what makes the case feel autoimmune rather than toxic.

## Where GLP-1 drugs fit in

GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide are widely used for obesity and diabetes. Most clinicians think first of nausea, vomiting, and delayed gastric emptying, not myositis.

But the literature is starting to evolve. There are reports of muscle-related injury with the class, including rhabdomyolysis-like presentations with tirzepatide. Those cases tend to look like conventional rhabdo: acute CK elevation, improvement with stopping the drug, and supportive care.

This case is different. The delayed, progressive course and the need for steroids/IVIG point away from direct toxicity and toward a possible immune-mediated mechanism.

That doesn’t prove causality — and the report is appropriately cautious about that — but it should broaden your differential when a patient on a GLP-1 agent develops unexplained weakness and CK elevation.

## Practical approach at the bedside

When you see CK elevation after a new metabolic drug, resist the urge to collapse everything into “rhabdo.”

Ask:

- Did symptoms start abruptly or evolve over weeks to months?
- Is the patient weak, or just biochemically abnormal?
- Are there bulbar symptoms like dysphagia or dysphonia?
- Is CK falling after stopping the medication, or persisting/rising?
- Is there renal injury consistent with rhabdo, or a separate systemic process?
- Is there evidence of inflammatory myopathy on MRI or biopsy?

In this case, MRI of the thighs showed symmetric muscular edema, and the biopsy confirmed necrotising myopathy. That’s the workup you need when the clinical course is not behaving like a simple toxic injury.

## A few clinical pearls

- **Rhabdomyolysis is usually fast; IMNM is usually stubborn.**
- **A modest CK early does not exclude later severe myopathy.**
- **Progressive proximal weakness plus dysphagia should push you toward inflammatory myopathy.**
- **A negative myositis panel does not rule out IMNM.**
- **If CK fails to normalize after the drug is stopped, don’t keep calling it toxic myopathy.**
- **Biopsy can be decisive when serologies are unrevealing.**

## Take-home points

- A delayed, progressive course after drug exposure argues against simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- In IMNM, weakness and CK elevation can persist or worsen after the trigger is removed.
- Muscle biopsy showing necrosis, regeneration, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex deposition supports IMNM.
- GLP-1 receptor agonists are not established causes of IMNM, but this case raises a plausible pharmacovigilance signal.
- When the story doesn’t fit rhabdo, think autoimmune myopathy early — because waiting can cost function.

## Bottom line

Semaglutide likely did not cause a one-time toxic muscle hit here. The patient’s months-long progression, bulbar involvement, CK rise after the initial event, biopsy findings, and response to steroids plus IVIG all fit immune-mediated necrotising myopathy much better than rhabdomyolysis.

The larger lesson is simple: **when CK elevation after a new drug doesn’t behave like rhabdo, don’t force the diagnosis.** Reframe the problem, look for inflammatory myopathy, and escalate the workup.



# Seronegative Necrotising Myopathy: What to Do When HMGCR and SRP Are Negative

## The case that should make you pause

A woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia over months, with a CK that climbed into the thousands. Nothing about that trajectory was subtle. What *was* surprising was the serology: her myositis panel was negative for the usual IMNM culprits, including HMGCR and SRP.

That’s the trap here. We tend to anchor on antibody-positive disease because it’s cleaner, easier to name, and easier to feel confident about. But immune-mediated necrotising myopathy does not require a positive panel to be real. In fact, seronegative disease is exactly where the diagnosis gets harder, and where muscle pathology, imaging, and clinical pattern matter most.

In this case, the biopsy did the heavy lifting: necrotic and regenerating fibres, myophagocytosis, macrophage-predominant inflammation, and membrane attack complex deposition. That constellation is very hard to dismiss. She improved with corticosteroids and IVIG, then returned to baseline off immunosuppression. The working question became not just “Is this IMNM?” but “What triggered it?”

## Why seronegative IMNM is such a practical headache

IMNM is a clinicopathologic diagnosis. The classic phenotype is aggressive proximal weakness with marked CK elevation and biopsy evidence of myofibre necrosis out of proportion to lymphocytic inflammation. HMGCR and SRP antibodies account for many cases, but not all. A substantial minority are seronegative, which creates two problems:

1. **Diagnosis becomes less straightforward**
2. **Causal inference becomes messier**

If the antibody panel is negative, clinicians can be tempted to downgrade the diagnosis to “toxic myopathy,” “deconditioning,” or “nonspecific inflammatory myositis.” That’s a mistake when the phenotype and biopsy line up.

In this report, the temporal association with semaglutide raised the possibility of a drug trigger. But because seronegative disease lacks a single biomarker, it’s harder to know whether the medication caused the disease, unmasked pre-existing autoimmunity, or simply happened to precede the presentation.

## Practical work-up when HMGCR and SRP are negative

When you suspect IMNM but the usual antibodies are negative, the goal is not to stop. It’s to widen the lens.

### 1) Confirm the phenotype
Look for the features that should keep myopathy high on the list:

- Symmetric proximal weakness
- Bulbar symptoms: dysphagia, dysphonia
- CK elevation, often substantial
- AST/ALT elevation that may track with muscle injury
- Lack of prominent rash, Raynaud’s, sicca, synovitis, or other connective tissue disease features

In this case, the patient had severe proximal weakness and bulbar involvement, with CK rising from modestly elevated to nearly 4000 U/L.

### 2) Use MRI as a map, not the diagnosis
MRI can help identify active muscle inflammation and choose a biopsy site. In this case, thigh MRI showed mild symmetric muscular edema, especially in proximal quadriceps distribution. That supports an active myopathic process, but MRI is not specific enough to distinguish IMNM from other inflammatory myopathies.

### 3) Don’t over-trust the antibody panel
A negative myositis panel does not rule out IMNM. Panels have real-world limitations:

- Not every pathogenic antibody is known
- Assays vary by lab and method
- Sensitivity is imperfect
- Seronegative disease exists by definition

So a negative HMGCR and SRP result should change your differential, not end it.

### 4) Muscle biopsy becomes decisive
This is where the case really teaches.

Pathologic features supporting IMNM include:

- **Necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres**
- **Myophagocytosis**
- **Macrophage-predominant inflammation**
- **Relative paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates**
- **Membrane attack complex deposition on fibres**

That pattern is exactly why biopsy matters in seronegative cases. In this patient, biopsy established a pathologic diagnosis that serology could not.

### 5) Keep an eye on the “extra-muscular” signals
This case also reminds us to look for cardiac and systemic involvement. She had elevated hs-troponin and proBNP, and cardiac MRI showed myocardial edema with transient abnormalities that later normalized. Whether this represented myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or inflammatory involvement wasn’t fully clear, but it reinforced that the illness was not just simple weakness.

## Interpreting the trigger: why seronegativity complicates causality

This case is especially interesting because symptoms began after a single semaglutide dose, but the course didn’t behave like straightforward toxic rhabdomyolysis. The CK did not spike and rapidly resolve with drug withdrawal. Instead, weakness progressed over months, and CK rose substantially later, which is much more compatible with an immune-mediated process.

That said, seronegativity makes the causal argument less certain. Without HMGCR or SRP, you lose one of the strongest anchors for a mechanistic link. You also have to think broadly about:

- Unrecognized pre-existing autoimmune myopathy
- A drug-triggered immune process
- A coincidental association
- A novel trigger not yet linked to this phenotype

This is the part that matters for pharmacovigilance. When a seronegative IMNM appears after a new drug exposure, the question is not just whether the drug is “known” to cause myopathy. It’s whether the timing, pathology, and clinical course support a probable relationship.

In this report, the authors argue that the pattern fits better with drug-associated IMNM than with direct toxic myopathy. The improvement after immunosuppression and avoidance of semaglutide strengthens that view, but it does not prove causality.

## Treatment: act like it’s IMNM if the biopsy says so

When the phenotype is severe and the biopsy supports IMNM, treatment should not wait for serology to become explanatory.

This patient received:

- IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 5 days
- Oral prednisone 1 mg/kg daily
- IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days

And she improved—biochemically and functionally.

That response is clinically important. True IMNM often needs immunotherapy, and severe bulbar or rapidly progressive weakness should push you toward early treatment rather than watchful waiting. In contrast, a purely toxic myopathy should improve mainly with withdrawal of the offending agent and supportive care.

## Clinical pearls

- Negative HMGCR and SRP do **not** rule out IMNM.
- If weakness is progressive and CK is rising, muscle biopsy matters.
- MRI helps localize active disease, but biopsy establishes the diagnosis.
- Pathology showing necrosis, myophagocytosis, macrophage-predominant inflammation, and MAC deposition is highly supportive of IMNM.
- Seronegative disease makes causal inference harder and should broaden the search for triggers, including medications.
- A course that improves with immunosuppression, rather than just drug withdrawal, argues against simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.

## Take-home points

- **Think IMNM even when the myositis panel is negative.**
- **Biopsy is the tie-breaker in seronegative cases.**
- **MRI is useful for targeting biopsy and confirming active myopathy, not for replacing tissue diagnosis.**
- **Seronegativity should widen your search for triggers, not weaken your suspicion.**
- **If the phenotype is severe, treat early—don’t wait for a perfect serologic answer.**



# Dysphagia, Dysphonia, and Weakness: Treating Severe IMNM Before the Window Closes

## Why this case should make you uncomfortable

A woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia after a single semaglutide dose. By the time she reached tertiary care, she was no longer able to sit or stand unassisted, and her CK had climbed into the thousands. The biopsy later showed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), and she improved with pulse steroids, high-dose prednisone, and IVIG.

The interesting part isn’t just the diagnosis. It’s the management problem: when a patient looks like they’re losing the ability to swallow and breathe before your workup is complete, how hard should you push treatment?

In this case, the team treated first and finished sorting out the details later. That is often the right move.

## Severe suspected inflammatory myopathy is a time-sensitive problem

Bulbar symptoms change the game.

Dysphagia and dysphonia in a patient with progressive proximal weakness should immediately raise concern for a serious inflammatory myopathy, especially when CK is elevated and the exam is convincingly myopathic. These symptoms are not just “extra findings.” They are markers of severity and a warning that the patient may be heading toward aspiration, respiratory compromise, or profound functional decline.

IMNM is particularly important because it can be aggressive. Unlike a benign myalgia syndrome or transient toxic myopathy, IMNM can keep progressing even after the presumed trigger is removed. That distinction matters here: the patient had only one semaglutide dose, but her weakness worsened over months and her CK rose 12-fold. That trajectory fits immune-mediated disease far better than simple drug toxicity.

## Don’t wait for every antibody before acting

In a perfect world, we would have the diagnosis, autoantibody profile, imaging, and biopsy before starting immunosuppression. In the real world, severe bulbar weakness is not a “wait and see” situation.

This patient’s management illustrates a practical approach:

- **Pulse-dose IV methylprednisolone** was started when she presented with severe weakness and dysphagia.
- She was then transitioned to **high-dose oral prednisone**.
- Because she remained weak and had severe features, **IVIG** was added early.

That sequence makes sense when the concern is that delay could cost function, airway protection, or recovery. The point is not to treat every myositis patient this aggressively. The point is that bulbar involvement and marked proximal weakness justify moving before the diagnostic dust fully settles.

## Why steroids first?

For severe inflammatory myopathy, corticosteroids remain the fastest broadly available anti-inflammatory option. In a patient with suspected IMNM, pulse steroids are often used when there is:

- Rapidly progressive weakness
- Dysphagia or dysphonia
- Marked CK elevation
- Concern for impending respiratory or nutritional compromise

In this case, the team used **1 g methylprednisolone daily for 5 days**, then **prednisone 1 mg/kg daily**. That is a classic “hit hard, then maintain” strategy.

A few practical pearls:

- CK is useful, but **clinical trajectory matters more than the number**.
- A patient who is steadily losing function should not be held hostage by diagnostic uncertainty.
- If you’re worried about myopathy with bulbar involvement, ask early about **swallowing, voice changes, neck flexor weakness, and orthopnea**.

## Why add IVIG early?

IVIG is often the move when disease severity is high, response to steroids needs reinforcement, or a faster bridge is needed while waiting for other treatments to work.

In IMNM, IVIG is attractive because it can act quickly and does not carry the same infectious risk profile as many steroid-sparing agents. In this case, the patient was still significantly weak after steroids, so IVIG was given at **2 g/kg over 4 days**.

That was reasonable for several reasons:

- She had **bulbar symptoms**
- She had **severe proximal weakness**
- Her CK had been markedly elevated
- The team needed a therapy that could work on a clinically useful timescale

The dramatic point here is that her CK improved over two weeks, and she later returned to baseline function without prolonged immunosuppression. That outcome supports the idea that early aggressive treatment may be enough in some drug-triggered cases.

## Drug-triggered IMNM may behave differently than idiopathic IMNM

This is where the case gets especially nuanced.

Classic idiopathic IMNM often requires prolonged immunosuppression. Patients may need months of therapy, multiple agents, and relapse prevention. This case did not follow that path. After a short course of steroids and a single IVIG course, the patient improved steadily and eventually came off immunosuppression.

That course raises the possibility that this was **drug-triggered immune disease** rather than persistent idiopathic IMNM. The authors describe a probable association with semaglutide, though causality is not proven. Still, the clinical pattern is important:

- Symptoms began after semaglutide exposure
- Weakness progressed after the drug was stopped
- CK rose substantially over months
- Biopsy supported IMNM
- The patient improved after immunosuppression and continued avoidance of GLP-1 receptor agonists

That combination is more suggestive of an immune-mediated process than a self-limited toxic myopathy.

## What should the ED or admitting team take from this?

If a patient has progressive proximal weakness plus dysphagia or dysphonia, think beyond “weakness workup” and ask whether this is an evolving inflammatory myopathy that needs treatment now.

Clues that should increase your concern:

- Progressive proximal weakness, especially difficulty rising from a chair or holding the torso upright
- Dysphagia, dysphonia, or other bulbar symptoms
- CK elevation that is not trivial
- Symptoms that continue to worsen even after stopping a possible offending agent
- Lack of a better explanation such as exertional rhabdomyolysis, statin myopathy, or endocrine disease

In this case, the patient had already lost the ability to sit or stand without assistance. That is not a “routine rheum consult next week” problem.

## Balancing urgency with diagnostic uncertainty

This is the core clinical tension.

You do not want to overcall IMNM and expose patients to unnecessary high-dose steroids. But you also do not want to under-treat a disease that can rapidly destroy function. The balance shifts toward treatment when the patient has:

- Bulbar involvement
- Rapidly progressive weakness
- Objective CK elevation
- MRI evidence of muscle edema
- Biopsy findings consistent with necrotising myopathy

The beauty of this case is that treatment was started before every result was back, but the team still pursued a thorough diagnostic evaluation, including MRI and muscle biopsy. That’s the right balance: treat emergently, confirm carefully, and reassess as the story evolves.

## Clinical pearls from this case

- **Dysphagia and dysphonia in myopathy are red flags**, not minor symptoms.
- **Severe suspected IMNM should be treated before full serologies return** if the patient is functionally deteriorating.
- **Pulse steroids plus high-dose prednisone** are a reasonable initial backbone for severe inflammatory myopathy.
- **IVIG is worth early consideration** when weakness is severe or bulbar symptoms are present.
- **Drug-triggered IMNM may improve with a shorter immunosuppressive course** than idiopathic disease, but that distinction is not guaranteed at presentation.
- **A rising CK over weeks to months after drug exposure argues against simple toxic rhabdomyolysis** and should keep autoimmune myopathy on the table.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that the treatment window in severe inflammatory myopathy can close quickly. When weakness is progressive and bulbar symptoms appear, waiting for perfect diagnostic certainty can be the wrong move. In the right clinical context, early steroids and IVIG are not “premature”—they are protective.

The harder question is not whether to treat. It’s whether you’ll recognize the patient who needs treatment now.



# AST, ALT, and CK: Don’t Let “Liver Disease” Hide a Myopathy

## The trap

A woman in her 60s had years of mildly elevated AST and ALT, obesity, and biopsy-proven steatotic liver disease. Reasonable conclusion, right? “It’s the NAFLD.”

Then she developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK in the thousands. Muscle biopsy showed necrotizing myopathy. Her earlier “liver enzymes” had been the warning sign we all too easily file away as hepatic.

That’s the teaching point: AST and ALT are not liver-specific. When muscle is injured, they can rise too — and in a patient with known fatty liver disease, it’s easy to anchor on the liver and miss a myopathy until the patient is already profoundly weak.

## Why this matters

AST and ALT are commonly called “liver enzymes,” but that shorthand is misleading. They’re aminotransferases found in hepatocytes, yes — but also in skeletal muscle. If muscle injury is the real driver, the liver workup can be unrevealing while the patient’s weakness progresses.

This is especially tricky when NAFLD is already on the chart. A plausible hepatic explanation can become a diagnostic dead end.

In this case, the patient had biopsy-proven steatohepatitis years before semaglutide exposure, so her baseline transaminase elevation was real. But later, when she became weak and her CK rose dramatically, the aminotransferases were no longer just a liver story. The key is not choosing liver versus muscle too early. Often, you need to think “both until proven otherwise.”

## How muscle elevates AST and ALT

Skeletal muscle injury can cause aminotransferase elevation from:

- **Direct release from damaged myocytes**
- **Inflammatory myopathy**
- **Rhabdomyolysis**
- **Severe exertional injury**
- **Drug-induced myotoxicity**

AST tends to rise more prominently than ALT in muscle injury, but either can be elevated. The degree of transaminase elevation does not reliably distinguish liver from muscle.

The bigger clue is the **pattern**:
- Aminotransferases elevated out of proportion to other liver markers
- **CK elevated**
- Symptoms of muscle disease: weakness, myalgia, dysphagia, dark urine, exercise intolerance

## A practical way to separate liver from muscle

When AST/ALT are abnormal, don’t stop at “fatty liver.” Ask: is there evidence of muscle injury?

A useful bedside framework:

### 1) Look for muscle symptoms
Weakness matters. So do dysphagia, dysphonia, and difficulty standing from a chair or climbing stairs. These are not liver symptoms.

### 2) Check CK early
If AST/ALT are elevated and the history is even vaguely compatible with muscle disease, get a CK. It’s cheap, fast, and often decisive.

### 3) Review the liver panel, not just AST/ALT
If bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, and INR are normal, isolated aminotransferase elevation is less convincing for intrinsic liver injury.

### 4) Trend the numbers together
If CK rises and falls with AST/ALT, muscle is likely contributing. In this case, the patient’s AST/ALT were elevated in the setting of high CK, and her transaminases remained mildly abnormal even after CK normalized — supporting a background hepatic source plus superimposed muscle injury.

### 5) Don’t let known NAFLD close the case
NAFLD is common, and common things do happen commonly. But it can also become a diagnostic distraction. A liver biopsy showing steatosis does not exclude concurrent myositis.

## What this case adds

This patient’s course is unusual for another reason: the myopathy appeared after a single semaglutide dose, and the authors consider immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy probably associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure. Whether or not that association proves causal, the diagnostic lesson is broader.

The transaminases were a clue, but only when interpreted in context:
- longstanding mild AST/ALT elevation from steatotic liver disease
- later profound weakness
- marked CK elevation
- muscle biopsy consistent with necrotizing myopathy

That’s the pivot point: once muscle symptoms appeared, the elevated aminotransferases could no longer be safely attributed to the liver alone.

## Clinical pearls

- **AST and ALT are not liver-specific.**
- **In any patient with elevated aminotransferases plus weakness, check CK early.**
- **Normal bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase make isolated hepatocellular disease less certain.**
- **NAFLD can coexist with myopathy and obscure the diagnosis.**
- **A “liver” explanation should not delay evaluation for inflammatory myopathy when the clinical story fits.**

## Take-home points

- Elevated AST/ALT do not equal liver disease.
- Muscle injury can produce transaminase elevations that mimic hepatic injury.
- In patients with known NAFLD, don’t anchor on steatosis if new weakness appears.
- CK is the key test that helps unmask occult myopathy.
- If the story includes proximal weakness, dysphagia, or dysphonia, think beyond the liver.



# The Semaglutide Era and Rare Harm Signals: How Clinicians Should Think About Pharmacovigilance

## Why this case matters

Semaglutide and other GLP-1 receptor agonists have quickly gone from “newish diabetes drugs” to mainstream therapy for obesity, diabetes, and metabolic disease. That scale matters. When a medication is prescribed to millions of people, rare adverse events stop being academic curiosities and become bedside problems.

This case is interesting because the patient’s course did **not** fit simple drug intolerance or straightforward toxic rhabdomyolysis. She had a dramatic GI reaction after her first semaglutide dose, but then—rather than improving—she developed a progressive, severe proximal myopathy over months, with dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK that rose from mildly elevated to nearly 4,000 U/L. Muscle biopsy supported **immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)**, and she improved with corticosteroids, IVIG, and stopping semaglutide.

That combination raises a bigger question: when a patient gets worse after starting a GLP-1 receptor agonist, are we always dealing with “expected” side effects, deconditioning, or sarcopenia? Or are we occasionally seeing an underrecognized autoimmune muscle syndrome?

## The bedside problem: distinguishing toxicity from autoimmunity

In day-to-day practice, muscle symptoms in a patient on a weight-loss medication can be easy to dismiss. Fatigue, weakness, reduced intake, vomiting, and generalized deconditioning are common enough in this population that clinicians can be falsely reassured.

But the timeline in this case should make you stop and think. A toxic or metabolic myopathy usually declares itself early and improves after drug withdrawal. This patient instead had:

- Progressive proximal weakness
- Bulbar symptoms: dysphagia and dysphonia
- Marked CK rise over time
- MRI evidence of muscle edema
- Biopsy with necrotic and regenerating fibers plus macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis
- Clinical response to immunosuppression

That is a different beast.

## Why IMNM is the key syndrome to recognize

IMNM is a distinct inflammatory myopathy characterized by aggressive proximal weakness, elevated CK, and muscle fiber necrosis with relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation. It is often associated with anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies, but not always—seronegative disease is real and creates diagnostic uncertainty.

A practical point: **CK elevation plus weakness is not enough to call something rhabdomyolysis**. Rhabdo is generally a toxic or physical injury pattern. IMNM is an immune disease that can masquerade as rhabdo early, especially if you focus only on the lab value and not the trajectory.

Clues favoring IMNM over direct drug toxicity include:

- Progressive weakness rather than rapid recovery
- Persistent or rising CK after the drug is stopped
- Bulbar involvement or severe proximal weakness
- MRI edema in a myositis pattern
- Muscle biopsy with necrosis/regeneration and macrophage infiltration
- Response to steroids, IVIG, or other immunotherapy

In this case, the patient’s CK would not be expected to climb 12-fold over 4 months from a one-time toxic exposure alone. That prolonged progression is what makes the autoimmune explanation compelling.

## Pharmacovigilance starts at the bedside

Single case reports are not proof of causality, but they are often the first signal that something rare is happening. That is exactly why they matter.

When assessing a possible drug-related immune toxicity, clinicians should think in terms of **plausibility, timing, exclusion of alternatives, and response to withdrawal/treatment** rather than demanding perfect proof. In this report, the authors applied WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre logic and judged the relationship as **probable**, not definite. That is the right level of humility.

A few practical principles:

- A temporal relationship is necessary, but not sufficient.
- If there is a plausible alternative explanation, don’t overcall causality.
- Improvement after stopping the drug supports the association, but immunosuppression can confound that interpretation.
- Rechallenge is usually the cleanest test scientifically, but often impossible or unsafe clinically.
- With severe immune-mediated toxicity, rechallenge is frequently **unethical**.

That last point is important. In this case, asking a patient to re-expose themselves to a medication after severe weakness, CK elevation, and biopsy-proven myopathy would be hard to justify. Pharmacovigilance has to work without perfect experiments.

## What this means for clinicians using GLP-1 receptor agonists

GLP-1 drugs are not “bad drugs.” Their benefits are real and often transformative. But the speed and scale of their adoption means we need a low threshold to recognize uncommon harm signals.

This case should push us to ask:

- Could some “medication intolerance” syndromes actually be early immune toxicities?
- Are we missing inflammatory myopathies because the initial symptoms are nonspecific?
- Are we attributing weakness to weight loss or deconditioning when the patient is actually developing a treatable autoimmune disease?

The literature already suggests GLP-1 receptor agonists can be associated with reduced skeletal muscle mass, and there are emerging reports of severe muscle injury with related agents. But muscle wasting, rhabdomyolysis, and IMNM are not the same diagnosis. The distinction matters because the treatment pathways differ dramatically.

## A pragmatic approach to the patient with weakness on a GLP-1 receptor agonist

If a patient on semaglutide or another GLP-1 agent presents with weakness, especially if it is progressive or accompanied by bulbar symptoms, don’t stop at “it’s probably the medication.”

Consider:

- CK and basic metabolic panel
- AST/ALT in context of possible muscle injury
- Focused neuro exam: proximal strength, neck flexors, bulbar function
- Urinalysis for myoglobin if rhabdo is suspected
- Review for statins, supplements, recent exertion, infection, or endocrine causes
- Autoimmune work-up if weakness is persistent or disproportionate
- MRI and muscle biopsy when the diagnosis remains unclear

If the clinical pattern suggests inflammatory myopathy, early rheumatology/neurology involvement is appropriate. And if the patient is significantly weak or has dysphagia, don’t wait for every serology before starting treatment.

## Take-home points

- **Not all muscle symptoms on GLP-1 therapy are benign intolerance or sarcopenia.**
- **Progressive weakness, dysphagia, and rising CK should trigger evaluation for inflammatory myopathy, including IMNM.**
- **Rising CK over weeks to months after stopping the drug argues against simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.**
- **Muscle biopsy can be decisive when antibodies are negative.**
- **Rechallenge is often not ethical after severe immune-mediated toxicity.**
- **Clinicians should report suspected rare adverse events to pharmacovigilance systems; single cases can become important safety signals.**

## Final thought

The semaglutide era is going to generate more “rare event” questions, not fewer. Most will not represent true causality. But some will. The challenge for bedside clinicians is to remain skeptical without becoming dismissive—to recognize that when a common drug meets an uncommon syndrome, the first clue may be a patient who doesn’t follow the expected recovery curve.

That is where pharmacovigilance stops being abstract and becomes clinical medicine.
