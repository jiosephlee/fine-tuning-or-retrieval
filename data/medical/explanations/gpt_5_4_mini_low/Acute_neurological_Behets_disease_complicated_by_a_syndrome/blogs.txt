Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When the MRI Is Normal: Diagnosing Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Without a Lesion

## The part that should make you pause

A febrile patient with headache, vertigo, and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis usually sends us straight into the “rule out infection” lane. That’s appropriate. But this case adds a twist that’s easy to miss: the brain MRI was normal.

In this man in his 60s, the real diagnosis was not HSV, not bacterial meningitis, not lymphoma, and not a bland viral syndrome. It was **newly diagnosed systemic Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease**, complicated by **SIADH-related hyponatremia**. And the key lesson is this: **early neuro-Behçet’s can present as aseptic meningitis without a visible parenchymal lesion on MRI**.

If you wait for the “classic” brainstem lesion before you call neuro-Behçet’s, you may miss the diagnosis.

## The clinical picture

He presented with:

- 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia
- Oral aphthae
- Folliculitis-like rash on the face and trunk
- Hyponatremia
- Later, a painful scrotal ulcer

He was alert, febrile, tachycardic, and appeared euvolemic. Initial labs showed marked inflammation, elevated CK, and hypo-osmolar hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium — a profile consistent with **SIADH**.

Then came the neurologic workup:

- Headache and vertigo worsened
- MRI showed **no significant abnormalities**
- CSF showed:
  - mildly elevated opening pressure
  - elevated protein
  - mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- CSF infectious studies were negative

At that point, the case shifted from “possible CNS infection” to “what inflammatory syndrome ties all of this together?”

## Why this is tricky

Behçet’s disease is a multisystem inflammatory disorder classically associated with:

- recurrent oral ulcers
- genital ulcers
- skin lesions
- uveitis

Neurologic involvement exists, but it’s less common and often delayed. Acute neuro-Behçet’s typically shows up as **meningoencephalitis** or **brainstem/parenchymal disease**. That’s why a normal MRI can falsely reassure clinicians.

But normal imaging does **not** rule it out.

This case had exactly that pitfall: no MRI lesion in the hypothalamus or elsewhere, yet the CSF and clinical syndrome fit inflammatory CNS involvement. In other words, **the MRI was negative because the disease was early or non-parenchymal**, not because the CNS was uninvolved.

## How the diagnosis emerged

The diagnosis of Behçet’s was made clinically. He had:

- oral aphthae
- genital aphthosis
- folliculitis-like skin lesions

That combination met criteria for **definite Behçet’s disease**. Once the systemic disease was recognized, the neurologic syndrome became much easier to frame as acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially after infectious, malignant, and other autoimmune causes were not supported by testing.

A few diagnostic pearls from the case:

- **Genital aphthosis is a high-value clue** — it’s much more specific than oral ulcers alone.
- **CSF matters** — mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein support inflammatory meningeal disease.
- **Normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s**, particularly early on.
- The diagnosis is often one of **pattern recognition plus exclusion**, not a single confirmatory test.

## SIADH in the background

The hyponatremia wasn’t incidental. His labs fit SIADH:

- low serum osmolality
- high urine osmolality
- high urine sodium
- elevated ADH
- clinically euvolemic exam
- normal thyroid and cortisol testing

The interesting part is the likely link to CNS inflammation. SIADH is well known in CNS disease, and inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 may contribute to non-osmotic ADH release. In this case, there was no drug trigger to blame, making the neuroinflammatory process the most plausible context.

That said, the authors are appropriately cautious: this is an association, not proof of causation.

## Management: sometimes less is more

The patient initially received broad empiric antimicrobials and acyclovir, which is exactly what most of us would do while infection is still on the table. Once the data came back, therapy narrowed.

The notable treatment decision was that he improved with **colchicine monotherapy** — without corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants.

That’s not the usual “textbook” neuro-Behçet’s playbook, since corticosteroids are commonly used for acute neuro-Behçet’s. But in this case:

- fever resolved quickly after colchicine
- mucocutaneous lesions improved
- neurologic symptoms improved
- sodium normalized
- ADH fell to normal
- no relapse occurred over long-term follow-up

Important caveat: this is a **single case**, and colchicine is not established as standard monotherapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s. Still, it’s a reminder that some patients with early or meningeal-predominant disease may improve without aggressive immunosuppression.

## Practical approach when you see this pattern

If a patient has fever + headache + CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis + normal MRI, don’t stop at “MRI negative, so probably not inflammatory.”

Instead, ask:

1. **Are there mucocutaneous clues?**
   - oral ulcers
   - genital ulcers
   - acneiform or folliculitis-like lesions
   - eye symptoms

2. **Does the CSF suggest aseptic meningitis?**
   - lymphocytic pleocytosis
   - elevated protein
   - negative cultures/PCRs

3. **Have the big mimics been addressed?**
   - HSV/VZV
   - bacterial meningitis
   - TB
   - malignancy
   - sarcoidosis
   - other autoimmune disease

4. **Is there unexplained hyponatremia?**
   - consider SIADH, especially if there are CNS symptoms or hiccups

5. **Could this be Behçet’s even if it’s “new”?**
   - yes — neuro-Behçet’s can occasionally be recognized concurrently with systemic disease

## Take-home points

- **A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s.**
- **Early neuro-Behçet’s may present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions.**
- **Mucocutaneous findings are often the diagnostic anchor** — especially oral + genital aphthosis with skin lesions.
- **CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein support CNS inflammation** when infection has been reasonably excluded.
- **Hyponatremia from SIADH can accompany CNS inflammation**, including neuro-Behçet’s.
- **Don’t over-rely on imaging alone**; in suspected neuro-Behçet’s, the diagnosis is clinicoradiologic, not radiologic alone.

## Bottom line

When a patient has fever, headache, and inflammatory CSF but a normal MRI, the temptation is to downgrade neuro-Behçet’s. This case argues the opposite: **if the mucocutaneous story fits, a normal MRI should not reassure you out of the diagnosis**. Early neuro-Behçet’s can be a meningeal, CSF-driven process — and sometimes the skin and mouth tell the story before the scan does.



# Hyponatremia With a Rash and a Headache: SIADH as a Clue to CNS Inflammation

## Why this case is worth your attention

Hyponatremia is common. Headache is common. Rash is common. But put them together in a febrile patient with oral ulcers and a later-appearing genital ulcer, and you should start thinking beyond “viral syndrome” or “dehydration.”

This case is a good reminder that **SIADH can be a clue to central nervous system inflammation**, not just a lab abnormality to chase in isolation. Here, a man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, malaise, hiccups, arthralgia, mucocutaneous lesions, and **hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia**. That combination ultimately led to a diagnosis of **systemic Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease**, complicated by SIADH.

Even more interesting: his neurologic and sodium abnormalities improved with **colchicine monotherapy**, without corticosteroids.

## Behçet’s disease: when the diagnosis is sitting on the skin and mucosa

Behçet’s disease is a multisystem inflammatory vasculitis classically associated with recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, skin lesions, and uveitis. Neurologic involvement is less common, and acute neuro-Behçet’s typically shows up later in the disease course. So when Behçet’s and neurologic disease are diagnosed at the same time, that’s unusual.

In this patient, the key clue was the evolving mucocutaneous picture:

- oral aphthae on presentation
- folliculitis-like rash on the face and trunk
- painful scrotal ulcer appearing later

That combination, plus HLA-B51 positivity, supported **definite Behçet’s disease** by international criteria. The CNS syndrome was then evaluated in that context.

## The hyponatremia workup: what made this SIADH?

This was not a “just give saline and see what happens” scenario. The hyponatremia was worked up as **hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia**, and the data fit SIADH well:

- serum sodium: 124 mmol/L
- low serum osmolality: 267 mOsm/kg
- high urine osmolality: 900 mOsm/kg
- urine sodium: 88 mmol/L
- elevated ADH
- normal thyroid function
- normal cortisol
- clinically euvolemic exam

That is the classic SIADH pattern: **inappropriately concentrated urine despite hypotonic plasma**, in a patient who appears euvolemic and in whom adrenal, thyroid, renal, and volume causes have been excluded.

The bedside pearl here is simple: if the patient has hyponatremia plus headache or other CNS symptoms, don’t stop at the sodium value. **Ask what is driving ADH release.**

## CNS inflammation is a known trigger for SIADH

SIADH is commonly associated with:

- CNS infection or inflammation
- pulmonary disease
- malignancy
- medications

This case adds an important inflammatory neurologic diagnosis to that list: **acute neuro-Behçet’s disease**.

The proposed mechanism is biologically plausible. Neuroinflammation may stimulate ADH release, and inflammatory cytokines—especially **interleukin-6**—have been implicated in this pathway. Behçet’s disease is a neutrophilic inflammatory disorder, and IL-6 activity has been described in neuro-Behçet’s. In this case, IL-6 was not measured, so causality can’t be proven. But the association between active CNS inflammation and SIADH is hard to ignore.

So the likely clinical logic is:

**neuroinflammation → cytokine surge → inappropriate ADH secretion → hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia**

That’s not unique to Behçet’s, but it’s a useful framework.

## Don’t let a normal MRI fool you

One of the trickiest parts of this case was that **brain MRI was normal**. That doesn’t exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s.

The patient had:

- headache
- vertigo
- fever
- CSF with elevated protein and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- negative infectious studies

That’s enough to make you think about **aseptic meningitis / meningoencephalitis**, especially once the mucocutaneous findings and genital ulcer emerged.

The lesson: in suspected neuroinflammatory disease, **CSF can be more revealing than MRI early on**. A normal scan should not end the diagnostic process if the clinical picture is still pointing to CNS inflammation.

## Differential diagnosis: stay broad, then narrow hard

This case was initially treated like a possible CNS infection, which is appropriate. Fever + headache + hyponatremia is not Behçet’s until proven otherwise.

The team considered and evaluated for:

- HSV and VZV encephalitis
- bacterial meningitis
- tuberculous meningitis
- malignancy
- sarcoidosis
- autoimmune disease

That’s the right kind of broad early differential. But the key is not just ordering tests; it’s knowing what result would actually move the needle.

A mild rise in soluble IL-2 receptor or a normal ACE level may be interesting, but in isolation they rarely solve the case. In contrast, the **clinical evolution** of oral ulcers, skin lesions, and genital aphthosis did.

## Hiccuping your way to the diagnosis

Persistent hiccups were part of the presentation. That’s a useful bedside reminder: hiccups can be more than a nuisance symptom, especially when they coexist with neurologic complaints and electrolyte disturbances.

Severe hyponatremia can present with:

- hiccups
- confusion
- gait instability
- nausea
- headache
- weakness

In this case, hiccups may have been a small clue pointing toward a systemic process with CNS involvement and electrolyte derangement.

## Treatment: conservative sodium management, anti-inflammatory therapy, and close monitoring

The patient was managed conservatively for SIADH with:

- fluid restriction to less than 800 mL/day
- daily sodium monitoring
- avoidance of rapid correction

He did **not** require hypertonic saline or a vasopressin receptor antagonist.

For Behçet’s disease and neuro-Behçet’s, colchicine was started after the diagnosis became clear. The patient’s fever improved the next day, and over the following two weeks his:

- headache/vertigo improved
- hiccups improved
- malaise improved
- mucocutaneous lesions improved
- sodium normalized
- ADH level fell

No corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were used.

That’s notable, but don’t overgeneralize it. Steroids remain a mainstay for many cases of acute neuro-Behçet’s. This case suggests colchicine may have been enough here, but spontaneous improvement and disease heterogeneity are real possibilities.

## What this case teaches at the bedside

If you’re staring at a patient with hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia, the essential question is not just “is this SIADH?” It’s “**why is ADH turned on?**”

When headache, fever, or other neurologic symptoms are part of the story, think about CNS inflammation. And if oral ulcers, genital ulcers, or a folliculitis-like rash are also present, Behçet’s should jump way up the list.

## Take-home points

- **SIADH can be a clue to CNS inflammation**, not just an isolated electrolyte problem.
- In **hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia**, confirm the pattern with serum osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium, and endocrine exclusion.
- A **normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s**, especially early.
- In a patient with **fever + headache + oral/genital ulcers + rash**, keep Behçet’s on the differential.
- **Persistent hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis/CK elevation** may be underrecognized manifestations of significant hyponatremia.
- IL-6 may help connect **neuroinflammation and inappropriate ADH release**, but this remains a biologically plausible mechanism rather than proven causality in an individual case.
- Treat the sodium safely, but don’t miss the **underlying inflammatory diagnosis** driving it.

## Bottom line

Hyponatremia is sometimes the signpost, not the destination. In this case, SIADH pointed toward CNS inflammation, and the CNS inflammation turned out to be acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. When the sodium is low and the headache is real, look for the rash, the ulcers, and the inflammatory story hiding underneath.



# The Behçet’s Diagnosis That Declares Itself Late: Why the Genital Ulcer Changed Everything

## The part that makes this case tricky

This is one of those cases that punishes premature closure.

A man in his 60s came in with fever, headache, malaise, hiccups, arthralgia, oral aphthae, and hyponatremia. At first glance, that sounds like a broad, frustrating differential: infection, systemic inflammation, malignancy, endocrine disease, maybe a drug effect if there were one. Nothing about the initial presentation screams Behçet’s disease.

And that’s the trap.

The diagnosis only became clear when a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on hospital day 5. That single finding changed the entire clinical picture. Suddenly, the constellation of oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, and genital aphthosis fit Behçet’s disease much more convincingly. In this case, the genital ulcer was the clue that turned a vague inflammatory syndrome into a definable vasculitic disorder.

## Behçet’s disease: when the syndrome arrives in pieces

Behçet’s disease is a multisystem inflammatory disorder classically associated with recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, skin lesions, and eye disease. Neurologic involvement occurs in a minority of patients, usually later in the disease course — so seeing systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease diagnosed at the same time is unusual.

That timing matters.

Early on, the patient had fever, headache, and oral aphthae — but no genital ulcer yet. Without that more specific mucocutaneous feature, the differential naturally leans toward more common problems:

- Viral or bacterial meningoencephalitis
- Tuberculous meningitis
- Autoimmune/inflammatory CNS disease
- Malignancy
- Neurosarcoidosis

That is exactly what happened here, and it was reasonable. But the case reinforces an important habit: in evolving syndromes, the initial exam is not the final exam.

## Why serial examination matters

The diagnosis of Behçet’s disease was not available at the front door. It emerged over time.

On admission, the patient had:
- Oral aphthae
- Folliculitis-like rashes
- Fever and systemic inflammation
- Headache and later vertigo
- Hyponatremia

Then, on day 5:
- A painful scrotal ulcer appeared

That is the kind of change that should trigger a fresh diagnostic reset. If you’re following a patient with a nonspecific inflammatory syndrome, repeating the skin and mucosal exam is not busywork — it is often the difference between “fever of unknown origin” and a unifying diagnosis.

Behçet’s lesions can be transient, subtle, and easy to miss unless you look again.

## The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease: practical bedside use

This case also nicely illustrates how the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease can help structure your thinking.

The scoring system is clinical, not biomarker-driven. In this patient:
- Oral aphthae: 2 points
- Genital aphthosis: 2 points
- Folliculitis-like rash: 1 point

Total: 5 points. A score of 4 or more supports definite Behçet’s disease.

That’s a useful reminder that Behçet’s is still, at its core, a bedside diagnosis. HLA-B51 may support the picture, but it does not make the diagnosis. In this case, HLA-B51 was positive, but the diagnosis rested on clinical features.

## Don’t anchor on infection just because the patient is febrile

Early on, this patient looked infectious. He had fever, headache, inflammatory markers, and CSF pleocytosis. Empiric cefepime and later acyclovir were both reasonable while the workup unfolded.

But the course also shows why clinicians need to keep a wide differential even after initial treatment begins. The CSF showed:
- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- Elevated protein
- Negative bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal studies

MRI was unrevealing. That does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s disease — especially early on. Acute neuro-Behçet’s can present as aseptic meningitis with no clear parenchymal lesion on MRI. A normal scan does not rule it out.

So if you have:
- Mucocutaneous lesions
- CNS symptoms
- CSF inflammation
- Negative infectious studies

…Behçet’s belongs on the short list.

## The hyponatremia is not a side quest

The other striking feature here is the SIADH.

The patient had:
- Hypotonic hyponatremia
- High urine osmolality
- High urine sodium
- Elevated ADH
- Clinical euvolemia
- Normal thyroid and cortisol testing

That is classic SIADH physiology.

The interesting question is why it happened. In this report, the authors argue that acute CNS inflammation from neuro-Behçet’s may have driven inappropriate ADH secretion. That’s plausible and clinically important, even if causality can’t be proven from a single case.

The teaching point is simpler: in a patient with headache, fever, and hyponatremia, SIADH may be a clue to CNS inflammation rather than an incidental lab abnormality.

And don’t forget the “odd” symptoms that can be caused by hyponatremia:
- Persistent hiccups
- Weakness or functional decline
- Possibly rhabdomyolysis, as suggested by the elevated CK in this case

Those are easy to dismiss unless you connect the dots.

## Treatment: colchicine alone did the job here

One of the most surprising parts of this report is that colchicine monotherapy was associated with improvement in:
- Fever
- Mucocutaneous lesions
- Neurologic symptoms
- Hyponatremia

That is notable because corticosteroids are generally considered the mainstay for acute neuro-Behçet’s, and colchicine is usually thought of more as a mucocutaneous drug. The authors are appropriately cautious: spontaneous improvement can’t be excluded, and this does not overturn standard neuro-Behçet’s treatment.

Still, it’s a useful reminder that not every patient follows the textbook path. In this case, the improvement was rapid after colchicine was started, and the patient ultimately did well without steroids or other immunosuppressants.

## What to remember at the bedside

- Behçet’s disease may announce itself gradually; early presentations can look like infection or nonspecific inflammation.
- Repeat the oral, genital, and skin exam — the diagnosis may be hiding in plain sight.
- Genital aphthosis is a high-yield clue and can be the feature that clarifies the syndrome.
- The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease are practical and bedside-friendly; use them to structure your reasoning.
- Normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially early.
- Hyponatremia with a euvolemic picture should prompt consideration of SIADH, including CNS inflammatory causes.
- Inflammatory neurologic symptoms plus mucocutaneous findings should make you think beyond infection.

## Bottom line

This case is a nice reminder that Behçet’s disease often doesn’t arrive as a complete package. It shows up in fragments — oral ulcers first, then a rash, then a genital ulcer that finally ties the whole thing together.

If you keep re-examining the patient and resist the urge to lock onto the first plausible diagnosis, the syndrome may eventually declare itself.



# Broad Differential, Narrow Signal: Testing Discipline in Aseptic Meningitis With Systemic Inflammation

## Why this case is interesting

Fever, headache, hyponatremia, very high inflammatory markers, and a bland initial exam can send clinicians down a long diagnostic road very quickly. That’s appropriate—until the workup starts generating noisy results that don’t actually move the case forward.

This patient is a great example of that tension. He ultimately had newly diagnosed Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, complicated by SIADH. But at presentation, that diagnosis was not obvious. He looked like a patient with possible CNS infection, inflammatory disease, or even malignancy. The challenge was to stay broad enough to not miss a dangerous cause, while still being disciplined enough to avoid low-yield testing that would muddy the waters.

## The initial problem: fever, headache, hyponatremia, inflammation

The presenting syndrome was not subtle:

- 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, hiccups, and arthralgia
- Oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash
- Hyponatremia at 124 mmol/L
- CRP 180 mg/L and ESR 70 mm/h
- Euvolemic on exam
- Later, worsening headache/vertigo and CSF pleocytosis

That constellation makes CNS infection and CNS inflammation hard to ignore. In the ED or on admission, this is exactly the moment to think broadly and act quickly.

The early empiric cefepime was not unreasonable while cultures were pending and the source was unclear. When neurologic symptoms worsened, empiric acyclovir was also defensible until HSV/VZV were excluded. This is the kind of “treat first, refine fast” approach that prevents delay in catastrophic disease.

## Let the CSF do the heavy lifting

The case became much more informative once CSF was obtained.

The CSF showed:

- Mildly elevated opening pressure
- Elevated protein
- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- Negative bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal studies
- No malignant cells

That profile supports aseptic meningitis rather than bacterial meningitis. In other words, the lumbar puncture didn’t just “rule things out”—it actively narrowed the differential.

A key clinical pearl here: in a patient with fever + headache + inflammatory markers, CSF is often more valuable than sending a sprawling panel of serum screening tests. It gives you real information about whether you’re dealing with infection, inflammation, or something else entirely.

## Hyponatremia was not just a side issue

The sodium abnormality was not incidental. This patient had true hypo-osmolar hyponatremia with:

- Low serum osmolality
- Inappropriately concentrated urine
- High urine sodium
- Elevated ADH
- Normal thyroid and adrenal evaluation
- Clinical euvolemia

That is textbook SIADH.

In patients with CNS inflammation, SIADH should always be on the table. The neurologic process may be driving the endocrine abnormality, rather than the other way around. In this case, the hyponatremia may also explain some of the “non-specific” symptoms that can be easy to dismiss—especially hiccups and possibly the elevated CK/rhabdomyolysis signal.

## The diagnosis emerged from the exam, not the screening labs

The turning point was not ACE, sIL-2R, or imaging. It was the appearance of a painful scrotal ulcer on day 5, in the setting of:

- Oral aphthae
- Folliculitis-like skin lesions
- Genital aphthosis
- CNS symptoms

That combination made Behçet’s disease clinically real.

This matters because Behçet’s is a diagnosis that lives in the history and physical. HLA-B51 may support the story, but it doesn’t make the diagnosis. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease are still fundamentally clinical, and this case fits that model well.

## Where testing discipline matters

The authors did a broad exclusion workup, which is understandable in a patient with encephalitic symptoms and a new systemic inflammatory syndrome. But they also make an important point: broad is not the same as indiscriminate.

### ACE and sIL-2R: why they didn’t help much

Sarcoidosis and lymphoma were considered, but the pretest probability was low based on the overall picture. In that setting:

- ACE has limited sensitivity for sarcoidosis
- Mildly elevated sIL-2R is nonspecific and can simply reflect inflammation

So although these tests were not “wrong,” they were low signal. They didn’t meaningfully advance the diagnosis, and they could easily have been misread as supportive of diseases that the rest of the data did not actually fit.

This is a common trap in inflammatory neurology: once you order broad screening tests, you create interpretive obligations. A borderline abnormal result can become a diagnostic distraction if it’s not anchored to the clinical picture.

### Better question: what was the pretest probability?

Before ordering tests for sarcoidosis or lymphoma in aseptic meningitis, ask:

- Is there a compatible systemic pattern?
- Are there imaging findings suggesting granulomatous disease or malignancy?
- Are there CSF features that support those diagnoses?
- Is there a more likely unifying explanation already emerging?

In this case, the answer increasingly pointed toward Behçet’s with neuroinflammatory involvement.

## Neuro-Behçet’s can be early, subtle, and MRI-negative

One of the most useful lessons here is that a normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, especially early.

This patient had:

- Fever
- Headache
- Vertigo
- Mildly inflammatory CSF
- No focal deficits
- No MRI lesion in the hypothalamus or elsewhere

That is still compatible with acute neuro-Behçet’s, particularly an aseptic meningitis-like presentation.

FOAM pearl: if the story fits and the CSF supports CNS inflammation, don’t let a normal MRI falsely reassure you out of the diagnosis.

## Treatment: don’t forget the low-tech parts

The hyponatremia was managed conservatively with fluid restriction and close sodium monitoring. No hypertonic saline or vaptans were required. That was appropriate because he was stable and the sodium corrected with treatment of the underlying inflammatory process.

Then colchicine was started after systemic Behçet’s and acute neuro-Behçet’s were recognized. Interestingly, he improved without corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants:

- Fever resolved the next day
- Appetite improved
- Over 2 weeks, vertigo, hiccups, malaise, and mucocutaneous lesions improved
- Sodium normalized by day 16
- ADH level fell
- CK normalized

You should not generalize from a single case and assume colchicine monotherapy is standard for acute neuro-Behçet’s. The authors themselves note that corticosteroids are generally considered the mainstay. But this case does show that treating the underlying inflammatory disease can reverse the SIADH physiology when CNS inflammation is the driver.

## Practical take-home approach

When you see fever, headache, hyponatremia, and inflammation:

1. **Assume dangerous disease until proven otherwise**
   - Infection and CNS inflammation must be evaluated urgently.

2. **Use CSF to narrow the field**
   - Aseptic meningitis pattern should shift your differential away from routine bacterial infection.

3. **Recognize SIADH as a CNS clue**
   - Hyponatremia may be a manifestation of the neurologic process, not a separate problem.

4. **Anchor on the exam**
   - Oral ulcers, genital ulcers, and skin lesions were the diagnostic signal here.

5. **Be skeptical of low-pretest-probability screening tests**
   - Mild ACE or sIL-2R abnormalities rarely diagnose sarcoid or lymphoma by themselves.

6. **Normal MRI does not exclude early neuroinflammatory disease**
   - Especially when CSF and clinical features point toward it.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that good diagnostic medicine is not about ordering everything—it’s about ordering the right things for the right reasons, then being willing to stop when the data have told you enough.

In aseptic meningitis with systemic inflammation, a broad differential is appropriate. But the signal often comes from a few high-value data points: the CSF profile, the mucocutaneous exam, and whether the hyponatremia is actually SIADH. The rest can be noise.

## Key points

- Fever, headache, hyponatremia, and high inflammatory markers should trigger a broad but structured evaluation.
- CSF is often the highest-yield test for narrowing infectious vs inflammatory CNS disease.
- SIADH can accompany CNS inflammation and may be a clue to the underlying process.
- Behçet’s disease is a clinical diagnosis; oral/genital ulcers and skin lesions matter more than nonspecific screening labs.
- Mildly elevated ACE or sIL-2R is low-value when the pretest probability for sarcoidosis or lymphoma is low.
- A normal MRI does not exclude early acute neuro-Behçet’s disease.



# Hiccups, CK Elevation, and Sodium 124: The Odd Clues of Clinically Important Hyponatremia

## Why this case is worth your attention

A man in his 60s came in with fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, arthralgia, oral aphthae, and a sodium of 124 mmol/L. Easy enough to anchor on “infection” or “nonspecific viral syndrome,” right?

But the case kept getting stranger: his creatine kinase was elevated, his inflammatory markers were very high, and his exam revealed mucocutaneous lesions that ultimately supported Behçet’s disease. CSF showed aseptic meningitis, brain MRI was unrevealing, and the final picture was acute neuro-Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH. Even more interesting: his symptoms and sodium improved with colchicine monotherapy and careful fluid restriction, without corticosteroids.

This is a great reminder that hyponatremia is not just a lab abnormality. It can have weird, underappreciated manifestations—and some of those manifestations can be the clue.

## Hiccups are not always benign

Persistent hiccups are easy to dismiss. Most of the time, they’re transient and annoying rather than dangerous. But persistent hiccups should make you widen the lens, especially when they coexist with other neurologic or systemic symptoms.

In this case, the hiccups were part of a broader inflammatory CNS syndrome. That matters because hiccups can be a clue to:

- CNS inflammation or irritation
- Metabolic disturbance, including significant hyponatremia
- Brainstem involvement
- Diaphragmatic or vagal irritation from non-neurologic causes

The takeaway is not that every hiccup needs a neuro workup. It’s that hiccups plus headache, fever, or electrolyte abnormalities should stop you from prematurely minimizing the presentation.

## Hyponatremia can present “sideways”

Severe or subacute hyponatremia does not always announce itself with seizures or coma. It can present with a vague, easy-to-miss symptom cluster:

- fatigue
- malaise
- anorexia
- headache
- dizziness/vertigo
- nausea
- hiccups

That symptom pattern is especially dangerous when it is embedded in another illness. In this case, the patient clearly had an inflammatory disorder—but the sodium abnormality was not incidental. It was part of the disease process and may have been contributing to the neuro symptoms.

A practical point: when a patient with nonspecific neurologic complaints also has hyponatremia, don’t stop at “SIADH” as a label. Ask what is driving the SIADH.

## When should you think about rhabdomyolysis?

This case also had an elevated CK. That’s an easy finding to overlook when the headline problem is “hyponatremia” or “fever.”

Hyponatremia can be associated with rhabdomyolysis, particularly when it is severe, prolonged, or accompanied by systemic illness. The mechanism is not always straightforward, but the association is real enough that CK can be worth checking in selected patients—especially those with:

- myalgias or muscle tenderness
- generalized weakness
- dark urine
- prolonged malaise
- significant inflammatory illness
- moderate-to-severe hyponatremia without an obvious explanation

In this patient, CK elevation may have reflected inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury, and it normalized as the clinical syndrome improved.

The lesson is not to reflexively send CK on every mild hyponatremia. The lesson is to think of CK as a reasonable add-on when the symptom pattern suggests more than isolated electrolyte disturbance.

## SIADH is often a diagnosis of mechanism, not of destination

The patient’s labs fit SIADH:

- low serum osmolality
- inappropriately concentrated urine
- elevated urine sodium
- euvolemic exam
- elevated ADH
- normal thyroid and cortisol testing

That’s a solid SIADH pattern. But SIADH itself is not the diagnosis you’re ultimately trying to make. It’s the mechanism.

Common causes include:

- CNS pathology or inflammation
- pulmonary disease
- malignancy
- medications

This case is a nice example of why that distinction matters. The sodium problem was downstream of acute neuroinflammation from Behçet’s disease. If you only treat the lab value and miss the CNS disease, you miss the real problem.

## Sodium correction still matters, even when the driver is inflammatory CNS disease

Whether the trigger is meningitis, encephalitis, malignancy, or a medication, the principles of safe sodium management do not change.

In this case, the patient was managed conservatively with:

- fluid restriction to less than 800 mL/day
- daily monitoring of serum sodium
- avoidance of overly rapid correction

No hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists were required.

That’s a good reminder that not every SIADH case needs aggressive correction. The core goals are:

- treat the underlying cause
- prevent neurologic deterioration from worsening hyponatremia
- avoid osmotic demyelination from overcorrection

Especially in inflammatory CNS disease, sodium can improve as the underlying process comes under control. The temptation to “push harder” should be tempered by disciplined monitoring.

## A few FOAM-style pearls from this case

This patient’s presentation ties together several underappreciated bedside clues. The important pattern recognition is not “Behçet’s disease causes SIADH,” because that association is still not firmly established. The more useful point is that CNS inflammation can cause SIADH, and SIADH can present with subtle neurologic and muscular manifestations that are easy to misread.

In practice, that means you should be alert to the combination of:

- headache plus hyponatremia
- hiccups plus hyponatremia
- malaise plus muscle symptoms plus low sodium
- inflammatory or infectious-appearing illness with unexplained euvolemic hyponatremia

When those features cluster, consider whether you are looking at a CNS process rather than a standalone electrolyte issue.

## Take-home points

- Persistent hiccups can be a real clue, not just a nuisance symptom, especially when paired with headache or hyponatremia.
- Hyponatremia may present with vague symptoms like malaise, anorexia, dizziness, and hiccups before severe neurologic decline appears.
- CK elevation can occur in significant hyponatremia and is reasonable to check when patients have myalgias, weakness, or systemic inflammatory illness.
- SIADH is a mechanism; the key is finding the underlying cause, including CNS inflammation.
- Careful sodium correction remains essential even when the root problem is inflammatory neurologic disease.
- In selected cases, treating the underlying inflammatory condition can lead to improvement in both neurologic symptoms and sodium.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder to respect the “small” clues. Hiccups, CK elevation, and sodium 124 may look like separate problems, but together they can point to clinically important CNS inflammation with SIADH. The sodium matters, the symptoms matter, and the context matters even more.



# Colchicine Instead of Steroids? Reconsidering Treatment Thresholds in Mild Acute Neuro-Behçet’s

## Why this case grabs your attention

Acute neuro-Behçet’s disease usually pushes clinicians toward corticosteroids and, often, broader immunosuppression. So a case in which a patient with new systemic Behçet’s, aseptic meningitis, and SIADH improved with **colchicine monotherapy plus fluid restriction** is instantly interesting.

The twist here is not just the treatment response. It’s the diagnostic timing. This patient’s **systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s were recognized concurrently**, which is unusual enough on its own. Add in **hyponatraemia from SIADH**, persistent hiccups, headache, and a normal brain MRI, and you get a presentation that could easily be misread as infection, nonspecific viral illness, or “just” systemic inflammation.

This is a useful case because it sits right at the edge of standard teaching: if acute neuro-Behçet’s is usually a steroid-responsive inflammatory CNS syndrome, what do we do when the presentation is relatively mild, imaging is unrevealing, and the patient improves without steroids?

## The clinical setup

The patient was a man in his 60s who came in with 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, arthralgia, and oral aphthae. Exam showed oral ulcers and folliculitis-like skin lesions; later he developed a painful scrotal ulcer. He was clinically euvolemic but had marked hyponatraemia.

The early workup showed:

- Na 124 mmol/L
- Serum osmolality low
- Urine osmolality high
- Urine sodium high
- ADH elevated
- TSH and cortisol normal

That pattern fits **SIADH**, and the euvolemic bedside exam backed it up.

Then the neurologic picture evolved. His headache and vertigo worsened. MRI was normal, but CSF showed:

- Mildly elevated opening pressure
- Elevated protein
- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- Negative infectious studies

So this looked like **aseptic meningitis**, and in the context of oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and folliculitis-like rash, Behçet’s jumped to the top of the list. HLA-B51 was positive, and he met criteria for definite Behçet’s disease.

## Why the diagnosis was tricky

This is a classic reminder that **acute neuro-Behçet’s can be MRI-negative early**, especially when the phenotype is more meningeal than parenchymal. Many clinicians are trained to think of neuro-Behçet’s as a brainstem/parenchymal syndrome with focal deficits, but that’s not the only face it wears.

In this case:

- There were **no focal deficits**
- No uveitis
- No obvious MRI lesion
- CSF was inflammatory but nonspecific
- The genital ulcer appeared only later

That means the diagnosis was made by assembling the whole syndrome over time rather than by any single “slam dunk” test.

## The management question: why no steroids?

Standard teaching still matters here: **corticosteroids are the mainstay of acute neuro-Behçet’s**, particularly when there is parenchymal disease, significant neurologic deficit, or severe inflammation.

But this case didn’t follow the usual playbook. The team used:

- Empiric cefepime initially, given fever and possible infection
- Empiric acyclovir when CNS infection remained in the differential
- Then, after Behçet’s became clear, **colchicine**
- For SIADH, **fluid restriction <800 mL/day** and close sodium monitoring

No steroids. No cyclophosphamide. No biologics.

And the patient improved:

- Fever resolved shortly after colchicine
- Headache/vertigo/hiccups improved over 2 weeks
- Sodium normalized by day 16
- ADH fell
- CK normalized
- He had no recurrence over long follow-up on colchicine

That’s compelling, but it does **not** prove colchicine is sufficient for acute neuro-Behçet’s in general. What it does suggest is that there may be a subgroup with **milder meningeal presentations** who improve without immediate steroids.

## What might explain the improvement?

There are a few plausible interpretations, and the honest answer is that the case can’t separate them cleanly.

First, this could have been a genuinely **mild, self-limited meningoencephalitic form** of neuro-Behçet’s. Some cases do improve spontaneously.

Second, colchicine may have had a meaningful anti-inflammatory effect. It’s classically used for mucocutaneous Behçet’s, but it also suppresses neutrophil activation and inflammatory cytokine signaling. That said, colchicine is **not established as effective monotherapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s**.

Third, the SIADH may have improved simply because the CNS inflammatory trigger resolved and because fluid restriction was effective.

The case authors also discuss a possible IL-6–mediated mechanism linking CNS inflammation and ADH dysregulation, which is biologically plausible. But in this patient, IL-6 was not measured, so that remains hypothesis rather than proof.

## How much should we generalize this?

Not much, at least not yet.

This case is interesting because it challenges habit, but it does not overturn current practice. The usual caution flags for acute neuro-Behçet’s still apply:

- Parenchymal brainstem involvement
- Focal neurologic deficits
- Progressive neurologic decline
- Significant MRI abnormalities
- Severe or refractory inflammation

Those patients are not good candidates for “colchicine only” management based on a single case report.

What this case does support is a more nuanced thought process:

- Not every Behçet’s CNS presentation is fulminant
- Not every inflammatory meningeal syndrome with hyponatraemia is infection
- Some patients may improve with conservative management if the presentation is mild and diagnostic certainty is high
- Close monitoring is essential if you choose not to use steroids

## Clinical pearls

A few practical points stand out:

- **Behçet’s can present concurrently with neuro involvement**, not just years later.
- **Normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s**, especially early or meningeal-predominant disease.
- **Hyponatraemia with high urine osmolality and high urine sodium in a euvolemic patient** should make you think of SIADH.
- In a patient with fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and aseptic CSF, **Behçet’s belongs on the differential**.
- **Persistent hiccups** can be a clue to significant hyponatraemia or CNS inflammation.
- **Colchicine is excellent for mucocutaneous Behçet’s**, but evidence for acute neuro-Behçet’s monotherapy is weak.

## Take-home points

- Acute neuro-Behçet’s may present as **aseptic meningitis with a normal MRI**, especially early.
- SIADH can accompany CNS inflammation in Behçet’s and may be missed if hyponatraemia is not pursued carefully.
- This case suggests that **selected mild presentations** may improve with colchicine and supportive care alone, but this should not replace steroids as standard therapy for more typical acute neuro-Behçet’s.
- A single case can expand our thinking, but it should not rewrite the treatment algorithm.

## Bottom line

This is a great reminder that Behçet’s is a protean disease and that neurologic symptoms can show up at diagnosis, not just in the chronic phase. It also raises a clinically awkward but important question: **must every acute neuro-Behçet’s patient get steroids immediately?**

The safest answer today is: **usually yes for established or significant neuro-Behçet’s, but maybe not always for very mild meningeal-predominant disease—if diagnosis is secure, the patient is stable, and you are monitoring closely.**

That said, this case should prompt curiosity, not complacency. In neuro-Behçet’s, conservative management may occasionally work—but we still need better evidence before calling it a strategy.
