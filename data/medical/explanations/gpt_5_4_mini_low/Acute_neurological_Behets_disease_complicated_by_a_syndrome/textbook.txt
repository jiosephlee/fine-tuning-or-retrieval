Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Behçet’s Disease, Neuro-Behçet’s Disease, and SIADH: Core Concepts for This Case

Behçet’s disease is a chronic, relapsing, multisystem inflammatory disorder, often described as a vasculitis because it can involve vessels of many sizes and affect several organ systems. The classic clinical picture includes recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, skin lesions, and ocular inflammation, but patients may also develop joint, vascular, gastrointestinal, and neurologic disease. In practice, the diagnosis is clinical rather than based on a single confirmatory laboratory test. That is why recognizing the pattern of mucocutaneous disease in this patient was central to the diagnostic reasoning.

The mucocutaneous lesions in this case fit well with typical Behçet’s disease. Recurrent oral aphthae are the most common and often earliest manifestation. Genital ulcers are particularly important because they are more specific than oral ulcers and strongly support the diagnosis when present. Skin findings such as folliculitis-like or papulopustular lesions are also characteristic. In the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, points are assigned to key findings, and a score of 4 or more supports a diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease. Oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis, ocular lesions, and skin lesions each contribute to the score, with genital ulcers and oral ulcers carrying major diagnostic weight. In this patient, oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and folliculitis-like rash were enough to establish Behçet’s disease clinically.

Neurological involvement, called neuro-Behçet’s disease, occurs in a minority of patients with Behçet’s disease and usually appears years after the initial diagnosis. Therefore, simultaneous recognition of systemic Behçet’s disease and neuro-Behçet’s disease at first presentation is unusual. Neuro-Behçet’s disease has two broad clinical patterns. The more typical and clinically important form is acute parenchymal disease, which behaves like a meningoencephalitic inflammatory syndrome and often involves the brainstem, diencephalon, or basal ganglia. A second pattern is non-parenchymal disease, including cerebral venous thrombosis and other vascular manifestations. Acute parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease can present with fever, headache, vertigo, meningitic symptoms, behavioral change, confusion, or focal neurologic deficits. In this case, the patient’s fever, headache, vertigo, and CSF pleocytosis fit an acute inflammatory CNS process even though he did not have frank focal weakness or cranial nerve palsy.

A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. MRI is useful because it often shows lesions in the brainstem, thalamus, basal ganglia, or other parenchymal sites, but early disease or a meningitic presentation may have no obvious radiologic abnormality. Thus, diagnosis still depends heavily on the clinical context and cerebrospinal fluid findings. In this patient, MRI was unrevealing, but CSF showed an inflammatory pattern with elevated protein and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, which supported aseptic meningitis and made acute neuro-Behçet’s disease plausible after infection and other causes were excluded.

The other key concept in this case is the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, or SIADH, which explains the patient’s hyponatremia. Normally, antidiuretic hormone, also called vasopressin, is released from the hypothalamus and posterior pituitary when serum osmolality rises or when effective circulating volume falls. ADH acts on the kidney’s collecting ducts to increase free water reabsorption, thereby concentrating the urine and lowering plasma osmolality. In SIADH, ADH secretion is inappropriate for the patient’s osmolality and volume status, so water retention occurs despite hyponatremia. The result is euvolemic hypo-osmolar hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urinary sodium. This pattern was present in the case: low serum sodium, low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, high urine sodium, and clinically euvolemic status.

The diagnosis of SIADH is made by integrating laboratory data with bedside assessment. Important features include hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, euvolemia, inappropriately high urine osmolality, and urinary sodium that is not low, along with exclusion of other causes such as hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, renal failure, and diuretic use. That is why thyroid function and cortisol were checked in this patient. Their normal results made endocrine mimics less likely and strengthened the SIADH diagnosis. In addition, the elevated measured ADH level was consistent with inappropriate secretion, although ADH testing is not always required in routine practice.

SIADH is commonly triggered by disorders affecting the central nervous system, the lungs, malignancy, or medications. CNS inflammation is a particularly relevant cause in this case because meningitis, encephalitis, and other inflammatory brain disorders can stimulate abnormal ADH release. In neuro-inflammatory states, cytokines may contribute to dysregulated hypothalamic-pituitary signaling. Interleukin-6 has been proposed as one mediator linking CNS inflammation to SIADH, because it can promote ADH release and is often elevated in inflammatory neurologic conditions. In acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, this offers a biologically plausible explanation for concurrent hyponatremia, although the association is still not proven as a direct disease-specific mechanism.

Understanding these core concepts helps explain the diagnostic sequence in the case. The mucocutaneous findings established systemic Behçet’s disease, while the CNS symptoms plus inflammatory CSF supported acute neuro-Behçet’s disease despite a normal MRI. The biochemical pattern of euvolemic hypo-osmolar hyponatremia confirmed SIADH. Taken together, the case illustrates that when a patient with Behçet-like mucocutaneous lesions develops headache, fever, vertigo, or other nonspecific neurologic symptoms along with hyponatremia, clinicians should consider acute neuro-Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH.

## Initial Presentation and Problem Representation

This patient’s early presentation was best understood as a febrile inflammatory syndrome with simultaneous mucocutaneous, neurologic, and electrolyte abnormalities. He came to the emergency department with only 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, poor appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia of the shoulders and ankles, along with oral aphthae. Although each symptom could be nonspecific in isolation, their clustering suggested a systemic process rather than a simple self-limited viral illness. The presence of headache and persistent hiccups was especially important because both can accompany central nervous system inflammation or severe metabolic disturbance, and in this case they later proved to be clues to neuroinflammatory disease complicated by hyponatraemia.

His background history helped frame the differential but did not immediately point to a single cause. He had untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus with poor glycaemic control, which increased concern for infection and could complicate recovery, but it did not itself explain the entire syndrome. Social and exposure history were unrevealing: he did not smoke, had only occasional alcohol use, had no recent travel, no animal exposure, and no reported sick contacts. Family history was negative for Behçet’s disease, which is useful but not exclusionary because most cases are sporadic. In short, the history lowered suspicion for certain infectious or environmental exposures, while leaving a broad inflammatory, infectious, and autoimmune differential open.

The physical examination was notable for fever, tachycardia, oral aphthae, and folliculitis-like lesions on the face and trunk. These skin and mucosal findings were diagnostically meaningful because they pointed toward a characteristic Behçet phenotype rather than a nonspecific fever syndrome. Oral aphthous ulcers are common in many disorders, but when they occur with papulopustular or folliculitis-like eruptions and later genital ulceration, they become highly suggestive of Behçet’s disease. At the initial visit, however, the phenotype was still incomplete, so the diagnosis could not yet be made confidently. The lack of cardiopulmonary or abdominal abnormalities also helped narrow attention toward a systemic inflammatory process affecting the mucocutaneous and nervous systems.

Neurologically, he was alert and fatigued but had no focal weakness, no cranial nerve deficits, and no meningismus. This absence of dramatic neurological signs is common in early or mild central nervous system involvement and should not be reassuring enough to dismiss neuroinflammation when headache is accompanied by systemic inflammatory features. The clinicians also assessed him as euvolemic, which was a critical bedside observation because it focused attention on a euvolemic hyponatraemia syndrome rather than hypovolaemic salt loss or hypervolaemic fluid overload. In a patient with CNS symptoms, euvolemic hyponatraemia immediately raises the possibility of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, especially when paired with low serum osmolality and inappropriately concentrated urine.

The laboratory profile reinforced the impression of an active inflammatory illness. Markedly elevated CRP and ESR indicated substantial systemic inflammation, while the mildly elevated transaminases and elevated creatine kinase suggested tissue injury or inflammatory myositis/rhabdomyolysis rather than isolated mucosal disease. The hyponatraemia was significant and clinically relevant, especially because it occurred with low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, and high urine sodium, a pattern consistent with impaired free-water excretion. In the context of normal renal function and euvolemic examination, these findings strongly supported SIADH. Normal thyroid and cortisol values were important because hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency are key alternative causes of euvolemic hyponatraemia that must be excluded before diagnosing SIADH.

The elevated creatine kinase deserves particular attention because it is easy to overlook in a febrile patient with myalgias or arthralgia. In this case, CK elevation may have reflected inflammatory muscle injury, but severe hyponatraemia itself can also contribute to rhabdomyolysis. This association is clinically important because rhabdomyolysis can worsen metabolic instability and may signal that hyponatraemia is more severe or more physiologically significant than the sodium number alone suggests. Thus, the CK elevation was not merely an incidental laboratory abnormality; it fit the broader picture of systemic inflammation plus electrolyte disturbance.

Taken together, the initial problem representation was: a middle-aged man with acute fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, arthralgia, oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, marked inflammatory markers, and euvolemic hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine. This is a classic example of how clinicians should synthesize scattered findings into a single working syndrome rather than pursue each abnormality separately. At this stage, the main priorities were stabilization, careful monitoring of sodium, and broad diagnostic triage for CNS infection, systemic inflammatory disease, and other causes of aseptic meningoencephalitis. The early combination of mucocutaneous lesions and neurological symptoms was the key clue that eventually led to recognition of Behçet’s disease with acute neuroinvolvement, while the hyponatraemia and hiccups suggested a concurrent endocrine complication requiring parallel evaluation.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning from Broad Differential to Definite Diagnosis

The patient’s presentation was initially nonspecific: fever, headache, malaise, arthralgia, oral aphthae, and marked hyponatraemia. In that setting, the clinicians appropriately began with a broad differential that included central nervous system infection, a systemic inflammatory disorder, malignancy, and autoimmune disease. This is a common and necessary approach when constitutional symptoms and neurological complaints occur together, because early neuroinflammatory disorders, infections, and even some cancers can look similar before targeted testing clarifies the diagnosis.

A key early problem was the hyponatraemia, which required its own structured evaluation. The patient had low serum sodium with low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, and elevated urinary sodium, all of which indicated impaired free-water excretion rather than true sodium loss. He was also clinically euvolemic, with normal renal function, and endocrine testing excluded hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency. Together, these findings fit the diagnostic pattern of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, or SIADH. The elevated plasma ADH level supported this impression, although in practice SIADH is diagnosed primarily from the clinical and biochemical pattern rather than ADH measurement alone. The absence of edema, dehydration, kidney injury, thyroid dysfunction, or cortisol deficiency made alternative causes of hyponatraemia less likely.

As the headache and vertigo worsened, lumbar puncture became important because the evolving neurological symptoms raised concern for meningoencephalitis or another inflammatory process affecting the central nervous system. The cerebrospinal fluid showed mildly elevated opening pressure, increased protein, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis. This profile is typical of aseptic meningitis and argues against pyogenic bacterial meningitis, which usually produces a neutrophil-predominant CSF response and more dramatic inflammatory findings. The normal brain MRI did not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. This is an important teaching point: early acute neuro-Behçet’s disease may present as aseptic meningitis without a visible parenchymal lesion, and MRI can be normal, especially early in the course. Thus, imaging is helpful but not definitive, and a normal scan should not stop further evaluation when the clinical picture remains concerning.

Infectious causes were then systematically excluded. CSF studies were negative for bacterial, mycobacterial, herpes simplex virus, varicella zoster virus, and cryptococcal infection, and there was no supportive evidence on culture or additional testing. This step was critical because fever, headache, and CSF pleocytosis can easily be mistaken for infection, and immunosuppressive therapy would be inappropriate until major infectious etiologies were ruled out. The team also considered CNS malignancy, especially because constitutional symptoms and elevated inflammatory markers can accompany lymphoma. However, the MRI was unremarkable, CSF cytology showed no malignant cells, and the mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor level was nonspecific and insufficient to support a neoplastic diagnosis.

Other inflammatory and autoimmune conditions, including sarcoidosis, were also considered but were not supported by the available data. In neurosarcoidosis, MRI and CSF findings can be variable, but the patient lacked characteristic systemic features, and serum ACE and lysozyme were not suggestive. More broadly, autoimmune serologies and the rest of the exclusionary workup did not reveal an alternative connective tissue disease. The clinicians’ use of these tests reflects an exclusion-based strategy: they were not ordering indiscriminate studies, but rather checking plausible mimics of inflammatory CNS disease before settling on a more specific diagnosis.

The diagnostic pivot came when a painful genital ulcer appeared on day 5. Combined with recurrent oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin lesions, this strongly suggested Behçet’s disease. This is a classic example of how the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease often depends on pattern recognition over time rather than on a single test. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease assign points to characteristic features, and this patient reached the threshold for definite disease with oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, and skin lesions. Genital ulceration is particularly helpful diagnostically because it is more specific for Behçet’s disease than oral ulcers alone. Once the mucocutaneous syndrome was recognized, the neurological syndrome could be reinterpreted as acute neuro-Behçet’s disease rather than an unrelated aseptic meningitis.

HLA-B51 positivity provided supportive background information but did not establish the diagnosis. This is consistent with current diagnostic standards: HLA-B51 is associated with Behçet’s disease epidemiologically, but it is neither sensitive nor specific enough to be required for diagnosis. The case therefore illustrates the practical limitation of genetic markers in Behçet’s disease; clinical criteria remain central.

The final synthesis was that the patient had concurrent definite systemic Behçet’s disease, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease presenting as aseptic meningitis, and SIADH. The link between the neurological inflammation and hyponatraemia was inferred from the temporal association and the absence of other SIADH triggers such as medications, pulmonary disease, malignancy, or endocrine failure. Although the exact mechanism cannot be proven in a single case, the overall reasoning was sound: a patient with newly recognized Behçet’s disease developed CNS inflammatory symptoms and biochemical SIADH, and both improved as the inflammatory process was treated. This case therefore teaches disciplined diagnostic sequencing: first identify and exclude life-threatening mimics, then use evolving mucocutaneous findings and formal criteria to establish Behçet’s disease, and finally connect the neurological and electrolyte abnormalities into a single inflammatory syndrome.

## Management Decisions: Treating Inflammation, Neurological Disease, and Hyponatraemia

The treatment approach in this case reflects a common problem in early neuro-infectious and neuro-inflammatory presentations: before the diagnosis is secure, clinicians must treat potentially serious infection while continuing to refine the differential. This patient presented with fever, headache, elevated inflammatory markers, and later cerebrospinal fluid pleocytosis, so empiric antimicrobial therapy was reasonable at the outset. Cefepime was started after blood cultures were obtained because bacterial infection could not yet be excluded. When his neurological symptoms worsened and viral meningoencephalitis remained a concern, acyclovir was added empirically to cover herpes simplex virus and varicella zoster virus. Once CSF studies, cultures, and viral testing were negative, these agents were stopped, illustrating appropriate antimicrobial de-escalation after microbiological exclusion of infection.

After Behçet’s disease was recognized from the mucocutaneous findings, the team treated the underlying inflammatory disorder. In acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, corticosteroids are usually the standard first-line therapy because the neurological syndrome is driven by active inflammation, often with meningoencephalitic features. If disease is severe, relapsing, or steroid-refractory, additional immunosuppressive agents such as azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, or biologic therapies may be considered depending on the organ involvement and clinical severity. In this case, however, the clinicians chose colchicine monotherapy rather than corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. That is unusual for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, because colchicine is generally considered most useful for mucocutaneous disease, not for central nervous system involvement. The decision was clinically notable because the patient improved without escalation to steroid-based therapy, although spontaneous improvement cannot be completely excluded.

Colchicine’s therapeutic value in Behçet’s disease comes from its anti-inflammatory effects on neutrophils, which are central to the disease’s pathobiology. It inhibits neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion, and activation, and it also reduces production of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6. These mechanisms explain why colchicine is well established for recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, and some skin lesions in Behçet’s disease. In this patient, topical corticosteroid ointment was used for the oral aphthae, while genital ulcers and cutaneous lesions received local wound care with silver sulfadiazine cream. These local measures are supportive rather than disease-modifying, but they can relieve pain, promote healing, and reduce secondary infection risk while systemic inflammation is brought under control.

The hyponatraemia was managed conservatively as syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion. The key principle in SIADH is to restrict free water intake, monitor serum sodium closely, and avoid correction that is too rapid, because overly rapid normalization can cause osmotic demyelination syndrome. Fluid restriction to less than 800 mL/day was appropriate here because the patient was euvolemic, had hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine, and did not show severe neurologic compromise from sodium depletion. Hypertonic saline is generally reserved for patients with severe symptoms such as seizures, coma, or marked encephalopathy, or when sodium is dangerously low and symptomatic. Vasopressin receptor antagonists are typically considered in selected refractory euvolemic hyponatraemia cases, but they were unnecessary here because the sodium level improved with fluid restriction and treatment of the underlying inflammatory process.

The fact that sodium and antidiuretic hormone levels normalized after colchicine therapy supports the clinical impression that the SIADH was linked to the acute inflammatory episode, although this association remains inferential rather than proven. For trainees, the important lesson is that acute neuro-Behçet’s disease does not always require an immediate assumption that high-dose steroids or multi-agent immunosuppression are mandatory in every case, particularly when the diagnosis is evolving and the clinical picture is mild, improving, or atypical. At the same time, this case should not be taken as evidence that colchicine is standard monotherapy for neuro-Behçet’s disease. Rather, it shows that careful diagnostic reasoning, empiric infection coverage, conservative SIADH management, and thoughtful selection of anti-inflammatory therapy can lead to recovery in selected patients, while reminding clinicians that corticosteroids remain the usual benchmark treatment for acute neurological involvement.

## Outcome, Pathophysiologic Interpretation, and Clinical Lessons

After colchicine was started, the patient improved quickly. His fever resolved the next day, and his appetite began to recover. Over the following two weeks, the neurologic and systemic inflammatory symptoms that had brought him to care—vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and the mucocutaneous lesions—gradually improved. Serum sodium normalized by day 16, and the ADH level fell in parallel, supporting the interpretation that the hyponatremia had been driven by SIADH rather than by renal, adrenal, or thyroid dysfunction. The elevated creatine kinase also returned to normal, consistent with improvement in the inflammatory/metabolic stress that had likely contributed to muscle injury. He did not require hypertonic saline, vasopressin antagonists, corticosteroids, or other immunosuppressants.

The patient remained hospitalized for about a month, in part because recovery from severe hyponatremia and the associated decline in physical function required monitoring and rehabilitation. This is an important practical point: even when the underlying inflammatory process begins to improve, patients with acute neuroinflammatory illness and significant electrolyte derangements may need supportive care before they are safe for discharge. In this case, colchicine was continued long term, and over approximately four years of follow-up there was no recurrence of Behçet’s symptoms or neurological disease, suggesting durable disease control in this individual.

The authors propose that the SIADH may have been linked to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. This is biologically plausible. Inflammatory involvement of the brain or meninges can stimulate inappropriate ADH release, likely through cytokine-mediated pathways. Interleukin-6 is often discussed in this context because it can promote ADH secretion, and Behçet-related inflammation is known to be cytokine-driven. The markedly elevated CRP in this patient is therefore best understood as an indirect marker of a strong systemic inflammatory response, not as proof of a specific mechanism. It is reasonable to suspect a connection among acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, inflammatory cytokine release, and SIADH, but this remains an inference rather than a proven causal chain.

That uncertainty matters. A single case report can suggest a relationship, but it cannot establish causality. SIADH can accompany many central nervous system disorders, infections, malignancies, and drug exposures. Likewise, the improvement after colchicine may reflect treatment of Behçet’s inflammation, spontaneous resolution of an acute meningoencephalitic process, or both. The case is therefore most useful as a clinical signal: when SIADH appears alongside mucocutaneous Behçet-like lesions and neurologic symptoms, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease should enter the differential early.

This case also illustrates why concurrent diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease and neuro-Behçet’s disease is difficult. Neuro-Behçet’s disease usually follows established systemic disease by years, so clinicians may not initially connect fever, headache, and hyponatremia to Behçet’s disease when the characteristic genital ulcer or other strongly suggestive mucocutaneous finding has not yet appeared. In addition, MRI can be normal in acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, especially early, so a negative scan does not exclude the diagnosis. The CSF pattern here—mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, and negative infectious studies—fit an aseptic meningitis-like presentation, which is a classic but nonspecific pattern of inflammatory CNS disease. This is a key teaching point: neuro-Behçet’s disease may be present even when imaging is unrevealing and the CSF looks “nonbacterial.”

The case also highlights two symptoms that should not be dismissed. Persistent hiccups can be a clue to significant hyponatremia and CNS disease, particularly when they occur with headache or other inflammatory symptoms. Similarly, an elevated creatine kinase may reflect rhabdomyolysis or muscle injury associated with severe electrolyte disturbance and systemic inflammation. Neither finding is specific, but both should prompt careful review of serum sodium and the broader clinical context.

For practice, the main diagnostic pearl is straightforward: in a patient with Behçet-type mucocutaneous lesions who presents with fever or headache plus hyponatremia, consider acute neuro-Behçet’s disease with SIADH. Recognition matters because the neurologic and endocrine abnormalities may be linked by a single inflammatory process. The therapeutic lesson is more cautious. Colchicine may be a reasonable option in selected early or mild cases, particularly when mucocutaneous disease is prominent and corticosteroids are being deferred, but the evidence base is limited and standard treatment for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease still generally centers on corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive therapy. This case expands awareness, but it should not be taken as proof that colchicine monotherapy is sufficient for most patients.
