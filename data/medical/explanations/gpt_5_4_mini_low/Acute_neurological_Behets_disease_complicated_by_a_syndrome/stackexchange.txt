Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient presenting with recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and folliculitis-like skin lesions, what clinical criteria support a diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease, and what score would this patient meet under the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease?

Definite Behçet’s disease is a **clinical diagnosis** supported by the characteristic constellation of **recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and typical skin lesions**; uveitis and a positive pathergy test can further support the diagnosis, but they are not required if the clinical score is sufficient. In this case, the patient had **oral aphthae**, a **painful genital ulcer (scrotal ulcer/genital aphthosis)**, and **folliculitis-like skin lesions**, which is highly suggestive of Behçet’s disease.

Under the **International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD)**, the score is:
- **Oral aphthosis** = 2 points  
- **Genital aphthosis** = 2 points  
- **Skin lesions** = 1 point  

**Total = 5 points**, and **≥4 points classifies a patient as having Behçet’s disease**.

So, this patient meets criteria for **definite Behçet’s disease with an ICBD score of 5**.

### [Morning Report] How would you diagnose SIADH in this patient with hyponatraemia, and which specific laboratory findings distinguish SIADH from hypovolaemic or endocrine causes of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia?

To diagnose SIADH in this patient, you first confirm **hypotonic hyponatraemia** and then show that the kidneys are inappropriately retaining water in the setting of **clinical euvolaemia**, while excluding other common causes.

In this case, the key findings were:

- **Low serum sodium**: 124 mmol/L  
- **Low serum osmolality**: 267 mOsm/kg, confirming **hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia**
- **Inappropriately concentrated urine**: urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg
- **Urine sodium elevated**: 88 mmol/L
- **Clinical euvolaemia**: no oedema, no signs of volume depletion
- **Normal thyroid and adrenal function**: normal TSH, free T4, and cortisol
- No diuretic use or renal failure, and no pulmonary, intracranial mass, or other obvious non-osmotic trigger on initial imaging

That pattern is classic for **SIADH**: serum is dilute, but urine remains concentrated and sodium-rich because ADH is not appropriately suppressed.

The laboratory findings that distinguish SIADH from **hypovolaemic hyponatraemia** are especially important. In **hypovolaemia**, the body appropriately conserves sodium and water, so you would expect:

- **Low urine sodium**, usually <20 mmol/L if the kidneys are responding normally
- Urine that may be concentrated, but in the setting of **clinical volume depletion**  
- Often signs such as tachycardia, orthostasis, dry mucous membranes, or elevated BUN/creatinine ratio

By contrast, in SIADH, the patient is **euvolaemic** and the urine sodium is **not low**, because natriuresis continues despite hyponatraemia.

The endocrine causes must also be excluded, because **adrenal insufficiency** and **hypothyroidism** can produce a similar biochemical picture. In this case, they were ruled out by:

- **Normal cortisol**: 42 µg/dL, arguing strongly against adrenal insufficiency
- **Normal TSH and free T4**, arguing against hypothyroidism

So, in practical terms, the diagnosis of SIADH rests on this triad: **hypotonic hyponatraemia, inappropriately concentrated urine with high urine sodium, and exclusion of hypovolaemia, thyroid disease, and adrenal insufficiency**. In this case, the patient’s euvolaemic exam plus the laboratory profile supported SIADH, likely in the setting of acute CNS inflammation from neuro-Behçet’s disease.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient with newly recognized Behçet’s disease develops fever, headache, vertigo, and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis but has a normal brain MRI, how should acute neuro-Behçet’s disease be considered in the differential, and why does a normal MRI not exclude the diagnosis?

Acute neuro-Behçet’s disease should remain high on the differential when a patient with newly recognized Behçet’s disease develops fever, headache, vertigo, and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, even if the brain MRI is normal. In this case, the key clue was the combination of a systemic Behçet phenotype—oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and folliculitis-like skin lesions—with CNS inflammatory findings on CSF: elevated protein and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, after infectious studies were negative. That pattern is consistent with acute neuro-Behçet’s, which often presents as a self-limited meningoencephalitic syndrome and may occur at the same time Behçet’s disease is first recognized.

A normal MRI does not exclude the diagnosis because acute neuro-Behçet’s can present as aseptic meningitis without visible parenchymal lesions, especially early in the course. MRI is most helpful when it shows the typical inflammatory lesions of the brainstem, diencephalon, or other parenchymal sites, but these abnormalities are not required for diagnosis. As this case illustrates, neurologic symptoms plus inflammatory CSF in the setting of definite Behçet’s disease are sufficient to strongly support acute neuro-Behçet’s once infection, malignancy, and other inflammatory disorders have been excluded.

### [Morning Report] What infectious, malignant, and inflammatory conditions should be excluded before diagnosing acute neuro-Behçet’s disease in a patient with aseptic meningitis and hyponatraemia, and which features in this case argued against each of them?

Before labeling this as acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, the key principle is to exclude other causes of aseptic meningitis and hyponatraemia—especially infection, malignancy, and systemic inflammatory disease—because the diagnosis of neuro-Behçet’s is clinical and one of exclusion.

**Infectious causes** to rule out include **viral meningoencephalitis** (particularly HSV and VZV), **bacterial meningitis**, and **tuberculous meningitis**, as well as **fungal infection such as cryptococcosis**. In this case, several features argued against infection: the cerebrospinal fluid showed **mild lymphocytic pleocytosis rather than neutrophilic predominance**, which is not typical of bacterial meningitis; **CSF cultures were negative**; **HSV and VZV testing was negative**; **acid-fast stain and tuberculosis DNA testing were negative**; and **cryptococcal antigen was negative**. The MRI was also unrevealing for a focal infectious lesion, and the patient lacked a clear exposure history or immunosuppression.

**Malignant causes** that should be excluded include **primary CNS lymphoma or leptomeningeal malignancy**. Here, the arguments against malignancy were that **brain MRI showed no mass lesion or abnormal parenchymal enhancement**, **CSF cytology showed no malignant cells**, and the **mildly elevated soluble IL-2 receptor level was nonspecific** and insufficient to support lymphoma in the absence of radiologic or cytologic evidence.

**Inflammatory causes** to consider include **sarcoidosis**, other connective tissue diseases, and vasculitic or autoimmune meningoencephalitides. In this patient, sarcoidosis was made less likely by **normal ACE and lysozyme levels**, the **absence of typical systemic features**, and the lack of supportive imaging or CSF findings. More broadly, the diagnosis was pushed toward Behçet’s by the later appearance of **genital ulceration**, together with **oral aphthae** and **folliculitis-like skin lesions**, which met the clinical criteria for definite Behçet’s disease and provided the unifying explanation for the aseptic meningitis.

So, in a patient like this, the diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s rests on: **aseptic meningitis on CSF, negative infectious and malignant workup, and concurrent mucocutaneous Behçet’s features**.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed pathophysiologic link between acute neuro-Behçet’s disease and SIADH, and how might IL-6-mediated neuroinflammation contribute to inappropriate ADH secretion?

The proposed link is that **acute neuro-Behçet’s disease causes central nervous system inflammation, and that inflammatory state—especially IL-6–driven neuroinflammation—can stimulate inappropriate ADH release**, leading to SIADH.

In this case, the patient had **aseptic meningitis on CSF**, elevated inflammatory markers, and **hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated ADH**, all consistent with SIADH occurring in parallel with acute neuro-BD. The authors suggest that **aberrant monocyte activation in Behçet’s disease promotes IL-6 production**, and IL-6 has been shown in other neuroinflammatory disorders to **increase ADH secretion**. Mechanistically, IL-6 may act at the hypothalamus and posterior pituitary to dysregulate osmoregulation and ADH release, so the patient continues to secrete ADH despite hypo-osmolality.

So, the teaching point is: **in acute neuro-Behçet’s, SIADH may reflect cytokine-mediated CNS inflammation rather than a structural hypothalamic lesion**. In this report, the normal MRI did not exclude the diagnosis, and the concurrent improvement in neurologic symptoms, mucocutaneous disease, and sodium after colchicine supports an inflammatory rather than a primary endocrine cause.

### [Clinical Pearls] Persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase were notable findings in this case; how can severe hyponatraemia from SIADH explain these manifestations, and why are they important bedside clues?

Severe hyponatraemia from SIADH can present with symptoms that seem nonspecific until you recognize them as metabolic clues. In this case, the persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase were both important bedside signals that the sodium abnormality was clinically significant, not merely an incidental lab finding.

Hiccups can occur with profound hyponatraemia because low serum sodium may irritate the central hiccup reflex arc, especially when the cause is a CNS inflammatory process such as acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. In other words, the hiccups are not just a GI complaint; in a patient with headache, fever, and hyponatraemia, they should prompt consideration of brainstem or meningeal involvement and should raise suspicion for SIADH related to neuroinflammation.

The elevated creatine kinase likely reflected hyponatraemia-associated muscle injury, including mild rhabdomyolysis. Severe hypotonicity can injure skeletal muscle through cellular swelling and membrane dysfunction, and CK elevation may be the only early clue before frank weakness or myoglobinuria appears. Importantly, this patient had no focal weakness, so the CK rise could easily have been missed if it were not checked.

These findings matter because they tell you that the hyponatraemia is symptomatic and potentially dangerous, and they support the diagnosis of SIADH in the setting of active CNS inflammation. In this case, the resolution of hiccups, normalization of sodium, and fall in CK after treatment of the underlying Behçet’s inflammation strongly reinforce that link.

### [Management] What is the usual first-line treatment for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, and in what clinical context might conservative management with colchicine monotherapy be considered despite the limited evidence for its efficacy in neuro-Behçet’s disease?

The usual **first-line treatment for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease is systemic corticosteroid therapy**, typically high-dose glucocorticoids, because acute neuro-Behçet’s is an inflammatory CNS syndrome and most patients improve with prompt steroid treatment. In more severe, relapsing, or steroid-refractory disease, a steroid-sparing immunosuppressant is often added.

**Colchicine monotherapy is not standard therapy for neuro-Behçet’s**, and the evidence for its efficacy in CNS disease is limited. However, as illustrated by this case, it may be considered in a **selected patient with a mild, self-limited acute meningoencephalitic presentation**, especially when:

- the diagnosis is secure but the neurological syndrome is relatively mild,
- MRI is normal or lacks parenchymal lesions,
- there are prominent **mucocutaneous Behçet manifestations** that colchicine is known to help,
- and **corticosteroids are undesirable or contraindicated** because of comorbidity or concern for adverse effects.

In this report, the patient’s neuro-Behçet’s presented as **aseptic meningitis with headache, vertigo, and hyponatremia from SIADH**, without focal deficits or MRI lesions, and he improved on **colchicine alone** with supportive management of SIADH. That outcome is notable, but it should be viewed as an exception rather than evidence to replace corticosteroids as standard first-line therapy.

### [Management] How should SIADH be managed safely in a patient with acute neuroinflammatory disease, and why were fluid restriction and close sodium monitoring appropriate in this case instead of hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists?

In this patient, **SIADH was managed appropriately with conservative therapy: fluid restriction and close serum sodium monitoring**, because his hyponatremia was **euvolemic, moderate (124 mmol/L), and not accompanied by seizures, coma, or other signs of life-threatening hyponatremic encephalopathy**. In SIADH, the primary problem is **inappropriately retained free water**, so the safest first step is to **limit water intake** while treating the underlying cause—in this case, the acute neuroinflammatory process of neuro-Behçet’s disease. As the inflammation improved, his ADH level fell and the sodium normalized.

The reason **hypertonic saline was not necessary** is that it is generally reserved for **severe or rapidly evolving symptomatic hyponatremia**—for example, seizures, marked confusion, or impending herniation. In a neurologically inflamed patient, the danger is not only the low sodium itself but also **overly rapid correction**, which can precipitate **osmotic demyelination syndrome**. Because this patient was alert and clinically stable, careful correction with restriction rather than immediate hypertonic therapy was the safer choice.

Likewise, **vasopressin receptor antagonists** were unnecessary. Vaptans can cause **unpredictably rapid aquaresis and overcorrection**, especially when the SIADH is likely **transient and inflammation-driven**, as in this case. They are therefore not first-line in an acutely ill patient whose sodium is only moderately reduced and who can be managed with restriction and observation.

So the key principle is: **treat the underlying CNS inflammation, restrict free water, and monitor sodium frequently to avoid both persistent hyponatremia and overly rapid correction**. That approach fit this case precisely, and it worked—his sodium normalized as the neuro-Behçet’s activity improved.
