Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Morning Report] How would you diagnose SIADH in this patient with hyponatraemia, and which specific laboratory findings distinguish SIADH from hypovolaemic or endocrine causes of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia?

To diagnose SIADH in this patient, you first confirm **hypotonic hyponatraemia** and then show that the kidneys are inappropriately retaining water in the setting of **clinical euvolaemia**, while excluding other common causes.

In this case, the key findings were:

- **Low serum sodium**: 124 mmol/L  
- **Low serum osmolality**: 267 mOsm/kg, confirming **hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia**
- **Inappropriately concentrated urine**: urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg
- **Urine sodium elevated**: 88 mmol/L
- **Clinical euvolaemia**: no oedema, no signs of volume depletion
- **Normal thyroid and adrenal function**: normal TSH, free T4, and cortisol
- No diuretic use or renal failure, and no pulmonary, intracranial mass, or other obvious non-osmotic trigger on initial imaging

That pattern is classic for **SIADH**: serum is dilute, but urine remains concentrated and sodium-rich because ADH is not appropriately suppressed.

The laboratory findings that distinguish SIADH from **hypovolaemic hyponatraemia** are especially important. In **hypovolaemia**, the body appropriately conserves sodium and water, so you would expect:

- **Low urine sodium**, usually <20 mmol/L if the kidneys are responding normally
- Urine that may be concentrated, but in the setting of **clinical volume depletion**  
- Often signs such as tachycardia, orthostasis, dry mucous membranes, or elevated BUN/creatinine ratio

By contrast, in SIADH, the patient is **euvolaemic** and the urine sodium is **not low**, because natriuresis continues despite hyponatraemia.

The endocrine causes must also be excluded, because **adrenal insufficiency** and **hypothyroidism** can produce a similar biochemical picture. In this case, they were ruled out by:

- **Normal cortisol**: 42 µg/dL, arguing strongly against adrenal insufficiency
- **Normal TSH and free T4**, arguing against hypothyroidism

So, in practical terms, the diagnosis of SIADH rests on this triad: **hypotonic hyponatraemia, inappropriately concentrated urine with high urine sodium, and exclusion of hypovolaemia, thyroid disease, and adrenal insufficiency**. In this case, the patient’s euvolaemic exam plus the laboratory profile supported SIADH, likely in the setting of acute CNS inflammation from neuro-Behçet’s disease.
