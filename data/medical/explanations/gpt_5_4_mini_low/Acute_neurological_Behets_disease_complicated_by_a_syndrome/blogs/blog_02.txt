Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Hyponatremia With a Rash and a Headache: SIADH as a Clue to CNS Inflammation

## Why this case is worth your attention

Hyponatremia is common. Headache is common. Rash is common. But put them together in a febrile patient with oral ulcers and a later-appearing genital ulcer, and you should start thinking beyond “viral syndrome” or “dehydration.”

This case is a good reminder that **SIADH can be a clue to central nervous system inflammation**, not just a lab abnormality to chase in isolation. Here, a man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, malaise, hiccups, arthralgia, mucocutaneous lesions, and **hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia**. That combination ultimately led to a diagnosis of **systemic Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease**, complicated by SIADH.

Even more interesting: his neurologic and sodium abnormalities improved with **colchicine monotherapy**, without corticosteroids.

## Behçet’s disease: when the diagnosis is sitting on the skin and mucosa

Behçet’s disease is a multisystem inflammatory vasculitis classically associated with recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, skin lesions, and uveitis. Neurologic involvement is less common, and acute neuro-Behçet’s typically shows up later in the disease course. So when Behçet’s and neurologic disease are diagnosed at the same time, that’s unusual.

In this patient, the key clue was the evolving mucocutaneous picture:

- oral aphthae on presentation
- folliculitis-like rash on the face and trunk
- painful scrotal ulcer appearing later

That combination, plus HLA-B51 positivity, supported **definite Behçet’s disease** by international criteria. The CNS syndrome was then evaluated in that context.

## The hyponatremia workup: what made this SIADH?

This was not a “just give saline and see what happens” scenario. The hyponatremia was worked up as **hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia**, and the data fit SIADH well:

- serum sodium: 124 mmol/L
- low serum osmolality: 267 mOsm/kg
- high urine osmolality: 900 mOsm/kg
- urine sodium: 88 mmol/L
- elevated ADH
- normal thyroid function
- normal cortisol
- clinically euvolemic exam

That is the classic SIADH pattern: **inappropriately concentrated urine despite hypotonic plasma**, in a patient who appears euvolemic and in whom adrenal, thyroid, renal, and volume causes have been excluded.

The bedside pearl here is simple: if the patient has hyponatremia plus headache or other CNS symptoms, don’t stop at the sodium value. **Ask what is driving ADH release.**

## CNS inflammation is a known trigger for SIADH

SIADH is commonly associated with:

- CNS infection or inflammation
- pulmonary disease
- malignancy
- medications

This case adds an important inflammatory neurologic diagnosis to that list: **acute neuro-Behçet’s disease**.

The proposed mechanism is biologically plausible. Neuroinflammation may stimulate ADH release, and inflammatory cytokines—especially **interleukin-6**—have been implicated in this pathway. Behçet’s disease is a neutrophilic inflammatory disorder, and IL-6 activity has been described in neuro-Behçet’s. In this case, IL-6 was not measured, so causality can’t be proven. But the association between active CNS inflammation and SIADH is hard to ignore.

So the likely clinical logic is:

**neuroinflammation → cytokine surge → inappropriate ADH secretion → hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia**

That’s not unique to Behçet’s, but it’s a useful framework.

## Don’t let a normal MRI fool you

One of the trickiest parts of this case was that **brain MRI was normal**. That doesn’t exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s.

The patient had:

- headache
- vertigo
- fever
- CSF with elevated protein and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- negative infectious studies

That’s enough to make you think about **aseptic meningitis / meningoencephalitis**, especially once the mucocutaneous findings and genital ulcer emerged.

The lesson: in suspected neuroinflammatory disease, **CSF can be more revealing than MRI early on**. A normal scan should not end the diagnostic process if the clinical picture is still pointing to CNS inflammation.

## Differential diagnosis: stay broad, then narrow hard

This case was initially treated like a possible CNS infection, which is appropriate. Fever + headache + hyponatremia is not Behçet’s until proven otherwise.

The team considered and evaluated for:

- HSV and VZV encephalitis
- bacterial meningitis
- tuberculous meningitis
- malignancy
- sarcoidosis
- autoimmune disease

That’s the right kind of broad early differential. But the key is not just ordering tests; it’s knowing what result would actually move the needle.

A mild rise in soluble IL-2 receptor or a normal ACE level may be interesting, but in isolation they rarely solve the case. In contrast, the **clinical evolution** of oral ulcers, skin lesions, and genital aphthosis did.

## Hiccuping your way to the diagnosis

Persistent hiccups were part of the presentation. That’s a useful bedside reminder: hiccups can be more than a nuisance symptom, especially when they coexist with neurologic complaints and electrolyte disturbances.

Severe hyponatremia can present with:

- hiccups
- confusion
- gait instability
- nausea
- headache
- weakness

In this case, hiccups may have been a small clue pointing toward a systemic process with CNS involvement and electrolyte derangement.

## Treatment: conservative sodium management, anti-inflammatory therapy, and close monitoring

The patient was managed conservatively for SIADH with:

- fluid restriction to less than 800 mL/day
- daily sodium monitoring
- avoidance of rapid correction

He did **not** require hypertonic saline or a vasopressin receptor antagonist.

For Behçet’s disease and neuro-Behçet’s, colchicine was started after the diagnosis became clear. The patient’s fever improved the next day, and over the following two weeks his:

- headache/vertigo improved
- hiccups improved
- malaise improved
- mucocutaneous lesions improved
- sodium normalized
- ADH level fell

No corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were used.

That’s notable, but don’t overgeneralize it. Steroids remain a mainstay for many cases of acute neuro-Behçet’s. This case suggests colchicine may have been enough here, but spontaneous improvement and disease heterogeneity are real possibilities.

## What this case teaches at the bedside

If you’re staring at a patient with hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia, the essential question is not just “is this SIADH?” It’s “**why is ADH turned on?**”

When headache, fever, or other neurologic symptoms are part of the story, think about CNS inflammation. And if oral ulcers, genital ulcers, or a folliculitis-like rash are also present, Behçet’s should jump way up the list.

## Take-home points

- **SIADH can be a clue to CNS inflammation**, not just an isolated electrolyte problem.
- In **hypotonic euvolemic hyponatremia**, confirm the pattern with serum osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium, and endocrine exclusion.
- A **normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s**, especially early.
- In a patient with **fever + headache + oral/genital ulcers + rash**, keep Behçet’s on the differential.
- **Persistent hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis/CK elevation** may be underrecognized manifestations of significant hyponatremia.
- IL-6 may help connect **neuroinflammation and inappropriate ADH release**, but this remains a biologically plausible mechanism rather than proven causality in an individual case.
- Treat the sodium safely, but don’t miss the **underlying inflammatory diagnosis** driving it.

## Bottom line

Hyponatremia is sometimes the signpost, not the destination. In this case, SIADH pointed toward CNS inflammation, and the CNS inflammation turned out to be acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. When the sodium is low and the headache is real, look for the rash, the ulcers, and the inflammatory story hiding underneath.
