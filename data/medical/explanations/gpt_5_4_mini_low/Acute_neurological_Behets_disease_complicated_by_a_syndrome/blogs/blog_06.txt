Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Colchicine Instead of Steroids? Reconsidering Treatment Thresholds in Mild Acute Neuro-Behçet’s

## Why this case grabs your attention

Acute neuro-Behçet’s disease usually pushes clinicians toward corticosteroids and, often, broader immunosuppression. So a case in which a patient with new systemic Behçet’s, aseptic meningitis, and SIADH improved with **colchicine monotherapy plus fluid restriction** is instantly interesting.

The twist here is not just the treatment response. It’s the diagnostic timing. This patient’s **systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s were recognized concurrently**, which is unusual enough on its own. Add in **hyponatraemia from SIADH**, persistent hiccups, headache, and a normal brain MRI, and you get a presentation that could easily be misread as infection, nonspecific viral illness, or “just” systemic inflammation.

This is a useful case because it sits right at the edge of standard teaching: if acute neuro-Behçet’s is usually a steroid-responsive inflammatory CNS syndrome, what do we do when the presentation is relatively mild, imaging is unrevealing, and the patient improves without steroids?

## The clinical setup

The patient was a man in his 60s who came in with 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, arthralgia, and oral aphthae. Exam showed oral ulcers and folliculitis-like skin lesions; later he developed a painful scrotal ulcer. He was clinically euvolemic but had marked hyponatraemia.

The early workup showed:

- Na 124 mmol/L
- Serum osmolality low
- Urine osmolality high
- Urine sodium high
- ADH elevated
- TSH and cortisol normal

That pattern fits **SIADH**, and the euvolemic bedside exam backed it up.

Then the neurologic picture evolved. His headache and vertigo worsened. MRI was normal, but CSF showed:

- Mildly elevated opening pressure
- Elevated protein
- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- Negative infectious studies

So this looked like **aseptic meningitis**, and in the context of oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and folliculitis-like rash, Behçet’s jumped to the top of the list. HLA-B51 was positive, and he met criteria for definite Behçet’s disease.

## Why the diagnosis was tricky

This is a classic reminder that **acute neuro-Behçet’s can be MRI-negative early**, especially when the phenotype is more meningeal than parenchymal. Many clinicians are trained to think of neuro-Behçet’s as a brainstem/parenchymal syndrome with focal deficits, but that’s not the only face it wears.

In this case:

- There were **no focal deficits**
- No uveitis
- No obvious MRI lesion
- CSF was inflammatory but nonspecific
- The genital ulcer appeared only later

That means the diagnosis was made by assembling the whole syndrome over time rather than by any single “slam dunk” test.

## The management question: why no steroids?

Standard teaching still matters here: **corticosteroids are the mainstay of acute neuro-Behçet’s**, particularly when there is parenchymal disease, significant neurologic deficit, or severe inflammation.

But this case didn’t follow the usual playbook. The team used:

- Empiric cefepime initially, given fever and possible infection
- Empiric acyclovir when CNS infection remained in the differential
- Then, after Behçet’s became clear, **colchicine**
- For SIADH, **fluid restriction <800 mL/day** and close sodium monitoring

No steroids. No cyclophosphamide. No biologics.

And the patient improved:

- Fever resolved shortly after colchicine
- Headache/vertigo/hiccups improved over 2 weeks
- Sodium normalized by day 16
- ADH fell
- CK normalized
- He had no recurrence over long follow-up on colchicine

That’s compelling, but it does **not** prove colchicine is sufficient for acute neuro-Behçet’s in general. What it does suggest is that there may be a subgroup with **milder meningeal presentations** who improve without immediate steroids.

## What might explain the improvement?

There are a few plausible interpretations, and the honest answer is that the case can’t separate them cleanly.

First, this could have been a genuinely **mild, self-limited meningoencephalitic form** of neuro-Behçet’s. Some cases do improve spontaneously.

Second, colchicine may have had a meaningful anti-inflammatory effect. It’s classically used for mucocutaneous Behçet’s, but it also suppresses neutrophil activation and inflammatory cytokine signaling. That said, colchicine is **not established as effective monotherapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s**.

Third, the SIADH may have improved simply because the CNS inflammatory trigger resolved and because fluid restriction was effective.

The case authors also discuss a possible IL-6–mediated mechanism linking CNS inflammation and ADH dysregulation, which is biologically plausible. But in this patient, IL-6 was not measured, so that remains hypothesis rather than proof.

## How much should we generalize this?

Not much, at least not yet.

This case is interesting because it challenges habit, but it does not overturn current practice. The usual caution flags for acute neuro-Behçet’s still apply:

- Parenchymal brainstem involvement
- Focal neurologic deficits
- Progressive neurologic decline
- Significant MRI abnormalities
- Severe or refractory inflammation

Those patients are not good candidates for “colchicine only” management based on a single case report.

What this case does support is a more nuanced thought process:

- Not every Behçet’s CNS presentation is fulminant
- Not every inflammatory meningeal syndrome with hyponatraemia is infection
- Some patients may improve with conservative management if the presentation is mild and diagnostic certainty is high
- Close monitoring is essential if you choose not to use steroids

## Clinical pearls

A few practical points stand out:

- **Behçet’s can present concurrently with neuro involvement**, not just years later.
- **Normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s**, especially early or meningeal-predominant disease.
- **Hyponatraemia with high urine osmolality and high urine sodium in a euvolemic patient** should make you think of SIADH.
- In a patient with fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and aseptic CSF, **Behçet’s belongs on the differential**.
- **Persistent hiccups** can be a clue to significant hyponatraemia or CNS inflammation.
- **Colchicine is excellent for mucocutaneous Behçet’s**, but evidence for acute neuro-Behçet’s monotherapy is weak.

## Take-home points

- Acute neuro-Behçet’s may present as **aseptic meningitis with a normal MRI**, especially early.
- SIADH can accompany CNS inflammation in Behçet’s and may be missed if hyponatraemia is not pursued carefully.
- This case suggests that **selected mild presentations** may improve with colchicine and supportive care alone, but this should not replace steroids as standard therapy for more typical acute neuro-Behçet’s.
- A single case can expand our thinking, but it should not rewrite the treatment algorithm.

## Bottom line

This is a great reminder that Behçet’s is a protean disease and that neurologic symptoms can show up at diagnosis, not just in the chronic phase. It also raises a clinically awkward but important question: **must every acute neuro-Behçet’s patient get steroids immediately?**

The safest answer today is: **usually yes for established or significant neuro-Behçet’s, but maybe not always for very mild meningeal-predominant disease—if diagnosis is secure, the patient is stable, and you are monitoring closely.**

That said, this case should prompt curiosity, not complacency. In neuro-Behçet’s, conservative management may occasionally work—but we still need better evidence before calling it a strategy.
