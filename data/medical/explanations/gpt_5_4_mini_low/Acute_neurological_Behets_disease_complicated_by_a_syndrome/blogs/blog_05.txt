Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Hiccups, CK Elevation, and Sodium 124: The Odd Clues of Clinically Important Hyponatremia

## Why this case is worth your attention

A man in his 60s came in with fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, arthralgia, oral aphthae, and a sodium of 124 mmol/L. Easy enough to anchor on “infection” or “nonspecific viral syndrome,” right?

But the case kept getting stranger: his creatine kinase was elevated, his inflammatory markers were very high, and his exam revealed mucocutaneous lesions that ultimately supported Behçet’s disease. CSF showed aseptic meningitis, brain MRI was unrevealing, and the final picture was acute neuro-Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH. Even more interesting: his symptoms and sodium improved with colchicine monotherapy and careful fluid restriction, without corticosteroids.

This is a great reminder that hyponatremia is not just a lab abnormality. It can have weird, underappreciated manifestations—and some of those manifestations can be the clue.

## Hiccups are not always benign

Persistent hiccups are easy to dismiss. Most of the time, they’re transient and annoying rather than dangerous. But persistent hiccups should make you widen the lens, especially when they coexist with other neurologic or systemic symptoms.

In this case, the hiccups were part of a broader inflammatory CNS syndrome. That matters because hiccups can be a clue to:

- CNS inflammation or irritation
- Metabolic disturbance, including significant hyponatremia
- Brainstem involvement
- Diaphragmatic or vagal irritation from non-neurologic causes

The takeaway is not that every hiccup needs a neuro workup. It’s that hiccups plus headache, fever, or electrolyte abnormalities should stop you from prematurely minimizing the presentation.

## Hyponatremia can present “sideways”

Severe or subacute hyponatremia does not always announce itself with seizures or coma. It can present with a vague, easy-to-miss symptom cluster:

- fatigue
- malaise
- anorexia
- headache
- dizziness/vertigo
- nausea
- hiccups

That symptom pattern is especially dangerous when it is embedded in another illness. In this case, the patient clearly had an inflammatory disorder—but the sodium abnormality was not incidental. It was part of the disease process and may have been contributing to the neuro symptoms.

A practical point: when a patient with nonspecific neurologic complaints also has hyponatremia, don’t stop at “SIADH” as a label. Ask what is driving the SIADH.

## When should you think about rhabdomyolysis?

This case also had an elevated CK. That’s an easy finding to overlook when the headline problem is “hyponatremia” or “fever.”

Hyponatremia can be associated with rhabdomyolysis, particularly when it is severe, prolonged, or accompanied by systemic illness. The mechanism is not always straightforward, but the association is real enough that CK can be worth checking in selected patients—especially those with:

- myalgias or muscle tenderness
- generalized weakness
- dark urine
- prolonged malaise
- significant inflammatory illness
- moderate-to-severe hyponatremia without an obvious explanation

In this patient, CK elevation may have reflected inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury, and it normalized as the clinical syndrome improved.

The lesson is not to reflexively send CK on every mild hyponatremia. The lesson is to think of CK as a reasonable add-on when the symptom pattern suggests more than isolated electrolyte disturbance.

## SIADH is often a diagnosis of mechanism, not of destination

The patient’s labs fit SIADH:

- low serum osmolality
- inappropriately concentrated urine
- elevated urine sodium
- euvolemic exam
- elevated ADH
- normal thyroid and cortisol testing

That’s a solid SIADH pattern. But SIADH itself is not the diagnosis you’re ultimately trying to make. It’s the mechanism.

Common causes include:

- CNS pathology or inflammation
- pulmonary disease
- malignancy
- medications

This case is a nice example of why that distinction matters. The sodium problem was downstream of acute neuroinflammation from Behçet’s disease. If you only treat the lab value and miss the CNS disease, you miss the real problem.

## Sodium correction still matters, even when the driver is inflammatory CNS disease

Whether the trigger is meningitis, encephalitis, malignancy, or a medication, the principles of safe sodium management do not change.

In this case, the patient was managed conservatively with:

- fluid restriction to less than 800 mL/day
- daily monitoring of serum sodium
- avoidance of overly rapid correction

No hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists were required.

That’s a good reminder that not every SIADH case needs aggressive correction. The core goals are:

- treat the underlying cause
- prevent neurologic deterioration from worsening hyponatremia
- avoid osmotic demyelination from overcorrection

Especially in inflammatory CNS disease, sodium can improve as the underlying process comes under control. The temptation to “push harder” should be tempered by disciplined monitoring.

## A few FOAM-style pearls from this case

This patient’s presentation ties together several underappreciated bedside clues. The important pattern recognition is not “Behçet’s disease causes SIADH,” because that association is still not firmly established. The more useful point is that CNS inflammation can cause SIADH, and SIADH can present with subtle neurologic and muscular manifestations that are easy to misread.

In practice, that means you should be alert to the combination of:

- headache plus hyponatremia
- hiccups plus hyponatremia
- malaise plus muscle symptoms plus low sodium
- inflammatory or infectious-appearing illness with unexplained euvolemic hyponatremia

When those features cluster, consider whether you are looking at a CNS process rather than a standalone electrolyte issue.

## Take-home points

- Persistent hiccups can be a real clue, not just a nuisance symptom, especially when paired with headache or hyponatremia.
- Hyponatremia may present with vague symptoms like malaise, anorexia, dizziness, and hiccups before severe neurologic decline appears.
- CK elevation can occur in significant hyponatremia and is reasonable to check when patients have myalgias, weakness, or systemic inflammatory illness.
- SIADH is a mechanism; the key is finding the underlying cause, including CNS inflammation.
- Careful sodium correction remains essential even when the root problem is inflammatory neurologic disease.
- In selected cases, treating the underlying inflammatory condition can lead to improvement in both neurologic symptoms and sodium.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder to respect the “small” clues. Hiccups, CK elevation, and sodium 124 may look like separate problems, but together they can point to clinically important CNS inflammation with SIADH. The sodium matters, the symptoms matter, and the context matters even more.
