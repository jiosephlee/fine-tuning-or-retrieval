Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Behçet’s Diagnosis That Declares Itself Late: Why the Genital Ulcer Changed Everything

## The part that makes this case tricky

This is one of those cases that punishes premature closure.

A man in his 60s came in with fever, headache, malaise, hiccups, arthralgia, oral aphthae, and hyponatremia. At first glance, that sounds like a broad, frustrating differential: infection, systemic inflammation, malignancy, endocrine disease, maybe a drug effect if there were one. Nothing about the initial presentation screams Behçet’s disease.

And that’s the trap.

The diagnosis only became clear when a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on hospital day 5. That single finding changed the entire clinical picture. Suddenly, the constellation of oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, and genital aphthosis fit Behçet’s disease much more convincingly. In this case, the genital ulcer was the clue that turned a vague inflammatory syndrome into a definable vasculitic disorder.

## Behçet’s disease: when the syndrome arrives in pieces

Behçet’s disease is a multisystem inflammatory disorder classically associated with recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, skin lesions, and eye disease. Neurologic involvement occurs in a minority of patients, usually later in the disease course — so seeing systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease diagnosed at the same time is unusual.

That timing matters.

Early on, the patient had fever, headache, and oral aphthae — but no genital ulcer yet. Without that more specific mucocutaneous feature, the differential naturally leans toward more common problems:

- Viral or bacterial meningoencephalitis
- Tuberculous meningitis
- Autoimmune/inflammatory CNS disease
- Malignancy
- Neurosarcoidosis

That is exactly what happened here, and it was reasonable. But the case reinforces an important habit: in evolving syndromes, the initial exam is not the final exam.

## Why serial examination matters

The diagnosis of Behçet’s disease was not available at the front door. It emerged over time.

On admission, the patient had:
- Oral aphthae
- Folliculitis-like rashes
- Fever and systemic inflammation
- Headache and later vertigo
- Hyponatremia

Then, on day 5:
- A painful scrotal ulcer appeared

That is the kind of change that should trigger a fresh diagnostic reset. If you’re following a patient with a nonspecific inflammatory syndrome, repeating the skin and mucosal exam is not busywork — it is often the difference between “fever of unknown origin” and a unifying diagnosis.

Behçet’s lesions can be transient, subtle, and easy to miss unless you look again.

## The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease: practical bedside use

This case also nicely illustrates how the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease can help structure your thinking.

The scoring system is clinical, not biomarker-driven. In this patient:
- Oral aphthae: 2 points
- Genital aphthosis: 2 points
- Folliculitis-like rash: 1 point

Total: 5 points. A score of 4 or more supports definite Behçet’s disease.

That’s a useful reminder that Behçet’s is still, at its core, a bedside diagnosis. HLA-B51 may support the picture, but it does not make the diagnosis. In this case, HLA-B51 was positive, but the diagnosis rested on clinical features.

## Don’t anchor on infection just because the patient is febrile

Early on, this patient looked infectious. He had fever, headache, inflammatory markers, and CSF pleocytosis. Empiric cefepime and later acyclovir were both reasonable while the workup unfolded.

But the course also shows why clinicians need to keep a wide differential even after initial treatment begins. The CSF showed:
- Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis
- Elevated protein
- Negative bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal studies

MRI was unrevealing. That does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s disease — especially early on. Acute neuro-Behçet’s can present as aseptic meningitis with no clear parenchymal lesion on MRI. A normal scan does not rule it out.

So if you have:
- Mucocutaneous lesions
- CNS symptoms
- CSF inflammation
- Negative infectious studies

…Behçet’s belongs on the short list.

## The hyponatremia is not a side quest

The other striking feature here is the SIADH.

The patient had:
- Hypotonic hyponatremia
- High urine osmolality
- High urine sodium
- Elevated ADH
- Clinical euvolemia
- Normal thyroid and cortisol testing

That is classic SIADH physiology.

The interesting question is why it happened. In this report, the authors argue that acute CNS inflammation from neuro-Behçet’s may have driven inappropriate ADH secretion. That’s plausible and clinically important, even if causality can’t be proven from a single case.

The teaching point is simpler: in a patient with headache, fever, and hyponatremia, SIADH may be a clue to CNS inflammation rather than an incidental lab abnormality.

And don’t forget the “odd” symptoms that can be caused by hyponatremia:
- Persistent hiccups
- Weakness or functional decline
- Possibly rhabdomyolysis, as suggested by the elevated CK in this case

Those are easy to dismiss unless you connect the dots.

## Treatment: colchicine alone did the job here

One of the most surprising parts of this report is that colchicine monotherapy was associated with improvement in:
- Fever
- Mucocutaneous lesions
- Neurologic symptoms
- Hyponatremia

That is notable because corticosteroids are generally considered the mainstay for acute neuro-Behçet’s, and colchicine is usually thought of more as a mucocutaneous drug. The authors are appropriately cautious: spontaneous improvement can’t be excluded, and this does not overturn standard neuro-Behçet’s treatment.

Still, it’s a useful reminder that not every patient follows the textbook path. In this case, the improvement was rapid after colchicine was started, and the patient ultimately did well without steroids or other immunosuppressants.

## What to remember at the bedside

- Behçet’s disease may announce itself gradually; early presentations can look like infection or nonspecific inflammation.
- Repeat the oral, genital, and skin exam — the diagnosis may be hiding in plain sight.
- Genital aphthosis is a high-yield clue and can be the feature that clarifies the syndrome.
- The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease are practical and bedside-friendly; use them to structure your reasoning.
- Normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially early.
- Hyponatremia with a euvolemic picture should prompt consideration of SIADH, including CNS inflammatory causes.
- Inflammatory neurologic symptoms plus mucocutaneous findings should make you think beyond infection.

## Bottom line

This case is a nice reminder that Behçet’s disease often doesn’t arrive as a complete package. It shows up in fragments — oral ulcers first, then a rash, then a genital ulcer that finally ties the whole thing together.

If you keep re-examining the patient and resist the urge to lock onto the first plausible diagnosis, the syndrome may eventually declare itself.
