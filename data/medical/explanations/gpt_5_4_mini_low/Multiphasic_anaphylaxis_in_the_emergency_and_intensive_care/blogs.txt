Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# When Anaphylaxis Comes Back Again: Managing Biphasic and Multiphasic Relapse in the ED and ICU

## The case that should make you uncomfortable

This case is unsettling for a simple reason: the patient *responded* to treatment, then relapsed, then relapsed again, and again. That’s exactly the sort of course that can lull clinicians into thinking the storm has passed—right before it hits again.

A woman in her 60s developed severe anaphylaxis after her first dose of clarithromycin, with hypotension, hypoxemia, flushing, urticaria, facial swelling, and agitation. She improved after intramuscular epinephrine, but then had recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock over the next 12 hours, ultimately totaling four episodes. She required repeated epinephrine, supplemental oxygen, IV fluids, and ICU-level monitoring before the relapses stopped.

That pattern raises the uncomfortable question at the bedside: are we seeing true biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, refractory anaphylaxis, or just ongoing absorption with transient improvement?

## Why this matters in real life

Anaphylaxis is usually taught as a diagnosis-and-treat-now problem. Epinephrine is the key move, and most patients improve quickly. The challenge comes after the first response.

A recurrent reaction after apparent resolution can mean different things:

- **Biphasic anaphylaxis**: symptoms recur after a symptom-free interval, without new allergen exposure.
- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis**: more than one recurrence after initial resolution.
- **Refractory anaphylaxis**: ongoing symptoms that never truly clear, or clear only briefly with incomplete response.
- **Ongoing allergen absorption**: especially relevant with oral exposures or sustained-release medications.

Clinically, these distinctions matter because they affect monitoring, disposition, and how aggressively you escalate care. But in the ED, they can be nearly impossible to separate in real time.

## Definitions are useful, but the bedside is messy

The Resuscitation Council UK definition of biphasic anaphylaxis is a recurrence of symptoms hours after apparent resolution, without further allergen exposure. Refractory anaphylaxis is different: it’s persistent anaphylaxis with inadequate response to appropriately administered epinephrine.

That sounds clean on paper. In practice, the boundary is blurry.

A patient who improves after epinephrine and then deteriorates again could have:

- true biphasic relapse
- delayed absorption of the trigger
- underdosed epinephrine
- ongoing mediator release from severe initial disease
- inadequate observation with premature de-escalation

This is why serial bedside reassessment matters more than trying to label the pattern too early.

## What this case teaches about relapse risk

This patient had several features that should make you think hard about prolonged monitoring:

- **Severe initial presentation**: hypotension and hypoxemia
- **Need for repeated epinephrine**
- **Relapse within hours**
- **Possible underlying mast cell disorder**
- **Adult weight >50 kg with initial 0.3 mg autoinjector dosing**

Those are not subtle clues. The important point is not that every patient with anaphylaxis needs ICU admission. It’s that once you’ve seen recurrence, your threshold for escalation should drop dramatically.

## The epinephrine issue nobody likes to talk about

This case also highlights a practical problem: many autoinjectors used in adult care contain **0.3 mg**, even though guideline dosing for adults is generally **0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg**.

For a 104 kg patient, 0.3 mg is a meaningful underdose.

That doesn’t mean autoinjectors are “bad”—they’re fast, familiar, and can be lifesaving, especially when delay is the biggest danger. But in a monitored medical setting, especially when a patient is large, severely ill, or already relapsing, you should think deliberately about whether you’re giving enough epinephrine and whether you need to escalate beyond repeat IM dosing.

## Corticosteroids: still common, but not the hero here

This case followed a common pattern: after relapse, corticosteroids were added because of the traditional belief that they might prevent biphasic reactions.

The problem is that the evidence doesn’t really support that practice. Contemporary literature increasingly shows no clear benefit for preventing relapse, and some studies suggest potential harm or at least no upside.

So the lesson is not “never give steroids,” but rather:

- don’t let steroids distract you from epinephrine
- don’t assume steroids will prevent relapse
- don’t use steroids as a substitute for appropriate monitoring

In other words: steroids may still be in the toolkit, but they’re not the cornerstone of anaphylaxis management.

## When should relapse push you toward ICU?

There’s no perfect rule, but this case supports a low threshold for ICU-level monitoring when relapse is already happening.

Think ICU or equivalent monitored setting if you have:

- recurrent hypotension
- repeated epinephrine requirement
- airway compromise or escalating respiratory symptoms
- severe initial shock
- concern for multiple further relapses
- need for an epinephrine infusion or close hemodynamic titration

The big practical issue is that biphasic/multiphasic reactions can recur outside the window of a standard ED observation period. A patient who looks “fixed” may not actually be safe.

## Observation: not every patient needs the same clock

The literature gives a wide range for biphasic anaphylaxis incidence, and timing of recurrence is variable. Some relapses happen within a few hours; others occur much later. That means a one-size-fits-all observation period is imperfect.

Risk stratification is probably the best we have:

- lower-risk patients may be observed for a shorter period after full resolution
- higher-risk patients deserve longer observation and/or admission
- patients who have already relapsed are no longer in the “routine observation” bucket

This case is a reminder that once recurrence occurs, you’re no longer asking, “How long should I watch this?” You’re asking, “How many more times is this going to happen, and do I have the right place to manage it?”

## A note on mast cell disease

The patient was later found to have a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. That’s not the same as saying mastocytosis caused the multiphasic course—but it does raise suspicion that an abnormal mast cell milieu may have amplified severity and recurrence risk.

This matters because patients with mast cell disorders can have:

- more severe reactions
- unusual or prolonged courses
- increased sensitivity to triggers

So if the clinical pattern feels disproportionate, don’t just anchor on the visible trigger. Ask whether there’s a deeper predisposition.

## Practical bedside approach

When anaphylaxis improves and then starts coming back, I’d think in this order:

1. **Re-dose epinephrine promptly**
2. **Reassess airway, breathing, circulation, and skin**
3. **Give fluids if there’s shock**
4. **Escalate to monitored care if relapse occurs**
5. **Consider whether the trigger could still be absorbing**
6. **Stop anchoring on “it got better once”**
7. **Treat the next episode as real anaphylaxis until proven otherwise**

And if the patient is already relapsing, don’t waste time debating labels more than necessary. The physiology is telling you the patient is not out of danger.

## Take-home points

- **Recurrent symptoms after initial improvement are a big deal**—don’t assume the first response means the danger is over.
- **Biphasic, multiphasic, refractory, and ongoing absorption can look similar at the bedside.**
- **Repeated epinephrine needs should push you toward ICU-level monitoring or an equivalent closely observed setting.**
- **Adult autoinjector dosing may underdose larger patients** compared with guideline-recommended adult dosing.
- **Corticosteroids are not reliable prevention for biphasic anaphylaxis** and should not drive management decisions.
- **If the reaction is relapsing, keep treating anaphylaxis, not the label.**

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that anaphylaxis is not always a one-and-done event. Some patients improve, then worsen again—sometimes more than once. In those patients, the real management question is not “Was this biphasic?” It’s “How do I keep this patient alive through the next recurrence?”

And the answer is usually the same as the first time: epinephrine, fluids, reassessment, and a much lower threshold to monitor them like they’re not done yet.



# 0.3 mg Isn’t 0.5 mg: Are We Underdosing Adult Anaphylaxis?

## The case that should make us pause

A woman in her 60s, weighing 104 kg, took her first dose of clarithromycin and within an hour developed pruritus, flushing, throat swelling sensation, urticaria, hypoxemia, and hypotension. This was true anaphylactic shock. She got what many of us would reach for first in a time-crunched ED: an intramuscular epinephrine auto-injector, plus antihistamine, oxygen, and fluids.

She improved — briefly.

Over the next 12 hours, she had recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock. Four episodes in total, each requiring epinephrine. She ultimately needed ICU monitoring, and her course raised a bigger question than the trigger itself: if this patient weighed 104 kg, how much epinephrine was she actually getting, and was 0.3 mg enough?

That’s the uncomfortable part of this case. We all know epinephrine is the drug for anaphylaxis. But in adults, especially larger adults, the difference between 0.3 mg and 0.5 mg is not trivial.

## Why this matters

Anaphylaxis is one of those diagnoses where delay kills. The treatment priority is not subtle: epinephrine first, epinephrine early, epinephrine intramuscularly.

But in the real world, many adults are treated with 0.3 mg auto-injectors because they are fast, familiar, and immediately available. That is especially appealing in high-pressure settings, or in places where clinicians don’t give anaphylaxis treatment every day.

The problem? Guideline dosing for adults is typically 0.01 mg/kg IM, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For many adults — especially those over 50 kg — 0.3 mg is an underdose relative to that target.

This case is a good reminder that convenience can come with a pharmacologic cost.

## The tension: speed versus dose

There’s a reason auto-injectors are popular:

- they reduce hesitation
- they simplify administration
- they get epinephrine in faster
- they’re useful when staff may be unfamiliar with vasoactive drugs

That last point matters. In a busy ED, or on a ward where anaphylaxis is rare, an auto-injector can be the difference between treatment now and treatment after a frustrating delay.

But the tradeoff is obvious: a standard adult auto-injector dose of 0.3 mg may leave larger patients underdosed. In this case, the patient weighed 104 kg. A 0.01 mg/kg calculation would suggest 1.04 mg in theory, but practice caps the dose at 0.5 mg. That means the “adult” 0.3 mg device delivered only 60% of the usual maximum recommended IM dose.

That gap may matter, particularly in severe presentations.

## What this case adds

This patient didn’t just have a single episode of anaphylaxis. She had a multiphasic course with repeated relapses despite initial response. That makes it hard to know how much of the recurrence was driven by underdosing versus the biology of her reaction versus persistent mediator release.

Still, a few points stand out:

- She was a large adult, and 0.3 mg is below the commonly recommended 0.5 mg adult dose.
- Her initial treatment with 0.3 mg epinephrine produced only temporary stabilization.
- She required repeated epinephrine doses and ICU-level monitoring.
- Her later doses were 0.5 mg intramuscularly.
- The severity of symptoms lessened with each episode, but the course still illustrates how anaphylaxis can relapse even after apparent initial improvement.

This doesn’t prove that 0.3 mg caused the multiphasic course. But it does reinforce that underdosing may contribute to persistent instability, repeat dosing, and escalation of care.

## Key clinical pearl: don’t confuse “adult” with “adequately dosed”

A lot of clinicians think of 0.3 mg as the default adult epinephrine dose because that’s what many auto-injectors contain. But guideline-based IM epinephrine dosing is weight-based, and the common adult ceiling is 0.5 mg.

For a substantial adult, 0.3 mg may be the dose you can give quickly — but it is not always the dose you ultimately want.

This distinction is especially important in:

- severe anaphylaxis with hypotension or hypoxemia
- larger adults
- patients needing repeat epinephrine
- settings where auto-injectors are being used as a bridge, not the final word

## Should we still use auto-injectors in the ED?

Yes — sometimes.

This case report makes a pragmatic argument that many clinicians will recognize. In time-sensitive settings, or where hesitation could delay treatment, a readily available auto-injector may be better than a perfectly calculated dose that never gets given.

That said, the presence of an auto-injector should not lull us into thinking the job is done. If the patient is large, unstable, or not responding as expected, escalate early:

- reassess the diagnosis
- repeat IM epinephrine promptly if needed
- give fluids aggressively
- move to a higher level of monitoring
- consider IV epinephrine infusion in refractory cases, with appropriate expertise and monitoring

## Steroids and antihistamines: useful adjuncts, not the main act

This case also reflects a common historical pattern: after recurrence, corticosteroids and antihistamines were added. But the modern anaphylaxis literature has moved away from routine steroids as a strategy to prevent biphasic reactions. Evidence for benefit is weak, and some data suggest harm or at least no meaningful advantage.

The core treatment remains epinephrine. Not antihistamines. Not steroids. Epinephrine.

Antihistamines may help with skin symptoms. Steroids may still be used in some protocols. But neither should distract from inadequate epinephrine dosing.

## Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, but it changes the risk conversation

The patient’s course was unusual because it was not just biphasic — it was multiphasic. That rarity is part of what makes case reports like this useful. There are no perfect predictors, no universally agreed observation period, and no robust evidence-based algorithm for these prolonged relapsing cases.

Still, this case reminds us that patients with:

- severe initial anaphylaxis
- hypotension
- hypoxemia
- recurrent symptoms
- multiple epinephrine doses

deserve close monitoring and a low threshold for ICU-level observation.

And in those patients, getting the first epinephrine dose right matters even more.

## Take-home points

- **0.3 mg IM epinephrine is not equivalent to the usual adult target dose of 0.5 mg.**
- **In larger adults, 0.3 mg may be a meaningful underdose.**
- **Auto-injectors are fast and practical, but convenience should not replace dose awareness.**
- **Repeat epinephrine, fluids, and escalation of monitoring are essential when symptoms recur or hemodynamics remain unstable.**
- **Steroids and antihistamines are adjuncts; epinephrine remains the treatment that changes outcomes.**
- **Severe or recurrent anaphylaxis should trigger consideration of higher-level care and, in selected refractory cases, IV epinephrine expertise.**

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that adult anaphylaxis dosing is not “one size fits all.” A 104 kg patient who gets 0.3 mg IM epinephrine is not getting the guideline-recommended adult maximum dose. Sometimes that’s an acceptable bridge. Sometimes it may be the difference between rapid stabilization and recurrent shock.

In anaphylaxis, speed matters — but so does dose.



# Observation After Anaphylaxis: Who Needs 4 Hours, Who Needs 24?

## The case that breaks the “4-hour rule”

Most of us have been taught some version of this: treat anaphylaxis fast, watch the patient for a few hours, and if they look good, they can go home. That works for a lot of patients.

But not for all of them.

This case is a reminder that anaphylaxis doesn’t always respect our neat observation windows. A woman in her 60s had a clear, severe anaphylactic reaction after her first dose of clarithromycin, improved after intramuscular epinephrine, and then relapsed repeatedly over the next 12 hours. In total, she had four anaphylactic episodes. Each recurrence required more epinephrine. She ultimately needed ICU-level monitoring, and her course raised concern for an underlying mast cell disorder.

That is the key problem: a patient can look fully rescued after the first dose of epinephrine and still be sitting on a time bomb.

## What makes biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis so tricky?

Anaphylaxis is usually straightforward at the bedside: sudden onset, multi-system involvement, and a trigger that often makes sense in retrospect. Biphasic and multiphasic reactions are the messy exceptions.

A biphasic reaction is recurrence of anaphylaxis after apparent resolution, without re-exposure. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is the more dramatic version: multiple relapses, again without repeat allergen exposure.

The problem is not just that these events happen. It’s that we do not have a universally reliable way to predict who will relapse, when they will relapse, or how bad the relapse will be.

In this case, the patient initially responded to treatment, then relapsed again and again despite stopping clarithromycin and receiving appropriate emergency care. The intensity of symptoms decreased with each recurrence, but the pattern still forced escalation in monitoring and treatment.

## Why fixed observation windows are attractive — and imperfect

Observation periods after anaphylaxis are popular because they are simple.

A 4-hour window is easy to remember, operationally convenient, and probably reasonable for many low-risk patients. But “reasonable for many” is not the same as “safe for all.” Literature on biphasic anaphylaxis shows a wide reported incidence, and recurrence can happen well beyond the first few hours. In this case, relapse occurred within 12 hours, which is squarely inside the range that makes a short observation period feel uncomfortable in hindsight.

The issue is not that a single universal observation time is wrong in every case. It’s that it is blunt. It treats all anaphylaxis as if the risk profile were identical, when it clearly is not.

## What features seem to raise concern for relapse?

We still do not have a validated crystal ball, but several features are repeatedly discussed as possible risk markers for biphasic or more complicated courses:

- Severe initial presentation
- Delay in epinephrine administration
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses
- Prior biphasic reaction
- Ongoing symptoms or incomplete resolution
- Possible mast cell disorders or elevated baseline tryptase

This patient had several concerning features. She presented with hypotension and hypoxemia, had diffuse cutaneous findings, and required repeated epinephrine for recurrent shock. Later workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not met.

That combination should make anyone pause before sending the patient home after a short observation period.

## The epinephrine lesson: don’t underdose the patient in front of you

One practical wrinkle in this case is worth emphasizing: the first two doses were 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors, even though the patient weighed 104 kg and guideline-recommended adult dosing is often simplified to 0.5 mg intramuscularly.

Auto-injectors are fast, and speed matters. In many environments, they are the best way to avoid hesitation. But in a large adult, 0.3 mg may be a smaller dose than we really want, especially when the patient is in shock.

That matters for observation strategy too. If the first dose is potentially submaximal, the threshold to worry about recurrence should probably drop.

## Steroids: still common, not clearly helpful

This case also reflects a common real-world pattern: corticosteroids were added after recurrence, largely out of concern for biphasic reactions.

That is understandable, but the evidence base is weak. Modern reviews and guidelines increasingly question routine steroid use in anaphylaxis, and they do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions. In this case, steroids were given, but the patient still had relapses before eventual stabilization.

The broader point: steroids should not be used as a substitute for adequate epinephrine, adequate fluids, and adequate observation.

## So who needs 4 hours?

A short observation period may be reasonable for patients who rapidly and completely respond, have no hypotension, no respiratory compromise, no need for repeated epinephrine, and no other red flags.

These are the patients who look like true low-risk cases: clean response, stable vitals, no recurrence, and a clear plan for avoidance and follow-up.

## Who needs longer?

This is where the case gets clinically useful. Patients who have any of the following deserve serious consideration for prolonged observation, admission, or higher-acuity monitoring:

- Hypotension or shock at presentation
- Hypoxemia or significant respiratory involvement
- Need for more than one dose of epinephrine
- Recurrent symptoms after initial improvement
- Concern for mast cell disease or elevated baseline tryptase
- Uncertain diagnosis or uncertain trigger
- Social or logistical barriers to safe discharge

In this patient, the relapsing course itself was the decisive warning sign. Once the first recurrence happened, the question stopped being “Can we observe a little longer?” and became “How much monitoring is enough to catch the next relapse?”

## A risk-stratified approach makes more sense than a universal clock

The cleanest lesson from this case is that observation after anaphylaxis should be risk-stratified.

A patient with isolated urticaria that resolves and never touches the airway, blood pressure, or oxygenation is not the same as a patient with shock who needs repeated epinephrine. Lumping them together under one observation rule is appealing for workflow, but it is not clinically satisfying.

A practical approach is to think in tiers:

- **Low risk:** rapid, complete response; no hypotension; no respiratory compromise; no repeat epinephrine
- **Intermediate risk:** severe initial symptoms but stable after treatment, or features that make relapse harder to dismiss
- **High risk:** recurrent symptoms, shock, multiple epinephrine doses, or concern for mast cell disease

That framework is not a rigid guideline, but it is more honest than pretending 4 hours is a universal answer.

## Take-home points

- Biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon, but they can be dangerous and unpredictable.
- A fixed observation window is a blunt tool; severe presentations and recurrent symptoms deserve longer monitoring.
- Repeated epinephrine requirement should immediately raise concern for relapse and admission needs.
- Steroids are not a reliable strategy to prevent biphasic reactions.
- In adults, 0.3 mg epinephrine may underdose large patients compared with the commonly recommended 0.5 mg IM dose.
- Patients with suspected mast cell disorders may warrant a lower threshold for prolonged observation and specialist follow-up.

## Bottom line

The question is not really “4 hours or 24 hours?”

The better question is: what is this patient telling you about their risk of relapse?

In a low-risk patient, a short observation period may be enough. In a patient like this one—shock, hypoxemia, and repeated relapses within 12 hours—short observation would have been false reassurance. The right answer is not a universal time cutoff. It’s a risk-based plan that matches the biology of the reaction in front of you.



# Steroids in Anaphylaxis: Tradition, Inertia, and the Evidence Gap

## Why this case is worth talking about

This patient had the kind of anaphylaxis that makes everyone in the room uneasy: initial improvement after epinephrine, then recurrent shock. By the time she had her second and third relapses, the temptation to “do more” was strong — and in many hospitals that “more” is a steroid.

That’s exactly why this case is useful. It highlights the gap between what many clinicians still do in practice and what the evidence actually supports. When anaphylaxis recurs, steroids feel reassuring. But reassurance is not the same as benefit.

This case also shows how persistent old habits can be, especially when protocols differ between the ward and ICU. She received prednisone and dexamethasone after the first recurrence, reflecting a common reflex: if the reaction is severe or relapsing, give corticosteroids to prevent another relapse. The problem is that the data for that strategy are weak.

## The case in brief

A woman in her 60s developed severe anaphylaxis about an hour after her first dose of oral clarithromycin. She arrived hypotensive, hypoxemic, flushed, urticarial, and with throat swelling symptoms. She improved after intramuscular epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, and clemastine.

Then the story became more interesting: she had multiple further episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours. Each recurrence responded to epinephrine. After the first relapse, she also received corticosteroids — prednisone and dexamethasone — in line with local practice. Ultimately she stabilized without further relapse after about 24 hours.

Later workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, which may have contributed to the severity and recurrence, although she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria.

## Why steroids keep showing up in anaphylaxis

There are a few reasons steroids remain sticky in anaphylaxis care:

- **They’re embedded in older teaching.** For years, many protocols included corticosteroids as part of standard anaphylaxis treatment.
- **They feel preventive.** Clinicians worry about biphasic reactions, so steroids seem like a reasonable hedge.
- **They’re low-friction.** Once the airway is open and epinephrine has worked, steroids can be ordered quickly and look like “something extra.”
- **Protocol variation reinforces habit.** If one unit’s pathway includes steroids and another’s does not, people tend to default to local custom.
- **Severe cases create therapeutic momentum.** The sicker the patient, the more we are drawn to add treatments, even when mechanistic plausibility outpaces clinical evidence.

But in anaphylaxis, epinephrine is the disease-modifying drug. Steroids are not a substitute, and the evidence that they prevent biphasic reactions is poor.

## What the evidence actually says

The old rationale for corticosteroids in anaphylaxis was prevention of delayed or biphasic recurrence. That theory has not held up well.

The literature summarized in the case report points in the same direction:

- A 2017 systematic review found **one supportive study and 21 studies showing no evidence** that corticosteroids reduce biphasic anaphylaxis.
- More recent work has raised concern that prehospital steroids may even be associated with **greater ICU or ward admission** in some settings.
- Current guidance increasingly advises **against routine corticosteroid use** in anaphylaxis.

So why the gap between evidence and practice? Because anaphylaxis is fast, frightening, and high stakes. When symptoms recur, it’s hard not to reach for anything that seems like it might reduce the chance of another event. But “seems like it might” is not the standard we should use for routine care.

## A useful distinction: biphasic vs refractory vs ongoing exposure

Part of the steroid debate gets muddled because not every recurrence is a true biphasic reaction.

A recurrent episode after apparent recovery could be:

- **Biphasic anaphylaxis**: recurrence after complete resolution, without re-exposure
- **Refractory anaphylaxis**: persistent symptoms with inadequate response to epinephrine
- **Ongoing absorption or continued exposure**: especially relevant with food or slow-release agents

In this case, clarithromycin was a single oral dose and was discontinued, making recurrent episodes more consistent with multiphasic/biphasic anaphylaxis rather than ongoing exposure. That matters, because it’s exactly the scenario where clinicians reach for steroids hoping to prevent further relapses — and exactly where the evidence remains thin.

## What should we do instead?

This case does not support withholding all adjuncts in every severe reaction. It does support keeping the priorities straight.

The core of anaphylaxis management remains:

- **Immediate intramuscular epinephrine**
- **Airway, breathing, circulation support**
- **Supplemental oxygen as needed**
- **IV fluids for shock**
- **Observation with attention to recurrence**

In this patient, repeated epinephrine worked. That’s the main lesson. The relapses were managed because the team recognized recurrence quickly and treated it decisively.

Steroids, by contrast, were an add-on based on tradition rather than strong evidence. They may still be used by some clinicians in selected scenarios, but they should not be presented as standard, evidence-based prevention for biphasic reactions.

## The educational problem: why this persists

This is not just a knowledge issue. It’s a systems issue.

If your local pathway still includes corticosteroids, trainees will assume they are required. If the ICU protocol and ED protocol disagree, clinicians will infer that the evidence is unsettled — which it is — but also that “doing more” is acceptable default behavior.

That’s where education matters. Teams need to hear a simple message repeatedly:

**Epinephrine is the treatment. Steroids are not the treatment for acute anaphylaxis, and they have not been shown to reliably prevent biphasic reactions.**

That message should be paired with nuance: severe, relapsing, or prolonged anaphylaxis still deserves close observation and escalation when needed. But escalation means more epinephrine, fluids, airway support, and monitoring — not faith-based steroid use.

## What this case adds

This case is especially helpful because it reminds us that recurrence after anaphylaxis does happen, even after a good initial response. That reality is one reason steroids remain entrenched. But recurrence does not prove steroid responsiveness.

The patient’s eventual stabilization after repeated epinephrine and observation cannot be attributed to corticosteroids with confidence. Her course may have reflected the natural history of the reaction, the avoidance of further exposure, or an underlying mast-cell disorder increasing susceptibility to relapse.

That uncertainty is important. It’s easy to confuse correlation with efficacy in dramatic cases. FOAM-friendly translation: just because the patient got better after steroids does not mean the steroids made them better.

## Take-home points

- **Epinephrine is the key intervention in anaphylaxis.**
- **Routine corticosteroids are not supported by strong evidence for preventing biphasic reactions.**
- **Recurrence after anaphylaxis should prompt reassessment for biphasic or refractory disease, not false reassurance.**
- **Local protocols often lag behind evidence, so education has to actively challenge old reflexes.**
- **In severe or relapsing cases, the answer is closer monitoring and repeat epinephrine when needed — not expecting steroids to rescue the situation.**

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that anaphylaxis is still often treated with a mix of evidence, habit, and anxiety. Steroids have a long history in the algorithm, but the rationale for routine use is increasingly shaky. If we want to improve care, we should keep epinephrine front and center, reserve steroids for truly individualized decisions, and make sure our protocols reflect the evidence — not just the inertia of prior practice.



# The Hidden Mast Cell Problem: When Recurrent Anaphylaxis Should Trigger a Mastocytosis Workup

## Why this case matters

Most anaphylaxis cases are straightforward in the emergency department: identify the trigger, give epinephrine, watch the patient improve, and make sure they leave with an autoinjector and follow-up. This case was not straightforward.

A woman in her 60s developed severe anaphylaxis after clarithromycin, improved, and then relapsed again and again — four episodes total. That alone should make you pause. But the real teaching point came later: her unusually severe, prolonged, multiphasic course prompted a mast cell workup that suggested systemic mastocytosis, even though she did not meet full formal diagnostic criteria.

That’s the key lesson here: sometimes the anaphylaxis is not the whole problem. Sometimes it is the first clue that a patient has an underlying mast cell disorder.

## The bedside story

The patient had a known beta-lactam allergy history, but this was her first true anaphylactic event. She had recently been switched from clindamycin to clarithromycin after a rash. About an hour after her first clarithromycin dose, she developed pruritus, flushing, sweating, agitation, and throat swelling sensation. In the ED she was hypotensive and hypoxemic, with diffuse erythema and urticaria.

She responded initially to intramuscular epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, and clemastine. Then the relapse pattern began. Within the first 12 hours she had recurrent anaphylactic episodes, each requiring more epinephrine. Symptoms became less intense over time, but the course was prolonged and unpredictable enough that she was admitted to ICU for monitoring.

That pattern — severe initial reaction, recurrence despite treatment, multiple epinephrine doses — is exactly the kind of presentation that should make you think beyond the trigger.

## When anaphylaxis should raise suspicion for mast cell disease

Not every bad allergic reaction needs a hematology or allergy workup beyond routine follow-up. But a few features should lower your threshold for thinking about mastocytosis or another mast cell disorder:

- Recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis
- Severe initial presentation, especially shock
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses
- Reactions that seem out of proportion to the trigger
- Unexplained elevated baseline tryptase
- History suggesting mediator-release symptoms outside the acute event
- Recurrent “allergic” symptoms without a clear culprit

This patient had several of these. Her course was not just “severe drug allergy.” It was unusually recurrent, and the follow-up workup raised concern for an underlying mast cell disorder.

## Tryptase: useful, but timing matters

Tryptase is one of the most useful labs in suspected anaphylaxis, but only if you respect the clock.

The case report reinforces the practical approach:

- Draw tryptase as soon as feasible without delaying treatment
- Repeat it 1–2 hours after symptom onset, ideally within 4 hours
- Obtain a baseline level at least 24 hours after complete resolution

In this case, the first tryptase wasn’t obtained during the initial episodes. That matters, because you lose part of the diagnostic story. They did obtain a level after the third episode and another after recovery, and both were elevated.

Two clinical pearls here:

1. An elevated acute tryptase supports mast cell activation, but a normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis.
2. A persistently elevated baseline tryptase should make you think about mast cell disease, especially if the clinical picture is severe or recurrent.

## Systemic mastocytosis: what are we looking for?

Systemic mastocytosis is an abnormal proliferation of mast cells with excessive mediator release. Clinically, this can mean recurrent flushing, anaphylaxis, GI symptoms, constitutional complaints, and sometimes organ involvement.

In this case, further analysis showed a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, but she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. That distinction matters. You don’t need a formal label to change your clinical suspicion, counseling, and follow-up.

Also worth remembering: mast cell disorders are not the only explanation for recurrent anaphylaxis, but they are one of the most important “can’t miss” downstream diagnoses when the presentation is atypical.

## What should the ED and ICU clinician do differently?

The acute management of anaphylaxis does not change just because you suspect mastocytosis: epinephrine remains first-line, full stop. But the downstream implications do change.

Patients with suspected mast cell disease may need:

- Allergy/immunology follow-up sooner rather than later
- Repeat baseline tryptase when well
- Consideration of further evaluation for mast cell disorders
- More careful counseling about future triggers and recurrence risk
- A low threshold for carrying epinephrine autoinjectors

This patient was ultimately discharged on levocetirizine and given an epinephrine autoinjector with teaching. No surprise there. But the follow-up concern for mastocytosis is what gives the case its long tail.

## A note on severity and recurrence

One of the more striking features in this case was that each episode became less intense. That can tempt clinicians into thinking the danger is passing. Be careful. Multiphasic anaphylaxis can lull you into false reassurance right before the next relapse.

Also, there is no universally accepted observation period that perfectly captures risk. The literature supports that biphasic reactions can occur hours later, and multiphasic cases are even less predictable. In patients with severe presentations or repeated relapses, the bar for higher-acuity monitoring should be low.

## Take-home points

- Severe or recurrent anaphylaxis is not just a treatment issue — it may be a diagnostic clue.
- Think about mast cell disease when anaphylaxis is unusually severe, multiphasic, or requires repeated epinephrine.
- Tryptase is most helpful when drawn in a timed series: acute sample, 1–2 hour sample, and baseline after recovery.
- A persistently elevated baseline tryptase should prompt consideration of systemic mastocytosis or another mast cell disorder.
- You do not need a formal mastocytosis diagnosis to change follow-up, counseling, and risk assessment.
- Epinephrine remains the treatment for anaphylaxis, regardless of whether mast cell disease is suspected.

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that “drug allergy” is sometimes just the opening act. When anaphylaxis is recurrent, prolonged, or out of proportion, look for the hidden mast cell problem. The trigger may be clarithromycin, but the underlying vulnerability may be mast cell disease.



# Tryptase Timing Matters: Getting the Lab Work Right in Anaphylaxis

## Why this case is worth talking about

This patient’s course was unusual enough on its own: clear anaphylaxis after clarithromycin, followed by recurrent relapses over the next 12 hours, with a workup that raised suspicion for underlying mast cell disease. But the real teaching point is quieter and more universal: the tryptase samples were not drawn at the ideal times.

That matters because tryptase is one of the few labs that can help confirm what your clinical exam already told you: this was anaphylaxis. When the timing is off, the diagnostic yield drops fast. And in a chaotic emergency setting, it is very easy to miss the narrow window.

## Tryptase is helpful, but it is not the diagnosis

Anaphylaxis remains a clinical diagnosis. You treat the patient, not the lab.

That said, tryptase can be useful when:

- the presentation is atypical
- the trigger is unclear
- there is concern for mast cell disease
- you want objective support for the diagnosis after the acute event

In this case, later testing showed persistently elevated tryptase and raised the possibility of systemic mastocytosis, though formal criteria were not met. That makes the lab even more relevant—but only if you collect it properly.

## The key concept: acute, peak, and baseline samples

The most important lesson from this case is that a single tryptase level is rarely enough.

The recommended approach is:

- **Acute sample:** as soon as feasible after symptom onset, without delaying treatment
- **Second sample:** ideally at **1–2 hours** after symptom onset, and **within 4 hours**
- **Baseline sample:** at least **24 hours after full clinical resolution**

Why this sequence?

Because tryptase typically rises during mast cell activation, peaks in the first few hours, and then falls toward baseline. If you only sample too early, too late, or without a baseline comparator, you can miss the signal entirely.

In this report, the first tryptase was obtained **1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode**, and the second sample was drawn **27 hours after the last recurrence**. That is better than nothing, but it was not the ideal sequence for interpreting the earliest events.

## What tryptase can and cannot tell you

Tryptase is most useful as supportive evidence of mast cell activation. It is not a perfect “yes/no” test for anaphylaxis.

A few practical points:

- A normal tryptase does **not** rule out anaphylaxis.
- Tryptase is more likely to be elevated in **severe, hypotensive, or systemic** reactions.
- Food-triggered anaphylaxis may have lower tryptase elevations than venom- or drug-triggered reactions.
- Persistent baseline elevation can suggest an underlying mast cell disorder, but you need the broader context.

This case fits that theme: the patient had severe recurrent episodes and a later workup suggesting mast cell disease, which makes the tryptase data clinically meaningful even though the timing was imperfect.

## Why the samples get missed in real life

This is the part that feels painfully familiar.

In the ED, the priorities are exactly what they should be:

- epinephrine
- airway/breathing/circulation support
- oxygen
- fluids
- close monitoring

Tryptase can easily fall off the radar because:

- anaphylaxis is time-critical, and labs feel secondary
- symptoms may improve after epinephrine, reducing urgency in the team’s mind
- staff may not know the timing rules
- the patient may move from ED to ward to ICU, and the sampling plan gets fragmented
- recurrent episodes create confusion about which “onset” time to anchor to

This case is a good reminder that documentation and workflow matter as much as awareness.

## A simple system can improve yield

The solution is not to slow down treatment. It is to build a process that runs in parallel with treatment.

A workable ED approach:

1. **Treat anaphylaxis immediately**
   - epinephrine first
   - do not wait for labs

2. **Order tryptase early**
   - ideally at the time of initial recognition

3. **Label the sample with the event time**
   - document symptom onset, epinephrine time, and sample time

4. **Plan the baseline draw before the patient leaves acute care**
   - ideally after complete resolution and at least 24 hours later

5. **Communicate across teams**
   - ED, ward, ICU, and allergy/immunology should all know the plan

A nursing prompt, ED order set, or anaphylaxis checklist can make this much more reliable than relying on memory during a resuscitation.

## How this case changes practice

The patient’s recurrent multiphasic course, later tryptase abnormalities, and possible mast cell disease all make the diagnostic trail important. But the key pearl is more general: if you are going to use tryptase, you need to respect the clock.

This is one of those tests where “close enough” can become “non-diagnostic.”

## Take-home points

- **Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis; tryptase is supportive, not definitive.**
- **Draw tryptase in a structured sequence:** acute sample, repeat at 1–2 hours, and baseline after recovery.
- **Do not delay epinephrine to obtain labs.**
- **Missed timing is common in real-world ED care, especially when patients improve quickly or move between units.**
- **System fixes beat memory:** order sets, checklists, and clear handoffs improve diagnostic yield.
- **Persistent baseline tryptase elevation should raise concern for mast cell disease in the right clinical context.**

In short: if you suspect anaphylaxis and want the lab to help you later, the best time to think about tryptase is right now.
