Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the recommended timing of serum tryptase measurements in suspected anaphylaxis, and how do elevated acute and persistently elevated baseline tryptase levels change your diagnostic reasoning in this case?

In suspected anaphylaxis, serum tryptase should be measured **serially**: obtain an **initial sample as soon as feasible** after presentation **without delaying treatment**, then a **second sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset** (and **within 4 hours**), when tryptase typically peaks, and a **third sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution** to establish the patient’s baseline level. That is the key point emphasized by this case report, in which the recommended sampling windows were not met initially.

How to interpret the results: an **elevated acute tryptase** supports that the event was a true **mast-cell–mediated anaphylactic reaction**, particularly when the clinical picture is less clear or when there are recurrent episodes. In this patient, the elevated tryptase during the third episode strengthened the diagnosis of anaphylaxis as the explanation for the recurrent shock-like episodes.

A **persistently elevated baseline tryptase** is a different and very important clue. It does **not** mean ongoing anaphylaxis; rather, it suggests an **underlying mast-cell disorder or increased mast-cell burden**, such as **systemic mastocytosis** or related clonal mast-cell disease. In this case, the persistently elevated baseline tryptase shifted the diagnostic reasoning away from “ordinary drug allergy alone” toward an **underlying mast cell activation predisposition**, which fit with the report’s conclusion of a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis despite not meeting full formal criteria. In other words: **acute elevation confirms the event; elevated baseline raises suspicion for an underlying mast-cell disorder and may explain unusually severe, recurrent, or multiphasic anaphylaxis.**
