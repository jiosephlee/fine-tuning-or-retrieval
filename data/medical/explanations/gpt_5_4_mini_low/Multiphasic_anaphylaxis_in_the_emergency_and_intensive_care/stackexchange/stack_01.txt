Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features in this patient establish the diagnosis of anaphylaxis, and which findings support classifying the initial presentation as anaphylactic shock rather than a limited allergic reaction?

This patient meets the clinical diagnosis of **anaphylaxis** because she had a **rapid-onset, systemic allergic reaction** after clarithromycin with **multiorgan involvement**: prominent **cutaneous and mucosal features** (generalized erythema, urticaria, facial swelling, pruritus, flushing), **respiratory compromise** (hypoxemia with oxygen saturation 88% on room air and dyspnea/“throat swelling” sensation), and **circulatory involvement** (**hypotension** to 74/50 mm Hg). The temporal relationship is also classic: symptoms began about **1 hour after the first dose** and progressed quickly.

The initial presentation should be classified as **anaphylactic shock**, not a limited allergic reaction, because she had **hemodynamic collapse**—marked hypotension requiring epinephrine and intravenous fluids—together with **hypoxemia** and systemic symptoms. A limited allergic reaction would be expected to remain confined to skin or mild local symptoms, such as isolated rash, itching, or facial swelling, without cardiovascular instability or respiratory compromise. In her prior amoxicillin reaction, the symptoms were limited to facial swelling without rash, dyspnea, or hypotension; by contrast, this episode clearly crossed the threshold into **life-threatening anaphylaxis with shock**.
