Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] What is the current evidence regarding corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis and prevention of biphasic reactions, and how should that evidence influence management decisions in a patient like this?

The best current evidence does **not support routine corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis to prevent biphasic reactions**.

Historically, steroids were given because biphasic anaphylaxis can recur hours after apparent resolution, and corticosteroids were presumed to blunt the late inflammatory phase. But that practice is now increasingly questioned. As highlighted in this case report, a systematic review of 22 studies found that **only one** suggested benefit, whereas **21 found no evidence that corticosteroids reduce biphasic anaphylaxis**. More recent observational data have also raised concern that corticosteroid use may be associated with greater subsequent resource use, including ICU or ward admission, likely reflecting confounding by severity but offering no signal of clear benefit. Accordingly, most contemporary guidelines now **advise against routine corticosteroid administration** in anaphylaxis.

How should that affect management in a patient like this? The key point is that **epinephrine remains the definitive treatment**, and the management priority in recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis is **repeat intramuscular epinephrine, airway/breathing/circulation support, and adequate observation**, not steroids. In this patient, the recurrent shock episodes were appropriately treated with additional epinephrine and supportive care. The steroids she received after the first relapse reflect older practice patterns, but current evidence would not support relying on them to prevent further episodes.

So in practice:

- **Give epinephrine early and repeat it as needed**
- **Observe closely for recurrence**, especially after severe initial reactions or need for multiple epinephrine doses
- **Do not use corticosteroids routinely to prevent biphasic reactions**
- Reserve steroids, if used at all, for selected situations such as concomitant asthma exacerbation or prominent protracted symptoms, recognizing that they should **never delay epinephrine**

In short, this case underscores an important teaching point: **steroids are not proven prophylaxis against biphasic anaphylaxis; epinephrine and monitoring are the evidence-based interventions that matter.**
