Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# 0.3 mg Isn’t 0.5 mg: Are We Underdosing Adult Anaphylaxis?

## The case that should make us pause

A woman in her 60s, weighing 104 kg, took her first dose of clarithromycin and within an hour developed pruritus, flushing, throat swelling sensation, urticaria, hypoxemia, and hypotension. This was true anaphylactic shock. She got what many of us would reach for first in a time-crunched ED: an intramuscular epinephrine auto-injector, plus antihistamine, oxygen, and fluids.

She improved — briefly.

Over the next 12 hours, she had recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock. Four episodes in total, each requiring epinephrine. She ultimately needed ICU monitoring, and her course raised a bigger question than the trigger itself: if this patient weighed 104 kg, how much epinephrine was she actually getting, and was 0.3 mg enough?

That’s the uncomfortable part of this case. We all know epinephrine is the drug for anaphylaxis. But in adults, especially larger adults, the difference between 0.3 mg and 0.5 mg is not trivial.

## Why this matters

Anaphylaxis is one of those diagnoses where delay kills. The treatment priority is not subtle: epinephrine first, epinephrine early, epinephrine intramuscularly.

But in the real world, many adults are treated with 0.3 mg auto-injectors because they are fast, familiar, and immediately available. That is especially appealing in high-pressure settings, or in places where clinicians don’t give anaphylaxis treatment every day.

The problem? Guideline dosing for adults is typically 0.01 mg/kg IM, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For many adults — especially those over 50 kg — 0.3 mg is an underdose relative to that target.

This case is a good reminder that convenience can come with a pharmacologic cost.

## The tension: speed versus dose

There’s a reason auto-injectors are popular:

- they reduce hesitation
- they simplify administration
- they get epinephrine in faster
- they’re useful when staff may be unfamiliar with vasoactive drugs

That last point matters. In a busy ED, or on a ward where anaphylaxis is rare, an auto-injector can be the difference between treatment now and treatment after a frustrating delay.

But the tradeoff is obvious: a standard adult auto-injector dose of 0.3 mg may leave larger patients underdosed. In this case, the patient weighed 104 kg. A 0.01 mg/kg calculation would suggest 1.04 mg in theory, but practice caps the dose at 0.5 mg. That means the “adult” 0.3 mg device delivered only 60% of the usual maximum recommended IM dose.

That gap may matter, particularly in severe presentations.

## What this case adds

This patient didn’t just have a single episode of anaphylaxis. She had a multiphasic course with repeated relapses despite initial response. That makes it hard to know how much of the recurrence was driven by underdosing versus the biology of her reaction versus persistent mediator release.

Still, a few points stand out:

- She was a large adult, and 0.3 mg is below the commonly recommended 0.5 mg adult dose.
- Her initial treatment with 0.3 mg epinephrine produced only temporary stabilization.
- She required repeated epinephrine doses and ICU-level monitoring.
- Her later doses were 0.5 mg intramuscularly.
- The severity of symptoms lessened with each episode, but the course still illustrates how anaphylaxis can relapse even after apparent initial improvement.

This doesn’t prove that 0.3 mg caused the multiphasic course. But it does reinforce that underdosing may contribute to persistent instability, repeat dosing, and escalation of care.

## Key clinical pearl: don’t confuse “adult” with “adequately dosed”

A lot of clinicians think of 0.3 mg as the default adult epinephrine dose because that’s what many auto-injectors contain. But guideline-based IM epinephrine dosing is weight-based, and the common adult ceiling is 0.5 mg.

For a substantial adult, 0.3 mg may be the dose you can give quickly — but it is not always the dose you ultimately want.

This distinction is especially important in:

- severe anaphylaxis with hypotension or hypoxemia
- larger adults
- patients needing repeat epinephrine
- settings where auto-injectors are being used as a bridge, not the final word

## Should we still use auto-injectors in the ED?

Yes — sometimes.

This case report makes a pragmatic argument that many clinicians will recognize. In time-sensitive settings, or where hesitation could delay treatment, a readily available auto-injector may be better than a perfectly calculated dose that never gets given.

That said, the presence of an auto-injector should not lull us into thinking the job is done. If the patient is large, unstable, or not responding as expected, escalate early:

- reassess the diagnosis
- repeat IM epinephrine promptly if needed
- give fluids aggressively
- move to a higher level of monitoring
- consider IV epinephrine infusion in refractory cases, with appropriate expertise and monitoring

## Steroids and antihistamines: useful adjuncts, not the main act

This case also reflects a common historical pattern: after recurrence, corticosteroids and antihistamines were added. But the modern anaphylaxis literature has moved away from routine steroids as a strategy to prevent biphasic reactions. Evidence for benefit is weak, and some data suggest harm or at least no meaningful advantage.

The core treatment remains epinephrine. Not antihistamines. Not steroids. Epinephrine.

Antihistamines may help with skin symptoms. Steroids may still be used in some protocols. But neither should distract from inadequate epinephrine dosing.

## Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, but it changes the risk conversation

The patient’s course was unusual because it was not just biphasic — it was multiphasic. That rarity is part of what makes case reports like this useful. There are no perfect predictors, no universally agreed observation period, and no robust evidence-based algorithm for these prolonged relapsing cases.

Still, this case reminds us that patients with:

- severe initial anaphylaxis
- hypotension
- hypoxemia
- recurrent symptoms
- multiple epinephrine doses

deserve close monitoring and a low threshold for ICU-level observation.

And in those patients, getting the first epinephrine dose right matters even more.

## Take-home points

- **0.3 mg IM epinephrine is not equivalent to the usual adult target dose of 0.5 mg.**
- **In larger adults, 0.3 mg may be a meaningful underdose.**
- **Auto-injectors are fast and practical, but convenience should not replace dose awareness.**
- **Repeat epinephrine, fluids, and escalation of monitoring are essential when symptoms recur or hemodynamics remain unstable.**
- **Steroids and antihistamines are adjuncts; epinephrine remains the treatment that changes outcomes.**
- **Severe or recurrent anaphylaxis should trigger consideration of higher-level care and, in selected refractory cases, IV epinephrine expertise.**

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that adult anaphylaxis dosing is not “one size fits all.” A 104 kg patient who gets 0.3 mg IM epinephrine is not getting the guideline-recommended adult maximum dose. Sometimes that’s an acceptable bridge. Sometimes it may be the difference between rapid stabilization and recurrent shock.

In anaphylaxis, speed matters — but so does dose.
