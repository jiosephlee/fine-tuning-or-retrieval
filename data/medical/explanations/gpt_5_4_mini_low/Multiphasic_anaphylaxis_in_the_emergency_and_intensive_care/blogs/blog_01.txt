Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# When Anaphylaxis Comes Back Again: Managing Biphasic and Multiphasic Relapse in the ED and ICU

## The case that should make you uncomfortable

This case is unsettling for a simple reason: the patient *responded* to treatment, then relapsed, then relapsed again, and again. That’s exactly the sort of course that can lull clinicians into thinking the storm has passed—right before it hits again.

A woman in her 60s developed severe anaphylaxis after her first dose of clarithromycin, with hypotension, hypoxemia, flushing, urticaria, facial swelling, and agitation. She improved after intramuscular epinephrine, but then had recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock over the next 12 hours, ultimately totaling four episodes. She required repeated epinephrine, supplemental oxygen, IV fluids, and ICU-level monitoring before the relapses stopped.

That pattern raises the uncomfortable question at the bedside: are we seeing true biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, refractory anaphylaxis, or just ongoing absorption with transient improvement?

## Why this matters in real life

Anaphylaxis is usually taught as a diagnosis-and-treat-now problem. Epinephrine is the key move, and most patients improve quickly. The challenge comes after the first response.

A recurrent reaction after apparent resolution can mean different things:

- **Biphasic anaphylaxis**: symptoms recur after a symptom-free interval, without new allergen exposure.
- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis**: more than one recurrence after initial resolution.
- **Refractory anaphylaxis**: ongoing symptoms that never truly clear, or clear only briefly with incomplete response.
- **Ongoing allergen absorption**: especially relevant with oral exposures or sustained-release medications.

Clinically, these distinctions matter because they affect monitoring, disposition, and how aggressively you escalate care. But in the ED, they can be nearly impossible to separate in real time.

## Definitions are useful, but the bedside is messy

The Resuscitation Council UK definition of biphasic anaphylaxis is a recurrence of symptoms hours after apparent resolution, without further allergen exposure. Refractory anaphylaxis is different: it’s persistent anaphylaxis with inadequate response to appropriately administered epinephrine.

That sounds clean on paper. In practice, the boundary is blurry.

A patient who improves after epinephrine and then deteriorates again could have:

- true biphasic relapse
- delayed absorption of the trigger
- underdosed epinephrine
- ongoing mediator release from severe initial disease
- inadequate observation with premature de-escalation

This is why serial bedside reassessment matters more than trying to label the pattern too early.

## What this case teaches about relapse risk

This patient had several features that should make you think hard about prolonged monitoring:

- **Severe initial presentation**: hypotension and hypoxemia
- **Need for repeated epinephrine**
- **Relapse within hours**
- **Possible underlying mast cell disorder**
- **Adult weight >50 kg with initial 0.3 mg autoinjector dosing**

Those are not subtle clues. The important point is not that every patient with anaphylaxis needs ICU admission. It’s that once you’ve seen recurrence, your threshold for escalation should drop dramatically.

## The epinephrine issue nobody likes to talk about

This case also highlights a practical problem: many autoinjectors used in adult care contain **0.3 mg**, even though guideline dosing for adults is generally **0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg**.

For a 104 kg patient, 0.3 mg is a meaningful underdose.

That doesn’t mean autoinjectors are “bad”—they’re fast, familiar, and can be lifesaving, especially when delay is the biggest danger. But in a monitored medical setting, especially when a patient is large, severely ill, or already relapsing, you should think deliberately about whether you’re giving enough epinephrine and whether you need to escalate beyond repeat IM dosing.

## Corticosteroids: still common, but not the hero here

This case followed a common pattern: after relapse, corticosteroids were added because of the traditional belief that they might prevent biphasic reactions.

The problem is that the evidence doesn’t really support that practice. Contemporary literature increasingly shows no clear benefit for preventing relapse, and some studies suggest potential harm or at least no upside.

So the lesson is not “never give steroids,” but rather:

- don’t let steroids distract you from epinephrine
- don’t assume steroids will prevent relapse
- don’t use steroids as a substitute for appropriate monitoring

In other words: steroids may still be in the toolkit, but they’re not the cornerstone of anaphylaxis management.

## When should relapse push you toward ICU?

There’s no perfect rule, but this case supports a low threshold for ICU-level monitoring when relapse is already happening.

Think ICU or equivalent monitored setting if you have:

- recurrent hypotension
- repeated epinephrine requirement
- airway compromise or escalating respiratory symptoms
- severe initial shock
- concern for multiple further relapses
- need for an epinephrine infusion or close hemodynamic titration

The big practical issue is that biphasic/multiphasic reactions can recur outside the window of a standard ED observation period. A patient who looks “fixed” may not actually be safe.

## Observation: not every patient needs the same clock

The literature gives a wide range for biphasic anaphylaxis incidence, and timing of recurrence is variable. Some relapses happen within a few hours; others occur much later. That means a one-size-fits-all observation period is imperfect.

Risk stratification is probably the best we have:

- lower-risk patients may be observed for a shorter period after full resolution
- higher-risk patients deserve longer observation and/or admission
- patients who have already relapsed are no longer in the “routine observation” bucket

This case is a reminder that once recurrence occurs, you’re no longer asking, “How long should I watch this?” You’re asking, “How many more times is this going to happen, and do I have the right place to manage it?”

## A note on mast cell disease

The patient was later found to have a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. That’s not the same as saying mastocytosis caused the multiphasic course—but it does raise suspicion that an abnormal mast cell milieu may have amplified severity and recurrence risk.

This matters because patients with mast cell disorders can have:

- more severe reactions
- unusual or prolonged courses
- increased sensitivity to triggers

So if the clinical pattern feels disproportionate, don’t just anchor on the visible trigger. Ask whether there’s a deeper predisposition.

## Practical bedside approach

When anaphylaxis improves and then starts coming back, I’d think in this order:

1. **Re-dose epinephrine promptly**
2. **Reassess airway, breathing, circulation, and skin**
3. **Give fluids if there’s shock**
4. **Escalate to monitored care if relapse occurs**
5. **Consider whether the trigger could still be absorbing**
6. **Stop anchoring on “it got better once”**
7. **Treat the next episode as real anaphylaxis until proven otherwise**

And if the patient is already relapsing, don’t waste time debating labels more than necessary. The physiology is telling you the patient is not out of danger.

## Take-home points

- **Recurrent symptoms after initial improvement are a big deal**—don’t assume the first response means the danger is over.
- **Biphasic, multiphasic, refractory, and ongoing absorption can look similar at the bedside.**
- **Repeated epinephrine needs should push you toward ICU-level monitoring or an equivalent closely observed setting.**
- **Adult autoinjector dosing may underdose larger patients** compared with guideline-recommended adult dosing.
- **Corticosteroids are not reliable prevention for biphasic anaphylaxis** and should not drive management decisions.
- **If the reaction is relapsing, keep treating anaphylaxis, not the label.**

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that anaphylaxis is not always a one-and-done event. Some patients improve, then worsen again—sometimes more than once. In those patients, the real management question is not “Was this biphasic?” It’s “How do I keep this patient alive through the next recurrence?”

And the answer is usually the same as the first time: epinephrine, fluids, reassessment, and a much lower threshold to monitor them like they’re not done yet.
