Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Observation After Anaphylaxis: Who Needs 4 Hours, Who Needs 24?

## The case that breaks the “4-hour rule”

Most of us have been taught some version of this: treat anaphylaxis fast, watch the patient for a few hours, and if they look good, they can go home. That works for a lot of patients.

But not for all of them.

This case is a reminder that anaphylaxis doesn’t always respect our neat observation windows. A woman in her 60s had a clear, severe anaphylactic reaction after her first dose of clarithromycin, improved after intramuscular epinephrine, and then relapsed repeatedly over the next 12 hours. In total, she had four anaphylactic episodes. Each recurrence required more epinephrine. She ultimately needed ICU-level monitoring, and her course raised concern for an underlying mast cell disorder.

That is the key problem: a patient can look fully rescued after the first dose of epinephrine and still be sitting on a time bomb.

## What makes biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis so tricky?

Anaphylaxis is usually straightforward at the bedside: sudden onset, multi-system involvement, and a trigger that often makes sense in retrospect. Biphasic and multiphasic reactions are the messy exceptions.

A biphasic reaction is recurrence of anaphylaxis after apparent resolution, without re-exposure. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is the more dramatic version: multiple relapses, again without repeat allergen exposure.

The problem is not just that these events happen. It’s that we do not have a universally reliable way to predict who will relapse, when they will relapse, or how bad the relapse will be.

In this case, the patient initially responded to treatment, then relapsed again and again despite stopping clarithromycin and receiving appropriate emergency care. The intensity of symptoms decreased with each recurrence, but the pattern still forced escalation in monitoring and treatment.

## Why fixed observation windows are attractive — and imperfect

Observation periods after anaphylaxis are popular because they are simple.

A 4-hour window is easy to remember, operationally convenient, and probably reasonable for many low-risk patients. But “reasonable for many” is not the same as “safe for all.” Literature on biphasic anaphylaxis shows a wide reported incidence, and recurrence can happen well beyond the first few hours. In this case, relapse occurred within 12 hours, which is squarely inside the range that makes a short observation period feel uncomfortable in hindsight.

The issue is not that a single universal observation time is wrong in every case. It’s that it is blunt. It treats all anaphylaxis as if the risk profile were identical, when it clearly is not.

## What features seem to raise concern for relapse?

We still do not have a validated crystal ball, but several features are repeatedly discussed as possible risk markers for biphasic or more complicated courses:

- Severe initial presentation
- Delay in epinephrine administration
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses
- Prior biphasic reaction
- Ongoing symptoms or incomplete resolution
- Possible mast cell disorders or elevated baseline tryptase

This patient had several concerning features. She presented with hypotension and hypoxemia, had diffuse cutaneous findings, and required repeated epinephrine for recurrent shock. Later workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not met.

That combination should make anyone pause before sending the patient home after a short observation period.

## The epinephrine lesson: don’t underdose the patient in front of you

One practical wrinkle in this case is worth emphasizing: the first two doses were 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors, even though the patient weighed 104 kg and guideline-recommended adult dosing is often simplified to 0.5 mg intramuscularly.

Auto-injectors are fast, and speed matters. In many environments, they are the best way to avoid hesitation. But in a large adult, 0.3 mg may be a smaller dose than we really want, especially when the patient is in shock.

That matters for observation strategy too. If the first dose is potentially submaximal, the threshold to worry about recurrence should probably drop.

## Steroids: still common, not clearly helpful

This case also reflects a common real-world pattern: corticosteroids were added after recurrence, largely out of concern for biphasic reactions.

That is understandable, but the evidence base is weak. Modern reviews and guidelines increasingly question routine steroid use in anaphylaxis, and they do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions. In this case, steroids were given, but the patient still had relapses before eventual stabilization.

The broader point: steroids should not be used as a substitute for adequate epinephrine, adequate fluids, and adequate observation.

## So who needs 4 hours?

A short observation period may be reasonable for patients who rapidly and completely respond, have no hypotension, no respiratory compromise, no need for repeated epinephrine, and no other red flags.

These are the patients who look like true low-risk cases: clean response, stable vitals, no recurrence, and a clear plan for avoidance and follow-up.

## Who needs longer?

This is where the case gets clinically useful. Patients who have any of the following deserve serious consideration for prolonged observation, admission, or higher-acuity monitoring:

- Hypotension or shock at presentation
- Hypoxemia or significant respiratory involvement
- Need for more than one dose of epinephrine
- Recurrent symptoms after initial improvement
- Concern for mast cell disease or elevated baseline tryptase
- Uncertain diagnosis or uncertain trigger
- Social or logistical barriers to safe discharge

In this patient, the relapsing course itself was the decisive warning sign. Once the first recurrence happened, the question stopped being “Can we observe a little longer?” and became “How much monitoring is enough to catch the next relapse?”

## A risk-stratified approach makes more sense than a universal clock

The cleanest lesson from this case is that observation after anaphylaxis should be risk-stratified.

A patient with isolated urticaria that resolves and never touches the airway, blood pressure, or oxygenation is not the same as a patient with shock who needs repeated epinephrine. Lumping them together under one observation rule is appealing for workflow, but it is not clinically satisfying.

A practical approach is to think in tiers:

- **Low risk:** rapid, complete response; no hypotension; no respiratory compromise; no repeat epinephrine
- **Intermediate risk:** severe initial symptoms but stable after treatment, or features that make relapse harder to dismiss
- **High risk:** recurrent symptoms, shock, multiple epinephrine doses, or concern for mast cell disease

That framework is not a rigid guideline, but it is more honest than pretending 4 hours is a universal answer.

## Take-home points

- Biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon, but they can be dangerous and unpredictable.
- A fixed observation window is a blunt tool; severe presentations and recurrent symptoms deserve longer monitoring.
- Repeated epinephrine requirement should immediately raise concern for relapse and admission needs.
- Steroids are not a reliable strategy to prevent biphasic reactions.
- In adults, 0.3 mg epinephrine may underdose large patients compared with the commonly recommended 0.5 mg IM dose.
- Patients with suspected mast cell disorders may warrant a lower threshold for prolonged observation and specialist follow-up.

## Bottom line

The question is not really “4 hours or 24 hours?”

The better question is: what is this patient telling you about their risk of relapse?

In a low-risk patient, a short observation period may be enough. In a patient like this one—shock, hypoxemia, and repeated relapses within 12 hours—short observation would have been false reassurance. The right answer is not a universal time cutoff. It’s a risk-based plan that matches the biology of the reaction in front of you.
