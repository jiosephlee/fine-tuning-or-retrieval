Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 2: ## Immediate Emergency Management: First-Line Therapy, Supportive Care, and Early Disposition

The first priorities in suspected anaphylaxis are the same as in any time-critical resuscitation: assess and support the airway, breathing, and circulation, and remove or stop the offending trigger if possible. In this case, clarithromycin was discontinued immediately after the initial reaction, which is essential because ongoing exposure can prolong or worsen the episode. Anaphylaxis is primarily a clinical diagnosis, so treatment should not be delayed while waiting for laboratory confirmation or extensive testing.

The cornerstone of treatment is intramuscular epinephrine, given as early as possible. Epinephrine reverses the major pathophysiologic features of anaphylaxis by causing vasoconstriction, improving blood pressure and mucosal edema; bronchodilation, improving respiratory symptoms; and reduced mediator release from mast cells and basophils. It is also the treatment most clearly associated with improved outcomes, especially in anaphylactic shock. In this patient, the first dose was given promptly in the emergency department as a 0.3 mg intramuscular auto-injector dose, together with clemastine, oxygen, and intravenous normal saline. Her initial clinical response was appropriate for effective epinephrine treatment: blood pressure improved, skin findings lessened, and oxygen could be discontinued temporarily after stabilization.

Supportive care is important, but it does not replace epinephrine. Oxygen is indicated when hypoxaemia or respiratory distress is present, and this patient appropriately received high-flow supplemental oxygen during the initial resuscitation. Intravenous fluids are particularly important in anaphylactic shock because widespread vasodilation and capillary leak can produce profound relative hypovolaemia. A bolus of isotonic saline helps restore preload and improve perfusion, especially when hypotension is present as in this case. Antihistamines such as clemastine can help with pruritus, urticaria, and flushing, but they do not treat airway compromise or shock and should be viewed only as adjunctive therapy.

The case also illustrates a practical dosing issue. Guideline dosing for epinephrine in anaphylaxis is 0.01 mg/kg intramuscularly, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg in adults. For many adults over 50 kg, this effectively means a 0.5 mg dose. The auto-injector used here delivered 0.3 mg, which is common in many settings and is often chosen because it is immediately available and easy to administer quickly. That speed matters in real-life emergencies, especially in less familiar environments where hesitation can delay treatment. However, for a 104 kg adult, 0.3 mg is below the usual recommended adult dose and may contribute to under-treatment, particularly in severe reactions. In this patient, repeated episodes required repeat epinephrine administration, highlighting the possibility that the initial dose was not enough to fully suppress the reaction.

A key teaching point is that an apparent initial response does not guarantee that the episode is over. Biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis can recur after a symptom-free interval, and severe initial presentation, need for repeated epinephrine, and recurrent shock all increase concern for further deterioration. For this reason, patients with significant anaphylaxis require ongoing observation even after early improvement. This patient was initially admitted to a monitored ward, but after the first relapse into anaphylactic shock she required transfer to the ICU for closer observation and rapid treatment of further episodes. The escalation was appropriate because she had recurrent hypotension and hypoxaemia, needed repeated epinephrine doses, and had a clinical course that was no longer predictable enough for standard ward-level monitoring.

This case also shows why epinephrine auto-injectors can be useful even in hospital settings. They are fast to prepare and administer, which is an advantage when treatment must not be delayed. Their limitation is dosing: many devices provide 0.3 mg, which may be suboptimal for larger adults when compared with the 0.5 mg guideline maximum. In patients with severe reactions, especially those with recurrent episodes, clinicians should be ready to transition from auto-injector use to weight-appropriate manually prepared intramuscular dosing and consider higher-acuity monitoring.

Overall, this early management phase demonstrates the practical sequence of anaphylaxis care: immediate trigger removal, early intramuscular epinephrine, supportive oxygen and fluids, adjunctive antihistamines for skin symptoms, and escalation of disposition when severe or recurrent shock occurs. The central lesson is that initial stabilization may not be enough; recurrent anaphylaxis requires a low threshold for ICU-level observation and repeated treatment.
