Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Recognizing Severe Anaphylaxis and the Clinical Problem of Multiphasic Reactions

Anaphylaxis is a rapidly developing, potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction. At the bedside, it is recognized by the combination of sudden onset and involvement of more than one organ system, typically the skin or mucosa together with respiratory and/or cardiovascular compromise. Classic features include flushing, urticaria, angioedema, throat tightness, wheeze, dyspnoea, hypotension, syncope, and gastrointestinal symptoms. In the emergency setting, the diagnosis is clinical and treatment should not wait for confirmatory testing. In this case, the patient’s pruritus, flushing, generalized erythema and urticaria, facial swelling, hypoxaemia, and marked hypotension soon after taking clarithromycin fit severe anaphylaxis, and the low blood pressure and oxygen saturation indicate progression to anaphylactic shock.

The timing strongly supported clarithromycin as the culprit. Her symptoms began about 1 hour after the first oral dose, which is typical for an immediate drug-triggered reaction. Although she had recently developed a rash while taking clindamycin, that eruption was limited to the skin and had already prompted the antibiotic switch. By contrast, the subsequent episode after clarithromycin had the full systemic pattern expected of true anaphylaxis. In practice, this distinction matters because a preceding nonspecific rash does not exclude a later, separate and more severe allergic reaction to a different drug.

This case also illustrates why recurrent anaphylaxis can be difficult to classify. Biphasic anaphylaxis refers to recurrence of symptoms after an initial complete resolution, without re-exposure to the allergen. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is the same phenomenon repeated more than once, with multiple relapses after apparent recovery. Refractory anaphylaxis is different: symptoms persist despite appropriate treatment, especially despite correctly administered epinephrine, rather than resolving and then returning. Sustained or prolonged anaphylaxis lies somewhere between these concepts and is used when there is incomplete or only transient response with ongoing allergen effect or slow absorption. In real cases, these categories can overlap clinically, and it can be hard to decide whether a new deterioration represents a true new phase or simply incomplete control of the original episode.

The timing of recurrence helps frame the diagnosis. Biphasic reactions most often recur within the first 6 to 12 hours, though reports describe later relapses up to several days after the initial event. Multiphasic courses are uncommon and involve more than one recurrence, as in this patient, who had four episodes in total. The literature reports a very wide incidence for biphasic anaphylaxis, from well under 1% to more than 20%, largely because studies use different definitions, include different severity mixes, and apply different observation periods. This variability is one reason why guidelines remain cautious and why observation after apparent recovery is still recommended.

Clinically, the key point is that recurrence can occur without any new allergen exposure. That means a patient who initially improves after epinephrine is not necessarily safe to discharge immediately, especially if the first episode was severe. This case showed several features that have been proposed as markers of higher relapse risk: severe initial presentation with shock, the need for repeated epinephrine, and ongoing symptoms despite initial improvement. These associations are not perfectly predictive, but they explain why patients with severe anaphylaxis are typically monitored after stabilization and why recurrent episodes such as this one create a distinct emergency and intensive care management challenge.
