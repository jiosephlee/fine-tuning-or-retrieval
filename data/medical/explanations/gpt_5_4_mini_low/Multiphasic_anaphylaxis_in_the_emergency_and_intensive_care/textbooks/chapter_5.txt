Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 5: ## Recovery, Long-Term Prevention, and Clinical Lessons for Practice

After the patient’s fourth anaphylactic episode, no further relapses occurred. Once she had remained symptom-free for about 24 hours, she was stepped down from ICU-level monitoring back to a standard ward and was discharged after a total hospital stay of 3 days. This course illustrates an important practical point: in multiphasic anaphylaxis, a patient may appear to have stabilized and then deteriorate again several hours later, so disposition decisions must be based on the full clinical trajectory rather than on the first response to epinephrine alone.

Secondary prevention focused on reducing the risk of future reactions and ensuring rapid self-treatment if an episode recurs. She was started on levocetirizine and was prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector for emergency use. Equally important, she was taught how and when to use the device, and she would need a clear action plan for future symptoms, including immediate epinephrine administration and urgent medical evaluation afterward. In any patient discharged after severe anaphylaxis, education is part of treatment: the goal is not only to provide medication, but also to ensure the patient recognizes early symptoms and responds without delay.

Because clarithromycin was the suspected trigger, she was counseled to avoid this drug in the future and the allergy history should be documented clearly in her records. The case also highlights why a previous “mild” beta-lactam reaction in adolescence did not reliably predict the severity of her later episode. Drug allergy histories can be informative, but they do not reliably forecast future reaction severity, and prior limited reactions do not exclude the possibility of life-threatening anaphylaxis on re-exposure to a different agent.

Follow-up with allergology was appropriate because the presentation raised concern for an underlying mast cell disorder, including possible systemic mastocytosis. Even though formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met, the persistently elevated tryptase and other findings warranted continued specialist assessment. In severe or atypical anaphylaxis, follow-up is not only about confirming the trigger; it may also uncover predisposing conditions that change future risk.

From a management standpoint, this case reinforces several key lessons. First, epinephrine should never be delayed in suspected anaphylaxis, and adults generally require adequate intramuscular dosing; underdosing may contribute to incomplete control and recurrent symptoms. Second, recurrent anaphylaxis requires prolonged observation and individualized disposition, often with escalation from ward to ICU if symptoms recur or become more severe. Third, corticosteroids should not be relied on to prevent biphasic reactions, because current evidence does not support that assumption. Their use should not replace epinephrine or observation, and routine administration remains controversial.

The case also underscores the tension between resource stewardship and patient safety. Most anaphylaxis cases resolve after standard treatment, but a small subset relapses unpredictably. Observation protocols therefore need to balance the burden of prolonged monitoring against the risk of missing a dangerous recurrence. For rare and complex presentations such as multiphasic anaphylaxis, the literature remains limited, and current practice still depends heavily on expert judgment.

For that reason, this type of case is valuable beyond the individual patient. Better consensus guidance is needed for ED and ICU management of recurrent anaphylaxis, including observation duration, escalation strategies, and the role of adjunctive therapies. Structured case registries would help capture clinical features, treatments, and outcomes in these uncommon presentations and could eventually support more evidence-based recommendations for future patients.
