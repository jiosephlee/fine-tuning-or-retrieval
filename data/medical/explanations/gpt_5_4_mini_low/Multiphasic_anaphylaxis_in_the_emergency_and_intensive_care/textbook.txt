Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Recognizing Severe Anaphylaxis and the Clinical Problem of Multiphasic Reactions

Anaphylaxis is a rapidly developing, potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction. At the bedside, it is recognized by the combination of sudden onset and involvement of more than one organ system, typically the skin or mucosa together with respiratory and/or cardiovascular compromise. Classic features include flushing, urticaria, angioedema, throat tightness, wheeze, dyspnoea, hypotension, syncope, and gastrointestinal symptoms. In the emergency setting, the diagnosis is clinical and treatment should not wait for confirmatory testing. In this case, the patient’s pruritus, flushing, generalized erythema and urticaria, facial swelling, hypoxaemia, and marked hypotension soon after taking clarithromycin fit severe anaphylaxis, and the low blood pressure and oxygen saturation indicate progression to anaphylactic shock.

The timing strongly supported clarithromycin as the culprit. Her symptoms began about 1 hour after the first oral dose, which is typical for an immediate drug-triggered reaction. Although she had recently developed a rash while taking clindamycin, that eruption was limited to the skin and had already prompted the antibiotic switch. By contrast, the subsequent episode after clarithromycin had the full systemic pattern expected of true anaphylaxis. In practice, this distinction matters because a preceding nonspecific rash does not exclude a later, separate and more severe allergic reaction to a different drug.

This case also illustrates why recurrent anaphylaxis can be difficult to classify. Biphasic anaphylaxis refers to recurrence of symptoms after an initial complete resolution, without re-exposure to the allergen. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is the same phenomenon repeated more than once, with multiple relapses after apparent recovery. Refractory anaphylaxis is different: symptoms persist despite appropriate treatment, especially despite correctly administered epinephrine, rather than resolving and then returning. Sustained or prolonged anaphylaxis lies somewhere between these concepts and is used when there is incomplete or only transient response with ongoing allergen effect or slow absorption. In real cases, these categories can overlap clinically, and it can be hard to decide whether a new deterioration represents a true new phase or simply incomplete control of the original episode.

The timing of recurrence helps frame the diagnosis. Biphasic reactions most often recur within the first 6 to 12 hours, though reports describe later relapses up to several days after the initial event. Multiphasic courses are uncommon and involve more than one recurrence, as in this patient, who had four episodes in total. The literature reports a very wide incidence for biphasic anaphylaxis, from well under 1% to more than 20%, largely because studies use different definitions, include different severity mixes, and apply different observation periods. This variability is one reason why guidelines remain cautious and why observation after apparent recovery is still recommended.

Clinically, the key point is that recurrence can occur without any new allergen exposure. That means a patient who initially improves after epinephrine is not necessarily safe to discharge immediately, especially if the first episode was severe. This case showed several features that have been proposed as markers of higher relapse risk: severe initial presentation with shock, the need for repeated epinephrine, and ongoing symptoms despite initial improvement. These associations are not perfectly predictive, but they explain why patients with severe anaphylaxis are typically monitored after stabilization and why recurrent episodes such as this one create a distinct emergency and intensive care management challenge.

## Immediate Emergency Management: First-Line Therapy, Supportive Care, and Early Disposition

The first priorities in suspected anaphylaxis are the same as in any time-critical resuscitation: assess and support the airway, breathing, and circulation, and remove or stop the offending trigger if possible. In this case, clarithromycin was discontinued immediately after the initial reaction, which is essential because ongoing exposure can prolong or worsen the episode. Anaphylaxis is primarily a clinical diagnosis, so treatment should not be delayed while waiting for laboratory confirmation or extensive testing.

The cornerstone of treatment is intramuscular epinephrine, given as early as possible. Epinephrine reverses the major pathophysiologic features of anaphylaxis by causing vasoconstriction, improving blood pressure and mucosal edema; bronchodilation, improving respiratory symptoms; and reduced mediator release from mast cells and basophils. It is also the treatment most clearly associated with improved outcomes, especially in anaphylactic shock. In this patient, the first dose was given promptly in the emergency department as a 0.3 mg intramuscular auto-injector dose, together with clemastine, oxygen, and intravenous normal saline. Her initial clinical response was appropriate for effective epinephrine treatment: blood pressure improved, skin findings lessened, and oxygen could be discontinued temporarily after stabilization.

Supportive care is important, but it does not replace epinephrine. Oxygen is indicated when hypoxaemia or respiratory distress is present, and this patient appropriately received high-flow supplemental oxygen during the initial resuscitation. Intravenous fluids are particularly important in anaphylactic shock because widespread vasodilation and capillary leak can produce profound relative hypovolaemia. A bolus of isotonic saline helps restore preload and improve perfusion, especially when hypotension is present as in this case. Antihistamines such as clemastine can help with pruritus, urticaria, and flushing, but they do not treat airway compromise or shock and should be viewed only as adjunctive therapy.

The case also illustrates a practical dosing issue. Guideline dosing for epinephrine in anaphylaxis is 0.01 mg/kg intramuscularly, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg in adults. For many adults over 50 kg, this effectively means a 0.5 mg dose. The auto-injector used here delivered 0.3 mg, which is common in many settings and is often chosen because it is immediately available and easy to administer quickly. That speed matters in real-life emergencies, especially in less familiar environments where hesitation can delay treatment. However, for a 104 kg adult, 0.3 mg is below the usual recommended adult dose and may contribute to under-treatment, particularly in severe reactions. In this patient, repeated episodes required repeat epinephrine administration, highlighting the possibility that the initial dose was not enough to fully suppress the reaction.

A key teaching point is that an apparent initial response does not guarantee that the episode is over. Biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis can recur after a symptom-free interval, and severe initial presentation, need for repeated epinephrine, and recurrent shock all increase concern for further deterioration. For this reason, patients with significant anaphylaxis require ongoing observation even after early improvement. This patient was initially admitted to a monitored ward, but after the first relapse into anaphylactic shock she required transfer to the ICU for closer observation and rapid treatment of further episodes. The escalation was appropriate because she had recurrent hypotension and hypoxaemia, needed repeated epinephrine doses, and had a clinical course that was no longer predictable enough for standard ward-level monitoring.

This case also shows why epinephrine auto-injectors can be useful even in hospital settings. They are fast to prepare and administer, which is an advantage when treatment must not be delayed. Their limitation is dosing: many devices provide 0.3 mg, which may be suboptimal for larger adults when compared with the 0.5 mg guideline maximum. In patients with severe reactions, especially those with recurrent episodes, clinicians should be ready to transition from auto-injector use to weight-appropriate manually prepared intramuscular dosing and consider higher-acuity monitoring.

Overall, this early management phase demonstrates the practical sequence of anaphylaxis care: immediate trigger removal, early intramuscular epinephrine, supportive oxygen and fluids, adjunctive antihistamines for skin symptoms, and escalation of disposition when severe or recurrent shock occurs. The central lesson is that initial stabilization may not be enough; recurrent anaphylaxis requires a low threshold for ICU-level observation and repeated treatment.

## Diagnostic Reasoning During Recurrence: Interpreting Relapse, Guideline Gaps, and Therapeutic Uncertainty

The key diagnostic challenge in this case was not the initial recognition of anaphylaxis, but the interpretation of the subsequent relapses. The patient experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes over the first 12 hours: the first in the emergency department shortly after clarithromycin ingestion, followed by three further recurrences after an initial clinical response. Each relapse occurred without any additional exposure to clarithromycin, which strongly argues against a new trigger and favors a multiphasic anaphylactic course. In true multiphasic anaphylaxis, symptoms recur after an apparent full or near-full resolution; this pattern differs from a single prolonged episode that only partially responds to treatment.

The evolution of the episodes also supported this interpretation. The first attack was the most severe, with hypotension, hypoxaemia, cyanosis, dyspnoea, flushing, urticaria, and facial swelling. Later episodes were clinically milder, with decreasing intensity of respiratory and cutaneous symptoms. A stepwise reduction in severity is often seen in recurrent anaphylaxis, although it does not eliminate the danger of further deterioration. For clinicians, the important point is that any relapse of airway, breathing, or circulatory symptoms after apparent recovery should be treated as recurrent anaphylaxis until proven otherwise.

Several alternative explanations had to be considered. Persistent or refractory anaphylaxis remains possible when symptoms do not fully resolve after epinephrine, or when improvement is only transient. Ongoing absorption of an allergen can also mimic biphasic disease, particularly with foods or slow-release formulations, but that explanation was less plausible here because the exposure was a single oral dose of clarithromycin with no further administration. Another trigger was also unlikely, as the relapses occurred in the controlled hospital setting without a new identifiable exposure. Thus, the recurrence after a clear initial response and in the absence of re-exposure was the most persuasive clinical pattern for multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Management remained anchored in standard anaphylaxis treatment: prompt intramuscular epinephrine for each recurrence. This is appropriate because epinephrine is the only treatment that addresses the immediate life-threatening features of anaphylaxis, including vasodilation, capillary leak, bronchospasm, and upper-airway edema. The repeated use of intramuscular dosing in this case reflects the principle that each clinically significant relapse should be treated promptly rather than observed in hopes of spontaneous resolution. In a rapidly evolving emergency, treatment delay is more dangerous than repeated administration when symptoms recur.

The case also illustrates why intravenous epinephrine infusion may be considered in some recurrent or severe cases. If repeated intramuscular doses are required, or if symptoms recur despite appropriate boluses, an infusion can provide more continuous adrenergic support and may prevent further collapses. However, intravenous epinephrine carries a higher risk of dosing error, arrhythmia, and hypertensive complications, so it is usually reserved for monitored settings and for clinicians experienced in its use. In this patient, the team explicitly considered an infusion after the first relapse, but because symptom intensity appeared to diminish and the relapse pattern was not clearly progressing, they weighed this against the risks of overtreatment.

This led to a genuine management dilemma: whether to adopt an expectant approach or to pre-empt further episodes with continuous epinephrine. Expectant management is reasonable when symptoms appear to be abating, but it requires confidence that the patient is truly past the dangerous phase. In multiphasic anaphylaxis, that confidence is often lacking because the timing and number of recurrences are unpredictable. The decision in this case was therefore made collaboratively with allergology, reflecting appropriate multidisciplinary reasoning in an area where evidence is sparse and clinical judgment must fill the gaps.

Corticosteroids were added after the first recurrence, reflecting older practice patterns and the long-standing belief that steroids might reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. This case is especially instructive because it shows how practice variation can influence treatment: different hospital protocols on the ward and in the ICU led to both prednisone and dexamethasone being given. Contemporary evidence, however, has increasingly questioned routine steroid use in anaphylaxis. Available studies do not show clear prevention of biphasic reactions, and some data suggest possible harms or confounding by indication, with steroid use associated with greater need for escalation of care. As a result, many modern guidelines no longer recommend steroids routinely, although they continue to be used in practice, particularly when clinicians are uneasy about recurrence.

Finally, the case highlights the uncertainty surrounding observation periods after anaphylaxis. Traditional monitoring windows of 4 to 6 hours may miss later relapses, while prolonged observation of all patients consumes substantial resources. Risk-stratified monitoring is therefore attractive, but reliable predictors of biphasic or multiphasic disease are limited. Severe initial presentation and the need for multiple epinephrine doses, both present in this case, are commonly cited as higher-risk features, yet they do not provide a precise prediction rule. This is why multiphasic anaphylaxis remains such a difficult problem: clinicians must balance the desire to avoid unnecessary ICU-level care against the danger of missing another life-threatening relapse. The case underscores that current guidelines offer broad principles, but not definitive answers, for these recurrent and unstable presentations.

## Searching for Predisposition: Tryptase Testing, Mast Cell Disorders, and Suspected Systemic Mastocytosis

When anaphylaxis is unusually severe, recurrent, prolonged, or out of proportion to the apparent trigger, clinicians should consider whether there is an underlying predisposition rather than a single isolated allergic event. In this case, the patient developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin, with repeated relapses despite appropriate epinephrine treatment. That pattern, together with persistent elevation of tryptase, prompted further evaluation for an underlying mast cell disorder, particularly systemic mastocytosis. This kind of investigation is important because mast cell disease can increase the risk of severe reactions and may change future counseling, avoidance strategies, and emergency preparedness.

Serum tryptase is a useful laboratory marker of mast cell activation. Tryptase is stored in mast cell granules and is released during degranulation, so a rise during an acute reaction supports the diagnosis of anaphylaxis, especially when the clinical picture is less straightforward. However, tryptase is not perfectly sensitive: a normal value does not exclude anaphylaxis, and the result is most useful when interpreted with proper timing and compared with a baseline level. The recommended approach is to obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, ideally within 1 to 2 hours of symptom onset and no later than 4 hours, and then a baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete resolution. In this case, sampling was suboptimal because the first tryptase was drawn only after the third episode and the baseline sample was delayed until after the last recurrence. Even so, both the event-related sample and the later baseline level were elevated, which strengthened concern for an underlying mast cell disorder rather than simple transient activation alone.

Persistently elevated baseline tryptase has a differential diagnosis. Systemic mastocytosis is an important cause, but other explanations include hereditary alpha-tryptasemia, chronic kidney disease, myeloid neoplasms, and, less commonly, other conditions associated with increased mast cell burden or impaired tryptase clearance. The key clinical clue in this patient was not just a single elevated value during anaphylaxis, but the fact that baseline levels remained high during follow-up. That pattern raised suspicion for a chronic mast cell disorder. In a patient with recurrent anaphylaxis, such a finding warrants hematologic and allergologic evaluation, because mast cell disorders are known to predispose to severe mediator-release events and to recurrent or atypically intense anaphylaxis.

Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal disorder characterized by abnormal accumulation of mast cells in one or more tissues, commonly bone marrow, skin, liver, spleen, or gastrointestinal tract. These mast cells can release histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, and other mediators, leading to flushing, hypotension, urticaria, gastrointestinal symptoms, syncope, and severe anaphylaxis. The disorder is relevant here because even a relatively small exposure to an allergen can trigger disproportionate systemic mediator release in patients with a high mast cell burden or heightened mast cell reactivity. That biological background helps explain why some patients have severe or recurrent anaphylaxis with only modest apparent triggers.

Diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis follows World Health Organization criteria. A major criterion is multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone marrow or another extracutaneous organ. Minor criteria include atypical or spindle-shaped mast cell morphology, KIT mutation, aberrant expression of CD25 and/or CD2 on mast cells, and persistently elevated baseline serum tryptase, typically above 20 ng/mL in the absence of another explanation. A formal diagnosis requires the major criterion plus one minor criterion, or at least three minor criteria if the major criterion is not met. In this case, the authors report only two WHO minor criteria, while the major criterion could not be assessed adequately because the bone marrow biopsy material was limited. As a result, the patient had a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, but not enough evidence for definitive classification. This distinction matters: “probable” or “highly suspicious” does not equal confirmed disease, and case reports should preserve that diagnostic caution.

The case also included supportive but nonspecific findings that fit with a mast cell disorder. Hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound can be seen in systemic involvement of mastocytosis, although it is not diagnostic on its own. The report also mentions possible neuropsychiatric symptoms, including depression and memory problems. These are not classic defining features, but they may accompany chronic disease burden and, in some patients, systemic mediator effects. Such findings are best interpreted as supportive context rather than standalone evidence.

Because clarithromycin exposure was already strongly linked to a severe anaphylactic reaction, confirmatory allergy testing was not pursued. In principle, drug testing can sometimes help clarify the culprit allergen, but in a patient who has already had life-threatening anaphylaxis and in whom the drug can be readily avoided, the risk of provoking another serious reaction may outweigh the diagnostic benefit. That reasoning is especially compelling when the clinical diagnosis is already sufficiently clear for practical management. The patient can be advised to avoid clarithromycin and related macrolides unless future specialist evaluation indicates otherwise.

Clinically, the main value of identifying suspected mast cell disease is risk stratification. A patient with recurrent anaphylaxis and persistently elevated tryptase should be regarded as having a higher-risk phenotype, even before formal confirmation of systemic mastocytosis. This affects counseling about trigger avoidance, the importance of carrying epinephrine, and the threshold for seeking emergency care after future exposures. It also supports referral to an allergologist or mast cell disorder specialist for longitudinal evaluation, because establishing or excluding systemic mastocytosis may meaningfully influence future management.

## Recovery, Long-Term Prevention, and Clinical Lessons for Practice

After the patient’s fourth anaphylactic episode, no further relapses occurred. Once she had remained symptom-free for about 24 hours, she was stepped down from ICU-level monitoring back to a standard ward and was discharged after a total hospital stay of 3 days. This course illustrates an important practical point: in multiphasic anaphylaxis, a patient may appear to have stabilized and then deteriorate again several hours later, so disposition decisions must be based on the full clinical trajectory rather than on the first response to epinephrine alone.

Secondary prevention focused on reducing the risk of future reactions and ensuring rapid self-treatment if an episode recurs. She was started on levocetirizine and was prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector for emergency use. Equally important, she was taught how and when to use the device, and she would need a clear action plan for future symptoms, including immediate epinephrine administration and urgent medical evaluation afterward. In any patient discharged after severe anaphylaxis, education is part of treatment: the goal is not only to provide medication, but also to ensure the patient recognizes early symptoms and responds without delay.

Because clarithromycin was the suspected trigger, she was counseled to avoid this drug in the future and the allergy history should be documented clearly in her records. The case also highlights why a previous “mild” beta-lactam reaction in adolescence did not reliably predict the severity of her later episode. Drug allergy histories can be informative, but they do not reliably forecast future reaction severity, and prior limited reactions do not exclude the possibility of life-threatening anaphylaxis on re-exposure to a different agent.

Follow-up with allergology was appropriate because the presentation raised concern for an underlying mast cell disorder, including possible systemic mastocytosis. Even though formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met, the persistently elevated tryptase and other findings warranted continued specialist assessment. In severe or atypical anaphylaxis, follow-up is not only about confirming the trigger; it may also uncover predisposing conditions that change future risk.

From a management standpoint, this case reinforces several key lessons. First, epinephrine should never be delayed in suspected anaphylaxis, and adults generally require adequate intramuscular dosing; underdosing may contribute to incomplete control and recurrent symptoms. Second, recurrent anaphylaxis requires prolonged observation and individualized disposition, often with escalation from ward to ICU if symptoms recur or become more severe. Third, corticosteroids should not be relied on to prevent biphasic reactions, because current evidence does not support that assumption. Their use should not replace epinephrine or observation, and routine administration remains controversial.

The case also underscores the tension between resource stewardship and patient safety. Most anaphylaxis cases resolve after standard treatment, but a small subset relapses unpredictably. Observation protocols therefore need to balance the burden of prolonged monitoring against the risk of missing a dangerous recurrence. For rare and complex presentations such as multiphasic anaphylaxis, the literature remains limited, and current practice still depends heavily on expert judgment.

For that reason, this type of case is valuable beyond the individual patient. Better consensus guidance is needed for ED and ICU management of recurrent anaphylaxis, including observation duration, escalation strategies, and the role of adjunctive therapies. Structured case registries would help capture clinical features, treatments, and outcomes in these uncommon presentations and could eventually support more evidence-based recommendations for future patients.
