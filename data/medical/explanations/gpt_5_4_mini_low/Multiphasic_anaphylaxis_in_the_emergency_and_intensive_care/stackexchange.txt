Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features in this patient establish the diagnosis of anaphylaxis, and which findings support classifying the initial presentation as anaphylactic shock rather than a limited allergic reaction?

This patient meets the clinical diagnosis of **anaphylaxis** because she had a **rapid-onset, systemic allergic reaction** after clarithromycin with **multiorgan involvement**: prominent **cutaneous and mucosal features** (generalized erythema, urticaria, facial swelling, pruritus, flushing), **respiratory compromise** (hypoxemia with oxygen saturation 88% on room air and dyspnea/“throat swelling” sensation), and **circulatory involvement** (**hypotension** to 74/50 mm Hg). The temporal relationship is also classic: symptoms began about **1 hour after the first dose** and progressed quickly.

The initial presentation should be classified as **anaphylactic shock**, not a limited allergic reaction, because she had **hemodynamic collapse**—marked hypotension requiring epinephrine and intravenous fluids—together with **hypoxemia** and systemic symptoms. A limited allergic reaction would be expected to remain confined to skin or mild local symptoms, such as isolated rash, itching, or facial swelling, without cardiovascular instability or respiratory compromise. In her prior amoxicillin reaction, the symptoms were limited to facial swelling without rash, dyspnea, or hypotension; by contrast, this episode clearly crossed the threshold into **life-threatening anaphylaxis with shock**.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How would you distinguish biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis from refractory anaphylaxis and from recurrent symptoms caused by ongoing allergen absorption, and which label best fits this case?

The key distinction is based on whether the patient has **true resolution between episodes** and whether there is **new allergen exposure or continued antigen absorption**.

**Biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis** refers to a **recurrence of anaphylaxis after an asymptomatic interval**, with **no further allergen exposure**. In biphasic disease there is one recurrence; in multiphasic disease there are **multiple relapses**, again after apparent recovery. The case report explicitly describes this pattern: the patient had a clear initial response to epinephrine, then developed **recurrent episodes within the first 12 hours**, each separated by improvement, and she had taken only **one oral dose of clarithromycin** with no re-exposure. That makes this a classic **multiphasic anaphylactic course**.

**Refractory anaphylaxis**, by contrast, is **persistent anaphylaxis that does not adequately respond to appropriately administered epinephrine**. The patient remains symptomatic without a meaningful symptom-free interval. That is not what happened here, because she repeatedly improved after each intramuscular epinephrine dose before relapsing.

**Recurrent symptoms from ongoing allergen absorption** should be considered when the trigger is still being absorbed, such as with **oral medications or food**, especially if symptoms seem more continuous or only transiently improved. In those cases, relapse can reflect **continued antigen exposure**, not a second immunologic wave. In this case, that possibility is less compelling because the report emphasizes an **asymptomatic phase after treatment** and describes the course as recurring episodes **without further exposure**. The clinical pattern and the authors’ discussion therefore favor **multiphasic anaphylaxis**, not refractory anaphylaxis.

So the best label for this case is **multiphasic anaphylaxis**—that is, repeated anaphylactic relapses after apparent recovery, without additional allergen exposure.

### [Management] In an adult weighing 104 kg, what is the guideline-recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose for anaphylaxis, how does that compare with a 0.3 mg auto-injector, and why might this difference matter clinically?

For an adult weighing 104 kg, the guideline dose of intramuscular epinephrine for anaphylaxis is **0.01 mg/kg**, which would calculate to **1.04 mg**; however, because guidelines cap the dose at **0.5 mg**, the **recommended adult IM dose is 0.5 mg**.

By comparison, a standard **0.3 mg auto-injector delivers 0.3 mg**, which is **0.2 mg less than the recommended adult maximum dose**—about **60% of the guideline dose**.

This matters clinically because **0.3 mg may underdose larger adults**, particularly those over 50 kg, and may lead to **inadequate initial treatment, slower symptom reversal, and a greater need for repeat epinephrine or escalation of care**. That issue is illustrated in the case report: the patient weighed **104 kg**, and her first two episodes were treated with **0.3 mg auto-injector epinephrine**, whereas subsequent relapses were treated with **0.5 mg IM epinephrine**. In severe anaphylaxis, especially with hypotension or hypoxemia, getting an adequate epinephrine dose up front is critical.

### [Morning Report] What are the key risk factors for biphasic anaphylaxis suggested in the literature, and which of them were present in this patient?

The literature suggests several **possible predictors of biphasic anaphylaxis**, although none are perfectly reliable. The most consistently cited are:

1. **Delayed administration of epinephrine**
2. **Severe initial anaphylaxis**, especially with **hypotension, hypoxemia, or airway/respiratory compromise**
3. **Need for multiple doses of epinephrine** to control the reaction
4. **Prior biphasic reaction** or a history suggesting recurrent severe reactions

In this patient, **at least three of these were present**:

- She had a **severe initial presentation** with **hypotension (74/50 mm Hg)** and **hypoxemia (SpO₂ 88%)**, plus generalized urticaria, flushing, and facial swelling.
- She required **more than one dose of epinephrine** over the course of her illness, ultimately having **four anaphylactic episodes**.
- Her first episode was treated promptly after ED presentation, but she still had a **progressive reaction before treatment** that began about an hour after the dose of clarithromycin and led her to seek care another hour later; so there was some **delay from symptom onset to epinephrine**, though she did present relatively quickly.

What was **not** present was a **prior biphasic reaction**, since this was her first episode of anaphylaxis.

So, in short, this case had the classic high-risk features of **severe initial anaphylaxis** and **need for repeated epinephrine**, with a **possible delay in epinephrine relative to symptom onset**—all of which fit the pattern associated with biphasic or multiphasic courses.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the recommended timing of serum tryptase measurements in suspected anaphylaxis, and how do elevated acute and persistently elevated baseline tryptase levels change your diagnostic reasoning in this case?

In suspected anaphylaxis, serum tryptase should be measured **serially**: obtain an **initial sample as soon as feasible** after presentation **without delaying treatment**, then a **second sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset** (and **within 4 hours**), when tryptase typically peaks, and a **third sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution** to establish the patient’s baseline level. That is the key point emphasized by this case report, in which the recommended sampling windows were not met initially.

How to interpret the results: an **elevated acute tryptase** supports that the event was a true **mast-cell–mediated anaphylactic reaction**, particularly when the clinical picture is less clear or when there are recurrent episodes. In this patient, the elevated tryptase during the third episode strengthened the diagnosis of anaphylaxis as the explanation for the recurrent shock-like episodes.

A **persistently elevated baseline tryptase** is a different and very important clue. It does **not** mean ongoing anaphylaxis; rather, it suggests an **underlying mast-cell disorder or increased mast-cell burden**, such as **systemic mastocytosis** or related clonal mast-cell disease. In this case, the persistently elevated baseline tryptase shifted the diagnostic reasoning away from “ordinary drug allergy alone” toward an **underlying mast cell activation predisposition**, which fit with the report’s conclusion of a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis despite not meeting full formal criteria. In other words: **acute elevation confirms the event; elevated baseline raises suspicion for an underlying mast-cell disorder and may explain unusually severe, recurrent, or multiphasic anaphylaxis.**

### [Pathophysiology] Given this patient’s severe, prolonged course and persistently elevated tryptase, what underlying mast cell disorder should be considered, and what clinical or laboratory findings in the case support that suspicion despite not meeting full formal criteria?

The underlying mast cell disorder to consider is **systemic mastocytosis**, or more broadly a **clonal mast cell disorder**. In this case, the suspicion is driven less by a single diagnostic test than by the overall pattern: **severe, prolonged, multiphasic anaphylaxis**, **persistently elevated baseline tryptase**, and additional findings that fit mast cell disease.

What supports the suspicion in this patient, even though she did not meet full WHO criteria, is:

- **Persistently elevated tryptase**, including an elevated level during the episode and an elevated **baseline** tryptase on follow-up. A chronically elevated baseline tryptase is a classic clue to an underlying mast cell burden or clonality, not just acute anaphylaxis.
- **Atypical severity and recurrence of anaphylaxis** after a single clarithromycin dose, with **multiple relapses despite appropriate epinephrine**, which is disproportionate and raises concern for mast cell instability.
- **Hepatomegaly** on abdominal sonography, a possible extracutaneous manifestation of systemic mast cell disease.
- Possible **neuropsychiatric symptoms**—depression and memory problems—which the authors note are in line with systemic mastocytosis.
- The allergology workup suggested a **high probability of systemic mastocytosis**, but the bone marrow material was limited, so the **major criterion could not be assessed**, and only **two minor WHO criteria** were documented—insufficient for formal diagnosis.

So the teaching point is: in a patient with **recurrent or unusually severe anaphylaxis plus persistently elevated baseline tryptase**, think of **systemic mastocytosis or another clonal mast cell disorder**, even if formal diagnostic criteria are not fully met.

### [Management] What is the current evidence regarding corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis and prevention of biphasic reactions, and how should that evidence influence management decisions in a patient like this?

The best current evidence does **not support routine corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis to prevent biphasic reactions**.

Historically, steroids were given because biphasic anaphylaxis can recur hours after apparent resolution, and corticosteroids were presumed to blunt the late inflammatory phase. But that practice is now increasingly questioned. As highlighted in this case report, a systematic review of 22 studies found that **only one** suggested benefit, whereas **21 found no evidence that corticosteroids reduce biphasic anaphylaxis**. More recent observational data have also raised concern that corticosteroid use may be associated with greater subsequent resource use, including ICU or ward admission, likely reflecting confounding by severity but offering no signal of clear benefit. Accordingly, most contemporary guidelines now **advise against routine corticosteroid administration** in anaphylaxis.

How should that affect management in a patient like this? The key point is that **epinephrine remains the definitive treatment**, and the management priority in recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis is **repeat intramuscular epinephrine, airway/breathing/circulation support, and adequate observation**, not steroids. In this patient, the recurrent shock episodes were appropriately treated with additional epinephrine and supportive care. The steroids she received after the first relapse reflect older practice patterns, but current evidence would not support relying on them to prevent further episodes.

So in practice:

- **Give epinephrine early and repeat it as needed**
- **Observe closely for recurrence**, especially after severe initial reactions or need for multiple epinephrine doses
- **Do not use corticosteroids routinely to prevent biphasic reactions**
- Reserve steroids, if used at all, for selected situations such as concomitant asthma exacerbation or prominent protracted symptoms, recognizing that they should **never delay epinephrine**

In short, this case underscores an important teaching point: **steroids are not proven prophylaxis against biphasic anaphylaxis; epinephrine and monitoring are the evidence-based interventions that matter.**

### [Morning Report] In a patient with recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock after an initial response to treatment, what factors would influence your decision to escalate observation from a ward to the ICU, and when would you consider continuous intravenous epinephrine rather than repeated intramuscular dosing?

In a patient whose anaphylaxis initially responds but then recurs, I would escalate from ward observation to the ICU when the course is no longer “single-episode, resolved anaphylaxis” and instead shows features of **ongoing hemodynamic or respiratory instability**. In the case report, the decisive factors were the **early relapse within 12 hours**, **recurrent anaphylactic shock**, and the need for **repeated epinephrine, supplemental oxygen, and intravenous fluids**. Those are exactly the features that make ward-level observation inadequate, because the patient may deteriorate again quickly and needs continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring and immediate access to vasopressors and airway support.

Practical factors favoring ICU-level care include:
- **Recurrent hypotension or hypoxemia**
- **Need for more than one epinephrine dose**
- **Severe initial presentation**, especially shock or respiratory compromise
- **Persistent or recurrent symptoms despite initial apparent resolution**
- Concern for a **mast-cell disorder** or other predisposition to severe or relapsing reactions, as suggested in this case by the later workup

As for **continuous intravenous epinephrine**, I would reserve that for **refractory or repeatedly relapsing anaphylaxis** when the patient requires ongoing epinephrine support rather than isolated rescue doses. In other words, if the patient has **persistent symptoms despite appropriately dosed intramuscular epinephrine**, or if symptoms **keep recurring and you are effectively treating one shock episode after another**, an epinephrine infusion becomes reasonable—provided it can be given in a setting with **close monitoring, usually the ICU**. This is different from the standard approach to most biphasic reactions, where **repeat intramuscular epinephrine** remains first-line.

The key teaching point from this case is that **multiphasic anaphylaxis with recurrent shock is not a routine observation problem**. Once the patient has demonstrated **relapsing cardiovascular or respiratory compromise and repeated epinephrine requirement**, ICU transfer is appropriate, and an **IV epinephrine infusion** should be considered if recurrent bolus dosing is becoming necessary or if the reaction is not fully controlled between episodes.
