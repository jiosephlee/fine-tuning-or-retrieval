Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis and Laboratory Confirmation of Autoimmune HIT

Severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery requires a structured differential diagnosis, because platelet counts commonly fall after cardiopulmonary bypass for reasons that are not immune-mediated and do not require HIT-specific treatment. In this case, the platelet count declined progressively after surgery, but the patient remained hemodynamically stable, had no evidence of major bleeding, and did not show laboratory signs of sepsis or disseminated intravascular coagulation. The hemoglobin level was relatively stable after the expected immediate postoperative drop, which argued against ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was normal, blood and urine cultures were negative, and chest radiography did not suggest a new infectious source. Coagulation tests were normal rather than consumptive, and the elevated fibrinogen level fit the early postoperative acute-phase response rather than a bleeding diathesis or overt DIC.

The peripheral blood film also helped narrow the differential. The absence of platelet clumping excluded pseudothrombocytopenia, and the initial lack of schistocytes made a primary thrombotic microangiopathy less likely. Normal renal and liver function further argued against severe systemic microvascular injury, and there was no biochemical evidence of hemolysis: haptoglobin was not low, bilirubin was normal, and the reticulocyte count was not suggestive of significant red cell destruction. These findings made thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and other thrombotic microangiopathies less likely at presentation, although schistocytes later appeared as the HIT-associated coagulopathy evolved. Other uncommon postoperative causes were also considered. Post-transfusion purpura was unlikely because no transfusions were given, and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was improbable because the platelet fall began too early for most newly administered perioperative medications to be responsible.

In the cardiac surgery setting, the most important diagnosis to consider is HIT. The timing is particularly informative: classic postoperative HIT usually begins around postoperative days 5 to 10, after a sensitizing heparin exposure has had time to trigger pathogenic antibodies. Here, the platelet decline began very early after surgery, which initially seemed too soon for the heparin given during cardiopulmonary bypass. However, careful history revealed a smaller but critical preoperative heparin exposure during cardiac catheterization one week earlier. That prior exposure made early-onset HIT biologically plausible, because circulating antibodies can react again when heparin is reintroduced. This temporal clue was essential in moving HIT to the top of the differential.

Laboratory testing then strengthened the diagnosis. A PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive, with a high optical density. In postoperative cardiac patients, this assay is sensitive but not fully specific, because many patients develop anti-PF4/heparin antibodies that are not clinically pathogenic. For that reason, the magnitude of ELISA reactivity matters: a stronger optical density generally increases the likelihood that the antibodies are clinically relevant, especially when paired with thrombocytopenia and thrombosis. In this case, the strongly positive ELISA supported HIT, but confirmatory functional testing was still needed.

The serotonin-release assay (SRA) is the key functional test because it demonstrates whether the patient’s antibodies activate donor platelets. In classic heparin-dependent HIT, patient serum causes platelet activation at low heparin concentrations, where PF4-heparin immune complexes form, but not at very high heparin concentrations, where complex formation is disrupted. That standard pattern was partly present here: the patient’s serum caused strong serotonin release at low UFH concentrations and no release at very high heparin concentration, which is characteristic of pathogenic HIT antibodies. However, the assay also showed something more unusual and more important: strong platelet activation even in the absence of heparin. Heparin-independent platelet activation is a hallmark of autoimmune HIT. It indicates that the antibodies can activate platelets without needing heparin to assemble PF4-containing complexes, which explains the often severe thrombocytopenia and ongoing hypercoagulability seen in this syndrome.

The Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 was expected to inhibit platelet activation if the response were mediated in the usual Fc-dependent manner. In this case, inhibition was incomplete, which initially led the reference laboratory to call the test indeterminate. But that result did not exclude HIT. Rather, it suggested an unusually strong platelet-activating antibody response, with only partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. When interpreted together with the clinical picture, the strong ELISA, the heparin-independent SRA activity, the thrombotic stroke, and the timing related to prior preoperative UFH exposure, the overall pattern was diagnostic of autoimmune HIT.

This case illustrates an important diagnostic principle: functional testing should be interpreted in context, not in isolation. An assay that appears atypical or even initially indeterminate can still support a definitive diagnosis when the clinical syndrome is highly characteristic. Here, early postoperative thrombocytopenia, thrombosis, strong PF4/heparin antibody positivity, and heparin-independent platelet activation all converged on severe autoimmune HIT. The diagnosis explained both the profound platelet fall and the unexpected thrombotic complication, and it guided the need for immediate cessation of heparin exposure and use of non-heparin anticoagulation.
