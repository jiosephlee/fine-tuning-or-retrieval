Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 1: ## Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia After Cardiac Surgery: Core Concepts and Case Framing

Cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is one of the classic settings in which heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) must be considered. Unfractionated heparin (UFH) remains the standard intraoperative anticoagulant because it acts rapidly, can be monitored with activated clotting time, is inexpensive, and can be reversed if needed. In the bypass circuit, heparin also helps suppress clotting on artificial surfaces. The tradeoff is that UFH can trigger an immune-mediated prothrombotic disorder, and postoperative cardiac patients are among the most diagnostically challenging populations because thrombocytopenia is expected after surgery even without HIT.

Understanding the normal postoperative platelet pattern is essential. After cardiac surgery, the platelet count usually falls early from hemodilution, consumption during bypass, and perioperative blood loss, then begins to recover over the next several days. This expected nadir is usually modest and occurs in the first 1 to 3 postoperative days. In contrast, HIT is suspected when thrombocytopenia is more pronounced than expected, when the platelet count falls again after an initial recovery, or when thrombocytopenia is accompanied by new thrombosis. In this case, the platelet count declined progressively rather than rebounding, which is not the usual benign postoperative pattern.

HIT is not rare after cardiac surgery. Depending on the heparin regimen used postoperatively, its frequency is roughly 0.5% to 1%, but its importance is greater than the incidence suggests because HIT strongly predisposes to thrombosis. The syndrome is therefore clinically paradoxical: platelet counts fall, yet the main danger is clot formation rather than bleeding. This happens because the immune response activates platelets and monocytes, amplifying thrombin generation and creating a profoundly hypercoagulable state.

Classic HIT is driven by IgG antibodies against complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to heparin. PF4 is a positively charged platelet protein that binds negatively charged heparin; the PF4-heparin complex becomes antigenic in some patients. The resulting IgG antibodies cross-link FcγRIIa receptors on platelets, causing platelet activation and release of procoagulant microparticles. This Fc receptor-mediated activation explains why thrombocytopenia in HIT signals platelet consumption in the context of thrombosis, not a simple failure of platelet production.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) is a more severe biologic variant within the HIT spectrum. In aHIT, antibodies can activate platelets even with little or no heparin present, and they often produce a more aggressive clinical course. This heparin-independent platelet activation distinguishes aHIT from typical HIT and helps explain why some patients have profound thrombocytopenia, persistent platelet activation after heparin is stopped, and signs of consumptive coagulopathy such as falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, or schistocytosis. These patients may also have unexpectedly severe or prolonged thrombosis despite appropriate initial treatment.

This case is notable because it combines several features that should immediately raise concern for aHIT rather than routine postoperative thrombocytopenia. The platelet fall began unusually early, likely because the patient had already been exposed to UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterization, allowing an immune response to be triggered before surgery. He then developed an ischemic stroke, showing that the thrombocytopenia was occurring in a prothrombotic setting. The platelet nadir was extremely low, and the laboratory profile showed strong HIT antibody positivity with atypical platelet activation behavior, including activation even without heparin. Together, these findings frame the case as severe autoimmune HIT, a diagnosis that is easy to miss unless the clinician recognizes that postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery may reflect a dangerous immune thrombotic syndrome rather than an expected surgical effect.
