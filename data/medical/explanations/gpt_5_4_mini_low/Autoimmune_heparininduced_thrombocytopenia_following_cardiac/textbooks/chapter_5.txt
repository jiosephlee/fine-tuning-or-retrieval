Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 5: ## Recovery, Serologic Evolution, and Clinical Lessons

This patient’s recovery illustrates an important feature of autoimmune HIT (aHIT): the clinical course can improve only gradually, even after the initial heparin exposure has stopped and alternative anticoagulation has been started. His platelet count recovered slowly over several weeks, reaching 155 × 10^9/L by postoperative day 62 and normalizing thereafter, while his neurologic deficits resolved completely. The stroke was therefore not a permanent disabling event, but it was a major clue that the thrombocytopenia reflected a prothrombotic immune process rather than routine postoperative platelet consumption. During the recovery phase, fondaparinux was used as a bridge, and his chronic oral anticoagulation with apixaban was later resumed once the acute HIT/aHIT episode had settled and the platelet count had normalized. In practice, the choice to return to a patient’s long-term oral anticoagulant depends on stabilization of the acute prothrombotic state and the need for ongoing indication-specific anticoagulation, such as atrial fibrillation in this case.

A particularly instructive aspect of this case was the persistence of HIT antibodies for months after the clinical event. That is not unusual in HIT, but it matters because a positive antibody test does not by itself define disease activity or dictate ongoing treatment. More important is the relationship between the serology, the platelet count trajectory, and the thrombotic phenotype. Here, repeat testing showed that the functional behavior of the antibody changed over time. Early samples produced the unusual pattern of strong platelet activation even without heparin, which is characteristic of aHIT, and the activation showed only partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade. Later samples became more clearly heparin-dependent, with less activation in the absence of heparin and strong activation when heparin was added. This evolution supports the concept that aHIT can be a dynamic syndrome rather than a fixed laboratory phenotype. As the antibody burden wanes, the serologic picture may shift toward that of more typical HIT.

This temporal evolution helped confirm the diagnosis. In a postoperative patient, especially after cardiac surgery, the temptation is to attribute thrombocytopenia to cardiopulmonary bypass, dilution, transfusion effects, infection, or general postoperative illness. Those explanations were not convincing here because the thrombocytopenia was progressive, profound, and accompanied by thrombosis and biochemical evidence of consumptive coagulopathy. The absence of a better alternative diagnosis mattered. When a patient has a strong-positive PF4 immunoassay, a compatible clinical course, and no other plausible cause for severe thrombocytopenia, the diagnosis of HIT remains likely even if the functional assay is atypical or initially “indeterminate.” In this case, expert review of the serotonin-release assay was essential. The final interpretation depended on understanding that unusual assay behavior may reflect particularly potent antibodies, not absence of disease.

Several clinical hallmarks of severe aHIT were present. The thrombocytopenia began unusually early, before the classic postoperative HIT window, because the sensitizing heparin exposure had occurred during preoperative cardiac catheterization. The platelet nadir was extremely low, falling to 13 × 10^9/L, which is more severe than in typical HIT. The patient developed arterial thrombosis with an ischemic stroke, and laboratory signs of ongoing thrombin generation appeared during treatment, including falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytes. These findings are consistent with the broader syndrome of HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy, a pattern that should raise concern for aHIT rather than routine HIT. The partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade in vitro adds another clue that this was not an ordinary heparin-dependent antibody response.

For learners, the main diagnostic pitfall is narrow anchoring on the most obvious postoperative explanation. In cardiac surgery patients, platelet counts often fall after cardiopulmonary bypass, and delirium is common after major surgery. But HIT should not be dismissed when the platelet fall is progressive, severe, or accompanied by thrombosis. Recent heparin exposure may have occurred outside the operating room, including during preoperative catheterization or other procedures, and that exposure can be the trigger for an earlier-than-expected platelet decline after surgery. Likewise, an atypical functional assay should not lead to premature exclusion of HIT. Instead, it should prompt expert laboratory review, because aHIT can produce unusual heparin-independent activation patterns and, as shown here, even partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

The case also has broader implications for diagnosis and research. It expands the recognized laboratory spectrum of aHIT and shows that the serologic phenotype may evolve over time from strongly heparin-independent activation to a more conventional heparin-dependent pattern as antibodies wane. That evolution reinforces the need for repeat testing when the initial assay result does not fit the clinical picture. More generally, the case teaches a practical approach: in a postcardiac surgery patient with falling platelets, first ask whether the timing and severity are compatible with HIT, then look for thrombosis or consumptive coagulopathy, then obtain HIT immunoassay testing, and if the functional assay is unusual or discordant, seek specialized interpretation rather than assuming the result is negative. In severe suspected aHIT, anticoagulation should be switched promptly to a non-heparin agent, and adjunctive therapy such as intravenous immunoglobulin may be considered when platelet activation is marked or persistent.

Overall, this patient’s favorable outcome depended on early recognition, continued diagnostic reassessment, and avoidance of diagnostic closure. The key teaching point is that postoperative thrombocytopenia is not always routine, and delirium is not always benign. When early thrombocytopenia, stroke, and laboratory evidence of HIT converge, aHIT should be strongly considered—even if the first functional assay is atypical.
