Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Presentation and Early Diagnostic Suspicion

The patient was an elderly man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation, chronic anticoagulation with apixaban, and structural heart disease that had not been fully appreciated until preoperative evaluation revealed a systolic murmur and subsequent catheterization showed moderate aortic stenosis, ascending aortic dilatation, and coronary disease. These baseline factors matter because cardiac surgery patients often have multiple reasons for postoperative platelet abnormalities and neurologic change, including advanced age, cardiopulmonary bypass, perioperative anticoagulant exposure, and postoperative delirium. In this setting, a new platelet fall must be interpreted against the expected postoperative pattern rather than in isolation.

A key early clue was the preoperative heart catheterization performed one week before surgery. Although the heparin exposure during that procedure was small—only 2000 units of unfractionated heparin—it was still clinically important. HIT can be triggered by even limited heparin exposure in a previously sensitized or newly primed patient, and in this case that earlier exposure helped explain why thrombocytopenia began unusually early after surgery. This is an important lesson in chronology: when platelet counts fall too soon after cardiac surgery to fit “classic” HIT timing from the bypass heparin alone, clinicians should look back for any preceding heparin exposure in the prior days to weeks.

The operation itself was extensive and involved several procedures known to require cardiopulmonary bypass and full intraoperative anticoagulation: aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic grafting, coronary artery bypass grafting, and left atrial appendage excision. The patient received a large dose of unfractionated heparin during bypass, which is standard practice in cardiac surgery because UFH remains the preferred anticoagulant for cardiopulmonary bypass. Postoperatively, he was started on routine low-molecular-weight heparin prophylaxis with dalteparin, which is a common management step after cardiac surgery but also provides additional heparin exposure in a patient at risk for HIT.

The platelet trajectory was the central diagnostic signal. His preoperative platelet count was normal at 210 × 10^9/L. On postoperative day 1 it fell to 155 × 10^9/L, which by itself is not unusual after cardiopulmonary bypass. After that, however, the decline did not stabilize or recover as expected; instead, the platelet count continued to fall day by day, reaching 50 × 10^9/L by postoperative day 5 and then dropping further to the mid-20s by postoperative day 6. The pattern was progressive and persistent rather than transient. After cardiac surgery, a modest early platelet drop is expected from hemodilution, platelet activation, and consumption during bypass, typically reaching a nadir around postoperative day 2 to 3 and then beginning to recover. In contrast, HIT is suggested when the platelet count falls again after an initial postoperative decrease, or when the fall is more severe, continues beyond the expected window, or is accompanied by thrombosis.

The neurologic picture added an important thrombotic clue. The patient had postoperative delirium, which is common after major cardiac surgery and can easily distract attention from a focal vascular event. Because the confusion worsened in parallel with further thrombocytopenia, brain imaging was obtained and showed an acute left parietal infarct. In the setting of suspected HIT, an arterial stroke is highly significant. Although HIT is classically associated with venous thrombosis, arterial thrombosis—including stroke, limb ischemia, and myocardial infarction—can occur and often signals a more severe prothrombotic state. Thus, the stroke transformed the platelet fall from a nonspecific postoperative abnormality into a syndrome strongly suggestive of HIT.

At the bedside, the pretest probability of HIT was raised by the combination of timing, severity, thrombosis, and lack of convincing alternative explanations. The platelet fall began very early, which initially seemed too soon for HIT from the bypass heparin alone, but the prior catheterization with UFH provided a plausible sensitizing exposure. The thrombocytopenia became substantial, the patient developed arterial thrombosis, and there was no strong evidence for sepsis, major bleeding, or other causes of consumptive thrombocytopenia. This kind of timeline-based reasoning is central to recognizing HIT early: after cardiac surgery, not every platelet fall is HIT, but progressive thrombocytopenia beyond the expected postoperative pattern—especially when paired with thrombosis—should prompt immediate testing and empiric management while confirmatory studies are pending.
