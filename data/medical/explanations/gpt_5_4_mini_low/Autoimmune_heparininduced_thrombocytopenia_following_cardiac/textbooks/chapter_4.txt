Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 4: ## Management Strategy in Severe Autoimmune HIT with Stroke

Once heparin-induced thrombocytopenia was suspected, the first and most important management step was to stop all heparin exposure. In practice, this means discontinuing not only unfractionated heparin but also all heparin-containing products, including low-molecular-weight heparin. This is essential because HIT is not a simple bleeding disorder; it is a highly prothrombotic immune-mediated syndrome, so continued heparin exposure can perpetuate platelet activation and thrombosis. In this case, prophylactic dalteparin had been started after surgery, but once the platelet fall, strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, and subsequent cerebral infarct made HIT the leading diagnosis, dalteparin was stopped and a non-heparin anticoagulant was started.

The initial anticoagulant chosen was argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor. This is a standard treatment option in acute HIT because it does not depend on heparin binding and provides immediate anticoagulation. Argatroban is particularly useful in unstable hospitalized patients because it is given intravenously and can be adjusted quickly. Its effect is monitored with the activated partial thromboplastin time, which in this case was targeted to a therapeutic range. That said, the postoperative setting makes argatroban management challenging: dose titration can be difficult, laboratory values may be influenced by acute illness, and any anticoagulant must be balanced against bleeding risk, especially after major cardiac surgery and a recent ischemic stroke. Here, argatroban was selected partly because it has a short half-life, allowing rapid discontinuation if hemorrhage occurred.

Fondaparinux had already been started at prophylactic dose before the diagnosis was fully established, but that dose was inadequate once the patient was recognized to have active HIT with thrombosis. Prophylactic fondaparinux may prevent routine postoperative venous thromboembolism, but it is not sufficient treatment for established HIT-associated thrombosis or the marked ongoing hypercoagulability seen in autoimmune HIT. The patient’s later course—progressive thrombocytopenia, cerebral infarction, falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytosis—showed that the disease was not merely being observed but was still actively consuming platelets and driving coagulation. This is why therapy had to be escalated from prophylaxis to full therapeutic anticoagulation.

High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, or IVIG, was added because the serologic and clinical picture fit autoimmune HIT. In classic HIT, platelet activation depends on heparin-dependent immune complexes that signal through platelet Fc receptors. In autoimmune HIT, antibodies can activate platelets even in little or no heparin, and this activation is still Fc receptor mediated. IVIG is used to block Fc receptors on platelets and immune effector cells, thereby interrupting antibody-driven platelet activation. In other words, IVIG does not remove the antibodies, but it can blunt their ability to trigger further platelet activation. A brisk platelet recovery is often expected when IVIG is effective in aHIT, so the modest rise in this patient’s platelet count despite several days of treatment was a warning sign that the antibody-mediated process was unusually severe and persistent.

Because the patient remained clinically and biochemically hypercoagulable, the team followed markers that could suggest whether the prothrombotic state was improving. Fibrinogen and D-dimer are not diagnostic tests for HIT, but in severe HIT they can reflect ongoing consumptive coagulopathy. Falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and later appearance of schistocytes on the peripheral smear supported continued thrombin generation and microvascular injury despite treatment. In this setting, the development of schistocytes did not indicate thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura; rather, it was part of severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy.

Therapy was then changed from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. This switch was driven by two practical considerations. First, fondaparinux is easier to administer because it is given once daily subcutaneously rather than by continuous intravenous infusion with aPTT titration. Second, there was concern that argatroban was not adequately suppressing the HIT-driven hypercoagulability, based on the laboratory trajectory and evolving blood film findings. Fondaparinux is an indirect factor Xa inhibitor that works through antithrombin, and although its use in HIT is off-label, it is commonly used as a non-heparin anticoagulant in selected patients when clinically appropriate.

Because fondaparinux depends on antithrombin for its anticoagulant effect, the patient also received antithrombin concentrate when a mildly reduced antithrombin level was found. This is a key point in interpreting the switch: in severe postoperative illness and active thrombosis, antithrombin can be consumed, potentially weakening the effect of fondaparinux. Supplementing antithrombin was therefore intended to ensure that fondaparinux would have its expected pharmacologic activity. The decision also reflected the need to maintain anticoagulation while minimizing hemorrhagic risk in a patient who had both a recent ischemic stroke and profound thrombocytopenia.

Overall, this case illustrates the central therapeutic dilemma in severe autoimmune HIT: clinicians must treat a dangerous clotting disorder aggressively even when the platelet count is very low and bleeding is a concern. Successful management required close coordination among hematology, cardiac surgery, neurology, and laboratory medicine, with treatment guided by both the evolving clinical picture and specialized HIT testing. The eventual platelet recovery and complete neurologic improvement show that even severe aHIT with stroke can resolve when the diagnosis is recognized and anticoagulation is promptly redirected away from heparin.
