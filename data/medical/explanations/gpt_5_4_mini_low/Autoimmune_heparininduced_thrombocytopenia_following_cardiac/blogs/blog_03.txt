Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# The Indeterminate SRA That Wasn’t: How to Read Unusual HIT Functional Assays

## Why this case is worth your attention

Post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia is common. HIT is less common, but it’s the diagnosis you don’t want to miss because the price of missing it is often thrombosis, stroke, or worse.

This case is interesting because the patient’s functional assay looked “indeterminate” at first glance — yet the clinical picture was strongly consistent with HIT, and the eventual answer was **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**. The twist: the serotonin-release assay (SRA) showed **strong platelet activation even at 0 heparin**, plus activation at low-dose heparin, and only **partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade**. That pattern is a teaching pearl all by itself.

If you see an unusual HIT functional assay and reflexively downgrade the diagnosis, you can get burned. Sometimes the assay is not “negative.” Sometimes it’s telling you you’re dealing with a more aggressive HIT phenotype.

## The clinical setup: why HIT was on the table

The patient was a man in his 70s who underwent major cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass and received large-dose intraoperative UFH. Postoperatively, he was also exposed to dalteparin thromboprophylaxis. His platelets fell early and progressively, reaching 50 × 10^9/L by POD5 and later a nadir of 13 × 10^9/L. He also developed a parietal ischemic stroke.

That combination should make you think hard about HIT, especially because:

- the platelet fall was not explained by bleeding, shock, or infection
- the timing fit with **preoperative heparin exposure** during catheterization
- he had **thrombosis** in the setting of thrombocytopenia
- the PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive

The diagnostic challenge wasn’t whether HIT was plausible. It was how to interpret the functional assay.

## What a classic HIT functional assay should look like

In classic HIT, the SRA usually shows:

- **low or absent serotonin release at 0 heparin**
- **strong serotonin release at low heparin concentrations**
- **loss of activation at high heparin concentrations**, because excess heparin disrupts PF4/heparin immune complex formation

That’s the usual “bell-shaped” HIT pattern.

In other words, low-dose heparin helps form the antigenic complex; very high-dose heparin tends to shut the process down.

## What made this SRA unusual

This patient’s SRA had three important features:

1. **Strong serotonin release at 0 heparin**
2. **Strong release at low-dose heparin**
3. **Only partial inhibition by the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3**

That’s not the pattern of straightforward classic HIT.

Strong activation at **0 heparin** points toward **aHIT**, in which HIT antibodies can activate platelets even without added heparin. That’s one reason these cases can present with:

- more severe thrombocytopenia
- ongoing thrombosis
- consumptive coagulopathy
- prolonged platelet recovery

The partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade was even more unusual. It suggests the antibody-mediated activation was intense enough that the usual inhibitory effect of IV.3 was incomplete. The key practical lesson is that **an atypical SRA doesn’t automatically rule out HIT**. It may actually be a clue to aHIT.

## So what is autoimmune HIT?

Autoimmune HIT is a more severe HIT subtype in which antibodies can activate platelets with little or no added heparin. Clinically, it often behaves like a hypercoagulable syndrome rather than a simple thrombocytopenic state.

Think of clues like:

- profound thrombocytopenia
- thrombosis despite stopping heparin
- persistently positive functional assays
- platelet activation at **0 heparin**
- consumptive features such as falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytes

This patient had several of those features, including falling fibrinogen and later schistocytes while on argatroban, consistent with severe HIT-associated hypercoagulability.

## Why the “indeterminate” label can mislead

Functional assays are powerful, but they are not magic. A result can be difficult to interpret for several reasons:

- the antibody phenotype may be atypical
- the assay conditions may not capture the full biology
- the lab may see a pattern that doesn’t fit the standard template
- the sample may be from a phase of disease in which antibody behavior is evolving

The danger is not the lab result itself — it’s over-trusting a binary interpretation.

In this case, the initial “indeterminate” SRA could have falsely weakened diagnostic confidence. But the clinical course, immunoassay positivity, and later functional assay behavior supported HIT, specifically aHIT.

## Clinician-laboratory communication matters

This case is a great reminder that HIT diagnosis is not something to outsource blindly to the test report.

When the story and the assay disagree, pick up the phone.

Useful questions for the lab include:

- Was there platelet activation at 0 heparin?
- Was activation suppressed at high heparin concentrations?
- Was the Fc receptor blockade complete or partial?
- Are there serial samples that show evolution of the functional profile?
- Does the pattern fit classic HIT or something like aHIT?

The McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory’s deeper review of this case was essential. Without that back-and-forth, this patient might have been mislabeled as having an equivocal functional assay rather than severe aHIT.

## Practical bedside takeaways

When the platelet count drops after cardiac surgery, don’t anchor on “post-op thrombocytopenia” and move on. Look at the pattern, the timing, and the thrombotic phenotype. In HIT, the lab report should support your clinical reasoning — not replace it.

### Key take-home points

- **A strong positive PF4 ELISA plus thrombosis and thrombocytopenia is still HIT until proven otherwise.**
- **SRA activation at 0 heparin is a red flag for autoimmune HIT, not a reason to dismiss the diagnosis.**
- **An “indeterminate” functional assay can still represent clinically important HIT.**
- **Partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade may reflect unusually aggressive platelet-activating antibodies.**
- **When the case doesn’t fit the template, talk to the reference lab early.**

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that HIT functional assays are patterns, not just numbers. The SRA wasn’t “indeterminate” because the diagnosis was weak — it was unusual because the antibody biology was unusual. In a patient with the right clinical story, **platelet activation at 0 heparin should increase your suspicion for aHIT**, not lower it.

If the clinical picture is convincing, don’t let an atypical assay talk you out of the diagnosis.
