Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Beyond the Platelet Count: Using Fibrinogen, D-Dimer, and Smear Findings to Track HIT Activity

## The part of HIT we don’t look at enough

HIT is usually taught as a platelet problem. The platelet count falls, the PF4 ELISA lights up, the SRA confirms it, and we move on to non-heparin anticoagulation.

But severe HIT — and especially autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — is not just thrombocytopenia. It is a hypercoagulable, consumptive, platelet-activating syndrome. In other words: the platelet count is only the headline. The rest of the story is in the coagulation markers, the smear, and the overall trajectory.

This case is a great reminder.

An elderly man underwent cardiac surgery with substantial intraoperative UFH exposure, then received postoperative dalteparin. By POD5 his platelets had already dropped to 50 × 10^9/L, and by POD6 they were 26 × 10^9/L. He then declared himself further with an acute parietal stroke. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive, and the SRA eventually showed a pattern consistent with aHIT — including heparin-independent platelet activation.

What makes this case especially educational is that the team didn’t rely on the platelet count alone. They followed fibrinogen, D-dimer, and the peripheral smear to gauge whether the disease process was still smoldering.

## Why the platelet count can be misleading

In classic HIT, the platelet count often starts to improve after heparin is stopped and a non-heparin anticoagulant is started. That improvement can be reassuring — but it is not always enough.

In aHIT, the syndrome can be more aggressive:
- profound thrombocytopenia
- ongoing thrombin generation
- consumptive coagulopathy
- thrombosis despite treatment

That means a rising platelet count doesn’t always equal disease control, and a persistently low platelet count doesn’t always tell you whether the process is still active versus simply slow to recover.

This is where “global” markers become useful.

## Fibrinogen and D-dimer: the hidden trend line

In this case, serial labs told a more ominous story than the platelet count alone.

While on argatroban, the patient developed:
- progressively falling fibrinogen, reaching a nadir of 2.49 g/L
- rising D-dimer
- new schistocytes on smear from POD9 to POD16

That pattern is hard to ignore. It suggests ongoing thrombin generation and consumption, not just isolated thrombocytopenia.

### Why this matters
In severe HIT/aHIT, falling fibrinogen can be a clue that the patient is in a consumptive state. Rising D-dimer adds support for ongoing clot breakdown and systemic coagulation activation. Together, they suggest the process is still biologically active — even if you’ve already “done the right thing” by stopping heparin and starting a direct thrombin inhibitor.

This is especially valuable when:
- the clinical picture is noisy after cardiac surgery
- the platelet count is slow to recover
- the diagnosis is uncertain
- you’re wondering whether current anticoagulation is enough

## The smear still earns its keep

The peripheral smear is often underused in postoperative thrombocytopenia. In this case, the later appearance of schistocytes mattered.

Not because schistocytes are specific for HIT — they aren’t — but because they support a consumptive microangiopathic picture when they appear in the right context. Here, the smear evolved from no schistocytes early on to detectable fragments later, paralleling the biochemical evidence of ongoing coagulopathy.

That combination — falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, schistocytes — is a red flag for ongoing HIT-associated hypercoagulability.

## When to think aHIT

Autoimmune HIT is the version of HIT that should make you uncomfortable.

Think about it when HIT behaves badly:
- very severe thrombocytopenia
- thrombosis, sometimes arterial
- platelet activation even without heparin
- persistent or progressive thrombocytopenia after heparin cessation
- consumptive coagulopathy
- incomplete response to standard management

This case had several of those features. The patient’s nadir platelet count reached 13 × 10^9/L, he had a stroke, and the serology was unusual — including strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and only partial inhibition with Fc receptor blockade.

The takeaway is not that you need an SRA subtype to diagnose aHIT at the bedside. It’s that the overall pattern matters. If the labs look like ongoing consumption and thrombosis, the disease may be more than “ordinary HIT.”

## How this can change bedside decisions

Serial fibrinogen, D-dimer, and smear findings won’t replace clinical judgment or formal HIT testing. But they can help answer practical questions:

Is the patient stabilizing?
- Platelets should eventually turn upward.
- Fibrinogen should stop falling.
- D-dimer should begin to settle.
- Schistocytes should not be increasing.

Is current therapy enough?
- If the patient is worsening biochemically despite argatroban or another agent, that should trigger concern.
- In this case, the team felt argatroban may not have adequately controlled the hypercoagulable state.

Does the overall picture fit aHIT better than classic HIT?
- Severe thrombocytopenia, consumptive markers, thrombosis, and prolonged seropositivity all push in that direction.

## Practical bedside approach

In a patient with suspected severe HIT after cardiac surgery, don’t stop at the platelet count. Consider trending:
- platelet count
- fibrinogen
- D-dimer
- peripheral smear for schistocytes
- clinical evidence of thrombosis or microvascular injury

A worsening consumptive pattern should make you ask:
1. Is this still active HIT biology?
2. Is the anticoagulant strategy sufficient?
3. Do I need to think about aHIT?

## Take-home points

- HIT is a prothrombotic consumptive syndrome, not just a thrombocytopenic one.
- In severe HIT/aHIT, the platelet count alone may underestimate ongoing disease activity.
- Falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and new schistocytes suggest persistent coagulation activation.
- These adjunctive markers can help you judge trajectory, not just diagnosis.
- After cardiac surgery, early and severe thrombocytopenia with thrombosis should raise concern for aHIT, especially when labs suggest ongoing consumption despite appropriate therapy.

## Bottom line

If the platelets are the alarm bell, fibrinogen, D-dimer, and the smear are the smoke. In severe HIT — especially aHIT — listening to the whole room can keep you from missing ongoing disease activity.
