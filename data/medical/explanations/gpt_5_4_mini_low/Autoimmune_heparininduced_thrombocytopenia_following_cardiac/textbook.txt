Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia After Cardiac Surgery: Core Concepts and Case Framing

Cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is one of the classic settings in which heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) must be considered. Unfractionated heparin (UFH) remains the standard intraoperative anticoagulant because it acts rapidly, can be monitored with activated clotting time, is inexpensive, and can be reversed if needed. In the bypass circuit, heparin also helps suppress clotting on artificial surfaces. The tradeoff is that UFH can trigger an immune-mediated prothrombotic disorder, and postoperative cardiac patients are among the most diagnostically challenging populations because thrombocytopenia is expected after surgery even without HIT.

Understanding the normal postoperative platelet pattern is essential. After cardiac surgery, the platelet count usually falls early from hemodilution, consumption during bypass, and perioperative blood loss, then begins to recover over the next several days. This expected nadir is usually modest and occurs in the first 1 to 3 postoperative days. In contrast, HIT is suspected when thrombocytopenia is more pronounced than expected, when the platelet count falls again after an initial recovery, or when thrombocytopenia is accompanied by new thrombosis. In this case, the platelet count declined progressively rather than rebounding, which is not the usual benign postoperative pattern.

HIT is not rare after cardiac surgery. Depending on the heparin regimen used postoperatively, its frequency is roughly 0.5% to 1%, but its importance is greater than the incidence suggests because HIT strongly predisposes to thrombosis. The syndrome is therefore clinically paradoxical: platelet counts fall, yet the main danger is clot formation rather than bleeding. This happens because the immune response activates platelets and monocytes, amplifying thrombin generation and creating a profoundly hypercoagulable state.

Classic HIT is driven by IgG antibodies against complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to heparin. PF4 is a positively charged platelet protein that binds negatively charged heparin; the PF4-heparin complex becomes antigenic in some patients. The resulting IgG antibodies cross-link FcγRIIa receptors on platelets, causing platelet activation and release of procoagulant microparticles. This Fc receptor-mediated activation explains why thrombocytopenia in HIT signals platelet consumption in the context of thrombosis, not a simple failure of platelet production.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) is a more severe biologic variant within the HIT spectrum. In aHIT, antibodies can activate platelets even with little or no heparin present, and they often produce a more aggressive clinical course. This heparin-independent platelet activation distinguishes aHIT from typical HIT and helps explain why some patients have profound thrombocytopenia, persistent platelet activation after heparin is stopped, and signs of consumptive coagulopathy such as falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, or schistocytosis. These patients may also have unexpectedly severe or prolonged thrombosis despite appropriate initial treatment.

This case is notable because it combines several features that should immediately raise concern for aHIT rather than routine postoperative thrombocytopenia. The platelet fall began unusually early, likely because the patient had already been exposed to UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterization, allowing an immune response to be triggered before surgery. He then developed an ischemic stroke, showing that the thrombocytopenia was occurring in a prothrombotic setting. The platelet nadir was extremely low, and the laboratory profile showed strong HIT antibody positivity with atypical platelet activation behavior, including activation even without heparin. Together, these findings frame the case as severe autoimmune HIT, a diagnosis that is easy to miss unless the clinician recognizes that postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery may reflect a dangerous immune thrombotic syndrome rather than an expected surgical effect.

## Clinical Presentation and Early Diagnostic Suspicion

The patient was an elderly man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation, chronic anticoagulation with apixaban, and structural heart disease that had not been fully appreciated until preoperative evaluation revealed a systolic murmur and subsequent catheterization showed moderate aortic stenosis, ascending aortic dilatation, and coronary disease. These baseline factors matter because cardiac surgery patients often have multiple reasons for postoperative platelet abnormalities and neurologic change, including advanced age, cardiopulmonary bypass, perioperative anticoagulant exposure, and postoperative delirium. In this setting, a new platelet fall must be interpreted against the expected postoperative pattern rather than in isolation.

A key early clue was the preoperative heart catheterization performed one week before surgery. Although the heparin exposure during that procedure was small—only 2000 units of unfractionated heparin—it was still clinically important. HIT can be triggered by even limited heparin exposure in a previously sensitized or newly primed patient, and in this case that earlier exposure helped explain why thrombocytopenia began unusually early after surgery. This is an important lesson in chronology: when platelet counts fall too soon after cardiac surgery to fit “classic” HIT timing from the bypass heparin alone, clinicians should look back for any preceding heparin exposure in the prior days to weeks.

The operation itself was extensive and involved several procedures known to require cardiopulmonary bypass and full intraoperative anticoagulation: aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic grafting, coronary artery bypass grafting, and left atrial appendage excision. The patient received a large dose of unfractionated heparin during bypass, which is standard practice in cardiac surgery because UFH remains the preferred anticoagulant for cardiopulmonary bypass. Postoperatively, he was started on routine low-molecular-weight heparin prophylaxis with dalteparin, which is a common management step after cardiac surgery but also provides additional heparin exposure in a patient at risk for HIT.

The platelet trajectory was the central diagnostic signal. His preoperative platelet count was normal at 210 × 10^9/L. On postoperative day 1 it fell to 155 × 10^9/L, which by itself is not unusual after cardiopulmonary bypass. After that, however, the decline did not stabilize or recover as expected; instead, the platelet count continued to fall day by day, reaching 50 × 10^9/L by postoperative day 5 and then dropping further to the mid-20s by postoperative day 6. The pattern was progressive and persistent rather than transient. After cardiac surgery, a modest early platelet drop is expected from hemodilution, platelet activation, and consumption during bypass, typically reaching a nadir around postoperative day 2 to 3 and then beginning to recover. In contrast, HIT is suggested when the platelet count falls again after an initial postoperative decrease, or when the fall is more severe, continues beyond the expected window, or is accompanied by thrombosis.

The neurologic picture added an important thrombotic clue. The patient had postoperative delirium, which is common after major cardiac surgery and can easily distract attention from a focal vascular event. Because the confusion worsened in parallel with further thrombocytopenia, brain imaging was obtained and showed an acute left parietal infarct. In the setting of suspected HIT, an arterial stroke is highly significant. Although HIT is classically associated with venous thrombosis, arterial thrombosis—including stroke, limb ischemia, and myocardial infarction—can occur and often signals a more severe prothrombotic state. Thus, the stroke transformed the platelet fall from a nonspecific postoperative abnormality into a syndrome strongly suggestive of HIT.

At the bedside, the pretest probability of HIT was raised by the combination of timing, severity, thrombosis, and lack of convincing alternative explanations. The platelet fall began very early, which initially seemed too soon for HIT from the bypass heparin alone, but the prior catheterization with UFH provided a plausible sensitizing exposure. The thrombocytopenia became substantial, the patient developed arterial thrombosis, and there was no strong evidence for sepsis, major bleeding, or other causes of consumptive thrombocytopenia. This kind of timeline-based reasoning is central to recognizing HIT early: after cardiac surgery, not every platelet fall is HIT, but progressive thrombocytopenia beyond the expected postoperative pattern—especially when paired with thrombosis—should prompt immediate testing and empiric management while confirmatory studies are pending.

## Differential Diagnosis and Laboratory Confirmation of Autoimmune HIT

Severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery requires a structured differential diagnosis, because platelet counts commonly fall after cardiopulmonary bypass for reasons that are not immune-mediated and do not require HIT-specific treatment. In this case, the platelet count declined progressively after surgery, but the patient remained hemodynamically stable, had no evidence of major bleeding, and did not show laboratory signs of sepsis or disseminated intravascular coagulation. The hemoglobin level was relatively stable after the expected immediate postoperative drop, which argued against ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was normal, blood and urine cultures were negative, and chest radiography did not suggest a new infectious source. Coagulation tests were normal rather than consumptive, and the elevated fibrinogen level fit the early postoperative acute-phase response rather than a bleeding diathesis or overt DIC.

The peripheral blood film also helped narrow the differential. The absence of platelet clumping excluded pseudothrombocytopenia, and the initial lack of schistocytes made a primary thrombotic microangiopathy less likely. Normal renal and liver function further argued against severe systemic microvascular injury, and there was no biochemical evidence of hemolysis: haptoglobin was not low, bilirubin was normal, and the reticulocyte count was not suggestive of significant red cell destruction. These findings made thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and other thrombotic microangiopathies less likely at presentation, although schistocytes later appeared as the HIT-associated coagulopathy evolved. Other uncommon postoperative causes were also considered. Post-transfusion purpura was unlikely because no transfusions were given, and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was improbable because the platelet fall began too early for most newly administered perioperative medications to be responsible.

In the cardiac surgery setting, the most important diagnosis to consider is HIT. The timing is particularly informative: classic postoperative HIT usually begins around postoperative days 5 to 10, after a sensitizing heparin exposure has had time to trigger pathogenic antibodies. Here, the platelet decline began very early after surgery, which initially seemed too soon for the heparin given during cardiopulmonary bypass. However, careful history revealed a smaller but critical preoperative heparin exposure during cardiac catheterization one week earlier. That prior exposure made early-onset HIT biologically plausible, because circulating antibodies can react again when heparin is reintroduced. This temporal clue was essential in moving HIT to the top of the differential.

Laboratory testing then strengthened the diagnosis. A PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive, with a high optical density. In postoperative cardiac patients, this assay is sensitive but not fully specific, because many patients develop anti-PF4/heparin antibodies that are not clinically pathogenic. For that reason, the magnitude of ELISA reactivity matters: a stronger optical density generally increases the likelihood that the antibodies are clinically relevant, especially when paired with thrombocytopenia and thrombosis. In this case, the strongly positive ELISA supported HIT, but confirmatory functional testing was still needed.

The serotonin-release assay (SRA) is the key functional test because it demonstrates whether the patient’s antibodies activate donor platelets. In classic heparin-dependent HIT, patient serum causes platelet activation at low heparin concentrations, where PF4-heparin immune complexes form, but not at very high heparin concentrations, where complex formation is disrupted. That standard pattern was partly present here: the patient’s serum caused strong serotonin release at low UFH concentrations and no release at very high heparin concentration, which is characteristic of pathogenic HIT antibodies. However, the assay also showed something more unusual and more important: strong platelet activation even in the absence of heparin. Heparin-independent platelet activation is a hallmark of autoimmune HIT. It indicates that the antibodies can activate platelets without needing heparin to assemble PF4-containing complexes, which explains the often severe thrombocytopenia and ongoing hypercoagulability seen in this syndrome.

The Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 was expected to inhibit platelet activation if the response were mediated in the usual Fc-dependent manner. In this case, inhibition was incomplete, which initially led the reference laboratory to call the test indeterminate. But that result did not exclude HIT. Rather, it suggested an unusually strong platelet-activating antibody response, with only partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. When interpreted together with the clinical picture, the strong ELISA, the heparin-independent SRA activity, the thrombotic stroke, and the timing related to prior preoperative UFH exposure, the overall pattern was diagnostic of autoimmune HIT.

This case illustrates an important diagnostic principle: functional testing should be interpreted in context, not in isolation. An assay that appears atypical or even initially indeterminate can still support a definitive diagnosis when the clinical syndrome is highly characteristic. Here, early postoperative thrombocytopenia, thrombosis, strong PF4/heparin antibody positivity, and heparin-independent platelet activation all converged on severe autoimmune HIT. The diagnosis explained both the profound platelet fall and the unexpected thrombotic complication, and it guided the need for immediate cessation of heparin exposure and use of non-heparin anticoagulation.

## Management Strategy in Severe Autoimmune HIT with Stroke

Once heparin-induced thrombocytopenia was suspected, the first and most important management step was to stop all heparin exposure. In practice, this means discontinuing not only unfractionated heparin but also all heparin-containing products, including low-molecular-weight heparin. This is essential because HIT is not a simple bleeding disorder; it is a highly prothrombotic immune-mediated syndrome, so continued heparin exposure can perpetuate platelet activation and thrombosis. In this case, prophylactic dalteparin had been started after surgery, but once the platelet fall, strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, and subsequent cerebral infarct made HIT the leading diagnosis, dalteparin was stopped and a non-heparin anticoagulant was started.

The initial anticoagulant chosen was argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor. This is a standard treatment option in acute HIT because it does not depend on heparin binding and provides immediate anticoagulation. Argatroban is particularly useful in unstable hospitalized patients because it is given intravenously and can be adjusted quickly. Its effect is monitored with the activated partial thromboplastin time, which in this case was targeted to a therapeutic range. That said, the postoperative setting makes argatroban management challenging: dose titration can be difficult, laboratory values may be influenced by acute illness, and any anticoagulant must be balanced against bleeding risk, especially after major cardiac surgery and a recent ischemic stroke. Here, argatroban was selected partly because it has a short half-life, allowing rapid discontinuation if hemorrhage occurred.

Fondaparinux had already been started at prophylactic dose before the diagnosis was fully established, but that dose was inadequate once the patient was recognized to have active HIT with thrombosis. Prophylactic fondaparinux may prevent routine postoperative venous thromboembolism, but it is not sufficient treatment for established HIT-associated thrombosis or the marked ongoing hypercoagulability seen in autoimmune HIT. The patient’s later course—progressive thrombocytopenia, cerebral infarction, falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytosis—showed that the disease was not merely being observed but was still actively consuming platelets and driving coagulation. This is why therapy had to be escalated from prophylaxis to full therapeutic anticoagulation.

High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, or IVIG, was added because the serologic and clinical picture fit autoimmune HIT. In classic HIT, platelet activation depends on heparin-dependent immune complexes that signal through platelet Fc receptors. In autoimmune HIT, antibodies can activate platelets even in little or no heparin, and this activation is still Fc receptor mediated. IVIG is used to block Fc receptors on platelets and immune effector cells, thereby interrupting antibody-driven platelet activation. In other words, IVIG does not remove the antibodies, but it can blunt their ability to trigger further platelet activation. A brisk platelet recovery is often expected when IVIG is effective in aHIT, so the modest rise in this patient’s platelet count despite several days of treatment was a warning sign that the antibody-mediated process was unusually severe and persistent.

Because the patient remained clinically and biochemically hypercoagulable, the team followed markers that could suggest whether the prothrombotic state was improving. Fibrinogen and D-dimer are not diagnostic tests for HIT, but in severe HIT they can reflect ongoing consumptive coagulopathy. Falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and later appearance of schistocytes on the peripheral smear supported continued thrombin generation and microvascular injury despite treatment. In this setting, the development of schistocytes did not indicate thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura; rather, it was part of severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy.

Therapy was then changed from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. This switch was driven by two practical considerations. First, fondaparinux is easier to administer because it is given once daily subcutaneously rather than by continuous intravenous infusion with aPTT titration. Second, there was concern that argatroban was not adequately suppressing the HIT-driven hypercoagulability, based on the laboratory trajectory and evolving blood film findings. Fondaparinux is an indirect factor Xa inhibitor that works through antithrombin, and although its use in HIT is off-label, it is commonly used as a non-heparin anticoagulant in selected patients when clinically appropriate.

Because fondaparinux depends on antithrombin for its anticoagulant effect, the patient also received antithrombin concentrate when a mildly reduced antithrombin level was found. This is a key point in interpreting the switch: in severe postoperative illness and active thrombosis, antithrombin can be consumed, potentially weakening the effect of fondaparinux. Supplementing antithrombin was therefore intended to ensure that fondaparinux would have its expected pharmacologic activity. The decision also reflected the need to maintain anticoagulation while minimizing hemorrhagic risk in a patient who had both a recent ischemic stroke and profound thrombocytopenia.

Overall, this case illustrates the central therapeutic dilemma in severe autoimmune HIT: clinicians must treat a dangerous clotting disorder aggressively even when the platelet count is very low and bleeding is a concern. Successful management required close coordination among hematology, cardiac surgery, neurology, and laboratory medicine, with treatment guided by both the evolving clinical picture and specialized HIT testing. The eventual platelet recovery and complete neurologic improvement show that even severe aHIT with stroke can resolve when the diagnosis is recognized and anticoagulation is promptly redirected away from heparin.

## Recovery, Serologic Evolution, and Clinical Lessons

This patient’s recovery illustrates an important feature of autoimmune HIT (aHIT): the clinical course can improve only gradually, even after the initial heparin exposure has stopped and alternative anticoagulation has been started. His platelet count recovered slowly over several weeks, reaching 155 × 10^9/L by postoperative day 62 and normalizing thereafter, while his neurologic deficits resolved completely. The stroke was therefore not a permanent disabling event, but it was a major clue that the thrombocytopenia reflected a prothrombotic immune process rather than routine postoperative platelet consumption. During the recovery phase, fondaparinux was used as a bridge, and his chronic oral anticoagulation with apixaban was later resumed once the acute HIT/aHIT episode had settled and the platelet count had normalized. In practice, the choice to return to a patient’s long-term oral anticoagulant depends on stabilization of the acute prothrombotic state and the need for ongoing indication-specific anticoagulation, such as atrial fibrillation in this case.

A particularly instructive aspect of this case was the persistence of HIT antibodies for months after the clinical event. That is not unusual in HIT, but it matters because a positive antibody test does not by itself define disease activity or dictate ongoing treatment. More important is the relationship between the serology, the platelet count trajectory, and the thrombotic phenotype. Here, repeat testing showed that the functional behavior of the antibody changed over time. Early samples produced the unusual pattern of strong platelet activation even without heparin, which is characteristic of aHIT, and the activation showed only partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade. Later samples became more clearly heparin-dependent, with less activation in the absence of heparin and strong activation when heparin was added. This evolution supports the concept that aHIT can be a dynamic syndrome rather than a fixed laboratory phenotype. As the antibody burden wanes, the serologic picture may shift toward that of more typical HIT.

This temporal evolution helped confirm the diagnosis. In a postoperative patient, especially after cardiac surgery, the temptation is to attribute thrombocytopenia to cardiopulmonary bypass, dilution, transfusion effects, infection, or general postoperative illness. Those explanations were not convincing here because the thrombocytopenia was progressive, profound, and accompanied by thrombosis and biochemical evidence of consumptive coagulopathy. The absence of a better alternative diagnosis mattered. When a patient has a strong-positive PF4 immunoassay, a compatible clinical course, and no other plausible cause for severe thrombocytopenia, the diagnosis of HIT remains likely even if the functional assay is atypical or initially “indeterminate.” In this case, expert review of the serotonin-release assay was essential. The final interpretation depended on understanding that unusual assay behavior may reflect particularly potent antibodies, not absence of disease.

Several clinical hallmarks of severe aHIT were present. The thrombocytopenia began unusually early, before the classic postoperative HIT window, because the sensitizing heparin exposure had occurred during preoperative cardiac catheterization. The platelet nadir was extremely low, falling to 13 × 10^9/L, which is more severe than in typical HIT. The patient developed arterial thrombosis with an ischemic stroke, and laboratory signs of ongoing thrombin generation appeared during treatment, including falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytes. These findings are consistent with the broader syndrome of HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy, a pattern that should raise concern for aHIT rather than routine HIT. The partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade in vitro adds another clue that this was not an ordinary heparin-dependent antibody response.

For learners, the main diagnostic pitfall is narrow anchoring on the most obvious postoperative explanation. In cardiac surgery patients, platelet counts often fall after cardiopulmonary bypass, and delirium is common after major surgery. But HIT should not be dismissed when the platelet fall is progressive, severe, or accompanied by thrombosis. Recent heparin exposure may have occurred outside the operating room, including during preoperative catheterization or other procedures, and that exposure can be the trigger for an earlier-than-expected platelet decline after surgery. Likewise, an atypical functional assay should not lead to premature exclusion of HIT. Instead, it should prompt expert laboratory review, because aHIT can produce unusual heparin-independent activation patterns and, as shown here, even partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

The case also has broader implications for diagnosis and research. It expands the recognized laboratory spectrum of aHIT and shows that the serologic phenotype may evolve over time from strongly heparin-independent activation to a more conventional heparin-dependent pattern as antibodies wane. That evolution reinforces the need for repeat testing when the initial assay result does not fit the clinical picture. More generally, the case teaches a practical approach: in a postcardiac surgery patient with falling platelets, first ask whether the timing and severity are compatible with HIT, then look for thrombosis or consumptive coagulopathy, then obtain HIT immunoassay testing, and if the functional assay is unusual or discordant, seek specialized interpretation rather than assuming the result is negative. In severe suspected aHIT, anticoagulation should be switched promptly to a non-heparin agent, and adjunctive therapy such as intravenous immunoglobulin may be considered when platelet activation is marked or persistent.

Overall, this patient’s favorable outcome depended on early recognition, continued diagnostic reassessment, and avoidance of diagnostic closure. The key teaching point is that postoperative thrombocytopenia is not always routine, and delirium is not always benign. When early thrombocytopenia, stroke, and laboratory evidence of HIT converge, aHIT should be strongly considered—even if the first functional assay is atypical.
