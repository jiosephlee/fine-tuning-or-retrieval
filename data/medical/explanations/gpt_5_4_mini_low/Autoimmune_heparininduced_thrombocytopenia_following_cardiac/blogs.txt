Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# When the Platelets Fall Too Soon: Early Post-Cardiac Surgery HIT From Preoperative Heparin Exposure

## The trap: “It’s too early for HIT”

After cardiac surgery, thrombocytopenia is expected. Platelets fall from hemodilution, consumption on the bypass circuit, surgical stress, and then usually start to recover. So when the platelet count drops on postoperative day 1 to 4, most of us think: *post-CPB thrombocytopenia, not HIT*.

This case is a reminder that timing can mislead you.

An elderly man underwent cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass and received a large intraoperative heparin dose. His platelet count fell early, reaching 50 × 10⁹/L by POD5, and he later proved to have an acute parietal stroke. The eventual diagnosis was **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**, but the key clinical clue was that the immune process had been **primed before surgery** by a small UFH exposure during preoperative cardiac catheterization.

That’s the pearl: **HIT that appears “too early” after cardiac surgery may actually be perfectly timed if the patient had heparin exposure days earlier.**

## What happened in this case

The patient was in his 70s with atrial fibrillation, dyspnea, and a newly recognized murmur. During diagnostic cardiac catheterization, he did **not** hold apixaban, so the procedure was done via radial access with only **2000 U of UFH**.

One week later, he underwent major cardiac surgery with CPB and received **45,000 U UFH** intraoperatively. Postoperatively, dalteparin thromboprophylaxis was started on POD1.

His platelet count pattern looked like this:

- Pre-op: **210 × 10⁹/L**
- POD1: **155 × 10⁹/L**
- POD5: **50 × 10⁹/L**
- POD6: **26 × 10⁹/L** (repeat 24)
- Nadir: **13 × 10⁹/L**

He was not in shock, not bleeding, and cultures were negative. Brain imaging, prompted by worsening delirium, showed an **acute left parietal infarct**.

HIT testing came back strongly positive by PF4/polyanion ELISA. The SRA was more unusual: strong platelet activation **at 0 heparin**, strong activation at low-dose heparin, and incomplete inhibition with Fc receptor blockade. That pattern supported **aHIT**, a more aggressive HIT phenotype with heparin-independent platelet activation.

## Why the timing matters

Classic teaching says HIT usually shows up **POD5 to POD10** after cardiac surgery. That’s still true in many cases. But this case shows why “before POD5” does **not** exclude HIT.

If a patient has been exposed to heparin in the preceding days or weeks, they may already have circulating antibodies and can present with an **accelerated** platelet fall after the next heparin hit.

In this case, the small catheterization dose of UFH a week before surgery likely served as the sensitizing exposure. The intraoperative heparin and postoperative LMWH then set off the full clinical syndrome.

## Post-CPB thrombocytopenia: what’s expected?

After cardiopulmonary bypass, the platelet count often drops early and then begins to rebound. A typical pattern is:

- early fall in the first 1–3 days
- nadir around POD2–3
- recovery thereafter

That pattern is usually from surgical consumption and dilution.

What should make you pause is **a second platelet fall**, or a fall that keeps worsening after the early post-op dip instead of recovering. That is much more concerning for HIT.

In this case, the platelet count kept falling from POD1 through POD5, which is not the usual benign CPB pattern.

## Clues that this was not just “post-op thrombocytopenia”

Several features pushed this beyond routine postoperative thrombocytopenia:

- Progressive platelet decline rather than recovery after the early post-CPB dip
- Very low nadir: **13 × 10⁹/L**
- Thrombotic complication: **ischemic stroke**
- Strongly positive HIT immunoassay
- SRA consistent with **aHIT**, including heparin-independent activation
- Evidence of consumptive hypercoagulability during treatment: falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, later schistocytes

The big takeaway: **severe thrombocytopenia with thrombosis after cardiac surgery should keep HIT high on the list, even if the timing feels “too early.”**

## aHIT: the more dangerous cousin

Autoimmune HIT behaves differently from classic HIT. The antibodies can activate platelets even in the **absence of heparin**, which helps explain:

- more profound thrombocytopenia
- more persistent platelet activation
- ongoing hypercoagulability
- atypical lab patterns on functional testing

This patient’s SRA was especially interesting because platelet activation was only **partially inhibited** by the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3. That’s not the usual HIT pattern and highlights how odd aHIT can look in the lab.

For bedside clinicians, the practical message is simpler:

> **If the clinical story screams HIT, don’t let an “indeterminate” functional assay talk you out of it.**

## Management lessons

Once HIT was suspected, all heparin products were stopped. He was switched to **argatroban**, and because of the severity of thrombocytopenia and recent stroke, he also received **high-dose IVIG**.

When markers suggested persistent hypercoagulability, therapy was changed to **therapeutic fondaparinux**, with antithrombin supplementation because fondaparinux is AT-dependent and his AT level was mildly low.

His platelets eventually recovered, and he had a complete neurologic recovery.

## Practical take-home for the ED, ICU, and perioperative team

When a post-cardiac surgery patient’s platelets fall early, don’t stop at “CPB did it.”

Ask:

1. **Was there heparin exposure before surgery?**  
   Even a small UFH dose during catheterization can prime HIT.

2. **Is the platelet count following the expected CPB pattern?**  
   Early fall is common; persistent decline or a second drop is not.

3. **Is there thrombosis, delirium, organ ischemia, or unexplained hypercoagulability?**  
   Those should raise suspicion fast.

4. **Don’t rely on timing alone to exclude HIT.**  
   HIT can present early if the patient was already sensitized.

5. **If the pretest probability is high, act first.**  
   Stop heparin, start a non-heparin anticoagulant, and send confirmatory testing.

## Bottom line

Early postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery is common, but **early-onset HIT is real**, especially when there has been **recent preoperative UFH exposure**. This case shows how a small catheterization heparin dose can set up a catastrophic immune response that declares itself after surgery.

## Key points

- **HIT before POD5 after cardiac surgery is uncommon, but not impossible.**
- **Recent preoperative heparin exposure can explain early-onset HIT.**
- **A falling platelet count that does not recover after CPB should prompt HIT evaluation.**
- **Thrombosis, especially stroke, should strongly increase suspicion.**
- **aHIT can be severe, prolonged, and functionally atypical on lab testing.**



# Not Just HIT, but Autoimmune HIT: Recognizing the Severe Hypercoagulable Phenotype

## The case that should make you sit up

Post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia is common. HIT is less common, but still very much on the board. What makes this case different is that the platelet count didn’t just fall — it cratered to 13 × 10^9/L, the patient developed a thrombotic stroke, and the labs started to look like a consumptive coagulopathy: rising d-dimer, falling fibrinogen, and schistocytes.

That pattern should make you think beyond “routine postoperative thrombocytopenia” and even beyond classic HIT.

This is autoimmune HIT (aHIT): a more aggressive, more persistent, and often more hypercoagulable HIT phenotype that can continue to drive platelet activation even after heparin is stopped.

## Why this wasn’t just expected post-bypass thrombocytopenia

After cardiac surgery, platelet counts usually dip early from hemodilution, consumption, and the bypass circuit itself. A modest fall on POD1 is not surprising.

But here, the trajectory was wrong for routine postoperative thrombocytopenia:

- Platelets were 210 on admission, then 155 on POD1
- Then a steady decline to 50 by POD5
- Then 26 on POD6 and ultimately a nadir of 13 × 10^9/L

That degree of thrombocytopenia is not what we usually see from cardiopulmonary bypass alone. And the timing mattered too. The HIT window after cardiac surgery is usually POD5–10, but this patient’s platelet fall started early because he had already been exposed to UFH during pre-op catheterization. That detail is easy to miss and clinically very important.

## The red flags for severe aHIT physiology

Classic HIT is a prothrombotic disorder, but aHIT is the version that behaves badly: more intense platelet activation, more persistent thrombocytopenia, and more “consumptive” features.

In this case, several features should push you to think aHIT rather than garden-variety HIT:

- Profound thrombocytopenia, with nadir 13 × 10^9/L
- Thrombotic stroke despite recognition of thrombocytopenia
- Rising d-dimer
- Falling fibrinogen
- Schistocytes appearing on smear during treatment
- Only modest platelet recovery after IVIG

That combination suggests ongoing platelet activation and thrombin generation, not just isolated immune-mediated platelet destruction.

A key practical pearl: in aHIT, the platelet count is not the only thing to track. The coagulation profile can start to look consumptive, and that should raise the level of alarm.

## What made the serology unusual

The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive, which fit HIT. But the functional testing was more interesting.

The serotonin-release assay showed:

- Strong serotonin release at low heparin concentrations
- No suppression at very high heparin, which is actually consistent with HIT biology
- But also strong release even with 0 heparin, which is a hallmark of aHIT
- And only partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade with monoclonal antibody IV.3, which is unusual and suggests an especially potent platelet-activating antibody response

That last point is the kind of laboratory nuance most clinicians won’t see often, but the clinical lesson is simpler: if the phenotype looks like severe HIT and the assay is messy, don’t let an “indeterminate” label lull you into missing aHIT.

## Why this matters at the bedside

This patient was not just “HIT positive.” He had a hypercoagulable syndrome with ongoing platelet activation and tissue injury.

That changes management urgency.

He was treated with:

- Stopping dalteparin
- Argatroban
- High-dose IVIG
- Later transition to fondaparinux, with antithrombin supplementation
- Eventual resumption of apixaban once stable

The choice of argatroban first makes sense when bleeding risk is a concern and close titration is desired, especially with a recent stroke. But the bigger teaching point is that aHIT may require more than just stopping heparin and starting a non-heparin anticoagulant. IVIG can be important when the immune activation is severe and persistent.

## When should you escalate from “possible HIT” to “severe aHIT physiology”?

You don’t need to diagnose aHIT on day one to act like it might be there.

Think in that direction when you see:

- Platelets falling to very low levels, especially <20 × 10^9/L
- Thrombosis occurring despite heparin cessation
- Falling fibrinogen or rising d-dimer suggesting consumptive coagulopathy
- Schistocytes or other signs of thrombotic microangiopathy-like physiology
- Persistent or worsening thrombocytopenia despite appropriate non-heparin anticoagulation
- Strong HIT immunoassay positivity with an atypical or surprising functional assay pattern

## Take-home points

- Profound thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery is not “just post-op” until proven otherwise.
- aHIT is a more aggressive HIT variant characterized by heparin-independent platelet activation and severe hypercoagulability.
- A platelet nadir of 13 × 10^9/L, plus rising d-dimer, falling fibrinogen, and schistocytosis, should push you toward aHIT physiology.
- An “indeterminate” functional assay does not exclude aHIT when the clinical picture is convincing.
- In severe suspected aHIT, stop all heparin, start a non-heparin anticoagulant promptly, and consider IVIG when ongoing immune platelet activation seems likely.
- Don’t miss preoperative heparin exposure in patients with “early” post-cardiac surgery HIT.

The big lesson here is that HIT is not one disease. Some patients have a much more dangerous, more thrombotic, more persistent syndrome — and recognizing that shift early can change the outcome.



# The Indeterminate SRA That Wasn’t: How to Read Unusual HIT Functional Assays

## Why this case is worth your attention

Post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia is common. HIT is less common, but it’s the diagnosis you don’t want to miss because the price of missing it is often thrombosis, stroke, or worse.

This case is interesting because the patient’s functional assay looked “indeterminate” at first glance — yet the clinical picture was strongly consistent with HIT, and the eventual answer was **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**. The twist: the serotonin-release assay (SRA) showed **strong platelet activation even at 0 heparin**, plus activation at low-dose heparin, and only **partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade**. That pattern is a teaching pearl all by itself.

If you see an unusual HIT functional assay and reflexively downgrade the diagnosis, you can get burned. Sometimes the assay is not “negative.” Sometimes it’s telling you you’re dealing with a more aggressive HIT phenotype.

## The clinical setup: why HIT was on the table

The patient was a man in his 70s who underwent major cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass and received large-dose intraoperative UFH. Postoperatively, he was also exposed to dalteparin thromboprophylaxis. His platelets fell early and progressively, reaching 50 × 10^9/L by POD5 and later a nadir of 13 × 10^9/L. He also developed a parietal ischemic stroke.

That combination should make you think hard about HIT, especially because:

- the platelet fall was not explained by bleeding, shock, or infection
- the timing fit with **preoperative heparin exposure** during catheterization
- he had **thrombosis** in the setting of thrombocytopenia
- the PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive

The diagnostic challenge wasn’t whether HIT was plausible. It was how to interpret the functional assay.

## What a classic HIT functional assay should look like

In classic HIT, the SRA usually shows:

- **low or absent serotonin release at 0 heparin**
- **strong serotonin release at low heparin concentrations**
- **loss of activation at high heparin concentrations**, because excess heparin disrupts PF4/heparin immune complex formation

That’s the usual “bell-shaped” HIT pattern.

In other words, low-dose heparin helps form the antigenic complex; very high-dose heparin tends to shut the process down.

## What made this SRA unusual

This patient’s SRA had three important features:

1. **Strong serotonin release at 0 heparin**
2. **Strong release at low-dose heparin**
3. **Only partial inhibition by the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3**

That’s not the pattern of straightforward classic HIT.

Strong activation at **0 heparin** points toward **aHIT**, in which HIT antibodies can activate platelets even without added heparin. That’s one reason these cases can present with:

- more severe thrombocytopenia
- ongoing thrombosis
- consumptive coagulopathy
- prolonged platelet recovery

The partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade was even more unusual. It suggests the antibody-mediated activation was intense enough that the usual inhibitory effect of IV.3 was incomplete. The key practical lesson is that **an atypical SRA doesn’t automatically rule out HIT**. It may actually be a clue to aHIT.

## So what is autoimmune HIT?

Autoimmune HIT is a more severe HIT subtype in which antibodies can activate platelets with little or no added heparin. Clinically, it often behaves like a hypercoagulable syndrome rather than a simple thrombocytopenic state.

Think of clues like:

- profound thrombocytopenia
- thrombosis despite stopping heparin
- persistently positive functional assays
- platelet activation at **0 heparin**
- consumptive features such as falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytes

This patient had several of those features, including falling fibrinogen and later schistocytes while on argatroban, consistent with severe HIT-associated hypercoagulability.

## Why the “indeterminate” label can mislead

Functional assays are powerful, but they are not magic. A result can be difficult to interpret for several reasons:

- the antibody phenotype may be atypical
- the assay conditions may not capture the full biology
- the lab may see a pattern that doesn’t fit the standard template
- the sample may be from a phase of disease in which antibody behavior is evolving

The danger is not the lab result itself — it’s over-trusting a binary interpretation.

In this case, the initial “indeterminate” SRA could have falsely weakened diagnostic confidence. But the clinical course, immunoassay positivity, and later functional assay behavior supported HIT, specifically aHIT.

## Clinician-laboratory communication matters

This case is a great reminder that HIT diagnosis is not something to outsource blindly to the test report.

When the story and the assay disagree, pick up the phone.

Useful questions for the lab include:

- Was there platelet activation at 0 heparin?
- Was activation suppressed at high heparin concentrations?
- Was the Fc receptor blockade complete or partial?
- Are there serial samples that show evolution of the functional profile?
- Does the pattern fit classic HIT or something like aHIT?

The McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory’s deeper review of this case was essential. Without that back-and-forth, this patient might have been mislabeled as having an equivocal functional assay rather than severe aHIT.

## Practical bedside takeaways

When the platelet count drops after cardiac surgery, don’t anchor on “post-op thrombocytopenia” and move on. Look at the pattern, the timing, and the thrombotic phenotype. In HIT, the lab report should support your clinical reasoning — not replace it.

### Key take-home points

- **A strong positive PF4 ELISA plus thrombosis and thrombocytopenia is still HIT until proven otherwise.**
- **SRA activation at 0 heparin is a red flag for autoimmune HIT, not a reason to dismiss the diagnosis.**
- **An “indeterminate” functional assay can still represent clinically important HIT.**
- **Partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade may reflect unusually aggressive platelet-activating antibodies.**
- **When the case doesn’t fit the template, talk to the reference lab early.**

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that HIT functional assays are patterns, not just numbers. The SRA wasn’t “indeterminate” because the diagnosis was weak — it was unusual because the antibody biology was unusual. In a patient with the right clinical story, **platelet activation at 0 heparin should increase your suspicion for aHIT**, not lower it.

If the clinical picture is convincing, don’t let an atypical assay talk you out of the diagnosis.



# Argatroban, Fondaparinux, and IVIG: Managing aHIT When Standard Therapy Seems Inadequate

## When HIT behaves like a runaway prothrombotic syndrome

Most clinicians are familiar with the “classic” HIT playbook: stop heparin, start a non-heparin anticoagulant, and wait for the platelets to recover. But this case is a reminder that some HIT is not classic at all.

This elderly man developed early postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, then an ischemic stroke, and then a platelet nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L with laboratory features suggesting ongoing consumptive coagulopathy. The serology and clinical course ultimately fit **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** — a more aggressive HIT phenotype in which antibodies can activate platelets even without added heparin.

That distinction matters at the bedside, because aHIT can keep behaving like an active thrombotic disease long after you think you’ve removed the trigger.

## Why this case is tricky

The immediate challenge was not just diagnosis; it was treatment intensity and sequencing.

The patient had:

- Recent major cardiac surgery
- New ischemic stroke
- Profound thrombocytopenia
- Evidence suggesting persistent hypercoagulability despite initial therapy

That combination creates a very narrow therapeutic lane. You need to anticoagulate, but you also need to be thoughtful about bleeding risk, especially after surgery and stroke. This is exactly where the choice of agent — and when to add IVIG — becomes clinically important.

## Why argatroban made sense first

Argatroban was a reasonable initial choice here.

It has a short half-life, is IV titratable, and can be turned off quickly if bleeding occurs. In a patient with:

- recent sternotomy and valve/aortic surgery,
- a recent cerebral infarct,
- and platelets falling into single digits,

the ability to stop anticoagulation rapidly is a major advantage.

That’s the practical bedside logic: when hemorrhage risk is real and dynamic, a short-acting infusion gives you control.

In this case, the treating team used therapeutic argatroban with a PTT target and followed indirect markers of disease activity, including:

- fibrinogen
- d-dimer
- peripheral smear for schistocytes

That’s a good example of thinking beyond the platelet count alone. In aHIT, the count may lag behind what the immune system is still doing.

## Why IVIG belongs on the table in aHIT

Standard HIT usually improves once heparin is stopped and an alternative anticoagulant is started. aHIT is different because the antibodies can drive platelet activation in a heparin-independent way.

That is where **high-dose IVIG** comes in.

The goal is to interrupt Fc-mediated platelet activation by saturating Fc receptors and dampening the immune complex effect. In other words, IVIG is being used not as “supportive care,” but as a way to blunt the underlying immune amplification loop.

In this case, the team gave high-dose IVIG because the thrombocytopenia was severe and the patient had evidence of ongoing thrombosis risk. Importantly, the platelet response was only modest. That’s a clue in itself: in severe aHIT, IVIG may help, but the response may not be immediate or dramatic.

## When the labs tell you the disease is still active

One of the most useful teaching points from this case is that management should not be guided by the platelet count alone.

During argatroban therapy, the patient showed signs concerning for ongoing consumptive activity:

- falling fibrinogen
- rising d-dimer
- schistocytes appearing on smear

That constellation suggests that the prothrombotic process is not yet fully controlled. In a patient with suspected aHIT, that should make you pause and ask: is the current regimen enough?

This is not “failed treatment” in a simplistic sense. It is a sign that the antibody-mediated process may still be generating thrombin and platelet consumption.

## Why switch to fondaparinux?

The switch from argatroban to therapeutic fondaparinux is interesting and practical.

Fondaparinux offers:

- once-daily subcutaneous dosing
- no continuous infusion
- no PTT titration burden
- a familiar off-label role in HIT

Here, the switch was driven by two concerns:

1. **Possible inadequate suppression of hypercoagulability on argatroban**
2. **Practical ease of administration**, especially if the patient remained stable enough for a less intensive regimen

The team also gave antithrombin concentrate before starting fondaparinux because fondaparinux is AT-dependent and the patient’s AT level was mildly low. That’s a useful nuance: if you’re choosing an AT-dependent agent, AT deficiency may matter.

## Bedside pearls from this case

This case is a good reminder that severe aHIT can be a moving target. A few practical lessons stand out:

- Early postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery is not automatically “expected.”
- If the platelet fall starts before POD5, think about **preoperative heparin exposure** as a trigger.
- In a patient with thrombocytopenia plus thrombosis after heparin, HIT remains a clinical diagnosis even before confirmatory testing returns.
- In aHIT, persistent thrombocytopenia and markers of consumptive coagulopathy may justify escalation beyond “stop heparin and wait.”
- IVIG is worth considering when platelet recovery is poor or platelet activation appears ongoing.
- Argatroban is attractive when bleeding risk is high and rapid reversibility matters.
- Fondaparinux can be a useful longer-acting alternative once the patient is more stable.

## Take-home points

- **aHIT is more aggressive than classic HIT** and can cause profound thrombocytopenia with ongoing thrombosis risk.
- **Argatroban is a sensible first move** in a high-bleeding-risk postoperative patient because it is short-acting and titratable.
- **IVIG can be an important adjunct** in aHIT because it helps interrupt Fc-mediated platelet activation.
- **Falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and schistocytes** can signal ongoing HIT-associated hypercoagulability even after heparin is stopped.
- **Therapy may need to be adjusted** if the platelet count does not rebound and the disease still looks biologically active.
- In severe aHIT, management is not just “pick a non-heparin anticoagulant” — it’s a dynamic process of reassessing whether the immune-thrombotic process is truly under control.

## Final thought

This case is a great example of why aHIT deserves a place in the differential when the clinical picture is worse than expected. If the platelet count keeps falling, the labs suggest ongoing thrombin generation, and the patient has already declared themselves with thrombosis, it may be time to think beyond standard HIT algorithms.

Sometimes the key question is not, “Did we stop heparin?”  
It’s, “Have we actually turned off the immune thrombosis?”



# Delirium, Stroke, and HIT: Why Subtle Neurologic Change After Cardiac Surgery Deserves a Thrombotic Lens

## The trap: “It’s just postop delirium”
After cardiac surgery, delirium is common enough that it can become background noise. But this case is a good reminder that not every mental status change is benign, and not every neurologic change needs to announce itself with dramatic focal deficits.

This elderly man developed a progressive platelet fall after cardiac surgery, then worsening delirium. He had no obvious lateralizing exam findings. Still, that subtle neurologic decline triggered brain imaging—and CT showed an acute left parietal infarct.

That’s the key teaching point: in a thrombocytopenic post-cardiac surgery patient, worsening delirium should not automatically be filed under “expected postop course.” If HIT is on the table, neurologic change may be the first clue to arterial thrombosis.

## HIT is not just a venous clotting disorder
HIT is often taught as a venous thrombosis syndrome, but that’s an incomplete mental model. In cardiac surgery patients, HIT can present with arterial events too, including stroke.

Why this matters:

- HIT creates a profoundly prothrombotic state
- Arterial thrombosis can occur early and may be clinically subtle
- Neurologic findings may be masked by sedation, delirium, fatigue, or baseline postop cognitive dysfunction

In this case, the stroke was discovered because the delirium worsened rather than because the patient suddenly developed hemiparesis or aphasia. That’s exactly the kind of presentation that can be missed if we wait for a “classic stroke exam.”

## Why this case should raise HIT alarm bells
The platelet count didn’t just drift down a little after surgery—it kept falling:

- Preop platelets: 210
- POD1: 155
- POD5: 50
- POD6: 26

That’s a concerning trajectory in a post-cardiac surgery patient, especially when the decline is progressive rather than the usual early postoperative nadir followed by recovery.

The neurologic event makes the story even more important. In this setting, the differential for thrombocytopenia + new brain injury should include HIT-associated arterial thrombosis until proven otherwise.

A few features in this case make HIT particularly plausible:

- Recent intraoperative heparin exposure for cardiopulmonary bypass
- Additional preoperative UFH during cardiac catheterization
- Strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA
- Ischemic stroke
- No better alternative explanation for the platelet fall and thrombosis

## Don’t let “no focal deficits” fool you
Postoperative delirium can be multifactorial, but in a thrombocytopenic patient it should not be treated as a diagnosis of exclusion without thinking about thrombosis.

Reasons neurologic events may be subtle in this setting:

- Delirium can be the only observable manifestation of stroke
- Sedatives, pain meds, sleep deprivation, and ICU factors obscure exam findings
- Small cortical infarcts may produce non-specific cognitive change before obvious deficits appear
- Exam reliability is often limited in early postop patients

So if the mental status is worsening, especially alongside falling platelets, you need a thrombotic lens.

## Practical bedside lesson: delirium + thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery = image sooner
This case supports a low threshold for neuroimaging when a post-cardiac surgery patient with thrombocytopenia develops worsening mental status.

You don’t need to wait for hemiparesis to justify a CT scan. In fact, waiting may delay recognition of:

- Ischemic stroke
- HIT-associated hypercoagulability
- The need to stop all heparin products and start non-heparin anticoagulation

That diagnostic delay matters, because HIT is not just a lab diagnosis—it’s a time-sensitive thrombotic emergency.

## The lab nuance: severe HIT can look “weird”
This case also shows that severe HIT can have atypical serologic behavior. The patient’s serotonin-release assay had features of autoimmune HIT, including platelet activation even without heparin.

For the bedside clinician, the practical point is simpler:

- A strongly positive HIT immunoassay plus thrombocytopenia plus thrombosis is enough to act
- Don’t wait for the “perfect” confirmatory test pattern before treating if the clinical probability is high

## What to do when HIT is suspected
In a patient like this, the response is not “watch and wait.”

Core steps:

- Stop all heparin products immediately
- Start a non-heparin anticoagulant
- Evaluate for thrombosis, including arterial events if there is any neurologic change
- In severe autoimmune HIT, consider adjunctive therapies such as IVIG in consultation with hematology

In this case, anticoagulation was changed to argatroban and IVIG was given, then therapy was transitioned to fondaparinux as the clinical course evolved.

## Take-home points
- Worsening delirium after cardiac surgery is not automatically “routine delirium,” especially when platelets are falling.
- HIT can cause arterial thrombosis, including ischemic stroke, not just venous thromboembolism.
- Neurologic injury in HIT may present subtly, with mental status change before focal deficits appear.
- In thrombocytopenic post-cardiac surgery patients, a low threshold for brain imaging is appropriate when delirium worsens.
- If HIT is suspected clinically, stop heparin and treat promptly with a non-heparin anticoagulant—don’t wait for a textbook presentation.



# Beyond the Platelet Count: Using Fibrinogen, D-Dimer, and Smear Findings to Track HIT Activity

## The part of HIT we don’t look at enough

HIT is usually taught as a platelet problem. The platelet count falls, the PF4 ELISA lights up, the SRA confirms it, and we move on to non-heparin anticoagulation.

But severe HIT — and especially autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — is not just thrombocytopenia. It is a hypercoagulable, consumptive, platelet-activating syndrome. In other words: the platelet count is only the headline. The rest of the story is in the coagulation markers, the smear, and the overall trajectory.

This case is a great reminder.

An elderly man underwent cardiac surgery with substantial intraoperative UFH exposure, then received postoperative dalteparin. By POD5 his platelets had already dropped to 50 × 10^9/L, and by POD6 they were 26 × 10^9/L. He then declared himself further with an acute parietal stroke. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive, and the SRA eventually showed a pattern consistent with aHIT — including heparin-independent platelet activation.

What makes this case especially educational is that the team didn’t rely on the platelet count alone. They followed fibrinogen, D-dimer, and the peripheral smear to gauge whether the disease process was still smoldering.

## Why the platelet count can be misleading

In classic HIT, the platelet count often starts to improve after heparin is stopped and a non-heparin anticoagulant is started. That improvement can be reassuring — but it is not always enough.

In aHIT, the syndrome can be more aggressive:
- profound thrombocytopenia
- ongoing thrombin generation
- consumptive coagulopathy
- thrombosis despite treatment

That means a rising platelet count doesn’t always equal disease control, and a persistently low platelet count doesn’t always tell you whether the process is still active versus simply slow to recover.

This is where “global” markers become useful.

## Fibrinogen and D-dimer: the hidden trend line

In this case, serial labs told a more ominous story than the platelet count alone.

While on argatroban, the patient developed:
- progressively falling fibrinogen, reaching a nadir of 2.49 g/L
- rising D-dimer
- new schistocytes on smear from POD9 to POD16

That pattern is hard to ignore. It suggests ongoing thrombin generation and consumption, not just isolated thrombocytopenia.

### Why this matters
In severe HIT/aHIT, falling fibrinogen can be a clue that the patient is in a consumptive state. Rising D-dimer adds support for ongoing clot breakdown and systemic coagulation activation. Together, they suggest the process is still biologically active — even if you’ve already “done the right thing” by stopping heparin and starting a direct thrombin inhibitor.

This is especially valuable when:
- the clinical picture is noisy after cardiac surgery
- the platelet count is slow to recover
- the diagnosis is uncertain
- you’re wondering whether current anticoagulation is enough

## The smear still earns its keep

The peripheral smear is often underused in postoperative thrombocytopenia. In this case, the later appearance of schistocytes mattered.

Not because schistocytes are specific for HIT — they aren’t — but because they support a consumptive microangiopathic picture when they appear in the right context. Here, the smear evolved from no schistocytes early on to detectable fragments later, paralleling the biochemical evidence of ongoing coagulopathy.

That combination — falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, schistocytes — is a red flag for ongoing HIT-associated hypercoagulability.

## When to think aHIT

Autoimmune HIT is the version of HIT that should make you uncomfortable.

Think about it when HIT behaves badly:
- very severe thrombocytopenia
- thrombosis, sometimes arterial
- platelet activation even without heparin
- persistent or progressive thrombocytopenia after heparin cessation
- consumptive coagulopathy
- incomplete response to standard management

This case had several of those features. The patient’s nadir platelet count reached 13 × 10^9/L, he had a stroke, and the serology was unusual — including strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and only partial inhibition with Fc receptor blockade.

The takeaway is not that you need an SRA subtype to diagnose aHIT at the bedside. It’s that the overall pattern matters. If the labs look like ongoing consumption and thrombosis, the disease may be more than “ordinary HIT.”

## How this can change bedside decisions

Serial fibrinogen, D-dimer, and smear findings won’t replace clinical judgment or formal HIT testing. But they can help answer practical questions:

Is the patient stabilizing?
- Platelets should eventually turn upward.
- Fibrinogen should stop falling.
- D-dimer should begin to settle.
- Schistocytes should not be increasing.

Is current therapy enough?
- If the patient is worsening biochemically despite argatroban or another agent, that should trigger concern.
- In this case, the team felt argatroban may not have adequately controlled the hypercoagulable state.

Does the overall picture fit aHIT better than classic HIT?
- Severe thrombocytopenia, consumptive markers, thrombosis, and prolonged seropositivity all push in that direction.

## Practical bedside approach

In a patient with suspected severe HIT after cardiac surgery, don’t stop at the platelet count. Consider trending:
- platelet count
- fibrinogen
- D-dimer
- peripheral smear for schistocytes
- clinical evidence of thrombosis or microvascular injury

A worsening consumptive pattern should make you ask:
1. Is this still active HIT biology?
2. Is the anticoagulant strategy sufficient?
3. Do I need to think about aHIT?

## Take-home points

- HIT is a prothrombotic consumptive syndrome, not just a thrombocytopenic one.
- In severe HIT/aHIT, the platelet count alone may underestimate ongoing disease activity.
- Falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and new schistocytes suggest persistent coagulation activation.
- These adjunctive markers can help you judge trajectory, not just diagnosis.
- After cardiac surgery, early and severe thrombocytopenia with thrombosis should raise concern for aHIT, especially when labs suggest ongoing consumption despite appropriate therapy.

## Bottom line

If the platelets are the alarm bell, fibrinogen, D-dimer, and the smear are the smoke. In severe HIT — especially aHIT — listening to the whole room can keep you from missing ongoing disease activity.
