# When a Young Adult With “Type 2” Diabetes Isn’t Type 2: Spotting HNF1A-MODY at the Bedside

## The case that should make you pause

A young adult shows up with hyperglycemia, glycosuria, and an HbA1c of 10.2%. He’s not in DKA. He’s not obviously insulin resistant. His BMI is 24.6 kg/m², there’s no acanthosis nigricans, and the family history is striking: diabetes in the maternal grandmother in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20.

That’s the kind of pattern that should make you stop thinking “routine type 2 diabetes” and start thinking about monogenic diabetes — especially MODY.

This patient was ultimately found to have a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant, consistent with HNF1A-MODY. The result mattered: treatment was de-escalated from insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, and his HbA1c improved from 10.2% to 6.0%.

## Why this matters in real life

MODY is uncommon, but not rare enough to ignore. It accounts for a meaningful fraction of young-onset diabetes, and it is frequently misclassified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. That mislabeling can lead to the wrong therapy, unnecessary insulin use, and missed opportunities for family counseling.

HNF1A-MODY is the most common MODY subtype. The classic phenotype is autosomal dominant early-onset diabetes, often with glycosuria out of proportion to the degree of hyperglycemia, preserved C-peptide early on, and negative autoantibodies. But real patients don’t always read the textbook. BMI may be borderline. Some will have metabolic-syndrome-ish features. And by the time they present, beta-cell function may already be falling.

## Bedside clues that should trigger suspicion for MODY

This case hits several of the high-yield clues:

- **Young onset**
- **Strong autosomal dominant family history**
- **Negative islet autoantibodies**
- **Preserved endogenous insulin secretion**
- **Glycosuria**
- **No convincing phenotype of type 1 diabetes or classic insulin resistance**

The glycosuria is particularly interesting in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A regulates renal glucose handling, and patients can spill glucose at lower plasma levels than you’d expect. If you see glycosuria early, especially with a family history of diabetes across generations, it should raise your antennae.

## The practical diagnostic problem: MODY can look like type 1 or type 2

The diagnostic trap is that HNF1A-MODY may present with features that overlap both major diabetes categories.

It can masquerade as **type 1 diabetes** because:
- patients are young
- insulin secretion declines over time
- weight loss and polyuria/polydipsia may appear
- clinicians may assume insulin deficiency means autoimmune diabetes

It can masquerade as **type 2 diabetes** because:
- patients may not be lean
- some have mildly elevated triglycerides or other metabolic features
- they may present with hyperglycemia discovered on routine screening, like this patient

So the question is not “Does this patient look exactly like MODY?” The better question is: **Does the pattern fit a monogenic, autosomal dominant beta-cell disorder better than autoimmune or insulin-resistant diabetes?**

## A pragmatic bedside approach

When a young adult presents with diabetes, a few data points can get you a long way:

### 1) Look hard at the family history
Ask specifically about:
- diabetes in successive generations
- age at diagnosis
- insulin use from the start vs later escalation
- family members who were told they had “type 1” or “type 2” without clear explanation

A multigenerational pattern should make MODY jump up the list.

### 2) Check islet autoantibodies
Negative GADA and IA-2A supported the diagnosis here. Ideally, a full autoimmune panel helps, including ZnT8 when available.

Negative antibodies don’t prove MODY, but they make autoimmune diabetes less likely.

### 3) Assess endogenous insulin secretion
This patient’s glucagon stimulation test showed a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, suggesting residual beta-cell function.

That’s not enough to rule out diabetes progression, but it argues against absolute insulin deficiency. In the right context, preserved C-peptide is a major clue.

### 4) Examine for insulin resistance
No acanthosis nigricans here. BMI was only mildly elevated. There wasn’t a strong insulin-resistant phenotype.

That doesn’t exclude type 2 diabetes, but it should keep MODY on the table.

### 5) Think about glycosuria
Glycosuria with relatively early or disproportionate hyperglycemia is a classic hint for HNF1A-MODY.

### 6) Don’t wait forever for genetics if the suspicion is high
Clinical suspicion should drive genetic testing. MODY probability calculators can help, but they’re imperfect and may be less reliable outside the populations they were derived from.

## Why the C-peptide matters — and why it doesn’t solve everything

C-peptide is useful, but it’s not a magic switch. This patient had preserved secretion, but still clearly insufficient beta-cell function. That’s exactly where MODY can live: not absent insulin, just progressively failing insulin production.

A key pitfall is assuming that any measurable C-peptide rules out type 1 diabetes, or that a young person with measurable C-peptide must have type 2 diabetes. Neither is true.

C-peptide is one piece of the puzzle:
- **Low/undetectable** → pushes you toward type 1 or advanced beta-cell failure
- **Preserved** in a young patient with dominant family history and negative antibodies → think MODY

## Treatment implications: this is why the diagnosis matters

HNF1A-MODY is often highly sensitive to sulfonylureas, because the main problem is defective insulin secretion rather than insulin resistance.

But this case is a good reminder that therapy should be individualized. The patient initially received basal insulin plus metformin and saxagliptin while the team was sorting out the diagnosis. After genetic confirmation and improvement in HbA1c, insulin was stopped and dulaglutide was used, with excellent glycemic response.

That’s the key point: **getting the diagnosis right opens the door to better, more targeted therapy.**

## A word on SGLT2 inhibitors

HNF1A-MODY is associated with glycosuria because of reduced renal glucose reabsorption. That makes SGLT2 inhibitors tempting at first glance — but this is exactly where physiology can mislead you.

The case report highlights concern that SGLT2 inhibitors may carry extra risk in HNF1A-MODY, including dehydration and ketoacidosis. In a patient who already spills glucose easily, that deserves caution.

## Take-home points

- **Think MODY in young-onset diabetes with a strong autosomal dominant family history.**
- **Negative islet autoantibodies + preserved C-peptide + no clear insulin resistance should prompt genetic testing.**
- **Glycosuria out of proportion to the hyperglycemia is a classic clue for HNF1A-MODY.**
- **MODY can be mistaken for both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, especially when BMI is borderline or metabolic features are present.**
- **A correct molecular diagnosis changes treatment, prognosis, and family counseling.**

## Bottom line

The “young type 2 diabetic” with a strong family history and negative antibodies may not have type 2 diabetes at all. This case is a reminder to slow down, look for the inheritance pattern, check endogenous insulin secretion, and let genetics confirm what the bedside clues are already whispering.

In the right patient, that one extra step can turn a lifelong misdiagnosis into precision medicine.



# Autoantibodies Negative, C-Peptide Present—Now What? Interpreting the Grey Zone in Early-Onset Diabetes

## The case that should make you pause

A young man walks into an annual health check with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. No DKA. BMI is not strikingly low, and he has symptoms that could fit almost any diabetes phenotype: thirst, polyphagia, weight loss. His HbA1c is 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and 2-hour OGTT glucose 19.8 mmol/L. The autoantibodies are negative. C-peptide is present, but not robust. Family history is the giveaway: diabetes in the maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and brother at 20.

This is the diagnostic grey zone where clinicians can get pulled in two directions:

- “Could still be early type 1 diabetes.”
- “Could be type 2 diabetes.”
- “Could this be MODY?”

In this case, genetic testing answered the question: a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant consistent with HNF1A-MODY. The practical lesson is not just about one mutation—it’s about how to think when the biomarkers are supportive, but not definitive.

## Why this is hard

Early-onset diabetes is messy. The classic teaching images are clean:

- Type 1 diabetes: autoantibodies positive, C-peptide low, ketosis risk
- Type 2 diabetes: insulin resistance, obesity, family history, metabolic syndrome
- MODY: young onset, autosomal dominant pattern, non-autoimmune, preserved endogenous insulin secretion

Real patients often blur those lines.

This patient had some features that pointed away from type 1 diabetes:

- No diabetic ketoacidosis
- Negative GAD and IA-2 antibodies
- Preserved stimulated C-peptide
- Strong multigenerational family history

But there were also reasons not to dismiss type 2 diabetes too quickly:

- BMI 24.6 kg/m²
- Hypertriglyceridaemia
- Initial presentation severe enough to prompt insulin

That combination is exactly why MODY gets missed.

## Interpreting C-peptide without overcalling it

C-peptide is useful because it reflects endogenous insulin secretion, but it is not a binary test. In this case, the glucagon stimulation test showed:

- C-peptide 0.39 nmol/L at baseline
- C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes

That matters because stimulated C-peptide above roughly 0.60 nmol/L generally suggests some preserved beta-cell function. Here, the result supported ongoing insulin production, but not normal beta-cell reserve.

A few clinical points:

- A detectable C-peptide argues against absolute insulin deficiency.
- A “normal-ish” C-peptide does not exclude early type 1 diabetes.
- A low or borderline stimulated value does not prove type 1 diabetes either.
- C-peptide is best used as one piece of the puzzle, not the final answer.

In this patient, the C-peptide result fit with a monogenic beta-cell disorder: insulin production was present, but insufficient for the degree of hyperglycaemia.

## What do negative autoantibodies really mean?

Negative GAD and IA-2 antibodies are helpful, but they are not a universal “rule-out” for type 1 diabetes. Autoantibody-negative diabetes exists, and antibody testing is not perfectly sensitive—especially if the autoimmune process is incomplete, evolving, or if the panel is limited.

That said, in the right clinical context, negative antibodies should raise your suspicion for MODY, especially when paired with:

- Early onset
- Family history across generations
- Preserved C-peptide
- Absence of ketoacidosis
- Lack of obvious insulin resistance phenotype

Key point: negative antibodies lower the probability of type 1 diabetes, but they do not close the case.

## Why phenotype and trajectory matter as much as labs

The diagnosis of MODY is not made by a single lab result. It’s a pattern recognition exercise.

This case had several classic HNF1A clues:

- Diabetes in successive generations
- Young age at diagnosis
- Glycosuria
- No DKA
- No acanthosis nigricans
- Preserved insulin secretion
- Rapid response to glucose-lowering therapy

HNF1A-MODY is particularly important because it often masquerades as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, yet treatment and counseling are different. Glycosuria is not just a random finding here—it fits the biology. HNF1A regulates renal glucose handling, and reduced renal threshold for glucose can produce glycosuria even when the rest of the presentation looks “non-specific.”

## When should you push for genetic testing?

If you see early-onset diabetes and the story does not fit cleanly, ask:

- Is there a multigenerational family history?
- Is the patient autoantibody negative?
- Is there preserved C-peptide?
- Is there no clear insulin resistance phenotype?
- Is there glycosuria out of proportion to the clinical picture?
- Has the patient been labeled “type 1” or “type 2” but not behaved like either?

The Exeter MODY probability calculator can help triage who should be tested, but it is not perfect and was derived from White European populations. In non-White populations, it may misestimate risk. Clinical suspicion still matters.

In this case, genetic testing identified a novel HNF1A variant, c.809A>G (p.N270S), supporting the diagnosis and changing management.

## Why the diagnosis changes treatment

This is where the payoff is huge.

At presentation, the patient was treated like type 2 diabetes: basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin. That was reasonable while the diagnosis was still uncertain and HbA1c was markedly elevated.

Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed, insulin was stopped and dulaglutide was started. HbA1c improved from 10.2% to 6.0%. He was later maintained on metformin and saxagliptin, with HbA1c remaining below 6.5%.

That clinical response reinforces an important principle: monogenic diabetes is not just a label—it can directly alter therapy.

A few practical treatment pearls from this case:

- HNF1A-MODY often responds well to sulfonylureas, but alternatives may be appropriate.
- GLP-1 receptor agonists can be effective and may reduce hypoglycaemia risk as beta-cell function declines.
- SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution in HNF1A-MODY because glycosuria is already part of the phenotype and dehydration/DKA risk may be higher.
- If the diagnosis is uncertain at presentation, temporary insulin is fine; the key is to revisit the diagnosis rather than anchoring on it.

## Clinical take-home from the grey zone

When antibodies are negative but C-peptide is present, don’t default to “type 2 until proven otherwise.” That mental shortcut misses a meaningful number of MODY cases.

The winning strategy is to integrate:

- phenotype
- family history
- glycaemic trajectory
- autoantibodies
- endogenous insulin secretion
- response to therapy

That combination often gets you closer than any single test.

## Bottom line

- Negative GAD and IA-2 antibodies do not rule out diabetes that is immune-mediated, and they do not rule in MODY.
- Preserved C-peptide argues against absolute insulin deficiency but does not exclude early type 1 diabetes.
- In young patients, a strong autosomal-dominant family history should trigger consideration of MODY.
- HNF1A-MODY can present with glycosuria, marked hyperglycaemia, and no ketoacidosis.
- Genetic testing is the tie-breaker when the phenotype and biomarkers live in the grey zone.
- Getting the diagnosis right changes treatment, prognosis, and family counseling.



# Precision Diabetes in Action: How a Genetic Diagnosis Changed Therapy

## The punchline

A young man showed up with very real diabetes: HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2-hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L, glycosuria, polyuria/polydipsia, and weight loss. On first pass, treating him like type 2 diabetes was reasonable. But the family history was the clue that should make you stop and ask, “Is this actually MODY?”

That question mattered. Once genetic testing confirmed a pathogenic HNF1A variant, the treatment plan changed. Insulin was stopped, and therapy shifted toward an incretin-based strategy, with excellent glycaemic response: HbA1c fell to 6.0% over 6 months.

This is precision diabetes medicine in real life: the subtype changes the drug, the risk profile, the counseling, and the long-term plan.

## Why this case is tricky

HNF1A-MODY can look like both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, which is why it gets missed.

This patient had:

- Young-onset diabetes
- Strong autosomal-dominant family history across generations
- Negative GADA and IA-2 antibodies
- No ketoacidosis
- Preserved, but not robust, C-peptide response
- No acanthosis nigricans
- Glycosuria

That combination should make MODY rise on the differential. But his BMI was 24.6 kg/m² and his triglycerides were elevated, so a provisional type 2 diabetes framework wasn’t absurd either. In other words: the case lived in the diagnostic gray zone where many patients get labeled incorrectly and left there.

## What HNF1A-MODY is doing biologically

HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in the pancreas, liver, and kidney. When it’s mutated, β-cell function progressively declines. The phenotype is not just “mild diabetes” — it’s a defect in insulin secretion, often with a characteristic tendency toward glycosuria because renal glucose handling is altered as well.

A few bedside implications follow from that biology:

- These patients may not be insulin resistant in the classic type 2 sense.
- They may have residual endogenous insulin production, especially earlier in the disease.
- They often respond very well to medications that leverage remaining β-cell function.
- Glycosuria can be a clue, not just a lab curiosity.

## Why initial treatment as type 2 diabetes made sense

At presentation, this patient had marked hyperglycaemia. Before the genetic diagnosis came back, he was started on:

- Basal insulin degludec
- Metformin
- Saxagliptin

That’s defensible. With an HbA1c of 10.2% and symptomatic hyperglycaemia, immediate treatment was needed. Insulin is often the fastest way to get glucose under control while the diagnostic workup is unfolding.

The key is to remember that “initially treat like type 2” does not mean “forever treat like type 2.” If the story later points to MODY, the regimen should evolve.

## The diagnostic pivot: why MODY became likely

Several features pushed this case away from classic type 1 or type 2 diabetes:

- Strong family history in successive generations
- Negative islet autoantibodies
- No DKA
- Preserved C-peptide on glucagon stimulation
- Lack of acanthosis nigricans
- Early onset

The glucagon stimulation test showed a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, which supports residual endogenous insulin secretion. That’s not definitive on its own, but in context it’s helpful.

The family history was especially important here:

- Maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s
- Mother in her late 20s
- Younger brother at 20

That pattern is hard to dismiss. This is the sort of pedigree that should trigger a low threshold for genetic testing.

## Genetic testing changed the game

Next-generation sequencing identified a heterozygous HNF1A variant: p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), reported as pathogenic in the case report and not previously listed in major variant databases.

That result matters for more than taxonomy. Once you know the diabetes subtype, you can make better treatment decisions:

- You can avoid reflexively escalating insulin
- You can choose therapies aligned with residual β-cell function
- You can counsel family members
- You can plan surveillance more appropriately

This is one of the best arguments for MODY testing: it’s not academic. It changes what you do.

## Why insulin came off the table

After genetic confirmation and with some improvement in glycaemia, insulin was discontinued. The rationale is straightforward:

- HNF1A-MODY is not primarily an insulin-deficient autoimmune state like type 1 diabetes.
- Many patients retain enough endogenous insulin secretion to come off insulin.
- Over-treatment with insulin can create unnecessary hypoglycaemia burden and treatment complexity.

This patient’s β-cell function was reduced, but not absent. That makes a non-insulin regimen plausible once acute glucotoxicity is addressed.

## Why incretin-based therapy fits here

He was switched to dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, and later maintained on metformin plus saxagliptin.

Why is that attractive in HNF1A-MODY?

GLP-1 receptor agonists can:

- Improve glucose-dependent insulin secretion
- Reduce postprandial excursions
- Lower HbA1c without the same hypoglycaemia risk as some other secretagogues
- Help preserve a simpler regimen when β-cell function is declining

This matters because classic first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY has often been sulfonylureas. Those can work very well, but they also come with a real hypoglycaemia risk, especially as diabetes progresses or if meals are irregular.

In this case, incretin-based therapy was a reasonable precision choice, and it worked.

## A note on sulfonylureas

If you’re thinking, “Wait — aren’t sulfonylureas the classic treatment for HNF1A-MODY?” yes, absolutely. They are often highly effective because the problem is impaired insulin secretion, not absent insulin sensitivity alone.

But this case shows why treatment is not one-size-fits-all:

- Some patients may do better with GLP-1 receptor agonists
- Hypoglycaemia risk can matter a lot
- The optimal regimen may change over time
- Treatment should be individualized to residual β-cell function and patient context

So the teaching point is not “ignore sulfonylureas.” It’s “know why they work, and know when other options may be preferable.”

## Why SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution here

This patient had glycosuria, which fits HNF1A-MODY biology. That’s also why SGLT2 inhibitors can be a little uncomfortable in this population.

The concern is that HNF1A-related disease may already involve reduced renal glucose reabsorption, so further pharmacologic inhibition could increase the risk of:

- Dehydration
- Genital infections
- Volume depletion
- Potential ketoacidosis risk in susceptible patients

The case report appropriately raises caution here. It’s not a blanket contraindication in every patient, but it is a reminder to think before defaulting to an SGLT2 inhibitor just because a person has diabetes.

## What the lipid panel and BMI do — and don’t — tell you

This patient had triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L and HDL of 1.01 mmol/L, which can point clinicians toward metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes. But that signal is imperfect.

As obesity and dyslipidaemia become more common in the general population, BMI and metabolic features become less useful as strict separators between MODY and type 2 diabetes. A normal-ish BMI does not rule in MODY, and a higher BMI does not rule it out.

The lesson: don’t let one “type 2-ish” feature blind you to the pedigree.

## Clinical pearls

- Young-onset diabetes plus a multigenerational family history should prompt you to think beyond type 1 and type 2.
- Negative islet autoantibodies and preserved C-peptide make MODY more likely, especially when DKA is absent.
- Glycosuria can be a clue in HNF1A-MODY, not just an incidental finding.
- Genetic diagnosis is actionable: it can justify stopping insulin and choosing a more targeted therapy.
- GLP-1 receptor agonists are a reasonable option in selected HNF1A-MODY patients, particularly when hypoglycaemia avoidance and treatment simplicity matter.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY because the renal glycosuria phenotype may amplify adverse effects.

## Take-home points

- **MODY is a precision diagnosis, not a label of convenience.**
- **HNF1A-MODY often masquerades as type 1 or type 2 diabetes early on.**
- **A strong family history, negative antibodies, and preserved C-peptide should trigger genetic testing.**
- **Confirming HNF1A-MODY can change therapy from empiric insulin to a more targeted non-insulin regimen.**
- **Incretin-based therapy may be a useful alternative or adjunct, especially when hypoglycaemia risk is a concern.**

## Bottom line

The big lesson from this case is not just that a rare mutation was found. It’s that the mutation changed care. In the right patient, genetic testing doesn’t merely explain diabetes — it rewrites the treatment plan.

That’s what precision medicine is supposed to do.



# Beyond Sulfonylureas: Are GLP-1 Receptor Agonists Underused in HNF1A-MODY?

## The case that makes you pause

A young man with hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, no ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes turned out not to have type 1 or typical type 2 diabetes — he had **HNF1A-MODY** from a **novel heterozygous HNF1A variant**. What makes this case especially interesting is not just the diagnosis, but the treatment course: after initial insulin, he was transitioned off insulin and ultimately did very well on a **GLP-1 receptor agonist plus metformin**, with HbA1c falling from **10.2% to 6.0%** and no reported hypoglycaemia.

That’s a useful reminder that while sulfonylureas are the classic first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY, they may not be the only practical option.

## Why HNF1A-MODY is easy to miss

HNF1A-MODY sits in the diagnostic gray zone between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. These patients often present young, may not be obese, and frequently have a strong autosomal dominant family history. But the phenotype can drift over time: later disease may look “type 2-ish,” and some patients have enough hyperglycaemia that they’re initially treated as type 1 or type 2 without anyone thinking about monogenic diabetes.

In this case, several clues lined up:

- Young age at presentation
- Family history across multiple generations
- Marked post-load hyperglycaemia on OGTT
- Glycosuria
- Negative GADA and IA-2 antibodies
- Preserved but insufficient C-peptide response
- No DKA

That’s the setup where MODY should be on the differential, and genetic testing becomes the key move.

## The treatment tradition: sulfonylureas first

For HNF1A-MODY, sulfonylureas have long been the standard because these patients often remain quite sensitive to insulin secretagogues. The biology makes sense: HNF1A mutations impair beta-cell function and insulin secretion, so “pushing” the beta cell with a sulfonylurea can be very effective.

But traditional doesn’t always mean optimal for every patient.

The problem with sulfonylureas is familiar:

- Hypoglycaemia risk
- Weight gain
- Variable durability as beta-cell function declines
- Tolerability issues in real-world use

So the question is not whether sulfonylureas work — they do — but whether they’re always the best option.

## Why a GLP-1 receptor agonist is biologically appealing

GLP-1 receptor agonists make a lot of physiologic sense in HNF1A-MODY. They enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, slow gastric emptying, and reduce postprandial glucose excursions. That glucose-dependent mechanism is a major advantage when you’re worried about hypoglycaemia.

In HNF1A-MODY, where beta-cell reserve is reduced but not absent, a GLP-1 receptor agonist may help “amplify” the remaining insulin secretory capacity without the same hypoglycaemia burden as a sulfonylurea.

This case fits that logic. The patient had:

- Some preserved C-peptide response, but not robust secretion
- A need for rapid glycaemic improvement
- A family history suggesting inherited beta-cell dysfunction
- A clinical course where insulin could be stopped once control improved

After genetic confirmation, the team moved from insulin to **dulaglutide**, and HbA1c improved to 6.0% over 6 months.

## What data do we actually have?

This is where the evidence base gets thin.

There is at least one small randomized, double-blind, crossover trial in HNF1A-MODY suggesting that **liraglutide and glimepiride both improve fasting and postprandial glucose**, with no major efficacy difference in that study. The interesting part is safety: GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy had **less mild hypoglycaemia** than glimepiride.

That matters clinically. In a disease where long-term beta-cell decline is expected, a therapy with a lower hypoglycaemia risk profile may be especially attractive.

Still, the data are limited:
- Small studies
- Short follow-up
- Few direct comparisons
- Limited diversity in patient populations

So we’re not talking about a wholesale replacement of sulfonylureas. We’re talking about a reasonable alternative in selected patients.

## Where GLP-1 receptor agonists may fit best

This case highlights a few situations where a GLP-1 receptor agonist may deserve serious consideration:

- **Declining beta-cell reserve** but not absolute insulin dependence
- **Hypoglycaemia risk** or concern about sulfonylurea tolerance
- **Weight or cardiometabolic considerations**
- **Need for flexible therapy** while awaiting genetic confirmation
- **Patient preference** for a non-insulin regimen

That last point matters more than we sometimes admit. In a chronic inherited condition, adherence and acceptability are part of the treatment decision, not afterthoughts.

## A note on metformin and DPP-4 inhibitors

This patient also received metformin and later saxagliptin alongside the GLP-1 receptor agonist. That’s a pragmatic real-world regimen, but the key teaching point is not that every HNF1A-MODY patient needs the same combination. It’s that once the diagnosis is known, treatment can be individualized rather than reflexively defaulting to “type 2” or “type 1” algorithms.

The diagnosis changes the frame:
- Less uncertainty
- Better matching of therapy to pathophysiology
- Better family counseling
- Less unnecessary insulin exposure

## Don’t forget the diagnostic pearl: glycosuria is a clue

One of the classic bedside clues in HNF1A-MODY is **glycosuria out of proportion to plasma glucose**, related to a lower renal threshold for glucose. That’s not pathognomonic, but in the right context it should raise your suspicion.

In this case, glycosuria plus early-onset familial diabetes plus negative antibodies should have made MODY high on the list even before genetic testing returned.

## What about SGLT2 inhibitors?

This is worth mentioning because HNF1A affects renal glucose handling. The case report highlights concern that SGLT2 inhibitors may be problematic in HNF1A-MODY, with theoretical and reported risks including dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis. That doesn’t mean they are universally forbidden, but they deserve caution and a strong rationale if considered.

## Take-home points

- **HNF1A-MODY should be suspected** in young patients with diabetes, strong autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide.
- **Sulfonylureas remain classic first-line therapy**, but they are not the only effective option.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists are biologically plausible and clinically promising** in HNF1A-MODY, especially when hypoglycaemia risk or beta-cell decline makes sulfonylureas less attractive.
- The evidence base is still **small**, but available data and this case suggest GLP-1 receptor agonists can achieve excellent glycaemic control.
- **Genetic confirmation matters** because it changes treatment, prognosis, and family screening.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that monogenic diabetes is not just about getting the label right — it’s about using the label to choose better therapy. Sulfonylureas are still the classic answer for HNF1A-MODY, but GLP-1 receptor agonists may be an underused alternative, especially when beta-cell reserve is waning and hypoglycaemia avoidance matters.

In other words: once you’ve made the diagnosis, don’t stop at the old playbook.



# Glycosuria Without SGLT2 Inhibitors: The Renal Clue to HNF1A-MODY—and Why It Matters

## The “diabetes” clue that shouldn’t be ignored

A young man shows up for a routine health exam and is found to have hyperglycemia and glycosuria. No DKA. No obesity-driven insulin resistance picture. No autoimmune markers. Instead, the family history is doing the loudest talking: diabetes in his mother in her late 20s, grandmother in her 30s, and younger brother at 20.

That combination should make you pause.

Because glycosuria in a young patient with diabetes is not just a random urinalysis finding. In HNF1A-MODY, it can be a physiologic clue—one that reflects an altered renal glucose threshold, not simply a high plasma glucose burden. In this case, the clue helped point toward a diagnosis that changed treatment, changed counseling, and may change how we think about a drug class that is often assumed to be “helpful” in nearly all diabetes phenotypes: SGLT2 inhibitors.

## Why HNF1A-MODY causes glycosuria

HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in multiple tissues, including the pancreas and kidney. In HNF1A-MODY, the β-cell problem is progressive insulin secretory failure, but there’s also a renal phenotype: reduced expression of SGLT2 in the proximal tubule lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption.

That means these patients can spill glucose into the urine at lower serum glucose levels than you’d expect in typical type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

Clinically, that matters because glycosuria may appear early and disproportionately. It may be one of the earliest “non-obvious” signs that the diabetes is not fitting the usual bins. In this case, glycosuria was detected on screening before the full phenotype was obvious.

The broader lesson: if you see glycosuria in a young person with diabetes, don’t reflexively assume it’s just “uncontrolled diabetes.” Ask why the kidney is letting glucose go.

## This is not type 1, and not classic type 2

The diagnosis here came together the way MODY often does: pattern recognition plus confirmation.

Features pointing away from type 1 diabetes:

- No diabetic ketoacidosis
- Negative GADA and IA-2A
- Preserved C-peptide on glucagon stimulation, even if not fully normal

Features pointing away from straightforward type 2 diabetes:

- Young age
- Strong autosomal-dominant-looking family history
- No acanthosis nigricans
- Glycosuria out of proportion to the usual T2D story

And then the genetic test sealed it: a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant, p.N270S (c.809A>G), consistent with HNF1A-MODY.

This is exactly the kind of patient who gets mislabeled early on. The initial treatment was essentially a “young person with diabetes, probably type 2” approach—basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin—until the genetic result clarified the underlying biology.

## Why the renal clue matters therapeutically

Here’s the practical issue: if HNF1A-MODY already lowers the renal threshold for glucose, what happens if you add an SGLT2 inhibitor?

You’re pharmacologically intensifying a pathway that may already be relatively underactive or dysregulated. That raises concern for:

- More glycosuria than intended
- Volume depletion/dehydration
- Genital mycotic infections
- Ketoacidosis risk, including euglycemic DKA

That doesn’t mean SGLT2 inhibitors are absolutely contraindicated in every patient with HNF1A-MODY, but the physiology should make you cautious. In a patient whose baseline renal handling of glucose is already abnormal, the “benefit” of an SGLT2 inhibitor may come with a larger-than-usual downside.

This is why the diagnosis matters. HNF1A-MODY is not just a label—it changes the therapeutic map.

## Treatment: think genetics, not habit

Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed, insulin was stopped and the patient was transitioned to dulaglutide. Glycemic control improved substantially, with HbA1c falling from 10.2% to 6.0%, and he remained controlled on metformin plus saxagliptin.

That outcome reinforces a few important points:

- HNF1A-MODY is often highly medication-responsive when you match the drug to the defect
- GLP-1 receptor agonists can be a useful option, especially when β-cell function is declining and hypoglycemia avoidance matters
- The right diagnosis can allow de-escalation from insulin, not just escalation of therapy

The classic teaching is that HNF1A-MODY is sulfonylurea-sensitive. That remains true, but this case underscores that GLP-1 receptor agonists can be effective and may offer a safer path in some patients.

## Clinical pearl: glycosuria is not always “too much glucose”

In routine practice, glycosuria often gets mentally filed under “diabetes severity marker.” But in HNF1A-MODY, it can be a phenotype clue.

Why that matters:

- It can help you suspect MODY earlier
- It may support genetic testing in the right clinical context
- It should make you think twice before prescribing an SGLT2 inhibitor without considering the underlying diagnosis

## When should you think about MODY?

In a young person with diabetes, consider HNF1A-MODY when you see:

- Early-onset diabetes in multiple generations
- Negative type 1 diabetes autoantibodies
- Preserved C-peptide
- No marked obesity or insulin resistance phenotype
- Glycosuria, especially if it seems disproportionate
- A course that doesn’t cleanly fit type 1 or type 2 diabetes

The big trap is assuming that “young diabetes” automatically means type 1, or that any patient with a BMI above ideal must have type 2. This case had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m²—enough to muddy the waters, but not enough to explain away the family history and biochemical pattern.

## Take-home points

- **Glycosuria can be an early clue to HNF1A-MODY**, not just a nonspecific diabetes finding.
- **HNF1A mutations lower the renal threshold for glucose** via reduced SGLT2 expression in the proximal tubule.
- **Think MODY** in young patients with diabetes, strong family history, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide.
- **Genetic diagnosis changes treatment** and can allow safer, more targeted therapy.
- **Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY** because of potential risks: dehydration, genital infections, and ketoacidosis.
- **The kidney can help you diagnose the pancreas.** Sometimes the urine tells the story before the blood work does.

## Bottom line

In this case, glycosuria was more than a lab abnormality—it was a physiologic breadcrumb leading to the correct diagnosis. HNF1A-MODY should be on the differential when glycosuria appears early in a young patient with diabetes, especially when the family history fits and autoimmune testing does not. Getting the diagnosis right matters because it changes both the choice of therapy and the risk profile of drugs we might otherwise prescribe reflexively.



# A Novel Variant, an Old Problem: What This Case Teaches About NGS and Variant Interpretation in MODY

## Why this case is worth your attention

A young man shows up with marked hyperglycemia, glycosuria, no DKA, a strong family history of early-onset diabetes, negative type 1 diabetes antibodies, and just enough preserved C-peptide to make you hesitate. That already sounds like possible MODY.

But the real teaching point here is not just “recognize MODY.” It’s what happened next: next-generation sequencing found a **previously unreported HNF1A variant**—and the team had to decide whether this novel change was actually disease-causing, or just an interesting genetic footnote.

That is the modern MODY problem. We are no longer asking only *“Is this monogenic diabetes?”* We are also asking *“How do I interpret a variant that has never been described before?”*

## MODY is still underdiagnosed because our screening net is too narrow

MODY gets missed for a lot of predictable reasons:

- It can look like type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, or neither
- Patients may not be obese, but they also may not be “classically lean”
- Family history may be present, but often gets under-recognized or incomplete
- Autoantibodies may be negative, but that’s not enough by itself
- C-peptide may still be detectable, especially early on
- Many clinicians still reserve genetic testing for a very stereotyped patient

This case had several clues that should raise the MODY flag:

- Diagnosis in young adulthood
- Multigenerational early-onset diabetes
- Negative GADA and IA-2A
- Glycosuria
- No ketoacidosis
- Preserved but insufficient endogenous insulin secretion

That’s the phenotype. The genomic question is what to do with it.

## Why NGS matters here

Historically, MODY testing was expensive and sequential: test the “most likely” gene first, then move down the list. That strategy made sense when only a few genes were known and when testing was hard to access.

Now, with **NGS panels**, clinicians can screen multiple MODY-associated genes at once. That matters because:

- Over 15 genes can produce a MODY phenotype
- The phenotype overlaps across gene subtypes
- Clinical diagnosis alone is imperfect
- Treatment can change depending on the gene involved

In this case, NGS identified a heterozygous **HNF1A:c.809A>G (p.N270S)** variant in exon 4. It was not reported in the literature and not found in common variant databases like ClinVar or VarSome. That sounds exciting, but novelty alone does not equal pathogenicity.

## The key question: how do you classify a novel variant?

When a variant has never been reported, you cannot stop at “new mutation found.” You need to build an argument.

That’s where the **ACMG framework** comes in. Variant interpretation should synthesize:

- **Population data**: Is the variant absent or extremely rare in controls?
- **Computational data**: Do in silico tools suggest functional impact?
- **Segregation data**: Does it track with disease in the family?
- **Phenotype match**: Does the patient’s clinical picture fit the gene?
- **Location/domain context**: Is the variant in a critical functional region?
- **Prior evidence**: Are nearby residues known to be important?

In this case, several lines of evidence pointed in the same direction:

### 1) The phenotype fit HNF1A-MODY
HNF1A-MODY classically presents with:

- Early-onset diabetes
- Progressive β-cell dysfunction
- Family history in successive generations
- Often negative autoantibodies
- Glycosuria can be a clue because HNF1A regulates renal glucose handling

This patient fit that pattern well enough that genetic testing made sense even before the result returned.

### 2) The variant segregated with diabetes in the family
The report notes diabetes in the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother. Segregation is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in rare disease genetics: if the variant travels with disease across affected relatives, that pushes interpretation toward pathogenicity.

### 3) The variant sits in the DNA-binding domain
HNF1A is a transcription factor, and domain location matters. Variants in functionally important domains—especially the **DNA-binding domain**—are often more likely to disrupt protein function than changes in less critical regions.

### 4) Computational prediction supported deleteriousness
The variant had a high **AlphaMissense score (0.995)**, supporting a damaging effect. Computational tools are not definitive, but they can add weight when they align with the phenotype and segregation data.

### 5) The residue is adjacent to known pathogenic positions
A change near established pathogenic residues is not proof, but it can strengthen the biological plausibility that the region is functionally important.

Put together, this is exactly how a novel variant becomes clinically meaningful: not because a database says so, but because multiple independent lines of evidence converge.

## A practical way to think about a novel HNF1A variant

When you see a previously unreported variant in a diabetes gene, ask:

- Does the phenotype fit the gene?
- Is there autosomal dominant transmission?
- Are islet autoantibodies negative?
- Is C-peptide preserved enough to argue against absolute insulin deficiency?
- Is the variant rare or absent from population databases?
- Is the variant in a known functional domain?
- Do in silico tools support deleteriousness?
- Does it segregate with disease in the family?

If several of those boxes are checked, the variant may be clinically actionable even if it is “novel.”

## Why this matters therapeutically

This isn’t just an academic genetics exercise. The diagnosis changes management.

HNF1A-MODY is often very sensitive to insulin secretagogues, especially sulfonylureas. In this case, the team initially treated him as possible type 2 diabetes with basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin while awaiting genetic confirmation. Once HNF1A-MODY was established, insulin was stopped and therapy was shifted to **dulaglutide**, with excellent glycemic response.

His HbA1c fell from **10.2% to 6.0%**, and later remained below 6.5% on simplified therapy.

The lesson: a molecular diagnosis can spare unnecessary insulin, guide drug selection, and help anticipate progression.

## Don’t over-rely on a “perfect” MODY phenotype

This patient was not the textbook MODY poster child.

He had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and some metabolic features, including elevated triglycerides. That could easily distract clinicians toward type 2 diabetes. And that’s the trap: MODY is often taught as lean, young, non-ketotic, autosomal dominant diabetes—but real patients often blur the edges.

As obesity and metabolic syndrome become more common globally, phenotype-based screening gets less reliable. That is one reason MODY remains underdiagnosed.

## Clinical pearls from this case

- **Young-onset diabetes + strong family history + negative autoantibodies should trigger MODY thinking.**
- **Preserved C-peptide does not rule out diabetes; it helps distinguish monogenic diabetes from classic type 1 diabetes.**
- **Glycosuria can be a clue in HNF1A-MODY because of reduced renal glucose reabsorption.**
- **NGS panels are increasingly useful because MODY is genetically heterogeneous.**
- **A novel variant is not automatically pathogenic; interpretation requires phenotype correlation, segregation, domain context, and ACMG-based evidence.**
- **Genetic diagnosis can change therapy, not just labeling.**

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that the hardest part of MODY is often not finding a gene variant—it’s deciding what that variant means.

NGS has made it easier to detect rare and previously unreported HNF1A variants, but interpretation still requires disciplined clinical genetics: phenotype matching, segregation, functional plausibility, and ACMG-guided classification. The result is a more precise diagnosis and, in this case, a better treatment plan.

## Take-home points

- MODY is still missed because we test too selectively and rely on an imperfect clinical stereotype.
- NGS is increasingly central in early-onset atypical diabetes because it can identify actionable monogenic causes.
- A novel HNF1A variant should be interpreted using the full package: phenotype, family segregation, domain location, rarity, and computational evidence.
- Database absence does not mean benign; clinical context matters.
- In suspected HNF1A-MODY, making the diagnosis can directly improve management and reduce unnecessary insulin use.
