Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with new-onset diabetes, which clinical features in this case most strongly suggest MODY rather than type 1 or typical type 2 diabetes?

The features in this case that most strongly point to MODY, specifically HNF1A-MODY, are the combination of **young onset, autosomal dominant family clustering, preserved endogenous insulin secretion, and absence of autoimmune markers**.

In this patient, several clues stand out:

- **Early-onset diabetes in multiple successive generations**: the maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at 20 years old. That vertical transmission is classic for an autosomal dominant disorder like MODY.
- **No diabetic ketoacidosis and negative islet autoantibodies**: GADA and IA-2A were negative, making classic type 1 diabetes much less likely.
- **Residual β-cell function despite marked hyperglycemia**: the glucagon-stimulated C-peptide was still measurable, which argues against absolute insulin deficiency and supports non–type 1 diabetes.
- **Phenotype not strongly insulin-resistant**: he was not obese, had no acanthosis nigricans, and had a BMI in the normal-to-overweight range rather than the typical phenotype of type 2 diabetes.
- **Glycosuria with hyperglycemia**: in HNF1A-MODY, renal glucose reabsorption is impaired, so glycosuria can be a particularly helpful clue.

So, the overall pattern is **young adult onset, strong family history across generations, antibody-negative diabetes, preserved C-peptide, and glycosuria without ketoacidosis**—a constellation much more consistent with MODY than with either type 1 or typical type 2 diabetes.
