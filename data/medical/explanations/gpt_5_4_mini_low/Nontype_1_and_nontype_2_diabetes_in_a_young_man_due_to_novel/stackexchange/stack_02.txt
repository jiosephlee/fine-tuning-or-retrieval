Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Morning Report] How do negative islet autoantibodies and a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon help refine the differential diagnosis in this patient?

The negative islet autoantibodies and the stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L shift the differential away from classic type 1 diabetes and toward a form of nonautoimmune, non–insulinopenic diabetes such as MODY.

First, the absence of GAD and IA-2 antibodies argues against autoimmune β-cell destruction. In a young patient with hyperglycemia, a positive antibody profile would support type 1 diabetes or latent autoimmune diabetes; here, the seronegativity makes those diagnoses less likely, especially in the absence of diabetic ketoacidosis.

Second, the glucagon-stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L shows that the pancreas still has meaningful endogenous insulin secretion. Although this is not normal and indicates impaired β-cell function, it is above the commonly used threshold of about 0.6 nmol/L that suggests preserved insulin production. That pattern is more consistent with MODY—particularly HNF1A-MODY, which causes progressive but incomplete β-cell dysfunction—than with established type 1 diabetes, where C-peptide is typically very low or absent.

So, in this case, the combination of autoantibody negativity, residual stimulated C-peptide, young age, and a strong multigenerational family history makes MODY the leading diagnosis and justifies genetic testing, which ultimately confirmed HNF1A-MODY.
