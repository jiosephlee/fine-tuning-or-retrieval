Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient with early-onset diabetes has a BMI in the overweight range and hypertriglyceridaemia, why should MODY still remain in the differential diagnosis?

Because **BMI and triglycerides are not specific for type 2 diabetes**, and their presence does **not rule out MODY**.

In this case, the features that should keep **HNF1A-MODY** on the table were more persuasive than the metabolic features that pointed toward type 2 diabetes: **young age at diagnosis, a strong autosomal dominant family history of early-onset diabetes, absence of diabetic ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved but inadequate C-peptide secretion**. Those are classic clues to a **monogenic β-cell disorder**.

The reason the BMI and triglycerides can mislead is that **MODY and type 2 diabetes can overlap clinically**. As obesity and metabolic syndrome become more common in the general population, a patient with MODY may also be overweight or have hypertriglyceridaemia simply by coincidence or due to concomitant insulin resistance. The case report explicitly notes that **BMI and metabolic syndrome are less useful discriminators than they once were**, because many MODY patients no longer fit the stereotype of being lean.

So the teaching point is: **overweight status does not exclude MODY**. In a young patient with early-onset diabetes, especially with a strong family history and negative autoantibodies, you should still think of MODY even if the phenotype looks “type 2–like.” Genetic testing is what establishes the diagnosis and can completely change treatment.
