Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with new-onset diabetes, which clinical features in this case most strongly suggest MODY rather than type 1 or typical type 2 diabetes?

The features in this case that most strongly point to MODY, specifically HNF1A-MODY, are the combination of **young onset, autosomal dominant family clustering, preserved endogenous insulin secretion, and absence of autoimmune markers**.

In this patient, several clues stand out:

- **Early-onset diabetes in multiple successive generations**: the maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at 20 years old. That vertical transmission is classic for an autosomal dominant disorder like MODY.
- **No diabetic ketoacidosis and negative islet autoantibodies**: GADA and IA-2A were negative, making classic type 1 diabetes much less likely.
- **Residual β-cell function despite marked hyperglycemia**: the glucagon-stimulated C-peptide was still measurable, which argues against absolute insulin deficiency and supports non–type 1 diabetes.
- **Phenotype not strongly insulin-resistant**: he was not obese, had no acanthosis nigricans, and had a BMI in the normal-to-overweight range rather than the typical phenotype of type 2 diabetes.
- **Glycosuria with hyperglycemia**: in HNF1A-MODY, renal glucose reabsorption is impaired, so glycosuria can be a particularly helpful clue.

So, the overall pattern is **young adult onset, strong family history across generations, antibody-negative diabetes, preserved C-peptide, and glycosuria without ketoacidosis**—a constellation much more consistent with MODY than with either type 1 or typical type 2 diabetes.

### [Morning Report] How do negative islet autoantibodies and a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon help refine the differential diagnosis in this patient?

The negative islet autoantibodies and the stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L shift the differential away from classic type 1 diabetes and toward a form of nonautoimmune, non–insulinopenic diabetes such as MODY.

First, the absence of GAD and IA-2 antibodies argues against autoimmune β-cell destruction. In a young patient with hyperglycemia, a positive antibody profile would support type 1 diabetes or latent autoimmune diabetes; here, the seronegativity makes those diagnoses less likely, especially in the absence of diabetic ketoacidosis.

Second, the glucagon-stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L shows that the pancreas still has meaningful endogenous insulin secretion. Although this is not normal and indicates impaired β-cell function, it is above the commonly used threshold of about 0.6 nmol/L that suggests preserved insulin production. That pattern is more consistent with MODY—particularly HNF1A-MODY, which causes progressive but incomplete β-cell dysfunction—than with established type 1 diabetes, where C-peptide is typically very low or absent.

So, in this case, the combination of autoantibody negativity, residual stimulated C-peptide, young age, and a strong multigenerational family history makes MODY the leading diagnosis and justifies genetic testing, which ultimately confirmed HNF1A-MODY.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the pathophysiologic role of HNF1A in pancreatic beta-cell function, and how do HNF1A mutations lead to progressive hyperglycaemia?

HNF1A encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha, a transcription factor that is highly expressed in pancreatic β cells and is essential for normal β-cell gene expression and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. In the pancreas, HNF1A regulates transcription of key genes involved in sensing and responding to glucose, including **insulin itself**, **GLUT2**, and enzymes such as **pyruvate kinase**. It also contributes to the maintenance of β-cell differentiation and functional reserve.

When HNF1A is mutated, as in this case of autosomal dominant HNF1A-MODY, the β cell does not fail abruptly; rather, it undergoes a **progressive decline in function**. Early on, patients may still have measurable C-peptide and no ketoacidosis, but their insulin secretory response to glucose is blunted. Over time, impaired transcription of these target genes leads to worsening postprandial hyperglycaemia, then fasting hyperglycaemia, and eventually more overt diabetes. This progressive defect explains why the patient initially had preserved but insufficient C-peptide and why his glycaemia improved with therapies that enhanced endogenous insulin secretion rather than requiring permanent insulin replacement.

The case also illustrates another HNF1A effect: reduced renal glucose reabsorption, causing **glycosuria at relatively lower glucose levels**, because HNF1A normally regulates tubular glucose transport, including SGLT2 expression. Thus, HNF1A mutations cause diabetes through a dual mechanism—**β-cell transcriptional dysfunction with progressive insulin secretory failure, plus lowered renal glucose threshold**—which is why these patients can present as young adults with glycosuria, marked hyperglycaemia, negative autoantibodies, and a strong autosomal dominant family history.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why can glycosuria occur early and disproportionately in HNF1A-MODY, even before severe fasting hyperglycaemia develops?

In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria can appear early and out of proportion to the degree of hyperglycaemia because the mutation does **two things at once**: it impairs pancreatic β-cell insulin secretion **and** lowers the **renal threshold for glucose reabsorption**.

The key renal mechanism is that **HNF1A normally regulates expression of SGLT2 in the proximal tubule**. When HNF1A function is reduced, **SGLT2 expression falls**, so the kidney reabsorbs less glucose. As a result, glucose spills into the urine at plasma glucose levels that would not cause glycosuria in most people. In other words, the patient may develop **glycosuria early, before fasting hyperglycaemia becomes severe**, because the kidney is “leaking” glucose more readily.

This is exactly the pattern highlighted in the case report: the patient had glycosuria at presentation, and the discussion notes that this is attributed to **reduced SGLT2 expression under HNF1A transcriptional control**. The β-cell defect in HNF1A-MODY is progressive, so fasting hyperglycaemia may worsen over time, but the **renal tubular defect can make glycosuria an early clue** to the diagnosis.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the major clues in the pedigree that support autosomal dominant inheritance, and why is this pattern important when evaluating early-onset diabetes?

The pedigree in this case has the classic hallmarks of **autosomal dominant inheritance**: diabetes appears in **successive generations** rather than skipping generations; there is **vertical transmission** from the maternal grandmother to the mother to the proband’s younger brother; and the affected relatives are not confined to one sex, consistent with a **non–sex-linked** trait. Another important clue is the **early age at diagnosis** in multiple family members—grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, brother at 20—which strongly suggests a familial monogenic form of diabetes rather than typical type 2 diabetes.

This pattern matters because **early-onset diabetes with autosomal dominant inheritance should prompt consideration of MODY**, particularly **HNF1A-MODY** in a family like this. Recognizing the inheritance pattern helps avoid misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, supports **genetic testing**, and has direct implications for management and family counseling. In this case, the pedigree, together with negative islet autoantibodies and preserved C-peptide, made MODY much more likely and led to the correct molecular diagnosis and more appropriate therapy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient with early-onset diabetes has a BMI in the overweight range and hypertriglyceridaemia, why should MODY still remain in the differential diagnosis?

Because **BMI and triglycerides are not specific for type 2 diabetes**, and their presence does **not rule out MODY**.

In this case, the features that should keep **HNF1A-MODY** on the table were more persuasive than the metabolic features that pointed toward type 2 diabetes: **young age at diagnosis, a strong autosomal dominant family history of early-onset diabetes, absence of diabetic ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved but inadequate C-peptide secretion**. Those are classic clues to a **monogenic β-cell disorder**.

The reason the BMI and triglycerides can mislead is that **MODY and type 2 diabetes can overlap clinically**. As obesity and metabolic syndrome become more common in the general population, a patient with MODY may also be overweight or have hypertriglyceridaemia simply by coincidence or due to concomitant insulin resistance. The case report explicitly notes that **BMI and metabolic syndrome are less useful discriminators than they once were**, because many MODY patients no longer fit the stereotype of being lean.

So the teaching point is: **overweight status does not exclude MODY**. In a young patient with early-onset diabetes, especially with a strong family history and negative autoantibodies, you should still think of MODY even if the phenotype looks “type 2–like.” Genetic testing is what establishes the diagnosis and can completely change treatment.

### [Management] After genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, what treatment changes become reasonable, and why are low-dose sulfonylureas and GLP-1 receptor agonists particularly relevant options?

Once **HNF1A-MODY is genetically confirmed**, the treatment plan can often be **de-escalated from a type 1/type 2 diabetes regimen to a MODY-specific regimen**. In practical terms, it becomes reasonable to **stop insulin if the patient is not insulin-deficient or ketotic and still has measurable endogenous insulin secretion**, and to simplify therapy away from agents chosen empirically for type 2 diabetes. In this case, after confirmation of an HNF1A pathogenic variant and with preserved but reduced C-peptide, insulin was discontinued and the patient was treated successfully with a **GLP-1 receptor agonist**, later maintained on non-insulin therapy with excellent glycemic control.

The reason **low-dose sulfonylureas** are first-line in HNF1A-MODY is pathophysiologic: HNF1A mutations cause a **primary β-cell secretory defect**, but the β cells are often still able to respond to pharmacologic stimulation. Sulfonylureas close the ATP-sensitive potassium channel and **stimulate endogenous insulin release**, so patients with HNF1A-MODY are often **highly sensitive** to them and may need only small doses. That same sensitivity is why hypoglycemia can be a problem if standard doses are used.

**GLP-1 receptor agonists** are particularly relevant when β-cell function is declining, when sulfonylurea-associated hypoglycemia is a concern, or when a patient needs an alternative that is less likely to cause lows. In the case report, dulaglutide provided effective glycemic control after insulin was stopped. Mechanistically, GLP-1 receptor agonists enhance **glucose-dependent insulin secretion** and suppress glucagon, which fits the defect in HNF1A-MODY while reducing hypoglycemia risk. The report also notes trial data showing GLP-1 receptor agonists can perform similarly to sulfonylureas for glycemic lowering, with fewer mild hypoglycemic events.

So, after genetic confirmation, the key management shift is to **match therapy to the monogenic defect**: consider **low-dose sulfonylurea as classic first-line therapy**, and use **GLP-1 receptor agonists** as an effective and often safer alternative or adjunct, especially as β-cell reserve wanes.

### [Management] Why might SGLT2 inhibitors be relatively unfavorable in patients with HNF1A-MODY, despite their common use in other forms of diabetes?

In **HNF1A-MODY**, SGLT2 inhibitors are relatively unfavorable because the renal glucose-handling defect is already part of the disease biology. HNF1A normally regulates expression of **SGLT2 in the proximal tubule**; when HNF1A is mutated, **SGLT2 expression is reduced**, lowering the renal threshold for glucose and causing **glycosuria even without SGLT2 inhibitor therapy**.  

If you then add an SGLT2 inhibitor, you may **further increase urinary glucose loss** and thereby worsen **osmotic diuresis, dehydration, and the risk of ketosis or diabetic ketoacidosis**. In the case report, the authors specifically note that glycosuria was present and that the risks of SGLT2 inhibition in HNF1A-MODY—**dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and DKA**—may outweigh the benefits.  

So, unlike in typical type 2 diabetes, where SGLT2 inhibitors are often beneficial, in HNF1A-MODY they can **accentuate an already abnormal renal glucose-wasting state** and carry disproportionate safety concerns.

### [Morning Report] How can a novel HNF1A variant be classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic even if it is absent from ClinVar, and what types of evidence from this case support that interpretation?

A variant does **not** need to be listed in ClinVar to be considered **pathogenic or likely pathogenic**. ClinVar is a useful catalog of previously reported variants, but ACMG classification is based on the **totality of evidence**—population data, computational predictions, functional data when available, segregation, and how well the variant matches the patient’s phenotype.

In this case, the HNF1A variant **c.809A>G (p.N270S)** was absent from ClinVar, VarSome, and the literature, but several independent lines of evidence supported a **likely pathogenic** interpretation:

- **Strong phenotype match:** The patient had early-onset diabetes, glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but insufficient C-peptide, and a **strong autosomal dominant family history** of early-onset diabetes across multiple generations—classic features of **HNF1A-MODY**.
- **Segregation with disease:** The variant **co-segregated with diabetes in the family**, which is important ACMG evidence that the variant tracks with the affected phenotype.
- **Location in a critical region:** The substitution lies in the **DNA-binding domain** of HNF1A, a region in which many pathogenic MODY variants occur.
- **Amino acid context:** The altered residue is **adjacent to known pathogenic residues**, increasing concern that this site is functionally important.
- **Computational support:** The variant had a **high AlphaMissense score (0.995)**, supporting deleteriousness.

So, even though the variant was **novel** and absent from ClinVar, the combination of a **highly specific clinical phenotype**, **family segregation**, **location in a functionally important domain**, and **supportive in silico prediction** allowed it to be classified under ACMG criteria as **likely pathogenic/pathogenic**. The key teaching point is that **absence from a database is not evidence of benignity**; classification depends on integrated genetic and clinical evidence.
