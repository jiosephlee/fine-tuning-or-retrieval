Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Recognizing Monogenic Diabetes in a Young Adult

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young, or MODY, is a form of monogenic diabetes caused by a single-gene defect that impairs pancreatic β-cell development or function. Unlike type 1 diabetes, it is not autoimmune, and unlike typical type 2 diabetes, it is not primarily driven by insulin resistance. MODY is usually inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, so diabetes often appears across successive generations of a family. Although it accounts for only a small fraction of all diabetes cases, MODY is clinically important because the diagnosis can change treatment, prognosis, and family counseling. It is frequently missed, especially when patients present with mild or nonspecific hyperglycaemia and are assumed to have type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

Several genes can cause MODY, but HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, and HNF1B are among the most common. HNF1A-MODY is one of the most frequent subtypes and classically presents in adolescence or early adulthood with progressive hyperglycaemia caused by declining insulin secretion. Patients often have a strong family history of early-onset diabetes, and the disorder may be passed from an affected parent to children in a vertical pattern. Because the phenotype can overlap with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, clinical suspicion is essential. In this case, the patient was a young adult with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria found incidentally on routine annual screening, a presentation that should prompt consideration of a monogenic cause rather than automatic classification as type 2 diabetes.

The patient also had symptoms of diabetes, including thirst, increased appetite, and recent weight loss, but he did not present with diabetic ketoacidosis. That pattern is important: MODY can cause symptomatic hyperglycaemia, but patients often retain enough endogenous insulin secretion to avoid ketosis, especially early in the course. On examination, he did not have acanthosis nigricans, which made marked insulin resistance less likely, and his BMI was only modestly elevated rather than in the range typically associated with classic type 2 diabetes. These findings do not exclude type 2 diabetes, but taken together they make the diagnosis less straightforward and should widen the differential.

The family history was one of the strongest clues in this case. Diabetes had affected the maternal grandmother, the mother, and the younger brother at young ages, suggesting autosomal dominant inheritance. A pedigree like this is a major reason to consider MODY in a young person with diabetes, particularly when the clinical picture is not typical for autoimmune diabetes and there is no strong evidence of severe insulin resistance. In contrast, type 1 diabetes would more often be associated with islet autoimmunity and a more abrupt insulin-deficient presentation, while type 2 diabetes is usually associated with older age, obesity, and a broader metabolic syndrome phenotype.

A key teaching point is that early-onset diabetes with preserved endogenous insulin secretion should trigger consideration of MODY. In this patient, later testing showed measurable C-peptide, supporting residual β-cell function. In the setting of young age, negative autoantibodies, and a strong multigenerational family history, this biochemical pattern is much more consistent with monogenic diabetes than with classic type 1 diabetes. Many patients with MODY are initially mislabeled because the diagnosis is often made before genetic testing is considered, and the overlapping features of diabetes subtypes can mislead clinicians. Recognizing these clues early is essential, because identifying HNF1A-MODY can lead to more appropriate therapy and avoids years of treatment based on an incorrect diabetes classification.

## Pathophysiology and Clinical Features of HNF1A-MODY

HNF1A-MODY is caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha gene, a transcription factor encoded on chromosome 12q24. The gene has 10 exons and is expressed in multiple tissues, especially the pancreatic islets, liver, and kidneys. In the pancreas, HNF1A is important for maintaining normal beta-cell differentiation and for regulating genes required for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. Because the disorder is inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion, diabetes often appears in multiple generations, as in this patient’s family.

At the cellular level, HNF1A controls transcription of several genes involved in beta-cell glucose sensing and insulin production. When HNF1A function is impaired, expression of insulin-related pathways falls, including genes involved in insulin synthesis, GLUT2-mediated glucose transport, and pyruvate kinase activity. These defects reduce the beta cell’s ability to detect glucose and mount an appropriate insulin response. The result is not immediate absolute insulin deficiency, as in classic type 1 diabetes, but a progressive decline in endogenous insulin secretion. This is why patients may initially retain measurable C-peptide, yet over time develop worsening hyperglycaemia as beta-cell reserve is gradually lost.

The clinical pattern in HNF1A-MODY often reflects this stepwise deterioration in beta-cell function. Early in the disease, fasting glucose may be only mildly abnormal or even near normal, while postprandial glucose rises disproportionately because the pancreas cannot increase insulin secretion appropriately after meals. This explains why an oral glucose tolerance test can be particularly revealing and why patients may first be recognized through elevated postprandial glucose or incidental glycosuria. In this case, the patient had marked hyperglycaemia on fasting and OGTT testing, with symptoms of polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss indicating more advanced beta-cell dysfunction.

A distinctive feature of HNF1A-MODY is glycosuria out of proportion to the level of hyperglycaemia. HNF1A regulates expression of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal proximal tubule, and reduced HNF1A activity lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. As a result, glucose spills into the urine at relatively lower plasma glucose levels than would be expected in other forms of diabetes. In the reported case, glycosuria was an important clue that the disorder was not typical type 2 diabetes alone, especially in a young patient without clear insulin resistance.

The phenotype of HNF1A-MODY can overlap with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, which is why it is often misclassified. Like type 1 diabetes, patients may present at a young age, with weight loss and marked hyperglycaemia. However, they usually lack autoimmune markers and often retain endogenous insulin secretion, at least initially. Like type 2 diabetes, they may show hyperglycaemia without ketoacidosis and may sometimes have dyslipidaemia or higher body mass index, particularly in populations where overweight is common. These overlapping features can obscure recognition of MODY and delay genetic testing. In this patient, the absence of acanthosis nigricans, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved though reduced C-peptide, and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes all supported a monogenic rather than typical polygenic diabetes diagnosis.

Although many patients with HNF1A-MODY are not obese, obesity or metabolic syndrome features do not exclude the diagnosis. Rising background rates of overweight, hypertriglyceridaemia, and insulin resistance can make HNF1A-MODY look more like type 2 diabetes. This is clinically important because reliance on BMI alone may miss the diagnosis. The key clue remains the combination of early onset, autosomal dominant family history, negative autoimmune testing, and evidence of residual beta-cell function.

Genotype also influences phenotype. Many pathogenic HNF1A variants cluster in the DNA-binding domain, which is critical for transcriptional activity. Variants in this region are often associated with clear loss of function and typical MODY features, although expressivity can vary between families and even between individuals carrying the same variant. This helps explain why HNF1A-MODY shows broad clinical diversity, ranging from mild asymptomatic hyperglycaemia to overt diabetes requiring medication. In the case reported here, the novel variant in the DNA-binding domain fit the expected disease mechanism and the family history of early-onset diabetes strengthened its clinical relevance.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning: From Hyperglycaemia to Genetic Diagnosis

This patient’s diagnostic evaluation began with recognition of unequivocal diabetes on routine screening. His HbA1c was 10.2%, fasting plasma glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and 2-hour glucose after a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test was 19.8 mmol/L. Together, these values confirm sustained hyperglycaemia rather than isolated impaired fasting glucose or borderline dysglycaemia. The markedly elevated post-load glucose is especially important in HNF1A-MODY, because early disease may present predominantly with postprandial hyperglycaemia before fasting glucose becomes abnormal. In this case, the degree of hyperglycaemia and the presence of osmotic symptoms such as polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent weight loss indicated clinically significant diabetes requiring treatment.

The absence of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) was another important clue. Patients with classic type 1 diabetes often present with absolute insulin deficiency and may develop DKA at diagnosis, particularly when hyperglycaemia is severe. By contrast, patients with MODY usually retain some endogenous insulin secretion, so ketosis is uncommon. The lack of DKA here did not by itself establish MODY, but it made fulminant autoimmune insulinopenia less likely and supported a broader differential that included type 2 diabetes and monogenic diabetes.

To assess residual β-cell function, the clinicians performed a glucagon stimulation test, which showed a fasting C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L rising to 0.83 nmol/L after stimulation. C-peptide reflects endogenous insulin production and is useful because it is not present in exogenous insulin preparations. A stimulated C-peptide above about 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved insulin secretion, whereas lower values suggest impaired β-cell reserve. In this patient, the stimulated value was above the usual threshold, showing that the pancreas was still producing insulin, but the overall response was inadequate for the degree of hyperglycaemia. This pattern fits a progressive β-cell defect such as HNF1A-MODY. However, C-peptide must be interpreted cautiously. Some patients with recent-onset type 1 diabetes may still have measurable C-peptide, so preserved secretion supports but does not prove MODY. Serial follow-up is therefore often needed when the clinical picture is not definitive.

Autoimmune markers further helped narrow the diagnosis. Both GADA and IA-2A were negative. In a young person with diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies reduce the likelihood of type 1 diabetes, especially when combined with preserved C-peptide and a strong multigenerational family history. They do not completely exclude autoimmune diabetes, because a minority of patients may have negative antibodies at presentation or lose detectable antibodies over time. Nevertheless, in this case the combination of negative autoantibodies, no DKA, and retained insulin secretion argued strongly against classic type 1 diabetes.

Several ancillary findings were also considered. The lipid profile showed elevated triglycerides and relatively low HDL, which can raise suspicion for insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes. However, these findings are not specific, and dyslipidaemia can coexist with monogenic diabetes, particularly as background rates of overweight and metabolic syndrome increase in the general population. The patient’s BMI of 24.6 kg/m² was only mildly elevated and he lacked acanthosis nigricans, another feature that would have supported significant insulin resistance. Microalbuminuria was absent, indicating no evident early nephropathy at diagnosis. Reduced sudomotor function on electrochemical skin conductance testing suggested autonomic small fibre dysfunction, but this was not diagnostic of any diabetes subtype; it mainly contributed to assessment of complication burden rather than etiology.

The differential diagnosis at this stage included type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and monogenic diabetes, especially MODY. Several features favored MODY over type 1 diabetes: diagnosis in young adulthood rather than childhood, a striking autosomal dominant family history across multiple generations, absence of ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, and measurable stimulated C-peptide. Features favoring MODY over type 2 diabetes included the strong family history of early-onset diabetes, absence of obvious insulin resistance, lack of acanthosis nigricans, and the pattern of hyperglycaemia detected on screening rather than presentation driven by obesity-associated metabolic syndrome. Although the triglyceride level and BMI could have pointed toward type 2 diabetes, these findings were not sufficient to outweigh the inherited pattern of disease and the β-cell–predominant phenotype.

To formalize the pretest probability of MODY, clinicians may use prediction tools such as the Exeter MODY calculator. These tools can be helpful in deciding whom to test, but they have important limitations. They were developed largely in White European populations, and their calibration may be less reliable in other ethnic groups. In addition, they depend on accurate clinical inputs and may not fully account for atypical presentations. In this patient, the calculator still yielded a substantial probability of MODY, reinforcing the decision to pursue molecular testing.

Genetic testing is indicated when the phenotype suggests monogenic diabetes, particularly in young patients with early-onset diabetes, a family history consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, negative autoantibodies, and preserved endogenous insulin secretion. The clinicians used a next-generation sequencing panel, which is a practical approach because it can evaluate multiple monogenic diabetes genes simultaneously, improving diagnostic yield compared with single-gene testing. This is especially useful when the phenotype is not classic enough to predict a single causative gene with confidence.

The panel identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant, c.809A>G, resulting in p.N270S. Interpretation of a new variant requires structured reasoning rather than reliance on a database label alone. Under ACMG principles, pathogenicity is supported by several converging lines of evidence: the variant was absent from common databases, it occurred in HNF1A, a well-established MODY gene, and it lay within the DNA-binding domain, a region in which many disease-causing variants cluster. The family history also supported segregation with diabetes across generations, which is a powerful argument for causality in an autosomal dominant disorder. In silico evidence further strengthened the case, although computational predictions are supportive rather than definitive on their own. Together, these findings justified classifying the variant as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in the clinical context.

The value of genetic confirmation is that it changes the diagnosis from a broad phenotypic category to a precise molecular subtype. Before genetic testing, the patient could reasonably have been treated as having type 2 diabetes because of age, BMI, and dyslipidaemia. After confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, the disease mechanism was understood as an inherited β-cell transcription factor defect, which has implications for treatment choice, prognosis, and family counselling. In this setting, molecular diagnosis is not merely confirmatory; it directs therapy and prevents ongoing misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

## Therapeutic Strategy and Precision Management in HNF1A-MODY

Management of this patient had to address two problems at once: he presented with clearly uncontrolled diabetes, yet the exact diabetes subtype was still uncertain at diagnosis. His HbA1c of 10.2% and marked fasting and post-load hyperglycaemia indicated sustained metabolic decompensation, so prompt glucose-lowering therapy was necessary to reduce symptoms and lower near-term risk. In this setting, the initial use of basal insulin together with metformin and saxagliptin was a pragmatic empiric approach. Insulin provides immediate and reliable glycaemic control, metformin can improve hepatic glucose output and insulin sensitivity, and a DPP-4 inhibitor adds incretin-based support without a high hypoglycaemia burden. This kind of initial treatment is common when a young adult presents with severe hyperglycaemia before the cause is known, because clinicians must balance urgency with diagnostic uncertainty.

Lifestyle therapy remained an essential part of care. The patient was counseled on a structured diet, regular exercise, and close endocrinology follow-up. These measures are not sufficient alone in HNF1A-MODY, but they support pharmacologic treatment and help assess whether glycaemic improvement reflects medication response, adherence, or both. Serial outpatient review is especially important in monogenic diabetes because therapy may need to be changed once the genetic diagnosis clarifies the underlying physiology.

Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed, treatment was reconsidered in light of the disorder’s typical pharmacologic responsiveness. In HNF1A-MODY, the main defect is progressive beta-cell dysfunction rather than primary insulin resistance, and many patients respond well to sulfonylureas, which stimulate endogenous insulin release by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels on beta cells. Historically, low-dose sulfonylureas are considered first-line therapy for many patients with HNF1A-MODY, often allowing lower insulin requirements or even complete discontinuation of insulin. In this case, insulin was stopped after genetic confirmation because the patient still had measurable, though insufficient, beta-cell reserve and had already shown early improvement in HbA1c. This illustrates a key principle in monogenic diabetes: once the diagnosis is corrected, treatment should be matched to the underlying defect rather than to the initial clinical label of type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

The team then switched to dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist. This was a rational precision-medicine choice because GLP-1 receptor agonists can improve glucose-dependent insulin secretion and reduce postprandial excursions while carrying a lower risk of hypoglycaemia than sulfonylureas. In patients with HNF1A-MODY, this is attractive when residual beta-cell function persists but is declining, or when avoiding hypoglycaemia is a priority. Small comparative studies suggest that GLP-1 receptor agonists can lower fasting and postprandial glucose effectively and may perform similarly to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY, with less hypoglycaemia. In this patient, glycaemic control improved from HbA1c 10.2% to 9.1% after one month and then to 6.0% after six months, consistent with a meaningful response to the revised regimen and supporting the appropriateness of discontinuing insulin.

After stabilization, therapy was simplified to metformin plus saxagliptin for maintenance. Although these agents are not the classic first-line drugs for HNF1A-MODY, they can be useful adjuncts in selected patients, particularly when glycaemia is controlled and the goal is to maintain stability with good tolerability. The choice of long-term therapy in monogenic diabetes should therefore be individualized according to residual beta-cell function, current HbA1c trajectory, weight, hypoglycaemia risk, and patient preference rather than based on a fixed algorithm.

The case also highlights why SGLT2 inhibitors may be problematic in HNF1A-MODY. This subtype is associated with renal glycosuria because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the kidney, so patients may already have a reduced renal threshold for glucose. Further pharmacologic blockade could intensify glycosuria and increase the risk of volume depletion. Concern has also been raised about diabetic ketoacidosis, particularly in patients with limited insulin reserve. For this reason, SGLT2 inhibitors are often approached cautiously in HNF1A-MODY, especially when beta-cell function is already compromised.

Overall, the therapeutic course in this case demonstrates the central principle of precision management in monogenic diabetes: treatment should evolve from empiric glucose control to genotype-guided therapy once the diagnosis is established. Genetic confirmation changed not only the label of diabetes, but the medication strategy, allowing insulin withdrawal and selection of a regimen better aligned with the patient’s underlying beta-cell defect.

## Follow-up, Prognosis, and Broader Clinical Lessons

This patient had an excellent early glycaemic response once the diagnosis was clarified. After the initial insulin-based regimen lowered HbA1c from 10.2% to 9.1%, genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY allowed treatment to be tailored more precisely, and insulin was stopped in favor of a GLP-1 receptor agonist with metformin. With this approach, HbA1c fell to 6.0% and was then maintained below 6.5% on follow-up. For trainees, this is an important reminder that a correct monogenic diagnosis can change not only the label attached to diabetes, but also the treatment strategy, medication burden, and expected clinical course.

Although early follow-up was reassuring, long-term surveillance remains essential in HNF1A-MODY because chronic hyperglycaemia can still lead to the same microvascular complications seen in other forms of diabetes. In this case, there was no microalbuminuria and no retinopathy at the time of follow-up, which is encouraging but should not be overinterpreted as proof of low lifetime risk. Rather, these findings indicate that end-organ damage had not yet become clinically evident. Because HNF1A-MODY is a progressive disorder with declining β-cell function over time, ongoing monitoring of renal function, urine albumin excretion, and retinal status is warranted even when glucose control is initially restored.

The report also noted abnormal sudomotor testing, suggesting autonomic small-fibre dysfunction. In a young patient, this finding does not establish a diabetic complication on its own, but it raises the possibility of early neuropathic involvement or autonomic impairment that merits longitudinal reassessment. Such ancillary abnormalities can be useful as baseline data, especially in monogenic diabetes where the natural history may differ from typical type 1 or type 2 diabetes, but they should be interpreted cautiously and in clinical context.

Understanding the natural history of HNF1A-MODY is central to prognosis. The disorder usually begins with relatively preserved endogenous insulin secretion, followed by progressive β-cell dysfunction over years. This explains why patients may initially be managed without insulin, yet later require escalation if glycaemic control worsens. Accurate diagnosis therefore helps clinicians anticipate future treatment needs rather than assuming the phenotype will behave like typical type 2 diabetes. It also prevents unnecessary long-term insulin use in some patients and supports individualized choices when β-cell reserve is still present.

Because HNF1A-MODY is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, the diagnosis has implications for the patient’s relatives as well. The family history in this case was strongly suggestive, with diabetes across multiple generations at young ages. Once a pathogenic variant is identified, cascade genetic testing and counselling become important so that at-risk family members can be tested, diagnosed earlier, and treated appropriately. This can improve glycaemic outcomes and reduce the chance of misclassification in relatives who may otherwise be treated empirically as having type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

This case also illustrates why novel HNF1A variants matter. Even when a variant has not been previously listed in databases, it may still be clinically meaningful if the phenotype, segregation pattern, bioinformatic prediction, and supporting laboratory findings are concordant. Expanding variant databases improves future diagnostic accuracy and reduces uncertainty for other patients with similar presentations. In that sense, case reports do more than describe an individual patient—they add to the collective reference set needed for precision medicine.

At a broader level, the report highlights the limitations of relying solely on routine clinical features to detect MODY. BMI, metabolic syndrome features, and even family history can be misleading, especially as obesity and type 2 diabetes become more common in younger populations. Autoantibody negativity and preserved C-peptide support a non-autoimmune form of diabetes, but they are not sufficient by themselves to distinguish MODY from atypical type 2 diabetes or early autoimmune disease. Clinical prediction tools can help select patients for testing, but they were developed in specific populations and may not generalize perfectly across ethnic groups. This is especially relevant because MODY prevalence and recognition vary substantially between populations, and much of the original evidence comes from White European cohorts.

Next-generation sequencing is therefore increasingly important in early-onset diabetes of unclear type. It increases diagnostic yield, can identify unexpected or novel variants, and may ultimately be more cost-effective when used in carefully selected patients with a high pre-test probability of monogenic diabetes. For trainees, the practical lesson is to suspect MODY when diabetes begins at a young age, autoantibodies are negative, C-peptide is still present, there is a strong family history across generations, and the phenotype does not fit classic type 1 or type 2 diabetes. When those features are present, genetic testing can directly affect prognosis, family counselling, and long-term therapy.

In summary, this case underscores that early and accurate identification of HNF1A-MODY can lead to excellent glycaemic control, more rational medication selection, and appropriate surveillance for complications. It also reinforces the value of family-based evaluation and genetic testing in young-onset diabetes, particularly when the presentation is atypical and standard biomarkers are insufficient to make the diagnosis.
