Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When “Angioedema” Isn’t: Dermatomyositis in the Swollen Neck

## The weird part first: a neck swelling that was not angioedema

A man in his 40s showed up with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling. Easy enough to anchor on “angioedema,” right? Except the story kept refusing to behave like angioedema.

He was afebrile, oxygenating well, not tachypnoeic, and had no ACE inhibitor, no DPP-4 inhibitor, no NSAID trigger, no personal or family history of angioedema, and no urticaria. ENT scoped him and didn’t find a structural airway catastrophe. The neck was swollen and erythematous, yes — but the rest of the exam started to whisper “inflammatory myopathy” rather than “allergic swelling.”

The clue that cracked the case was a creatine kinase level. It was massively elevated: 16,184 U/L.

This was dermatomyositis presenting as pseudoangioedema, with prominent cervical soft tissue oedema, dysphagia, and myositis. It’s uncommon, easy to mislabel, and clinically important because the treatment path is very different from histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

## Why this matters

Neck swelling in the ED is high-stakes. The default job is to rule out the lethal stuff first: deep neck space infection, Ludwig’s angina, airway compromise, anaphylaxis, SVC syndrome, and true angioedema.

But this case is a good reminder that not all “swelling” is the same pathology. In dermatomyositis, the neck can look like angioedema while the mechanism is really inflammatory edema associated with active myositis. If you miss that distinction, you can lose time before starting immunosuppression and malignancy screening.

## How this presented

The case had a few features that should make you pause before calling it angioedema:

The neck swelling was subacute, evolving over weeks rather than hours. That’s already a mismatch for classic histaminergic angioedema, which tends to come on more abruptly, and for many bradykinin-mediated episodes as well.

He also had proximal muscle weakness, mild myalgia, severe dysphagia, and subtle skin/nail findings. On exam there was facial erythema including the nasolabial folds, periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages/avascular areas, and a rash on the thigh. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were absent, which is exactly the trap — dermatomyositis does not always read the textbook to you.

The CT neck showed diffuse oedema involving the strap muscles with extension into the retropharyngeal space, which understandably raised concern for infection. But he lacked the systemic toxicity you’d expect with an aggressive deep neck infection, and nasendoscopy didn’t show a focal obstructing lesion.

Once the CK came back, the diagnostic frame changed.

## Distinguishing inflammatory cervical oedema from angioedema

This is the practical bedside lesson.

Histaminergic angioedema usually gives you a history of rapid swelling, often with urticaria, pruritus, known triggers, and sometimes anaphylaxis physiology. Bradykinin-mediated angioedema often lacks urticaria, but it still tends to have a recurring pattern, a medication/family history, or associated complement abnormalities depending on the subtype.

Inflammatory oedema from dermatomyositis behaves differently:
- it may be more subacute or persistent
- it may come with muscle pain or weakness
- the skin exam may show subtle dermatomyositis stigmata
- CK is often a huge clue
- swallowing dysfunction can be prominent
- imaging may show soft tissue and muscle involvement rather than isolated submucosal airway swelling

In this patient, complement studies and C1 esterase testing were normal, which helped push away hereditary/acquired angioedema. The absence of a clear histamine or bradykinin story plus the abnormal muscle/skin findings made dermatomyositis the right hypothesis.

## The diagnosis: anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis

The myositis panel was strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibody. Muscle MRI showed active myositis. PET demonstrated diffuse abnormal uptake in multiple muscle groups, especially in the head and neck, and muscle biopsy showed perifascicular changes with C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — classic dermatomyositis pathology.

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is not the most common serologic subtype, but it matters because it can present with striking skin disease, dysphagia, and interstitial lung disease. In the literature, dysphagia and ILD are common enough in SAE1-positive disease to deserve real respect, and malignancy risk is also part of the conversation.

In this case, the “angioedema” was the visible tip of a broader inflammatory syndrome.

## A practical diagnostic approach for the swollen neck

When the neck is swollen and the diagnosis is not obvious, think in layers.

First, stabilize the airway and rule out immediate threats. That means looking for stridor, muffled voice, inability to handle secretions, drooling, rapid progression, and focal exam findings that suggest infection or space-occupying pathology.

Then ask: does this smell like true angioedema?
- rapid onset?
- urticaria or pruritus?
- trigger medication?
- prior episodes?
- family history?
- response to antihistamines/epinephrine/steroids?

If not, widen the net. Look for:
- proximal weakness
- dysphagia
- myalgia
- rashes over the face, hands, trunk, or thighs
- nailfold changes
- elevated CK, AST/ALT, LDH
- interstitial lung disease or aspiration changes
- malignancy clues

In this case, the “can’t miss” test was CK. If you remember one thing from this story, remember that.

## Treatment: this was a myositis emergency, not an allergy problem

Because there was significant airway muscle involvement and severe dysphagia, he was treated aggressively with:
- 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily
- oral prednisone taper
- IVIG
- mycophenolate mofetil
- rituximab

He also needed nutrition support via nasogastric tube for persistent dysphagia and Pneumocystis prophylaxis, with TMP-SMX stopped because of haemolytic anaemia and pentamidine used instead.

That’s a good reminder that dermatomyositis with severe bulbar involvement is not a “watch and wait” disease. It can require real immunosuppression early, plus airway, swallow, and nutrition planning.

## Malignancy screening is not optional

Dermatomyositis is one of those diagnoses that should trigger cancer vigilance. This patient was considered high risk by IMACS criteria because he had dermatomyositis and dysphagia, plus intermediate risk factors including male sex and SAE1 positivity.

His workup included:
- CT chest/abdomen/pelvis
- PET scan
- tumour markers
- planned upper and lower endoscopy

Nothing malignant was identified at the time, but the plan for ongoing surveillance was appropriate.

## What made this case so easy to miss

The hardest part is that the early story sounded like common ED pathology:
- neck swelling
- elevated CRP
- CT with deep soft tissue oedema
- concern for Ludwig’s angina
- then “maybe idiopathic angioedema”

That’s a very reasonable first-pass differential. The miss would have been to stop there.

The clues that should break the cognitive lock were:
- subacute course over weeks
- proximal weakness
- dysphagia
- subtle rash and nailfold changes
- no medication trigger
- normal complement/C1 esterase studies
- markedly elevated CK

Once you see that pattern, dermatomyositis becomes much less exotic.

## Take-home points

- Not all neck swelling is angioedema; dermatomyositis can present with pseudoangioedema and cervical soft tissue oedema.
- Subacute progression, proximal weakness, dysphagia, and subtle skin/nailfold findings should push you away from isolated allergic angioedema.
- A CK level is a high-yield test in unexplained neck swelling when myositis is on the table.
- Normal complement/C1 esterase studies and the absence of a trigger/history make hereditary or bradykinin-mediated angioedema less likely.
- Severe bulbar involvement in dermatomyositis can require urgent immunosuppression, swallow evaluation, nutrition support, and malignancy screening.

## Bottom line

If a “swollen neck” doesn’t fit the usual angioedema script, don’t force it. Examine the skin carefully, check muscle strength, order a CK, and keep dermatomyositis on the list. In this case, that shift in thinking changed the diagnosis from presumed angioedema to a treatable inflammatory myopathy with airway-adjacent involvement — exactly the kind of diagnostic pivot that matters in the ED and beyond.



# The CK That Changed the Case: Using Small Exam Findings to Unmask Inflammatory Myopathy

## The setup: when neck swelling is not what it seems

A man in his 40s walked into the ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The initial lane of thinking made perfect sense: deep neck infection, then Ludwig’s angina, then—after ENT evaluation and a negative structural workup—idiopathic angioedema.

But the case took a sharp turn when someone paused long enough to look beyond the swelling itself.

A few subtle findings changed everything: periungual nailfold abnormalities, facial erythema that included the nasolabial folds, a patchy rash on the thigh, mild proximal hip flexor weakness, and dysphagia. A creatine kinase was sent. It came back at 16,184 U/L.

That’s the pivot point in this case. Not the scan. Not the nasendoscopy. The bedside exam.

## Why this case matters

Neck swelling tends to trigger two reflexive pathways:

- infection until proven otherwise
- angioedema until proven otherwise

Both are reasonable. Both can be wrong.

This case is a good reminder that dermatomyositis can masquerade as facial or cervical edema, including a pseudoangioedema picture. When the swelling is non-tender, persistent, not fitting histamine-mediated or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, and accompanied by muscle symptoms or odd skin/nail findings, inflammatory myopathy needs to move up the list.

In this patient, the diagnosis was ultimately anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with significant myositis, severe dysphagia, and inflammatory neck swelling.

## The bedside clues that mattered

The early presentation was easy to anchor on because the neck looked swollen and inflammatory on CT. But the exam contained the clues that steered the team away from ENT-only thinking:

- **Neck swelling was atypical for angioedema**
  - persistent over weeks
  - non-tender
  - associated with other systemic findings
- **Proximal weakness was present**
  - hip flexors 4/5
- **Dysphagia was present**
  - later confirmed as severe pharyngeal dysphagia on swallow study
- **Subtle skin/nail findings were present**
  - facial erythema
  - periungual erythema
  - nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy
  - thigh rash
- **Classic dermatomyositis rash was absent**
  - no Gottron’s papules
  - no heliotrope rash

That’s the teaching point: you do not need the full textbook rash to make the diagnosis suspect. Sometimes the exam gives you fragments, not the full picture.

## The CK is the clue you don’t want to miss

When a patient has unexplained swelling plus weakness, a CK is cheap, fast, and high-yield.

In this case, CK was dramatically elevated, and that reframed the problem from “is this angioedema?” to “why is there severe muscle injury with inflammatory features?”

That single lab result helped connect the dots with:

- elevated AST/ALT/LDH
- proximal myositis on MRI
- diffuse FDG uptake in multiple muscle groups on PET
- severe dysphagia
- biopsy-proven dermatomyositis

The CK didn’t make the diagnosis alone, but it forced the right diagnostic lane.

## Dermatomyositis can look like ENT disease

This patient was initially handled like a deep neck space process, which is understandable. Neck edema and retropharyngeal inflammatory changes on CT will make most clinicians think infection first.

But dermatomyositis can produce **subcutaneous edema and inflammatory swelling**, especially in the face and neck. That can mimic:

- Ludwig’s angina
- cellulitis/erysipelas
- idiopathic angioedema
- hereditary or acquired angioedema
- sialadenitis
- superior vena cava syndrome

The key discriminator is that dermatomyositis usually brings along other inflammatory clues if you look carefully enough: weakness, dysphagia, rash, nailfold changes, elevated muscle enzymes.

## Why the skin and nail exam matters so much

This is the exam pearl worth carrying into practice: **nailfolds are often where inflammatory myopathy quietly announces itself**.

In this patient, nailfold capillaroscopy showed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, and bedside exam found periungual erythema. Those are not dramatic findings, but they are very real ones.

If you see unexplained facial or neck edema and you’re wondering about angioedema, don’t stop at the airway exam. Look for:

- periungual erythema
- nailfold capillary dropout or hemorrhage
- shawl-type or V-sign distribution
- violaceous facial erythema
- holster-type thigh rash
- subtle proximal weakness

Classic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash are helpful when present, but their absence does not rule out dermatomyositis.

## The broader workup: once the diagnosis was suspected

After CK came back, the case expanded appropriately.

He had:

- ANA 1:1280, speckled
- strongly positive **anti-SAE1 antibody**
- MRI thighs showing active myositis
- PET scan showing diffuse muscle uptake without evidence of malignancy
- muscle biopsy consistent with dermatomyositis, including perifascicular C5b-9 deposition
- severe pharyngeal dysphagia on swallow study
- CT chest showing nodules and aspiration-type changes, without pulmonary fibrosis

His infectious and angioedema workup was unrevealing, including normal complement and C1 esterase studies.

That’s another useful lesson: a negative angioedema evaluation should not force you to keep calling the syndrome angioedema if the phenotype doesn’t fit.

## Treatment implications: why recognition matters

Dermatomyositis is not a “watch and wait” diagnosis when there is airway involvement, severe dysphagia, or marked myositis. This patient received aggressive immunosuppression:

- IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days
- prednisone taper
- IVIG
- mycophenolate mofetil
- rituximab

He also needed enteral feeding support because dysphagia was profound and prolonged.

The big practical point: **earlier recognition changes disposition, specialty involvement, and treatment intensity**. Mislabeling this as idiopathic angioedema could delay immunosuppression, delay swallow precautions, and miss the need for malignancy screening.

## Malignancy screening still matters

This case also reminds us that dermatomyositis is not just a skin-and-muscle disease. It can be a paraneoplastic clue.

In this patient, malignancy evaluation was reassuring, but he appropriately underwent extensive screening and ongoing surveillance. The case report notes that under IMACS guidance, his risk profile placed him in a high-risk screening category because of dermatomyositis plus dysphagia, with additional intermediate-risk features.

So once dermatomyositis is on the table, the workup is not only about treating inflammation; it’s also about asking **why** this inflammatory syndrome is occurring.

## Take-home points

- **Unexplained neck swelling is not always angioedema or infection.**
- **If swelling is accompanied by weakness, dysphagia, rash, or nailfold changes, think inflammatory myopathy.**
- **Send a CK early when the exam doesn’t fit the presumed diagnosis.**
- **Classic dermatomyositis rash may be absent. Subtle periungual and nailfold findings can be the giveaway.**
- **Dermatomyositis can present with pseudoangioedema of the face/neck and mimic ENT disease.**
- **Severe dysphagia and marked myositis warrant prompt immunosuppression and swallowing precautions.**
- **Once dermatomyositis is diagnosed, malignancy screening is part of the package.**

## Final thought

This case is a great reminder that the best diagnostic test is often still the physical exam—especially when the findings are small, easy to miss, and sitting just outside the chief complaint.

The neck swelling brought the patient into the hospital.  
The CK changed the case.  
And the nailfolds, rash, and proximal weakness made the diagnosis possible.



# SAE1 Dermatomyositis: Why This Antibody Matters at the Front Door

## The case that should make you pause

A man in his 40s walked into the ED with what looked, at first glance, like a neck emergency: 3 weeks of progressive cervical swelling, CT evidence of diffuse neck edema, elevated inflammatory markers, and enough airway-adjacent anatomy on the scan to make Ludwig’s angina or deep neck infection feel very plausible.

But the story never quite fit.

He was afebrile, oxygenating well, and didn’t look septic. ENT scope showed no obstructing lesion or deep space catastrophe. Then the physical exam started to give the game away: facial erythema that included the nasolabial folds, subtle periungual erythema/microhemorrhages, a rash on the thigh, and mild proximal hip flexor weakness. A CK was sent because the “angioedema” felt off.

It came back at 16,184 U/L.

This was dermatomyositis presenting as pseudoangioedema, ultimately linked to anti-SAE1 antibodies. The neck swelling wasn’t histamine-driven, and it wasn’t bradykinin-driven. It was inflammatory muscle disease with dramatic soft tissue edema.

That’s the lesson: when “angioedema” doesn’t behave like angioedema, think beyond the allergy bucket.

## Why anti-SAE1 matters

Anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis is uncommon, but it has a recognizable clinical flavor.

In broad strokes, this phenotype tends to feature:

- Prominent cutaneous disease
- Dysphagia, sometimes severe
- Myositis that may be clinically subtle early on
- Possible interstitial lung disease or other pulmonary involvement
- Non-trivial malignancy risk

In the case above, the diagnosis was confirmed by a positive SAE1 antibody, MRI evidence of active myositis, PET scan uptake in multiple muscle groups, and muscle biopsy showing perifascicular pathology with C5b-9 deposition—classic dermatomyositis territory.

The antibody is not just a lab curiosity. It helps frame what to look for next, how aggressively to screen, and how closely to follow.

## The phenotype: don’t wait for textbook skin

Dermatomyositis is famous for Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash, but real patients don’t always read the textbook. In this case, those hallmark findings were absent. Instead, the clues were more subtle:

- Facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds
- Periungual erythema and nailfold microvascular changes
- A rash on the thigh
- Proximal weakness
- Dysphagia
- Marked CK elevation

That combination should trigger a myositis workup, even if the patient arrived under a completely different label.

A few practical pearls:

- Neck swelling can be a dermatomyositis presentation.
- Pseudoangioedema is rare, but it happens.
- Dysphagia may be a major clue and may be underreported unless you ask directly.
- Nailfold capillary changes are often more helpful than the “classic” rash when skin findings are subtle.

## Acute care takeaway: the neck is not always the problem

This case is especially relevant to ED, ENT, and admitting teams because the initial differential was appropriately airway-centric: infection, angioedema, or another neck-space process.

That’s good medicine. But once CT, nasendoscopy, and the bedside exam fail to support infection or true angioedema, the next step is not to stop thinking. It’s to widen the differential.

Consider dermatomyositis when neck swelling is accompanied by:

- Proximal muscle weakness
- Dysphagia
- Unexplained rash or facial erythema
- Nailfold abnormalities
- Elevated CK or transaminases out of proportion to the “neck problem”
- Lack of response to standard angioedema therapy
- No ACE inhibitor, no culprit drug, no urticaria, no airway compromise pattern typical of allergic swelling

A CK is cheap, fast, and high-yield. In this case, it was the turning point.

## SAE1 and organ risk: what to watch for

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is associated with significant systemic disease burden. In the literature cited in the case report, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were both common, and malignancy was reported in a meaningful minority of patients.

For acute and inpatient clinicians, that means antibody status should shape your “what next?” thinking:

### 1. Swallowing and aspiration risk
This patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy with aspiration risk and required nasogastric feeding for a prolonged period.

If the patient has dermatomyositis and any swallowing complaint, assume aspiration risk until proven otherwise.

### 2. Pulmonary involvement
He had an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on PFTs and CT evidence of aspiration-related changes but no frank pulmonary fibrosis. In SAE1 disease, ILD is something to look for and follow, even if the first chest CT is not dramatic.

### 3. Malignancy screening
Dermatomyositis is a cancer-associated myositis until proven otherwise, and SAE1 positivity adds to the risk calculus. This patient was managed as high risk per IMACS-style risk framing and underwent CT imaging, PET, tumor markers, and planned GI endoscopy.

The practical point: antibody results don’t replace malignancy screening; they help you justify being systematic and persistent about it.

## Diagnosis: what made this case work

The diagnosis came together because someone stepped back from the initial label and asked: does this behave like angioedema?

What helped:

- Re-examining the skin carefully
- Noticing nailfold changes
- Sending CK early
- Recognizing dysphagia as part of the syndrome
- Using MRI, PET, and muscle biopsy to support inflammatory myopathy
- Getting a myositis antibody panel, which identified SAE1 positivity

That sequence matters. A swollen neck can create tunnel vision. Dermatomyositis doesn’t always announce itself with flamboyant rash and obvious proximal weakness. Sometimes it masquerades as a localized ENT or allergy problem.

## Treatment: aggressive up front, then steroid-sparing

Because of significant airway-adjacent muscle involvement and dysphagia, the patient was treated aggressively:

- 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily
- Prednisone taper
- IVIG loading, then monthly cycles
- Mycophenolate mofetil
- Rituximab
- Pneumocystis prophylaxis, with TMP-SMX stopped due to hemolytic anemia and nebulized pentamidine used instead

That’s very FOAM-worthy because it reflects a real-world principle: severe dermatomyositis often needs more than one immunomodulator. If swallowing is unsafe or disease is extensive, don’t under-treat it.

In this case, the response was excellent:
- CK normalized
- Cervical swelling resolved
- Strength recovered
- Dysphagia gradually improved
- Respiratory status stabilized

## Clinical pearls

- Not all “angioedema” is angioedema.
- Neck swelling plus rash, nailfold changes, proximal weakness, or dysphagia should trigger a myositis workup.
- CK is a fast discriminator in atypical soft tissue swelling.
- SAE1 dermatomyositis often has substantial cutaneous disease and can come with severe dysphagia.
- Consider ILD and malignancy risk early; antibody status should sharpen surveillance, not reassure you.
- Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is not a side note—it is a major driver of aspiration, nutrition issues, and morbidity.

## Take-home points

- **Dermatomyositis can present as pseudoangioedema, including prominent neck swelling.**
- **If swelling is atypical for histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, send CK and re-examine the skin and nailfolds.**
- **Anti-SAE1 positivity suggests a phenotype with significant cutaneous disease, frequent dysphagia, possible ILD, and meaningful malignancy risk.**
- **Early multidisciplinary care matters: ENT, immunology/rheumatology, speech pathology, nutrition, and oncology-style surveillance may all be needed.**
- **When dysphagia and airway-adjacent myositis are present, treat promptly and aggressively.**

## Bottom line

SAE1 dermatomyositis is one of those diagnoses that rewards pattern recognition. The antibody doesn’t just label the disease—it helps explain why the patient looks the way they do, what complications are likely next, and how hard you should push on treatment and surveillance.

So the next time the “angioedema” story feels wrong, don’t just ask, “What kind of swelling is this?”

Ask, “Could this be myositis wearing an allergy mask?”



# Neck Swelling, Dysphagia, and Aspiration: Airway Thinking Beyond Infection

## The case that should make you pause

A previously healthy man in his 40s showed up with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling. The initial instinct was understandable: deep neck space infection, then Ludwig’s angina, then maybe idiopathic angioedema. But the oxygen saturation was normal, he wasn’t toxic, and the neck swelling didn’t behave like classic histamine- or bradykinin-mediated edema.

The real clue was that this wasn’t “just swelling.” He also had proximal weakness, subtle skin and nailfold changes, and then—when the workup was broadened—severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration risk, a CK of 16,184 U/L, MRI evidence of active myositis, and muscle biopsy consistent with dermatomyositis. His “angioedema” was actually inflammatory myopathy involving the head and neck.

That’s the take-home here: airway complaints can be misleading when the problem is not an obstructed lumen, but a dysfunctional swallow.

## Why this matters in the ED and early inpatient course

Neck swelling plus dysphagia usually triggers an airway/infection reflex. That’s appropriate. You do need to rule out the dangerous stuff first:

- Deep neck space infection
- Ludwig’s angina
- Epiglottitis
- Angioedema
- SVC syndrome
- Abscess, cellulitis, sialadenitis

But inflammatory myopathy belongs on that list, especially when the patient looks “too well” for the degree of swelling, or when the swelling is accompanied by weakness, rash, periungual changes, or unexplained aspiration symptoms.

In this case, several features were quietly off:
- Afebrile, saturating 99% on room air
- No tachypnea
- Flexible nasendoscopy without a structural obstructive lesion
- Neck swelling that was erythematous, non-tender, and persistent
- Proximal weakness
- Severe dysphagia out of proportion to the apparent airway findings

That mismatch is what should make you think beyond infection.

## Oxygenation can be reassuring while swallowing is failing

One of the most important lessons from this case is that normal oxygen saturation does not rule out clinically significant upper aerodigestive dysfunction.

This patient had:
- Increased secretions and coughing during nasendoscopy
- A video fluoroscopic swallow study showing severe pharyngeal dysphagia
- Severe pyriform sinus residue
- Post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the vocal cords
- Evidence of aspiration-related lung changes on CT chest

So although he looked “stable” from a classic respiratory standpoint, he was actually at substantial risk of aspiration and nutritional compromise.

That distinction matters. A patient may have:
- No hypoxemia
- No obvious airway obstruction
- No stridor

…but still be unsafe to eat and unable to protect the airway during swallowing.

## What nasendoscopy and swallow studies added

The nasendoscopy was useful because it helped exclude a gross structural ENT cause. There was no major abnormality in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space. But the exam did reveal increased secretions and coughing, which should raise suspicion for pharyngeal dysfunction.

The swallow study then provided the clincher: this was not a “benign” neck swelling problem. It was a functional swallowing failure with penetration to the cords and severe residue. In inflammatory myopathy, that can be the difference between oral intake and a feeding tube.

Practical pearl: if a patient with neck swelling has coughing with secretions, wet voice, choking, recurrent chest changes, or unexplained inability to swallow, don’t stop at a reassuring oxygen saturation. Get a formal swallow evaluation early.

## Aspiration pneumonitis is often the downstream clue

The CT chest showed patchy peribronchial consolidation thought to be aspiration pneumonitis, plus multiple non-cavitating nodules. In this case, aspiration wasn’t just a consequence—it was part of the disease burden.

When dysphagia is severe, aspiration can show up before anyone has put together the neuromuscular picture. The chest imaging may be the first sign that swallowing is failing.

A useful mental model:
- Head/neck inflammation or myositis → pharyngeal dysfunction
- Pharyngeal dysfunction → silent or overt aspiration
- Aspiration → peribronchial opacities, consolidation, cough, secretion burden

If you see aspiration-type imaging in a patient with neck swelling and weakness, think beyond infection.

## Why CK matters in “angioedema” that doesn’t fit

In this patient, the immunology team appropriately noticed the phenotype wasn’t consistent with acquired or hereditary angioedema. The swelling was atypical, and there were muscle and skin clues. A CK was ordered and came back massively elevated.

That is a high-yield move.

When neck swelling is accompanied by:
- Proximal weakness
- Dysphagia
- Rash
- Nailfold changes
- Elevated AST/ALT out of proportion to liver symptoms
- Aspiration on swallow study

…a CK can quickly redirect the entire differential toward inflammatory myopathy.

This case also shows why transaminases can be misleading. AST and ALT were elevated here, but the source was muscle injury, not primary hepatocellular disease.

## Dermatomyositis can present where the airway meets the swallow

Dermatomyositis classically makes us think of:
- Proximal muscle weakness
- Gottron’s papules
- Heliotrope rash
- Shawl sign
- Interstitial lung disease
- Dysphagia

But this case reminds us it can also present with prominent cervical soft tissue swelling that mimics angioedema or an ENT infection.

The patient had anti-SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, and his phenotype included:
- Severe cervical swelling
- Dysphagia
- Myositis on MRI
- Positive muscle biopsy with perifascicular changes and C5b-9 deposition
- Cutaneous and nailfold changes, albeit subtle

In other words: the airway issue was really a systemic autoimmune muscle disease announcing itself in the neck.

## When severe dysphagia should trigger urgent immunosuppression

This is the key management point. In inflammatory myopathy, dysphagia is not a nuisance symptom—it can be a marker of severe disease and a driver of morbidity through aspiration, malnutrition, and inability to take oral medications.

In this case, the patient required:
- 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily
- High-dose oral prednisone taper
- IVIG
- Mycophenolate mofetil
- Rituximab

That level of treatment reflects the severity of head and neck involvement and the risk of ongoing aspiration.

The practical implication is straightforward: if dysphagia is severe, progressive, and tied to active inflammatory myopathy, don’t wait for perfect certainty before escalating therapy. You still need reasonable exclusion of infection and mechanical obstruction, but once the pattern fits, the threshold for urgent immunosuppression should be low.

## What to watch for at the bedside

A few bedside clues in this case were more important than any single test:

- Facial erythema that included the nasolabial folds
- Erythematous, swollen neck that was non-tender
- Subtle periungual erythema and nailfold microhemorrhages
- Reduced hip flexor power
- Increased secretions/cough during nasendoscopy

These findings are easy to miss if you anchor on infection or angioedema. A careful skin and neuromuscular exam can completely change the diagnostic direction.

## Take-home points

- Not all neck swelling is infection or angioedema—think inflammatory myopathy when the story doesn’t fit.
- Normal oxygenation does not exclude serious swallowing dysfunction or aspiration risk.
- Nasendoscopy helps exclude structural obstruction, but formal swallow study is often what reveals the true problem.
- CK is a high-yield test in unexplained neck swelling with weakness, rash, or dysphagia.
- Severe dysphagia in dermatomyositis should prompt urgent treatment because aspiration and malnutrition can become the immediate threats.
- A “well-looking” airway complaint can still be a major immunologic emergency.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that the airway is not just about patency—it’s about function. Inflammatory myopathy can present as neck swelling with an apparently reassuring respiratory exam, while the real danger is pharyngeal failure, aspiration, and delayed diagnosis. If the swelling is atypical and the patient has any hint of weakness or skin change, widen the frame early and check a CK.



# Treating Severe Dermatomyositis Up Front: Steroids, IVIG, Rituximab, and a Steroid-Sparing Strategy

## Why this case is worth pausing on

This patient didn’t walk in looking like “classic dermatomyositis.” He came in with progressive neck swelling and was first worked up for Ludwig’s angina and angioedema. But once the dust settled, the real problem was much bigger than isolated soft tissue edema: he had moderate-to-severe dermatomyositis with profound inflammatory involvement of the neck, dysphagia, proximal myositis, and aspiration-related lung findings.

That’s the management lesson here: when dermatomyositis is severe, especially with swallowing compromise and extensive inflammatory burden, waiting for a slow, stepwise outpatient regimen may be the wrong move. In this case, the team treated aggressively up front with pulse steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab—and the patient improved.

## Why aggressive therapy was reasonable

The decision to treat this patient as a severe inflammatory myopathy wasn’t based on one lab value alone. It was the full picture:

- Marked cervical soft tissue swelling
- Severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy
- Creatine kinase 16,184 U/L
- MRI evidence of active myositis
- PET showing diffuse muscle inflammation
- Muscle biopsy consistent with dermatomyositis
- SAE1 antibody positivity

This was not mild skin-limited disease. He had significant muscle involvement and functional compromise, including inability to safely swallow. That raises the stakes quickly because the immediate threats are not just weakness and discomfort, but aspiration, malnutrition, and airway-adjacent inflammatory swelling.

## Pulse methylprednisolone: the usual first move when disease is severe

For hospitalized dermatomyositis with major organ involvement, high-dose glucocorticoids remain the backbone of initial therapy. This patient received 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily, then transitioned to oral prednisone starting at 60 mg daily with a slow taper.

Why pulse steroids here?

- Rapid anti-inflammatory effect when swallowing and airway-adjacent tissues are involved
- Helpful when you need disease control before slower steroid-sparing agents kick in
- Appropriate for severe myositis with major CK elevation and systemic involvement

A key practical point: steroids work fast, but they are not a long-term solution. In inflammatory myopathy, they are often the bridge—not the destination. That’s why early planning for steroid-sparing therapy matters.

## IVIG: a useful adjunct when you need faster functional recovery

IVIG was a smart addition here. The patient got loading IVIG at 2 g/kg over 5 days, then monthly cycles afterward.

Why IVIG makes sense in severe dermatomyositis:

- Can help when there is prominent dysphagia
- Often used as adjunctive therapy in severe or refractory disease
- Useful when you want additional immunomodulation without further increasing infection risk as much as stacking multiple cytotoxic agents
- Often favored when rapid improvement is needed while other agents take effect

This case is a good reminder that IVIG is not just “salvage.” In the right patient, it can be part of the initial strategy, particularly when swallowing dysfunction and extensive muscle inflammation are present.

## Mycophenolate mofetil: a practical steroid-sparing choice

The team started mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily. That’s a very defensible steroid-sparing choice in dermatomyositis, especially when there are pulmonary considerations and a need for longer-term control.

Why mycophenolate was reasonable here:

- Established use as a steroid-sparing agent in dermatomyositis
- Generally well tolerated
- Useful when interstitial lung disease is a concern or when clinicians want to avoid methotrexate
- Oral daily regimen may be more acceptable for some patients

The case report notes methotrexate was avoided because of abnormal lung function testing and concern about lung disease/pulmonary fibrosis. That’s a common real-world consideration. In a patient with restrictive physiology and pulmonary abnormalities, mycophenolate is often attractive.

## Rituximab: escalation for severe or high-risk disease

He also received two doses of rituximab 1000 mg IV two weeks apart. In a severe case like this, that escalation makes sense as part of a multi-agent regimen aimed at durable disease control.

Why rituximab can be considered:

- Useful in refractory or severe inflammatory myopathy
- May be chosen when disease burden is high and clinicians want a deeper steroid-sparing strategy
- Can be paired with steroids and IVIG when the initial presentation is aggressive

This case isn’t proving rituximab is mandatory in all severe dermatomyositis. It’s showing that in a patient with extensive myositis, dysphagia, and major soft tissue inflammation, early combination therapy can be justified.

## Monitoring response: don’t just watch the CK

The patient improved clinically over weeks, with normalization of CK and recovery of proximal strength. That’s reassuring, but in dermatomyositis you want to follow more than one marker.

Useful targets for monitoring include:

- Strength and function
- Swallowing safety
- CK and other muscle enzymes
- Respiratory symptoms and pulmonary function
- Imaging when lung or aspiration-related disease is part of the picture
- Toxicities from immunosuppression

In this case, improvement was seen in strength, cervical swelling, dysphagia, and follow-up pulmonary findings. That’s the kind of multi-domain response you want.

## Don’t forget the infection-prevention package

High-dose steroids plus multiple immunosuppressants means opportunistic infection prophylaxis needs attention.

He was started on trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis, but it caused hemolytic anemia despite no G6PD deficiency, so it was stopped. He then tolerated nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks.

That’s an important practical point: if TMP-SMX isn’t tolerated, you still need a backup plan.

Common PJP prophylaxis alternatives include:

- Nebulized pentamidine
- Atovaquone
- Dapsone if appropriate and G6PD status allows

Choice depends on patient factors, local practice, and drug tolerance. The key is not to let prophylaxis become an afterthought when immunosuppression is intense.

## Practical takeaways from this case

- Severe dermatomyositis can present with dramatic neck swelling and be mistaken for angioedema or deep neck infection.
- Dysphagia and extensive inflammatory swelling should push you toward early aggressive treatment.
- Pulse methylprednisolone is reasonable when there is major functional compromise.
- IVIG is particularly useful when swallowing is impaired or rapid immunomodulation is needed.
- Mycophenolate is a sensible steroid-sparing agent, especially when lung involvement is a concern.
- Rituximab can be part of upfront combination therapy in severe disease.
- Always pair immunosuppression with infection prophylaxis planning.
- If TMP-SMX is not tolerated, pivot to an alternative PJP prophylaxis strategy rather than abandoning prophylaxis altogether.

## The bigger lesson

This case is a good reminder that dermatomyositis is not just a rash and some proximal weakness. In severe presentations, it can become a multisystem, aspiration-prone, airway-adjacent inflammatory disease that deserves early recognition and decisive treatment.

The management win here wasn’t a single magic drug. It was recognizing severity early, using combination immunosuppression thoughtfully, and building in supportive care and prophylaxis from the start.



# Cancer Screening in Dermatomyositis: How Much Is Enough, and for Whom?

## Why this case should make you pause

A man in his 40s shows up with “neck swelling,” gets worked up for Ludwig’s angina and angioedema, and only later is found to have dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia, marked CK elevation, and anti-SAE1 positivity. That’s already a good reminder that dermatomyositis can wear strange disguises.

But the part that matters for disposition, prognosis, and downstream management is this: dermatomyositis is not just a muscle and skin disease. It can be a cancer signal.

So the real question in this case becomes: **how aggressively should we screen for malignancy, and how long should we keep looking?**

## Dermatomyositis and malignancy: why we worry

Dermatomyositis is one of the classic idiopathic inflammatory myopathies linked to malignancy. The association is strongest around the time of diagnosis, which is why the initial workup matters so much. The goal is not to “rule out every cancer forever,” but to identify occult malignancy early enough to change outcomes without drowning patients in low-yield testing.

This patient had several features that push him into a higher-risk bucket:

- Dermatomyositis itself
- Dysphagia
- Male sex
- Anti-SAE1 antibody positivity

In the case report, the authors note that anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis has reported malignancy in about **18%** of cases in one large literature review. That’s not deterministic, but it is enough to justify a serious cancer search.

## Risk-stratified screening: the key idea

Modern myositis cancer screening is increasingly **risk-stratified** rather than “one size fits all.” That’s important because not every patient with dermatomyositis needs the same intensity of testing.

In broad terms:

- **Higher-risk patients** warrant more comprehensive baseline screening
- **Lower-risk patients** may need more selective evaluation guided by age, symptoms, exam, labs, and local practice
- **Ongoing surveillance** should be individualized rather than automatic endless imaging

This case sits firmly on the “high-risk” end of the spectrum.

## What was done in this case

The malignancy evaluation was appropriately broad:

- CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis
- PET scan
- Tumor markers: CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA
- Planned gastroscopy and colonoscopy
- Ongoing follow-up with serial imaging and clinical review

That approach reflects a practical reality: in dermatomyositis, you often need to search in more than one place, because the associated cancer may be occult and clinically silent.

The CT chest also showed nodules and aspiration-related changes, which underscores another point: **abnormal imaging in dermatomyositis is not always cancer, but it does not let you off the hook either**. PET helped show there was no other metabolically active malignancy.

## What baseline screening should try to cover

For higher-risk dermatomyositis, baseline screening usually aims to cover the common cancer sites seen in adult myositis-associated malignancy:

- Lung
- Gastrointestinal tract
- Genitourinary tract
- Breast/gynecologic sites in appropriate patients
- Hematologic malignancy when suggested clinically

In practice, the workup often includes:

- Careful history and exam, including constitutional symptoms
- Age- and sex-appropriate routine cancer screening
- CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or equivalent cross-sectional imaging
- Additional targeted tests if symptoms or risk factors point somewhere specific
- Endoscopic evaluation when suspicion remains or risk is high

In this case, the severe dysphagia, abnormal chest findings, and high-risk myositis phenotype all justified a more exhaustive baseline assessment.

## PET scan: useful, but not magic

FDG-PET can be very helpful when you’re dealing with a patient at high risk for occult malignancy, especially if conventional imaging is unrevealing or if you want one study to survey multiple regions.

But PET has limitations:

- False positives from inflammation are common
- Myositis itself can be intensely FDG-avid
- A negative PET does not eliminate the need for age-appropriate screening and follow-up

In this case, PET showed diffuse muscle uptake consistent with active myositis and no separate metabolically active cancer. That was reassuring, but not a “done forever” result.

## Tumor markers: why they rarely solve the problem

This case included normal CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, and PSA. That’s nice to have, but it’s easy to overinterpret tumor markers.

Clinical pearl: **normal tumor markers do not rule out malignancy in dermatomyositis**.

They may support the broader picture, but they are not sensitive enough to stand alone as a screening strategy. They are best thought of as adjuncts, not gatekeepers.

## Why dysphagia matters so much

Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is more than a quality-of-life issue. It is also a **risk marker**.

In the case report, dysphagia contributed to classifying the patient as high-risk under the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group guidance. That aligns with the general idea that patients with more severe systemic involvement deserve more aggressive cancer evaluation.

Dysphagia can also muddy the waters clinically:

- It may be mistaken for ENT pathology
- It may prompt workup for structural lesions, infection, or neuromuscular disease
- It may delay recognition of the underlying inflammatory myopathy

But once dermatomyositis is on the table, dysphagia should raise the stakes for malignancy screening.

## How long should surveillance continue?

This is where practice becomes less tidy.

The highest cancer risk is near the time of dermatomyositis diagnosis, but some clinicians continue surveillance beyond the initial workup because the association is not perfectly front-loaded and because initial studies can miss small or early cancers.

What this case shows is a reasonable real-world approach:

- Broad baseline screen
- Ongoing symptom review and examination
- Repeat labs and imaging based on risk and evolution
- Age-appropriate endoscopy and other tests when indicated

There is no universal “magic duration” that fits every patient. The intensity and duration of follow-up should reflect:

- Baseline risk profile
- Myositis subtype and antibody status
- Clinical trajectory
- New symptoms or exam findings
- Local access and resources

## Practical take-home points

- **Dermatomyositis is a cancer-associated autoimmune disease until proven otherwise.**
- **Dysphagia, male sex, and anti-SAE1 positivity push risk higher.**
- **Baseline malignancy screening should be broad in high-risk patients and include cross-sectional imaging plus age-appropriate cancer evaluation.**
- **PET can be helpful, but inflammation can confound interpretation.**
- **Tumor markers are adjuncts, not rule-out tests.**
- **Surveillance should be individualized; the first workup is crucial, but follow-up still matters.**

## Bottom line

This case is a reminder that the diagnostic job in dermatomyositis is not just “confirm the myositis.” You also have to ask whether the myositis is a paraneoplastic signal.

For this patient, the combination of dermatomyositis, dysphagia, male sex, and anti-SAE1 positivity justified a thorough malignancy search with CT, PET, tumor markers, and planned endoscopy. That’s the kind of risk-stratified approach that balances vigilance with restraint.

In dermatomyositis, cancer screening is not about doing everything for everyone. It’s about doing **the right amount for the right patient**—and in high-risk cases like this one, that amount is substantial.
