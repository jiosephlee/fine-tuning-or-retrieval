Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] How do you interpret the combination of severe creatine kinase elevation, AST/ALT elevation, elevated LDH, normal renal function, and negative urine myoglobin in the context of inflammatory myopathy?

In this setting, the laboratory pattern is most consistent with **active skeletal muscle inflammation and injury from inflammatory myopathy**, not primary liver disease or pigment nephropathy.

The key abnormality is the **marked creatine kinase elevation**: CK is the most sensitive routine marker of myofiber necrosis, and a level in the tens of thousands, as in this case, strongly supports substantial muscle involvement. The accompanying **AST and ALT elevation should not be assumed to be hepatic**. Both transaminases can rise in myositis because they are present in skeletal muscle; in inflammatory myopathy, **AST often exceeds ALT**, as it did here. **LDH** is also a nonspecific marker of tissue injury and commonly rises with active myositis.

The **normal renal function and negative urine myoglobin** are reassuring, but they do **not negate the diagnosis**. They simply indicate that, despite severe muscle breakdown, the patient had not developed clinically significant myoglobinuria or acute kidney injury. In inflammatory myopathy, you can have profound CK elevation with preserved kidney function, especially when the process is recognized early and hydration is maintained.

So, taken together, this constellation—**very high CK, muscle-pattern transaminase elevation, elevated LDH, preserved creatinine, and absent myoglobinuria**—points to **severe active dermatomyositis with muscle necrosis**, rather than isolated hepatitis or established rhabdomyolysis with renal injury. In the reported case, this biochemical profile appropriately prompted further evaluation and was confirmed by MRI, biopsy, and myositis-specific serology.
