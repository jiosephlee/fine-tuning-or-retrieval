Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient presenting with progressive neck swelling initially thought to be angioedema, what clinical features in this case argued against histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and prompted consideration of dermatomyositis instead?

Several features made this presentation atypical for either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis.

First, the swelling was **subacute and progressive over 3 weeks**, not the abrupt, episodic course usually seen with angioedema. Second, it was accompanied by **objective inflammatory and myopathic clues**: **proximal hip flexor weakness**, **mild myalgia**, and later **severe dysphagia**. Third, on examination he had **erythematous, non-tender neck swelling**, **facial erythema involving the nasolabial folds**, and **subtle periungual/nailfold changes**—findings that are much more suggestive of connective tissue disease than angioedema. The **markedly elevated creatine kinase** level and transaminases further pointed to active muscle injury. Finally, the standard angioedema workup was unrevealing: he had **no ACE inhibitor or other typical drug trigger**, **no personal or family history**, and **normal C3/C4 and normal C1 esterase function/level**, arguing strongly against bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

In short, the combination of **persistent “angioedema-like” neck swelling plus rash, nailfold changes, proximal weakness, dysphagia, and very high CK** is the key clue that the edema was not angioedema at all, but **pseudoangioedema from dermatomyositis**, as this case ultimately confirmed.
