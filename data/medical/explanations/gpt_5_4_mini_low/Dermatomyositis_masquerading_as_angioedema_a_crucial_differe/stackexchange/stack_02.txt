Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] What bedside skin and nail findings are particularly helpful in suggesting dermatomyositis when classic features such as Gottron’s papules or a heliotrope rash are absent?

When Gottron’s papules and a heliotrope rash are absent, the most helpful bedside clues are **subtle periungual and photodistributed skin changes**, especially **periungual erythema**, **nailfold capillary abnormalities** on inspection or capillaroscopy, and sometimes a **violaceous rash over the thighs/hips or trunk**.

In this case, the diagnosis was suggested not by classic facial or hand lesions, but by **proximal nailfold microhemorrhages with avascular areas**, **periungual erythema**, and a **rash on the lateral thigh** consistent with the **Holster sign**. These findings are important because dermatomyositis often produces **microangiopathic injury at the nailfolds**, so careful examination of the cuticles and periungual skin can reveal disease even when the textbook rashes are absent. In practice, if you see unexplained **periungual erythema, dilated or disrupted nailfold capillaries, microhemorrhages, or capillary dropout**, you should strongly consider dermatomyositis and check a **CK** promptly.
