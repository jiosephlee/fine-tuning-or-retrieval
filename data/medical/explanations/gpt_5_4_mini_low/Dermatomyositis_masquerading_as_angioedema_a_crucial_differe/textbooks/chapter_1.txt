Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Dermatomyositis in Context: Core Disease Concepts and the Significance of Pseudoangioedema

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy in which immune-mediated injury targets skeletal muscle and skin, and in some patients also involves the lungs, swallowing musculature, joints, gastrointestinal tract, and heart. It is uncommon, with an estimated incidence of roughly 10 cases per million population, and it has a bimodal age distribution, affecting children and adults most often in midlife. In clinical practice, the diagnosis is suggested by a combination of characteristic rash, proximal muscle weakness, elevated muscle enzymes, and supportive serology, imaging, or biopsy findings. The case described here fits that framework, but it is notable because the patient’s earliest dominant symptom was neck swelling rather than the more familiar presentation of rash and weakness.

The pathophysiology of dermatomyositis is classically centered on a microangiopathic process. Complement activation injures endomysial capillaries, leading to ischemia, particularly in perifascicular muscle fibers. This pattern explains the hallmark biopsy findings of perifascicular atrophy and complement deposition, often demonstrated by membrane attack complex staining on capillaries. Muscle injury then produces weakness, myalgia, and marked elevation of creatine kinase, as seen in this patient. When the inflammatory process is severe, edema can develop in the involved soft tissues, including the subcutaneous tissues of the face and neck, which creates a misleading appearance of angioedema.

The skin findings of dermatomyositis are often the earliest clues if they are actively sought. Classic features include Gottron’s papules over the metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints, a heliotrope rash around the eyelids, shawl sign over the shoulders and upper back, V-neck sign on the anterior chest, holster sign over the lateral thighs, and periungual erythema or telangiectatic nailfold changes. These findings may be subtle, incomplete, or absent early in disease, which is why careful full-skin examination matters. In this case, the absence of prominent Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash initially made the diagnosis less obvious, but nailfold and facial changes, together with proximal weakness, provided important diagnostic hints.

Swallowing difficulty and respiratory compromise are clinically important manifestations of inflammatory myopathy. Dysphagia usually reflects weakness and incoordination of the oropharyngeal and upper esophageal striated muscles, which can lead to pharyngeal residue, aspiration, and poor oral intake. Respiratory symptoms may arise from interstitial lung disease, respiratory muscle weakness, or aspiration-related lung injury secondary to dysphagia. The patient in this report had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration changes on chest imaging, illustrating how muscle involvement can have downstream pulmonary consequences even without primary fibrotic lung disease.

The neck swelling in this case represents pseudoangioedema, an uncommon manifestation of dermatomyositis in which inflammatory edema mimics allergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. This is clinically important because true angioedema is managed through a different diagnostic and therapeutic pathway, whereas inflammatory edema from dermatomyositis requires recognition of the underlying myositis and prompt immunosuppression. Pseudoangioedema may be especially confusing when it presents in the face or neck, where it can resemble cellulitis, deep neck space infection, or idiopathic angioedema. The proposed mechanism is not mast-cell degranulation or C1-esterase dysfunction, but rather inflammatory swelling related to active myositis and increased vascular permeability in involved tissues. In a patient with neck edema plus proximal weakness, skin changes, or dysphagia, a creatine kinase level is an essential and often decisive test.

Myositis-specific autoantibodies are useful because they help define phenotype, predict organ involvement, and support diagnostic confidence. Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme, a component of the sumoylation pathway. SAE1-positive dermatomyositis often has prominent skin disease and can be associated with dysphagia and interstitial lung disease; reported malignancy risk is also clinically relevant. In this case, the positive SAE1 antibody helped unify the presentation of inflammatory neck swelling, rash, dysphagia, and myositis. Importantly, the case also illustrates why dermatomyositis remains an important malignancy-associated syndrome: once diagnosed, patients require systematic cancer screening based on risk features, not just symptom-driven evaluation.
