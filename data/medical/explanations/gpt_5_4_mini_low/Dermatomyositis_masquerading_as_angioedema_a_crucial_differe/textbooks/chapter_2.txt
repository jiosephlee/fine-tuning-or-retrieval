Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## Initial Presentation and Diagnostic Reframing: From Neck Swelling to Suspected Inflammatory Myopathy

The patient first presented with a subacute, 3-week history of progressive neck swelling accompanied by mild myalgia and proximal weakness, particularly affecting the hip flexors. In the emergency setting, this combination can be alarming because neck swelling raises immediate concern for conditions that threaten the airway, while new weakness suggests a systemic process rather than a purely local one. At this stage, the clinical problem was not yet recognized as a myopathy; instead, the dominant question was whether the neck swelling represented infection, angioedema, or another obstructive process.

Ludwig’s angina and other deep neck space infections were reasonable early considerations because the CT neck showed diffuse soft tissue oedema involving the strap muscles with inflammatory change extending into the retropharyngeal space. Imaging of this type can be seen in serious infection, and in a patient with progressive submandibular or cervical swelling, it appropriately triggers urgent evaluation. However, radiology alone does not establish infection. The rest of the clinical picture was less convincing: he was afebrile, not tachypnoeic, and maintaining normal oxygen saturation, which argued against a fulminant septic process. There was also no obvious focal collection or suppuration to suggest a drainable abscess.

ENT examination helped further narrow the differential. Flexible nasendoscopy showed no major structural abnormality in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, making a mechanical upper airway lesion less likely. The increased secretions and coughing during the examination were important because they hinted at impaired swallowing and aspiration risk rather than a fixed anatomic obstruction. In other words, the airway looked structurally intact, but there was evidence that something was functionally wrong with bulbar swallowing.

When the patient was reviewed by immunology for suspected angioedema, the presentation was still atypical for either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated disease. Angioedema usually causes abrupt, episodic swelling and is often associated with urticaria, medication triggers, or a personal/family history of similar events. This patient instead had a progressive 3-week course, prominent neck erythema and oedema, and no history of ACE inhibitor, DPP-4 inhibitor, NSAID, or other relevant drug exposure. He also lacked a family history of angioedema. Those negative historical features matter because they make common forms of angioedema less likely and should prompt clinicians to search for alternative explanations.

The key diagnostic shift came from the bedside examination. Careful review revealed generalized facial erythema, periungual changes, nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, and a subtle rash on the thigh. These findings are easy to miss if attention is focused only on the swollen neck, but they are classic clues to inflammatory myopathy, particularly dermatomyositis. The combination of skin changes and proximal hip flexor weakness should immediately raise suspicion for a muscle disease rather than isolated edema. In dermatomyositis, swelling may reflect inflammatory involvement of the subcutaneous tissues and muscles, and the neck can appear edematous enough to mimic angioedema or deep neck infection.

At that point, checking a creatine kinase level was the pivotal move. In unexplained soft tissue swelling, especially when there are clues to muscle involvement or characteristic skin changes, CK provides a fast and highly informative screen for myositis. In this case it was markedly elevated, confirming that the process was not isolated neck edema but part of a broader inflammatory muscle disease. The later diagnosis of dermatomyositis was therefore not made by a single test, but by integrating the progressive course, atypical “angioedema” history, normal ENT anatomy, subtle dermatomyositis skin signs, and the unexpected discovery of severe myositis biochemistry.
