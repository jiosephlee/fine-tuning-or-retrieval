Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Treating Severe Dermatomyositis Up Front: Steroids, IVIG, Rituximab, and a Steroid-Sparing Strategy

## Why this case is worth pausing on

This patient didn’t walk in looking like “classic dermatomyositis.” He came in with progressive neck swelling and was first worked up for Ludwig’s angina and angioedema. But once the dust settled, the real problem was much bigger than isolated soft tissue edema: he had moderate-to-severe dermatomyositis with profound inflammatory involvement of the neck, dysphagia, proximal myositis, and aspiration-related lung findings.

That’s the management lesson here: when dermatomyositis is severe, especially with swallowing compromise and extensive inflammatory burden, waiting for a slow, stepwise outpatient regimen may be the wrong move. In this case, the team treated aggressively up front with pulse steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab—and the patient improved.

## Why aggressive therapy was reasonable

The decision to treat this patient as a severe inflammatory myopathy wasn’t based on one lab value alone. It was the full picture:

- Marked cervical soft tissue swelling
- Severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy
- Creatine kinase 16,184 U/L
- MRI evidence of active myositis
- PET showing diffuse muscle inflammation
- Muscle biopsy consistent with dermatomyositis
- SAE1 antibody positivity

This was not mild skin-limited disease. He had significant muscle involvement and functional compromise, including inability to safely swallow. That raises the stakes quickly because the immediate threats are not just weakness and discomfort, but aspiration, malnutrition, and airway-adjacent inflammatory swelling.

## Pulse methylprednisolone: the usual first move when disease is severe

For hospitalized dermatomyositis with major organ involvement, high-dose glucocorticoids remain the backbone of initial therapy. This patient received 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily, then transitioned to oral prednisone starting at 60 mg daily with a slow taper.

Why pulse steroids here?

- Rapid anti-inflammatory effect when swallowing and airway-adjacent tissues are involved
- Helpful when you need disease control before slower steroid-sparing agents kick in
- Appropriate for severe myositis with major CK elevation and systemic involvement

A key practical point: steroids work fast, but they are not a long-term solution. In inflammatory myopathy, they are often the bridge—not the destination. That’s why early planning for steroid-sparing therapy matters.

## IVIG: a useful adjunct when you need faster functional recovery

IVIG was a smart addition here. The patient got loading IVIG at 2 g/kg over 5 days, then monthly cycles afterward.

Why IVIG makes sense in severe dermatomyositis:

- Can help when there is prominent dysphagia
- Often used as adjunctive therapy in severe or refractory disease
- Useful when you want additional immunomodulation without further increasing infection risk as much as stacking multiple cytotoxic agents
- Often favored when rapid improvement is needed while other agents take effect

This case is a good reminder that IVIG is not just “salvage.” In the right patient, it can be part of the initial strategy, particularly when swallowing dysfunction and extensive muscle inflammation are present.

## Mycophenolate mofetil: a practical steroid-sparing choice

The team started mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily. That’s a very defensible steroid-sparing choice in dermatomyositis, especially when there are pulmonary considerations and a need for longer-term control.

Why mycophenolate was reasonable here:

- Established use as a steroid-sparing agent in dermatomyositis
- Generally well tolerated
- Useful when interstitial lung disease is a concern or when clinicians want to avoid methotrexate
- Oral daily regimen may be more acceptable for some patients

The case report notes methotrexate was avoided because of abnormal lung function testing and concern about lung disease/pulmonary fibrosis. That’s a common real-world consideration. In a patient with restrictive physiology and pulmonary abnormalities, mycophenolate is often attractive.

## Rituximab: escalation for severe or high-risk disease

He also received two doses of rituximab 1000 mg IV two weeks apart. In a severe case like this, that escalation makes sense as part of a multi-agent regimen aimed at durable disease control.

Why rituximab can be considered:

- Useful in refractory or severe inflammatory myopathy
- May be chosen when disease burden is high and clinicians want a deeper steroid-sparing strategy
- Can be paired with steroids and IVIG when the initial presentation is aggressive

This case isn’t proving rituximab is mandatory in all severe dermatomyositis. It’s showing that in a patient with extensive myositis, dysphagia, and major soft tissue inflammation, early combination therapy can be justified.

## Monitoring response: don’t just watch the CK

The patient improved clinically over weeks, with normalization of CK and recovery of proximal strength. That’s reassuring, but in dermatomyositis you want to follow more than one marker.

Useful targets for monitoring include:

- Strength and function
- Swallowing safety
- CK and other muscle enzymes
- Respiratory symptoms and pulmonary function
- Imaging when lung or aspiration-related disease is part of the picture
- Toxicities from immunosuppression

In this case, improvement was seen in strength, cervical swelling, dysphagia, and follow-up pulmonary findings. That’s the kind of multi-domain response you want.

## Don’t forget the infection-prevention package

High-dose steroids plus multiple immunosuppressants means opportunistic infection prophylaxis needs attention.

He was started on trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis, but it caused hemolytic anemia despite no G6PD deficiency, so it was stopped. He then tolerated nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks.

That’s an important practical point: if TMP-SMX isn’t tolerated, you still need a backup plan.

Common PJP prophylaxis alternatives include:

- Nebulized pentamidine
- Atovaquone
- Dapsone if appropriate and G6PD status allows

Choice depends on patient factors, local practice, and drug tolerance. The key is not to let prophylaxis become an afterthought when immunosuppression is intense.

## Practical takeaways from this case

- Severe dermatomyositis can present with dramatic neck swelling and be mistaken for angioedema or deep neck infection.
- Dysphagia and extensive inflammatory swelling should push you toward early aggressive treatment.
- Pulse methylprednisolone is reasonable when there is major functional compromise.
- IVIG is particularly useful when swallowing is impaired or rapid immunomodulation is needed.
- Mycophenolate is a sensible steroid-sparing agent, especially when lung involvement is a concern.
- Rituximab can be part of upfront combination therapy in severe disease.
- Always pair immunosuppression with infection prophylaxis planning.
- If TMP-SMX is not tolerated, pivot to an alternative PJP prophylaxis strategy rather than abandoning prophylaxis altogether.

## The bigger lesson

This case is a good reminder that dermatomyositis is not just a rash and some proximal weakness. In severe presentations, it can become a multisystem, aspiration-prone, airway-adjacent inflammatory disease that deserves early recognition and decisive treatment.

The management win here wasn’t a single magic drug. It was recognizing severity early, using combination immunosuppression thoughtfully, and building in supportive care and prophylaxis from the start.
