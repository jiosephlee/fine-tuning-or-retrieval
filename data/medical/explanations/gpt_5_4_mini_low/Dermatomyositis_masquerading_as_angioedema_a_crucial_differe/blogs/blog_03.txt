Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# SAE1 Dermatomyositis: Why This Antibody Matters at the Front Door

## The case that should make you pause

A man in his 40s walked into the ED with what looked, at first glance, like a neck emergency: 3 weeks of progressive cervical swelling, CT evidence of diffuse neck edema, elevated inflammatory markers, and enough airway-adjacent anatomy on the scan to make Ludwig’s angina or deep neck infection feel very plausible.

But the story never quite fit.

He was afebrile, oxygenating well, and didn’t look septic. ENT scope showed no obstructing lesion or deep space catastrophe. Then the physical exam started to give the game away: facial erythema that included the nasolabial folds, subtle periungual erythema/microhemorrhages, a rash on the thigh, and mild proximal hip flexor weakness. A CK was sent because the “angioedema” felt off.

It came back at 16,184 U/L.

This was dermatomyositis presenting as pseudoangioedema, ultimately linked to anti-SAE1 antibodies. The neck swelling wasn’t histamine-driven, and it wasn’t bradykinin-driven. It was inflammatory muscle disease with dramatic soft tissue edema.

That’s the lesson: when “angioedema” doesn’t behave like angioedema, think beyond the allergy bucket.

## Why anti-SAE1 matters

Anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis is uncommon, but it has a recognizable clinical flavor.

In broad strokes, this phenotype tends to feature:

- Prominent cutaneous disease
- Dysphagia, sometimes severe
- Myositis that may be clinically subtle early on
- Possible interstitial lung disease or other pulmonary involvement
- Non-trivial malignancy risk

In the case above, the diagnosis was confirmed by a positive SAE1 antibody, MRI evidence of active myositis, PET scan uptake in multiple muscle groups, and muscle biopsy showing perifascicular pathology with C5b-9 deposition—classic dermatomyositis territory.

The antibody is not just a lab curiosity. It helps frame what to look for next, how aggressively to screen, and how closely to follow.

## The phenotype: don’t wait for textbook skin

Dermatomyositis is famous for Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash, but real patients don’t always read the textbook. In this case, those hallmark findings were absent. Instead, the clues were more subtle:

- Facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds
- Periungual erythema and nailfold microvascular changes
- A rash on the thigh
- Proximal weakness
- Dysphagia
- Marked CK elevation

That combination should trigger a myositis workup, even if the patient arrived under a completely different label.

A few practical pearls:

- Neck swelling can be a dermatomyositis presentation.
- Pseudoangioedema is rare, but it happens.
- Dysphagia may be a major clue and may be underreported unless you ask directly.
- Nailfold capillary changes are often more helpful than the “classic” rash when skin findings are subtle.

## Acute care takeaway: the neck is not always the problem

This case is especially relevant to ED, ENT, and admitting teams because the initial differential was appropriately airway-centric: infection, angioedema, or another neck-space process.

That’s good medicine. But once CT, nasendoscopy, and the bedside exam fail to support infection or true angioedema, the next step is not to stop thinking. It’s to widen the differential.

Consider dermatomyositis when neck swelling is accompanied by:

- Proximal muscle weakness
- Dysphagia
- Unexplained rash or facial erythema
- Nailfold abnormalities
- Elevated CK or transaminases out of proportion to the “neck problem”
- Lack of response to standard angioedema therapy
- No ACE inhibitor, no culprit drug, no urticaria, no airway compromise pattern typical of allergic swelling

A CK is cheap, fast, and high-yield. In this case, it was the turning point.

## SAE1 and organ risk: what to watch for

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is associated with significant systemic disease burden. In the literature cited in the case report, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were both common, and malignancy was reported in a meaningful minority of patients.

For acute and inpatient clinicians, that means antibody status should shape your “what next?” thinking:

### 1. Swallowing and aspiration risk
This patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy with aspiration risk and required nasogastric feeding for a prolonged period.

If the patient has dermatomyositis and any swallowing complaint, assume aspiration risk until proven otherwise.

### 2. Pulmonary involvement
He had an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on PFTs and CT evidence of aspiration-related changes but no frank pulmonary fibrosis. In SAE1 disease, ILD is something to look for and follow, even if the first chest CT is not dramatic.

### 3. Malignancy screening
Dermatomyositis is a cancer-associated myositis until proven otherwise, and SAE1 positivity adds to the risk calculus. This patient was managed as high risk per IMACS-style risk framing and underwent CT imaging, PET, tumor markers, and planned GI endoscopy.

The practical point: antibody results don’t replace malignancy screening; they help you justify being systematic and persistent about it.

## Diagnosis: what made this case work

The diagnosis came together because someone stepped back from the initial label and asked: does this behave like angioedema?

What helped:

- Re-examining the skin carefully
- Noticing nailfold changes
- Sending CK early
- Recognizing dysphagia as part of the syndrome
- Using MRI, PET, and muscle biopsy to support inflammatory myopathy
- Getting a myositis antibody panel, which identified SAE1 positivity

That sequence matters. A swollen neck can create tunnel vision. Dermatomyositis doesn’t always announce itself with flamboyant rash and obvious proximal weakness. Sometimes it masquerades as a localized ENT or allergy problem.

## Treatment: aggressive up front, then steroid-sparing

Because of significant airway-adjacent muscle involvement and dysphagia, the patient was treated aggressively:

- 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily
- Prednisone taper
- IVIG loading, then monthly cycles
- Mycophenolate mofetil
- Rituximab
- Pneumocystis prophylaxis, with TMP-SMX stopped due to hemolytic anemia and nebulized pentamidine used instead

That’s very FOAM-worthy because it reflects a real-world principle: severe dermatomyositis often needs more than one immunomodulator. If swallowing is unsafe or disease is extensive, don’t under-treat it.

In this case, the response was excellent:
- CK normalized
- Cervical swelling resolved
- Strength recovered
- Dysphagia gradually improved
- Respiratory status stabilized

## Clinical pearls

- Not all “angioedema” is angioedema.
- Neck swelling plus rash, nailfold changes, proximal weakness, or dysphagia should trigger a myositis workup.
- CK is a fast discriminator in atypical soft tissue swelling.
- SAE1 dermatomyositis often has substantial cutaneous disease and can come with severe dysphagia.
- Consider ILD and malignancy risk early; antibody status should sharpen surveillance, not reassure you.
- Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is not a side note—it is a major driver of aspiration, nutrition issues, and morbidity.

## Take-home points

- **Dermatomyositis can present as pseudoangioedema, including prominent neck swelling.**
- **If swelling is atypical for histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, send CK and re-examine the skin and nailfolds.**
- **Anti-SAE1 positivity suggests a phenotype with significant cutaneous disease, frequent dysphagia, possible ILD, and meaningful malignancy risk.**
- **Early multidisciplinary care matters: ENT, immunology/rheumatology, speech pathology, nutrition, and oncology-style surveillance may all be needed.**
- **When dysphagia and airway-adjacent myositis are present, treat promptly and aggressively.**

## Bottom line

SAE1 dermatomyositis is one of those diagnoses that rewards pattern recognition. The antibody doesn’t just label the disease—it helps explain why the patient looks the way they do, what complications are likely next, and how hard you should push on treatment and surveillance.

So the next time the “angioedema” story feels wrong, don’t just ask, “What kind of swelling is this?”

Ask, “Could this be myositis wearing an allergy mask?”
