Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When “Angioedema” Isn’t: Dermatomyositis in the Swollen Neck

## The weird part first: a neck swelling that was not angioedema

A man in his 40s showed up with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling. Easy enough to anchor on “angioedema,” right? Except the story kept refusing to behave like angioedema.

He was afebrile, oxygenating well, not tachypnoeic, and had no ACE inhibitor, no DPP-4 inhibitor, no NSAID trigger, no personal or family history of angioedema, and no urticaria. ENT scoped him and didn’t find a structural airway catastrophe. The neck was swollen and erythematous, yes — but the rest of the exam started to whisper “inflammatory myopathy” rather than “allergic swelling.”

The clue that cracked the case was a creatine kinase level. It was massively elevated: 16,184 U/L.

This was dermatomyositis presenting as pseudoangioedema, with prominent cervical soft tissue oedema, dysphagia, and myositis. It’s uncommon, easy to mislabel, and clinically important because the treatment path is very different from histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

## Why this matters

Neck swelling in the ED is high-stakes. The default job is to rule out the lethal stuff first: deep neck space infection, Ludwig’s angina, airway compromise, anaphylaxis, SVC syndrome, and true angioedema.

But this case is a good reminder that not all “swelling” is the same pathology. In dermatomyositis, the neck can look like angioedema while the mechanism is really inflammatory edema associated with active myositis. If you miss that distinction, you can lose time before starting immunosuppression and malignancy screening.

## How this presented

The case had a few features that should make you pause before calling it angioedema:

The neck swelling was subacute, evolving over weeks rather than hours. That’s already a mismatch for classic histaminergic angioedema, which tends to come on more abruptly, and for many bradykinin-mediated episodes as well.

He also had proximal muscle weakness, mild myalgia, severe dysphagia, and subtle skin/nail findings. On exam there was facial erythema including the nasolabial folds, periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages/avascular areas, and a rash on the thigh. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were absent, which is exactly the trap — dermatomyositis does not always read the textbook to you.

The CT neck showed diffuse oedema involving the strap muscles with extension into the retropharyngeal space, which understandably raised concern for infection. But he lacked the systemic toxicity you’d expect with an aggressive deep neck infection, and nasendoscopy didn’t show a focal obstructing lesion.

Once the CK came back, the diagnostic frame changed.

## Distinguishing inflammatory cervical oedema from angioedema

This is the practical bedside lesson.

Histaminergic angioedema usually gives you a history of rapid swelling, often with urticaria, pruritus, known triggers, and sometimes anaphylaxis physiology. Bradykinin-mediated angioedema often lacks urticaria, but it still tends to have a recurring pattern, a medication/family history, or associated complement abnormalities depending on the subtype.

Inflammatory oedema from dermatomyositis behaves differently:
- it may be more subacute or persistent
- it may come with muscle pain or weakness
- the skin exam may show subtle dermatomyositis stigmata
- CK is often a huge clue
- swallowing dysfunction can be prominent
- imaging may show soft tissue and muscle involvement rather than isolated submucosal airway swelling

In this patient, complement studies and C1 esterase testing were normal, which helped push away hereditary/acquired angioedema. The absence of a clear histamine or bradykinin story plus the abnormal muscle/skin findings made dermatomyositis the right hypothesis.

## The diagnosis: anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis

The myositis panel was strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibody. Muscle MRI showed active myositis. PET demonstrated diffuse abnormal uptake in multiple muscle groups, especially in the head and neck, and muscle biopsy showed perifascicular changes with C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — classic dermatomyositis pathology.

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is not the most common serologic subtype, but it matters because it can present with striking skin disease, dysphagia, and interstitial lung disease. In the literature, dysphagia and ILD are common enough in SAE1-positive disease to deserve real respect, and malignancy risk is also part of the conversation.

In this case, the “angioedema” was the visible tip of a broader inflammatory syndrome.

## A practical diagnostic approach for the swollen neck

When the neck is swollen and the diagnosis is not obvious, think in layers.

First, stabilize the airway and rule out immediate threats. That means looking for stridor, muffled voice, inability to handle secretions, drooling, rapid progression, and focal exam findings that suggest infection or space-occupying pathology.

Then ask: does this smell like true angioedema?
- rapid onset?
- urticaria or pruritus?
- trigger medication?
- prior episodes?
- family history?
- response to antihistamines/epinephrine/steroids?

If not, widen the net. Look for:
- proximal weakness
- dysphagia
- myalgia
- rashes over the face, hands, trunk, or thighs
- nailfold changes
- elevated CK, AST/ALT, LDH
- interstitial lung disease or aspiration changes
- malignancy clues

In this case, the “can’t miss” test was CK. If you remember one thing from this story, remember that.

## Treatment: this was a myositis emergency, not an allergy problem

Because there was significant airway muscle involvement and severe dysphagia, he was treated aggressively with:
- 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily
- oral prednisone taper
- IVIG
- mycophenolate mofetil
- rituximab

He also needed nutrition support via nasogastric tube for persistent dysphagia and Pneumocystis prophylaxis, with TMP-SMX stopped because of haemolytic anaemia and pentamidine used instead.

That’s a good reminder that dermatomyositis with severe bulbar involvement is not a “watch and wait” disease. It can require real immunosuppression early, plus airway, swallow, and nutrition planning.

## Malignancy screening is not optional

Dermatomyositis is one of those diagnoses that should trigger cancer vigilance. This patient was considered high risk by IMACS criteria because he had dermatomyositis and dysphagia, plus intermediate risk factors including male sex and SAE1 positivity.

His workup included:
- CT chest/abdomen/pelvis
- PET scan
- tumour markers
- planned upper and lower endoscopy

Nothing malignant was identified at the time, but the plan for ongoing surveillance was appropriate.

## What made this case so easy to miss

The hardest part is that the early story sounded like common ED pathology:
- neck swelling
- elevated CRP
- CT with deep soft tissue oedema
- concern for Ludwig’s angina
- then “maybe idiopathic angioedema”

That’s a very reasonable first-pass differential. The miss would have been to stop there.

The clues that should break the cognitive lock were:
- subacute course over weeks
- proximal weakness
- dysphagia
- subtle rash and nailfold changes
- no medication trigger
- normal complement/C1 esterase studies
- markedly elevated CK

Once you see that pattern, dermatomyositis becomes much less exotic.

## Take-home points

- Not all neck swelling is angioedema; dermatomyositis can present with pseudoangioedema and cervical soft tissue oedema.
- Subacute progression, proximal weakness, dysphagia, and subtle skin/nailfold findings should push you away from isolated allergic angioedema.
- A CK level is a high-yield test in unexplained neck swelling when myositis is on the table.
- Normal complement/C1 esterase studies and the absence of a trigger/history make hereditary or bradykinin-mediated angioedema less likely.
- Severe bulbar involvement in dermatomyositis can require urgent immunosuppression, swallow evaluation, nutrition support, and malignancy screening.

## Bottom line

If a “swollen neck” doesn’t fit the usual angioedema script, don’t force it. Examine the skin carefully, check muscle strength, order a CK, and keep dermatomyositis on the list. In this case, that shift in thinking changed the diagnosis from presumed angioedema to a treatable inflammatory myopathy with airway-adjacent involvement — exactly the kind of diagnostic pivot that matters in the ED and beyond.
