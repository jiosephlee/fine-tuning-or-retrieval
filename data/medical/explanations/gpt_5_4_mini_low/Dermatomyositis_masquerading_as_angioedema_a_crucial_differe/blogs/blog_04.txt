Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Neck Swelling, Dysphagia, and Aspiration: Airway Thinking Beyond Infection

## The case that should make you pause

A previously healthy man in his 40s showed up with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling. The initial instinct was understandable: deep neck space infection, then Ludwig’s angina, then maybe idiopathic angioedema. But the oxygen saturation was normal, he wasn’t toxic, and the neck swelling didn’t behave like classic histamine- or bradykinin-mediated edema.

The real clue was that this wasn’t “just swelling.” He also had proximal weakness, subtle skin and nailfold changes, and then—when the workup was broadened—severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration risk, a CK of 16,184 U/L, MRI evidence of active myositis, and muscle biopsy consistent with dermatomyositis. His “angioedema” was actually inflammatory myopathy involving the head and neck.

That’s the take-home here: airway complaints can be misleading when the problem is not an obstructed lumen, but a dysfunctional swallow.

## Why this matters in the ED and early inpatient course

Neck swelling plus dysphagia usually triggers an airway/infection reflex. That’s appropriate. You do need to rule out the dangerous stuff first:

- Deep neck space infection
- Ludwig’s angina
- Epiglottitis
- Angioedema
- SVC syndrome
- Abscess, cellulitis, sialadenitis

But inflammatory myopathy belongs on that list, especially when the patient looks “too well” for the degree of swelling, or when the swelling is accompanied by weakness, rash, periungual changes, or unexplained aspiration symptoms.

In this case, several features were quietly off:
- Afebrile, saturating 99% on room air
- No tachypnea
- Flexible nasendoscopy without a structural obstructive lesion
- Neck swelling that was erythematous, non-tender, and persistent
- Proximal weakness
- Severe dysphagia out of proportion to the apparent airway findings

That mismatch is what should make you think beyond infection.

## Oxygenation can be reassuring while swallowing is failing

One of the most important lessons from this case is that normal oxygen saturation does not rule out clinically significant upper aerodigestive dysfunction.

This patient had:
- Increased secretions and coughing during nasendoscopy
- A video fluoroscopic swallow study showing severe pharyngeal dysphagia
- Severe pyriform sinus residue
- Post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the vocal cords
- Evidence of aspiration-related lung changes on CT chest

So although he looked “stable” from a classic respiratory standpoint, he was actually at substantial risk of aspiration and nutritional compromise.

That distinction matters. A patient may have:
- No hypoxemia
- No obvious airway obstruction
- No stridor

…but still be unsafe to eat and unable to protect the airway during swallowing.

## What nasendoscopy and swallow studies added

The nasendoscopy was useful because it helped exclude a gross structural ENT cause. There was no major abnormality in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space. But the exam did reveal increased secretions and coughing, which should raise suspicion for pharyngeal dysfunction.

The swallow study then provided the clincher: this was not a “benign” neck swelling problem. It was a functional swallowing failure with penetration to the cords and severe residue. In inflammatory myopathy, that can be the difference between oral intake and a feeding tube.

Practical pearl: if a patient with neck swelling has coughing with secretions, wet voice, choking, recurrent chest changes, or unexplained inability to swallow, don’t stop at a reassuring oxygen saturation. Get a formal swallow evaluation early.

## Aspiration pneumonitis is often the downstream clue

The CT chest showed patchy peribronchial consolidation thought to be aspiration pneumonitis, plus multiple non-cavitating nodules. In this case, aspiration wasn’t just a consequence—it was part of the disease burden.

When dysphagia is severe, aspiration can show up before anyone has put together the neuromuscular picture. The chest imaging may be the first sign that swallowing is failing.

A useful mental model:
- Head/neck inflammation or myositis → pharyngeal dysfunction
- Pharyngeal dysfunction → silent or overt aspiration
- Aspiration → peribronchial opacities, consolidation, cough, secretion burden

If you see aspiration-type imaging in a patient with neck swelling and weakness, think beyond infection.

## Why CK matters in “angioedema” that doesn’t fit

In this patient, the immunology team appropriately noticed the phenotype wasn’t consistent with acquired or hereditary angioedema. The swelling was atypical, and there were muscle and skin clues. A CK was ordered and came back massively elevated.

That is a high-yield move.

When neck swelling is accompanied by:
- Proximal weakness
- Dysphagia
- Rash
- Nailfold changes
- Elevated AST/ALT out of proportion to liver symptoms
- Aspiration on swallow study

…a CK can quickly redirect the entire differential toward inflammatory myopathy.

This case also shows why transaminases can be misleading. AST and ALT were elevated here, but the source was muscle injury, not primary hepatocellular disease.

## Dermatomyositis can present where the airway meets the swallow

Dermatomyositis classically makes us think of:
- Proximal muscle weakness
- Gottron’s papules
- Heliotrope rash
- Shawl sign
- Interstitial lung disease
- Dysphagia

But this case reminds us it can also present with prominent cervical soft tissue swelling that mimics angioedema or an ENT infection.

The patient had anti-SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, and his phenotype included:
- Severe cervical swelling
- Dysphagia
- Myositis on MRI
- Positive muscle biopsy with perifascicular changes and C5b-9 deposition
- Cutaneous and nailfold changes, albeit subtle

In other words: the airway issue was really a systemic autoimmune muscle disease announcing itself in the neck.

## When severe dysphagia should trigger urgent immunosuppression

This is the key management point. In inflammatory myopathy, dysphagia is not a nuisance symptom—it can be a marker of severe disease and a driver of morbidity through aspiration, malnutrition, and inability to take oral medications.

In this case, the patient required:
- 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily
- High-dose oral prednisone taper
- IVIG
- Mycophenolate mofetil
- Rituximab

That level of treatment reflects the severity of head and neck involvement and the risk of ongoing aspiration.

The practical implication is straightforward: if dysphagia is severe, progressive, and tied to active inflammatory myopathy, don’t wait for perfect certainty before escalating therapy. You still need reasonable exclusion of infection and mechanical obstruction, but once the pattern fits, the threshold for urgent immunosuppression should be low.

## What to watch for at the bedside

A few bedside clues in this case were more important than any single test:

- Facial erythema that included the nasolabial folds
- Erythematous, swollen neck that was non-tender
- Subtle periungual erythema and nailfold microhemorrhages
- Reduced hip flexor power
- Increased secretions/cough during nasendoscopy

These findings are easy to miss if you anchor on infection or angioedema. A careful skin and neuromuscular exam can completely change the diagnostic direction.

## Take-home points

- Not all neck swelling is infection or angioedema—think inflammatory myopathy when the story doesn’t fit.
- Normal oxygenation does not exclude serious swallowing dysfunction or aspiration risk.
- Nasendoscopy helps exclude structural obstruction, but formal swallow study is often what reveals the true problem.
- CK is a high-yield test in unexplained neck swelling with weakness, rash, or dysphagia.
- Severe dysphagia in dermatomyositis should prompt urgent treatment because aspiration and malnutrition can become the immediate threats.
- A “well-looking” airway complaint can still be a major immunologic emergency.

## Bottom line

This case is a good reminder that the airway is not just about patency—it’s about function. Inflammatory myopathy can present as neck swelling with an apparently reassuring respiratory exam, while the real danger is pharyngeal failure, aspiration, and delayed diagnosis. If the swelling is atypical and the patient has any hint of weakness or skin change, widen the frame early and check a CK.
