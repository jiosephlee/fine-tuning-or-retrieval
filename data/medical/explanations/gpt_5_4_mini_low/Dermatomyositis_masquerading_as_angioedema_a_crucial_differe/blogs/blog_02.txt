Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The CK That Changed the Case: Using Small Exam Findings to Unmask Inflammatory Myopathy

## The setup: when neck swelling is not what it seems

A man in his 40s walked into the ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The initial lane of thinking made perfect sense: deep neck infection, then Ludwig’s angina, then—after ENT evaluation and a negative structural workup—idiopathic angioedema.

But the case took a sharp turn when someone paused long enough to look beyond the swelling itself.

A few subtle findings changed everything: periungual nailfold abnormalities, facial erythema that included the nasolabial folds, a patchy rash on the thigh, mild proximal hip flexor weakness, and dysphagia. A creatine kinase was sent. It came back at 16,184 U/L.

That’s the pivot point in this case. Not the scan. Not the nasendoscopy. The bedside exam.

## Why this case matters

Neck swelling tends to trigger two reflexive pathways:

- infection until proven otherwise
- angioedema until proven otherwise

Both are reasonable. Both can be wrong.

This case is a good reminder that dermatomyositis can masquerade as facial or cervical edema, including a pseudoangioedema picture. When the swelling is non-tender, persistent, not fitting histamine-mediated or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, and accompanied by muscle symptoms or odd skin/nail findings, inflammatory myopathy needs to move up the list.

In this patient, the diagnosis was ultimately anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with significant myositis, severe dysphagia, and inflammatory neck swelling.

## The bedside clues that mattered

The early presentation was easy to anchor on because the neck looked swollen and inflammatory on CT. But the exam contained the clues that steered the team away from ENT-only thinking:

- **Neck swelling was atypical for angioedema**
  - persistent over weeks
  - non-tender
  - associated with other systemic findings
- **Proximal weakness was present**
  - hip flexors 4/5
- **Dysphagia was present**
  - later confirmed as severe pharyngeal dysphagia on swallow study
- **Subtle skin/nail findings were present**
  - facial erythema
  - periungual erythema
  - nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy
  - thigh rash
- **Classic dermatomyositis rash was absent**
  - no Gottron’s papules
  - no heliotrope rash

That’s the teaching point: you do not need the full textbook rash to make the diagnosis suspect. Sometimes the exam gives you fragments, not the full picture.

## The CK is the clue you don’t want to miss

When a patient has unexplained swelling plus weakness, a CK is cheap, fast, and high-yield.

In this case, CK was dramatically elevated, and that reframed the problem from “is this angioedema?” to “why is there severe muscle injury with inflammatory features?”

That single lab result helped connect the dots with:

- elevated AST/ALT/LDH
- proximal myositis on MRI
- diffuse FDG uptake in multiple muscle groups on PET
- severe dysphagia
- biopsy-proven dermatomyositis

The CK didn’t make the diagnosis alone, but it forced the right diagnostic lane.

## Dermatomyositis can look like ENT disease

This patient was initially handled like a deep neck space process, which is understandable. Neck edema and retropharyngeal inflammatory changes on CT will make most clinicians think infection first.

But dermatomyositis can produce **subcutaneous edema and inflammatory swelling**, especially in the face and neck. That can mimic:

- Ludwig’s angina
- cellulitis/erysipelas
- idiopathic angioedema
- hereditary or acquired angioedema
- sialadenitis
- superior vena cava syndrome

The key discriminator is that dermatomyositis usually brings along other inflammatory clues if you look carefully enough: weakness, dysphagia, rash, nailfold changes, elevated muscle enzymes.

## Why the skin and nail exam matters so much

This is the exam pearl worth carrying into practice: **nailfolds are often where inflammatory myopathy quietly announces itself**.

In this patient, nailfold capillaroscopy showed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, and bedside exam found periungual erythema. Those are not dramatic findings, but they are very real ones.

If you see unexplained facial or neck edema and you’re wondering about angioedema, don’t stop at the airway exam. Look for:

- periungual erythema
- nailfold capillary dropout or hemorrhage
- shawl-type or V-sign distribution
- violaceous facial erythema
- holster-type thigh rash
- subtle proximal weakness

Classic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash are helpful when present, but their absence does not rule out dermatomyositis.

## The broader workup: once the diagnosis was suspected

After CK came back, the case expanded appropriately.

He had:

- ANA 1:1280, speckled
- strongly positive **anti-SAE1 antibody**
- MRI thighs showing active myositis
- PET scan showing diffuse muscle uptake without evidence of malignancy
- muscle biopsy consistent with dermatomyositis, including perifascicular C5b-9 deposition
- severe pharyngeal dysphagia on swallow study
- CT chest showing nodules and aspiration-type changes, without pulmonary fibrosis

His infectious and angioedema workup was unrevealing, including normal complement and C1 esterase studies.

That’s another useful lesson: a negative angioedema evaluation should not force you to keep calling the syndrome angioedema if the phenotype doesn’t fit.

## Treatment implications: why recognition matters

Dermatomyositis is not a “watch and wait” diagnosis when there is airway involvement, severe dysphagia, or marked myositis. This patient received aggressive immunosuppression:

- IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days
- prednisone taper
- IVIG
- mycophenolate mofetil
- rituximab

He also needed enteral feeding support because dysphagia was profound and prolonged.

The big practical point: **earlier recognition changes disposition, specialty involvement, and treatment intensity**. Mislabeling this as idiopathic angioedema could delay immunosuppression, delay swallow precautions, and miss the need for malignancy screening.

## Malignancy screening still matters

This case also reminds us that dermatomyositis is not just a skin-and-muscle disease. It can be a paraneoplastic clue.

In this patient, malignancy evaluation was reassuring, but he appropriately underwent extensive screening and ongoing surveillance. The case report notes that under IMACS guidance, his risk profile placed him in a high-risk screening category because of dermatomyositis plus dysphagia, with additional intermediate-risk features.

So once dermatomyositis is on the table, the workup is not only about treating inflammation; it’s also about asking **why** this inflammatory syndrome is occurring.

## Take-home points

- **Unexplained neck swelling is not always angioedema or infection.**
- **If swelling is accompanied by weakness, dysphagia, rash, or nailfold changes, think inflammatory myopathy.**
- **Send a CK early when the exam doesn’t fit the presumed diagnosis.**
- **Classic dermatomyositis rash may be absent. Subtle periungual and nailfold findings can be the giveaway.**
- **Dermatomyositis can present with pseudoangioedema of the face/neck and mimic ENT disease.**
- **Severe dysphagia and marked myositis warrant prompt immunosuppression and swallowing precautions.**
- **Once dermatomyositis is diagnosed, malignancy screening is part of the package.**

## Final thought

This case is a great reminder that the best diagnostic test is often still the physical exam—especially when the findings are small, easy to miss, and sitting just outside the chief complaint.

The neck swelling brought the patient into the hospital.  
The CK changed the case.  
And the nailfolds, rash, and proximal weakness made the diagnosis possible.
