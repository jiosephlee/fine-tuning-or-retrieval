Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Dermatomyositis in Context: Core Disease Concepts and the Significance of Pseudoangioedema

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy in which immune-mediated injury targets skeletal muscle and skin, and in some patients also involves the lungs, swallowing musculature, joints, gastrointestinal tract, and heart. It is uncommon, with an estimated incidence of roughly 10 cases per million population, and it has a bimodal age distribution, affecting children and adults most often in midlife. In clinical practice, the diagnosis is suggested by a combination of characteristic rash, proximal muscle weakness, elevated muscle enzymes, and supportive serology, imaging, or biopsy findings. The case described here fits that framework, but it is notable because the patient’s earliest dominant symptom was neck swelling rather than the more familiar presentation of rash and weakness.

The pathophysiology of dermatomyositis is classically centered on a microangiopathic process. Complement activation injures endomysial capillaries, leading to ischemia, particularly in perifascicular muscle fibers. This pattern explains the hallmark biopsy findings of perifascicular atrophy and complement deposition, often demonstrated by membrane attack complex staining on capillaries. Muscle injury then produces weakness, myalgia, and marked elevation of creatine kinase, as seen in this patient. When the inflammatory process is severe, edema can develop in the involved soft tissues, including the subcutaneous tissues of the face and neck, which creates a misleading appearance of angioedema.

The skin findings of dermatomyositis are often the earliest clues if they are actively sought. Classic features include Gottron’s papules over the metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints, a heliotrope rash around the eyelids, shawl sign over the shoulders and upper back, V-neck sign on the anterior chest, holster sign over the lateral thighs, and periungual erythema or telangiectatic nailfold changes. These findings may be subtle, incomplete, or absent early in disease, which is why careful full-skin examination matters. In this case, the absence of prominent Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash initially made the diagnosis less obvious, but nailfold and facial changes, together with proximal weakness, provided important diagnostic hints.

Swallowing difficulty and respiratory compromise are clinically important manifestations of inflammatory myopathy. Dysphagia usually reflects weakness and incoordination of the oropharyngeal and upper esophageal striated muscles, which can lead to pharyngeal residue, aspiration, and poor oral intake. Respiratory symptoms may arise from interstitial lung disease, respiratory muscle weakness, or aspiration-related lung injury secondary to dysphagia. The patient in this report had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration changes on chest imaging, illustrating how muscle involvement can have downstream pulmonary consequences even without primary fibrotic lung disease.

The neck swelling in this case represents pseudoangioedema, an uncommon manifestation of dermatomyositis in which inflammatory edema mimics allergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. This is clinically important because true angioedema is managed through a different diagnostic and therapeutic pathway, whereas inflammatory edema from dermatomyositis requires recognition of the underlying myositis and prompt immunosuppression. Pseudoangioedema may be especially confusing when it presents in the face or neck, where it can resemble cellulitis, deep neck space infection, or idiopathic angioedema. The proposed mechanism is not mast-cell degranulation or C1-esterase dysfunction, but rather inflammatory swelling related to active myositis and increased vascular permeability in involved tissues. In a patient with neck edema plus proximal weakness, skin changes, or dysphagia, a creatine kinase level is an essential and often decisive test.

Myositis-specific autoantibodies are useful because they help define phenotype, predict organ involvement, and support diagnostic confidence. Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme, a component of the sumoylation pathway. SAE1-positive dermatomyositis often has prominent skin disease and can be associated with dysphagia and interstitial lung disease; reported malignancy risk is also clinically relevant. In this case, the positive SAE1 antibody helped unify the presentation of inflammatory neck swelling, rash, dysphagia, and myositis. Importantly, the case also illustrates why dermatomyositis remains an important malignancy-associated syndrome: once diagnosed, patients require systematic cancer screening based on risk features, not just symptom-driven evaluation.

## Initial Presentation and Diagnostic Reframing: From Neck Swelling to Suspected Inflammatory Myopathy

The patient first presented with a subacute, 3-week history of progressive neck swelling accompanied by mild myalgia and proximal weakness, particularly affecting the hip flexors. In the emergency setting, this combination can be alarming because neck swelling raises immediate concern for conditions that threaten the airway, while new weakness suggests a systemic process rather than a purely local one. At this stage, the clinical problem was not yet recognized as a myopathy; instead, the dominant question was whether the neck swelling represented infection, angioedema, or another obstructive process.

Ludwig’s angina and other deep neck space infections were reasonable early considerations because the CT neck showed diffuse soft tissue oedema involving the strap muscles with inflammatory change extending into the retropharyngeal space. Imaging of this type can be seen in serious infection, and in a patient with progressive submandibular or cervical swelling, it appropriately triggers urgent evaluation. However, radiology alone does not establish infection. The rest of the clinical picture was less convincing: he was afebrile, not tachypnoeic, and maintaining normal oxygen saturation, which argued against a fulminant septic process. There was also no obvious focal collection or suppuration to suggest a drainable abscess.

ENT examination helped further narrow the differential. Flexible nasendoscopy showed no major structural abnormality in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, making a mechanical upper airway lesion less likely. The increased secretions and coughing during the examination were important because they hinted at impaired swallowing and aspiration risk rather than a fixed anatomic obstruction. In other words, the airway looked structurally intact, but there was evidence that something was functionally wrong with bulbar swallowing.

When the patient was reviewed by immunology for suspected angioedema, the presentation was still atypical for either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated disease. Angioedema usually causes abrupt, episodic swelling and is often associated with urticaria, medication triggers, or a personal/family history of similar events. This patient instead had a progressive 3-week course, prominent neck erythema and oedema, and no history of ACE inhibitor, DPP-4 inhibitor, NSAID, or other relevant drug exposure. He also lacked a family history of angioedema. Those negative historical features matter because they make common forms of angioedema less likely and should prompt clinicians to search for alternative explanations.

The key diagnostic shift came from the bedside examination. Careful review revealed generalized facial erythema, periungual changes, nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, and a subtle rash on the thigh. These findings are easy to miss if attention is focused only on the swollen neck, but they are classic clues to inflammatory myopathy, particularly dermatomyositis. The combination of skin changes and proximal hip flexor weakness should immediately raise suspicion for a muscle disease rather than isolated edema. In dermatomyositis, swelling may reflect inflammatory involvement of the subcutaneous tissues and muscles, and the neck can appear edematous enough to mimic angioedema or deep neck infection.

At that point, checking a creatine kinase level was the pivotal move. In unexplained soft tissue swelling, especially when there are clues to muscle involvement or characteristic skin changes, CK provides a fast and highly informative screen for myositis. In this case it was markedly elevated, confirming that the process was not isolated neck edema but part of a broader inflammatory muscle disease. The later diagnosis of dermatomyositis was therefore not made by a single test, but by integrating the progressive course, atypical “angioedema” history, normal ENT anatomy, subtle dermatomyositis skin signs, and the unexpected discovery of severe myositis biochemistry.

## Definitive Workup and Diagnostic Reasoning: Establishing SAE1-Positive Dermatomyositis

The diagnostic turning point in this case was recognizing that the patient’s “neck swelling” was not behaving like simple angioedema or infection. The presentation combined subacute cervical edema, proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, skin findings, and a markedly elevated creatine kinase, which together strongly suggested an inflammatory myopathy. In myositis, creatine kinase is often the most useful initial laboratory marker of muscle injury. Here, the CK was profoundly elevated at 16,184 U/L, and this was accompanied by elevated AST, ALT, and LDH. These transaminase abnormalities can be misleading if interpreted as primary hepatic disease, but AST and ALT are also released from injured skeletal muscle, and LDH is a nonspecific marker of tissue breakdown. In this context, the laboratory pattern supported active myocyte damage rather than a primary liver process.

Normal renal function and a negative urine myoglobin screen did not argue against severe myositis. Rhabdomyolysis severe enough to cause kidney injury is a complication of muscle breakdown, but inflammatory myopathy can produce very high CK values without acute kidney injury, especially if recognized early and treated. Likewise, urine myoglobin can be absent or transiently negative despite substantial muscle inflammation. Therefore, the absence of renal injury did not reduce concern for clinically important myositis or exclude a potentially serious process.

Because the initial working diagnosis included angioedema, the team appropriately evaluated for bradykinin-mediated swelling. Normal C3 and C4 levels, along with normal C1 esterase inhibitor level and function, made hereditary or acquired C1 inhibitor deficiency unlikely. This is an important distinction: true angioedema is a vascular permeability disorder, whereas the swelling in this case ultimately reflected inflammatory edema of muscle and soft tissues. The patient’s lack of ACE inhibitor exposure, DPP-4 inhibitor use, NSAID trigger, family history, or recurrent episodic swelling further weakened the angioedema hypothesis.

Autoimmune serology helped place the syndrome into the idiopathic inflammatory myopathy spectrum. The ANA was strongly positive with a speckled pattern, which is common in systemic autoimmune disease but nonspecific on its own. The more informative test was the myositis-specific antibody panel, which showed a strong anti-SAE1 antibody result and was negative for other common myositis antibodies such as Mi-2, TIF1-γ, Jo-1, and OJ. Myositis-specific antibodies are useful because they help define clinical subtypes, predict associated features, and guide malignancy and lung screening. Anti-SAE1 is particularly associated with dermatomyositis, often with prominent skin disease, dysphagia, and sometimes interstitial lung disease or malignancy. In this patient, the antibody result helped explain the combination of cutaneous changes, muscle involvement, and pharyngeal dysfunction.

MRI of the thighs was valuable both diagnostically and practically. In inflammatory myopathy, MRI can detect muscle edema and active inflammation, even when weakness is clinically subtle or patchy. It also helps select an optimal biopsy site by targeting muscles with active disease while avoiding end-stage fatty replacement. In this case, MRI showed active myositis in several pelvic and proximal thigh muscles, supporting the impression of diffuse inflammatory muscle involvement rather than a focal compressive or infectious process.

FDG-PET added two important pieces of information. First, it demonstrated diffuse abnormal uptake in multiple muscle groups, consistent with widespread active inflammation. Second, it did not show metabolically active lesions elsewhere to suggest occult malignancy. This matters because dermatomyositis, particularly in adults, can be a paraneoplastic syndrome. PET imaging does not replace age-appropriate cancer screening, but it can be helpful when malignancy is a concern and when one wants to assess both inflammatory burden and hidden neoplasia in a single study.

Swallowing dysfunction was objectively confirmed with a video fluoroscopic swallow study, which showed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue and penetration of contrast toward the vocal cords. This finding was clinically important because dysphagia is both a marker of more severe dermatomyositis and a major driver of aspiration risk. Inflammatory involvement of the oropharyngeal and upper esophageal musculature can produce malnutrition, aspiration pneumonitis, and prolonged dependence on enteral feeding, all of which were relevant in this case.

Pulmonary testing also supported neuromuscular involvement. The restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests and the chest CT findings of aspiration-related changes fit with impaired swallowing and possible respiratory muscle or extrathoracic limitation, rather than primary obstructive lung disease. Importantly, there was no convincing radiologic evidence of interstitial lung disease, although this remained a relevant concern in a dermatomyositis patient and influenced treatment selection.

Muscle biopsy provided the definitive histopathologic confirmation. Dermatomyositis classically shows perifascicular injury, including necrosis, regeneration, and atrophy, along with perimysial and perivascular inflammation. A key supportive feature is deposition of complement membrane attack complex, C5b-9, in perifascicular capillaries, reflecting immune-mediated microangiopathy. The biopsy in this case showed perifascicular necrotic and regenerative changes, myophagocytosis, perimysial inflammation, and C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is highly characteristic of dermatomyositis. Together with the clinical syndrome and anti-SAE1 positivity, this established a clinicopathologic diagnosis.

The differential diagnosis was broad at presentation because neck swelling is not a classic first complaint of dermatomyositis. Deep neck infection, Ludwig’s angina, cellulitis, bacterial sialadenitis, and other ENT emergencies had to be considered given the CT appearance and elevated inflammatory markers. However, the patient was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, and had no clear infectious source on nasendoscopy or subsequent evaluation. Idiopathic or hereditary angioedema was also considered, but the laboratory workup and clinical pattern were not supportive. Other myopathies were in the differential as well. Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy usually causes very high CK and weakness, but it lacks the typical cutaneous and biopsy features seen here. Inclusion body myositis tends to present more chronically, often in older patients, with characteristic finger flexor and quadriceps weakness and poor steroid responsiveness. Myasthenia gravis can cause bulbar symptoms and weakness but does not raise CK to this degree and does not explain the biopsy findings or skin changes.

The final diagnosis was therefore SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with prominent inflammatory cervical soft tissue edema, proximal myositis, and severe dysphagia with aspiration-related pulmonary changes. The key lesson is that unexplained “angioedema” or neck swelling, especially when paired with weakness, rash, nailfold changes, or dysphagia, should prompt immediate evaluation for inflammatory myopathy. A simple CK measurement, followed by targeted serology, MRI, swallow assessment, and biopsy when needed, can prevent delayed diagnosis and allow timely immunosuppressive treatment.

## Management Strategy in Severe Dermatomyositis with Airway and Swallowing Involvement

Severe dermatomyositis is treated urgently when there is major muscle inflammation affecting the airway, swallowing, or respiratory system, because these features can rapidly lead to aspiration, malnutrition, and further functional decline. In this case, the combination of marked cervical soft tissue swelling, severe pharyngeal dysphagia, and extensive active myositis on MRI and PET scan indicated more than a mild cutaneous-predominant presentation. The management goal was therefore not simply symptom control, but rapid suppression of immune-mediated muscle and tissue inflammation while simultaneously protecting the airway and preventing aspiration-related complications.

The initial step was pulse intravenous methylprednisolone at 1 g daily for 3 days. High-dose intravenous corticosteroids are commonly used when dermatomyositis is severe or when there is concern for threatened airway, significant bulbar involvement, or rapidly progressive weakness, because they act faster than oral therapy. After this acute phase, he was transitioned to high-dose oral prednisone with a gradual taper. The purpose of the taper is to maintain disease control after the initial crisis while reducing the long-term toxicity of glucocorticoids, such as hyperglycaemia, infection, osteoporosis, myopathy, and adrenal suppression. In practice, the taper is individualized to the patient’s clinical response, and in this case it was slowed by the severity of the initial presentation and the ongoing need for other immunosuppressive agents.

Because dermatomyositis often requires prolonged treatment and steroids alone are rarely sufficient, a steroid-sparing regimen was added. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen as a long-term immunosuppressant because it is commonly used in inflammatory myopathies, is generally well tolerated, and can help reduce corticosteroid exposure. It requires monitoring for cytopenias, liver abnormalities, gastrointestinal toxicity, and infection risk. In this patient, mycophenolate also fit the practical need for a maintenance oral agent after stabilization.

Intravenous immunoglobulin was used because it is a useful adjunct in severe myositis, particularly when dysphagia is prominent. IVIG can improve muscle inflammation and swallowing function, and it is often selected when rapid clinical improvement is needed or when clinicians want to avoid further escalation of cytotoxic immunosuppression. In this case, repeated monthly courses were used after the initial loading dose, reflecting the severity of disease and the need for sustained control while other agents took effect.

Rituximab was added because the patient had a severe inflammatory myopathy phenotype with extensive tissue involvement and a need for multidrug therapy. B-cell depletion is typically considered in refractory or aggressive dermatomyositis, or when clinicians need another steroid-sparing agent after initial therapy. Although rituximab is not first-line for every patient, it can be valuable in difficult cases such as this one, particularly when rapid control is needed and the disease burden is high.

Choice of immunosuppressive agent was influenced by pulmonary findings. Methotrexate was avoided because the patient had abnormal lung function and chest imaging abnormalities, and methotrexate can complicate evaluation of pulmonary symptoms because of its own potential for pneumonitis and pulmonary toxicity. In dermatomyositis patients with possible interstitial lung disease or other lung involvement, clinicians often prefer agents such as mycophenolate. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were also reasonable alternatives, but they were not selected because mycophenolate was well tolerated and provided a suitable maintenance option in this case.

Supportive care for swallowing dysfunction was just as important as immunosuppression. His videofluoroscopic swallow study showed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with post-swallow penetration, which placed him at high risk for aspiration and inadequate oral intake. To reduce aspiration risk, oral intake was restricted and nutrition was maintained through nasogastric feeding while swallowing function recovered. This approach is common when dysphagia is expected to improve with treatment but is not yet safe enough for unrestricted oral feeding. His cervical and pulmonary findings also fit this mechanism: the neck swelling and peribronchial consolidation were interpreted as manifestations of inflammatory myositis with micro-aspiration rather than primary infection.

The pulmonary abnormalities required close attention because aspiration pneumonitis can accompany bulbar weakness in dermatomyositis. Patchy peribronchial consolidation on chest CT was consistent with aspiration-related change, and follow-up imaging later showed improvement as swallowing recovered. This illustrates an important point: in severe inflammatory myopathy, respiratory findings are not always due to primary lung disease; they may reflect consequences of dysphagia and impaired airway protection.

Because he required intensive and prolonged immunosuppression, infection prevention was built into the treatment plan. Baseline screening for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, varicella zoster virus, cytomegalovirus, and tuberculosis is standard before starting or escalating immunosuppressive therapy, since latent or occult infection can reactivate under treatment. In this case, the screening workup was performed before full immunosuppression was continued. He also required Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis because the combination of high-dose steroids and additional immunosuppressants places patients at increased risk for opportunistic infection.

Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole was initially used for prophylaxis but had to be stopped when hemolytic anemia developed, despite normal G6PD status. This is a useful reminder that adverse drug reactions can occur even without classic risk factors. Nebulised pentamidine was then substituted and was tolerated. The case therefore shows the practical need to balance disease control with ongoing surveillance for treatment toxicity, infection, and nutrition.

Overall, the treatment strategy reflected a severe dermatomyositis flare with bulbar and cervical involvement: immediate corticosteroid pulse therapy, early addition of steroid-sparing immunosuppressants, IVIG for rapid functional improvement, rituximab for disease control, aggressive dysphagia support, and infection prophylaxis. The patient’s subsequent recovery of strength, normalization of creatine kinase, and resolution of swelling and dysphagia demonstrate the value of early recognition and prompt multimodal therapy in severe dermatomyositis.

## Outcome, Surveillance, and Clinical Lessons for Practice

This patient improved rapidly once dermatomyositis was recognized and immunosuppressive therapy was started, but his recovery occurred on different timelines for different disease manifestations. During the initial 3-week hospitalization, the most obvious clinical gains were a reduction in the cervical soft-tissue swelling and better proximal hip flexor strength. By that stage, his creatine kinase had already fallen substantially and then normalized soon after discharge, showing that biochemical improvement can precede full functional recovery. In contrast, dysphagia lagged behind the muscle enzyme response and remained severe enough to require nasogastric feeding for several weeks after discharge. This mismatch is important clinically: a falling CK is reassuring, but it does not mean that swallowing function or airway-adjacent muscle involvement has resolved.

Over follow-up, the patient’s residual skin and neck findings gradually disappeared. By 2 to 3 months, the cervical swelling had resolved completely, proximal strength had stabilized, and only subtle violaceous facial erythema and periungual changes remained. Prednisone was successfully stopped by 7 months, and dysphagia fully resolved by that point. His respiratory course also improved: follow-up pulmonary function testing showed only mild residual restriction, and serial chest CT demonstrated near-complete resolution of the basal inflammatory changes previously felt to represent aspiration-related pneumonitis rather than primary interstitial lung disease. This longitudinal improvement supports the idea that his chest findings were secondary to swallowing dysfunction and microaspiration, and it also illustrates why early speech/swallow assessment matters in dermatomyositis with bulbar involvement.

Because dermatomyositis carries an associated malignancy risk, this patient required structured cancer surveillance rather than a one-time exclusion of tumor. Using International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group risk principles, he was categorized as high risk because he had dermatomyositis itself plus dysphagia, and he also had additional risk features including male sex and anti-SAE1 positivity. That risk stratification justified broad baseline screening with CT imaging, PET scanning, tumor markers, serial clinical review, and a plan for upper and lower endoscopy to look for occult gastrointestinal malignancy. Importantly, negative initial studies do not end surveillance; cancer detection can lag the myositis diagnosis, so follow-up remains symptom-driven and imaging-based when indicated.

The case also highlights the need for ongoing monitoring of treatment toxicity and disease relapse. Long-term immunosuppression with glucocorticoids, mycophenolate mofetil, IVIG, and rituximab can be highly effective, but it requires surveillance for infection, cytopenias, hepatic effects, and medication tolerance, as well as readiness to adjust therapy if muscle, skin, pulmonary, or swallowing symptoms recur. In this patient, prophylaxis for opportunistic infection had to be modified when trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole caused hemolytic anemia, emphasizing that management of severe dermatomyositis often involves balancing disease control against adverse effects of treatment.

The key diagnostic lesson is that unexplained localized neck swelling is not always angioedema, even when it appears acute and dramatic. When soft-tissue edema is accompanied by subtle erythema, nailfold changes, proximal weakness, dysphagia, or elevated muscle enzymes, dermatomyositis should move high on the differential. In this case, checking a creatine kinase level and carefully examining the skin and nailfolds were decisive. The case also reinforces a systems-based lesson: timely diagnosis depended on coordination between emergency medicine, ENT, immunology, radiology, pathology, rheumatology, and speech/swallow services. That multidisciplinary approach prevented the patient from being anchored to a diagnosis of idiopathic angioedema and allowed treatment to begin before further progression of muscle and bulbar dysfunction.

For acute care clinicians, this case broadens the differential of “angioedema-like” presentations. True histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema usually presents as noninflammatory swelling without marked myositis, markedly elevated CK, or dermatomyositis-type rash and nailfold abnormalities. Pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis is uncommon, but when it occurs it can mimic ENT emergencies or isolated allergic swelling. Recognizing that pattern can shorten diagnostic delay, prompt appropriate malignancy screening, and lead to early immunosuppression in a disease where delayed treatment can result in significant functional impairment.
