Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient presenting with progressive neck swelling initially thought to be angioedema, what clinical features in this case argued against histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and prompted consideration of dermatomyositis instead?

Several features made this presentation atypical for either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis.

First, the swelling was **subacute and progressive over 3 weeks**, not the abrupt, episodic course usually seen with angioedema. Second, it was accompanied by **objective inflammatory and myopathic clues**: **proximal hip flexor weakness**, **mild myalgia**, and later **severe dysphagia**. Third, on examination he had **erythematous, non-tender neck swelling**, **facial erythema involving the nasolabial folds**, and **subtle periungual/nailfold changes**—findings that are much more suggestive of connective tissue disease than angioedema. The **markedly elevated creatine kinase** level and transaminases further pointed to active muscle injury. Finally, the standard angioedema workup was unrevealing: he had **no ACE inhibitor or other typical drug trigger**, **no personal or family history**, and **normal C3/C4 and normal C1 esterase function/level**, arguing strongly against bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

In short, the combination of **persistent “angioedema-like” neck swelling plus rash, nailfold changes, proximal weakness, dysphagia, and very high CK** is the key clue that the edema was not angioedema at all, but **pseudoangioedema from dermatomyositis**, as this case ultimately confirmed.

### [Clinical Pearls] What bedside skin and nail findings are particularly helpful in suggesting dermatomyositis when classic features such as Gottron’s papules or a heliotrope rash are absent?

When Gottron’s papules and a heliotrope rash are absent, the most helpful bedside clues are **subtle periungual and photodistributed skin changes**, especially **periungual erythema**, **nailfold capillary abnormalities** on inspection or capillaroscopy, and sometimes a **violaceous rash over the thighs/hips or trunk**.

In this case, the diagnosis was suggested not by classic facial or hand lesions, but by **proximal nailfold microhemorrhages with avascular areas**, **periungual erythema**, and a **rash on the lateral thigh** consistent with the **Holster sign**. These findings are important because dermatomyositis often produces **microangiopathic injury at the nailfolds**, so careful examination of the cuticles and periungual skin can reveal disease even when the textbook rashes are absent. In practice, if you see unexplained **periungual erythema, dilated or disrupted nailfold capillaries, microhemorrhages, or capillary dropout**, you should strongly consider dermatomyositis and check a **CK** promptly.

### [Morning Report] How do you interpret the combination of severe creatine kinase elevation, AST/ALT elevation, elevated LDH, normal renal function, and negative urine myoglobin in the context of inflammatory myopathy?

In this setting, the laboratory pattern is most consistent with **active skeletal muscle inflammation and injury from inflammatory myopathy**, not primary liver disease or pigment nephropathy.

The key abnormality is the **marked creatine kinase elevation**: CK is the most sensitive routine marker of myofiber necrosis, and a level in the tens of thousands, as in this case, strongly supports substantial muscle involvement. The accompanying **AST and ALT elevation should not be assumed to be hepatic**. Both transaminases can rise in myositis because they are present in skeletal muscle; in inflammatory myopathy, **AST often exceeds ALT**, as it did here. **LDH** is also a nonspecific marker of tissue injury and commonly rises with active myositis.

The **normal renal function and negative urine myoglobin** are reassuring, but they do **not negate the diagnosis**. They simply indicate that, despite severe muscle breakdown, the patient had not developed clinically significant myoglobinuria or acute kidney injury. In inflammatory myopathy, you can have profound CK elevation with preserved kidney function, especially when the process is recognized early and hydration is maintained.

So, taken together, this constellation—**very high CK, muscle-pattern transaminase elevation, elevated LDH, preserved creatinine, and absent myoglobinuria**—points to **severe active dermatomyositis with muscle necrosis**, rather than isolated hepatitis or established rhabdomyolysis with renal injury. In the reported case, this biochemical profile appropriately prompted further evaluation and was confirmed by MRI, biopsy, and myositis-specific serology.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the significance of anti-SAE1 antibody positivity in dermatomyositis, and which clinical manifestations are especially associated with this autoantibody phenotype?

Anti-SAE1 positivity identifies a distinct dermatomyositis autoantibody phenotype. Clinically, it often signals a presentation in which **cutaneous disease is prominent** and **muscle and extramuscular involvement can be substantial**, even when the classic rash is incomplete or subtle at first, as in this case. Importantly, it should prompt clinicians to think beyond “skin-limited” disease and to screen carefully for **dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and malignancy**.

In the case report, the patient had **severe dysphagia**, marked **myositis with very high CK**, and **respiratory/aspiration-related abnormalities**, all fitting the SAE1-associated pattern of significant systemic involvement. The literature summarized in the report also shows that SAE1 dermatomyositis is especially associated with **heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck and shawl signs**, and that **dysphagia and interstitial lung disease** are common—reported in about **46%** and **43%** of cases, respectively—while **malignancy** was present in about **18%**. 

A useful teaching point from this case is that **edema or “pseudoangioedema” of the face or neck is uncommon but can occur**, and in dermatomyositis it reflects **inflammatory myositis and soft-tissue involvement**, not histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. Thus, anti-SAE1 positivity should heighten suspicion for a **more severe dermatomyositis phenotype with dysphagia and lung involvement**, even when the rash is not classic.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What features on muscle biopsy support a diagnosis of dermatomyositis rather than immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy or inclusion body myositis?

On muscle biopsy, **dermatomyositis** is supported by a **perifascicular pattern of injury** with **perimysial/perivascular inflammation** and **complement-mediated microangiopathy**. In this case, the biopsy showed **necrotic, regenerating, and myophagocytosed myofibers in a perifascicular distribution**, **scattered perimysial inflammation with histiocytes**, and—most importantly—**C5b-9 (membrane attack complex) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries**, which is a classic dermatomyositis feature.

This differs from the other inflammatory myopathies in a few key ways:

- **Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM):** biopsy typically shows **prominent myofiber necrosis and regeneration with minimal inflammatory infiltrate**, usually **without the characteristic perifascicular distribution** or capillary MAC pattern seen in dermatomyositis.
- **Inclusion body myositis (IBM):** biopsy usually shows **endomysial inflammation that invades non-necrotic fibers**, often with **rimmed vacuoles** and other degenerative changes; a **perifascicular capillary C5b-9 pattern** is not the defining feature.

So, in short: **perifascicular atrophy/necrosis, perimysial inflammation, and capillary C5b-9 deposition favor dermatomyositis** over IMNM or IBM.

### [Pathophysiology] In this patient with neck swelling, coughing on nasendoscopy, and severe pharyngeal dysphagia on swallow study, what is the likely mechanism of his respiratory findings and chest CT abnormalities?

The respiratory findings were most likely due to **bulbar and pharyngeal muscle involvement from dermatomyositis, leading to impaired swallowing and recurrent microaspiration**.

In this case, the key clues were the **coughing and excess secretions on nasendoscopy**, the **severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy**, and the **pyriform sinus residue with penetration to the vocal cords**. Those findings indicate failure of the oropharyngeal phase of swallowing, so saliva, thin liquids, and food were being aspirated into the airway. That explains both the **cough** and the **chest CT abnormalities**: the **patchy peribronchial consolidation** and **noncavitating nodules** were interpreted as **aspiration pneumonitis/inflammatory change**, not primary interstitial lung disease.

So the mechanism is not angioedema or a primary pulmonary process; it is **myositis-related oropharyngeal dysfunction causing aspiration**, with secondary airway-centered inflammatory opacities on CT.

### [Management] Why was this patient considered high risk for malignancy according to idiopathic inflammatory myopathy-associated cancer screening principles, and what screening strategy is appropriate in such a case?

He was considered **high risk for malignancy** because, under the **IMACS idiopathic inflammatory myopathy–associated cancer screening guideline**, he had **two high-risk features** and **two intermediate-risk features**:

- **High-risk features:**  
  - **Dermatomyositis** itself  
  - **Dysphagia**  
- **Intermediate-risk features:**  
  - **SAE1 antibody positivity**  
  - **Male sex**

In idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, cancer risk is greatest in dermatomyositis, particularly when there is dysphagia and cancer-associated autoantibody positivity. In this case, the combination of dermatomyositis plus significant dysphagia was enough to place him in the high-risk category, with SAE1 positivity and male sex adding further concern.

The appropriate screening approach is **enhanced baseline malignancy screening at the time of diagnosis**, followed by **ongoing surveillance**. In practice, that means:

- **Thorough history and physical examination**
- **Basic laboratory testing**
- **Cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis**  
- **Age- and sex-appropriate cancer screening**
- **Further directed testing if baseline studies are unrevealing or suspicion remains**, such as **PET scanning** and **upper and lower GI endoscopy**, as was planned in this case

For **high-risk patients**, the key principle is not a single negative test, but **comprehensive initial screening plus interval follow-up for occult malignancy**, typically over the first few years after diagnosis, when cancer risk is highest. This is exactly why this patient underwent CT imaging, tumor markers, PET-CT, and planned gastroscopy and colonoscopy.

### [Management] What is the rationale for using pulse intravenous methylprednisolone, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab in a patient with moderate to severe dermatomyositis with dysphagia and airway-related muscle involvement?

In this case, the combination was chosen because the patient had **moderate to severe dermatomyositis with organ-threatening muscle involvement**—specifically **severe pharyngeal dysphagia, cervical/airway-related muscle edema, and active multifocal myositis**. In that setting, treatment must do two things at once: **rapidly suppress active inflammation** and **maintain durable control while tapering steroids**.

**Pulse intravenous methylprednisolone** is used first because it provides the **fastest anti-inflammatory effect** in severe dermatomyositis. When swallowing and airway-adjacent muscles are involved, delay risks aspiration, nutritional failure, and progressive respiratory compromise. The 3-day pulse in this patient was appropriate for urgent disease control, followed by oral prednisone taper.

**IVIG** is often added in severe or rapidly progressive dermatomyositis, especially when there is **prominent dysphagia**, because it can produce a relatively **rapid clinical response** and is useful as an adjunct when immediate improvement is needed. In this case, the patient had severe pharyngeal dysfunction with aspiration risk and required nasogastric feeding, so IVIG was a rational escalation.

**Mycophenolate mofetil** serves as a **steroid-sparing maintenance agent** to help sustain remission and allow prednisone tapering. Dermatomyositis commonly relapses if treated with glucocorticoids alone, and this patient had extensive MRI and PET evidence of active myositis, so a conventional long-term immunosuppressant was warranted.

**Rituximab** was added because the disease burden was high and the case was antibody-positive dermatomyositis with severe extracutaneous involvement. Rituximab is commonly used in **refractory or severe idiopathic inflammatory myopathy** as a more potent steroid-sparing therapy when disease is extensive or when a faster, deeper immunologic suppression is desired. In the case report, the patient improved after this intensified regimen, with normalization of CK, recovery of strength, and eventual resolution of dysphagia.

So the rationale is that this was **not simple cutaneous dermatomyositis**, but **systemic, severe muscle disease threatening swallowing and airway function**. The regimen combined:
- **Pulse steroids** for immediate control,
- **IVIG** for rapid improvement in dysphagia/severe weakness,
- **Mycophenolate** for maintenance and steroid sparing,
- **Rituximab** for escalation in a high-burden autoimmune myopathy.

This is a classic example of why severe dermatomyositis requires **early, aggressive, multi-agent immunosuppression** to prevent aspiration, prolonged disability, and further multiorgan injury.
