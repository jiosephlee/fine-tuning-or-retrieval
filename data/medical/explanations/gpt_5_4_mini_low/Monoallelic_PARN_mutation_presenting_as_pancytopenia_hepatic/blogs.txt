Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Young Adult, Old Cells: When Pancytopenia Plus Fibrosis Should Trigger a Telomere Biology Workup

## The pattern that should make you pause

A woman in her late 20s shows up with 5 years of progressive fatigue, worsening weakness, dyspnea, weight loss, pancytopenia, premature greying, liver fibrosis, and fibrotic changes in the lungs.

That is not a typical “anemia workup.” That is a “something is systemically aging this patient” workup.

This case is a great reminder that when cytopenias, hepatic fibrosis, and interstitial lung disease cluster in a young adult, we should stop thinking in single-organ silos. Even if the classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad is absent, a telomere biology disorder (TBD) belongs on the differential.

## Why this case is so easy to miss

The patient had several plausible explanations for parts of her presentation:

- Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency
- Ineffective erythropoiesis on smear and marrow
- A positive DAT
- Chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells
- Liver disease of unclear cause

Any one of those could anchor the team early. And in fairness, the B12 deficiency was real and clinically important. But it did not explain the whole story.

That’s the key lesson: **nutritional deficiency may explain the blood counts, but not the entire phenotype**. When the degree of disease feels out of proportion, or when a second organ system is involved in a fibrotic way, you need to widen the lens.

## What pointed away from an isolated deficiency

This patient had several red flags for an inherited premature-aging syndrome:

- Young age with progressive multisystem disease
- Premature greying of hair
- Pancytopenia with low reticulocyte count
- Hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- Ground-glass and fibrotic changes on chest imaging
- Osteoporosis and recurrent fractures
- Peripheral neuropathy

The absence of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, or classic reticular pigmentation does **not** rule out TBD. In fact, many adult-presenting telomere disorders show up without the textbook mucocutaneous findings.

## Telomere biology disorders in practical terms

Telomeres are the protective caps on chromosomes. They shorten with cell division, and when they get too short, cells stop dividing or undergo apoptosis. The telomerase complex normally helps maintain them.

If you inherit a pathogenic variant in a telomere-related gene, you get premature telomere attrition. Tissues with high turnover suffer most:

- Bone marrow
- Lung
- Liver
- Skin and mucosa
- Hair

That’s why TBDs can present as:

- Bone marrow failure or cytopenias
- Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- Premature aging features
- Malignancy risk

PARN is one of the telomere-related genes implicated here. Monoallelic PARN variants are more often associated with pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure, though the phenotype can be broader. In this case, exome sequencing found a monoallelic PARN missense variant, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), reported as a VUS but clinically suspicious in context.

## How to think about the pancytopenia

The hematologic picture was mixed, and that’s part of what made this tricky.

The patient had:

- Pancytopenia
- Low reticulocyte count
- Macro/dimorphic features on smear
- Hypersegmented neutrophils
- Severe B12 and folate deficiency
- Bone marrow with erythroid hyperplasia and no malignancy/dysplasia

So yes, there was strong evidence for ineffective erythropoiesis from nutritional deficiency. But in a telomere disorder, the marrow may have less reserve. In other words, **B12 deficiency may be the straw that breaks the marrow’s back** in someone with underlying telomere dysfunction.

That’s an important clinical pearl: a “reversible” explanation does not exclude an underlying predisposition. If the phenotype is severe, persistent, or multisystem, keep looking.

## When should you suspect a telomere biology disorder?

Think TBD when a young or middle-aged patient has any combination of:

- Unexplained cytopenias or marrow failure
- Pulmonary fibrosis, especially if “idiopathic” and early onset
- Cryptogenic liver fibrosis/cirrhosis
- Premature greying or other signs of accelerated aging
- Osteoporosis, endocrine issues, or other multisystem involvement
- Family history of marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, cirrhosis, or early death — though family history may be absent

The big trap is assuming that TBDs are only pediatric or classic dyskeratosis congenita cases. Adult presentations are real and often subtle.

## Diagnostic approach: don’t stop at one organ

Once you see the pattern, the workup should move beyond organ-specific testing.

Useful next steps include:

- Careful family history for marrow failure, lung disease, liver disease, and early greying
- Chest imaging if pulmonary symptoms or exam findings exist
- Liver evaluation when transaminases, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, or fibrosis are present
- Bone marrow evaluation when cytopenias are unexplained
- Genetic testing with a telomere-related gene panel or exome sequencing
- Telomere length testing, when available, as part of the diagnostic workup

This case was ultimately unified by exome sequencing, which is often how these patients are found after a long diagnostic detour.

## A note on the DAT

The positive direct antiglobulin test could have pulled the team toward autoimmune hemolysis. But the rest of the picture didn’t cooperate:

- Low reticulocyte response
- Marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis
- No convincing hemolytic syndrome
- Negative autoimmune serologies

This is a good reminder that a positive DAT is not a diagnosis by itself. Always ask whether it actually explains the clinical physiology.

## Why the liver and lungs matter

Pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis in a young adult should ring the TBD bell loudly.

In telomere disorders, these organs are vulnerable because of ongoing cellular turnover and repair demands. Importantly, liver disease in TBD may be under-recognized until fibrosis is advanced. Similarly, lung abnormalities may be subtle early on and only become obvious on CT or PET-CT done for another reason.

This patient’s combination of pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis is what made the genetic diagnosis feel much less “incidental” and much more like a syndrome.

## Management: supportive, multidisciplinary, and long view

There is no simple curative fix for most TBDs outside selected transplant contexts. Management in this case appropriately focused on:

- Correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency
- Folate replacement
- Transfusion support as needed
- Vitamin D, calcium, and osteoporosis treatment
- Electrolyte repletion
- Physiotherapy
- Genetic counseling
- Ongoing surveillance for marrow, liver, and lung progression

That is exactly the right mindset: treat what is treatable now, but don’t let improvement in one domain lull you into closing the case.

## Clinical pearls

- **Young adult + cytopenias + pulmonary/liver fibrosis = think telomere disorder.**
- **Absence of classic mucocutaneous dyskeratosis congenita findings does not rule out TBD.**
- **Severe nutritional deficiency may coexist with an inherited marrow vulnerability.**
- **A positive DAT is not enough to explain pancytopenia if reticulocyte response is poor and marrow suggests ineffective hematopoiesis.**
- **Genetic testing is often the key to unifying a multisystem “old cells in a young body” presentation.**

## Take-home points

- Telomere biology disorders can present in adulthood with nonclassic features.
- Bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis in a young patient should trigger a syndromic differential.
- Don’t let a correct partial diagnosis, like B12 deficiency, prevent you from seeking the unifying cause.
- Early recognition matters for surveillance, counseling, and transplant planning.
- When the phenotype looks like accelerated aging, think beyond isolated organ disease and ask: **is this a telomere problem?**



# B12 Deficiency Was Real—But Not the Whole Story: Avoiding Premature Closure in Ineffective Erythropoiesis

## The trap: a true diagnosis that still doesn’t explain everything

This case is a great reminder that finding a real abnormality doesn’t mean you’ve found the whole answer.

A woman in her late 20s presented with years of fatigue, progressive weakness, dyspnea, weight loss, and anorexia. Her labs gave an obvious lead: pancytopenia with a very low reticulocyte count, plus smear findings classic for megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed. Easy, right?

Not quite.

Even after nutrient repletion, the broader syndrome was too big to ignore: premature greying, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, neuropathy, and a general “premature aging” phenotype. That’s where the case shifts from common medicine to high-yield diagnostic humility.

## When B12 deficiency explains a lot—but not the whole phenotype

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can absolutely cause:

- Pancytopenia
- Low reticulocyte count
- Hypersegmented neutrophils
- Dimorphic anemia
- Elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin from ineffective erythropoiesis
- Neurologic symptoms
- Mucosal changes

And in this patient, the marrow fit the story: trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, without malignancy or infiltrative disease.

So yes, the nutritional deficiency was real. But the key question in FOAM-land is always:

**Does this diagnosis unify the entire case?**

Here, the answer was no.

## The clue that should make you pause

The biggest red flags for “something else is going on” were not subtle:

- Young age
- Multisystem involvement
- Premature greying
- Hepatic fibrosis
- Pulmonary fibrosis
- Osteoporosis and fractures
- Neuropathy
- Persistent concern despite correction of deficiencies

B12 deficiency can be dramatic, but it does not usually explain a telomere biology disorder phenotype. When cytopenias coexist with fibrotic disease in the liver and lungs, you should start thinking beyond nutrition.

## Ineffective erythropoiesis is not the same as “just B12 deficiency”

This is an important cognitive distinction.

The marrow can look megaloblastic and the patient can be B12 deficient, but that does not exclude a second process that is lowering physiologic reserve. In this case, the authors suspected that a telomere biology disorder may have made the hematopoietic system more vulnerable to stress from severe deficiency.

That matters because telomere disorders can present as:

- Bone marrow failure
- Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- Premature aging features
- Variable or absent classic mucocutaneous findings

So the nutritional deficiency may be the visible problem, while the inherited disorder is the substrate.

## Why telomere biology disorder entered the differential

Once the team stepped back, the pattern was hard to miss: a young adult with unexplained cytopenia plus liver and lung fibrosis.

That constellation should trigger consideration of a telomere biology disorder, even if you don’t see the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad. In this case, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN variant, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. The variant was not definitive on its own, but the phenotype fit the broader telomere disorder spectrum.

This is where medicine gets messy: sometimes the genetics are not crystal clear, but the clinical correlation still matters.

## Practical bedside lesson: don’t stop at the first answer

A confirmed deficiency should not end the workup if the syndrome is larger than the deficiency.

Ask yourself:

- Does the nutritional deficiency explain all findings?
- Is there organ fibrosis that feels out of proportion?
- Are there “premature aging” features?
- Is cytopenia persistent despite repletion?
- Is there a low reticulocyte count suggesting marrow underproduction rather than simple peripheral loss?
- Are there neurologic, hepatic, pulmonary, or skeletal findings that need a unifying diagnosis?

If the answer is “no, B12 doesn’t explain all of this,” keep going.

## Clinical pearls

- A low reticulocyte count in pancytopenia should immediately push you toward impaired marrow production or ineffective erythropoiesis.
- Severe B12 deficiency can produce striking smear and marrow changes, but it should not be used as a diagnostic “full stop” when the phenotype is multisystemic.
- Pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis in a young adult should raise concern for an inherited telomere disorder.
- Telomere biology disorders may present without classic mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita.
- Genetic testing becomes important when the clinical picture is broader than the initial lab abnormality.

## Take-home points

- **A real diagnosis can be incomplete.** B12 deficiency may be present and clinically important, but still not explain the entire case.
- **Premature closure is the danger.** If cytopenia comes with lung, liver, skeletal, or aging phenotypes, keep looking.
- **Think telomeres.** Young patients with unexplained marrow suppression plus pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis deserve consideration of a telomere biology disorder.
- **Clinical context matters more than a single lab abnormality.** Genetics may not always provide a clean yes/no answer, but it can still support the unifying diagnosis.
- **Repletion is treatment, not closure.** Correct the deficiency, but don’t stop surveillance if the phenotype suggests an inherited vulnerability.

## Bottom line

This case is a good antidote to diagnostic tunnel vision. The B12 deficiency was real and needed treatment, but it was not the whole story. When a patient’s phenotype is bigger than the lab abnormality in front of you, step back and ask what else is being missed. In young adults with pancytopenia plus hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, a telomere biology disorder should be on the list—even if the classic clues are absent.



# The DAT Trap: Why a Positive Coombs Test Doesn’t Automatically Mean Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia

## The setup: a hemolysis workup that looks convincing—until it doesn’t

A patient with anemia, elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) can trigger a very familiar reflex: “This is autoimmune hemolytic anemia.”

But this case is a good reminder that bedside pattern recognition can mislead us when we stop too early.

This young woman had pancytopenia, fatigue, and biochemical hints of hemolysis. She also had a positive DAT for IgG. Yet the full picture pointed away from peripheral immune-mediated RBC destruction and toward ineffective erythropoiesis from severe vitamin B12 deficiency, with an underlying telomere biology disorder likely lowering her marrow reserve.

That distinction matters, because DAT positivity is not a diagnosis by itself.

## Why this case is interesting

The surprising part is not that she had anemia. It’s that the anemia came wrapped in multiple layers of diagnostic noise:

- Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency
- Elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin
- Positive DAT
- Pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count
- Bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia rather than aplasia or malignancy
- Multisystem fibrosis: liver and lung
- Premature greying, osteoporosis, neuropathy, and other “accelerated aging” clues

If you anchor on the DAT, you may miss the real mechanism: this was not a classic hemolytic anemia problem. It was a marrow production problem, with intramedullary destruction/ineffective erythropoiesis generating hemolysis-like labs.

## DAT-positive anemia: what the test can and cannot tell you

The DAT detects immunoglobulin and/or complement bound to red cells. That makes it useful, but not definitive. A positive result can occur in true autoimmune hemolytic anemia, but also in other settings where antibody coating is present without clinically meaningful hemolysis.

The key clinical mistake is to assume:

**DAT positive + LDH up + indirect bilirubin up = autoimmune hemolysis**

That formula is incomplete.

In this case, several features argued against a peripheral hemolytic process:

- **Reticulocyte count was low**: in significant peripheral hemolysis, the marrow should usually respond robustly unless it cannot.
- **Bone marrow was not failing from infiltration or aplasia**: it showed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia.
- **Peripheral smear showed megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis features**: dimorphic cells, hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings, basophilic stippling.
- **Severe B12 deficiency was present**: a major cause of intramedullary RBC death and “pseudo-hemolysis.”
- **There was no convincing autoimmune syndrome**: serologies were negative, and the broader clinical picture didn’t fit.

## Intramedullary hemolysis: the lab pattern that fools you

In severe megaloblastic anemia, RBC precursors die in the marrow before they ever become mature circulating red cells. That is ineffective erythropoiesis, not classic peripheral hemolysis.

What that can look like:

- LDH: very high
- Indirect bilirubin: elevated
- Haptoglobin: may be low
- Reticulocytes: inappropriately low
- Peripheral smear: hypersegmented neutrophils, macro-ovalocytes, dimorphism, other dysplastic-appearing changes
- Bone marrow: erythroid hyperplasia

This is the trap: the chemistry resembles hemolysis, but the marrow response tells the real story. A low reticulocyte response is the red flag that should force you to rethink the mechanism.

## How to approach the DAT in practice

A positive DAT should make you ask, not conclude.

Before labeling someone with autoimmune hemolytic anemia, step through the following:

1. **Is there true hemolysis?**  
   Look for a coherent hemolytic pattern, not just one or two lab abnormalities.

2. **Is the marrow responding?**  
   A reticulocyte count that is low or inappropriately normal argues for underproduction or ineffective erythropoiesis.

3. **What does the smear show?**  
   Hypersegmented neutrophils and megaloblastic changes push you toward B12/folate deficiency.

4. **Could there be a mixed process?**  
   DAT positivity can coexist with nutritional deficiency or systemic disease. One abnormal test doesn’t exclude another diagnosis.

5. **Does the broader phenotype suggest something systemic?**  
   In a young adult with pancytopenia, fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy, think beyond “simple hemolysis.”

## Why marrow failure entered the story

This patient’s anemia was initially explained by severe vitamin B12 deficiency, and that was real. But it wasn’t the whole story.

The reason the case became especially important is that her findings were disproportionate and multisystemic. She had:

- pancytopenia
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- pulmonary fibrosis
- premature greying
- osteoporosis
- neuropathy
- apparent signs of premature aging

That constellation should raise suspicion for a telomere biology disorder. In this case, exome sequencing found a monoallelic PARN missense variant, supporting that diagnosis even though the exact variant was classified as a VUS.

The practical point: a patient can have a nutritional deficiency and still have a deeper biologic vulnerability that explains why the marrow is failing under stress.

## Clinical pearls from this case

The “DAT trap” is really a reminder to synthesize the entire hematology picture, not just the immunohematology result.

- A **positive DAT is not synonymous with autoimmune hemolytic anemia**.
- **Low reticulocytes** should make you think of **underproduction or ineffective erythropoiesis**, not just hemolysis.
- **LDH and indirect bilirubin can be elevated in megaloblastic anemia** due to intramedullary cell death.
- **Peripheral smear matters**: hypersegmented neutrophils and megaloblastic changes are major clues.
- In a **young adult with pancytopenia plus fibrosis in other organs**, think about **telomere biology disorders**.
- Correcting the obvious deficiency is necessary, but it may not be sufficient if there is an underlying inherited marrow vulnerability.

## Take-home points

- **DAT positivity is a clue, not a diagnosis.**
- **High LDH and indirect bilirubin do not automatically mean autoimmune hemolysis.**
- **A low reticulocyte count should push you toward marrow underproduction or ineffective erythropoiesis.**
- **Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can mimic hemolysis biochemically.**
- **Multisystem fibrosis and premature aging features in a young patient should trigger consideration of a telomere biology disorder.**

## Bottom line

If the hemolysis labs look real but the reticulocyte response is flat, pause before calling it autoimmune hemolytic anemia. In this case, the positive DAT was part of the noise; the marrow physiology told the truth.

The lesson is simple but easy to miss: **follow the retic count, the smear, and the phenotype—not just the DAT.**



# Cryptogenic Cirrhosis and Early Pulmonary Fibrosis: The Liver-Lung Axis of Telomere Disease

## When “idiopathic” isn’t really idiopathic

A woman in her late 20s with fatigue, dyspnea, weight loss, pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis is not a routine consult. What makes this case especially interesting is that the abnormalities were initially easy to silo: hematology for the pancytopenia, gastroenterology/hepatology for the cirrhosis, pulmonology for the fibrotic lung disease, and neurology for the neuropathy. But once you step back, the pattern starts to look like a single unifying diagnosis: a telomere biology disorder.

That’s the key lesson here. In young adults, “cryptogenic” fibrosis of the liver and “idiopathic” fibrosis of the lungs should trigger a broader search for inherited premature-aging syndromes, even when the classic mucocutaneous signs of dyskeratosis congenita are absent.

## The case pattern that should make you pause

This patient had a five-year history of progressive weakness and early fatigability, then worsening dyspnea, chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia. Exam showed pallor, icterus, premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, bald tongue, thoracic scoliosis, thenar atrophy, hepatomegaly, and decreased breath sounds at the lung bases.

The initial laboratory picture could have pulled you toward a nutritional explanation, and that wasn’t wrong: she had pancytopenia, a low reticulocyte count, severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, dimorphic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, elevated LDH, and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. Bone marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores, not malignancy or infiltrative disease. So yes, there was real megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis in the mix.

But the case didn’t end there. Even after nutritional deficiency was addressed, the overall phenotype was still too big for simple deficiency alone. She also had hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis on transient elastography, pulmonary fibrosis on PET-CT, osteoporosis, neuropathy, premature greying, and multisystem involvement in a woman in her 20s.

That’s the telomere clue.

## Why telomere disease causes liver and lung problems

Telomeres cap chromosome ends and protect genomic integrity. Every time a cell divides, telomeres shorten a little. Telomerase helps preserve telomere length, especially in tissues with ongoing turnover. When genes involved in telomere maintenance are disrupted, rapidly dividing tissues fail earlier than they should.

That’s why telomere biology disorders disproportionately affect:

- Bone marrow
- Lung
- Liver
- Skin and mucosa
- Hair
- Gonads and other high-turnover tissues

Classically, we think of dyskeratosis congenita with its mucocutaneous triad, but that’s only one face of the syndrome. Adults may present instead with isolated pulmonary fibrosis, unexplained cirrhosis, marrow failure, or a blend of all three. In real life, the organ systems often arrive on different time schedules, which makes the diagnosis easy to miss.

## The liver-lung axis: why this pairing matters

Pulmonary fibrosis in a young person is uncommon enough to deserve a second look. Add liver fibrosis or cirrhosis, and telomere disease should move much higher on the differential.

This case is a good example of why the “idiopathic” label can be misleading. The patient had diffuse ground-glass and fibrotic/subpleural changes in both lungs, plus hepatic fibrosis and ascites. Workup for common acquired causes did not explain the full picture:

- Autoimmune serologies were negative
- Viral/infectious studies were unrevealing
- No malignancy or granulomatous disease was seen on PET-CT
- Nutritional deficiencies explained some findings, but not the whole syndrome

When liver disease and fibrotic lung disease coexist in a young adult, the right question is not just “What is causing each organ problem?” It’s “What single diagnosis can connect them?”

Telomere biology disorders are one of those diagnoses.

## Don’t get trapped by the hematology-first mindset

This patient also had pancytopenia, which can make clinicians anchor on marrow failure alone. But the marrow findings were not diagnostic of aplasia or malignancy. The reticulocyte count was low, and the smear showed ineffective erythropoiesis, which fit her severe B12 deficiency. Still, the presence of a telomere disorder matters because it may lower the reserve of the marrow and make a patient less resilient to stressors like nutritional deficiency.

That’s an important practical point: telomere disease and another process can coexist.

In this case, the cytopenias were most likely driven by vitamin B12 deficiency, but the telomere variant raises concern that the marrow may be more vulnerable over time. In other words, the genetic diagnosis doesn’t replace the nutritional diagnosis; it reframes the long-term risk.

## The genetic clue: PARN

Clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in PARN: c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg). It was classified as a variant of uncertain significance, so this is not the kind of result that gives you a neat one-line answer. But the clinical-genetic correlation strongly supported a telomere biology disorder.

PARN is an exoribonuclease involved in RNA processing and telomerase biology. Pathogenic variants have been associated with dyskeratosis congenita and with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. Biallelic variants are classically linked to a more syndromic dyskeratosis congenita phenotype, while monoallelic variants are more often tied to adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and marrow failure.

The important bedside lesson: a VUS does not automatically mean “not relevant.” In the right phenotype, it may be the thread that ties the case together.

## What should trigger suspicion for telomere disease?

You do not need the full mucocutaneous triad to think about telomere biology disorders. In fact, waiting for it will miss patients.

Red flags include:

- Pulmonary fibrosis at a young age
- Cryptogenic cirrhosis or unexplained hepatic fibrosis
- Bone marrow failure or persistent cytopenias
- Premature greying
- Osteoporosis or other premature-aging features
- Family history of pulmonary fibrosis, marrow failure, or liver disease
- Multisystem disease with negative standard workup

This patient had several of those clues, especially premature greying, fibrosis in two organs, and cytopenia.

## Practical approach in the ED or early inpatient setting

You’re not going to diagnose every telomere disorder from the bedside, but you can avoid missing the pattern.

A reasonable approach when you see a younger patient with “idiopathic” fibrosis and cytopenias is:

1. Confirm the organ phenotype  
   Look for objective evidence of lung fibrosis, liver fibrosis/cirrhosis, marrow dysfunction, and extrapulmonary features.

2. Rule out common acquired causes  
   Infection, autoimmune disease, medication toxicity, alcohol-related liver disease, nutritional deficiencies, and malignancy.

3. Look for premature-aging clues  
   Early greying, nail/skin/oral findings, osteoporosis, infertility, unexplained neuropathy, or family history.

4. Think syndromic disease early  
   If the phenotype spans multiple systems, consider telomere biology disorder rather than treating each organ as a separate mystery.

5. Refer for genetics and longitudinal surveillance  
   Diagnosis changes counseling, transplant planning, and monitoring.

## Why this diagnosis matters beyond curiosity

Telomere biology disorders aren’t just academic. They affect prognosis, transplant decisions, family counseling, and surveillance.

Some reasons to identify them:

- They may explain progressive pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis
- Bone marrow failure can evolve over time
- Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may be considered in selected patients with marrow failure
- Family members may need counseling and possibly testing
- Organ transplant outcomes can be influenced by underlying telomere biology
- Patients need surveillance for progression and malignancy risk

This patient improved symptomatically with vitamin repletion and supportive care, but she still needs longitudinal follow-up for liver, lung, and marrow disease. That’s exactly the kind of patient where diagnosis matters even when immediate treatment is supportive.

## The bigger clinical pearl

“Cryptogenic cirrhosis” and “idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis” in a young adult are not separate endpoints; they may be breadcrumbs leading to the same inherited disorder. Telomere disease is one of the main unifiers.

If you only look organ-by-organ, you can spend months chasing isolated abnormalities. If you look at the phenotype as a whole, you may recognize a premature-aging syndrome hiding in plain sight.

## Take-home points

- Young patients with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis should prompt consideration of telomere biology disorders.
- Absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad does not exclude telomere disease.
- Cytopenias may reflect marrow vulnerability, coexist with nutritional deficiency, or evolve over time.
- A variant of uncertain significance can still be clinically meaningful when the phenotype fits.
- Early recognition changes genetic counseling, surveillance, and transplant planning.



# A VUS with a Phenotype: How to Use Clinical-Genetic Correlation Without Overcalling the Variant

## Why this case matters

A woman in her late 20s comes in with fatigue, pancytopenia, liver fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. That’s already a “something systemic is going on” story.

Then genetics returns a **monoallelic PARN missense variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)**.

And here’s the teaching point: the result is **not** a molecular free pass to stop thinking, but it’s also **not** a license to declare causality with certainty. This is exactly where clinicians have to do the hard work of **clinical-genetic correlation**—using the gene, the phenotype, and the biology together, while staying honest about uncertainty.

## The case in one line

A young woman with progressive multisystem disease had severe B12 deficiency and ineffective erythropoiesis, but the broader pattern—**premature ageing features, cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis**—pointed toward a **telomere biology disorder (TBD)**, and exome sequencing found a **PARN VUS** that fit the story biologically, even if it didn’t meet the bar for definitive pathogenicity.

## Why the variant was tempting to “call”

The instinct to over-interpret is understandable. The gene-level story is compelling:

PARN is a telomere-related gene. Pathogenic variants are associated with **dyskeratosis congenita / telomere biology disorders**, and monoallelic variants have been linked particularly with **pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure** phenotypes. In this patient, the phenotype was not subtle—there was multisystem involvement in a young adult, including fibrotic disease in organs that are classic telomere targets.

The variant itself was also not random background noise. It was a **missense change in a conserved residue** within the **R3H domain**, and multiple in silico tools predicted it to be damaging. That gives you a biologically plausible candidate.

But plausibility is not proof.

## What a VUS actually means

A VUS means exactly what it says: **the laboratory cannot confidently classify the variant as either pathogenic or benign with the current evidence**.

That’s the key discipline here:

- A **VUS is not diagnostic**
- A **VUS should not be used alone for irreversible decisions**
- A **VUS can still be clinically useful** when the phenotype strongly suggests the right disease process

This distinction matters because genetics can create false certainty if we let a report’s wording outrun the evidence.

## How to think about clinical-genetic correlation

In real life, variant interpretation is rarely binary. The question isn’t, “Is this variant definitely causal?” It’s often, “Does this variant help explain the phenotype enough to change what I do next?”

In this case, the answer was “yes, but carefully.”

The patient had a pattern that made a **telomere biology disorder** much more likely than isolated nutritional deficiency alone:

- young age
- premature greying
- persistent cytopenia
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- pulmonary fibrosis
- osteoporosis
- neuropathy

Even though severe **vitamin B12 and folate deficiency** clearly contributed to the cytopenias and ineffective erythropoiesis, they did **not** neatly explain the whole picture. That’s where the gene result becomes clinically relevant—not as a final answer, but as a strong piece of the puzzle.

## A practical framework for VUS interpretation at the bedside

When you get a VUS, try this sequence:

### 1) Start with phenotype first
Ask: does the patient’s presentation match a known disease mechanism for this gene?

Here, yes:
- PARN is a telomere-related gene
- Telomere disorders can present with marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, and liver disease
- The patient had all of those elements, plus premature ageing features

### 2) Ask whether the variant itself has supportive features
In this case:
- missense variant
- conserved residue
- predicted damaging by multiple in silico tools
- located in a functional domain

That’s supportive, but still not enough by itself for pathogenicity.

### 3) Check whether the lab’s classification reflects true uncertainty
The report labeled it a **VUS**. That should slow you down, not shut you down.

### 4) Look for orthogonal support
Do you have:
- segregation data?
- telomere length testing?
- prior case reports of the exact variant?
- a second hit?
- family history?

Not necessarily. And if not, you still may have enough to act on the phenotype.

### 5) Use the result to guide surveillance, not overconfident labeling
This is where the value often lies.

## What you can do with a VUS, clinically

A VUS can justify a **higher index of suspicion** and more structured follow-up when the phenotype is convincing.

In this case, that meant:

- **genetic counseling**
- ongoing surveillance of **hepatic and pulmonary function**
- monitoring for evolving **bone marrow failure**
- coordination with relevant subspecialists

That is different from saying, “This variant caused everything.”

It’s more accurate to say, “This variant, in this clinical context, supports a telomere biology disorder phenotype and should change how closely we watch this patient.”

## Don’t let one diagnosis explain away the whole case

One of the best lessons here is that multiple things can be true:

- She had **severe B12 deficiency**, which likely explained much of the ineffective erythropoiesis and pancytopenia
- She also had a broader syndrome suggestive of **telomere biology disorder**
- The **PARN VUS** may be part of that syndrome, but it is not definitive proof

That’s a healthy diagnostic posture: resist the urge to collapse a complex case into a single tidy explanation.

## Why this matters for telomere disorders specifically

Telomere biology disorders are notorious for being under-recognized because they don’t always present with the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita.

Instead, they can show up as:
- unexplained cytopenias
- idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- premature ageing features
- variable multiorgan dysfunction

That means a VUS in a relevant gene may be the first molecular clue in a disease that is otherwise clinically “messy.”

In that setting, the job is not to force certainty—it’s to identify risk early enough to change care.

## Clinical pearls

- A **VUS is not a diagnosis**, but it can still be a useful clue when the phenotype fits.
- In suspected **telomere biology disorders**, think beyond the marrow: **lung, liver, skin/hair, bone, and neurologic features** may all matter.
- **Severe nutritional deficiency and inherited predisposition can coexist**; one does not exclude the other.
- If the phenotype is strong, use the genetic result to trigger **surveillance and counseling**, not definitive language you can’t support.
- Be careful with ACMG-style evidence: supportive in silico and domain data are helpful, but they rarely settle causality on their own.
- When the presentation is syndromic, **family history can be absent**; de novo disease, variable penetrance, and incomplete expression are real.

## Take-home points

- **Don’t overcall a VUS.** The label means uncertainty, and that matters.
- **Do integrate phenotype and biology.** A VUS in a gene like **PARN** may carry meaningful clinical weight when the syndrome fits.
- **Use the result to guide follow-up.** Surveillance, counseling, and subspecialty involvement are appropriate even without definitive variant classification.
- **Keep the differential broad.** Nutritional deficiency, marrow failure, autoimmune disease, and inherited syndromes can overlap.
- **The patient’s phenotype is the diagnosis engine.** Genetics should refine your suspicion, not replace clinical reasoning.

## Bottom line

This case is a great reminder that genomics in the ED/inpatient world is rarely black-and-white. A **PARN VUS** did not prove causality—but in the context of **young-onset multisystem fibrosis, cytopenia, and premature ageing features**, it strongly supported a **telomere biology disorder** and justified ongoing surveillance and counseling.

The art is knowing when to say:  
**“I can’t call this variant pathogenic yet, but I can absolutely call this phenotype concerning.”**



# What Changes After the Diagnosis? Management Pearls in Adult Telomere Biology Disorders

## The diagnosis doesn’t just explain the case — it changes the roadmap

A young adult with pancytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis is not a “one-organ-system-at-a-time” problem. That’s the big lesson from this case.

What makes this presentation so striking is that the initial abnormalities could easily be pulled into separate buckets: severe vitamin B12 deficiency for the cytopenias, fatty liver/cirrhosis for the hepatic findings, incidental pulmonary fibrosis, maybe even an autoimmune overlay because of a positive DAT. But once you step back and see the pattern — premature ageing, marrow stress, lung and liver fibrosis, neuropathy, and bone disease in a woman in her late 20s — the unifying diagnosis becomes a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

And once that diagnosis is on the table, management changes in a meaningful way, even if the day-to-day treatment still looks “supportive.”

## Why the diagnosis matters even when you don’t have a disease-specific cure

TBDs are not just rare genetics trivia. They are syndromes of impaired cellular reserve. Bone marrow, lung, liver, skin, mucosa, and the germline are the tissues that tend to show the stress first. That means the patient may look “stable” at a single visit while quietly accumulating risk across multiple organs.

So the key shift after diagnosis is this: you stop treating isolated abnormalities and start anticipating progression.

That means:
- serial surveillance for marrow failure
- tracking pulmonary and hepatic decline over time
- watching for malignancy
- counseling family members
- being careful about transplant decisions, because these patients can behave very differently than the average aplastic anemia or pulmonary fibrosis patient

## Bone marrow: watch for evolution, not just the current CBC

In this case, the pancytopenia was likely driven largely by severe vitamin B12 deficiency and ineffective erythropoiesis. But the presence of a PARN variant raises the possibility that marrow reserve is already limited, and that stressors like nutritional deficiency may have unmasked an underlying predisposition.

That’s the practical point: a normal marrow biopsy today does not eliminate future marrow failure in a patient with a TBD.

What to do in practice:
- follow CBC trends over time, not just a single post-treatment value
- include reticulocyte response when you’re checking recovery
- re-evaluate if cytopenias recur or become disproportionate to nutritional status
- keep aplastic anemia, MDS, and marrow failure on the differential if the counts drift downward

A helpful pearl: in TBDs, marrow can be “holding on” until it isn’t. The clinical trajectory matters more than one snapshot.

## Pulmonary disease: fibrosis may be the clue that outlives the hematology

Monoallelic PARN variants are often discussed in the context of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and this case reinforces that pulmonary involvement may be part of a broader telomere syndrome rather than a standalone lung disease.

The patient had diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes. Even if respiratory symptoms are modest initially, lung disease in TBD is important because it can progress silently.

Management implication:
- establish baseline pulmonary function and imaging
- follow for progression with serial PFTs and clinical assessment
- have a low threshold to involve pulmonology early
- avoid anchoring on “idiopathic” when the rest of the phenotype points toward inherited telomere dysfunction

In a young adult, pulmonary fibrosis should trigger a search for a systemic explanation, especially if there is concomitant marrow, liver, or premature-ageing phenotype.

## Liver disease: cirrhosis in TBD is real, and often under-recognized

This case had hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis on elastography, plus hepatosplenomegaly and ascites on imaging. That matters because liver involvement in TBD has historically been underappreciated.

The management take-home is not just “watch the LFTs.” It’s broader than that:
- monitor synthetic function and fibrosis progression
- follow portal hypertension features if present
- involve hepatology when fibrosis is established
- avoid assuming that fatty liver, autoimmune hepatitis, or infection explains everything if the clinical pattern doesn’t fit

The literature suggests that clinically important hepatic fibrosis may be more common in adult telomere disorders than many clinicians realize. So when liver disease shows up alongside cytopenias and lung fibrosis, think “systemic telomere problem” rather than separate coincidences.

## Malignancy risk: don’t forget the long game

TBDs carry an increased risk of hematologic and solid malignancies. That doesn’t mean every patient is heading for cancer, but it does mean surveillance and counseling matter.

What this changes:
- maintain vigilance for evolving marrow dysplasia or hematologic malignancy
- don’t dismiss unexplained constitutional symptoms
- keep age-appropriate cancer screening on the radar, and consider whether the threshold for investigation should be lower in a patient with a known TBD

This is one of the reasons early recognition matters: the diagnosis changes how aggressively you follow new symptoms later.

## Family counseling: the diagnosis is rarely only about the patient

This patient’s monoallelic PARN variant was labeled a Variant of Uncertain Significance, but the clinical-genetic correlation still supported a TBD phenotype. That nuance is important for counseling.

What families need to hear:
- this may be inherited or de novo
- variable penetrance is common
- a “negative family history” does not rule out a telomere disorder
- relatives may still warrant genetic evaluation depending on the final interpretation and the broader clinical context

Genetic counseling is not optional window dressing here. It’s part of the management.

## Transplant matters — but timing and toxicity matter even more

In patients with progressive marrow failure, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be considered, but TBD patients are not standard transplant candidates. Their biology can change the risk-benefit equation.

Why this matters:
- conditioning regimens may need modification because telomere disorders can increase treatment toxicity
- organ involvement outside the marrow can affect candidacy
- early hematology involvement is essential if transplant is even a possibility down the line

In other words, if a patient with suspected TBD is already trending toward marrow failure, don’t wait until they are in crisis to start transplant conversations.

## Supportive care is still real care

This patient improved with correction of vitamin B12 deficiency, folate, vitamin D, calcium, and supportive transfusion care. That’s not a trivial outcome.

Supportive management in TBD often includes:
- correction of nutritional deficiencies
- transfusion support when needed
- bone health treatment
- physiotherapy and functional rehab
- organ-specific specialty follow-up

Even when the syndrome itself is not immediately reversible, optimizing the reversible pieces matters. In fact, it may be the difference between decompensation and stability.

## Practical bedside pearl: when should you think “telomere disorder”?

Think TBD when you see a combination of:
- cytopenias or marrow failure
- pulmonary fibrosis in a young or middle-aged adult
- hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis without a clear cause
- premature greying or other accelerated-ageing features
- mucocutaneous abnormalities, even if incomplete
- bone disease, low reserve, or unexplained multisystem involvement

The absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad does not exclude a telomere disorder. Adult TBDs often present in a quieter, more fragmented way.

## Take-home points

- A telomere biology disorder should be on the differential in young adults with unexplained marrow, lung, liver, or bone disease — especially when multiple systems are involved.
- Diagnosis changes management from “fix the current problem” to “monitor for progression across organs.”
- Serial CBCs, pulmonary follow-up, and hepatic surveillance are central.
- Family counseling and genetic interpretation are part of the treatment plan.
- Transplant may be relevant, but TBD patients need special consideration because toxicity risk and organ involvement can change candidacy.
- Early recognition matters even when immediate treatment remains supportive, because it affects surveillance, counseling, and future therapeutic planning.

## Final thought

The most useful thing a TBD diagnosis does is not to provide an instant cure. It gives you a framework. It tells you that the anemia, the fibrosis, the premature greying, and the frailty are not random. And once you see the pattern, you can stop chasing single-organ explanations and start managing the patient the way they actually are: someone with limited biologic reserve who needs longitudinal, system-wide follow-up.
