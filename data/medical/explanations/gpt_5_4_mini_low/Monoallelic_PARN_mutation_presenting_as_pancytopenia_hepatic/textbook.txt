Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and the Initial Problem Representation

This patient’s presentation was clinically important not simply because she was fatigued, but because the symptom pattern suggested a chronic, progressive, multisystem illness. Over 5 years, she developed worsening generalized weakness, early fatigability, exertional dyspnoea, weight loss, anorexia, and a steady decline in functional capacity. In a young adult, such a prolonged course should raise concern for more than isolated anaemia or a transient nutritional problem, especially when the decline is out of proportion to ordinary deconditioning. The more rapid deterioration over the preceding 3 months signalled either progression of an underlying systemic disorder or superimposed decompensation, and it increased the urgency of evaluation.

The physical examination added several important clues. She had pallor and icterus, which fit with anaemia and increased red cell turnover or ineffective erythropoiesis. However, the striking findings were the features of premature ageing: premature greying of hair and knuckle hyperpigmentation. In a woman in her late 20s, these are not trivial cosmetic findings; they are red flags for an inherited disorder affecting cellular maintenance and tissue renewal, particularly a telomere biology disorder. The bald, atrophic tongue supported mucosal atrophy and nutritional deficiency, while thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, and generalized weakness suggested broader skeletal and neurologic involvement rather than isolated haematologic disease. Hepatomegaly and reduced breath sounds at the lung bases pointed to additional hepatic and pulmonary pathology.

Taken together, the case was not just one of anaemia. It was a multisystem syndrome involving the haematologic system, liver, lungs, nervous system, and skeleton. That broader pattern is the key to the initial problem representation: a young adult with pancytopenia and ineffective erythropoiesis, together with premature-ageing stigmata and evidence of hepatic and pulmonary disease. Such a representation helps clinicians move beyond single-cause explanations and consider unifying diagnoses.

This is where recognition of clinical red flags becomes essential. Pancytopenia in a young person can result from nutritional deficiency, marrow failure, infection, autoimmune disease, or malignancy, but the coexistence of premature greying, organ fibrosis, osteoporosis, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly should prompt suspicion for a telomere biology disorder even if the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad is absent. Telomere disorders often present incompletely in adults, and the mucocutaneous signs may be subtle or missing. In this case, the initial bedside impression was therefore not “simple vitamin deficiency,” but rather “young adult with chronic cytopenia, premature ageing features, and multisystem involvement,” which appropriately framed the subsequent diagnostic workup.

## Diagnostic Workup: Interpreting the Haematologic, Hepatic, Pulmonary, and Neurometabolic Findings

The diagnostic workup was important not simply for confirming individual abnormalities, but for showing that the patient’s presentation was multisystemic and not fully explained by a single acquired deficiency state. The initial complete blood count showed pancytopenia, and the reticulocyte count was inappropriately low. In a patient with anaemia, a low reticulocyte response indicates that the bone marrow is not mounting an adequate compensatory response, which narrows the differential toward ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow failure rather than isolated peripheral blood loss or haemolysis. In this case, the low reticulocyte count was a key clue that the anaemia was not simply from destruction of red cells in the circulation.

The peripheral smear reinforced this interpretation. Dimorphic red cells suggested a mixed population of erythrocytes, which is often seen when there are concurrent nutritional deficiencies or after transfusion. Basophilic stippling and Cabot rings are markers of disturbed erythrocyte maturation, and hypersegmented neutrophils are classic for megaloblastic change. Together, these smear findings pointed toward impaired DNA synthesis and ineffective haematopoiesis, rather than a primary destructive process. This was later supported by biochemical testing, which showed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. In megaloblastic anaemia, red cell precursors in the marrow fail to mature normally because DNA synthesis is impaired, leading to intramedullary cell death and a low reticulocyte output despite anaemia.

The iron studies also fit with ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum iron and ferritin were elevated, which can occur when defective erythroid production reduces iron utilisation and iron becomes sequestered in the marrow and reticuloendothelial system. This pattern can be misleading if interpreted in isolation, but in the context of megaloblastic change it supports a marrow production problem rather than iron deficiency.

Bone marrow examination helped exclude more ominous causes of pancytopenia. It showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, but no malignancy, infiltrative disease, or overt dysplasia. Erythroid hyperplasia is expected when the marrow is responding to anaemia, but in megaloblastic states those precursors are ineffective, so blood counts still fall. The absence of frank dysplasia or blast excess made primary myelodysplastic or malignant marrow disease less likely, although marrow failure syndromes could not be excluded solely by morphology.

Markers of cell breakdown were also abnormal: lactate dehydrogenase was elevated and indirect bilirubin was increased, which can be seen in ineffective erythropoiesis because immature erythroid precursors undergo intramedullary destruction. A positive direct antiglobulin test raised the possibility of immune haemolysis, but the overall pattern did not fit well with autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The reticulocyte response was low rather than brisk, the marrow findings supported ineffective production, and there was no convincing evidence of ongoing peripheral red cell destruction. Thus, the DAT positivity was interpreted cautiously and not allowed to override the stronger evidence for megaloblastic ineffective erythropoiesis.

Because severe vitamin B12 deficiency can result from impaired absorption, the team evaluated the gastrointestinal tract. Upper endoscopy was macroscopically normal, but histology showed mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells. This is clinically relevant because parietal cells produce intrinsic factor, which is required for vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum. Reduced parietal cell mass therefore suggests a pernicious-type physiology or at least impaired intrinsic factor–dependent absorption. However, the severity and breadth of the patient’s manifestations were disproportionate to nutritional deficiency alone, which is why the workup continued rather than stopping at a potentially satisfying but incomplete explanation.

The liver and spleen findings were another major clue. Ultrasound showed hepatosplenomegaly, and transient elastography demonstrated steatosis with a liver stiffness value consistent with cirrhosis or advanced hepatic fibrosis. This helped establish that the liver disease was not incidental. Autoimmune and infectious causes of liver injury were assessed because they are common, potentially treatable explanations for fibrosis and hepatosplenomegaly. Negative autoimmune serologies and a largely unrevealing infectious evaluation made these causes less likely. In a young adult, unexplained hepatic fibrosis alongside cytopenia should raise concern for an inherited systemic disorder, particularly when accompanied by premature ageing features.

Pulmonary involvement was supported by PET-CT, which showed diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes in both lungs. While ground-glass opacities can reflect inflammation, infection, or early interstitial lung disease, the fibrotic and subpleural distribution made pulmonary fibrosis more plausible in this clinical context. The absence of an obvious malignancy or granulomatous process on PET-CT further narrowed the differential. In a young patient, the coexistence of pulmonary fibrosis with marrow and liver disease is especially suggestive of a telomere biology disorder rather than isolated organ-specific disease.

The inflammatory, autoimmune, and infectious testing mattered because it excluded common acquired mimics. Negative antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies argued against connective tissue disease and autoimmune hepatitis as unifying causes. The infectious workup was also largely negative, making chronic infection an unlikely explanation for the hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenia, and constitutional symptoms. Excluding these conditions was essential before turning to rarer inherited syndromes.

Several associated systemic findings strengthened the impression of a multisystem disorder. The patient had osteoporosis, vitamin D deficiency, secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte disturbances, and recurrent fractures. These abnormalities may occur in chronic illness and malnutrition, but in combination with premature greying, hepatopulmonary fibrosis, and cytopenia they suggested a broader defect in tissue maintenance and ageing. Finally, electrodiagnostic testing showed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes. Neuropathy is not specific, but in this setting it contributed to the overall picture of widespread organ involvement rather than an isolated haematologic or nutritional problem.

Taken together, the workup showed severe megaloblastic ineffective erythropoiesis from vitamin deficiency, but also revealed hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature ageing features, osteoporosis, and neuropathy that were not adequately explained by nutritional deficiency alone. That mismatch between the severity of the phenotype and the apparent acquired causes was what ultimately prompted genetic testing and the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder.

## From Differential Diagnosis to Unifying Syndrome: Why a Telomere Biology Disorder Was Suspected

This patient’s presentation initially fit several common acquired explanations for pancytopenia, fatigue, weight loss, and hepatosplenomegaly in a young adult. Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was an especially plausible starting point because it can cause profound fatigue, glossitis, neuropathy, pancytopenia, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and a low reticulocyte count from ineffective erythropoiesis. The peripheral smear and marrow findings supported this: dimorphic red cells, hypersegmented neutrophils, erythroid hyperplasia, elevated iron stores, and no evidence of malignant infiltration all pointed toward impaired DNA synthesis rather than primary marrow destruction. In that sense, nutritional deficiency explained much of the hematologic picture.

However, nutritional deficiency did not fully explain the overall illness. The degree of multisystem involvement was disproportionate, and several features persisted beyond what would be expected from B12 and folate deficiency alone. In particular, premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, hepatic fibrosis, and fibrotic lung disease suggested a broader disorder affecting tissues with high proliferative turnover. This mismatch between a correct partial explanation and the severity of disease was the key clue that the case was not simply one of isolated malnutrition.

A positive direct antiglobulin test briefly raised the possibility of autoimmune hemolysis or an autoimmune cytopenia, but the rest of the data did not support that interpretation. The reticulocyte count was low rather than elevated, which argues against robust peripheral hemolysis. There was also no convincing serologic evidence of systemic autoimmune disease, and the marrow did not show an immune-mediated process. In other words, the DAT result was a potentially misleading finding that did not fit the overall pattern of ineffective erythropoiesis and systemic fibrosis.

The team also had to consider primary bone marrow failure syndromes, infiltrative malignancy, granulomatous disease, and chronic infection. These are important causes of pancytopenia with constitutional symptoms and organomegaly in younger patients. Yet the marrow biopsy showed preserved trilineage haematopoiesis without dysplasia, blasts, or infiltrative disease, and cytogenetics were normal. PET-CT did not reveal a malignant or granulomatous process. These negative studies made an acquired marrow neoplasm or infiltrative disorder less likely and pushed the differential away from a single local haematologic cause.

The liver and lung findings were especially important in reframing the diagnosis. Hepatic stiffness consistent with fibrosis or cirrhosis in a woman in her 20s, together with diffuse fibrotic changes in the lungs, is unusual and should prompt consideration of systemic disorders rather than unrelated organ-specific disease. Common causes of hepatic fibrosis, including autoimmune and viral hepatitis, were not supported by the evaluation. Likewise, the pulmonary changes were not explained by infection or malignancy. When chronic liver disease, pulmonary fibrosis, cytopenia, and premature ageing features appear together in a young adult, an inherited disorder affecting cellular maintenance becomes much more likely.

At that point, the combination of premature greying, pancytopenia, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis suggested a telomere biology disorder. Telomere disorders are classically associated with dyskeratosis congenita, but many patients do not present with the full mucocutaneous triad. Adult-onset disease may manifest instead as marrow dysfunction, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, liver disease, or a mixed multisystem phenotype. That is why the absence of nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, or classic skin pigmentation does not exclude the diagnosis.

The absence of a family history also did not rule it out. Telomere biology disorders can show variable penetrance, delayed onset, and incomplete expression even within the same family. De novo variants are also possible, and mildly affected relatives may go unrecognized. In practice, the clinical question is not whether every affected person will have the textbook phenotype, but whether the pattern of organ involvement is better explained by a single inherited defect than by several unrelated acquired diseases.

In this case, the major diagnostic lesson was the importance of synthesis. Once reversible deficiencies were corrected, the remaining pattern was too coherent to ignore: a young adult with accelerated ageing signs, persistent cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and skeletal fragility. Rather than accepting these as disconnected problems, the team correctly looked for a unifying mechanism. That reasoning led to telomere biology disorder as the most plausible explanation and justified genetic testing, which ultimately supported the diagnosis.

## PARN and Telomere Biology Disorders: Essential Pathophysiology and Genetic Interpretation

Telomeres are repetitive DNA-protein structures at the ends of chromosomes that act like protective caps. Their job is to preserve genomic stability by preventing chromosome ends from being misread as DNA damage. With each round of cell division, telomeres shorten naturally. When they become critically short, the cell enters senescence or undergoes apoptosis. This is usually a controlled part of aging biology, but in tissues that must continually renew themselves, excessive telomere shortening produces stem-cell exhaustion and organ dysfunction. That is why telomere disorders so often affect highly proliferative tissues such as bone marrow, mucosal epithelium, hair follicles, and, in some patients, hepatocytes and alveolar epithelial cells.

Telomere length is maintained by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein complex that includes the reverse transcriptase component TERT and the RNA template component TERC. Telomerase adds telomeric repeats back to chromosome ends, partly offsetting normal attrition. When genes involved in telomere maintenance are mutated, telomeres shorten prematurely, and cells with high turnover cannot sustain normal regeneration. The resulting group of inherited conditions is called telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Dyskeratosis congenita is the prototypical TBD, but the clinical spectrum is broad and includes bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, liver disease, cancer predisposition, and variable premature-aging features.

Classically, dyskeratosis congenita is recognized by the mucocutaneous triad of abnormal skin pigmentation, nail dystrophy, and oral leukoplakia. However, adults with TBDs often do not show this full triad. Instead, they may present with isolated or predominant marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or combined multisystem disease. This is important for the case at hand: the patient’s presentation was dominated by pancytopenia, premature greying, liver fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis rather than classic mucocutaneous findings. In practice, the absence of the triad does not exclude a telomere disorder, especially in young adults with unexplained disease in multiple organs.

The tissues involved in TBDs are those with high replicative demand or high dependence on epithelial and stem-cell renewal. Bone marrow failure reflects impaired hematopoietic stem-cell renewal. Oral and skin findings arise from epithelial turnover defects. Hair greying and alopecia reflect follicular stem-cell exhaustion. Liver fibrosis may reflect chronic injury and impaired repair in hepatocytes and supporting stromal cells, while pulmonary fibrosis is linked to defective alveolar epithelial repair. This biology explains why a single telomere maintenance defect can produce a seemingly unrelated combination of cytopenias, fibrosis, and premature aging.

PARN is one of the telomere-related genes increasingly recognized in this spectrum. It encodes poly(A)-specific ribonuclease, a deadenylase that shortens poly(A) tails on RNA molecules and thereby regulates RNA stability and gene expression. In telomere biology, PARN has a specific role in the maturation of TERC, the RNA component of telomerase. By participating in processing of the 3′ end of TERC, PARN helps ensure that functional telomerase RNA is available. If PARN function is impaired, TERC maturation is disrupted, telomerase activity falls, and telomeres shorten more rapidly. Thus, even though PARN is not itself a telomerase catalytic subunit, it is biologically upstream of telomere maintenance.

PARN-associated disease shows an important genotype-phenotype distinction. Biallelic PARN variants have been reported in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita and more severe childhood-onset telomere syndromes. Monoallelic PARN variants, in contrast, are more often associated with adult-onset telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. This difference likely reflects partial loss of function or haploinsufficiency in the heterozygous state, producing a milder or later phenotype than complete loss of both alleles. The patient in this case had a monoallelic PARN variant and developed pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and cytopenia, which fits within the expanding clinical range of monoallelic PARN-associated telomere disease.

The identified variant, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), was found in exon 8 and affects a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain of the PARN protein. Conservation across species suggests that this amino acid is functionally important, because evolution tends to preserve residues critical for protein structure or activity. The R3H domain is a recognized functional region, so a substitution there increases suspicion that the change could alter protein behavior. In addition, the variant was rare or absent in population databases and was predicted to be damaging by multiple in silico tools. These observations do not prove pathogenicity, but they strengthen biological plausibility.

This is where ACMG/AMP variant interpretation is useful. The variant meets several supportive criteria: PM1 because it lies in a critical functional domain with no established benign variation; PM2 because it is absent or extremely rare in population databases; PP2 because PARN is a gene in which missense variation is a known disease mechanism and benign missense variation is relatively uncommon; and PP3 because multiple computational algorithms predict a deleterious effect. Despite this evidence, the variant was still classified formally as a Variant of Uncertain Significance because the available data did not satisfy the threshold for likely pathogenic or pathogenic classification. A VUS should not be treated as definitive proof of disease-causing status in isolation. However, it can become clinically persuasive when the phenotype closely matches the gene’s known disease spectrum and alternative explanations have been excluded.

That genotype-phenotype correlation is exactly what happened here. The patient had a pattern of young-onset multisystem disease involving marrow, liver, and lung, along with premature greying and osteoporosis, which strongly suggested a telomere disorder. Even though the PARN variant remained formally uncertain, the overall clinical context made a TBD the most coherent unifying diagnosis. In other words, the variant was not interpreted in a vacuum; it was interpreted alongside the patient’s age, organ involvement, and exclusion of other causes.

The severe vitamin B12 deficiency in this case is also clinically important because it can obscure the underlying diagnosis. B12 deficiency can cause pancytopenia, macrocytosis or dimorphic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and a low reticulocyte count from ineffective erythropoiesis. Those findings were present here and explain much of the hematologic presentation. However, the persistence of syndromic features such as premature greying, fibrosis, and osteoporosis suggested that nutritional deficiency was not the whole story. In a genetically vulnerable marrow, severe B12 deficiency may unmask or amplify cytopenia, because stem cells with already limited replicative reserve are less able to recover from hematopoietic stress.

When TBD is suspected, telomere length testing and next-generation sequencing are the key confirmatory investigations. Telomere length can be measured by flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization, which is often considered the clinical standard in specialized centers. Short telomeres support the diagnosis, particularly when they are markedly below age-adjusted norms. Sequencing then identifies an underlying telomere-related gene defect and helps with counseling, surveillance, and risk assessment. In this case, exome sequencing provided the molecular clue, while the clinical pattern supplied the interpretive framework.

The main lesson is that telomere biology disorders should be considered in young adults with otherwise unexplained combinations of marrow failure, lung fibrosis, liver fibrosis, and signs of premature aging, even when the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad is absent. A monoallelic PARN variant may be enough to produce disease, but its significance must be judged carefully. A VUS does not end the diagnostic process; rather, it requires integration of genetics, phenotype, exclusion of competing diagnoses, and, when available, telomere length testing.

## Management, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons for Practice

Management in this case addressed both the immediately reversible abnormalities and the broader concern for an underlying telomere biology disorder. The patient had severe vitamin B12 deficiency with pancytopenia and biochemical evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis, so she was started on parenteral cyanocobalamin to ensure rapid and reliable repletion. The regimen used—intravenous vitamin B12 daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance—is appropriate when deficiency is severe or when malabsorption is suspected. In patients with megaloblastic anaemia, a reticulocyte response is usually expected within about a week, followed by gradual improvement in haemoglobin and cell counts over subsequent weeks if marrow reserve is intact. Because she was symptomatic and had significant anaemia, she also received whole blood transfusion for immediate support while the underlying deficiency was being corrected.

Correction of concomitant deficiencies was also important. Folate was replaced, since folate deficiency can coexist with B12 deficiency and contribute to megaloblastic change. Vitamin D and calcium supplementation were given because she had biochemical deficiency, secondary hyperparathyroidism, osteoporosis, bone pain and a history of fractures. Electrolyte abnormalities such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia were corrected in the usual manner. These measures were supportive but clinically important because nutritional and metabolic derangements can worsen weakness, neuromuscular symptoms and bone fragility, even when they are not the primary cause of the multisystem syndrome.

Her skeletal disease required symptom-directed care and rehabilitation. Osteoporosis in telomere biology disorders may reflect chronic illness, endocrine disturbance and impaired tissue maintenance, and in this patient it likely contributed to pain and fracture risk. Physiotherapy was therefore included to preserve mobility, improve function and reduce deconditioning. This is particularly relevant in young adults with chronic fatigue and multisystem disease, where loss of conditioning can quickly amplify disability.

Despite the identification of a monoallelic PARN variant, treatment remained supportive rather than disease-specific because there is no established curative medical therapy for telomere biology disorders. In practice, management is guided by the dominant organ involvement: cytopenias, lung disease, liver disease, and complications such as osteoporosis or malignancy risk. For the marrow component, the key issue is whether the patient develops progressive marrow failure. A telomere disorder can make the bone marrow more vulnerable to stress, so serial blood counts and clinical review are essential even if the initial cytopenia is partly explained by nutritional deficiency. In this patient, the short-term improvement after repletion supported a major contribution from B12 deficiency, but the genetic finding means that future haematologic decline cannot be excluded.

Because telomere biology disorders often evolve over time, ongoing surveillance is central to care. This includes monitoring full blood counts, reticulocyte response, liver function and evidence of portal hypertension or worsening fibrosis, as well as pulmonary follow-up for progression of interstitial lung disease. In a young adult with hepatic fibrosis and CT evidence of fibrotic lung change, periodic reassessment is important even if symptoms are mild initially, since organ dysfunction may advance silently. The case illustrates that a telomere disorder may first present to haematology, hepatology or pulmonology, and the diagnosis may only become apparent when the different organ manifestations are considered together.

Genetic counselling was an important part of management once a telomere biology disorder was suspected. This helps the patient and family understand the possible inherited nature of the condition, the uncertainty surrounding a monoallelic variant of uncertain significance, and the limits of current interpretation. Even when the variant is not definitively pathogenic, the clinical phenotype may still justify counselling and surveillance of first-degree relatives, particularly if they have suggestive symptoms or organ disease. Counselling also prepares the family for the possibility that more definitive genetic reclassification may occur as variant databases and functional evidence expand.

Advanced therapies may be considered in selected telomere biology disorders, but they are context-dependent. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be curative for severe marrow failure, but it carries special risks in telomere disorders because of tissue fragility and organ involvement, so careful selection and conditioning are required. Liver or lung transplantation may be considered for end-stage organ disease, but these decisions are complex and require multidisciplinary assessment because of the systemic nature of telomere dysfunction. Emerging approaches such as androgen-based therapy have shown potential to improve telomere length or blood counts in some settings, but they remain investigational or incompletely validated for routine use.

At short-term follow-up, the patient improved symptomatically and her blood counts remained stable, which supports the immediate benefit of nutritional repletion and supportive care. However, the case’s main lesson is not the acute response alone, but the need to recognize the underlying predisposition to future progression. Young adults with unexplained cytopenias, premature greying, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis or other signs of accelerated ageing should prompt consideration of a telomere biology disorder even when the classic mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita are absent. Early recognition matters because it changes surveillance, informs family counselling, affects transplant planning, and prevents the disorder from being mistaken for isolated nutritional, autoimmune or infectious disease.
