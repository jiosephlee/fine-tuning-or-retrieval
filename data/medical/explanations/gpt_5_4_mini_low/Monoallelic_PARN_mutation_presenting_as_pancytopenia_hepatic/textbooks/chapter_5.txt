Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 5: ## Management, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons for Practice

Management in this case addressed both the immediately reversible abnormalities and the broader concern for an underlying telomere biology disorder. The patient had severe vitamin B12 deficiency with pancytopenia and biochemical evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis, so she was started on parenteral cyanocobalamin to ensure rapid and reliable repletion. The regimen used—intravenous vitamin B12 daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance—is appropriate when deficiency is severe or when malabsorption is suspected. In patients with megaloblastic anaemia, a reticulocyte response is usually expected within about a week, followed by gradual improvement in haemoglobin and cell counts over subsequent weeks if marrow reserve is intact. Because she was symptomatic and had significant anaemia, she also received whole blood transfusion for immediate support while the underlying deficiency was being corrected.

Correction of concomitant deficiencies was also important. Folate was replaced, since folate deficiency can coexist with B12 deficiency and contribute to megaloblastic change. Vitamin D and calcium supplementation were given because she had biochemical deficiency, secondary hyperparathyroidism, osteoporosis, bone pain and a history of fractures. Electrolyte abnormalities such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia were corrected in the usual manner. These measures were supportive but clinically important because nutritional and metabolic derangements can worsen weakness, neuromuscular symptoms and bone fragility, even when they are not the primary cause of the multisystem syndrome.

Her skeletal disease required symptom-directed care and rehabilitation. Osteoporosis in telomere biology disorders may reflect chronic illness, endocrine disturbance and impaired tissue maintenance, and in this patient it likely contributed to pain and fracture risk. Physiotherapy was therefore included to preserve mobility, improve function and reduce deconditioning. This is particularly relevant in young adults with chronic fatigue and multisystem disease, where loss of conditioning can quickly amplify disability.

Despite the identification of a monoallelic PARN variant, treatment remained supportive rather than disease-specific because there is no established curative medical therapy for telomere biology disorders. In practice, management is guided by the dominant organ involvement: cytopenias, lung disease, liver disease, and complications such as osteoporosis or malignancy risk. For the marrow component, the key issue is whether the patient develops progressive marrow failure. A telomere disorder can make the bone marrow more vulnerable to stress, so serial blood counts and clinical review are essential even if the initial cytopenia is partly explained by nutritional deficiency. In this patient, the short-term improvement after repletion supported a major contribution from B12 deficiency, but the genetic finding means that future haematologic decline cannot be excluded.

Because telomere biology disorders often evolve over time, ongoing surveillance is central to care. This includes monitoring full blood counts, reticulocyte response, liver function and evidence of portal hypertension or worsening fibrosis, as well as pulmonary follow-up for progression of interstitial lung disease. In a young adult with hepatic fibrosis and CT evidence of fibrotic lung change, periodic reassessment is important even if symptoms are mild initially, since organ dysfunction may advance silently. The case illustrates that a telomere disorder may first present to haematology, hepatology or pulmonology, and the diagnosis may only become apparent when the different organ manifestations are considered together.

Genetic counselling was an important part of management once a telomere biology disorder was suspected. This helps the patient and family understand the possible inherited nature of the condition, the uncertainty surrounding a monoallelic variant of uncertain significance, and the limits of current interpretation. Even when the variant is not definitively pathogenic, the clinical phenotype may still justify counselling and surveillance of first-degree relatives, particularly if they have suggestive symptoms or organ disease. Counselling also prepares the family for the possibility that more definitive genetic reclassification may occur as variant databases and functional evidence expand.

Advanced therapies may be considered in selected telomere biology disorders, but they are context-dependent. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be curative for severe marrow failure, but it carries special risks in telomere disorders because of tissue fragility and organ involvement, so careful selection and conditioning are required. Liver or lung transplantation may be considered for end-stage organ disease, but these decisions are complex and require multidisciplinary assessment because of the systemic nature of telomere dysfunction. Emerging approaches such as androgen-based therapy have shown potential to improve telomere length or blood counts in some settings, but they remain investigational or incompletely validated for routine use.

At short-term follow-up, the patient improved symptomatically and her blood counts remained stable, which supports the immediate benefit of nutritional repletion and supportive care. However, the case’s main lesson is not the acute response alone, but the need to recognize the underlying predisposition to future progression. Young adults with unexplained cytopenias, premature greying, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis or other signs of accelerated ageing should prompt consideration of a telomere biology disorder even when the classic mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita are absent. Early recognition matters because it changes surveillance, informs family counselling, affects transplant planning, and prevents the disorder from being mistaken for isolated nutritional, autoimmune or infectious disease.
