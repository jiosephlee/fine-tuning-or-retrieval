Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and the Initial Problem Representation

This patient’s presentation was clinically important not simply because she was fatigued, but because the symptom pattern suggested a chronic, progressive, multisystem illness. Over 5 years, she developed worsening generalized weakness, early fatigability, exertional dyspnoea, weight loss, anorexia, and a steady decline in functional capacity. In a young adult, such a prolonged course should raise concern for more than isolated anaemia or a transient nutritional problem, especially when the decline is out of proportion to ordinary deconditioning. The more rapid deterioration over the preceding 3 months signalled either progression of an underlying systemic disorder or superimposed decompensation, and it increased the urgency of evaluation.

The physical examination added several important clues. She had pallor and icterus, which fit with anaemia and increased red cell turnover or ineffective erythropoiesis. However, the striking findings were the features of premature ageing: premature greying of hair and knuckle hyperpigmentation. In a woman in her late 20s, these are not trivial cosmetic findings; they are red flags for an inherited disorder affecting cellular maintenance and tissue renewal, particularly a telomere biology disorder. The bald, atrophic tongue supported mucosal atrophy and nutritional deficiency, while thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, and generalized weakness suggested broader skeletal and neurologic involvement rather than isolated haematologic disease. Hepatomegaly and reduced breath sounds at the lung bases pointed to additional hepatic and pulmonary pathology.

Taken together, the case was not just one of anaemia. It was a multisystem syndrome involving the haematologic system, liver, lungs, nervous system, and skeleton. That broader pattern is the key to the initial problem representation: a young adult with pancytopenia and ineffective erythropoiesis, together with premature-ageing stigmata and evidence of hepatic and pulmonary disease. Such a representation helps clinicians move beyond single-cause explanations and consider unifying diagnoses.

This is where recognition of clinical red flags becomes essential. Pancytopenia in a young person can result from nutritional deficiency, marrow failure, infection, autoimmune disease, or malignancy, but the coexistence of premature greying, organ fibrosis, osteoporosis, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly should prompt suspicion for a telomere biology disorder even if the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad is absent. Telomere disorders often present incompletely in adults, and the mucocutaneous signs may be subtle or missing. In this case, the initial bedside impression was therefore not “simple vitamin deficiency,” but rather “young adult with chronic cytopenia, premature ageing features, and multisystem involvement,” which appropriately framed the subsequent diagnostic workup.
