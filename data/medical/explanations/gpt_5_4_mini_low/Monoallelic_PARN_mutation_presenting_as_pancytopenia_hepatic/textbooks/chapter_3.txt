Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 3: ## From Differential Diagnosis to Unifying Syndrome: Why a Telomere Biology Disorder Was Suspected

This patient’s presentation initially fit several common acquired explanations for pancytopenia, fatigue, weight loss, and hepatosplenomegaly in a young adult. Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was an especially plausible starting point because it can cause profound fatigue, glossitis, neuropathy, pancytopenia, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and a low reticulocyte count from ineffective erythropoiesis. The peripheral smear and marrow findings supported this: dimorphic red cells, hypersegmented neutrophils, erythroid hyperplasia, elevated iron stores, and no evidence of malignant infiltration all pointed toward impaired DNA synthesis rather than primary marrow destruction. In that sense, nutritional deficiency explained much of the hematologic picture.

However, nutritional deficiency did not fully explain the overall illness. The degree of multisystem involvement was disproportionate, and several features persisted beyond what would be expected from B12 and folate deficiency alone. In particular, premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, hepatic fibrosis, and fibrotic lung disease suggested a broader disorder affecting tissues with high proliferative turnover. This mismatch between a correct partial explanation and the severity of disease was the key clue that the case was not simply one of isolated malnutrition.

A positive direct antiglobulin test briefly raised the possibility of autoimmune hemolysis or an autoimmune cytopenia, but the rest of the data did not support that interpretation. The reticulocyte count was low rather than elevated, which argues against robust peripheral hemolysis. There was also no convincing serologic evidence of systemic autoimmune disease, and the marrow did not show an immune-mediated process. In other words, the DAT result was a potentially misleading finding that did not fit the overall pattern of ineffective erythropoiesis and systemic fibrosis.

The team also had to consider primary bone marrow failure syndromes, infiltrative malignancy, granulomatous disease, and chronic infection. These are important causes of pancytopenia with constitutional symptoms and organomegaly in younger patients. Yet the marrow biopsy showed preserved trilineage haematopoiesis without dysplasia, blasts, or infiltrative disease, and cytogenetics were normal. PET-CT did not reveal a malignant or granulomatous process. These negative studies made an acquired marrow neoplasm or infiltrative disorder less likely and pushed the differential away from a single local haematologic cause.

The liver and lung findings were especially important in reframing the diagnosis. Hepatic stiffness consistent with fibrosis or cirrhosis in a woman in her 20s, together with diffuse fibrotic changes in the lungs, is unusual and should prompt consideration of systemic disorders rather than unrelated organ-specific disease. Common causes of hepatic fibrosis, including autoimmune and viral hepatitis, were not supported by the evaluation. Likewise, the pulmonary changes were not explained by infection or malignancy. When chronic liver disease, pulmonary fibrosis, cytopenia, and premature ageing features appear together in a young adult, an inherited disorder affecting cellular maintenance becomes much more likely.

At that point, the combination of premature greying, pancytopenia, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis suggested a telomere biology disorder. Telomere disorders are classically associated with dyskeratosis congenita, but many patients do not present with the full mucocutaneous triad. Adult-onset disease may manifest instead as marrow dysfunction, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, liver disease, or a mixed multisystem phenotype. That is why the absence of nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, or classic skin pigmentation does not exclude the diagnosis.

The absence of a family history also did not rule it out. Telomere biology disorders can show variable penetrance, delayed onset, and incomplete expression even within the same family. De novo variants are also possible, and mildly affected relatives may go unrecognized. In practice, the clinical question is not whether every affected person will have the textbook phenotype, but whether the pattern of organ involvement is better explained by a single inherited defect than by several unrelated acquired diseases.

In this case, the major diagnostic lesson was the importance of synthesis. Once reversible deficiencies were corrected, the remaining pattern was too coherent to ignore: a young adult with accelerated ageing signs, persistent cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and skeletal fragility. Rather than accepting these as disconnected problems, the team correctly looked for a unifying mechanism. That reasoning led to telomere biology disorder as the most plausible explanation and justified genetic testing, which ultimately supported the diagnosis.
