Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 4: ## PARN and Telomere Biology Disorders: Essential Pathophysiology and Genetic Interpretation

Telomeres are repetitive DNA-protein structures at the ends of chromosomes that act like protective caps. Their job is to preserve genomic stability by preventing chromosome ends from being misread as DNA damage. With each round of cell division, telomeres shorten naturally. When they become critically short, the cell enters senescence or undergoes apoptosis. This is usually a controlled part of aging biology, but in tissues that must continually renew themselves, excessive telomere shortening produces stem-cell exhaustion and organ dysfunction. That is why telomere disorders so often affect highly proliferative tissues such as bone marrow, mucosal epithelium, hair follicles, and, in some patients, hepatocytes and alveolar epithelial cells.

Telomere length is maintained by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein complex that includes the reverse transcriptase component TERT and the RNA template component TERC. Telomerase adds telomeric repeats back to chromosome ends, partly offsetting normal attrition. When genes involved in telomere maintenance are mutated, telomeres shorten prematurely, and cells with high turnover cannot sustain normal regeneration. The resulting group of inherited conditions is called telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Dyskeratosis congenita is the prototypical TBD, but the clinical spectrum is broad and includes bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, liver disease, cancer predisposition, and variable premature-aging features.

Classically, dyskeratosis congenita is recognized by the mucocutaneous triad of abnormal skin pigmentation, nail dystrophy, and oral leukoplakia. However, adults with TBDs often do not show this full triad. Instead, they may present with isolated or predominant marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or combined multisystem disease. This is important for the case at hand: the patient’s presentation was dominated by pancytopenia, premature greying, liver fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis rather than classic mucocutaneous findings. In practice, the absence of the triad does not exclude a telomere disorder, especially in young adults with unexplained disease in multiple organs.

The tissues involved in TBDs are those with high replicative demand or high dependence on epithelial and stem-cell renewal. Bone marrow failure reflects impaired hematopoietic stem-cell renewal. Oral and skin findings arise from epithelial turnover defects. Hair greying and alopecia reflect follicular stem-cell exhaustion. Liver fibrosis may reflect chronic injury and impaired repair in hepatocytes and supporting stromal cells, while pulmonary fibrosis is linked to defective alveolar epithelial repair. This biology explains why a single telomere maintenance defect can produce a seemingly unrelated combination of cytopenias, fibrosis, and premature aging.

PARN is one of the telomere-related genes increasingly recognized in this spectrum. It encodes poly(A)-specific ribonuclease, a deadenylase that shortens poly(A) tails on RNA molecules and thereby regulates RNA stability and gene expression. In telomere biology, PARN has a specific role in the maturation of TERC, the RNA component of telomerase. By participating in processing of the 3′ end of TERC, PARN helps ensure that functional telomerase RNA is available. If PARN function is impaired, TERC maturation is disrupted, telomerase activity falls, and telomeres shorten more rapidly. Thus, even though PARN is not itself a telomerase catalytic subunit, it is biologically upstream of telomere maintenance.

PARN-associated disease shows an important genotype-phenotype distinction. Biallelic PARN variants have been reported in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita and more severe childhood-onset telomere syndromes. Monoallelic PARN variants, in contrast, are more often associated with adult-onset telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. This difference likely reflects partial loss of function or haploinsufficiency in the heterozygous state, producing a milder or later phenotype than complete loss of both alleles. The patient in this case had a monoallelic PARN variant and developed pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and cytopenia, which fits within the expanding clinical range of monoallelic PARN-associated telomere disease.

The identified variant, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), was found in exon 8 and affects a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain of the PARN protein. Conservation across species suggests that this amino acid is functionally important, because evolution tends to preserve residues critical for protein structure or activity. The R3H domain is a recognized functional region, so a substitution there increases suspicion that the change could alter protein behavior. In addition, the variant was rare or absent in population databases and was predicted to be damaging by multiple in silico tools. These observations do not prove pathogenicity, but they strengthen biological plausibility.

This is where ACMG/AMP variant interpretation is useful. The variant meets several supportive criteria: PM1 because it lies in a critical functional domain with no established benign variation; PM2 because it is absent or extremely rare in population databases; PP2 because PARN is a gene in which missense variation is a known disease mechanism and benign missense variation is relatively uncommon; and PP3 because multiple computational algorithms predict a deleterious effect. Despite this evidence, the variant was still classified formally as a Variant of Uncertain Significance because the available data did not satisfy the threshold for likely pathogenic or pathogenic classification. A VUS should not be treated as definitive proof of disease-causing status in isolation. However, it can become clinically persuasive when the phenotype closely matches the gene’s known disease spectrum and alternative explanations have been excluded.

That genotype-phenotype correlation is exactly what happened here. The patient had a pattern of young-onset multisystem disease involving marrow, liver, and lung, along with premature greying and osteoporosis, which strongly suggested a telomere disorder. Even though the PARN variant remained formally uncertain, the overall clinical context made a TBD the most coherent unifying diagnosis. In other words, the variant was not interpreted in a vacuum; it was interpreted alongside the patient’s age, organ involvement, and exclusion of other causes.

The severe vitamin B12 deficiency in this case is also clinically important because it can obscure the underlying diagnosis. B12 deficiency can cause pancytopenia, macrocytosis or dimorphic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and a low reticulocyte count from ineffective erythropoiesis. Those findings were present here and explain much of the hematologic presentation. However, the persistence of syndromic features such as premature greying, fibrosis, and osteoporosis suggested that nutritional deficiency was not the whole story. In a genetically vulnerable marrow, severe B12 deficiency may unmask or amplify cytopenia, because stem cells with already limited replicative reserve are less able to recover from hematopoietic stress.

When TBD is suspected, telomere length testing and next-generation sequencing are the key confirmatory investigations. Telomere length can be measured by flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization, which is often considered the clinical standard in specialized centers. Short telomeres support the diagnosis, particularly when they are markedly below age-adjusted norms. Sequencing then identifies an underlying telomere-related gene defect and helps with counseling, surveillance, and risk assessment. In this case, exome sequencing provided the molecular clue, while the clinical pattern supplied the interpretive framework.

The main lesson is that telomere biology disorders should be considered in young adults with otherwise unexplained combinations of marrow failure, lung fibrosis, liver fibrosis, and signs of premature aging, even when the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad is absent. A monoallelic PARN variant may be enough to produce disease, but its significance must be judged carefully. A VUS does not end the diagnostic process; rather, it requires integration of genetics, phenotype, exclusion of competing diagnoses, and, when available, telomere length testing.
