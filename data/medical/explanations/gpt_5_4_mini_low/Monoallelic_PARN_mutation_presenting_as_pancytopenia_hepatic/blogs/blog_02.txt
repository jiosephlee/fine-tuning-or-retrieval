Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# B12 Deficiency Was Real—But Not the Whole Story: Avoiding Premature Closure in Ineffective Erythropoiesis

## The trap: a true diagnosis that still doesn’t explain everything

This case is a great reminder that finding a real abnormality doesn’t mean you’ve found the whole answer.

A woman in her late 20s presented with years of fatigue, progressive weakness, dyspnea, weight loss, and anorexia. Her labs gave an obvious lead: pancytopenia with a very low reticulocyte count, plus smear findings classic for megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis. Severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed. Easy, right?

Not quite.

Even after nutrient repletion, the broader syndrome was too big to ignore: premature greying, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, neuropathy, and a general “premature aging” phenotype. That’s where the case shifts from common medicine to high-yield diagnostic humility.

## When B12 deficiency explains a lot—but not the whole phenotype

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency can absolutely cause:

- Pancytopenia
- Low reticulocyte count
- Hypersegmented neutrophils
- Dimorphic anemia
- Elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin from ineffective erythropoiesis
- Neurologic symptoms
- Mucosal changes

And in this patient, the marrow fit the story: trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, without malignancy or infiltrative disease.

So yes, the nutritional deficiency was real. But the key question in FOAM-land is always:

**Does this diagnosis unify the entire case?**

Here, the answer was no.

## The clue that should make you pause

The biggest red flags for “something else is going on” were not subtle:

- Young age
- Multisystem involvement
- Premature greying
- Hepatic fibrosis
- Pulmonary fibrosis
- Osteoporosis and fractures
- Neuropathy
- Persistent concern despite correction of deficiencies

B12 deficiency can be dramatic, but it does not usually explain a telomere biology disorder phenotype. When cytopenias coexist with fibrotic disease in the liver and lungs, you should start thinking beyond nutrition.

## Ineffective erythropoiesis is not the same as “just B12 deficiency”

This is an important cognitive distinction.

The marrow can look megaloblastic and the patient can be B12 deficient, but that does not exclude a second process that is lowering physiologic reserve. In this case, the authors suspected that a telomere biology disorder may have made the hematopoietic system more vulnerable to stress from severe deficiency.

That matters because telomere disorders can present as:

- Bone marrow failure
- Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- Premature aging features
- Variable or absent classic mucocutaneous findings

So the nutritional deficiency may be the visible problem, while the inherited disorder is the substrate.

## Why telomere biology disorder entered the differential

Once the team stepped back, the pattern was hard to miss: a young adult with unexplained cytopenia plus liver and lung fibrosis.

That constellation should trigger consideration of a telomere biology disorder, even if you don’t see the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad. In this case, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN variant, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. The variant was not definitive on its own, but the phenotype fit the broader telomere disorder spectrum.

This is where medicine gets messy: sometimes the genetics are not crystal clear, but the clinical correlation still matters.

## Practical bedside lesson: don’t stop at the first answer

A confirmed deficiency should not end the workup if the syndrome is larger than the deficiency.

Ask yourself:

- Does the nutritional deficiency explain all findings?
- Is there organ fibrosis that feels out of proportion?
- Are there “premature aging” features?
- Is cytopenia persistent despite repletion?
- Is there a low reticulocyte count suggesting marrow underproduction rather than simple peripheral loss?
- Are there neurologic, hepatic, pulmonary, or skeletal findings that need a unifying diagnosis?

If the answer is “no, B12 doesn’t explain all of this,” keep going.

## Clinical pearls

- A low reticulocyte count in pancytopenia should immediately push you toward impaired marrow production or ineffective erythropoiesis.
- Severe B12 deficiency can produce striking smear and marrow changes, but it should not be used as a diagnostic “full stop” when the phenotype is multisystemic.
- Pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis in a young adult should raise concern for an inherited telomere disorder.
- Telomere biology disorders may present without classic mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita.
- Genetic testing becomes important when the clinical picture is broader than the initial lab abnormality.

## Take-home points

- **A real diagnosis can be incomplete.** B12 deficiency may be present and clinically important, but still not explain the entire case.
- **Premature closure is the danger.** If cytopenia comes with lung, liver, skeletal, or aging phenotypes, keep looking.
- **Think telomeres.** Young patients with unexplained marrow suppression plus pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis deserve consideration of a telomere biology disorder.
- **Clinical context matters more than a single lab abnormality.** Genetics may not always provide a clean yes/no answer, but it can still support the unifying diagnosis.
- **Repletion is treatment, not closure.** Correct the deficiency, but don’t stop surveillance if the phenotype suggests an inherited vulnerability.

## Bottom line

This case is a good antidote to diagnostic tunnel vision. The B12 deficiency was real and needed treatment, but it was not the whole story. When a patient’s phenotype is bigger than the lab abnormality in front of you, step back and ask what else is being missed. In young adults with pancytopenia plus hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, a telomere biology disorder should be on the list—even if the classic clues are absent.
