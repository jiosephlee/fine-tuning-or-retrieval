Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# A VUS with a Phenotype: How to Use Clinical-Genetic Correlation Without Overcalling the Variant

## Why this case matters

A woman in her late 20s comes in with fatigue, pancytopenia, liver fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. That’s already a “something systemic is going on” story.

Then genetics returns a **monoallelic PARN missense variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)**.

And here’s the teaching point: the result is **not** a molecular free pass to stop thinking, but it’s also **not** a license to declare causality with certainty. This is exactly where clinicians have to do the hard work of **clinical-genetic correlation**—using the gene, the phenotype, and the biology together, while staying honest about uncertainty.

## The case in one line

A young woman with progressive multisystem disease had severe B12 deficiency and ineffective erythropoiesis, but the broader pattern—**premature ageing features, cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis**—pointed toward a **telomere biology disorder (TBD)**, and exome sequencing found a **PARN VUS** that fit the story biologically, even if it didn’t meet the bar for definitive pathogenicity.

## Why the variant was tempting to “call”

The instinct to over-interpret is understandable. The gene-level story is compelling:

PARN is a telomere-related gene. Pathogenic variants are associated with **dyskeratosis congenita / telomere biology disorders**, and monoallelic variants have been linked particularly with **pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure** phenotypes. In this patient, the phenotype was not subtle—there was multisystem involvement in a young adult, including fibrotic disease in organs that are classic telomere targets.

The variant itself was also not random background noise. It was a **missense change in a conserved residue** within the **R3H domain**, and multiple in silico tools predicted it to be damaging. That gives you a biologically plausible candidate.

But plausibility is not proof.

## What a VUS actually means

A VUS means exactly what it says: **the laboratory cannot confidently classify the variant as either pathogenic or benign with the current evidence**.

That’s the key discipline here:

- A **VUS is not diagnostic**
- A **VUS should not be used alone for irreversible decisions**
- A **VUS can still be clinically useful** when the phenotype strongly suggests the right disease process

This distinction matters because genetics can create false certainty if we let a report’s wording outrun the evidence.

## How to think about clinical-genetic correlation

In real life, variant interpretation is rarely binary. The question isn’t, “Is this variant definitely causal?” It’s often, “Does this variant help explain the phenotype enough to change what I do next?”

In this case, the answer was “yes, but carefully.”

The patient had a pattern that made a **telomere biology disorder** much more likely than isolated nutritional deficiency alone:

- young age
- premature greying
- persistent cytopenia
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- pulmonary fibrosis
- osteoporosis
- neuropathy

Even though severe **vitamin B12 and folate deficiency** clearly contributed to the cytopenias and ineffective erythropoiesis, they did **not** neatly explain the whole picture. That’s where the gene result becomes clinically relevant—not as a final answer, but as a strong piece of the puzzle.

## A practical framework for VUS interpretation at the bedside

When you get a VUS, try this sequence:

### 1) Start with phenotype first
Ask: does the patient’s presentation match a known disease mechanism for this gene?

Here, yes:
- PARN is a telomere-related gene
- Telomere disorders can present with marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, and liver disease
- The patient had all of those elements, plus premature ageing features

### 2) Ask whether the variant itself has supportive features
In this case:
- missense variant
- conserved residue
- predicted damaging by multiple in silico tools
- located in a functional domain

That’s supportive, but still not enough by itself for pathogenicity.

### 3) Check whether the lab’s classification reflects true uncertainty
The report labeled it a **VUS**. That should slow you down, not shut you down.

### 4) Look for orthogonal support
Do you have:
- segregation data?
- telomere length testing?
- prior case reports of the exact variant?
- a second hit?
- family history?

Not necessarily. And if not, you still may have enough to act on the phenotype.

### 5) Use the result to guide surveillance, not overconfident labeling
This is where the value often lies.

## What you can do with a VUS, clinically

A VUS can justify a **higher index of suspicion** and more structured follow-up when the phenotype is convincing.

In this case, that meant:

- **genetic counseling**
- ongoing surveillance of **hepatic and pulmonary function**
- monitoring for evolving **bone marrow failure**
- coordination with relevant subspecialists

That is different from saying, “This variant caused everything.”

It’s more accurate to say, “This variant, in this clinical context, supports a telomere biology disorder phenotype and should change how closely we watch this patient.”

## Don’t let one diagnosis explain away the whole case

One of the best lessons here is that multiple things can be true:

- She had **severe B12 deficiency**, which likely explained much of the ineffective erythropoiesis and pancytopenia
- She also had a broader syndrome suggestive of **telomere biology disorder**
- The **PARN VUS** may be part of that syndrome, but it is not definitive proof

That’s a healthy diagnostic posture: resist the urge to collapse a complex case into a single tidy explanation.

## Why this matters for telomere disorders specifically

Telomere biology disorders are notorious for being under-recognized because they don’t always present with the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita.

Instead, they can show up as:
- unexplained cytopenias
- idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- premature ageing features
- variable multiorgan dysfunction

That means a VUS in a relevant gene may be the first molecular clue in a disease that is otherwise clinically “messy.”

In that setting, the job is not to force certainty—it’s to identify risk early enough to change care.

## Clinical pearls

- A **VUS is not a diagnosis**, but it can still be a useful clue when the phenotype fits.
- In suspected **telomere biology disorders**, think beyond the marrow: **lung, liver, skin/hair, bone, and neurologic features** may all matter.
- **Severe nutritional deficiency and inherited predisposition can coexist**; one does not exclude the other.
- If the phenotype is strong, use the genetic result to trigger **surveillance and counseling**, not definitive language you can’t support.
- Be careful with ACMG-style evidence: supportive in silico and domain data are helpful, but they rarely settle causality on their own.
- When the presentation is syndromic, **family history can be absent**; de novo disease, variable penetrance, and incomplete expression are real.

## Take-home points

- **Don’t overcall a VUS.** The label means uncertainty, and that matters.
- **Do integrate phenotype and biology.** A VUS in a gene like **PARN** may carry meaningful clinical weight when the syndrome fits.
- **Use the result to guide follow-up.** Surveillance, counseling, and subspecialty involvement are appropriate even without definitive variant classification.
- **Keep the differential broad.** Nutritional deficiency, marrow failure, autoimmune disease, and inherited syndromes can overlap.
- **The patient’s phenotype is the diagnosis engine.** Genetics should refine your suspicion, not replace clinical reasoning.

## Bottom line

This case is a great reminder that genomics in the ED/inpatient world is rarely black-and-white. A **PARN VUS** did not prove causality—but in the context of **young-onset multisystem fibrosis, cytopenia, and premature ageing features**, it strongly supported a **telomere biology disorder** and justified ongoing surveillance and counseling.

The art is knowing when to say:  
**“I can’t call this variant pathogenic yet, but I can absolutely call this phenotype concerning.”**
