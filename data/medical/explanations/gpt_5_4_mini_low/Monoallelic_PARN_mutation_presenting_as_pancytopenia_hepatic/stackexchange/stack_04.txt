Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] What is the normal biologic role of PARN, and how can a pathogenic PARN variant lead to telomere shortening and multisystem manifestations such as bone marrow dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis?

PARN, or poly(A)-specific ribonuclease, is a highly conserved 3′-5′ exoribonuclease whose normal biologic role is to shorten the poly(A) tail of mRNA and thereby regulate RNA stability and gene expression. In telomere biology, its key additional function is more specific and clinically important: PARN is required for proper maturation of TERC, the RNA template component of telomerase. By removing aberrant oligoadenylated tails from TERC, PARN helps stabilize TERC and supports assembly and activity of the telomerase complex.

When PARN is pathogenic, TERC processing is impaired, telomerase function falls, and telomere maintenance becomes defective. The result is accelerated telomere shortening and premature cellular senescence or apoptosis, especially in tissues with high turnover or regenerative demand. That is why telomere biology disorders preferentially affect the bone marrow, lungs, liver, skin, and other proliferative compartments. In the case presented, this biology explains the combination of pancytopenia, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, premature graying, and osteoporosis in a young woman. Even though her severe vitamin B12 deficiency contributed to ineffective erythropoiesis, the broader multisystem pattern was disproportionate to nutritional deficiency alone and fit a telomere biology disorder.

A useful teaching point is that monoallelic PARN variants, as in this case, are often associated with adult-onset telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure, whereas biallelic variants more classically cause the more severe dyskeratosis congenita spectrum. Thus, the pathogenic mechanism is not a classic single-organ defect, but a systemic failure of telomere maintenance that gradually unmasks fibrosis and marrow dysfunction in multiple organs.
