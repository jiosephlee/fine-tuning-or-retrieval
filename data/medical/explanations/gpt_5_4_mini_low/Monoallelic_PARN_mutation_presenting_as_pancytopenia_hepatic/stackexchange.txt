Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Morning Report] In a young adult with pancytopenia, low reticulocyte count, hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings, basophilic stippling, and severe vitamin B12 deficiency, what hematologic process is most strongly suggested, and which findings help distinguish ineffective erythropoiesis from peripheral hemolysis?

This constellation most strongly suggests **ineffective erythropoiesis from megaloblastic anemia due to severe vitamin B12 deficiency**, rather than true peripheral hemolysis.

The key pathophysiology is that impaired DNA synthesis causes **nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony in erythroid precursors**: the marrow is working hard, but many developing red cells die **within the marrow before they ever reach the circulation**. That is why patients can have anemia, elevated LDH, and indirect hyperbilirubinemia, yet still show a **low reticulocyte count**—the hallmark of ineffective erythropoiesis.

Several findings in this case support that mechanism:

- **Hypersegmented neutrophils**: classic for megaloblastic anemia  
- **Low reticulocyte count**: argues against an appropriate marrow response to peripheral destruction  
- **Cabot rings and basophilic stippling**: reflect abnormal erythrocyte maturation and dyserythropoiesis  
- **Bone marrow erythroid hyperplasia** with no malignancy: consistent with a marrow trying, but failing, to produce effective red cells  
- **Elevated iron and ferritin**: often seen when iron is not being efficiently incorporated into mature erythrocytes

By contrast, **peripheral hemolysis** typically shows:
- a **high reticulocyte count** if the marrow is intact,
- more evidence of circulating RBC destruction,
- and, depending on the cause, spherocytes, schistocytes, or a clearly hemolytic smear pattern.

So in this patient, the lab pattern—especially the **low reticulocyte count plus megaloblastic smear findings**—points to **ineffective erythropoiesis**, even though LDH and bilirubin are elevated and the DAT is positive. The case report correctly emphasizes that these hemolysis markers can be misleading when the primary problem is **intramedullary precursor destruction** rather than peripheral RBC loss.

### [Clinical Pearls] How would you interpret a positive direct antiglobulin test for IgG in this patient when the reticulocyte count is low and the bone marrow shows erythroid hyperplasia without evidence of immune-mediated destruction?

In this patient, the positive direct antiglobulin test for IgG should be interpreted cautiously: it is **not, by itself, evidence of clinically important autoimmune hemolysis**.

The key point is the **physiology does not fit immune-mediated peripheral RBC destruction**. In true warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia, one expects a **reticulocytosis** as the marrow compensates for hemolysis, and the marrow may show active erythropoiesis in response to peripheral destruction. Here, however, the **reticulocyte count was low**, and the bone marrow showed **erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective erythropoiesis**, not destruction. That pattern is much more consistent with **intramedullary RBC failure** from severe **vitamin B12/folate deficiency**, which was clearly present in this case.

So, the DAT likely represents an **incidental or nonspecific positive finding** rather than the primary explanation for the cytopenia. This is a recognized pitfall: a DAT can be positive in patients who do **not** have autoimmune hemolytic anemia, especially when another process—such as severe megaloblastic anemia—better explains the laboratory and marrow findings.

In short, the positive IgG DAT should be viewed as a **supportive but non-diagnostic abnormality** here; the patient’s anemia was driven mainly by **ineffective erythropoiesis** in the setting of nutritional deficiency, with the broader clinical picture ultimately pointing to an underlying **telomere biology disorder**.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical features in this case should prompt suspicion for a telomere biology disorder despite the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita?

Even without the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, this case should raise strong suspicion for a telomere biology disorder because it shows a **premature-aging, multisystem fibrotic phenotype in a young adult**. The key clues are:

- **Unexplained cytopenias with a low reticulocyte count**, suggesting impaired marrow production rather than peripheral destruction  
- **Pulmonary fibrosis** in a woman in her late 20s, which is highly atypical for an acquired process at that age  
- **Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis with hepatosplenomegaly**, another recognized telomere-related manifestation  
- **Premature greying of hair**, a classic “accelerated aging” clue in telomere disorders  
- **Osteoporosis and fractures** at a young age, reflecting impaired tissue renewal  
- **Neuropathy and other systemic involvement**, which make a simple nutritional deficiency or isolated hematologic disorder less likely  
- Persistence of the syndrome **despite correction of vitamin B12/folate deficiency**, indicating that deficiency alone did not explain the full picture

The teaching point is that **telomere biology disorders often present in adulthood with organ-specific disease—especially bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, and liver disease—before, or instead of, the classic skin, nail, and oral findings**. In this case, the combination of **early-onset cytopenia plus pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis with premature greying** is what should trigger suspicion.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the normal biologic role of PARN, and how can a pathogenic PARN variant lead to telomere shortening and multisystem manifestations such as bone marrow dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis?

PARN, or poly(A)-specific ribonuclease, is a highly conserved 3′-5′ exoribonuclease whose normal biologic role is to shorten the poly(A) tail of mRNA and thereby regulate RNA stability and gene expression. In telomere biology, its key additional function is more specific and clinically important: PARN is required for proper maturation of TERC, the RNA template component of telomerase. By removing aberrant oligoadenylated tails from TERC, PARN helps stabilize TERC and supports assembly and activity of the telomerase complex.

When PARN is pathogenic, TERC processing is impaired, telomerase function falls, and telomere maintenance becomes defective. The result is accelerated telomere shortening and premature cellular senescence or apoptosis, especially in tissues with high turnover or regenerative demand. That is why telomere biology disorders preferentially affect the bone marrow, lungs, liver, skin, and other proliferative compartments. In the case presented, this biology explains the combination of pancytopenia, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, premature graying, and osteoporosis in a young woman. Even though her severe vitamin B12 deficiency contributed to ineffective erythropoiesis, the broader multisystem pattern was disproportionate to nutritional deficiency alone and fit a telomere biology disorder.

A useful teaching point is that monoallelic PARN variants, as in this case, are often associated with adult-onset telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure, whereas biallelic variants more classically cause the more severe dyskeratosis congenita spectrum. Thus, the pathogenic mechanism is not a classic single-organ defect, but a systemic failure of telomere maintenance that gradually unmasks fibrosis and marrow dysfunction in multiple organs.

### [Morning Report] What is the differential diagnosis for a young woman with pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, pulmonary fibrotic changes, premature greying, and hepatic fibrosis, and which elements of the workup in this case argue against autoimmune, infectious, malignant, and infiltrative causes?

In a young woman with **pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, fibrotic lung disease, premature greying, and hepatic fibrosis**, the differential should first include **nutritional megaloblastosis or other causes of ineffective hematopoiesis**, **bone marrow failure syndromes**, **autoimmune cytopenias or connective-tissue disease**, **chronic infection**, **hematologic malignancy**, and **infiltrative/granulomatous disease**. In this case, however, the broader phenotype points strongly toward a **telomere biology disorder**—the dyskeratosis congenita spectrum—including **PARN-associated disease**, which can present in adulthood with marrow dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, liver fibrosis, and premature aging features even without the classic mucocutaneous triad.

Several elements of the workup argue against the major acquired causes:

- **Against autoimmune disease:** although the DAT was positive, the picture did not support immune hemolysis or autoimmune cytopenia. The **reticulocyte count was low**, not appropriately elevated for hemolysis; marrow showed **ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral destruction**; and **ANA and autoimmune hepatitis serologies were negative**. There were no convincing systemic connective-tissue disease features to unify the findings.

- **Against infection:** the infectious evaluation was unrevealing, with testing for **viral hepatitis, HIV, CMV, and scrub typhus negative**, and the isolated **EBV IgG positivity** indicated prior exposure rather than active disease. The chronic multisystem fibrotic phenotype is also not explained by an acute or subacute infection.

- **Against malignancy:** bone marrow examination showed **trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia**, but **no blasts, dysplasia, or infiltrative process**, and **cytogenetics were normal**. PET-CT did not show a focal hypermetabolic malignant process.

- **Against infiltrative/granulomatous disease:** PET-CT showed **no evidence of granulomatous disease or overt infiltrative malignancy**, and marrow histology likewise did not demonstrate infiltration. The liver and lung findings were fibrotic rather than mass-like or granulomatous.

So the teaching point is that when a young patient has **multiorgan fibrosis plus marrow failure and premature aging features**, and the usual autoimmune, infectious, malignant, and infiltrative causes are not supported by targeted testing, you should think of a **telomere disorder** early. In this case, exome sequencing identifying a **monoallelic PARN variant** provided the unifying explanation.

### [Clinical Pearls] When a clinical exome identifies a monoallelic PARN missense variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, what factors can still support a clinicogenetic diagnosis of telomere biology disorder, and what are the limitations of that inference?

A monoallelic **PARN** missense variant classified as a **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)** does **not, by itself, establish causality**. In this case, however, the **clinicogenetic context** can still strongly support a diagnosis of a **telomere biology disorder (TBD)**.

What supports the diagnosis is the **phenotype**, not the variant alone: a young adult with **premature greying**, **pancytopenia/ineffective hematopoiesis**, **hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis**, **pulmonary fibrosis**, **osteoporosis**, and other features of accelerated aging. That pattern is highly suggestive of a TBD, especially once more common explanations—malignancy, autoimmune disease, infection, and isolated nutritional deficiency—were not sufficient to explain the multisystem disease. The genetic finding becomes more persuasive because the variant lies in **PARN**, a gene already established in telomere maintenance disorders, and the specific missense change was in a **highly conserved residue within a critical functional domain**, was **rare/absent in population databases**, and was **predicted in silico to be deleterious**. In a patient whose clinical picture fits the known spectrum of **PARN-associated telomere disease**, these features can justify a **probable clinicogenetic diagnosis** and guide surveillance and counseling.

The limitation is equally important: a **VUS is not proof of pathogenicity**. Without **segregation data**, **functional assays**, or ideally **telomere length testing** showing marked shortening, one cannot definitively say that this exact variant caused the disease. In this case, the pancytopenia also had a plausible competing explanation—**severe vitamin B12 deficiency**—so the variant may reflect a **predisposing or contributory** defect rather than the sole cause of marrow failure. Thus, the correct inference is not “this VUS diagnoses TBD,” but rather: **the patient’s multisystem phenotype is highly consistent with a TBD, and the PARN VUS provides supportive but not definitive genetic corroboration**.

### [Management] What additional diagnostic test is considered a key confirmatory study in suspected telomere biology disorders beyond next-generation sequencing, and how would its result strengthen the diagnosis in this patient?

A key confirmatory test in suspected telomere biology disorder is **telomere length assessment by flow cytometry–fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow-FISH)**.

In this patient, a **telomere length below the age-adjusted normal range** would strongly support the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder, especially when interpreted alongside her clinical phenotype: premature greying, cytopenias, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. In practice, a markedly shortened telomere length provides the functional evidence that the identified **monoallelic PARN variant** is biologically relevant, helping move the diagnosis from a genetic suspicion based on next-generation sequencing alone to a **confirmed telomere-mediated disorder**.

### [Management] How should long-term management and surveillance be structured for a patient with suspected PARN-associated telomere biology disorder, including monitoring for marrow failure progression, pulmonary and hepatic complications, genetic counseling, and consideration of disease-modifying or transplant-based therapies?

In a patient like this woman in her late 20s, the long-term approach is best organized as management of a **telomere biology disorder with multisystem surveillance**, rather than treatment of isolated cytopenia, fibrosis, or nutritional deficiency alone. Her case is typical of this problem: the severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency explained much of the pancytopenia, but the combination of **premature greying, osteoporosis, pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and interstitial lung disease** makes an inherited telomere disorder the unifying diagnosis, even without classic mucocutaneous dyskeratosis congenita features.

**First, the marrow must be followed for evolution from reversible cytopenia to true marrow failure.** Serial complete blood counts, reticulocyte counts, and clinical assessment for bleeding, infections, and transfusion needs are essential. In this case, the marrow initially showed trilineage hematopoiesis without dysplasia or malignancy, so there is not yet evidence of aplastic anemia or MDS; however, patients with PARN-associated telomere disease can progress over time. Any worsening cytopenias should prompt repeat marrow evaluation, including cytogenetics, because clonal evolution and myelodysplasia are recognized late complications of telomere disorders.

**Second, pulmonary surveillance is mandatory.** Because monoallelic PARN variants are strongly linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis, baseline and serial pulmonary function testing, high-resolution chest imaging when clinically indicated, and close symptom review for dyspnea and cough are appropriate. In this patient, PET-CT already showed diffuse ground-glass and fibrotic change, so follow-up should focus on functional decline, oxygen requirement, and referral to pulmonology. She should also avoid pulmonary toxicities where possible, since telomere-shortened patients can be unusually vulnerable to physiologic stress.

**Third, liver disease requires ongoing hepatology follow-up.** Hepatic fibrosis is increasingly recognized in telomere disorders and, in this case, was confirmed by transient elastography. Surveillance should include periodic liver chemistries, synthetic function, platelet count as a portal hypertension clue, and repeat noninvasive fibrosis assessment. Because cirrhosis and portal hypertension may evolve silently, abdominal imaging and hepatology review are important, especially if there is splenomegaly, ascites, thrombocytopenia, or worsening transaminases.

**Fourth, genetic counseling is essential.** The family should be counseled that PARN-associated disease can show variable penetrance and may be inherited in an autosomal dominant or recessive pattern depending on the variant context; this patient’s monoallelic variant may still be clinically relevant despite being reported as a VUS. Counseling should cover recurrence risk, the possibility of other affected relatives, and the need to avoid unnecessary donor selection from biologically related family members until their telomere biology status is understood. In practice, cascade testing and telomere-length assessment can help refine risk in relatives.

**Fifth, treatment remains largely supportive, but disease-modifying and definitive options should be considered early in progressive disease.** Supportive measures include correction of vitamin deficiencies, transfusion support when needed, infection prophylaxis if neutropenia develops, osteoporosis management, and avoidance of marrow-toxic or hepatotoxic agents. Androgens such as **danazol** have been used in telomere disorders and may improve telomere attrition or blood counts in selected patients, but evidence is limited and long-term benefit is uncertain; they are not standard curative therapy. For patients with progressive marrow failure, **hematopoietic stem cell transplantation** is the only potentially curative option for the hematologic component, but it requires special caution because telomere disorder patients have increased transplant toxicity, pulmonary and hepatic vulnerability, and need carefully reduced-intensity conditioning and expert donor selection. If lung or liver disease becomes advanced, organ-specific transplantation may also enter discussion, but the multisystem nature of telomere disease makes this complex and center-dependent.

So, the practical plan is: **serial marrow monitoring, scheduled pulmonary and hepatic surveillance, genetic counseling and family evaluation, and early referral to specialized centers for consideration of androgens or transplant if progression occurs.** In this case, the appropriate next step is exactly what was begun: correction of deficiencies, supportive care, and close longitudinal follow-up, because the major risk is not the current blood count alone, but progression of the underlying telomere disorder.
