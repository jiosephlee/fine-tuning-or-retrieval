# The Scary Post-TAVR Gradient That Improved: PPM, Underexpansion, or Flow?

What makes this case catchy is embarrassingly familiar: immediate post-implant echo shows a terrifying mean gradient (67 mm Hg) after a technically successful TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, and then—surprise—gradients settle to a reasonable 26–27 mm Hg by 1 month with clinical improvement. That trajectory forces you to ask: was this true severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) that somehow resolved, mechanical underexpansion that self-corrected, a temporary low-flow/high-gradient physiology, or simply an echo/flow artefact? The answer matters because our management options (watchful waiting, balloon post‑dilation, acceptable reintervention) carry very different risks in a heavily stented root.

This post walks through a practical, CT- and echo‑driven approach to a high immediate post‑TAVI gradient—how to think through the mechanisms, what measurements help you decide, and pragmatic thresholds for observation, further dilation, or reintervention anchored to VARC-3 endpoints.

## Why this pattern happens (mechanisms)
Several non‑mutually exclusive mechanisms can produce an alarming immediate high gradient that improves over days–weeks:

- Mechanical underexpansion: incomplete radial expansion of the new THV due to a rigid prior frame(s). Post‑dilatation or gradual radial creep of a self‑expanding frame can improve orifice area over time.
- Low‑flow/high‑gradient physiology: immediately post‑implant the LV may be stunned, pacing may transiently alter hemodynamics, or low stroke volume may create artificially high gradients despite small effective orifice area. As LV output recovers (or volumes change), gradients fall.
- Pressure recovery / Doppler misinterpretation: in small aortic roots or overlapping frames, Doppler may overestimate gradients; careful use of continuity equation and DVI mitigates this.
- True severe PPM: the implanted valve’s effective orifice area (EOA) is too small for patient body size and will remain high gradient chronically.
- Valve leaflet restriction from residual tissue, thrombus, or incomplete expansion—may improve with dilation or time if partial expansion occurs.

In the presented case the immediate mean gradient was 67 mm Hg after an Acurate neo2 was implanted inside an Evolut inside a Lotus. Post‑dilation was performed and coronary flow remained preserved. By 1 month the mean gradient had fallen to 27 mm Hg with AVA 1.1 cm² and clinical recovery—favouring partially reversible underexpansion/flow effects rather than fixed catastrophic PPM.

## Key measurements to help discriminate causes
Don’t rely on gradient alone. Use multiple, concordant datapoints:

- Indexed AVA (AVA/BSA): best metric for PPM. Severe PPM typically indexed AVA ≤0.65 cm²/m² (sometimes ≤0.6 depending on source); moderate 0.65–0.85 cm²/m². In the case the indexed AVA immediately post‑implant was 0.4 cm²/m² (severe), later improved to an AVA 1.1 cm² (indexed ~0.44–0.5 depending on BSA)—still small but much better hemodynamics.
- Doppler Velocity Index (DVI = LVOT VTI / aortic VTI): less flow‑dependent than calculated AVA. DVI <0.25 suggests severe stenosis; 0.25–0.50 is moderate‑severe zone. Here DVI was 0.24 immediately (severe), but clinical course matters.
- Stroke volume index / flow status: low stroke volume index (<35 mL/m²) can create low‑flow, high‑gradient states. Document whether stroke volume/EF are depressed immediately post‑implant and whether they recover.
- Valve morphology on TTE/TEE: leaflet motion, presence of paravalvular leak, gradients across frame layers.
- CT imaging: helps identify underexpansion, frame interaction, valve‑to‑coronary distances and whether there is room for further dilation or potential for coronary compromise with post‑dilation.
- Temporal trend: repeat TTE at 24–72 hours and at 1 month. Rapid improvement points to reversible causes.

## Practical algorithm: what to do in the cath lab, immediate post‑implant, and early follow‑up

1. Immediate intra‑procedure (after deployment)
   - Confirm correct position and absence of significant paravalvular leak on angiography and echo.
   - If gradient is high, first exclude technical issues: malposition, wire entrapment, severe regurgitation, or catastrophic coronary obstruction.
   - Check LV function and hemodynamics: measure transvalvular gradient invasively if uncertain.
   - If underexpansion is suspected and anatomy allows (CT review supports safety), consider cautious balloon post‑dilatation. In heavily layered frames (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) anticipate higher radial resistance and risk to coronary flow—plan for coronary protection if repeat dilation is aggressive.
   - If borderline but anatomy risky for further dilation (or if coronary risk unacceptable), consider conservative management with close monitoring.

2. Immediately post‑procedure (first 24–72 hours)
   - Obtain transthoracic echo with DVI, AVA (continuity), peak/mean gradients, stroke volume index, and evaluation of leaflet motion.
   - If DVI <0.25 and indexed AVA ≤0.6 with preserved stroke volume and symptoms, strongly consider reintervention/optimization if safe to do so.
   - If low stroke volume or depressed LV function is present, and no mechanical obstruction is obvious, observe 48–72 h for hemodynamic recovery; repeat TTE. Immediate reintervention may be premature.

3. Early follow‑up (1 month)
   - Repeat TTE. If gradients fall substantially (as in this case to ~27 mm Hg) and patient is clinically improved, conservative management with surveillance is reasonable.
   - Persistent severe gradients with consistent low indexed AVA and DVI suggest true severe PPM or fixed underexpansion → consider reintervention strategies (post‑dilation, valve fracture if feasible, or surgery—tailored to anatomy and risk).

## Special considerations in layered (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) anatomy
- Radial resistance is higher; full expansion may require higher‑pressure balloons; but higher pressure increases risk of annular/root injury and coronary compromise.
- Commissural alignment and valve selection matter for maximizing effective orifice area and future coronary access.
- CT planning is essential before any aggressive post‑dilation: assess valve‑to‑coronary distance and sinus dimensions. If balloon fracture or aggressive dilation is considered, know whether the inner valve frame is amenable to fracture (some platforms are not).
- In cases with prior endocarditis, ensure infection is completely resolved before reintervention.

## VARC‑3 endpoints and decision thresholds
Use VARC‑3 definitions to judge procedural success and guide next steps:
- Procedural success includes acceptable gradient and no major complications. An immediate mean gradient >40 mm Hg or indexed AVA consistent with severe PPM suggests incomplete success.
- However, VARC‑3 recognizes that hemodynamics can evolve; early device success (within 72 hours) and final device success (up to 30 days) are both relevant—so do not label the case failed based only on immediate echo if the patient is clinically improving and imaging suggests reversible causes.
- Reintervention is indicated when there is true valve dysfunction meeting VARC criteria (symptomatic severe stenosis/regurgitation, haemodynamic compromise) after careful exclusion of reversible contributors.

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t panic on the table solely because Doppler gradients are high—synthesize DVI, indexed AVA, stroke volume index, invasive gradients, and CT anatomy.
- DVI is your friend in layered frames: less influenced by LVOT sizing errors and helpful when LVOT measurement is complicated by prior stents.
- If immediate gradients are high but stroke volume is low or LV is recovering, observe 48–72 h and repeat imaging—many patients improve.
- If you plan post‑dilation in a multi‑stent root, pre‑procedural CT review must confirm coronary safety; consider prophylactic coronary protection if distances are borderline.
- Use VARC‑3 windows: early haemodynamic derangement may not equate to procedural failure. Reassess at 24–72 h and 30 days before radical steps unless the patient is unstable.

## Take‑home messages
- High immediate post‑TAVR gradients can reflect underexpansion, low‑flow physiology, Doppler artefact, or true PPM—distinguish using DVI, indexed AVA, stroke volume index, and temporal trends.
- In layered TAV-in-TAV anatomies, CT-guided planning dictates whether safe post‑dilatation is an option; if unsafe, short-term observation is reasonable when clinical status allows.
- Use early repeat imaging (24–72 h) and a 1‑month reassessment—many gradients improve as LV function and valve expansion evolve; management should be individualized and guided by VARC‑3 success criteria.
- When in doubt, a multidisciplinary heart team discussion that integrates echo, CT, invasive hemodynamics and the patient’s clinical course will yield the safest plan.

This patient’s course—immediate mean gradient 67 mm Hg falling to 27 mm Hg at 1 month with clinical recovery—illustrates the nuance: scary echoes deserve rigorous assessment, not reflexive escalation.
