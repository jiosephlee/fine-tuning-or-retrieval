Title: Blog about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

# Keeping the doors open: preserving coronary access through layered frames

This case is a neat illustration of one of the hard lessons from “TAVI for life”: every valve we put in today shapes the options for tomorrow. A man in his late 70s with two prior TAVIs (Lotus → Evolut) developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, was successfully treated, and later presented with severe restenosis of the TAV-in-TAV. The team performed a third transcatheter implantation (Acurate neo2) guided by CT-based planning, deliberate commissural alignment and a high implant strategy. No coronary protection was used because CT modelling showed adequate clearance, and at 1 year the patient is asymptomatic with stable valve function.

Why this case is instructive
- Triple-stented aortic anatomy is becoming more common as TAVI expands. The Achilles’ heel in repeat TAV procedures is coronary reaccess — and once you “bury” the coronaries behind multiple frames and neo-skirts, future PCI becomes much harder or impossible.
- The team translated pre-procedure CT into a clear, actionable implant plan: target a device with favourable haemodynamics and a low/short skirt, align commissures to predictable coronary windows, and implant high to avoid creating a blocking neo-skirt. The one-year result validates this strategy in a very high-risk, post‑endocarditis patient.

How to translate this into practice — a practical roadmap

1) CT-based commissural-to-coronary mapping (do this every time you’re re‑intervening)
- Reconstruct the prosthesis frames and map commissural posts relative to coronary ostia in 3D. Measure valve-to-coronary (VTC) distances and sinus of Valsalva volumes. When multiple frames are present, assess the cumulative mesh and the projected neo‑skirt height.
- Identify the target landing zone relative to the existing leaflets (are you going to land at the level of the inner valve leaflets to pin them open, or higher/lower?). In this case the CT showed feasibility for a high implant that would fix the Evolut leaflets against the stent frame and minimise neo‑skirt below the coronaries.
- Use the CT to choose fluoroscopic projection angles and to plan the delivery orientation needed to place the new valve commissures away from the coronary ostia.

2) Valve platform selection — pick devices that help you keep the doors open
- Prioritise devices with:
  - Short skirts or predictable neo-skirt geometry
  - Low frame height or large cell geometry for future catheter access
  - Established techniques for commissural alignment
  In this case the team selected Acurate neo2 for its supra‑annular leaflets, relatively low frame, and favourable haemodynamics in narrow anatomies.
- If you need maximal radial expansion of the old frame, know which frames are amenable to bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) and which are not — self‑expanding nitinol frames (e.g., Evolut) are not reliably fractured like balloon‑expandable frames.

3) Implant strategy — align, go high, minimise the neo-skirt
- Commissural alignment: rotate the delivery system based on CT/fluoro landmarks so that new commissures sit between coronary ostia. Predictable commissural placement reduces the chance of coronary columns being behind heavy mesh.
- High implantation: when safe (i.e., not risking coronary obstruction or paravalvular leak), a higher implant can pin previous leaflets outward, reduce the amount of neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia and preserve coronary windows. That was the deliberate choice here.
- Post‑dilatation can be valuable: in this case post‑dilatation and partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame contributed to improved gradients over weeks — but be cautious: aggressive post‑dilatation in layered frames can risk annular injury or conduction system damage.

4) Intraprocedural coronary protection and bailouts — have a plan and equipment ready
- Routine coronary protection (prophylactic wire ± balloon/stent) is not always required, but in any case where CT shows borderline VTC or small sinuses, position a guidewire in the target coronary and keep a balloon/stent ready.
- Chimney stenting is a lifesaving bailout for acute coronary obstruction but has downsides: it creates a permanent conduit that complicates future interventions and can cause stent thrombosis or flow turbulence in sinuses.
- BASILICA (leaflet laceration) prevents leaflet-related coronary obstruction but is of uncertain utility in triple-stented anatomies: prior multiple frames and an already-altered leaflet geometry reduce predictability of laceration and may blunt the intended effect.
- BVF can increase effective orifice area in some balloon-expandable surgical or transcatheter valves; in layered configurations and with nitinol frames its effectiveness is limited and unpredictable. In this case no intentional BVF was performed — balloon post‑dilation was used to improve expansion.
- Always have a written bailout corridor: which coronary to protect first, what stent size you will use for a chimney, who will initiate emergency CABG if needed, and how you will manage access if coronary wiring becomes difficult.

5) Documentation — make life easier for the next operator
- Save and store:
  - The CT commissural-to-coronary maps and intended fluoroscopic angles
  - The delivery system orientation/rotation used for commissural alignment (frames are labelled; note rotations in degrees)
  - Implant depth (distance from annulus to valve frame), valve model/size, and exact deployment height relative to prior valve landmarks
  - Whether coronary protection was used and the location of any jailed wires or stents
- When discharging or handing over, provide a concise “future-PCI note”: predicted coronary windows, recommended C-arm views, and which stent cells offer the best access path.

Why some bailout strategies are limited in triple‑stent anatomy
- BASILICA depends on lacerating native/prosthetic leaflets to create a channel. When leaflets are sandwiched between two or more frames, laceration may not reliably create a wide, durable escape path for coronaries — the stent mesh can still obstruct.
- BVF is effective for certain balloon‑expandable frames (stent fracture to increase EOA), but with nested self‑expanding frames the central obstruction source is often pinned leaflets and limiting neo‑skirt geometry rather than a single rigid frame that can be fractured.
- Chimney stenting works as a rescue but converts a surgical problem into a long-term PCI problem; in a patient with multivessel CAD and prior PCI (as in this case), that may complicate future management.

What this case showed — practical outcomes
- The team used CT-driven planning to decide that coronary protection was not needed and to orient commissures and implant depth. They chose a valve with favourable geometry (Acurate neo2) and performed high implantation with post‑dilatation. Immediately post‑implant gradients were high (mean 67 mm Hg) but improved markedly over the following month (mean 27 mm Hg), reflecting mechanical expansion and optimisation. At 1 year the patient remained active, asymptomatic, with stable gradients and no recurrent infection.

Clinical pearls
- Never assume coronary access will be possible after TAV-in-TAV. Always do CT mapping in re‑interventions and plan commissural orientation relative to the coronaries.
- Choose the valve platform and an implantation depth that balance immediate haemodynamics with future coronary access — sometimes a “good enough” valve now that leaves coronary windows is better than the lowest possible gradient that sacrifices access.
- Have coronary protection equipment ready whenever VTC is borderline; chimney stenting and BASILICA are useful tools but have limitations in layered frames.
- Document everything: CT maps, orientation/rotation, implant depth, and whether coronaries were protected. That record is invaluable for the next operator.

Take‑home messages
- Preprocedural CT commissural-to-coronary mapping is mandatory in complex TAV-in-TAV (and especially TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) — use it to guide valve selection, rotation and implant depth.
- Select devices and deployments that prioritise predictable coronary windows (low skirt, favourable cell geometry, commissural alignment) when lifetime management is a concern.
- Be ready for coronary protection and have a clear bailout plan — BASILICA and BVF have limits in multi‑frame anatomies.
- Document orientations, angles, implant depth and protection steps meticulously to preserve options for future interventions.

Preserving the “doors” into the coronaries is a cognitive and technical discipline — do the CT work, pick the right tools, deploy with intent, and write down what you did. This patient’s 1‑year result shows it can pay off.
