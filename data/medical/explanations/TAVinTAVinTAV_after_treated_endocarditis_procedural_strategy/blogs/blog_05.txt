# Vascular Access After a Pseudoaneurysm: Reducing Complications in Redo TAVR

What jumps out from this case isn’t just the technical feat of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated infective endocarditis — it’s that the same groin that once bled and required open repair was later used successfully for another large-bore TAVR. That prior pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm (post–TAV-in-TAV in 2022) could easily have derailed any attempt at repeat transfemoral access. Instead, careful preprocedural planning, targeted imaging and an evidence-based access strategy helped the team reuse the femoral route in 2024 without vascular complications.

If you’re planning redo large-bore access in a patient with prior femoral injury, surgical repair or chronic scarring, here are the pragmatic lessons — distilled from this case and from practical vascular-access best practices — that should be part of every heart-team checklist.

## Start with anatomy: iliofemoral CT mapping is non-negotiable
This patient had MSCT as part of valve planning, and that same scan provides the roadmap for vascular access. For redo or scarred groins you must know:

- Luminal diameter along the iliofemoral tract (minimums for sheath sizes).
- Calcification distribution (concentric vs eccentric) and femoral plaque that increases rupture risk.
- Vessel tortuosity that can make sheath passage or closure device apposition difficult.
- Prior repair material (stitches, patches) and residual pseudoaneurysm cavity or false lumen.
- Distance from skin to femoral artery and depth of artery — affects ultrasound visualization and closure-device choice.

MSCT lets you pick the optimal side and puncture level (common femoral artery vs external iliac), anticipate difficulties, and decide in advance whether surgical exposure or an alternate route is safer.

## Use ultrasound every time — especially in scarred groins
Ultrasound-guided puncture is standard of care for large-bore access. In the setting of prior hematoma/pseudoaneurysm, ultrasound helps you:

- Avoid scar tissue and hematoma cavities.
- Identify the true common femoral artery and the bifurcation.
- Choose the most favorable puncture site where artery is soft, non-calcified, and compressible.
- Guide micropuncture technique to reduce the chance of failure or posterior-wall puncture.

Take your time. If you can’t get a safe window, abort and consider an alternative plan.

## Think contralateral angiographic support and safety wires
When re-accessing a previously injured femoral, have a bailout strategy wired and ready:

- Contralateral femoral or radial secondary access for roadmapping and angiography.
- Place a contralateral crossover wire and an angioplasty balloon parked in the ipsilateral external iliac/common femoral artery before upsizing — ready to tamponade bleeding.
- A safety covered stent should be on the shelf (or the cath lab team briefed and primed) for catastrophic vessel injury.

In this case the team used left radial and left femoral arterial access — having radial adjuncts helps with angiography and reduces risk.

## Plan puncture level and closure device selection up front
Prior pseudoaneurysm or open repair changes how closure devices behave. Choices to consider:

- Suture-based devices (ProGlide) are preferred for their track record in controlled large-bore closures. However, in scarred tissue their footplate may sit improperly and failure rates increase.
- Collagen plugs (e.g., Angio-Seal) are less suitable alone for very large sheaths but can be used as adjuncts in hybrid strategies.
- In heavily scarred tissue or prior failed percutaneous closure, surgical cutdown may be safest.
- Consider a preclosure technique (two ProGlide sutures) if vessel anatomy allows — but be ready to supplement with balloon tamponade or surgical repair if sutures don’t seal.

Pre-procedural imaging helps you choose the device: shallow vessel vs deep vessel, calcified anterior wall, or prior groin surgery all push toward surgical exposure.

## Consider alternate access early — don’t stick with the groin for pride
If CT or ultrasound suggests unsafe transfemoral access because of severe scarring, small lumen, heavy calcification or a prior complicated repair, move to an alternate access rather than forcing the femoral route:

- Transaxillary/subclavian (surgical cutdown or percutaneous): good option if vessels are suitable.
- Transcarotid: increasing in use with favorable outcomes in selected patients.
- Direct aortic (surgical mini-sternotomy or mini-thoracotomy): considers higher surgical risk but sometimes the only option.
- Femoral surgical cutdown with vascular surgeon standby: hybrid solution when transfemoral is preferable but percutaneous closure is judged risky.

Shared decision-making with cardiac surgery and vascular surgery — early in planning — keeps options realistic and safe.

## Procedural tactics that reduce vascular complication risk
Small steps in technique lower the odds of catastrophe:

- Start with micropuncture and upsizing over a wire; avoid “stabbing” with a large-bore needle in scarred tissue.
- Use fluoroscopic and ultrasound landmarks together for precise anterior-wall puncture.
- Keep sheath dwell time minimal and exchange wires only when stable.
- Consider balloon occlusion from contralateral side for controlled hemostasis during sheath removal or suture tying.
- If performing preclosure, cinch sutures sequentially and check leak under angiography before removing the sheath.
- Have a plan for immediate conversion to open repair and the right surgical instruments available.

## Postprocedural surveillance and early intervention
Patients with prior pseudoaneurysm deserve early and deliberate post-op vascular checks:

- Immediate angiography after closure to assess for extravasation or flow-limiting dissection.
- Early duplex ultrasound (within 24 hours) to look for a new pseudoaneurysm or hematoma.
- Low threshold to re-image or consult vascular surgery if any groin pain, expanding hematoma or drop in hemoglobin is seen.

In the presented case, the team avoided repeat vascular complications by combining preprocedural CT, appropriate imaging-guided puncture and a cautious procedural approach — and surveillance confirmed an uncomplicated recovery.

## Practical checklist for redo large-bore access after a prior pseudoaneurysm

- Preprocedural:
  - Review prior vascular complication details and any operative notes.
  - Obtain dedicated iliofemoral CT angiography for vessel diameter, calcification, scarring, and prior repair assessment.
  - Multidisciplinary discussion (interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, vascular surgery, imaging).
  - Decide preferred access side and backup access/route.

- In the lab:
  - Ultrasound-guided micropuncture technique for all femoral access.
  - Contralateral/radial access for angiographic mapping and safety wire.
  - Park an internal iliac/contralateral balloon and/or covered stent on standby when appropriate.
  - Use preclosure with suture-based devices when anatomy permits; be ready with adjunct balloon tamponade or surgical backup.
  - Keep closure strategy documented and rehearsed (who will tie sutures, who will inflate balloon, etc).

- If anatomy unfavourable:
  - Move early to alternate access (axillary, carotid, direct aortic) or surgical cutdown; do not force a risky transfemoral approach.

- Postprocedure:
  - Immediate angiographic check of closure site.
  - Early bedside duplex ultrasound (within 24 hours).
  - Low threshold for vascular surgery consultation for any concern.

Takeaway: a prior femoral pseudoaneurysm is not an absolute contraindication to future transfemoral TAVR, but it raises the stakes. Detailed CT-based vascular mapping, ultrasound-guided puncture, contralateral/radial adjuncts and clear bailout plans (balloons, covered stents, surgical cutdown) are essential. When in doubt, choose the safest access — the patient will thank you for it later.
