# Antithrombotics After TAVR With Recurrent GI Bleeding and CAD: Finding the Sweet Spot

What makes this case interesting? A man in his late 70s with multivessel CAD and multiple prior PCIs, a pacemaker, recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022, and — critically — multiple transcatheter aortic valves (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis. He needs a balanced antithrombotic strategy that protects coronary stents and the new transcatheter valve while minimising bleeding and not complicating infective endocarditis follow-up. That tension — ischaemic protection versus catastrophic bleeding — is common in everyday TAVR care, but it becomes acute in patients like this.

Below I pull together practical, evidence-informed guidance you can use at the bedside when faced with TAVR patients who have high bleeding risk and prior PCI.

Why re-think routine DAPT after TAVR?
- Randomised trials and meta-analyses over the past decade have moved the field away from routine dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) after TAVR for most patients. The POPular-TAVI (cohort A) trial demonstrated that single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) reduced bleeding without increasing thrombotic events compared with aspirin plus clopidogrel in patients without an indication for oral anticoagulation. Anticoagulation strategies that add antiplatelets increase bleeding risk; rivaroxaban-based strategies (eg, GALILEO) raised safety concerns. In short: bleeding harm from more antithrombotic therapy often outweighs modest and uncertain ischemic benefit after TAVR in most patients.
- The calculus flips only when there is a recent coronary stent (<1 month, sometimes <6 months depending on stent type and clinical scenario), high thrombotic risk, or another compelling indication for antiplatelet combination.

Applying this to our case
- This patient’s coronary stents were implanted years ago (2009–2014). Late stent thrombosis risk after modern DES beyond the first year is low. There is no indication from the case timeline that he had recent PCI that would mandate DAPT.
- He has a high bleeding burden (recurrent GI bleeding since 2022). That pushes strongly toward minimising antithrombotic intensity.
- No chronic atrial fibrillation or other ongoing indication for oral anticoagulation is described; if present, anticoagulation strategy would change (see below).
- Prior prosthetic valve endocarditis was treated and resolved; no ongoing infection or suppressive antibiotic therapy was in place. That means standard antithrombotic considerations apply (no extra antiplatelet role for PVE).

Practical antithrombotic strategy — a framework
1. Assess indications for antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy independently:
   - Recent PCI (<1–6 months): consider cardiology consensus — usually continue DAPT for the minimal effective duration then stop to SAPT.
   - Longstanding stents (>1 year) without recent events: SAPT only is reasonable.
   - Atrial fibrillation or other indications for chronic oral anticoagulation: DOAC/VKA alone is generally preferred after TAVR; avoid routine addition of antiplatelet unless recent PCI mandates short overlap.
2. Default for high-bleeding-risk TAVR patients without other indications: single antiplatelet therapy (usually low-dose aspirin) rather than DAPT.
3. If already on single antiplatelet but significant GI bleeding risk exists: discuss switching to clopidogrel vs aspirin based on bleeding profile, or consider stopping antiplatelet therapy altogether after shared decision-making — some very high bleeding‑risk patients may be managed without antiplatelet therapy if coronary stents are remote and there is no other indication.
4. If oral anticoagulation is required:
   - Use DOAC or VKA alone post‑TAVR; avoid routine addition of antiplatelet drugs.
   - If recent DES: use the shortest possible period of combined therapy (eg, DOAC + single antiplatelet for 1 month), then step down to DOAC alone, balancing bleeding risk.
5. Always personalise duration: the “one-size-fits-all” DAPT prescriptions after PCI do not apply post-TAVR.

Coordination with GI and use of protective strategies
- Partner with gastroenterology early to identify and treat bleeding sources (eg, angiodysplasia, peptic disease, malformations) before or after TAVR when possible.
- Start proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy when antiplatelet therapy is given for upper GI protection, after assessing risks and interactions.
- Consider endoscopic therapy or interventional radiology for recurrent or ongoing bleeding sources; in some cases stabilising the GI bleed allows safer antithrombotic management.
- If bleeding is active or uncontrolled, prioritise haemorrhage control and minimise antithrombotics; re-evaluate coronary and valve thrombosis risk after stabilisation.

Endocarditis considerations — what to tell the patient and team
- Prior treated prosthetic valve endocarditis does not, by itself, mandate a special long-term antithrombotic regimen. What matters is that infection is fully treated and no suppressive antibiotics are ongoing.
- Continue standard infective endocarditis prevention measures per current guidelines: emphasise dental hygiene, prompt treatment of bacteraemia sources, and selective antibiotic prophylaxis for high‑risk dental procedures (valve prosthesis is a recognized high‑risk condition for which dental prophylaxis is recommended).
- Any new febrile illness, persistent bacteremia, or change in valve function should trigger urgent evaluation (blood cultures, TEE, CT) given the patient’s prosthetic history.

Monitoring and follow-up
- Close follow-up after TAVR in high-bleeding-risk patients: early clinical checks for bleeding or ischaemia at 1 month, and echocardiography to assess valve function as clinically indicated.
- Reassess the need for ongoing antithrombotic therapy at defined intervals (eg, 1, 3, and 6 months), and after any bleeding or ischemic event.
- If antiplatelet therapy is continued, educate the patient about bleeding signs, medication interactions, and the need for PPI if upper GI risk is present.

Clinical pearls
- For most TAVR patients without a separate indication for antithrombotic therapy, SAPT is sufficient and safer than routine DAPT.
- In patients with remote (>1 year) coronary stents and a high bleeding risk, SAPT — or even no antiplatelet in extreme bleeding risk after multidisciplinary discussion — is reasonable.
- If chronic anticoagulation (eg, AF) is required, DOAC or VKA alone is usually best; adding antiplatelet increases bleeding without clear benefit except when recent stenting forces short-term combination therapy.
- Coordinate early with GI and interventional teams to treat bleeding sources, start PPI where appropriate, and limit antithrombotic exposure when bleeding risk is high.
- Prior prosthetic valve endocarditis does not necessarily change antithrombotic decisions if infection is resolved; however, it mandates vigilant infection surveillance and standard prophylaxis measures.

Bottom line for this patient
Given his recurrent GI bleeding and remote coronary stents, the balance favours minimising antithrombotic intensity after the third TAVR. Unless he has a concurrent, separate indication for anticoagulation or a recent PCI, single antiplatelet therapy (usually low‑dose aspirin) or even stopping antiplatelet therapy after careful multidisciplinary discussion is defensible. Coordinate with GI to aggressively pursue bleeding source control and use PPI prophylaxis where indicated. Maintain vigilant endocarditis surveillance and standard dental prophylaxis practices.
