Title: Blog about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

# Playing the long game: lifetime management after the first TAVR

What makes this case interesting?
A man in his late 70s with a Lotus implanted in 2014, a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut in 2022, intervening Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis treated medically, and then a CT-planned Acurate neo2 implanted as a third transcatheter valve in 2024 — with preserved function at 1 year. It’s a striking example of how TAVR is no longer a “one-and-done” procedure for many patients. The team used preprocedural MSCT modelling, planned commissural alignment and a high implant strategy to protect the coronaries and optimise the residual effective orifice area (EOA). The immediate postimplant transvalvular gradient was very high (mean 67 mm Hg) but fell to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and 26 mm Hg at 1 year — reminding us that frame interaction, post-dilation and cardiac remodeling can change the haemodynamic picture after valve-in-valve-in-valve (V-i-V-i-V) procedures.

If you do TAVR, think re-do from day one
This case is a practical prompt to stop thinking of TAVR as an isolated index procedure and to start planning lifetime management at the time of the first implant. Every decision you make at implant — valve type, size, orientation, implantation depth — influences the difficulty and safety of the next interventions, coronary access and the likelihood of prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) down the road.

Key planning principles illustrated by the case

1) Choose the first valve with redoability in mind
- Supra-annular valves (or valves with better EOA for a given annulus) often provide superior haemodynamics in small annuli and can reduce future PPM risk. In this case the team ultimately chose a supra-annular Acurate neo2 for the third implant to maximise EOA in a narrow, layered anatomy.
- Frame height and skirt location matter: lower skirts and frames that preserve sinus clearance make coronary access and future interventions easier.
- Retrievable/recapturable features and predictable deployment characteristics help achieve commissural alignment and controlled depth.

2) Make commissural alignment the default
- Aligning the new valve commissures away from coronary ostia preserves access and reduces the cumulative mesh burden across the coronary ostia on future procedures.
- Use fluoroscopic landmarks, manufacturer orientation markers and CT-derived cusp overlap planning to orient the valve. Document the final orientation on the procedural record.
- In multi-valve scenarios this becomes critical: the cumulative frame mesh can block coronary catheters or make coronary protection/BASILICA more difficult.

3) Proactively preserve future coronary access
- Before implant, use MSCT to measure coronary heights, sinus of Valsalva diameter and valve-to-coronary distances. If these are short, plan coronary protection or consider leaflet laceration strategies.
- In this case CT showed adequate clearance and the team proceeded without coronary protection — but that decision was CT guided.
- Consider valve selection and implantation depth to minimise a neo-skirt that could cover the ostia. When coronary disease is present or likely to be needed later, bias toward devices and implant strategies that facilitate reaccess.

4) Balance haemodynamics versus frame layering to mitigate future PPM
- Good haemodynamics now reduces the chance of heart failure and readmission, but a large rigid frame placed first may limit options later. Conversely, selecting a smaller device now to “preserve anatomy” can worsen baseline EOA and functional status.
- In small annuli think supra‑annular valves or plan for future bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) when applicable — noting BVF is established for many surgical bioprostheses but is device- and frame-dependent for transcatheter valves.
- Expect that in multi-frame situations you will rely on CT modelling, high-pressure post-dilation and sometimes mechanical expansion of prior self-expanding frames to improve final haemodynamics (as happened here without deliberate frame fracture).

5) Infection history is not an absolute contraindication to TAV-in-TAV — but timing and proof of eradication are essential
- This patient had Enterococcus faecalis PVE treated with full antimicrobial therapy, and preprocedural TEE and MSCT showed no active infection. With prohibitive surgical risk and good imaging evidence of infection resolution, repeat TAVR was pursued.
- Key: ensure infection is controlled, get negative blood cultures, and involve ID and cardiac surgery in shared decision-making.

Surveillance rhythm: practical proposal (grounded in this case)
- Baseline: TTE prior to discharge from index TAVR (to document gradient, EOA, PVL), and a post-procedure clinical & echo report filed in the EHR with device type, size and orientation.
- Early follow-up: clinical review + TTE at 30 days and 3 months to catch early dysfunction or PPM and to document remodeling (this case showed striking early gradient evolution).
- Medium/long term: annual clinical review and TTE thereafter if stable.
- Triggered CT angiography (MSCT): reserve for rising gradients (>10 mm Hg from baseline or symptomatic deterioration), suspected endocarditis, planning for redo procedures, or pre-PCI when coronary anatomy is complex or access may be difficult. MSCT played a central role here — measuring the Evolut lumen (13.7 mm) and guiding decision-making on coronary risk and valve sizing.
- Bloodwork/cultures: pursue cultures if any fever or suspicious symptoms; prior PVE warrants low threshold for imaging and multidisciplinary discussion.

Make the heart‑team record future-proof
If lifetime management matters, then documentation must too. At minimum include in the procedural note:
- Valve make, model, size and lot number; orientation marker used and final fluoroscopic orientation (include saved cine images).
- Implantation depth relative to annulus/cusp landmarks.
- Commissural alignment attempt and final alignment assessment.
- Balloon sizes used for pre- and post-dilation; final valve haemodynamics on table (mean gradient, DVI).
- MSCT-derived valve-to-coronary distances and aorta/sinus measurements that guided the procedural strategy.
- Explicit “lifetime plan” statement: what the team expects for surveillance, what reintervention options remain, and any concerns about future coronary access or explantation complexity. Share this with the patient and primary cardiologist.

Practical pitfalls and pearls from the case
- Don’t assume haemodynamics measured immediately post-implant are static in valve-in-valve constructs. High immediate gradients may improve after post dilation, partial frame expansion, and LV remodeling; but persistent very high gradients still require attention.
- CT matters. MSCT planning — not just for sizing but for valve-to-coronary distance, predicted neo-skirt, and frame interaction modelling — can change the plan from “protect the coronaries” to “no protection needed.”
- High implants can be lifesaving for coronary preservation in stacked valves, but they must be balanced against the risk of valve embolisation and paravalvular leak.
- Document everything. The orientation images you save at index TAVR will be worth their weight in gold when the patient returns in 5–10 years.

Take-home bullets
- Think lifetime at the time of the first TAVR: valve choice, skirt height, commissural alignment and documentation all matter for future re-interventions.
- Make commissural alignment the default strategy to preserve coronary access and reduce cumulative mesh across the ostia.
- Use MSCT to plan: assess coronary heights, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances and frame interactions before redo TAVR.
- Adopt a surveillance rhythm: baseline post-procedure echo, early follow-up (30 days, 3 months), then annual TTE; reserve CT for rising gradients, symptoms, endocarditis suspicion or pre-reintervention/coronary planning.
- Record a lifetime-management plan in the heart-team note and share it with the patient and outpatient team.

This triple-TAVR story doesn’t mean every patient should get three transcatheter valves. It reminds us that TAVR starts a trajectory — one we shape with our device choices, our implant technique and the quality of our planning and documentation. The better we plan today, the more options our patients will have when the valve inevitably fails.
