Title: Blog about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

# Triple Frames, Open Coronaries: CT-First Strategy to Prevent Coronary Obstruction

What grabs you about this case? A man in his late 70s with two prior TAVIs (Lotus 2014 → Evolut 2022), treated Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, and then a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2, 2024) implanted into the double-stented anatomy. No coronary protection was used. With CT-led planning, high implantation, commissural alignment and targeted device choice, the team avoided coronary obstruction and delivered durable 1‑year relief (mean gradient ~26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%). Triple-frame TAVI is uncommon, and the PVE history makes the case feel even riskier — yet it teaches a practical, reproducible imaging-first approach.

## Why CT-first matters in valve-in-valve-in-valve cases
Each added stent frame changes the landing zone geometry, creates potential neo-skirts and increases the mesh burden that coronary catheters must traverse later. Plain fluoroscopy or transthoracic echo won’t reliably quantify the spatial relationships that matter most for coronary safety:

- Coronary ostial height alone is insufficient once you have stacked frames — you must measure valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance and model the anticipated neo-skirt.
- Sinus of Valsalva dimensions determine whether leaflets or skirt material can be pushed aside or will create a funnel that blocks flow.
- CT lets you see how the previous valve frames sit relative to the sinuses, commissures and coronary ostia and estimate whether a new supra-annular leaf will create a covered column.

In this case MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut frame and enough clearance to proceed without protection after careful modelling. That CT guided valve selection (Acurate neo2), implantation depth and the decision against prophylactic coronary wiring or chimney stenting.

## The practical CT playbook (stepwise)
1. Start with high-resolution MSCT with thin-slice, contrast-enhanced gated acquisition through the aortic root and ascending aorta.
2. Measure:
   - Coronary ostial heights from native annulus (but interpret cautiously with prior frames).
   - Sinus of Valsalva diameters and height.
   - Valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance — distance from anticipated neo‑skirt plane to coronary ostium.
   - Internal lumen and frame geometry of prior THVs (minimum lumen diameter, frame orientation).
3. Model the anticipated neo-skirt: plan where the new valve’s skirt/leaflets will sit relative to coronary ostia — if a supra-annular valve’s leaflets will align above the coronary ostia, risk is lower.
4. Assess commissural orientation of prior implants and feasibility of achieving commissural alignment with the planned device.
5. Decide on coronary protection strategy only after the above: if VTC is short, sinuses small, or model predicts skirt-coronary overlap → prepare for protection (wiring ± undeployed stent or BASILICA if applicable).

## How the imaging findings drive strategy
- Small VTC (<4 mm in many expert centers) + shallow sinuses → high risk of coronary obstruction; plan for protection or alternative strategy.
- Adequate VTC and roomy sinuses → consider high implantation, supra-annular device with low frame height to minimize neo-skirt and preserve access.
- If prior THV leaflets are bulky/fused (degeneration or post-endocarditis restriction), high implantation that tethers those leaflets against the frame can prevent inward leaflet folding that would further reduce the orifice area.

In our case the team had clear CT evidence that a high implant of a short-framed, supra-annular Acurate neo2 would place the new leaflets in a position that minimized the neo-skirt under the coronaries and preserved access — so they proceeded without coronary protection.

## Device selection: prioritize what preserves coronaries and EOAi
When you're dealing with stacked frames, think performance AND future access:
- Supra-annular valves (Acurate neo2, some self-expanding systems) often give better EOA in small effective annuli and can sit higher, reducing skirt coverage of coronaries.
- Low/short frames reduce mesh burden across the sinuses and facilitate later coronary catheterization.
- Devices that permit commissural alignment are preferred — if you can align new commissures to avoid overlapping existing posts, you decrease the chance of ostial coverage and simplify future PCI.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen here for supra‑annular leaflets, shorter frame height and commissural alignment capability — all helpful in triple-frame anatomy.

## Implant technique pearls
- Aim for high implantation when CT suggests that keeping the new leaflet plane at or above prior leaflet tips will prevent inward leaflet folding and minimize neo-skirt at the coronary level.
- Use CT and fluoroscopic landmarks for commissural alignment during deployment to keep posts away from coronaries.
- Balloon predilation can help assess coronary flow dynamically (as done here) — run an aortogram during balloon inflation to confirm no compromise before valve release.
- Post-dilation can expand prior frames (partial mechanical expansion) and improve gradients — but be mindful of risks (annular injury, embolization). In this case, post-dilation improved effective orifice and reduced gradients.
- Coronary protection should remain an on‑table bailout plan even if not used prophylactically: have guidewires and undeployed stents ready if angiography suggests slowing flow.

## When prior endocarditis changes the equation
A history of treated PVE raises infection‑related concerns (ongoing bacteremia, paravalvular extension) — but once infection is proven resolved clinically, microbiologically and on TEE/CT, decision-making reverts to anatomy and surgical risk. This patient had completed targeted antibiotics, had no active infection on TEE/MSCT, and was prohibitive for redo surgery — so CT-based TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV was reasonable and successful.

## Risks and the limits of strategy
- Even with ideal CT planning, triple frames increase the chance of reduced EOA and clinically significant patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). Expect possible high immediate gradients, which may improve after post-dilation or remodeling — as occurred here (initial mean gradient 67 mm Hg → 27 mm Hg at 1 month).
- Future coronary access may still be technically challenging despite commissural alignment; plan accordingly, and keep meticulous documentation of commissural orientation for downstream operators.
- Surgical explant after multiple TAVIs is technically demanding and high-risk — discuss limited future options with the patient and family during consent.

## The bottom line — clinical takeaways
- Detailed CT assessment (coronary height, sinus dimensions, VTC, predicted neo‑skirt and frame interaction) is indispensable before undertaking TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV.
- Device selection should prioritize supra‑annular leaflet position, shorter frame height and commissural alignment capacity when preserving coronary flow and future access is a priority.
- High implantation, guided by CT modelling, can tether old leaflets and minimize neo‑skirt formation beneath coronary ostia — reducing obstruction risk without routine prophylactic coronary protection.
- Have coronary protection as an on‑table bailout; use it prophylactically when CT predicts short VTC, small sinuses or likely skirt‑coronary overlap.
- Even in patients with prior treated PVE, if infection is eradicated and CT/TEE exclude active disease, a CT-first transcatheter strategy can be an acceptable alternative to high‑risk surgery — but requires multidisciplinary discussion and informed consent about limited future options.

Key takeaways:
- CT-first: always quantify VTC, sinus dimensions and frame anatomy before repeat TAVI.
- Choose a supra‑annular, low/short‑frame valve when coronary preservation and EOA matter.
- Aim for high implantation and commissural alignment to minimize neo-skirt and preserve access.
- Reserve routine coronary protection for high-risk CT features; keep it ready as a bailout.
- In treated PVE with no active infection, CT-guided TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV can be feasible — but plan lifetime management up front.

This patient’s result — symptom-free at 1 year with sustained haemodynamic improvement — shows the payoff of meticulous imaging, device choice and strategy in the triple-frame setting.
