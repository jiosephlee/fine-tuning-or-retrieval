# Sizing the Unsizable: Measuring True Inner Lumen for ViV-in-ViV

What makes this case worth a close look is obvious: a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV in a frail, comorbid septuagenarian after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis — and a great 1‑year outcome. The technical secret sauce wasn’t luck; it was CT‑guided humility. When you already have one metallic scaffolding in the root — let alone two — the job isn’t “measure annulus and pick size.” It’s about mapping the remaining true inner lumen, understanding how frames and leaflets interact, and tailoring dilation and device choice to preserve coronary flow and optimize residual effective orifice area (EOA) without blowing up the root.

Below are the imaging nuances that matter, illustrated by this patient’s journey (Lotus → Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV → Acurate neo2 ViV‑in‑ViV). Think of this as a checklist for sizing the unsizable.

## What CT must answer in layered‑frame anatomy

1. Minimum true inner lumen (TIL)
- The single most important metric. Measure the smallest lumen within the inner-most frame at the intended landing zone (often the level of prior valve leaflets). Use multiplanar reconstructions and centerline perpendicular sections. In the case above the Evolut TIL was 13.7 mm — the number that drove the whole plan.
- Don’t rely on labelled “label” valve sizes or nominal internal diameters — the stacked frames and leaflet tissue will reduce the functional lumen.

2. 3D shape and ellipticity
- Measure orthogonal diameters and report an eccentricity index (max/min). Elliptical lumens alter contact pressures and balloon behavior; a nominal diameter may be misleading.
- High ellipticity predicts asymmetric expansion, focal high stresses, and potential for underexpansion or root injury if oversized balloons are used.

3. Leaflet status and calcification
- Are prior valve leaflets fibrosed, calcified, fused, or mobile? Infected/treated leaflets can be stiff and folded inward, causing obstruction. Calcified or stiff leaflets limit frame expansion and increase risk of leaflet “napkin‑ring” obstruction.
- CT can also show leaflet bulk that will form the neo‑skirt after your implant — critical for coronary obstruction risk.

4. Frame‑to‑frame relationships
- Map the vertical and radial position of each frame, stent heights, and overlap zones. Identify where frames buttress each other and where there are gaps.
- Note frame material and radial strength (e.g., supra‑annular self‑expanding Evolut vs low‑profile frames). This informs whether balloon post‑dilation will meaningfully expand the prior frame or only risk scaffold fracture/root injury.

5. Valve‑to‑coronary distance and sinus anatomy
- Standard valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) and sinus of Valsalva measurements still apply, but now consider the height of a neo‑skirt created by two layers of leaflets plus your new implant.
- Even if nominal VTC looks acceptable, cumulative skirt height and commissural misalignment can turn an acceptable anatomy into one that requires coronary protection or BASILICA planning.

6. Landing zone selection
- Decide whether a higher (leaflet‑level) implant to tether old leaflets against the frame is preferable versus a deeper implant. The case above used high implantation to fix prior leaflets outward and minimize neo‑skirt, improving EOA.

## Translating measurements into procedural strategy

1. Device choice: match design to the constrained lumen
- Supra‑annular designs with low frame height (e.g., Acurate neo2) can preserve EOA in tight anatomies. If the TIL is very small, consider valves known for better hemodynamics at small ID.
- Avoid tall, bulky frames that will further encroach on sinuses or hinder coronary access unless CT supports it.

2. Balloon sizing for pre‑ and post‑dilation
- Balloon diameter should be chosen against TIL, not nominal surgical annulus. In the presence of ellipticity, choose a balloon that respects the smaller diameter; oversizing risks aortic root injury or stent distortion.
- If you anticipate expanding a chronically embedded self‑expanding frame (Evolut, CoreValve family), high‑pressure post‑dilation can partially increase lumen and leaflet motion — but expect resistance. Know the limits: attempting to “force” a rigid, layered anatomy can lead to aortic injury.
- Consider gradual step‑wise dilation (undersized → nominal) with hemodynamic assessment between inflations.

3. Pre‑dilation strategy
- Pre‑dilation helps assess how the old leaflets and frames will behave. In this case, a 22 mm balloon was used for predilation under rapid pacing to test coronary flow and leaflet movement.
- If pre‑dilation shows coronary compromise or excessive leaflet prolapse, be ready with coronary protection or bail‑out plans.

4. Coronary protection and commissural alignment
- CT should guide whether you need upfront coronary protection (wires, undeployed stents) or a BASILICA strategy. Absence of a low VTC doesn’t mean no risk — cumulative skirt and unpredictable leaflet folding can obstruct coronaries.
- Try to plan commissural alignment with fluoroscopic landmarks from CT: aim to orient the new frame to avoid overlapping coronary columns; it preserves future access and reduces obstruction risk.

5. Implantation depth and the neo‑skirt
- High implant of the third valve in front of the Evolut leaflets can tack the leaflets open and minimize the neo‑skirt under the coronaries — improving EOA and coronary safety.
- But high implants can increase gradient if you seat leaflets too supra‑annularly or if sealing is suboptimal. Balance hemodynamics and coronary preservation.

6. Expect and plan for prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM)
- Triple frames have limited ceiling for EOA. Even with perfect technique, immediate post‑implant gradients may be high (as in this case: mean gradient 67 mm Hg immediately post‑op then improved over weeks to 26–27 mm Hg).
- Communicate realistic expectations: early high gradients may decrease as frames settle and prior self‑expanding scaffolds allow partial recoil.

## Avoiding root injury — practical cautions

- Respect the TIL and ellipticity. Don’t use balloon diameters that exceed the smallest inner dimension without a compelling reason.
- High‑pressure or large balloon bursts in a layered metallic root are a potential recipe for aortic rupture or stent fracture.
- If you plan aggressive expansion, have surgical backup or covered stent options ready.
- In infected prostheses that are treated and sterile, tissue planes may still be friable; avoid traumatic maneuvers.

## Imaging workflow recommendations

- Start with thin‑slice gated CT with centerline reconstructions. Measure TIL at multiple short‑axis levels (annulus, leaflet tips, mid‑frame).
- Generate 3D volume renderings and virtual valve implantation to estimate neo‑skirt and VTC; dedicated ViV software adds value where available.
- Correlate with TEE/fluoroscopy for leaflet mobility and to confirm no active infection.
- Share CT maps with the entire heart team: interventionalist, imager, surgeon, and infectious disease.

## Clinical pearl: size the lumen, not the label
The single most common misstep in ViV‑in‑ViV planning is relying on valve labels or surgical annulus sizing. In layered frames, the functional constraint is the minimal true inner lumen and its shape. Let that number drive your balloon choice, device selection, and implantation depth.

## Take‑home points
- Measure the minimum true inner lumen (TIL) inside the innermost frame — that’s the critical sizing metric in ViV‑in‑ViV.
- Report ellipticity, leaflet bulk/calcification, frame‑to‑frame overlap zones, and valve‑to‑coronary distances; these change strategy.
- Choose valve type and size to maximize EOA in the constrained lumen (supra‑annular, low‑frame options often preferred).
- Balloon sizing must be based on TIL and the eccentricity; avoid aggressive oversizing that risks root injury.
- Pre‑dilation is a test — be ready with coronary protection or BASILICA if the neo‑skirt threatens coronaries.
- Plan commissural alignment and a high implant when appropriate to tack old leaflets outward, reduce neo‑skirt, and preserve coronary access.
- Expect early high gradients; documented partial expansion of prior frames and post‑dilation can improve hemodynamics over weeks.
- Multidisciplinary CT‑led planning is non‑negotiable for complex ViV‑in‑ViV cases.

This patient’s success hinged on meticulous CT workup, conservative and pragmatic balloon strategy, device selection that favored supra‑annular EOA, and deliberate high implantation to manage the neo‑skirt. When the anatomy looks “unsizable,” it usually isn’t unsizable — it’s under‑measured. Measure smarter, not bigger.
