# Fever After TAVR: Don’t Miss Enterococcal PVE

What makes this case interesting? A man in his late 70s with two prior TAVI implants (Lotus → Evolut) developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) after a TAV-in-TAV and, once treated, later required a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) — a true TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. The team used careful CT- and TEE-guided planning, deferred surgery because of prohibitive risk, completed a full course of targeted antibiotics, ensured no residual infection on imaging and blood cultures, and went on to a successful CT-planned reintervention with good 1‑year outcomes. This is a neat, practical lesson-set for anyone seeing fever after TAVR.

## Why Enterococcus matters after TAVR
- Enterococcus faecalis is a common culprit in late PVE and can be indolent: low‑grade fevers, constitutional symptoms, or heart failure may be the only clues.
- Prosthetic material (stent frames, sewing rings, valve leaflets) + prior instrumentation = fertile ground for enterococcal seeding.
- Enterococci can adhere and damage leaflets leading to restenosis or regurgitation that mimics structural valve degeneration.

## Red flags at the bedside
Think PVE in any patient with a transcatheter aortic valve who presents with:
- Persistent fever without clear source, especially >48–72 hours or relapsing episodes.
- New/worsening heart failure symptoms (dyspnoea, fatigue), new murmur, or unexplained decline in LVEF.
- Recurrent bacteremia with enteric organisms (Enterococcus spp.), especially after urogenital/GI procedures or bleeding episodes.
- New conduction disturbances (eg, AV block) or embolic phenomena.
In the reported case the patient had fever, worsening heart failure and positive blood cultures for E. faecalis — classic warning signs.

## Culture strategy — get it right up front
- Obtain multiple sets (3 if possible) of blood cultures from separate venipuncture sites before starting antibiotics. Enterococcus can be transient; repeated sampling improves sensitivity.
- If cultures are positive, save isolates for susceptibility testing and consider PCR/16S if cultures are negative but suspicion remains.
- Repeat blood cultures during therapy to document clearance. Persistent bacteremia mandates re-evaluation for uncontrolled focus (vegetation, abscess, paravalvular leak).

## Imaging: TTE, TEE and MSCT — complementary roles
- TTE is first line but has limited sensitivity for prosthetic valves and layered frames.
- TEE is essential for prosthetic valve endocarditis — higher sensitivity for vegetations, leaflet perforation, paravalvular abscess. In the case, TEE helped exclude active endocarditis before reintervention.
- Multislice CT (MSCT) excels at:
  - Assessing frame integration, stent interactions and landing zones when planning redo TAVI.
  - Detecting paravalvular extension, root/annular abscess and relationship to coronaries.
  - Measuring valve-to-coronary distances and predicting coronary obstruction risk for valve‑in‑valve scenarios.
- Use imaging serially: at diagnosis to confirm/exclude active infection, and after therapy to document resolution before elective reintervention.

## Antibiotic pathway for Enterococcus faecalis PVE
- Enterococcus faecalis PVE requires prolonged, targeted therapy — standard regimens differ from native valve disease.
- Current practice (as reflected in the case): prolonged IV therapy guided by ID specialists (commonly 6 weeks of ampicillin-based therapy for susceptible isolates). Alternatives include ampicillin + ceftriaxone for aminoglycoside-sparing synergy when gentamicin unacceptable.
- Tailor therapy to susceptibility (ampicillin-resistant, VRE change the approach), patient renal function and tolerability.
- Long‑term suppressive therapy is not routine unless eradication is in doubt or prosthetic material cannot be removed — in the case there was no chronic suppressive therapy once infection was cleared.

## When is reintervention (including TAVI) safe after treated PVE?
There’s no one-size-fits-all timing; decisions combine infection control, imaging, valve dysfunction severity and surgical risk.
Key practical checkpoints before elective reintervention:
- Completed recommended full course of appropriate antibiotics.
- Clinical improvement with no fever or signs of ongoing infection.
- Serial negative blood cultures.
- TEE/CT showing no vegetations, no paravalvular extension or abscess.
- Multidisciplinary Heart Team consensus involving interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, infectious diseases and imaging.
In this case the team waited until after therapy, had negative cultures and clear imaging, and chose TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV because surgical risk was prohibitive.

## Procedural planning pearls for redo TAV (especially TAV-in-TAV and beyond)
- Assume layered stent frames change anatomy: smaller effective lumen, potential for coronary obstruction, and tight landing zones.
- Preprocedural MSCT is indispensable: measure residual lumen, valve-to-coronary distances, sinus dimensions, and model leaflet/neo‑skirt positions.
- Select valve type and intended implantation depth to preserve coronary flow and maximise effective orifice area (supra‑annular valves can help haemodynamics in tight anatomies).
- Aim for commissural alignment where possible — preserves coronary access for future interventions.
- Consider coronary protection strategies (wiring/balloon) if CT shows borderline distances — but if CT indicates safe clearance and the team is confident, it may be omitted as in this case.
- Balloon pre- and post-dilation can expand prior frames and improve leaflet motion; intentional valve fracture is another tool but may be limited in some valve types and in multiple layered frames.
- Plan for the worst: ensure surgical backup availability and clear contingency plans.

## Common pitfalls that delay diagnosis or push premature procedures
- Attributing low‑grade fevers and malaise to COPD, pulmonary causes, or other comorbidities without considering PVE.
- Starting antibiotics before drawing adequate blood cultures — reduces diagnostic yield and hides bacteremia.
- Relying solely on TTE in prosthetic patients — underestimates vegetations.
- Proceeding to reintervention based on haemodynamics alone without confirming eradication of infection on serial cultures and imaging.
- Underestimating the cumulative impact of multiple stent frames on coronary access and future treatment options.

## Take-home messages
- Always consider PVE in any TAVR patient with fever, new heart failure, or bacteremia — Enterococcus faecalis is a classic culprit.
- Draw multiple blood cultures before antibiotics. Repeat cultures to document clearance.
- Use TEE + MSCT: TEE for infection, MSCT for anatomy and reintervention feasibility. Both are required for safe planning.
- Treat enterococcal PVE with prolonged, targeted IV therapy under ID guidance; do not rush reintervention until infection is controlled clinically, microbiologically and on imaging.
- Preprocedural CT-guided planning, commissural alignment and valve selection are critical for redo TAV, especially when multiple frames are present.
- Multidisciplinary decision-making is essential — the reported case shows that with careful infection control and imaging-guided strategy, even a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV can be feasible and durable at 1 year.

This patient’s course is a good reminder: fever after TAVR is not benign. Early recognition, disciplined culture practice, paired TEE/MSCT assessment and team-based timing decisions can make the difference between failed therapy and a durable, well-planned reintervention.



## Lifetime Valve Strategy in 2025: Making Today’s TAVR Compatible With Tomorrow’s Needs

We used to think of TAVR as a one-and-done therapy for elderly, high-risk patients. The landscape has changed — younger patients, longer survival, and rising numbers of valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures mean you must plan the first TAVR with the next one (and the one after that) in mind. A recent case of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2) nicely illustrates both what can go right with meticulous planning and what you risk leaving yourself with if you don’t plan ahead.

Why this case is instructive
- The team faced a triple-stented aortic root with prior endocarditis, prohibitive surgical risk, and limited annular area — a worst-case “lifetime” scenario.
- CT-guided planning, commissural alignment and a deliberate choice of a low-profile, supra‑annular valve (Acurate neo2) allowed a successful third TAVR with preserved coronary flow and sustained 1‑year function.
- The case highlights the trade-offs every operator must consider at index TAVR: immediate haemodynamics vs preserving future options.

What to think about at your index TAVR (and why it matters)
- Coronary preservation is everything. When you implant a THV, you’re defining future valve-to-coronary (VTC) geometry and the cumulative mesh that can “jail” coronaries in subsequent procedures. Low VTC and high frame height limit future reaccess and increase risk of coronary obstruction on repeat ViV.
- Commissural alignment now matters. Rotational alignment of the THV relative to native commissures reduces overlap of valve frame posts with coronary ostia and eases future coronary access and commissural alignment for subsequent THVs.
- Choose frame and leaflet position with an eye to future EOA. Supra‑annular leaflets yield better EOA in small annuli and narrow ViV landing zones — important to minimize prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) down the road.
- Keep cumulative frame height low. Taller frames stacked create more metal mesh and reduce effective orifice area; consider lower-profile frames when anatomy allows.
- Preserve the landing zone. Implant depth at the index TAVR can influence how much leaflet tissue or native calcium will be pushed outward/inward on future ViV deployments. A high, controlled implantation may help preserve coronary clearance later.

Practical pre‑procedural planning — imaging and CT work-up
- Do MSCT in ViV planning mode from day one. Measure sinus of Valsalva, coronary heights, VTC (projected distance after anticipated valve implantation), sinotubular junction, and valve frame-to-coronary relationships.
- Model future scenarios. If possible, run virtual valve overlay and simulate post‑implantation VTC and neo-skirt position. That’s what guided the Acurate neo2 implantation in the triple‑TAVR case.
- Ask: will the next valve create a neo-skirt at coronary level? If yes, consider strategies to avoid it at the index implant.

Choice of valve at index — key considerations
- Supra‑annular vs intra‑annular: in small annuli or younger patients where future ViV is likely, supra‑annular valves often give better haemodynamics and lower PPM risk on reinterventions.
- Frame height and cell architecture: low frame height and larger open cells ease coronary access. Balloon-expandable frames have different re-expansion and fracture characteristics compared with self‑expanding frames — choose based on both index haemodynamics and future plan.
- Commissural alignment capability: some devices offer markers or implantation techniques that facilitate predictable orientation — use them.

Strategies to mitigate PPM across the lifetime
- Use supra‑annular valves where appropriate.
- Consider initial slight oversizing or high implant positioning to preserve leaflet opening — but balance against risk of paravalvular leak or annular injury.
- Plan for bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) at the time of ViV when using balloon-expandable surgical bioprostheses or some THV frames (note: not universally feasible). BVF can increase EOA substantially in certain constructs, but it requires the right valve type and careful preplanning.
- When stented frames are already multiple, the haemodynamic gains from BVF may be limited or unsafe — assess case-by-case.

Where BASILICA and BVF fit — and where they don’t
- BASILICA (intentional leaflet laceration) is a powerful tool to prevent coronary obstruction by splitting native or bioprosthetic leaflets that would otherwise be pinned up during ViV. It’s best used when you have an intact leaflet that will form a neo‑skirt threatening the coronary ostium.
- BASILICA limitations: its efficacy is unproven in heavily layered frames (multiple THVs) where the skirt is a metal mesh rather than native leaflets; traversing and lacerating layers becomes nearly impossible. In triple-stent anatomies, BASILICA often won’t help.
- BVF can be very useful for surgical bioprostheses or some THVs where ring fracture is possible to boost EOA. But BVF requires that the outer frame can be fractured without catastrophic annular injury or coronary compromise. In multiple THV frames, fracture might not transmit effectively to inner frames or might have unpredictable effects on coronary clearance.
- Bottom line: both BASILICA and BVF are adjuncts, not panaceas. Their utility depends on device type, anatomy, and the number/layering of existing frames.

Procedural tactics that helped in the TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV case (and you can borrow)
- CT-guided selection of a low‑height, supra‑annular valve (Acurate neo2) to maximize residual EOA.
- Aim for commissural alignment to preserve coronary access.
- High implantation relative to prior leaflets to prevent inward leaflet folding and avoid creating a neo-skirt under the coronaries.
- Pre‑ and post‑dilation strategically used to optimize expansion of prior frames (post‑dilation can partially expand a self-expanding frame like Evolut and improve orifice area).
- Meticulous exclusion of active infection before reintervention — prior endocarditis is not an absolute contraindication once treated, but it changes risk calculus.

When to favor surgery instead of serial TAVR
- Active uncontrolled prosthetic infection, annular/root abscess, or complex paravalvular destruction usually mandates surgery.
- If serial ViV (or ViV-in-ViV) would produce unacceptably small EOA with high PPM risk and the patient is a reasonable surgical candidate, root replacement might be the better durable solution.
- In very young patients, consider lifetime strategy and counsel on the likelihood of future surgical reintervention.

Takeaways — practical clinical pearls
- Think lifetime, not just index: choose a device and implant strategy that balance immediate hemodynamics with future coronary access and reintervention options.
- Use pre-procedural CT and virtual valve simulation to estimate VTC, neo-skirt formation, and cumulative frame effects.
- Favor commissural alignment and consider supra‑annular valves in small annuli to reduce future PPM.
- Realize the limits of rescue tools: BASILICA is less helpful in multilayered stent anatomies; BVF only works with susceptible valve constructs.
- When infection has been treated and excluded by imaging and cultures, ViV (even repeat ViV) can be feasible — but decisions must be individualized and multidisciplinary.
- Document your implant orientation and depth clearly — that record may be lifesaving for the next operator.

Final thought
Every TAVR implant writes the next chapter of that patient’s valve story. If you want options later, you must preserve them now — through device selection, commissural alignment, CT-driven planning, and a clear discussion with the patient about lifetime strategy. The triple‑TAVR case shows it can be done — but it takes forethought, imaging, and a team willing to plan for the next chapter before closing the cover on the first.

Key take-home messages
- Plan index TAVR with future ViV in mind: commissural alignment, VTC preservation, low frame height, and supra‑annular leaflet choice matter.
- Use MSCT and virtual valve overlays to predict neo-skirt and coronary clearance.
- BASILICA and BVF are useful tools but have limits in multi-layered THV anatomies.
- In treated endocarditis without active infection, re‑TAVR can be considered when surgical risk is prohibitive — multidisciplinary decision-making is essential.
- Document valve orientation and implantation details to aid future interventions.
