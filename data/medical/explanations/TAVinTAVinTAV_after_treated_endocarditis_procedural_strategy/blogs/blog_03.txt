Title: Blog about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

# Third act device selection: why Acurate neo2 and a high implant won the day

This is one of those cases that makes you stop and redraw your mental map of “what’s reasonable” when managing failing transcatheter valves. A man in his late 70s with a 2014 Lotus valve, a 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV with a 23 mm Evolut and an episode of Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (treated and resolved) came back with severe restenosis and intraprosthetic regurgitation. Surgical explant was prohibitive (EuroSCORE II 27%), so the team planned a third transcatheter valve — a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV — using CT-guided planning, commissural alignment and a deliberate high implant of a self‑expanding Acurate neo2 (size S). The procedure succeeded, the immediate gradient was high (mean 67 mm Hg) but improved to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and remained 26 mm Hg at 1 year with symptomatic recovery and no recurrent infection.

Why this is interesting
- Serial layering of stent frames is no longer a theoretical curiosity — it’s a real and growing clinical problem as TAVI patients live longer. Each additional frame changes landing-zone geometry, the “neo‑skirt” relationship to coronary ostia, and residual effective orifice area.
- This case shows how device selection and implantation strategy (supra‑annular valve + high implant + commissural alignment) can be planned and executed to preserve haemodynamics and coronary access even in triple‑stented anatomy after treated PVE.

Why Acurate neo2?
The choice was not random — it was driven by anatomy on MSCT and by the specific mechanical goals for a third valve inside previously implanted prostheses.

Key practical reasons for Acurate neo2 in this case
- Supra‑annular leaflet position: maximises effective orifice area relative to intra‑annular designs in a constrained lumen.
- Relatively lower frame height and open cell design compared with some alternatives: reduces the amount of “neo‑skirt” projecting toward the coronary ostia and facilitates coronary preservation and future access.
- Self‑expanding design and radial behaviour: allows gradual expansion within the constrained lumen and, with post‑dilation, can coax additional expansion of a previously underexpanded Evolut frame (without needing intentional frame fracture here).
- Device ergonomics for commissural alignment: radiopaque markers and known orientation tactics facilitate aligning the neo‑valve to prior commissures to preserve coronary windows.

Mechanics of the “high implant + pin prior leaflets” strategy
- Problem: prior leaflets (and crushed leaflet tissue) can fold inward when a new valve is implanted, creating a functional reduction of the orifice and a thick “neo‑skirt” that can both obstruct coronaries and worsen PPM.
- Strategy: a deliberately high implantation places the Acurate leaflets at or just above the level of the prior Evolut leaflets, physically pinning those leaflets open against the Evolut stent frame. That minimizes inward leaflet folding and mitigates a neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia.
- Secondary benefit: by avoiding an excessively deep implant you reduce the chance that the neo‑skirt will sit beneath the coronaries, lowering coronary obstruction risk and preserving future access.

Imaging and fluoroscopic planning — what they actually did
- MSCT was used to measure the constrained internal lumen (minimum lumen diameter in the Evolut = 13.7 mm), valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions, and to model different landing zones. This was the decision point that allowed the team to proceed without prophylactic coronary protection.
- CT modelling was used to define the optimal implantation depth to align Acurate neo2 leaflets with the Evolut leaflets.
- Fluoroscopic landmarks and device markers: alignment of the Acurate orientation marker to fluoroscopic views planned on CT (sometimes using cusp overlap / enface techniques) helped achieve commissural alignment in a way that best preserved coronary windows.

Pacing and (pre/post) dilatation strategy used
- Pre‑dilation: balloon predilation with a 22/40 mm balloon under rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) — creates space, lyses fusion tissue and allows assessment of coronary flow during balloon inflation.
- Valve deployment: Acurate neo2 was deployed in a high position, aligned as planned.
- Post‑dilation: post‑dilate with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing to optimise expansion. In this case post‑dilation likely partially expanded the Evolut frame as well, improving leaflet mobility and reducing gradient over time.
- Tip: aggressive sizing/dilation must be balanced against annular injury risk; CT sizing plus an incremental balloon strategy helps manage that tension.

Pitfalls and how they mitigated them
- Early high transvalvular gradient: immediate post‑implant mean gradient was 67 mm Hg. This can happen with constrained anatomy and layered frames. Don’t reflexively label the procedure a failure — optimise with post‑dilation and allow for early haemodynamic improvement (in this case gradients improved to 27 mm Hg at 1 month).
- Embolisation risk with high implant: self‑expanding valves can be more prone to migration if not adequately anchored. The team ensured anchoring by careful depth planning and pre‑dilation that produced a stable landing zone. A safety wire or snare bailout plan should be available if anchoring is inadequate.
- Malapposition / paravalvular leak: high implants can under‑seat in the annulus. Use intra‑procedural TEE or high-quality angiography to confirm sealing and be prepared for immediate post‑dilation.
- Coronary obstruction despite planning: CT modelling reduces but does not eliminate risk. In borderline cases have coronary protection (guide + balloon/stent or BASILICA strategy) ready.
- Future coronary access: even with commissural alignment, cumulative mesh can complicate PCI later. Document orientation and take high-quality CT/fluoro images for future operators.

Why not a balloon‑expandable or another self‑expanding platform?
- Balloon‑expandable valves (Sapien family) have high radial force but taller/denser stent skirts in some configurations and may create a thicker neo‑skirt in layered anatomies; supra‑annular self‑expanding valves like Acurate neo2 often give better EOA in constrained lumens. The team chose the device whose design features best matched the procedural goals: pin prior leaflets, preserve coronaries and maximise orifice area.

Outcome and the sobering reality
- The immediate haemodynamics were imperfect but improved substantially with the planned strategy: mean gradient went from 67 mm Hg immediately post‑op to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and stayed 26 mm Hg at 1 year with AVA ~1.1 cm² and EF 65%. The patient remained asymptomatic without recurrent endocarditis.
- This underscores two points: (1) in complex redo settings, early haemodynamics may not reflect the final functional result, and (2) meticulous preprocedural planning and intra‑procedural techniques (high implant + commissural alignment + post‑dilation) can convert an anatomically hostile situation into a durable clinical result.

Clinical pearls / take‑home messages
- Tailor device choice to the anatomic problem: in layered valve anatomy, a supra‑annular, low‑frame self‑expanding valve can maximise effective orifice area and reduce neo‑skirt burden.
- Use MSCT and 3D modelling to rehearse landing depth, estimate valve‑to‑coronary distance and plan commissural alignment — this dictates whether coronary protection is required.
- A deliberate high implant that pins prior leaflets can prevent inward leaflet folding and preserve the orifice; plan for secure anchoring and have bailout plans for embolization.
- Pre‑dilate to create space and test coronary flow; post‑dilate to optimise expansion — expect early gradients to improve after these steps and with time.
- Document implant orientation and imaging for future coronary or reintervention planning — lifetime management thinking starts at the first TAVI.

This case doesn’t make serial TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV routine, but it shows that with patient‑specific imaging, device‑platform thinking and precise implantation strategy, a third transcatheter valve can be a reasonable, durable option even after treated PVE in a prohibitive‑risk surgical patient.
