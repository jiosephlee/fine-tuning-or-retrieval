Title: Blog about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

# TAVR after treated PVE: when does the door reopen?

What makes this case unusual (and instructive) is simple to state: a man in his late 70s with Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) following a TAV-in-TAV, who completed targeted antimicrobial therapy, demonstrated no residual infection on imaging and cultures, and—because surgery was prohibitive—went on to receive a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) with CT‑guided planning and a good 1‑year result. TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated PVE is rare in the literature; this case is a practical primer on when and how TAVR can reasonably be reconsidered after PVE.

Why this matters
- PVE is a classical surgical indication. Guidelines generally favour surgery for PVE when there is heart failure, uncontrolled infection, abscess, prosthetic dehiscence or large vegetations. But many patients are not surgical candidates. As TAVI use expands and patients live longer, teams will increasingly face the question: can we re‑treat a failed transcatheter valve after proven, treated PVE?
- The answer isn’t “no” or “always yes.” It’s a conditional “yes” when infection is demonstrably cured and anatomy and haemodynamics favour a percutaneous solution that the heart team judges lower risk than surgery.

The anchors from the presented case
- Infective episode: Enterococcus faecalis PVE diagnosed after a TAV-in-TAV (Evolut) with positive blood cultures and symptomatic deterioration. The patient received an appropriate full course of targeted antibiotics (6 weeks IV ampicillin‑based therapy, then oral per ID advice). No lifelong suppressive therapy was being used at the time of reintervention.
- Confirmation of cure: repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echo (TEE) and multislice CT (MSCT) showing no vegetations, paravalvular complications or abscess — the team explicitly documented “resolution of infection.”
- Surgical risk and shared decision‑making: EuroSCORE II 27% with multiple comorbidities; the heart team and patient/family accepted a transcatheter approach as the lower‑risk option.
- Procedural strategy: CT modelling focused on valve‑to‑coronary distances, anticipated neo‑skirt, and commissural alignment; choice of a supra‑annular, low‑frame valve (Acurate neo2) and high implantation with post‑dilation aimed to preserve coronary flow and optimise effective orifice area.
- Outcome: successful implantation with early elevated gradients that improved by 1 month and remained stable at 1 year (mean gradient ~26 mm Hg, AVA ~1.1 cm²), and no recurrent endocarditis.

When you can consider TAVR after PVE — practical prerequisites
1. Documented completion of targeted antimicrobial therapy for the infecting organism(s), with an adequate duration guided by the ID team. In this case, standard IV therapy was completed and oral regimens followed per ID.
2. Microbiologic silence: repeated negative blood cultures after antibiotics and no signs of bacteremia or systemic infection.
3. Imaging clearance:
   - TEE without vegetations, prosthetic dehiscence, abscess or new paravalvular leak.
   - Cross‑sectional imaging (MSCT) showing no abscess or structural complications that mandate surgery and providing the anatomical information necessary for safe TAVR planning.
   The case explicitly used TEE and MSCT to confirm absence of active endocarditis.
4. Haemodynamic need and symptom burden: symptomatic prosthetic dysfunction (stenosis, regurgitation or mixed) that would benefit from valve intervention.
5. Heart team judgement that surgical risk is prohibitive or unacceptably high, and that percutaneous reintervention is technically feasible with an acceptable risk/benefit profile.
6. Shared decision‑making with the patient/family about the knowns and unknowns, including the limited data on outcomes after TAVR for prior PVE.

When TAVR is probably not appropriate
- Active infection (positive cultures, persistent fever, vegetations, abscess, annular destruction, prosthetic dehiscence).
- Complications that require root/annular repair (abscess, significant dehiscence or fistula) — these remain surgical indications in most guidelines.
- Inability to obtain imaging that excludes active infection or defines anatomy well enough to plan a safe procedure.

Key procedural and planning considerations (lessons from the case)
- CT is central. In multi‑stented anatomies, MSCT should guide valve choice, implantation depth, commissural alignment strategy, and assess risk of coronary obstruction (valve‑to‑coronary distance, sinus dimensions, expected neo‑skirt).
- Commissural alignment matters. Intentionally aligning the new valve commissures to existing frames can preserve coronary access — critical in patients with multivessel coronary disease.
- Valve choice matters: in tight landing zones choose devices with favourable supra‑annular haemodynamics and lower frame heights if that helps preserve coronary access and effective orifice area. In this case the team selected Acurate neo2 for its supra‑annular leaflets and low frame.
- High implantation + targeted post‑dilation can reduce leaflet infolding/neoskirt effects and may partially expand prior frames (no deliberate valve fracture in this case, but high‑pressure balloons can increase effective orifice).
- Coronary protection: plan for it if anatomy is borderline. In this patient, preprocedural CT and intra‑procedural angiography showed preserved flow without prophylactic protection. BASILICA or other leaflet‑laceration techniques are options in high coronary‑risk cases but their utility in triple‑stented anatomy is uncertain and technically demanding.
- Expect haemodynamics to evolve: immediate post‑implant gradients can be high and improve over days/weeks as frames settle and prior frames expand after post‑dilation (as occurred in this case).

Counselling the patient: recurrence risk and expectations
- Recurrent PVE is possible but not inevitable. Once infection has been convincingly eradicated by appropriate therapy and imaging/cultures are clear, prior PVE alone should not categorically exclude TAVR.
- There are limited data on long‑term reinfection risk after TAVR in patients with prior PVE; explicit counseling should acknowledge this uncertainty.
- Discuss the procedural trade‑offs: higher likelihood of patient‑prosthesis mismatch in layered frames, potential for impaired future coronary access, and the reality that future options become very limited if the new valve fails (surgical explant in a heavily stented root is high risk).
- Lay out the follow‑up plan: close clinical surveillance, early and periodic TTE (eg, baseline post‑procedure, 1 month, 3–6 months, then annually or more often if symptomatic), low threshold for blood cultures and TEE if febrile or bacteremic, and coordination with ID for prompt management of any recurrence.

Putting the guidance in perspective
- Contemporary ESC guidance (as cited in the case) still emphasizes surgical management for many PVE scenarios, particularly heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But the same guidance supports individualized decision‑making when anatomy, infection control and surgical risk favour a percutaneous solution.
- This case demonstrates a reasonable pathway: treat the infection fully, confirm microbiologic and imaging resolution, convene a heart team with imaging and ID input, plan the procedure with CT modelling, and proceed only when the team and patient accept the risks.

Clinical pearls
- “Prior PVE” is not an automatic disqualifier for TAVR; documented cure is the key.
- MSCT is not optional in complex reinterventions — it shapes valve choice, depth, commissural alignment, and decisions about coronary protection.
- Immediate post‑implant transvalvular gradients may overestimate long‑term function; re‑assessment at 1 month is often informative.
- Each additional valve compounds future access and reintervention complexity — plan the first valve with “lifetime management” thinking when feasible.

Take‑home messages
- Consider TAVR after PVE only when infection is convincingly eradicated (completed targeted therapy, negative blood cultures, TEE/MSCT without vegetations/abscess), and the heart team judges surgery to be prohibitive.
- CT‑guided procedural planning (valve‑to‑coronary distance, neo‑skirt, commissural alignment) is essential in redo and multi‑stented anatomies.
- Counsel patients on recurrence risk and the increasing limitations imposed by each layer of valve frame — shared decision‑making is mandatory.
- Prior infection history should not be the sole determinant once cure is established; anatomy, haemodynamics and lifetime strategy should guide the choice between surgery and transcatheter reintervention.

This case adds useful, pragmatic evidence that with careful multidisciplinary assessment, imaging confirmation of cure, and strategy‑driven planning, TAVR can be a durable option after treated PVE in select high‑risk patients — but the decision must be individualized and the team must prepare for limited future options should the new valve fail.



# CT or bust: imaging the road to TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

This one catches the eye because it’s not “TAV-in-TAV” — it’s a third valve inside two prior transcatheter frames, after a treated episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis. The team used multislice CT (MSCT) as the backbone of decision‑making and pulled off a technically demanding Acurate neo2 implantation with a planned high implant, no coronary protection, and a 1‑year durable clinical result. If you’re going to consider repeat transcatheter interventions, especially serial reinterventions, this case shows why CT should drive everything.

Below I run through the practical CT‑first playbook used here: what to measure, how those numbers drive valve choice and implant strategy, and the specific CT‑guided decisions that made a high implant feasible and safe in this patient.

## Why CT first matters more than ever in layered valve anatomy
Every additional stent frame changes the landing zone geometry, the skirt that can obstruct coronaries, and the residual effective orifice area. In triple‑stented anatomy you’re juggling:
- frame‑to‑frame interactions,
- potential inward folding of prior leaflets that further narrows the lumen,
- cumulative metal mesh that can impede coronary access or make leaflet motion worse.

MSCT gives you the anatomy and the spatial relationships you need to plan valve type, size, depth, and backup strategies (coronary protection, BASILICA, or conversion to surgery). In this case MSCT showed a minimum internal lumen of 13.7 mm through the Evolut frame and adequate valve‑to‑coronary clearance — information that directly allowed the team to opt for a high implant of a supra‑annular device without pre‑emptive coronary protection.

## What to measure on CT (and why it matters)
Measure these and put the numbers into your implant algorithm:

- Coronary ostial heights (left and right)
  - Why: low coronary heights increase obstruction risk; help decide need for protection or leaflet laceration (BASILICA).
- Sinus of Valsalva dimensions (width and height)
  - Why: small sinuses limit space for displaced leaflets/neo‑skirt; narrow sinuses raise obstruction risk.
- Sinotubular junction (STJ) diameter and height
  - Why: a tight STJ can trap the neo‑skirt and increase obstruction or upstairs impingement.
- Valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance (measured from anticipated skirt/neo‑skirt to coronary ostium)
  - Why: the best single predictor of coronary obstruction — VTC <~4 mm (often used cutoff) is high risk.
- Minimal internal diameter (ID) of the existing stented column (the “true” residual lumen)
  - Why: governs the achievable effective orifice area (EOA), risk of severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM), and whether a planned prosthesis can expand sufficiently. In our case the minimal lumen was 13.7 mm.
- Neo‑skirt/anticipated skirt height relative to coronary ostia
  - Why: model where a new device’s skirt will land relative to coronaries — crucial when previous leaflets and frames stack.
- Commissural and leaflet orientation of prior valves
  - Why: to plan commissural alignment of the new valve to preserve coronary access and reduce cumulative mesh burden.

Tip: when available, run virtual valve implantation or 3D modeling to visualize skirt position and estimate VTC under deployment conditions. If not available, combine careful multiplanar measurements with fluoroscopic landmark planning.

## How CT findings drove choices in this case
- Minimal lumen 13.7 mm and CT modelling showed sufficient VTC and sinus space → team decided coronary protection was not required.
- The expected landing zone aligned with the Evolut leaflet tips; a high implant was planned so the new frame would pin the prior leaflets outward (reducing inward leaflet folding and maximising EOA).
- Valve choice: Acurate neo2 (self‑expanding, supra‑annular leaflets, relatively low frame height) was selected to maximise effective orifice area in a narrow residual lumen and to facilitate commissural alignment.
- Commissural alignment: planned using CT and fluoroscopic landmarks to preserve future coronary access.
- Post‑dilation strategy: planned to optimise expansion and possibly partially expand the Evolut frame to improve flow — they post‑dilated (20/40 balloon) after implantation and saw haemodynamic improvement over time.

All of these decisions were CT‑driven.

## Procedural maneuvers that were CT‑informed in practice
This case used CT to justify and execute several key steps:
- Skip upfront coronary protection because CT modelled sufficient valve‑to‑coronary clearance.
- Aim for a higher implant (landing at the level of Evolut leaflets) to pin old leaflets outward and avoid creating a low neo‑skirt that could block coronaries.
- Choose a supra‑annular valve with a low frame and favorable EOA profile (Acurate neo2) to mitigate PPM risk in a small residual lumen.
- Use pre‑dilation (22/40 balloon under rapid pacing) to test leaflet mobility and lumen compliance, and plan post‑dilation to improve expansion and transvalvular gradients.
- Attempt commissural alignment during deployment to preserve future coronary access in a patient with known CAD.

The immediate post‑implant mean gradient was high (67 mm Hg) — a reminder that early gradients after multiple frames can be misleading. With post‑dilation and partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame, gradients fell to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and 26 mm Hg at 1 year, with AVA ~1.1 cm2 and clinical improvement.

## Caveats and when CT may point you elsewhere
- If VTC is small (eg <4 mm), coronary protection strategies — guidewires/balloons in coronaries, pre‑positioned stents, or BASILICA — should be on the table. In triple frames, BASILICA feasibility is uncertain and technically demanding.
- Very small residual IDs (eg <12 mm) make acceptable EOA unlikely with another valve; surgical options or alternative strategies may need consideration.
- Prior endocarditis must be resolved. In this case the infection had been treated with no evidence of active endocarditis on cultures, TEE and CT before reintervention — a non‑negotiable prerequisite.
- CT modelling is only as good as the assumptions you feed it. Consider dynamic factors (pulsatility, balloon‑induced expansion) and have a contingency plan for coronary compromise.

## Clinical pearls
- In complex reinterventions, CT is not optional — it’s the map that tells you if the road is passable and where the cliffs are.
- Measure everything: coronary heights, sinus widths, STJ, valve‑to‑coronary distance, minimal internal diameter, neo‑skirt height, and commissural orientation.
- Use CT to decide whether you can safely skip coronary protection — but have a bailout plan if angiography suggests unexpected compromise.
- High implantation that pins prior leaflets outward can preserve EOA in layered frames; combine this with a supra‑annular valve where possible.
- Expect immediate post‑implant gradients to be higher than the 1‑month value; post‑dilation and frame mechanical expansion can substantially improve haemodynamics over weeks.
- Prior treated PVE is not an absolute contraindication to TAV‑in‑TAV reintervention — but confirm eradication with cultures, TEE, and CT and proceed only after multidisciplinary review.

## Take‑home bullets
- MSCT is the cornerstone of planning for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV — measure coronaries, sinuses, STJ, VTC, neo‑skirt, commissures, and the minimal residual lumen.
- Use CT measurements to pick valve type (supra‑annular vs intra‑annular), decide implant depth, and assess the need for coronary protection.
- A planned high implant to pin old leaflets outward, combined with commissural alignment and post‑dilation, can rescue effective orifice area in triple‑stented anatomy.
- Confirm eradication of prosthetic valve endocarditis before reintervention and use a multidisciplinary heart team for these high‑stakes decisions.
- Expect haemodynamics to improve after optimisation — immediate gradients may overestimate long‑term obstruction.

This case demonstrates the practical payoff: CT‑led planning enabled a third TAVI in a prohibitive‑risk patient with prior PVE and two prior prostheses, producing durable symptomatic and haemodynamic improvement at 1 year. If you’re planning serial transcatheter interventions, make CT your first move — and measure everything.



# Third act device selection: why Acurate neo2 and a high implant won the day

This is one of those cases that makes you stop and redraw your mental map of “what’s reasonable” when managing failing transcatheter valves. A man in his late 70s with a 2014 Lotus valve, a 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV with a 23 mm Evolut and an episode of Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (treated and resolved) came back with severe restenosis and intraprosthetic regurgitation. Surgical explant was prohibitive (EuroSCORE II 27%), so the team planned a third transcatheter valve — a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV — using CT-guided planning, commissural alignment and a deliberate high implant of a self‑expanding Acurate neo2 (size S). The procedure succeeded, the immediate gradient was high (mean 67 mm Hg) but improved to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and remained 26 mm Hg at 1 year with symptomatic recovery and no recurrent infection.

Why this is interesting
- Serial layering of stent frames is no longer a theoretical curiosity — it’s a real and growing clinical problem as TAVI patients live longer. Each additional frame changes landing-zone geometry, the “neo‑skirt” relationship to coronary ostia, and residual effective orifice area.
- This case shows how device selection and implantation strategy (supra‑annular valve + high implant + commissural alignment) can be planned and executed to preserve haemodynamics and coronary access even in triple‑stented anatomy after treated PVE.

Why Acurate neo2?
The choice was not random — it was driven by anatomy on MSCT and by the specific mechanical goals for a third valve inside previously implanted prostheses.

Key practical reasons for Acurate neo2 in this case
- Supra‑annular leaflet position: maximises effective orifice area relative to intra‑annular designs in a constrained lumen.
- Relatively lower frame height and open cell design compared with some alternatives: reduces the amount of “neo‑skirt” projecting toward the coronary ostia and facilitates coronary preservation and future access.
- Self‑expanding design and radial behaviour: allows gradual expansion within the constrained lumen and, with post‑dilation, can coax additional expansion of a previously underexpanded Evolut frame (without needing intentional frame fracture here).
- Device ergonomics for commissural alignment: radiopaque markers and known orientation tactics facilitate aligning the neo‑valve to prior commissures to preserve coronary windows.

Mechanics of the “high implant + pin prior leaflets” strategy
- Problem: prior leaflets (and crushed leaflet tissue) can fold inward when a new valve is implanted, creating a functional reduction of the orifice and a thick “neo‑skirt” that can both obstruct coronaries and worsen PPM.
- Strategy: a deliberately high implantation places the Acurate leaflets at or just above the level of the prior Evolut leaflets, physically pinning those leaflets open against the Evolut stent frame. That minimizes inward leaflet folding and mitigates a neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia.
- Secondary benefit: by avoiding an excessively deep implant you reduce the chance that the neo‑skirt will sit beneath the coronaries, lowering coronary obstruction risk and preserving future access.

Imaging and fluoroscopic planning — what they actually did
- MSCT was used to measure the constrained internal lumen (minimum lumen diameter in the Evolut = 13.7 mm), valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions, and to model different landing zones. This was the decision point that allowed the team to proceed without prophylactic coronary protection.
- CT modelling was used to define the optimal implantation depth to align Acurate neo2 leaflets with the Evolut leaflets.
- Fluoroscopic landmarks and device markers: alignment of the Acurate orientation marker to fluoroscopic views planned on CT (sometimes using cusp overlap / enface techniques) helped achieve commissural alignment in a way that best preserved coronary windows.

Pacing and (pre/post) dilatation strategy used
- Pre‑dilation: balloon predilation with a 22/40 mm balloon under rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) — creates space, lyses fusion tissue and allows assessment of coronary flow during balloon inflation.
- Valve deployment: Acurate neo2 was deployed in a high position, aligned as planned.
- Post‑dilation: post‑dilate with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing to optimise expansion. In this case post‑dilation likely partially expanded the Evolut frame as well, improving leaflet mobility and reducing gradient over time.
- Tip: aggressive sizing/dilation must be balanced against annular injury risk; CT sizing plus an incremental balloon strategy helps manage that tension.

Pitfalls and how they mitigated them
- Early high transvalvular gradient: immediate post‑implant mean gradient was 67 mm Hg. This can happen with constrained anatomy and layered frames. Don’t reflexively label the procedure a failure — optimise with post‑dilation and allow for early haemodynamic improvement (in this case gradients improved to 27 mm Hg at 1 month).
- Embolisation risk with high implant: self‑expanding valves can be more prone to migration if not adequately anchored. The team ensured anchoring by careful depth planning and pre‑dilation that produced a stable landing zone. A safety wire or snare bailout plan should be available if anchoring is inadequate.
- Malapposition / paravalvular leak: high implants can under‑seat in the annulus. Use intra‑procedural TEE or high-quality angiography to confirm sealing and be prepared for immediate post‑dilation.
- Coronary obstruction despite planning: CT modelling reduces but does not eliminate risk. In borderline cases have coronary protection (guide + balloon/stent or BASILICA strategy) ready.
- Future coronary access: even with commissural alignment, cumulative mesh can complicate PCI later. Document orientation and take high-quality CT/fluoro images for future operators.

Why not a balloon‑expandable or another self‑expanding platform?
- Balloon‑expandable valves (Sapien family) have high radial force but taller/denser stent skirts in some configurations and may create a thicker neo‑skirt in layered anatomies; supra‑annular self‑expanding valves like Acurate neo2 often give better EOA in constrained lumens. The team chose the device whose design features best matched the procedural goals: pin prior leaflets, preserve coronaries and maximise orifice area.

Outcome and the sobering reality
- The immediate haemodynamics were imperfect but improved substantially with the planned strategy: mean gradient went from 67 mm Hg immediately post‑op to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and stayed 26 mm Hg at 1 year with AVA ~1.1 cm² and EF 65%. The patient remained asymptomatic without recurrent endocarditis.
- This underscores two points: (1) in complex redo settings, early haemodynamics may not reflect the final functional result, and (2) meticulous preprocedural planning and intra‑procedural techniques (high implant + commissural alignment + post‑dilation) can convert an anatomically hostile situation into a durable clinical result.

Clinical pearls / take‑home messages
- Tailor device choice to the anatomic problem: in layered valve anatomy, a supra‑annular, low‑frame self‑expanding valve can maximise effective orifice area and reduce neo‑skirt burden.
- Use MSCT and 3D modelling to rehearse landing depth, estimate valve‑to‑coronary distance and plan commissural alignment — this dictates whether coronary protection is required.
- A deliberate high implant that pins prior leaflets can prevent inward leaflet folding and preserve the orifice; plan for secure anchoring and have bailout plans for embolization.
- Pre‑dilate to create space and test coronary flow; post‑dilate to optimise expansion — expect early gradients to improve after these steps and with time.
- Document implant orientation and imaging for future coronary or reintervention planning — lifetime management thinking starts at the first TAVI.

This case doesn’t make serial TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV routine, but it shows that with patient‑specific imaging, device‑platform thinking and precise implantation strategy, a third transcatheter valve can be a reasonable, durable option even after treated PVE in a prohibitive‑risk surgical patient.



# The gradient that got better: decoding early severe PPM after triple TAVR

What grabbed my attention in this case was how alarming the immediate post‑implant Doppler looked — a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg after a carefully CT‑planned TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV — and then how it settled to the mid‑20s by 1 month and stayed there at 1 year with an excellent clinical result. That trajectory raises two questions every implanter and echo person will ask: why was the gradient so high right away, and how do we decide whether to re‑intervene immediately or watch and wait?

Below I unpack the plausible mechanisms, what to check at the bedside and in the cath lab, how to interpret Doppler indices in layered frames, and practical thresholds to counsel patients about realistic haemodynamic goals after complex re‑interventions.

## Why a very high gradient can be seen immediately after a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV
Think of the procedure as dropping a new valve into a narrow, metal‑reinforced chimney. Multiple overlapping frames and trapped leaflets create a mechanical environment very different from a native annulus. The most likely contributors to an early, very high transvalvular gradient are:

- Under‑expansion / constrainment of the new stent frame. When you implant a valve inside one or two prior stents, radial expansion can be limited by the existing cages. Even after ballooning, the new frame may not reach its full diameter immediately. Time and additional mechanical stress (e.g., post‑dilatation, intrinsic radial recoil of prior frames) can allow gradual expansion over hours–weeks.
- Leaflet restriction from layered leaflets and pinned native/prosthetic leaflets. Overlapping leaflets can initially restrict central orifice area (inward folding). Post‑dilation and mechanical settling can re‑orient leaflets and restore more normal opening.
- Immediate hemodynamic and measurement factors. Post‑procedure haemodynamics are labile. Rapid pacing, transient hypotension, tachycardia, altered stroke volume, and suboptimal Doppler angle can exaggerate velocities. Pressure recovery phenomena and small sinuses exacerbated by stacked frames can also distort gradients.
- Residual effective orifice limitation (true prosthesis–patient mismatch, PPM). Even in the best placement, a small final orifice may produce high gradients — but in layered frames that “final” orifice can improve after the stents settle or after high‑pressure post‑dilation expands upstream frames.
- Valve thrombosis (less likely immediately but crucial to consider). Thrombosis can produce increased gradients in early weeks and would often improve with anticoagulation; it’s usually diagnosed by reduced leaflet motion on CT/TEE or rising gradients in the absence of mechanical under‑expansion.

In the reported case, the team performed pre‑ and post‑dilatation and achieved good angiographic results with no regurgitation and preserved coronary flow — but the immediate echo still showed a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. Plausible explanations are incomplete mechanical expansion and early leaflet restriction that improved with further mechanical remodeling and settling over days to weeks, plus changes in flow dynamics as the left ventricle remodeled.

## What to check immediately when you see a very high post‑implant gradient
When confronted with a very high mean gradient right after valve deployment:

1. Review the procedural fluoroscopy and aortography
   - Valve depth and final frame expansion: is the device seated where planned? Any obvious pinching or compression?
   - Any residual paravalvular leak or obstruction of coronary flow?

2. Obtain focused TTE/TEE (if available)  
   - Assess leaflet motion (are leaflets mobile or tethered?), presence of thrombosis, paravalvular regurgitation, LV function, and transvalvular velocities with careful Doppler alignment.
   - Calculate DVI (Doppler velocity index) and AVA by continuity if feasible — but interpret with caution (see below).

3. Consider immediate balloon post‑dilation if fluoroscopy/angiography suggests under‑expansion and clinical team believes it’s safe (coronary protection, rupture risk, prior stent composition).

4. If gradients remain high and there’s clinical concern over thrombosis or leaflet immobility, use gated CT (4D‑CT) to evaluate leaflet motion and frame expansion. If thrombosis is suspected, therapeutic anticoagulation can be trialed depending on bleeding risk.

5. Balance aggressiveness against limited future options. In triple‑stented anatomies (and particularly in patients with prior endocarditis or prohibitive surgical risk), each additional maneuver carries long‑term implications for coronary access and explant difficulty. Multidisciplinary discussion is essential.

## How to interpret Doppler indices in layered frames: DVI, AVA, indexed AVA
Doppler and continuity equation metrics are still useful, but they have caveats in prosthetic and layered anatomy:

- DVI (VTI LVOT / VTI across valve, or Vmax LVOT / Vmax across valve) is less dependent on LVOT area measurement errors and is often more robust than AVA when stent frames alter LVOT geometry. DVI values around 0.25 or less usually indicate severe obstruction for prosthetic valves — in this case immediate DVI 0.24 was consistent with severe obstruction.
- AVA calculated by continuity equation can be unreliable if LVOT diameter is hard to measure because of stent frames or if the LVOT velocity profile is altered. Errors in LVOT area are squared in the calculation, so be cautious.
- Indexed AVA is critical for assessing PPM: generally, indexed AVA ≤0.65 cm²/m² is considered severe PPM (≤0.60 cm²/m² in obese patients). This patient had an indexed AVA 0.4 immediately (severe PPM territory) improving to a better but still limited value (AVA 1.1 cm²) — in triple frames you may have to accept higher gradients than after a single, supra‑annular valve.

Bottom line: rely on multiple concordant data points (DVI, AVA, mean gradient, clinical status, imaging of leaflet motion) rather than a single Doppler number.

## Why gradients can improve over days to weeks
- Mechanical settling/creep of stent frames: self‑expanding frames continue to expand over time; constraining frames may plastically deform and allow greater lumen.
- Post‑dilation effects may take effect beyond the immediate imaging window. High‑pressure dilatation can remodel prior frames and release pinched leaflets.
- Leaflet flexibility can improve: tissue that is initially stiff or pinched may move better after mechanical re‑orientation.
- LV remodeling and improved forward stroke volume may paradoxically alter gradients: gradients are flow‑dependent — but in this case the initial very high gradient likely reflected obstruction; as the valve effective orifice improved, LV recovered and flow normalized, producing the stable mid‑20s mean gradient seen later.
- If thrombosis were present and treated with anticoagulation (not in this case), gradients can fall over days.

## When to observe vs re‑intervene
Watching and waiting is reasonable when:
- The patient is hemodynamically stable and asymptomatic or clinically improved.
- Imaging (fluoroscopy/angiography, TEE) shows acceptable valve position, no significant paravalvular leak, and no clear mechanical problem that must be fixed.
- There’s a plausible mechanism for improvement (e.g., immediate under‑expansion expected to relax) and the team can follow closely (48–72 hours echo, 1 month echo and clinical review).
- Reintervention options are limited or high‑risk (as in triple‑stented anatomy or after treated PVE), so conservative management is preferred when safe.

Consider reintervention (or immediate additional maneuvers) when:
- The patient is unstable, symptomatic with heart failure, or has worsening LV function attributable to the valve.
- Imaging shows clear mechanical malpositioning that can be corrected (e.g., very low implant, severe pinching correctable with ballooning).
- Thrombosis is likely and the patient cannot be anticoagulated or does not respond to anticoagulation and gradients stay high.

In this case the team accepted early severe PPM, watched the patient closely, and serial echo showed dramatic improvement without additional interventions — a reasonable approach given the prohibitive surgical risk and limited salvage options.

## Realistic haemodynamic targets and how to counsel patients
After complex repeat TAVR, especially with three stents, haemodynamic goals are pragmatic rather than ideal:

- Mean gradient: <20–25 mm Hg is ideal after aortic valve replacement, but in TAV‑in‑TAV and triple configurations, a mean gradient in the mid‑20s can be an acceptable and durable outcome if the patient is asymptomatic and LV function is preserved.
- Indexed AVA: aim for as high as possible; severe PPM (indexed AVA ≤0.65 cm²/m²) predicts worse outcomes. In some complex anatomies you may have to accept moderate PPM while balancing procedural risks.
- Symptoms and LV function trump numbers. If the patient is asymptomatic with preserved LVEF and reverse remodeling (as in this report), mid‑20s gradients can be compatible with excellent clinical status.

Counsel patients that:
- Immediate post‑procedure numbers can be misleading; gradients can fall over days to weeks as the valve and surrounding stents settle.
- Close early follow‑up with TTE is essential — 24–72 hours, 1 month, and ongoing surveillance thereafter.
- If gradients fail to improve or symptoms recur, therapeutic options are limited and higher risk — but balloon valvuloplasty, targeted post‑dilation, anticoagulation for suspected thrombosis (if appropriate), or in extreme cases surgery, remain possibilities.

## Final takeaways
- A very high immediate mean gradient after complex repeat TAVR is alarming but not uniformly an indication for immediate re‑intervention. Consider mechanical under‑expansion, leaflet restriction, measurement artefacts, and thrombosis.
- Use a stepwise approach: reassess with imaging (fluoroscopy, aortography, TEE), consider safe post‑dilation if under‑expansion is suspected, and if stable, observe with close echo follow‑up before committing to more invasive options.
- In layered frame anatomy, DVI is often more reliable than continuity‑derived AVA, but use multiple concordant measures plus clinical status.
- Realistic haemodynamic expectations after TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV may be higher gradients than after a single well‑expanded valve; a mean gradient in the mid‑20s with symptom relief and preserved LV function can be an acceptable and durable outcome.

This case is a great reminder: don’t panic at the Doppler number alone — look, think, and follow.



# Keeping the doors open: preserving coronary access through layered frames

This case is a neat illustration of one of the hard lessons from “TAVI for life”: every valve we put in today shapes the options for tomorrow. A man in his late 70s with two prior TAVIs (Lotus → Evolut) developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, was successfully treated, and later presented with severe restenosis of the TAV-in-TAV. The team performed a third transcatheter implantation (Acurate neo2) guided by CT-based planning, deliberate commissural alignment and a high implant strategy. No coronary protection was used because CT modelling showed adequate clearance, and at 1 year the patient is asymptomatic with stable valve function.

Why this case is instructive
- Triple-stented aortic anatomy is becoming more common as TAVI expands. The Achilles’ heel in repeat TAV procedures is coronary reaccess — and once you “bury” the coronaries behind multiple frames and neo-skirts, future PCI becomes much harder or impossible.
- The team translated pre-procedure CT into a clear, actionable implant plan: target a device with favourable haemodynamics and a low/short skirt, align commissures to predictable coronary windows, and implant high to avoid creating a blocking neo-skirt. The one-year result validates this strategy in a very high-risk, post‑endocarditis patient.

How to translate this into practice — a practical roadmap

1) CT-based commissural-to-coronary mapping (do this every time you’re re‑intervening)
- Reconstruct the prosthesis frames and map commissural posts relative to coronary ostia in 3D. Measure valve-to-coronary (VTC) distances and sinus of Valsalva volumes. When multiple frames are present, assess the cumulative mesh and the projected neo‑skirt height.
- Identify the target landing zone relative to the existing leaflets (are you going to land at the level of the inner valve leaflets to pin them open, or higher/lower?). In this case the CT showed feasibility for a high implant that would fix the Evolut leaflets against the stent frame and minimise neo‑skirt below the coronaries.
- Use the CT to choose fluoroscopic projection angles and to plan the delivery orientation needed to place the new valve commissures away from the coronary ostia.

2) Valve platform selection — pick devices that help you keep the doors open
- Prioritise devices with:
  - Short skirts or predictable neo-skirt geometry
  - Low frame height or large cell geometry for future catheter access
  - Established techniques for commissural alignment
  In this case the team selected Acurate neo2 for its supra‑annular leaflets, relatively low frame, and favourable haemodynamics in narrow anatomies.
- If you need maximal radial expansion of the old frame, know which frames are amenable to bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) and which are not — self‑expanding nitinol frames (e.g., Evolut) are not reliably fractured like balloon‑expandable frames.

3) Implant strategy — align, go high, minimise the neo-skirt
- Commissural alignment: rotate the delivery system based on CT/fluoro landmarks so that new commissures sit between coronary ostia. Predictable commissural placement reduces the chance of coronary columns being behind heavy mesh.
- High implantation: when safe (i.e., not risking coronary obstruction or paravalvular leak), a higher implant can pin previous leaflets outward, reduce the amount of neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia and preserve coronary windows. That was the deliberate choice here.
- Post‑dilatation can be valuable: in this case post‑dilatation and partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame contributed to improved gradients over weeks — but be cautious: aggressive post‑dilatation in layered frames can risk annular injury or conduction system damage.

4) Intraprocedural coronary protection and bailouts — have a plan and equipment ready
- Routine coronary protection (prophylactic wire ± balloon/stent) is not always required, but in any case where CT shows borderline VTC or small sinuses, position a guidewire in the target coronary and keep a balloon/stent ready.
- Chimney stenting is a lifesaving bailout for acute coronary obstruction but has downsides: it creates a permanent conduit that complicates future interventions and can cause stent thrombosis or flow turbulence in sinuses.
- BASILICA (leaflet laceration) prevents leaflet-related coronary obstruction but is of uncertain utility in triple-stented anatomies: prior multiple frames and an already-altered leaflet geometry reduce predictability of laceration and may blunt the intended effect.
- BVF can increase effective orifice area in some balloon-expandable surgical or transcatheter valves; in layered configurations and with nitinol frames its effectiveness is limited and unpredictable. In this case no intentional BVF was performed — balloon post‑dilation was used to improve expansion.
- Always have a written bailout corridor: which coronary to protect first, what stent size you will use for a chimney, who will initiate emergency CABG if needed, and how you will manage access if coronary wiring becomes difficult.

5) Documentation — make life easier for the next operator
- Save and store:
  - The CT commissural-to-coronary maps and intended fluoroscopic angles
  - The delivery system orientation/rotation used for commissural alignment (frames are labelled; note rotations in degrees)
  - Implant depth (distance from annulus to valve frame), valve model/size, and exact deployment height relative to prior valve landmarks
  - Whether coronary protection was used and the location of any jailed wires or stents
- When discharging or handing over, provide a concise “future-PCI note”: predicted coronary windows, recommended C-arm views, and which stent cells offer the best access path.

Why some bailout strategies are limited in triple‑stent anatomy
- BASILICA depends on lacerating native/prosthetic leaflets to create a channel. When leaflets are sandwiched between two or more frames, laceration may not reliably create a wide, durable escape path for coronaries — the stent mesh can still obstruct.
- BVF is effective for certain balloon‑expandable frames (stent fracture to increase EOA), but with nested self‑expanding frames the central obstruction source is often pinned leaflets and limiting neo‑skirt geometry rather than a single rigid frame that can be fractured.
- Chimney stenting works as a rescue but converts a surgical problem into a long-term PCI problem; in a patient with multivessel CAD and prior PCI (as in this case), that may complicate future management.

What this case showed — practical outcomes
- The team used CT-driven planning to decide that coronary protection was not needed and to orient commissures and implant depth. They chose a valve with favourable geometry (Acurate neo2) and performed high implantation with post‑dilatation. Immediately post‑implant gradients were high (mean 67 mm Hg) but improved markedly over the following month (mean 27 mm Hg), reflecting mechanical expansion and optimisation. At 1 year the patient remained active, asymptomatic, with stable gradients and no recurrent infection.

Clinical pearls
- Never assume coronary access will be possible after TAV-in-TAV. Always do CT mapping in re‑interventions and plan commissural orientation relative to the coronaries.
- Choose the valve platform and an implantation depth that balance immediate haemodynamics with future coronary access — sometimes a “good enough” valve now that leaves coronary windows is better than the lowest possible gradient that sacrifices access.
- Have coronary protection equipment ready whenever VTC is borderline; chimney stenting and BASILICA are useful tools but have limitations in layered frames.
- Document everything: CT maps, orientation/rotation, implant depth, and whether coronaries were protected. That record is invaluable for the next operator.

Take‑home messages
- Preprocedural CT commissural-to-coronary mapping is mandatory in complex TAV-in-TAV (and especially TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) — use it to guide valve selection, rotation and implant depth.
- Select devices and deployments that prioritise predictable coronary windows (low skirt, favourable cell geometry, commissural alignment) when lifetime management is a concern.
- Be ready for coronary protection and have a clear bailout plan — BASILICA and BVF have limits in multi‑frame anatomies.
- Document orientations, angles, implant depth and protection steps meticulously to preserve options for future interventions.

Preserving the “doors” into the coronaries is a cognitive and technical discipline — do the CT work, pick the right tools, deploy with intent, and write down what you did. This patient’s 1‑year result shows it can pay off.



# Playing the long game: lifetime management after the first TAVR

What makes this case interesting?
A man in his late 70s with a Lotus implanted in 2014, a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut in 2022, intervening Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis treated medically, and then a CT-planned Acurate neo2 implanted as a third transcatheter valve in 2024 — with preserved function at 1 year. It’s a striking example of how TAVR is no longer a “one-and-done” procedure for many patients. The team used preprocedural MSCT modelling, planned commissural alignment and a high implant strategy to protect the coronaries and optimise the residual effective orifice area (EOA). The immediate postimplant transvalvular gradient was very high (mean 67 mm Hg) but fell to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and 26 mm Hg at 1 year — reminding us that frame interaction, post-dilation and cardiac remodeling can change the haemodynamic picture after valve-in-valve-in-valve (V-i-V-i-V) procedures.

If you do TAVR, think re-do from day one
This case is a practical prompt to stop thinking of TAVR as an isolated index procedure and to start planning lifetime management at the time of the first implant. Every decision you make at implant — valve type, size, orientation, implantation depth — influences the difficulty and safety of the next interventions, coronary access and the likelihood of prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) down the road.

Key planning principles illustrated by the case

1) Choose the first valve with redoability in mind
- Supra-annular valves (or valves with better EOA for a given annulus) often provide superior haemodynamics in small annuli and can reduce future PPM risk. In this case the team ultimately chose a supra-annular Acurate neo2 for the third implant to maximise EOA in a narrow, layered anatomy.
- Frame height and skirt location matter: lower skirts and frames that preserve sinus clearance make coronary access and future interventions easier.
- Retrievable/recapturable features and predictable deployment characteristics help achieve commissural alignment and controlled depth.

2) Make commissural alignment the default
- Aligning the new valve commissures away from coronary ostia preserves access and reduces the cumulative mesh burden across the coronary ostia on future procedures.
- Use fluoroscopic landmarks, manufacturer orientation markers and CT-derived cusp overlap planning to orient the valve. Document the final orientation on the procedural record.
- In multi-valve scenarios this becomes critical: the cumulative frame mesh can block coronary catheters or make coronary protection/BASILICA more difficult.

3) Proactively preserve future coronary access
- Before implant, use MSCT to measure coronary heights, sinus of Valsalva diameter and valve-to-coronary distances. If these are short, plan coronary protection or consider leaflet laceration strategies.
- In this case CT showed adequate clearance and the team proceeded without coronary protection — but that decision was CT guided.
- Consider valve selection and implantation depth to minimise a neo-skirt that could cover the ostia. When coronary disease is present or likely to be needed later, bias toward devices and implant strategies that facilitate reaccess.

4) Balance haemodynamics versus frame layering to mitigate future PPM
- Good haemodynamics now reduces the chance of heart failure and readmission, but a large rigid frame placed first may limit options later. Conversely, selecting a smaller device now to “preserve anatomy” can worsen baseline EOA and functional status.
- In small annuli think supra‑annular valves or plan for future bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) when applicable — noting BVF is established for many surgical bioprostheses but is device- and frame-dependent for transcatheter valves.
- Expect that in multi-frame situations you will rely on CT modelling, high-pressure post-dilation and sometimes mechanical expansion of prior self-expanding frames to improve final haemodynamics (as happened here without deliberate frame fracture).

5) Infection history is not an absolute contraindication to TAV-in-TAV — but timing and proof of eradication are essential
- This patient had Enterococcus faecalis PVE treated with full antimicrobial therapy, and preprocedural TEE and MSCT showed no active infection. With prohibitive surgical risk and good imaging evidence of infection resolution, repeat TAVR was pursued.
- Key: ensure infection is controlled, get negative blood cultures, and involve ID and cardiac surgery in shared decision-making.

Surveillance rhythm: practical proposal (grounded in this case)
- Baseline: TTE prior to discharge from index TAVR (to document gradient, EOA, PVL), and a post-procedure clinical & echo report filed in the EHR with device type, size and orientation.
- Early follow-up: clinical review + TTE at 30 days and 3 months to catch early dysfunction or PPM and to document remodeling (this case showed striking early gradient evolution).
- Medium/long term: annual clinical review and TTE thereafter if stable.
- Triggered CT angiography (MSCT): reserve for rising gradients (>10 mm Hg from baseline or symptomatic deterioration), suspected endocarditis, planning for redo procedures, or pre-PCI when coronary anatomy is complex or access may be difficult. MSCT played a central role here — measuring the Evolut lumen (13.7 mm) and guiding decision-making on coronary risk and valve sizing.
- Bloodwork/cultures: pursue cultures if any fever or suspicious symptoms; prior PVE warrants low threshold for imaging and multidisciplinary discussion.

Make the heart‑team record future-proof
If lifetime management matters, then documentation must too. At minimum include in the procedural note:
- Valve make, model, size and lot number; orientation marker used and final fluoroscopic orientation (include saved cine images).
- Implantation depth relative to annulus/cusp landmarks.
- Commissural alignment attempt and final alignment assessment.
- Balloon sizes used for pre- and post-dilation; final valve haemodynamics on table (mean gradient, DVI).
- MSCT-derived valve-to-coronary distances and aorta/sinus measurements that guided the procedural strategy.
- Explicit “lifetime plan” statement: what the team expects for surveillance, what reintervention options remain, and any concerns about future coronary access or explantation complexity. Share this with the patient and primary cardiologist.

Practical pitfalls and pearls from the case
- Don’t assume haemodynamics measured immediately post-implant are static in valve-in-valve constructs. High immediate gradients may improve after post dilation, partial frame expansion, and LV remodeling; but persistent very high gradients still require attention.
- CT matters. MSCT planning — not just for sizing but for valve-to-coronary distance, predicted neo-skirt, and frame interaction modelling — can change the plan from “protect the coronaries” to “no protection needed.”
- High implants can be lifesaving for coronary preservation in stacked valves, but they must be balanced against the risk of valve embolisation and paravalvular leak.
- Document everything. The orientation images you save at index TAVR will be worth their weight in gold when the patient returns in 5–10 years.

Take-home bullets
- Think lifetime at the time of the first TAVR: valve choice, skirt height, commissural alignment and documentation all matter for future re-interventions.
- Make commissural alignment the default strategy to preserve coronary access and reduce cumulative mesh across the ostia.
- Use MSCT to plan: assess coronary heights, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances and frame interactions before redo TAVR.
- Adopt a surveillance rhythm: baseline post-procedure echo, early follow-up (30 days, 3 months), then annual TTE; reserve CT for rising gradients, symptoms, endocarditis suspicion or pre-reintervention/coronary planning.
- Record a lifetime-management plan in the heart-team note and share it with the patient and outpatient team.

This triple-TAVR story doesn’t mean every patient should get three transcatheter valves. It reminds us that TAVR starts a trajectory — one we shape with our device choices, our implant technique and the quality of our planning and documentation. The better we plan today, the more options our patients will have when the valve inevitably fails.
