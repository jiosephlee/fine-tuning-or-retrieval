### [Morning Report] In a triple-stented aortic position, what are the main trade-offs between high vs low implantation depth of the new valve with respect to effective orifice area, anchoring, conduction disturbance risk, and coronary access?

Think of implantation depth in a triple‑stented aortic position as a three‑dimensional trade‑off between flow, fixation, rhythm and coronary access; the right choice depends on valve design, the anatomy of the existing frames and the priorities for lifetime management.

High implantation (more aortic/supra‑annular)
- Effective orifice area (EOA): tends to preserve or increase EOA because the new valve’s leaflets sit above and can “pin” the old leaflets outward against the prior frame rather than folding them into the flow orifice. In this case the team intentionally landed the Acurate neo2 high to avoid inward leaflet folding and reduce prosthesis–patient mismatch.
- Anchoring/stability: anchoring is potentially reduced when the frame has less contact with the annulus/LVOT. In single‑valve anatomy this raises embolization risk; in a multi‑frame situation you may still gain secondary fixation from the prior stent(s), but reliance on that is less predictable and requires CT/fluoro confirmation.
- Conduction disturbance: high implants spare the membranous septum and conduction tissue more than deep implants, so they reduce the risk of new AV block and pacemaker implantation—important here because the patient already had a pacemaker.
- Coronary access: high implantation minimizes the neo‑skirt beneath the coronary ostia and preserves coronary access and flow; this is often the dominant reason to aim high in TAV‑in‑TAV situations and was a stated goal in the presented case.

Low implantation (more ventricular/deep)
- Effective orifice area: deeper implants increase the likelihood that the new frame or the constrained leaflets of prior prostheses will encroach on the central orifice, worsening EOA and increasing the risk of PPM—especially problematic when the internal lumen is already small (as with a 13.7 mm Evolut lumen in this patient).
- Anchoring/stability: deeper seating gives more contact with the annulus/LVOT and generally provides more secure mechanical fixation and lower risk of embolization, and may allow better radial expansion of prior frames with post‑dilation.
- Conduction disturbance: deeper implants increase compression of the membranous septum and the His bundle area and therefore markedly raise the risk of new conduction disease and need for pacemaker.
- Coronary access: deeper implants tend to create a larger neo‑skirt above the annulus and can bury commissures, increasing the chance of coronary obstruction now and making future coronary engagement more difficult or impossible.

Putting it together for triple‑stented anatomy
- The cumulative stent mesh and small residual lumen magnify the consequences of whichever depth you choose: a low implant that improves anchoring may salvage immediate stability but often sacrifices EOA and coronary access; a high implant preserves flow and coronary access but may rely on unpredictable secondary fixation from prior frames and increase procedural technical risk (embolization, paravalvular leak if underseated).
- Device selection and commissural alignment become critical: choose a valve with favorable supra‑annular leaflet position and lower frame height if the plan is high, and use CT modelling to ensure valve‑to‑coronary distances and that the prior frame can provide sufficient support.
- Procedural mitigations: detailed MSCT planning, possible coronary protection, careful rapid pacing during deployment, and aggressive post‑dilation (or planned frame expansion/fracture when feasible) are tools to balance EOA and anchoring. In this report the team used CT guidance, commissural alignment, and post‑dilation to achieve acceptable gradients despite a high implant.

Clinical principle: optimize the depth to match the dominant clinical goal—if preserving coronary access and maximizing EOA (to avoid PPM) is paramount in a layered valve, favor a high, commissure‑aligned implant with careful planning; if immediate mechanical fixation is the limiting factor and coronary anatomy is generous, a deeper implant favors stability but raises pacemaker and PPM risks. In every triple‑stented case the decision should be individualized via preprocedural CT and heart‑team discussion.
