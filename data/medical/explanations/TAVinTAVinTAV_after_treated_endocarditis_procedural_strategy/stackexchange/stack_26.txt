### [Management] In a patient with multivessel coronary artery disease and triple-layered transcatheter frames, what are anticipated challenges for future coronary angiography or PCI, and how does commissural alignment mitigate (but not eliminate) these issues?

In a patient like this—three nested transcatheter frames and known multivessel coronary disease—you should expect coronary angiography and any future PCI to be substantially more difficult than in a native valve or single TAVI. The practical problems arise from the anatomy created by the stacked frames rather than from the coronary lesions themselves.

Mechanistically, multiple overlapping stent cages create a mechanical barrier at the coronary ostia: the ostial orifice can be partially or completely covered by frame struts and by a neo‑skirt formed by immobilised leaflets, metal mesh and tissue overgrowth. That produces unpredictable cell sizes and shapes, angulation of the ostium relative to the aortic root, and restricted radial windows through which wires, catheters and devices must pass. The consequences you will encounter are harder selective engagement (loss of standard coaxiality), poor guiding catheter support, difficulty advancing wires or microcatheters through small or tortuous frame cells, impaired contrast opacification and fluoroscopic landmarks (metallic artifact), longer procedure time and higher radiation/contrast use, and a greater risk of technical failure or complications (dissection, embolisation, coronary obstruction during device manipulation).

Commissural alignment is a deliberate strategy to decrease one major component of this problem: by orienting the prosthetic valve posts and leaflet commissures so they avoid the coronary ostia, you reduce the likelihood that a strut or a leaflet will sit directly in front of an ostium. In practical terms alignment increases the probability of a reasonably sized and predictable cell over the coronary, preserves a straighter catheter approach, and frequently allows selective engagement with conventional guide catheters and standard techniques. In this case the team used pre‑procedural CT and fluoroscopic landmarks to align the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut leaflets—an approach aimed squarely at preserving future coronary access.

Important caveats: commissural alignment mitigates but does not eliminate the problems. Even with perfect alignment you still have multiple metal layers, possible neo‑skirt formation and endothelialisation that narrow the working window; radial recoil or asymmetric expansion of previous frames can deform cells; future frames or post‑dilatations may change the relationship later; and imaging artifacts may still obscure ostial anatomy. Therefore you must plan any future coronary work as high‑risk: obtain recent CT to map ostial windows, consider upfront coronary protection (wires, balloons or chimney stent strategies) if intervening on critical territories, prepare specialty equipment (angled and extra‑backup guides, guide extension catheters, microcatheters, low‑profile devices), and discuss the feasibility of alternative strategies (BASILICA or hybrid/surgical options) in the heart team. In short, commissural alignment improves the odds of successful reaccess but does not remove the need for detailed preprocedural imaging, procedural contingency planning, and readiness for complex coronary techniques.
