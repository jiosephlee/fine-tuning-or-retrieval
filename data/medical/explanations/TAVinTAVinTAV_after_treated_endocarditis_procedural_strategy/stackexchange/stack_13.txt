Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Pathophysiology] Define valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance and valve-to-sinotubular junction (VTSTJ) distance on CT. How do these predict the need for coronary protection strategies (e.g., chimney stenting) or leaflet laceration (BASILICA)?

Valve-to‑coronary (VTC) and valve‑to‑sinotubular junction (VTSTJ) distances are CT-derived measurements used to predict whether transcatheter valve implantation (native or valve‑in‑valve) will obstruct coronary flow and to guide prophylactic strategies such as chimney stenting or leaflet laceration (BASILICA).

VTC is the shortest distance from the anticipated neo‑skirt (the final outer margin of the transcatheter valve leaflets / skirt after implantation) to the coronary ostium. On gated contrast CT you simulate the transcatheter valve frame/ skirt at the planned implantation depth and measure from that skirt plane to the coronary ostium in the appropriate oblique plane. Clinically, a small VTC (commonly ≤4 mm; many centres use ≤3–4 mm as the high‑risk cutoff) identifies patients at high risk for direct coronary obstruction from displaced leaflet tissue or a neo‑skirt. The smaller the VTC, the less room there is for coronary flow and for a chimney stent to be delivered and to function long‑term without compromised flow or thrombosis.

VTSTJ is the distance from the anticipated neo‑skirt to the sinotubular junction (STJ). It predicts the ability of the sinus/STJ complex to accommodate displaced native or bioprosthetic leaflets. A low VTSTJ (eg, minimal distance or <4 mm, often <2–3 mm considered particularly concerning) means the leaflet will be pushed up into a narrow sinus/STJ and can completely seal the sinus outlet — a situation termed sinus sequestration. Sinus sequestration carries two implications: (1) the coronary ostium may be occluded not by direct impingement at the ostium but by the leaflet sealing the entire sinus outflow tract, and (2) chimney stenting is often ineffective or unsafe because a stent external to the neo‑skirt cannot restore physiologic sinus washout and is at high risk of thrombosis.

How this guides the choice between prophylactic chimney versus BASILICA (or no protection)
- If VTC is large (>~4 mm), and sinuses and STJ are roomy, the risk of coronary obstruction is low and routine protection is not required — standard CT planning suffices.
- If VTC is small (≤3–4 mm) but the VTSTJ is relatively large (sinus wide, STJ high), the dominant mechanism is direct ostial impingement. In that setting prophylactic coronary protection (wiring and standby or planned chimney stent) is reasonable because a stented channel can preserve ostial patency and allow bail‑out.
- If VTC is small and VTSTJ is also small (predicting sinus sequestration), BASILICA (intentional leaflet laceration) is the preferred preventive strategy because lacerating the leaflet avoids creation of a sealed sinus and preserves native sinus washout; chimney stenting alone is unlikely to restore flow or may leave a thrombogenic blind pouch.
- Additional modifiers: low coronary height (<10–12 mm), small sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and bulky/bicuspid or externally mounted leaflet bioprostheses raise the risk and lower the VTC/VTSTJ thresholds at which you intervene. Commissural alignment and predicted neo‑skirt height (especially after TAV‑in‑TAV where frames stack) must be assessed because layered frames can effectively reduce VTC and VTSTJ compared with native anatomy.

Applying this to the presented case: the team used CT simulation and found adequate clearance between the anticipated neo‑skirt and the coronary ostia and STJ, so the VTC and VTSTJ were not in the high‑risk zone; therefore they proceeded without prophylactic chimney or BASILICA and instead planned high implantation and commissural alignment to maximize coronary clearance — a strategy justified by the CT measurements and borne out by successful coronary perfusion during balloon inflation and final angiography.

In short, VTC assesses how close the neo‑skirt comes to the coronary ostium (direct obstruction risk); VTSTJ assesses whether the sinus/STJ can accommodate displaced leaflets (sinus sequestration risk). Small VTC favors coronary protection; small VTSTJ (sinus sequestration) favors leaflet laceration (BASILICA) over chimney stenting. Always integrate these distances with coronary height, sinus anatomy, valve type, and planned implantation depth when deciding protection.
