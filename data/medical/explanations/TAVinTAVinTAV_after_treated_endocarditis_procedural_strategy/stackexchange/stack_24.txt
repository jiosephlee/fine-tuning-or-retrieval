### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the clinical indicators to suspect PVE in a TAVI recipient presenting with low-grade fever and new or worsening heart failure, and which tests should be ordered first-line?

Think of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) whenever a TAVI recipient presents with new low‑grade fever plus new or worsening heart failure — the combination raises concern that prosthetic dysfunction (leaflet infection, dehiscence, obstruction or paravalvular extension) is driving haemodynamic deterioration rather than a purely medical cause of dyspnoea.

Key clinical indicators that should increase your suspicion are a new or changing murmur or new/worsening aortic regurgitation or stenosis on exam or echo; unexplained increase in transvalvular gradients or sudden drop in valve area; persistent or relapsing low‑grade fever; persistent bacteremia or bacteremia with organisms classically associated with PVE (Staphylococcus, Enterococcus, streptococci); new conduction disturbance (new PR prolongation or heart block) suggesting root/annular extension; evidence of embolic phenomena or splenic/renal infarcts; raised inflammatory markers without alternate source; and any antecedent invasive procedure or bloodstream infection. In a TAVI patient the threshold for concern is lower because prosthetic frames and prior endocarditis increase both infection risk and the likelihood that infection will cause structural malfunction.

First‑line tests and the rationale, in order of priority
1) Blood cultures (set of three from separate venipunctures) obtained before antibiotics. Persistent or reproducible bacteremia is a major diagnostic criterion and guides targeted therapy.  
2) Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) immediately to assess valve hemodynamics and look for large vegetations or new regurgitation — rapid, bedside, helpful as first screen.  
3) Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) as soon as possible (ideally within 24 hours) because TEE has far greater sensitivity than TTE for prosthetic valve vegetations, abscesses and paravalvular leaks; use 3‑D TEE when available to improve visualization around stent frames. If TEE is limited by shadowing from multiple frames, note that negative TEE does not exclude PVE.  
4) ECG to detect new conduction abnormalities that point to annular or root extension (a surgical red flag).  
5) Basic labs: CBC, CRP/ESR, renal/hepatic panel to assess inflammatory response and guide antibiotic dosing.  
6) If echocardiography is inconclusive or you need anatomic detail for planning or to detect perivalvular complications not well seen on TEE (common in TAVI with layered frames), perform gated multislice CT (MSCT) to define abscesses, pseudoaneurysm, prosthesis position and relation to coronaries.  
7) 18F‑FDG PET/CT (or radiolabelled leukocyte imaging) when diagnosis remains uncertain after echo and CT, or in late‑PVE (>3 months) — PET adds sensitivity for prosthetic infection and perivalvular inflammation and is incorporated in modern diagnostic algorithms for PVE.  
8) Early infectious‑disease and heart‑team consultation to integrate microbiology, imaging, and consideration of surgical versus transcatheter management.

Clinical principle to keep in mind: obtain blood cultures before antibiotics and use a stepwise imaging approach — TTE → urgent TEE → MSCT and/or FDG‑PET when echo is limited or equivocal — because prosthetic materials and prior TAVI frames frequently reduce echocardiographic sensitivity and perivalvular complications (abscess, pseudoaneurysm, prosthesis dehiscence) are the findings that most often mandate surgical intervention. In the case you reviewed, Enterococcus bacteremia, new prosthetic dysfunction and TEE/MSCT evidence of resolution guided management; the same pathway, inverted for diagnostic urgency, should be followed when infection is suspected.
