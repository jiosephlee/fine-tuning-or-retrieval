Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Management] What is the role of antibiotic prophylaxis for invasive dental procedures in patients with transcatheter aortic valves, particularly those with a history of PVE?

Short answer: Transcatheter aortic valves are managed the same as surgical bioprostheses for endocarditis prevention — patients with prosthetic valves (including TAVI) and those with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis are considered high risk and should receive antibiotic prophylaxis for invasive dental procedures that are likely to produce bacteremia (manipulation of gingiva, the periapical region of teeth, or perforation of the oral mucosa). This recommendation is guideline‑based despite limited trial evidence because the consequences of PVE are severe and the absolute risk from a single prophylactic dose is small.

Clinical reasoning and practical points grounded in the case
This patient has a prosthetic aortic valve complex (three stacked TAVs) and a history of Enterococcus faecalis PVE. That prior PVE places him in the highest‑risk category for recurrent PVE, and although enterococci are more commonly associated with gastrointestinal/urogenital sources, oral procedures can produce transient bacteremia with organisms capable of seeding prosthetic material. Elective invasive dental work should be deferred until infection is fully treated and the patient is stable — in this case antimicrobial therapy was completed and imaging showed no active infection, so proceeding with dental care with prophylaxis is appropriate.

What to give and when
Follow standard prophylaxis regimens. For dental procedures that warrant prophylaxis:
- Amoxicillin 2 g orally 30–60 minutes before the procedure (adult dose).
- If allergic to penicillin: clindamycin 600 mg orally, or azithromycin/clarithromycin 500 mg orally.
No routine post‑procedure antibiotics are recommended.

Other important principles
Prioritize excellent chronic oral hygiene and regular dental care — preventing invasive procedures by reducing periodontal disease likely does more to lower long‑term endocarditis risk than episodic prophylaxis. Avoid giving prophylaxis for routine everyday activities or for noninvasive procedures (nasal, gastrointestinal) unless guidelines specify otherwise. For patients with recent PVE or ongoing signs of infection, coordinate with infectious disease/cardiology and defer elective dental interventions until infection is resolved. Document shared decision‑making and communicate with the dental team about the prosthetic valve and the need for prophylaxis.
