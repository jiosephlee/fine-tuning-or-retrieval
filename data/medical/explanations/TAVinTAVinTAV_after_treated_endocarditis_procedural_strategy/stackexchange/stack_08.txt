Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Differential Diagnosis] Immediately after a third TAVI, TTE shows a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 0.9 cm² (indexed 0.4 cm²/m²), and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 without regurgitation. What diagnoses should be considered for a high immediate post-implant gradient, and which findings favor prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM)?

When you see a very high mean gradient immediately after TAVI (67 mm Hg here) the differential is centered on acute mechanical problems versus a true small effective orifice (PPM). The immediate possibilities to consider are: acute device underexpansion or malposition (including implantation too high or too low), interference or obstruction of the new leaflets by the previous prosthetic leaflets or frame (leaflet entrapment or leaflet restriction), an acute transvalvular obstruction from trapped tissue/vegetation or thrombus (less common immediately but possible), functional measurement error (incorrect LVOT diameter or Doppler misalignment), and true prosthesis–patient mismatch because the final effective orifice is too small for the patient’s body size.

Distinguishing features that favour prosthesis–patient mismatch in this case
The findings that point toward PPM rather than an isolated acute technical failure are the markedly reduced valve area by continuity equation (AVA 0.9 cm2, indexed AVA 0.4 cm2/m2), the very low Doppler velocity index (DVI 0.24 — values <0.35 indicate severe obstruction and <0.25 strong evidence of severe PPM/obstruction), the absence of regurgitation (rules against a significant paravalvular leak altering gradients), and the known anatomy: multiple overlapping frames with a small residual lumen (MSCT minimum lumen 13.7 mm) that mechanically limit the achievable effective orifice. Those features together make a fixed small EOA the most likely explanation.

What argues against PPM and toward a reversible mechanical cause
Immediate improvement after post‑dilation or over the first weeks (as occurred here, gradients falling to 27 mm Hg by 1 month) suggests that acute underexpansion or radial expansion of prior frames contributed to the high gradient. Evidence of restricted leaflet motion on fluoroscopy/TEE or a visible malposition would point to a technical/positional problem rather than intrinsic PPM. Acute thrombus or trapped tissue would usually be suggested by focal reduced leaflet excursion and might respond to anticoagulation.

Practical approach
Confirm measurements (LVOT diameter and VTI alignment), inspect valve frame and leaflet motion on fluoroscopy/TEE, review procedural record for implantation depth and ballooning, and correlate with CT if available. If underexpansion or malposition is suspected, careful post‑dilation can improve gradients; if fixed PPM from an irreducibly small EOA caused by multiple frames is the dominant mechanism, expect persistent elevated gradients and manage according to symptoms and haemodynamics. In this patient the combination of low indexed AVA, low DVI, no regurgitation and a triple‑stented small lumen strongly supports PPM as the principal diagnosis, while the later gradient improvement indicates a component of immediate underexpansion that was at least partially relieved by ballooning and mechanical expansion of prior frames.
