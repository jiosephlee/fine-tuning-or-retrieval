### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient with two prior transcatheter aortic valves presents with fever and worsening heart failure; blood cultures grow Enterococcus faecalis and TTE suggests prosthetic valve dysfunction. What components of the modified Duke criteria and adjunct imaging would you use to confirm prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) in this setting?

The diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) in a patient with prior TAVI (and here two prior transcatheter valves) rests on applying the modified Duke criteria while recognising the lower sensitivity of transthoracic imaging for prosthetic devices and the value of adjunct multimodality imaging.

Clinically you would count the following Duke elements first. A sustained bacteremia with Enterococcus faecalis from multiple separate blood culture sets fulfils a major microbiologic criterion. Fever and a predisposing prosthetic valve are supportive minor criteria. If transthoracic echo (TTE) shows prosthetic valve dysfunction only, that is suggestive but not definitive because TTE is insensitive for prosthetic vegetations, paravalvular abscesses and dehiscence.

To confirm PVE in this setting you therefore need one of the following combinations: (1) a major microbiologic criterion (positive cultures) plus a major imaging criterion (definite evidence of endocardial involvement), or (2) the microbiologic major criterion plus enough minor criteria to reach “definite” by Duke (less common). Practically, obtain the imaging that defines a major imaging criterion:

1) Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) — first-line for prosthetic valves. TEE is far more sensitive than TTE for detecting vegetations, prosthetic dehiscence, paravalvular abscess/pseudoaneurysm or new regurgitation; demonstration of any of these is a major Duke imaging criterion and together with the positive Enterococcus blood cultures establishes definite PVE.

2) If TEE is non-diagnostic or equivocal (common with multiple stent frames), use adjunct advanced imaging. CT angiography / multislice CT (MSCT) of the aortic root can identify abscesses, pseudoaneurysm, prosthetic dehiscence and structural complications that TEE may miss and supports the imaging major criterion when concordant. 18F-FDG PET/CT (or radiolabelled leukocyte SPECT if PET unavailable) is recommended for prosthetic valves when TEE is inconclusive or to detect extracardiac septic foci; abnormal focal FDG uptake around the prosthetic sewing ring or frame is now considered a major criterion for PVE in prosthetic devices and, combined with positive blood cultures, converts a probable to a definite diagnosis. PET is particularly useful months to years after implantation where nonspecific postoperative uptake is unlikely.

3) Repeat blood cultures and clinical correlation. Persistent bacteremia despite therapy, new embolic or vascular phenomena, and rising inflammatory markers provide supportive minor criteria and help judge active infection versus treated past infection.

Apply this to the case: the Enterococcus faecalis positive blood cultures already provide a major microbiologic criterion. TTE evidence of prosthetic dysfunction is suggestive but insufficient. You must perform TEE to seek vegetations, paravalvular leak, abscess or dehiscence—if present, that plus the positive cultures makes definite PVE. If TEE is negative or equivocal (likely given layered TAVI frames), obtain MSCT of the aortic root and 18F-FDG PET/CT; focal abnormal uptake or CT-proven abscess/pseudoaneurysm constitutes a major imaging criterion and, with the positive cultures, confirms PVE. Finally, correlate with serial blood cultures, clinical course and inflammatory markers to distinguish active infection from resolved, treated PVE as was done in this patient before reintervention.
