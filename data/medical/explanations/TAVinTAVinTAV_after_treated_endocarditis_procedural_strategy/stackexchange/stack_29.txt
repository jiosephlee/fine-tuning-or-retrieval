### [Differential Diagnosis] Outline a practical algorithm to evaluate a new high gradient after TAVI: which echocardiographic parameters, invasive hemodynamics, and CT findings help distinguish PPM, valve underexpansion, malposition, leaflet thrombosis, and high-flow states.

When confronted with a newly elevated transvalvular gradient after TAVI, treat the problem as you would any acute prosthetic valve dysfunction: confirm the finding, then work through a structured imaging → physiologic → anatomic algorithm aimed at separating true patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM), frame underexpansion or malposition, leaflet thrombosis, and physiologic high‑flow states. The following practical algorithm is what I use in morning report and on the ward.

Step 1 — confirm and quantify with transthoracic Doppler before assuming a mechanical problem
Always start with careful Doppler TTE because measurement error is common. Confirm Vmax, mean gradient, continuity‑equation AVA, Doppler velocity index (DVI = LVOT VTI / aortic VTI), and stroke volume index (SVI). Key thresholds and patterns to remember: severe obstruction yields DVI ≤0.25 and mean gradient elevated; indexed AVA ≤0.85 cm2/m2 suggests PPM (≤0.65 severe). A very low DVI with small calculated AVA and a low SVI suggests true obstruction; conversely, high gradients with preserved DVI and large SVI point toward a high‑flow state or measurement artefact. Compare to prior post‑implant baseline gradients — an abrupt rise over weeks favors thrombosis or structural change; a chronic persistent high gradient since implantation suggests PPM or chronic frame underexpansion.

Step 2 — discriminate flow versus fixed obstruction
Measure SVI, heart rate, blood pressure, hemoglobin, and check for sepsis, AV fistula, or thyrotoxicosis. If SVI is high (>40–45 mL/m2) and DVI is preserved, suspect high‑flow physiology; treat reversible causes and repeat imaging. If low‑flow low‑gradient physiology is possible (low SVI with low gradients but reduced AVA), consider dobutamine evaluation (to differentiate pseudo‑severe from true obstruction) as for native AS.

Step 3 — look for leaflet motion abnormalities with TEE (including 3D) and gated CT
Transesophageal echo (preferably 3D) is useful to inspect leaflet mobility and to detect restricted cusps or vegetations. If TEE suggests immobile leaflets without vegetations, proceed to ECG‑gated contrast MSCT. On CT, two findings are decisive: hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RLM) — HALT/RLM are diagnostic of (sub)acute leaflet thrombosis when present together and correlate with reversible gradients after anticoagulation. By contrast, CT will also show structural frame problems: true frame underexpansion (small inner frame diameter, eccentric expansion, inward leaflet stent collapse) and malposition (valve plane markedly supra‑ or infra‑annular relative to surgical landmarks or to prior valves). PPM, however, is a physiologic diagnosis (small EOA relative to body size): CT will show a fully expanded frame but a small residual effective orifice (small internal diameter and small calculated EOA) consistent with intrinsic mismatch rather than a deformity that can be fixed.

Step 4 — invasive hemodynamics when noninvasive testing is ambiguous or when intervention is contemplated
Obtain simultaneous LV and aortic pressures to confirm transvalvular mean gradient (beat‑to‑beat). Peak‑to‑peak gradients can underestimate obstruction; use mean gradient from simultaneously recorded pressures. Perform pullback to exclude dynamic LVOT obstruction. If underexpansion is suspected and you plan potential therapy, a balloon retrograde catheter test (balloon valvuloplasty) can be diagnostic: a marked immediate fall in gradient with balloon expansion suggests a mechanical underexpansion that may be responsive to post‑dilation or frame fracture. If thrombosis is suspected, catheterization is not diagnostic but helps exclude other causes before committing to anticoagulation or reintervention.

Putting the findings together — distinguishing the five common causes

- Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM)
Clinical reasoning: PPM is an expected physiologic consequence when the prosthesis effective orifice area is too small for the patient’s body size. TTE shows persistently high gradients from the time of implantation, a small indexed EOA (<0.85 moderate, ≤0.65 severe), DVI low, but CT demonstrates a well‑expanded frame and normal leaflet mobility (no HALT/RLM). Invasive pressures confirm a fixed, non‑improvable gradient. Management is supportive/medical or reintervention if symptomatic and severe; ballooning generally will not help because frame is already expanded.

- Frame underexpansion (mechanical constriction)
Clinical reasoning: Gradients may be higher soon after or later if the frame never fully expanded (e.g., heavily calcified annulus or confinement by prior frames). TTE shows elevated gradients; CT shows an inner lumen diameter smaller than expected, asymmetric or eccentric frame shape, and often a minimum lumen diameter well below labelled valve size (as in the case’s Evolut minimum 13.7 mm). Invasive simultaneous pressures or a diagnostic balloon valvuloplasty that acutely reduces gradient points to mechanical underexpansion amenable to post‑dilation (or high‑pressure techniques/valve fracture where applicable). Improvement of gradients after ballooning confirms mechanical underexpansion.

- Malposition (too high or too low, or axial misplacement)
Clinical reasoning: Malposition often causes mixed findings — significant regurgitation (paravalvular) or obstructive gradients if valve sits suboptimally. TTE may show eccentric jets or unusual flow patterns; CT clearly localizes the prosthesis relative to annular plane and coronary ostia and reveals whether the skirt is intruding into the LVOT or obstructing the valve orifice. Hemodynamics may show gradients and abnormal pressure tracing; correction is rarely achieved by simple ballooning if the valve sits incorrectly — reintervention strategy depends on exact anatomy.

- Leaflet thrombosis (HALT/RLM)
Clinical reasoning: Presents subacutely with rising gradient over days–weeks. TTE shows new/worsening gradients and reduced leaflet opening on TEE; CT shows HALT and RLM. The diagnosis is supported if gradients and leaflet motion improve with therapeutic anticoagulation within days–weeks. Invasive hemodynamics confirm obstruction but are not specific. If anticoagulation fails or obstructive thrombosis is severe, consider thrombectomy or reintervention.

- High‑flow states (physiologic)
Clinical reasoning: Systemic high flow raises velocities across any valve. TTE will show high gradients but preserved DVI and an AVA that may be normal by continuity equation; SVI will be high and there will be clinical clues (anemia, sepsis, thyrotoxicosis, arteriovenous fistula). CT and TEE show normal frame and leaflet motion. Correct the precipitant and repeat imaging; no mechanical intervention is needed.

Practical sequence at the bedside
First, repeat high‑quality Doppler TTE with careful LVOT measurement and SVI. Second, review prior post‑implant baseline studies to see whether the gradient is new or chronic. Third, perform TEE (plus gated CT) if TTE is abnormal or unclear — CT gives the decisive anatomic answer for underexpansion, malposition, and HALT/RLM. Fourth, if CT/echo suggest mechanical restriction and reintervention is being considered, perform invasive simultaneous LV–aortic pressure measurement and consider diagnostic balloon dilation to test reversibility. Fifth, if HALT/RLM is found, start anticoagulation as a therapeutic trial and repeat CT/TTE to document resolution.

Application to this case
This patient had a very small CT inner lumen of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame and immediate post‑implant gradients that dropped substantially after post‑dilation and over the first month. That combination (CT demonstrating severe confinement of the prior frame, acute gradient improvement after ballooning, and sustained lower gradients thereafter) best fits mechanical underexpansion of the Evolut frame (with an element of PPM because a triple‑stented orifice will always be relatively small). There was no CT evidence of HALT/RLM to suggest thrombosis, and no high‑flow state. The team’s approach — CT‑guided valve choice, high implantation, and aggressive post‑dilation — is exactly the appropriate strategy when CT identifies frame constriction as the principal driver of high gradients.

Key teaching pearl: always integrate Doppler parameters (DVI, AVA indexed, SVI), dynamic testing (dobutamine or balloon), CT anatomic detail (inner diameter, eccentricity, HALT/RLM), and invasive simultaneous pressures. The pattern of (1) CT‑confirmed constrained frame + immediate gradient fall with ballooning = underexpansion; (2) CT with normal expanded frame + chronically high, unresponsive gradient = PPM; (3) CT showing HALT/RLM + subacute gradient rise = thrombosis amenable to anticoagulation — that triad will solve most dilemmas after TAVI.
