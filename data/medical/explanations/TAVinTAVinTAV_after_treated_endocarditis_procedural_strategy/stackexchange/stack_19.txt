Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Clinical Pearls] During a complex redo TAVI, why is rapid ventricular pacing used during balloon pre- or post-dilation, and what hemodynamic effect does it achieve?

Rapid ventricular pacing is used during balloon pre‑ and post‑dilation to transiently and predictably suppress ventricular ejection so that the balloon (or valve) is exposed to minimal transvalvular flow and cardiac motion during the critical inflation/deployment period. By pacing the ventricle very fast (commonly 160–220 bpm; 180 bpm in this case) you drastically shorten diastole, reduce stroke volume and cardiac output, and drop arterial pressure to near‑zero for a few seconds. The hemodynamic effect is a controlled, transient hypotension with minimal forward flow and almost no systolic ejection, which stabilizes the balloon/prosthesis against the aortic annulus, prevents balloon or valve displacement and reduces the opposing transvalvular pressure that would otherwise impede full expansion. In complex redo TAVI (and here, a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV), that brief period of very low flow and motion makes precise positioning and effective radial expansion possible; the trade‑offs are transient systemic hypotension, myocardial ischemia risk and potential arrhythmia, so pacing is applied for only a few seconds with monitoring and a temporary wire in place.
