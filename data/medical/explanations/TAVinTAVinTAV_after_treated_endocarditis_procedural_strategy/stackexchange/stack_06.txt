Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Management] For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis in an elderly TAVI recipient with renal vulnerability, what antibiotic regimens and durations are guideline-concordant, and in what situations is long-term suppressive therapy indicated or not?

Bottom line
- For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) in an elderly patient with renal vulnerability, guideline‑concordant therapy is 6 weeks of bactericidal, synergy‑based therapy; the preferred regimen in older or renally vulnerable patients is ampicillin plus ceftriaxone (both IV) for 6 weeks rather than an aminoglycoside‑containing regimen. Aminoglycoside (eg, gentamicin) can provide synergy but is generally avoided or limited to the first 1–2 weeks in patients at risk for nephrotoxicity. Long‑term suppressive antibiotics are reserved only for exceptional situations (persistent infection or when infection cannot be eradicated); they are not indicated when a full course of IV therapy has sterilized the blood, clinical/imaging follow‑up shows resolution, and the patient is clinically well — as in the presented case.

Why these regimens
- Enterococcus faecalis is relatively tolerant to beta‑lactams alone; killing depends on paired mechanisms that achieve synergy. Two accepted, guideline‑supported approaches for ampicillin‑susceptible E. faecalis PVE:
  - Ampicillin (eg, 2 g IV q4h or equivalent continuous infusion) plus ceftriaxone (eg, 2 g IV q12h) for 6 weeks. This dual‑beta‑lactam combination gives reliable synergistic activity without the nephrotoxicity of aminoglycosides and is the regimen of choice in elderly patients and those with renal impairment.
  - Ampicillin (same dosing) plus gentamicin (synergy) can be used, but because gentamicin causes nephrotoxicity it is generally avoided in frail/renally vulnerable patients; if used it is usually limited to the first 1–2 weeks only, while ampicillin is continued for 6 weeks.
- If the isolate is ampicillin‑resistant or the patient is severely beta‑lactam‑allergic, alternatives depend on susceptibility: vancomycin (with dosing/monitoring) or for vancomycin‑resistant enterococci (VRE) agents such as linezolid or high‑dose daptomycin ± combination therapy guided by ID and susceptibility testing.

Duration and monitoring
- Prosthetic‑valve infection requires a prolonged course: at least 6 weeks of definitive IV therapy is standard for enterococcal PVE.
- Monitor clinical status, serial blood cultures until negative, renal function, and repeat echocardiography (TEE preferred) and/or CT when indicated to document resolution and exclude paravalvular complications.

When to consider long‑term (chronic) suppressive therapy
- Indications to consider indefinite (or prolonged) oral suppressive therapy are limited and include:
  - Infection that cannot be definitively eradicated (eg, retained infected prosthetic material that cannot be removed and persistent or relapsing bacteremia despite appropriate therapy),
  - Recurrent/progressive infection in a patient who is not a surgical candidate,
  - Persistent cultures or imaging evidence of ongoing infection after an appropriate course.
- Suppressive therapy is not routine when: the patient has completed a guideline‑directed 6‑week IV course, blood cultures are sterile, symptoms have resolved, and TEE/CT show no active infection — in that situation chronic suppression confers little benefit and exposes the patient to toxicity, resistance, and drug interactions.

How this applies to the case
- The patient in the report received a 6‑week IV ampicillin‑based course for E. faecalis PVE, followed by oral therapy per ID; at the time of reintervention there were negative follow‑up blood cultures, TEE and MSCT without vegetations or paravalvular complications and no ongoing suppressive antibiotics. That approach is consistent with guideline principles: a full IV course with documented clinical and microbiologic resolution obviates routine long‑term suppression. Given his age and renal vulnerability, avoidance of prolonged aminoglycoside exposure (and choosing ampicillin + ceftriaxone when appropriate) is the safer, guideline‑concordant strategy.

Practical points for the team
- Discuss antibiotic strategy with ID early; obtain susceptibilities including high‑level aminoglycoside resistance.
- For elderly/renal‑vulnerable patients favour ampicillin + ceftriaxone over ampicillin + gentamicin.
- Ensure blood cultures are repeatedly negative and TEE (or CT) demonstrates resolution before forgoing suppressive therapy.
- Reserve lifelong suppressive antibiotics for true inability to eradicate prosthetic infection or recurrent/persistent disease despite optimal therapy.
