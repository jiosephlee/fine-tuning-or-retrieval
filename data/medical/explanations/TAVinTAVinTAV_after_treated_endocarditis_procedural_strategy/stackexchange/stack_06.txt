### [Morning Report] In a patient with severe prosthetic valve stenosis after TAVI and prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II ~27%), what factors favor TAV-in-TAV reintervention over surgical explantation?

The decision to pursue TAV‑in‑TAV rather than surgical explantation in a patient like this one rests on a balance of procedural feasibility, expected benefit, and the patient’s operative risk. In practical terms, several factors in this case favored a transcatheter re‑intervention over surgery.

First, the patient’s calculated operative risk was prohibitive (EuroSCORE II ≈27%) and he had multiple comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding, prior CABG/PCI and pacemaker). High surgical mortality and the very real likelihood of an extensive, technically demanding operation (explantation of multiple integrated stent frames with possible aortic root/ascending aorta replacement) strongly bias toward a less invasive option when it can reasonably be expected to work.

Second, active infection had been eradicated. Persistent or uncontrolled prosthetic valve endocarditis, paravalvular abscess, valve dehiscence or large residual vegetations would mandate surgery; by contrast, documented resolution of infection on TEE, negative blood cultures and completion of tailored antibiotic therapy made repeat TAVI ethically and clinically acceptable.

Third, detailed anatomic imaging demonstrated procedural feasibility and an acceptable coronary obstruction risk. Multislice CT quantified the residual lumen, valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions and frame relationships and allowed planning for high implantation, commissural alignment and post‑dilatation strategies. When CT shows adequate coronary clearance and the ability to position a new valve to preserve coronary flow and optimise effective orifice area, TAV‑in‑TAV becomes a viable choice.

Fourth, technical considerations suggested a reasonable chance of improved haemodynamics without intolerable patient‑prosthesis mismatch. In this case the team could choose a supra‑annular valve with low frame profile (Acurate neo2), plan commissural alignment and use post‑dilatation to partially expand the prior frame—strategies that together increased the likelihood of acceptable gradients. If imaging suggests that further layering will leave a critically small effective orifice area or irreversible coronary compromise, surgery would be favored; here CT and modelling supported a good haemodynamic outcome.

Fifth, prior interventions had resulted in deep integration of stent frames into the aortic wall. That makes surgical explantation not only higher risk for mortality but also technically complex, with potential need for root reconstruction. This anatomic reality often tips the balance toward a percutaneous approach when infection is controlled and CT planning deems it feasible.

Finally, shared decision‑making and multidisciplinary heart‑team review weighed the marginal procedural risk of a carefully planned TAV‑in‑TAV against the substantial immediate and long‑term risks of open surgery in this patient. The patient’s values and the expectation of symptomatic and LV functional benefit also supported transcatheter reintervention.

In short: prohibitive surgical risk and comorbidity, eradication of infection, CT‑demonstrated anatomic feasibility with low coronary obstruction risk, the ability to select a valve and implantation strategy to preserve or optimise effective orifice area and coronary access, and the technical hazards of explanting multiple integrated frames all favor TAV‑in‑TAV in this scenario. Conversely, ongoing infection, root abscess, prosthetic dehiscence or CT features predicting untenable PPM or coronary compromise would instead direct toward surgery.
