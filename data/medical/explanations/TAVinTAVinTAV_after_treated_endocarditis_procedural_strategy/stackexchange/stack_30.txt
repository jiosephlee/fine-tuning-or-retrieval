### [Clinical Pearls] How can lifetime management principles be applied at the time of the first TAVI to preserve future options, specifically regarding frame height, commissural alignment, coronary access, and the risk of patient–prosthesis mismatch?

When you plan the first TAVI in a patient who will likely need valves for life, you must think beyond the immediate procedure and deliberately preserve anatomical and technical options for future reinterventions. The practical principles are simple in concept but require disciplined planning and execution.

Choose a valve and implantation strategy with future access and haemodynamics in mind. Valve frame height and leaflet position matter: shorter frames or those with large open cells (and balloon‑expandable platforms with lower skirts) facilitate coronary access and future procedures, whereas tall, densely meshed frames increase the risk of coronary obstruction and make reaccess or BASILICA-type maneuvers more difficult. Conversely, supra‑annular valves often give superior effective orifice area (EOA) in small annuli and reduce the lifetime risk of patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM). You therefore must weigh coronary reaccess needs against haemodynamic performance for the individual patient.

Make commissural alignment routine. Pre‑procedure CT should define the orientation of the coronary ostia and provide fluoroscopic landmarks. During deployment, align the prosthesis so its commissures do not sit in front of the coronary ostia (use manufacturer marker guidance and rotation maneuvers). Good commissural alignment preserves coronary access, reduces the cumulative obstruction when a second valve is implanted, and simplifies any future coronary intervention.

Protect coronary access proactively. Measure coronary heights, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and valve‑to‑coronary distances on CT. If margins are small, prefer valve choices and implantation depths that minimize neo‑skirt formation at the ostia; consider prophylactic strategies (guidewire protection, chimney stenting, or planning for BASILICA if appropriate) when anatomy is at risk. Document valve orientation fluoroscopically and in the CT repository so future operators know where commissures and open cells are.

Minimise long‑term PPM from the start. In patients with small annuli or younger patients, preferentially choose a valve with the largest expected EOA (often supra‑annular designs) and avoid undersizing. If surgical root enlargement is an option at the outset, discuss it with the patient. When TAVI is chosen, aim for full expansion (careful sizing, balloon post‑dilation where safe) and plan implants that leave room for future valve expansion or deliberate bioprosthetic valve fracture if that is part of the lifetime plan.

Tie these elements together with imaging and documentation. High‑quality pre‑TAVI CT, intent to achieve commissural alignment, explicit choice of valve based on the trade‑offs between EOA and frame geometry, and clear operative notes (marker orientation, implant depth, any post‑dilation pressures used) all preserve options. In the case you presented, CT‑guided high implantation, deliberate commissural alignment and selection of a low‑height supra‑annular valve balanced the competing goals: maintained coronary flow and access while maximising EOA in a constrained triple‑stented anatomy—exactly the lifetime‑minded decisions you want at the first TAVI.

In short: plan for the next procedure at the first procedure—select valve platform with future access and haemodynamic needs in mind, align commissures, optimise implant depth to protect coronaries, and choose sizing/technique to minimise PPM. Document everything so future teams can execute those plans.
