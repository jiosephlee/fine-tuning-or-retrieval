Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Differential Diagnosis] Immediately after a third TAVI, a patient has a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm², and DVI 0.24 with no regurgitation, which improves to a mean gradient of 27 mm Hg and AVA 1.1 cm² at 1 month. What are the leading causes of an early high postprocedural gradient after TAV-in-TAV, how would you differentiate among them (e.g., underexpansion, PPM, leaflet thrombosis, flow conditions), and what immediate and short-term management steps are appropriate?

Short answer
The immediate differential for a very high mean gradient right after a TAV‑in‑TAV (mean 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm², DVI 0.24) includes (1) mechanical underexpansion/constrainment of the new prosthesis (including incomplete radial expansion of the new frame or inwardly displaced leaflets from prior frames), (2) true prosthesis–patient mismatch (an anatomically small effective orifice after layering of frames), (3) early leaflet thrombosis (HALT with reduced leaflet motion), and (4) reversible flow/measurement factors (low stroke volume, tachycardia, pressure‑recovery or Doppler mismeasurement). In the presented case the most likely explanation was mechanical underexpansion/PPM with subsequent partial mechanical expansion of the frames after implantation (made plausible by use of a self‑expanding Acurate neo2, balloon post‑dilatation and documented reduction of gradient to 27 mm Hg at 1 month).

How to distinguish them at the bedside and with imaging
- First step: confirm the numbers and physiology. Repeat Doppler/TTE measurements (align beam, measure VTI, continuity equation, DVI) and calculate stroke‑volume index (SVi). DVI <0.25 and AVA <1.0 cm² indicate severe obstruction, but interpretation requires SVi: if SVi <35 ml/m² you may have low‑flow physiology that alters gradients.
- Underexpansion / constrained frame:
  - Clues: immediate high gradient after implantation, fluoroscopic evidence of reduced frame diameter or restricted leaflet excursion, small intraluminal diameter on CT/MSCT compared with the expected valve diameter.
  - Diagnostic test: gated contrast CT to measure stent inner diameter and leaflet opening; fluoroscopy or TEE to assess leaflet excursion.
  - Behavior: may improve over days–weeks with self‑expanding frames or after balloon expansion; improvement without anticoagulation favors mechanical expansion rather than thrombosis.
- Prosthesis–patient mismatch (true PPM):
  - Clues: small calculated EOA indexed to BSA (iAVA ≤0.65 cm²/m² moderate‑severe), persistent elevation of gradients despite fully expanded frame on CT and mobile leaflets.
  - Diagnostic test: CT/fluoroscopy confirming full frame expansion and TEE showing normal leaflet motion; clinical persistence of high gradient after wait period.
  - Behavior: unlikely to remit without mechanical intervention.
- Leaflet thrombosis (HALT/RLM):
  - Clues: subacute rise in gradient (days–weeks), reduced leaflet motion on echo/fluoroscopy, CT shows hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening.
  - Diagnostic test: MSCT is the gold standard to detect HALT/reduced leaflet motion. Start therapeutic anticoagulation if suspected and confirm with follow‑up CT/TTE.
  - Behavior: typically improves with anticoagulation over weeks.
- Flow/measurement artefact:
  - Clues: discordant AVA/gradient (low gradient but small AVA), tachycardia, transient hypotension/low flow states, or pressure‑recovery effects.
  - Diagnostic steps: reassess hemodynamics, measure SVi, consider invasive catheter gradient if noninvasive data are equivocal.

Immediate and short‑term management principles
1. Verify and risk‑stratify
   - Recheck TTE Doppler and SVi immediately; review fluoroscopy and procedural notes for whether post‑dilatation was performed (it was, here).
   - If doubts persist, obtain intra‑aortic invasive gradients or TEE in the cath lab.
2. Stabilize hemodynamics and optimize loading conditions
   - Address hypovolemia, anemia or tachycardia that might affect Doppler readings; maintain adequate blood pressure and heart rate.
3. If mechanical underexpansion is suspected and the patient is unstable or gradients remain severe and symptomatic:
   - Consider repeat balloon post‑dilatation with coronary protection ready — but only after CT/planning review because in TAV‑in‑TAV configurations the risk of annular/root injury and coronary compromise is higher.
   - In multi‑stented anatomies this maneuver is higher risk and should be weighed carefully.
4. If leaflet thrombosis is suspected:
   - Start therapeutic anticoagulation (unless contraindicated) and obtain MSCT to document HALT/RLM; repeat imaging at 4–6 weeks to document resolution.
5. If the scenario looks like PPM after confirmed full expansion:
   - Medical optimization and close surveillance if patient stable; definitive options are limited in triple‑stented anatomy (surgical explant is high risk; further transcatheter strategies are complex and case‑specific).
6. Watchful waiting is reasonable when the patient is stable and underexpansion of a self‑expanding valve is plausible
   - Self‑expanding frames frequently continue to expand over days–weeks; this was exactly the course in this case (immediate mean gradient 67 mm Hg fell to 27 mm Hg at 1 month without evidence of HALT or regurgitation).
7. Follow‑up surveillance
   - Repeat TTE at 1 month (earlier if symptomatic) and MSCT if gradients fail to improve or there is suspicion of thrombosis or incomplete expansion.
   - If gradient remains high and symptomatic after a reasonable observation window (and CT confirms full expansion), convene heart team for consideration of reintervention vs. high‑risk surgery.

Why the case behaved as it did
- In this patient the team selected a supra‑annular self‑expanding Acurate neo2, performed balloon post‑dilatation, and documented immediate leaflet mobility without regurgitation. The large immediate gradient most likely reflected mechanical constrainment/PPM in a triple‑stented, small intraluminal lumen; subsequent progressive expansion of the new and prior frames and relief of inward leaflet restriction produced the marked hemodynamic improvement at 1 month (mean 27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²). There was no clinical, echocardiographic or management signal (anticoagulation and CT findings) suggesting leaflet thrombosis in this report.

Take‑home pearls
- Immediate high gradients after TAV‑in‑TAV commonly reflect mechanical underexpansion or PPM; thrombosis is an important but usually subacute cause that should be sought with MSCT.
- Differentiate causes with careful Doppler assessment (including SVi and DVI), fluoroscopy/TEE, and gated CT; management options (observe, anticoagulate, balloon post‑dilate, or re‑intervene) depend on the diagnosis, patient stability and anatomical risks.
- In self‑expanding valves constrained by prior frames, a period of observation is often justified because passive expansion can markedly improve gradients, as demonstrated by this patient.
