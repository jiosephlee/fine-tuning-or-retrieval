Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Morning Report] Immediately after the third TAVI, TTE showed mean gradient 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm² (indexed 0.4 cm²/m²) and DVI 0.24 with no regurgitation—yet by 1 month the mean gradient fell to 27 mm Hg and AVA rose to 1.1 cm². How would you interpret the initial haemodynamics (including the likelihood of severe patient–prosthesis mismatch), outline the differential for high early gradients after TAVI-in-TAV-in-TAV, and explain the subsequent improvement?

The immediate post‑implant TTE looked alarming and, taken at face value, met criteria for severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (mean gradient 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm2, DVI 0.24). But in the context of a third transcatheter valve implanted into two prior frames that were already constrained, those numbers are best interpreted as “functional” or “dynamic” obstruction rather than proof of an irreversibly undersized valve. In other words, the early haemodynamics reflected an acute reduction in effective orifice produced by the interaction of three frames and leaflets at the time of implantation—not necessarily a fixed true PPM that will persist long‑term.

When gradients are unexpectedly high immediately after complex TAV‑in‑TAV procedures the differential is dominated by mechanical and measurement issues. The most likely causes are under‑expansion of the newly implanted nitinol frame (temporary incomplete radial expansion), inward displacement or pinning of native/prosthetic leaflets between frames (reduced leaflet excursion), suboptimal implantation depth or frame malapposition, and incomplete effective orifice because the previously implanted Evolut frame remained constricted. Less likely but important considerations are acute valve thrombosis (rare immediately post‑implant), prosthetic leaflet immobility from entrapment in residual material, and Doppler/pressure‑recovery or technical measurement artifact (high heart rate, non‑steady flows, transducer alignment). In this case leaflet motion and positioning were reported as mobile and there was no regurgitation, arguing against leaflet destruction or major malposition but not excluding transient mechanical restriction.

The fall in gradient by one month is readily explained by known mechanical behavior of self‑expanding frames and the effect of aggressive post‑dilatation performed at the index procedure. Self‑expanding nitinol stents often continue to expand over hours to days; balloon post‑dilatation can partially fracture or radially expand an inner constrained frame (here the Evolut) and relieve circumferential compression. That progressive expansion restores leaflet coaptation geometry and enlarges the effective orifice. Concurrently, any trapped leaflets can recoil to a more mobile configuration as the scaffolding settles, and haemodynamics normalize as heart rate and loading conditions stabilize after the procedure. Minor Doppler measurement variability also contributes. Taken together these mechanisms explain why a gradient that appeared consistent with “severe PPM” immediately after TAVI resolved into a much more acceptable gradient (mean 27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm2) by one month.

The clinical teaching point is twofold: first, in multi‑frame TAV‑in‑TAV procedures an elevated immediate post‑implant gradient commonly reflects dynamic mechanical factors and does not always equal fixed severe PPM; second, management should combine careful intra‑procedural assessment (fluoroscopy/echocardiography), considered use of post‑dilatation to optimise expansion, and short‑interval surveillance rather than reflexive reintervention. If gradients remain high after a period of observation, then true structural PPM, leaflet thrombosis or malposition should be investigated and treated accordingly.
