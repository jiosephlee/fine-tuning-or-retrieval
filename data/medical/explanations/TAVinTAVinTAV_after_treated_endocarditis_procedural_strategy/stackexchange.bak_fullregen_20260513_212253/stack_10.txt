Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Management] In a high bleeding risk TAVI patient without another indication for oral anticoagulation, what is the preferred postprocedural antithrombotic regimen, and under what circumstances would you escalate evaluation and therapy for suspected leaflet thrombosis (e.g., 4D-CT and short-term anticoagulation)?

In a patient like this — high bleeding risk after TAVI and no other indication for long‑term oral anticoagulation — the default post‑procedural strategy is single antiplatelet therapy rather than dual therapy or routine anticoagulation. Randomised and guideline data show that dual antiplatelet therapy after TAVI increases bleeding without clear ischemic benefit; for most patients without an indication for anticoagulation, one antiplatelet agent (typically low‑dose aspirin) started immediately and continued long‑term is preferred. In a patient with very high bleeding risk, limited duration (for example indefinite low‑dose aspirin with careful attention to bleeding mitigation) or even individualized de‑escalation after the immediate periprocedural window is reasonable, but routine post‑TAVI anticoagulation is not recommended solely to prevent subclinical leaflet thrombosis.

When to escalate evaluation and treat for suspected leaflet thrombosis
Assess first with transthoracic echocardiography and the clinical picture. Escalation to advanced imaging (4D‑CT) and to a time‑limited anticoagulant trial is indicated when there is objective evidence of impaired valve function or clinical concern rather than isolated, incidental findings:

- New or unexplained rise in transvalvular gradient on serial TTE (for example a sustained increase in mean gradient of ~10 mm Hg or more from a recent baseline, or an absolute mean gradient that becomes clinically significant or accompanied by a drop in DVI/AVA), new heart‑failure symptoms out of proportion to other causes, or thromboembolic events should prompt further evaluation.
- If TTE raises concern for restricted leaflet motion or thrombus, obtain 4D‑CT (gated multidetector CT with cine review) to look for hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RLM), which are the imaging hallmarks of leaflet thrombosis.
- If 4D‑CT confirms HALT/RLM and the lesion is haemodynamically or clinically meaningful (elevated gradients, symptoms, or embolic phenomena), a trial of short‑term oral anticoagulation is appropriate. In practice this is usually a direct oral anticoagulant or vitamin K antagonist for about 3 months with clinical and imaging reassessment; most thrombotic HALT resolves with anticoagulation and gradients improve.
- If HALT is incidentally detected on CT but gradients are normal and the patient is asymptomatic, the approach can be conservative: observe and repeat imaging (TTE ± CT) rather than automatically anticoagulating, especially in patients at high bleeding risk.

Apply individualized risk‑benefit reasoning in every case. Prior GI bleeding or other major bleeding risks do not prohibit investigation, but they raise the threshold for therapeutic anticoagulation; in those patients reserve anticoagulation for confirmed, clinically meaningful thrombosis and use the shortest effective course with close follow‑up and multidisciplinary input (cardiology, imaging, and if needed hematology/gastroenterology). Reassess after treatment with TTE and, if necessary, repeat CT to confirm resolution before stopping anticoagulation.
