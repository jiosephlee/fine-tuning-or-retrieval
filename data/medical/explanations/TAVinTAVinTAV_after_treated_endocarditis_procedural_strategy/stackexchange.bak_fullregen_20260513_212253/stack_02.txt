Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Clinical Pearls] Before considering a redo TAVI after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, what clinical, microbiological and imaging criteria should be met to reasonably exclude active infection and proceed safely?

When you consider a repeat transcatheter valve implantation after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, the decision must be driven first and foremost by confident exclusion of ongoing infection. Implanting additional prosthetic material into an infected field risks persistent seeding, recurrent endocarditis and catastrophic prosthesis failure; conversely, delaying a needed intervention in a high‑risk patient carries its own harms. Practically, a reasonable threshold to proceed is met when clinical, microbiological and imaging data together make active infection unlikely — and this determination should be documented and endorsed by infectious diseases, imaging and heart‑team specialists.

Clinical criteria
The patient should be clinically well from an infectious standpoint: afebrile for a sustained period, without new constitutional symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss) or localizing infectious findings, and with improvement or stability of heart‑failure status that is attributable to structural valve dysfunction rather than ongoing sepsis. Any extracardiac infectious source (eg, urologic, dental, gastrointestinal) must be identified and treated or shown not to be active. The overall clinical course should be consistent with resolution rather than smouldering disease.

Microbiological criteria
Documented completion of an appropriate, guideline‑directed antimicrobial course for the identified pathogen is essential (eg, full 6 weeks for Enterococcus prosthetic valve endocarditis unless alternative duration is specified by ID). Blood cultures must be repeatedly negative: multiple sets (at least three sets) demonstrating no bacteremia after therapy, and ideally negative cultures both while on therapy and after completion. Many teams require a period free of bacteremia off intravenous antibiotics (commonly 7–14 days) before elective reintervention to reduce false reassurance from culture suppression by ongoing antibiotics. In addition, inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) should show a downward trend or normalization consistent with clinical resolution. If long‑term suppressive therapy was considered, the team must weigh risks: absence of need for indefinite suppression supports proceeding.

Imaging criteria
Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) should show no residual vegetations, no new mobile masses, and no paravalvular complications (abscess, pseudoaneurysm, fistula) or progressive dehiscence. High‑resolution gated CT (MSCT) should demonstrate absence of perivalvular collections, pseudoaneurysm or tissue disruption and be used to plan anatomy for the new valve. When available and interpretation will change management, nuclear imaging (FDG‑PET/CT) can be helpful: lack of focal abnormal FDG uptake around the prosthesis supports quiescence of infection (bearing in mind false positives early after implant and false negatives with low‑grade infections). All imaging should be recent (eg, within days to a few weeks of the planned procedure) and correlated with clinical status.

Process and team judgment
Clear documentation of infectious‑disease clearance that states the infection is considered cured and names the evidence supporting that conclusion is required. The heart team should confirm that the urgency of intervention balances the residual risk of occult infection. If there is any imaging or microbiologic uncertainty, delay for further evaluation or consider surgical options if infection control is incomplete. Finally, plan peri‑procedural antimicrobial prophylaxis and close post‑procedure surveillance (clinical follow‑up, serial blood cultures if clinically indicated, early imaging) because recurrence risk, although low when criteria are met, carries high morbidity.

Applying these principles to the presented case: the team had a completed guideline‑directed 6‑week course for Enterococcus, subsequent negative blood cultures, TEE showing no vegetations or paravalvular complication, MSCT without perivalvular collections, no suppressive antibiotics, and ID sign‑off — a reasonable bundle of evidence to conclude absence of active PVE and proceed with a carefully planned TAVI.
