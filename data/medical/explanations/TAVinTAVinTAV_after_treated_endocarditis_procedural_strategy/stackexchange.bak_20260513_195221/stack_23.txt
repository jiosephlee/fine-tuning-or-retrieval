Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Morning Report] List the key CT measurements that inform valve sizing and feasibility when the prior transcatheter valve’s internal lumen measures approximately 14 mm, and explain how these data guide selection of the next valve size and type.

The key CT measurements are those that tell you (1) what lumen you actually have to work with, (2) how a new valve will interact with the prior frames and the coronaries, and (3) whether you can reasonably expect an acceptable effective orifice area after implantation. In a scenario where the prior valve’s internal lumen measures ≈14 mm, the CT-derived parameters that drive valve choice and procedural strategy are the following.

First, measure the true internal lumen at the intended landing zone: the minimum (narrowest) inner diameter and the corresponding cross‑sectional area of the existing prosthetic frame and leaflets on thin‑slice, contrast‑enhanced MSCT. This determines the maximal external diameter of any new device that can expand into the stack and therefore predicts residual effective orifice area and the likelihood of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch. An internal diameter around 14 mm is extremely small for most transcatheter valves and predicts high gradients unless you choose a valve with supra‑annular leaflets and optimize expansion.

Second, perform virtual valve implantation (CT simulation) to derive the valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance and the predicted neo‑skirt relation to the coronary ostia. A VTC <4 mm (and coronary heights <10–12 mm or small sinus of Valsalva diameters) identifies high risk for coronary obstruction and mandates protection strategies (wires/stents, BASILICA planning) or changing the plan. In this case the team used CT modelling to confirm adequate clearance and proceeded without coronary protection.

Third, measure the sinus of Valsalva and sinotubular junction diameters and heights, and the annular geometry below the existing frame. These measurements determine whether there is room for a supra‑annular valve frame and whether high implantation (to pin prior leaflets outwards) will leave a safe path for coronary flow. Small sinuses or low sinotubular junctions increase obstruction risk and limit the ability to implant high.

Fourth, quantify the relation of prior valve frames to the coronaries and to each other: frame heights, leaflet heights, leaflet apposition against the stent, and the distance from the inner frame surface to the coronary ostia. This helps predict how much of the new valve will protrude above/below the prior leaflets and whether commissural alignment is feasible. It also informs whether post‑dilation or attempt at frame expansion (or fracture) is possible or likely to help.

Fifth, evaluate access, tortuosity and the aortic root angulation to judge deliverability of a specific valve type and whether controlled high implantation and precise commissural alignment are achievable.

How these data guide valve size and type selection in a 14 mm internal lumen:

- Realize that a nominal label size cannot be used without considering the 14 mm constraint; most balloon‑expandable valves or large frames will not fully expand or will create a very small EOA. Therefore select a valve design that maximizes valve area for a small internal diameter: a supra‑annular leaflet prosthesis with low frame height and good hemodynamics in small annuli (the Acurate neo2 S in this case) is preferable because supra‑annular leaflets place the orifice above the constrained frame and generally deliver larger effective orifice areas for the same inner diameter.

- Favor self‑expanding valves with some conformability and lower profile if you anticipate partial mechanical expansion of the existing nitinol frame will improve function. Self‑expanding devices can also be implanted higher to pin the old leaflets outwards, improving effective orifice area and preserving coronary flow.

- Use the CT‑derived VTC and sinus measurements to decide implantation depth and whether to attempt commissural alignment. If VTC is marginal (<4 mm) or sinuses are small, plan for coronary protection or consider alternative strategies (surgical redo if feasible, BASILICA, or different valve orientation).

- Decide on planned post‑dilation based on predicted residual stenosis and the mechanical properties of the existing frames. CT showing a circular lumen with some room may support aggressive post‑dilation to improve expansion; if prior frames are not fractureable (eg, nitinol Evolut), valve‑in‑valve fracture is not an option, and one must accept higher gradients or choose a valve with supra‑annular leaflets.

- Anticipate prosthesis–patient mismatch: CT predicts that with an internal diameter ≈14 mm you will likely have a small EOA and high gradients unless you use a supra‑annular valve, implant high to avoid leaflet infolding, and optimize expansion. Communicate this risk with the patient and team.

In short, CT gives you the working lumen (≈14 mm), the spatial relationship to coronaries (VTC), and the envelope of expansion available inside stacked frames. Those measurements lead you to choose a small, supra‑annular, low‑profile valve that can be implanted high with commissural alignment and aggressive but safe post‑dilation to maximize EOA while confirming coronary safety. The Acurate neo2 S chosen in this report exemplifies that reasoning: its supra‑annular design and deliverability made it the best option in a constrained 14 mm internal lumen after multidisciplinary CT planning.
