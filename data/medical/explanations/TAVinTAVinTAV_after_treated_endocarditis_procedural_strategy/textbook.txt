Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

## Case Context and Pathophysiologic Foundations

This patient’s course illustrates a predictable but clinically demanding trajectory after transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI): initial treatment of native severe aortic stenosis with a transcatheter prosthesis, later structural deterioration of that prosthesis, infection of the prosthesis with secondary leaflet injury, and ultimately progressive haemodynamic failure that required repeat transcatheter interventions. The chronological landmarks are important for understanding causation and decision-making: a 27 mm Lotus valve was implanted in 2014 for native severe aortic stenosis; progressive bioprosthetic stenosis led to a TAV‑in‑TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve in May 2022; Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) occurred in September 2022 and was treated with a complete antibiotic course with clinical and imaging resolution; despite treated infection, by January–June 2024 the bioprosthesis demonstrated severe restenosis and mixed aortic dysfunction (mean gradient ~49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm2), prompting a third transcatheter implantation (Acurate neo2 S) in June 2024. At 1 year the patient remained asymptomatic with improved gradients (mean ~26 mm Hg) and preserved left ventricular function.

The choice to pursue a third transcatheter procedure was driven as much by the patient’s comorbidity burden and operative risk as by anatomy. He had multivessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior percutaneous interventions, a permanent pacemaker for complete heart block, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, and a history of a major vascular complication after the second procedure. These factors, together with a calculated EuroSCORE II of approximately 27%, rendered surgical explantation and surgical root replacement prohibitively high risk—pushing the team to favour a catheter-based, CT‑guided reintervention despite its technical complexities.

Understanding why the prosthesis failed and why reintervention is technically challenging requires separating mechanism from consequence. Structural valve deterioration (SVD) of bioprosthetic leaflets is a time‑dependent process that commonly involves leaflet fibrosis, tissue degeneration and calcification leading to stiffening and restricted opening—producing progressive transvalvular gradients and reduced effective orifice area. Superimposed prosthetic valve endocarditis can further accelerate dysfunction by causing leaflet destruction, scarring and fixation, or by promoting thrombus or pannus formation; the Enterococcus faecalis infection in this case likely contributed to additional leaflet trauma and mechanical restriction even after microbiological cure. The net haemodynamic consequence in 2024 was severe stenosis (very low AVA) with some regurgitant component, reflecting both restricted leaflet motion and incomplete coaptation.

Serial layering of transcatheter frames creates a second set of pathophysiologic and anatomic problems distinct from native or single‑prosthesis failure. First, each added stent frame reduces the available lumen and can create a “neo‑skirt” or internal leaflet fold that narrows the flow channel, producing a progressively smaller effective orifice area and an increasing risk of prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM). This is particularly relevant when the minimum internal diameter measured on MSCT is small (in this patient the Evolut frame lumen measured ~13.7 mm), because a new valve must function within that constrained cylinder. Second, displacement of previously implanted leaflets and their stent material can obstruct coronary ostia or bring leaflet tissue into close proximity with the coronary orifices; the risk of coronary obstruction rises with overlapping leaflets and low valve‑to‑coronary distances. Third, multiple frames complicate commissural alignment: implanted valves with misaligned commissures can create mesh or strut coverage over coronary takeoffs and make future coronary access or percutaneous interventions difficult or impossible. Fourth, interaction between frames may lead to incomplete expansion or eccentric opening of the newly implanted valve; stiff or rigid prior frames (or dense leaflet fibrosis) can limit radial expansion, maintain high residual gradients, and sometimes require high‑pressure post‑dilatation to achieve adequate orifice area. In this case the procedural team used preprocedural MSCT to evaluate valve‑to‑coronary distances and frame interactions, planned a relatively high implantation to pin prior leaflets outward and minimise a neo‑skirt beneath the coronaries, and performed post‑dilatation—which likely afforded partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame and contributed to the improved gradients seen at 1 month.

These pathophysiologic considerations explain key intra‑ and post‑procedural findings in the report. The immediate post‑implant mean gradient remained high (67 mm Hg) because residual narrowing and leaflet restriction can persist until the new valve and underlying frames reach a more favourable geometry after post‑dilatation and tissue remodeling; gradients improved substantially over the first month as the valve expanded and flow dynamics optimized. The team’s focus on commissural alignment, coronary protection planning, and CT‑based assessment of frame geometry directly targeted the principal hazards of serial TAV‑in‑TAV interventions: PPM, coronary obstruction, difficult future coronary access, and underexpansion.

In summary, this patient’s need for a third transcatheter valve resulted from a combination of time‑related structural degeneration, superimposed infection‑related leaflet injury, and progressive haemodynamic compromise within an increasingly constrained multi‑frame environment. His high operative risk and comorbid state made repeat transcatheter therapy the preferred option, but the already complex anatomy demanded meticulous imaging, individualized valve selection, a high implantation strategy, and readiness to address frame interaction and residual gradients—principles that were applied in the case and underpin lifetime planning for patients who undergo TAVI.

## Diagnostic Evaluation and CT-Guided Planning

When this patient re-presented in 2024 with exertional dyspnoea, dizziness and fatigue, the diagnostic work-up needed to answer three linked questions: what was causing the prosthetic valve dysfunction, was there active infection that would contraindicate a transcatheter reintervention, and was a third TAVI technically feasible and safe with respect to coronary flow and future coronary access. The team used a stepwise, multimodality approach centred on transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography, repeated microbiology, and gated multislice CT (MSCT) to integrate clinical, haemodynamic and anatomic data and to guide anatomic modelling and procedural strategy.

Echocardiography established the clinical severity of prosthetic dysfunction. Transthoracic echo (TTE) documented a mean transvalvular gradient of ~49 mmHg, an aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm² calculated by the continuity equation, and moderate-to-severe intraprosthetic regurgitation, with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (66%). In prosthetic valves the continuity equation remains the standard for AVA estimation: it combines the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) area and the velocity–time integrals (VTIs) of LVOT and across the valve to calculate effective orifice. Complementary Doppler indices such as the Doppler velocity index (DVI = VTI_LVOT / VTI_AorticJet) are useful when LVOT measurement is uncertain; low DVI values (typically ≤0.25–0.35, depending on context) support severe obstruction and are less sensitive to LVOT diameter measurement error. In this case the very low AVA plus high mean gradient and clinical symptoms confirmed severe prosthetic dysfunction requiring intervention.

Interpreting these echocardiographic findings in a patient with multiple stented valves requires attention to prosthesis-specific and reintervention-related confounders. Overlapping frames reduce the effective internal diameter and commonly produce elevated velocities and gradients even when leaflet motion is adequate; this predisposes to prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM). Conversely, restricted leaflet motion from thrombus or residual infection can present similarly. The differential diagnosis therefore included (1) structural degeneration with leaflet fibrosis and retraction from prior wear and possible infection-related injury, (2) pannus or thrombus causing leaflet immobility, (3) recurrent prosthetic valve endocarditis with vegetations or abscess, and (4) malposition/inadequate expansion or PPM from the small internal lumen created by stacked frames. The clinical history—recent treated Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis with completed targeted antimicrobial therapy, absence of fever at reassessment, and improvement of EF after the prior TAV-in-TAV—favoured structural degeneration and mechanical restriction rather than active infection as the main driver of the current stenosis. Nevertheless, excluding persistent or recurrent infection was mandatory before reintervention.

Infection clearance was documented by a combination of microbiology and imaging. The heart team required repeated blood cultures that remained negative, a transoesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) that showed no vegetations or paravalvular complications, and an MSCT without evidence of abscess or perivalvular extension. The patient had completed the full, targeted intravenous antibiotic course for Enterococcus faecalis and had no ongoing clinical or laboratory evidence of infection; no suppressive antibiotics were being administered at the time of planning. This constellation met contemporary clinical standards for declaring resolved PVE and supported proceeding with planned valve intervention, provided anatomical feasibility and surgical risk assessment also favoured a transcatheter approach.

MSCT was the critical imaging modality for procedural feasibility and coronary safety in this triple-stented anatomy. Beyond routine annular sizing, CT allowed direct visualisation of the internal lumen of the existing transcatheter prostheses and quantification of the smallest luminal diameter—measured here as 13.7 mm within the in-situ Evolut frame—confirming marked haemodynamic compromise and informing valve selection and expected residual effective orifice area. CT also provided precise measurements of coronary ostial heights, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and the valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance: a key metric that estimates the clearance between an implanted leaflet or anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia. Although absolute risk cut-offs vary by device and anatomy, small VTC values (often cited in the literature around <4 mm) and low coronary heights increase the risk of coronary obstruction after valve-in-valve procedures. In this patient’s case, CT modelling showed sufficient distances and sinus geometry to make coronary obstruction unlikely with a carefully planned, higher implantation and an appropriately selected valve type, permitting the team to proceed without prophylactic coronary protection.

CT also enabled assessment of frame–frame interactions and a virtual appreciation of the anticipated neo-skirt created when a new valve displaces the existing leaflets. In valve-in-valve scenarios the pre-existing leaflets can be pushed outward to form a continuous “cylinder” that can cover coronary ostia or reduce the effective orifice; CT-based simulation helps predict the height and radial position of that neo-skirt relative to coronary ostia. For this patient, MSCT modelling indicated that a supra-annular, low‑frame-height valve implanted relatively high (aligned with the Evolut leaflets) would (1) lift and fix the prior leaflets against the frame to minimise inward leaflet folding and preserve the central orifice, (2) keep the neo-skirt below or clear of the coronary ostia, and (3) offer improved haemodynamics in the constrained lumen. This reasoning underpinned selection of the Acurate neo2 device and a high implantation strategy aimed at maximising effective orifice area while preserving coronary perfusion and future coronary access.

Commissural alignment was explicitly planned using CT-derived anatomic landmarks. Deliberate rotational positioning at deployment seeks to align the neo-commissures of the new valve with those of the prior frame to reduce overlap of commissural posts with coronary ostia and to keep cell geometry favourable for future coronary catheterisation. In layered valve constructs, imperfect alignment increases the cumulative mesh burden and can make future coronary access difficult; the preprocedural CT review and fluoroscopic planning in this case aimed to minimise that risk. The team also planned to use balloon pre-dilatation under pacing as an intra-procedural test of coronary patency—if balloon inflation had produced impaired coronary flow, the strategy would have been revisited—providing an additional safety check beyond static CT modelling. In fact, intra-procedural aortography during pre-dilation confirmed preserved coronary flow, validating the CT-based plan.

Finally, the decision to pursue transcatheter reintervention rather than surgical explantation was made in a multidisciplinary heart-team context. The patient’s EuroSCORE II and comorbid burden (COPD, diabetes, recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding), prior surgical history including deep integration of two prior frames into the aortic root, and the expectation that surgical explantation would likely require complex root and ascending aorta replacement rendered open surgery prohibitively high risk. These surgical realities, combined with MSCT-confirmed technical feasibility of a carefully planned TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, supported an individualized strategy favouring a CT-guided transcatheter approach. This planning process illustrates how rigorous imaging, microbiologic clearance and multidisciplinary risk assessment can identify patients for whom complex repeat transcatheter therapy is a reasonable, safe alternative to surgery.

## Third TAVI Strategy and Execution (Acurate neo2 in a Double-Framed Anatomy)

The procedural plan for this patient was shaped by three dominant constraints: a double-layered transcatheter frame (Lotus → Evolut), treated prosthetic valve endocarditis with infection resolved, and prohibitive surgical risk. Multislice CT (minimum lumen diameter inside the Evolut 13.7 mm) and transoesophageal echocardiography confirmed anatomy and absence of active infection; these data directed a tailored, CT-guided transcatheter strategy focused on preserving coronary flow, maximising effective orifice area (EOA) and minimising future coronary-access problems.

Access and setup were deliberately simple and pragmatic. The team used local anaesthesia with percutaneous left femoral arterial access for device delivery and left radial access for contrast angiography. A pigtail catheter in the aortic root provided initial aortography that confirmed the severe intraprosthetic regurgitation and served as the angiographic roadmap during key manoeuvres. This configuration minimised anaesthetic risk in an elderly, multimorbid patient while preserving high-quality fluoroscopic and angiographic imaging for intra‑procedural decision-making.

Hemodynamic support and wire strategy were standard for high-risk TAVI but tuned to the triple-stented anatomy. A Safari guidewire was positioned in the left ventricle to stabilise the delivery system and transmit forces for accurate positioning. Rapid ventricular pacing (~180 bpm) was used for balloon predilation and again for controlled deployment and post-dilation: it reduces cardiac output and valve motion, thereby lowering the risk of device embolisation and facilitating precise valve expansion inside the existing frames.

Predilation served two purposes—testing the resistance of the existing frames and dynamically assessing coronary patency. A 22/40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid pacing while simultaneous aortography visualised coronary flow. This step confirmed that ballooning and subsequent valve expansion could be performed without immediate coronary compromise, which supported the decision not to deploy prophylactic coronary protection. Predilation also helps to partially expand prior self‑expanding frames and can improve leaflet mobility of the degenerated valve before implanting the new prosthesis.

Device selection centred on the self‑expanding Acurate neo2 (size S) for several anatomy-driven reasons. Its supra‑annular leaflet position tends to provide superior haemodynamics in small internal diameters, an important consideration given the 13–14 mm lumen within the Evolut. The neo2 has a relatively low external frame height and design features that facilitate commissural alignment—key attributes when multiple frames are present and future coronary access must be preserved. In this patient, CT modelling indicated that the neo2 would achieve adequate clearance of the coronary ostia while offering improved effective orifice area compared with other available options.

The implantation technique emphasised a high implant with deliberate leaflet “tacking.” By aligning the new valve with the Evolut leaflets, the team intended to pin the prior leaflets outward against their stent frame, preventing inward folding that would otherwise create a restrictive neo‑skirt and further reduce the EOA. High implantation also shifts any new skirt or displaced leaflet tissue supra‑annularly, minimising the potential for obstructing coronary ostia and improving prospects for future coronary engagement. Preprocedural CT provided orientation cues for commissural alignment, and fluoroscopic landmarks identified the intended rotational and vertical implant position.

Postdilation was performed after deployment with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing to optimise expansion and leaflet motion. Although no intentional bioprosthetic valve fracture was performed, aggressive postdilation can impart partial radial expansion to the previously implanted self‑expanding Evolut frame, improving the effective orifice and lowering gradients, as was likely in this case. Postdilation was balanced against the risk of annular or aortic injury; balloon size and pressure were chosen conservatively within the constraints defined by preprocedural imaging.

Commissural alignment was an explicit objective. CT-based 3D assessment provided the expected orientation of existing commissures and coronary ostia; during deployment, the operators used fluoroscopic markers and the known radiopaque alignment features of the neo2 to orient the valve so that its commissures would avoid occluding coronary trajectories. Good alignment reduces the mesh burden across coronary ostia and eases future coronary access—critical in a patient with extensive coronary disease.

Risk mitigation and bailouts were planned in advance. Although CT and intraprocedural angiography suggested coronary protection was not required, the team remained prepared for emergent measures: immediate coronary wiring and stenting (chimney technique), retrieval/manipulation tools, and rapid conversion strategies. BASILICA (leaflet laceration) and intentional bioprosthetic fracture were considered as theoretical options but not performed because CT modelling and the successful predilation/aortography indicated acceptable valve-to-coronary relationships and the likelihood that a high implant with postdilation would achieve satisfactory haemodynamics. Other anticipated complications—access-site vascular injury (notably relevant because the patient had a prior femoral pseudoaneurysm), conduction disturbances (patient already had a permanent pacemaker), annular/aortic injury, embolic stroke—were mitigated by careful vascular access technique, neuroprotection considerations, continuous haemodynamic monitoring and having vascular‑surgical and perfusion back-up available.

In summary, the third TAVI in this double‑framed anatomy combined CT-informed device selection (Acurate neo2 size S), a high implantation and leaflet‑tacking strategy, measured predilation with angiographic coronary assessment, and cautious postdilation under pacing. These steps—performed with readiness for bailouts but without prophylactic coronary protection—balanced the competing goals of maximising EOA, preserving coronary flow and minimising procedural risk in a patient for whom open surgery was not a viable option. The subsequent haemodynamic improvement and 1‑year clinical stability indicate that this targeted, image‑driven approach was effective in this case.

## Outcomes, Hemodynamics, and Interpretation

The immediate procedural result in this patient met contemporary criteria for procedural success. The valve was implanted in the intended position, there was no residual paravalvular leak or transvalvular aortic regurgitation, coronary flow was preserved on angiography, and no major periprocedural complications were reported. Using Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC‑3) concepts, these findings constitute a technically successful TAVI: appropriate device position, absence of significant regurgitation and no procedural adverse events that would negate the primary device objective.

Despite this technical success, the early haemodynamic picture was striking: an immediate post‑procedure mean gradient of approximately 67 mmHg, an aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.9 cm2 and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24. These values fall into the range of severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM). In clinical practice an indexed AVA and DVI are used to quantify PPM severity; a DVI ≲0.25 and a low indexed AVA are consistent with severe mismatch and explain the high transvalvular velocities observed immediately after implantation.

Several mechanical and physiological mechanisms can account for markedly elevated early gradients in the setting of multiple layered transcatheter frames. First, transient underexpansion of the newly deployed valve (and residual underexpansion of previously implanted frames) may restrict effective orifice area until the stent frames undergo further radial expansion. Second, the stacked frames behave as a composite structure with altered compliance—multiple layers increase resistance to full expansion and can inwardly displace leaflets from prior prostheses, reducing the instantaneous effective lumen. Third, leaflet motion can be mechanically restricted in the acute phase by contact with inner frames or by incomplete apposition; with time and with post‑dilatation the leaflet opening area may improve. Finally, immediate postoperative haemodynamics (heart rate, blood pressure, LV filling) may transiently affect measured gradients; as the ventricle remodels and flow dynamics normalize, gradients frequently fall.

The clinical course in this case illustrates that improvement can be rapid once the mechanical factors are optimised and physiological remodeling occurs. By 1 month the mean gradient fell to about 27 mmHg and AVA increased to ~1.1 cm2, with sustained values (mean gradient ~26 mmHg, AVA 1.1 cm2) at 1 year. This trajectory suggests that the early very high gradient was largely due to reversible mechanical factors—further radial expansion of the combined frames (facilitated by post‑dilatation), improved leaflet excursion and normalization of flow—rather than immediate definitive failure of the prosthetic function. The absence of residual regurgitation, sustained left ventricular reverse remodeling and improvement in symptoms further support a favourable functional outcome. In the specific context of a triple‑stented aortic root, the 1‑year haemodynamic profile represents an acceptable result—clinically meaningful relief of obstruction with stable valve function—recognizing that an entirely normal transvalvular gradient may not be achievable.

Durability through 1 year was favourable in this patient: he remained asymptomatic, had a preserved and improved ejection fraction (~65%), no echocardiographic evidence of regurgitation and no clinical or microbiologic evidence of recurrent prosthetic valve endocarditis. In a high‑risk, multiple‑frame anatomy this represents a successful medium‑term outcome emphasizing symptom relief, LV recovery and infection control as key endpoints alongside absolute gradient values.

Follow‑up after complex repeat TAVI should be systematic. Baseline transthoracic echocardiography immediately after the procedure establishes a reference. Early reassessment at 1–3 months evaluates the expected haemodynamic improvement and LV remodeling, with further imaging at 6–12 months and then annually if stable. Clinical surveillance for recurrent heart failure, new murmurs, fever or bacteremia should prompt urgent evaluation. Any unexplained rise in gradient, new regurgitation or clinical deterioration warrants repeat imaging: TTE first, with transesophageal echocardiography and multislice CT employed when structural failure, leaflet thrombosis, pannus, or recurrent/endocarditis are suspected or when CT is needed to plan further intervention. Decisions about reintervention should be based on a combination of symptoms, progressive haemodynamic deterioration and anatomic feasibility rather than a single echocardiographic number, particularly in anatomically constrained, multi‑frame scenarios.

## Clinical Lessons, Decision Pathways, and Lifetime Management

This case illustrates pragmatic, patient-centred decision making when facing a failed transcatheter aortic valve in the setting of prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) and multiple prior TAVI frames. The core teaching point is that a third transcatheter intervention can be feasible and durable when strict prerequisites are met and when CT-guided procedural strategy addresses the unique anatomical and haemodynamic challenges of layered valve frames. Below are the practical lessons and decision pathways distilled from the case.

Candidacy for a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure must be conditional rather than automatic. Before considering reintervention, infection must be controlled and reasonably eradicated: the patient should have completed targeted antimicrobial therapy, persistently negative blood cultures, and no morphological evidence of active endocarditis on transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or CT. In addition, CT-derived measures must demonstrate coronary safety (adequate valve-to-coronary distances and sinus anatomy) and a realistic plan for implantation depth and commissural alignment that preserves coronary flow and allows acceptable effective orifice area (EOA). The patient’s surgical risk and comorbidities must make repeat sternotomy or root replacement prohibitive; in this case a EuroSCORE II of ~27% and prior deep stent integration into the aortic wall supported a catheter-based strategy. Finally, multidisciplinary heart-team consensus and a documented informed-consent discussion with the patient and family about limited future options are essential prerequisites.

Preprocedural imaging drives feasibility and technique. Multislice CT should be used to measure internal valve diameters (true internal lumen of the existing frame), coronary ostial heights, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary (VTC) distances and valve-to-sinotubular junction relationships. The team must model how a new valve will sit relative to prior leaflets and frames and anticipate the neo-skirt that could cover coronary ostia or further reduce the EOA. In this report MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame; that finding directly informed valve selection (a supra‑annular, low‑height device) and the high‑implant strategy. CT planning should also define fluoroscopic cusp‑overlap views and landmarks for commissural alignment and document likely coronary reaccess routes if future PCI is needed.

Device selection and implantation technique should be chosen to minimise additional frame burden and to maximise haemodynamic gain. When anatomy allows, favour prostheses with supra‑annular leaflet position and a frame geometry that limits extra vertical height and mesh density. Aim for the highest safe implantation that will pin the incompetent leaflets of the prior valve outwards rather than create an obstructive neo‑skirt beneath the coronaries; this was the rationale for placing an Acurate neo2 supra‑annular valve in the presented patient. Commissural alignment is important to preserve coronary access; plan alignment using CT landmarks and fluoroscopy. Coronary protection or BASILICA should be reserved for borderline VTC or unfavourable sinus anatomy; in this case CT modelling demonstrated adequate clearance so the team proceeded without routine coronary protection. Consider the option of bioprosthetic valve fracture to improve expansion where a rigid frame limits EOA, but weigh this against risks in a multi‑frame setting: fracture may be less predictable and could damage surrounding structures when several stents are layered, so it should not be routine. Balloon post‑dilatation can produce partial mechanical expansion of previous frames and can meaningfully improve gradients, as likely occurred here after post‑dilatation reduced the patient’s transvalvular gradient from an immediate post‑implant mean of 67 mm Hg to mid‑term values in the mid‑20s.

Management after treated PVE requires close infectious-disease collaboration. Before reintervention there must be documented completion of an appropriate antibiotic regimen (the patient received 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin for Enterococcus faecalis), persistently negative cultures, and no echocardiographic or CT signs of active infection or paravalvular complications. Routine long‑term suppressive antibiotics are not mandated if infection is eradicated; decisions about suppressive therapy should be individualized and supervised by infectious disease specialists. After implantation, maintain standard endocarditis prevention counselling and surveillance; any new febrile illness or unexplained symptoms should prompt urgent evaluation.

Plan the patient’s valve‑lifetime trajectory and document details that will matter for future teams. Each new frame increases mesh burden and may compromise coronary reaccess and future interventions. Record commissural orientation, fluoroscopic implantation depth, device type and CT images in the chart and imaging archive so that subsequent operators can make informed decisions. Inform patients and families up front that options become progressively limited with each reintervention and that, although a transcatheter approach may be feasible now, surgical explant later will be technically challenging and high risk.

Finally, recognise the evidence gaps and need for systematic data collection. Outcomes after TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV—especially in patients with prior PVE—are reported only in small series and case reports. Important unanswered questions include the durability of third‑valve implants, the role of specific CT‑driven modelling protocols and the safety of adjunctive maneuvers such as valve fracture or leaflet laceration in triple‑stented anatomy. Where possible, cases like the one presented should be entered into registries and multidisciplinary databases to refine patient selection, procedural algorithms and long‑term surveillance strategies.

In summary, a third transcatheter valve can be a reasonable, effective option in carefully selected patients with treated PVE when infection is resolved, CT anatomy allows coronary safety and acceptable residual EOA, surgical risk is prohibitive, and a clear, image‑guided implantation strategy is agreed by a heart team. Meticulous imaging, device choice prioritising supra‑annular performance and commissural alignment, and transparent lifetime planning are the pillars that make such complex reinterventions feasible and safe.
