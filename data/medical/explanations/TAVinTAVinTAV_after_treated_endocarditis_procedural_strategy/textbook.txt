## Clinical Presentation and Initial Approach in Failed TAVI with Prior Endocarditis

Patients with a history of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) who present with new or worsening symptoms require a systematic, case-driven evaluation because multiple pathologies can produce similar clinical pictures. In the presented case the late‑70s man reported progressive exertional dyspnoea, a peculiar chest “spasm” on exertion, dizziness and reduced exercise tolerance. These are classic triggers for re‑evaluation after TAVI: any new dyspnoea, a decline in functional capacity, angina or angina equivalents, presyncope or syncope, new or worsening heart failure signs, unexplained fever, or hemolysis should prompt urgent reassessment. The initial clinical approach focuses on defining the dominant syndrome (predominantly stenosis, regurgitation or heart failure), identifying red flags for active infection, and rapidly assembling the multidisciplinary team that will guide further testing and therapeutic choices.

History and physical examination provide the first discrimination among likely causes. Important elements include the timing and trajectory of symptoms relative to the index valve implantation(s), prior complications (vascular injury, pacemaker), any systemic symptoms suggestive of infection (fever, night sweats, weight loss), recent procedures or bacteremia risk factors (dental work, indwelling lines), bleeding history, and functional baseline. On exam, new murmurs, signs of congestive failure, peripheral stigmata of endocarditis or embolic phenomena merit particular attention. In this case the combination of progressive exertional symptoms with prior procedural history triggered a focused diagnostic pathway.

Differential diagnosis in a failing transcatheter valve is broad and must be approached deliberately because management differs dramatically between entities. Key diagnoses to consider are: structural valve deterioration (SVD) manifesting as leaflet thickening, fibrosis, calcification or tear leading to stenosis or regurgitation; patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) from an effective orifice area that is small for the patient’s body size (or that has become functionally reduced by additional implanted frames); leaflet thrombosis or subclinical leaflet thrombosis (which may be reversible with anticoagulation); paravalvular leak from incomplete sealing; and prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), which may present with valve dysfunction from vegetations, leaflet destruction or annular/abscess pathology. Hemodynamic contributors such as high cardiac output states or unmasked left ventricular dysfunction should also be considered. Clinical clues, the time course, and simple tests (e.g., sudden deterioration suggests thrombosis or endocarditis; gradual progression points to SVD or PPM) help prioritise further tests, but definitive differentiation depends on imaging and microbiology.

Imaging and microbiology guide both diagnosis and feasibility of intervention. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the first-line test to quantify gradients (mean and peak), valve area (continuity equation), Doppler velocity index, the presence and degree of regurgitation (transvalvular and paravalvular), leaflet mobility and left ventricular response. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is essential when endocarditis is suspected: it is more sensitive for vegetations, prosthesis‑related abscess, and paravalvular complications. Multidetector CT (MSCT) complements echocardiography by characterising leaflet morphology (hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening, leaflet motion abnormalities), prosthesis frame geometry, intra‑stent lumen diameter, valve‑to‑coronary distances and sinus anatomy — information that is pivotal for planning a redo transcatheter intervention and for estimating coronary obstruction risk. In the case presented, MSCT defined a minimal lumen of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame and allowed virtual planning of the landing zone, coronary clearance and commissural alignment strategy. When infection is a concern, serial blood cultures (multiple sets obtained prior to antibiotics, and repeat cultures until clearance) are mandatory; in this case cultures documented Enterococcus faecalis PVE that required a full antibiotic course before any reintervention was contemplated.

Active infective endocarditis is a contraindication to performing TAVI. Management of PVE generally prioritises infection control and anatomic eradication before considering definitive valve therapy. For patients with prior PVE who have completed appropriate antimicrobial therapy, consideration for a redo-TAVI requires confirmation of eradication: sustained clinical improvement, repeated negative blood cultures, and imaging (TEE ± MSCT) without vegetations, leaflet destruction or paravalvular abscess. The timing of reintervention should be individualized and guided by the infectious diseases specialist in conjunction with the heart team; there is no universally accepted “safe interval,” but most teams wait until there is convincing evidence of microbiological and radiological resolution.

Choosing redo‑TAVI versus surgical re‑replacement requires balancing the risks and benefits in a patient‑centred manner. Surgical explantation and root reconstruction remain preferred when there is active infection involving the annulus or when complex debridement is needed, or when coronary anatomy requires root replacement. But in patients with prohibitive surgical risk or when infection has been eradicated and anatomy is favourable, a transcatheter reintervention is a reasonable alternative. Important variables to weigh include global surgical risk scores (EuroSCORE II, STS), the patient’s frailty and functional status, comorbidities (severe COPD, diabetes, gastrointestinal bleeding which may complicate anticoagulation), prior cardiac surgeries or multiple prior TAVIs that have become incorporated into the aortic root, coronary artery disease burden and prior revascularisation status, expected difficulty of surgical explantation (for example deep ingrowth of stent frames or need for root replacement), vascular access feasibility, and patient preferences. In the presented patient the EuroSCORE II was markedly elevated (27.4%), and the combination of COPD, recurrent GI bleeding, prior multiple coronary interventions and deep fixation of two prior transcatheter frames made surgical reoperation exceptionally high risk and likely to demand complex aortic root replacement — factors that favoured a carefully planned transcatheter approach.

A multidisciplinary heart team is central to safe and individualised decision‑making in these cases. The team should include interventional cardiologists experienced in structural procedures, cardiac surgeons familiar with explant and salvage strategies, advanced cardiac imagers (echocardiography and CT specialists) to interpret complex imaging and to perform procedural planning, infectious disease specialists to adjudicate the status of PVE and direct antimicrobial therapy, anesthesiologists and perioperative care specialists, vascular surgeons when access is marginal, electrophysiologists when pacing issues are present, and representatives of geriatrics or palliative care when frailty and goals of care need emphasis. The team discusses the timing of intervention, procedural strategy (valve type, size, implantation depth, need for coronary protection or BASILICA), contingency for surgical bailout, antithrombotic management, and post‑procedure surveillance. In the presented case this collaborative process determined that, after documented infection resolution and multidisciplinary agreement, a CT‑guided TAV‑in‑TAV strategy with emphasis on commissural alignment and preservation of coronary access offered the best balance of risk and benefit.

Preprocedural planning should set a clear baseline status and expectations using standardized outcome definitions. Employing VARC‑3 definitions for device success and complications establishes objective endpoints for intra‑ and postprocedural assessment and provides a benchmark for follow‑up. Baseline documentation should include symptom status and NYHA class, indexed AVA, mean gradient, DVI, presence/degree of regurgitation, ventricular size and function, vascular access mapping, and a record of any conduction system disease or prior pacemaker. Microbiological clearance should be documented by serial negative blood cultures and infectious disease approval. The expected procedural goals (reduction in gradient, elimination of significant regurgitation, preservation of coronary perfusion, avoidance of major complications) should be discussed with the patient as part of shared decision‑making, outlining the realistic possibility of residual PPM, need for future interventions, and the limited options should the new prosthesis fail.

Finally, the initial approach incorporates a practical, stepwise checklist anchored in the multidisciplinary discussion: confirm the clinical trigger and symptom trajectory; obtain TTE and, when indicated, TEE; draw serial blood cultures before antibiotics if infection is suspected; perform MSCT for anatomical mapping; obtain formal risk estimation (EuroSCORE II/STS, frailty assessment); convene the heart team to weigh surgical versus transcatheter options; obtain informed consent that emphasises individualized risks (including the unique considerations after prior PVE and multiple frames); and plan peri‑procedural antibiotic strategy and follow‑up surveillance using VARC‑3 endpoints. The presented patient’s course exemplifies this approach — his fever and positive cultures mandated full infectious workup and therapy prior to any intervention; after documented eradication the heart team used CT‑guided planning and individualised technical strategy to proceed with a third transcatheter valve, accepting the tradeoffs inherent in a high‑risk, repeat intervention.

## Pathophysiology of Transcatheter Prosthetic Valve Failure After TAVI and Treated PVE

Understanding why a transcatheter aortic valve (TAV) fails requires integrating mechanical, biological and haemodynamic processes. Structural valve deterioration (SVD) is the dominant long‑term mechanism and encompasses intrinsic leaflet dysfunction — calcification, fibrosis, leaflet tears or flail — together with stent or frame problems such as fatigue, fracture or persistent underexpansion. These processes are not independent: a small effective orifice area (EOA) creates high transvalvular shear and turbulent flow that accelerate leaflet wear, promote pannus formation and increase the risk of thrombosis. In serial transcatheter procedures the pathobiology of SVD is further compounded by the presence of prior frames that limit expansion and alter the local haemodynamic environment, making progressive stenosis and elevated gradients more likely.

When prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) has occurred and been treated, additional injury patterns can explain valve dysfunction even after microbiologic cure. Infection can directly damage bioprosthetic leaflets (enzymatic degradation, necrosis) or produce vegetations that, when healed, leave fibrosis, retraction or perforation. Healed vegetations and leaflet scarring reduce leaflet mobility and may produce either stenosis (restricted opening) or regurgitation (incomplete coaptation), depending on the distribution of injury. Subvalvular structures and the sewing ring (or frame tissue interface in TAVs) may also become adherent or distorted, so that even when no active infection remains the mechanical behaviour of the valve is altered. The case presented—Enterococcus faecalis PVE followed by progressive restenosis—illustrates how an infectious episode can accelerate or unmask leaflet dysfunction that manifests clinically within months.

Layering transcatheter valves compounds the mechanical constraints. Each additional stent frame occupies space within the annulus and sinuses and can impinge on the leaflet opening area of the new valve. The effective inflow area is therefore the net lumen after accounting for the inner diameter of the most recently implanted device and any residual inward displacement of previously implanted leaflets. This cumulative metal burden and restricted lumen produce patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM), defined as an EOA too small for the patient’s body size, with attendant higher transvalvular gradients and limited cardiac output reserve. In the present patient, MSCT demonstrated a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame prior to the third intervention — a quantitative illustration of how prior implants can constrict the available orifice and predispose to recurrent high gradients.

Leaflet thrombosis — manifesting as clinical thrombosis or subclinical hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) detectable on gated multislice CT — is a distinct and important cause of elevated gradients after TAVI and can mimic or coexist with SVD. HALT often presents with increased transvalvular gradients and reduced leaflet mobility but without obvious structural leaflet damage on echocardiography. It may respond to anticoagulation and can be reversible, distinguishing it from irreversible leaflet fibrosis or calcification. In a patient with sudden or subacute increases in gradient and no visible structural abnormality, CT to evaluate for HALT and consideration of a trial of anticoagulation (when not contraindicated) are reasonable steps before assuming irreversible SVD.

Paravalvular leak (PVL) is classically a consequence of incomplete apposition of the prosthetic frame to the native annulus. In redo‑TAVI, however, prior rigid frames often act as a scaffold that reduces the risk of PVL arising from annular irregularity; paradoxically, that same rigidity constrains full expansion of a newly deployed valve and can therefore exacerbate transvalvular stenosis. In other words, serial implants commonly trade decreased PVL for increased transvalvular gradient because the new valve cannot achieve its intended circular expansion. The immediate postprocedural high gradient seen in the case (mean 67 mm Hg) despite angiographic competence likely reflected underexpansion of the layered frames and restriction of leaflet motion; subsequent high‑pressure post‑dilatation partially expanded the Evolut frame and improved gradients, highlighting how mechanical interactions between frames determine haemodynamics.

Mechanical factors beyond simple lumen reduction also contribute. Commissural misalignment between successive valves can cause leaflet impingement, altered opening angles and localized high shear zones that predispose to mechanical fatigue and thrombosis. Supra‑annular versus intra‑annular leaflet positioning, frame height and the radial force of different valve platforms all influence how new and old devices interact. For example, the Acurate neo2’s supra‑annular leaflets and lower frame height were chosen in this patient to maximize leaflet opening and minimize additional obstruction in a constrained anatomy. Similarly, high implantation can intentionally appose prior leaflets against the frame to prevent inward leaflet folding, but this must be balanced against the risk of creating a neo‑skirt that threatens coronary flow.

Triple‑frame anatomies produce a qualitatively different flow environment compared with native or single‑stent valves. The cumulative metal and polymer burden alters sinus washout, increases stasis zones and may impede coronary access. Reduced sinus clearance is relevant both for thrombosis risk and for the potential of recurrent endocarditis — a poorly washed sinus could facilitate bacterial seeding if bacteremia occurs. Commissural alignment strategies aim to preserve coronary ostial windows and simplify future coronary interventions, but with multiple frames the mesh can still make catheter access technically challenging or sometimes impossible. These anatomical realities should be anticipated at the time of each reintervention and considered in lifetime planning.

From a diagnostic and management perspective, distinguishing the dominant mechanism of dysfunction is critical because it changes therapy. Progressive calcific or fibrotic SVD usually requires valve-in-valve therapy or surgical explant; HALT may respond to anticoagulation; leaflet perforation from prior infection may be best managed surgically if feasible. Imaging plays a central role: transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography quantify gradients, PVL and leaflet motion; gated MSCT assesses frame geometry, leaflet thickening/HALT, valve-to-coronary distances and the minimal internal diameters that dictate feasibility of further valve implantation. In the case discussed, MSCT informed the decision to proceed with a third TAV and guided implantation depth and valve selection; post‑dilatation then produced additional radial expansion of the Evolut frame and a durable haemodynamic improvement.

Finally, the biological sequelae of infection deserve emphasis. Even after adequate antimicrobial therapy, prior PVE alters the tissue–prosthesis interface — scar tissue, pannus formation and healed vegetations change mechanics and can accelerate degenerative processes. This underlines the importance of ensuring complete infection resolution before reintervention and of close post‑treatment surveillance. It also argues for the early adoption of a lifetime management strategy at the time of initial TAVI: valve choice, sizing, commissural alignment and consideration of future coronary access should all be made with an eye toward minimizing the cumulative adverse haemodynamic and anatomical consequences that complicate serial valve procedures.

In summary, prosthetic valve failure after TAVI — and particularly after treated PVE and serial valve‑in‑valve procedures — is the result of interacting mechanical constraints, altered haemodynamics, biological tissue changes from infection and the prothrombotic milieu of constrained sinuses. Careful multimodality imaging, a mechanistic approach to diagnosis (distinguishing SVD, HALT, PVL and post‑infectious scarring), and procedural strategies tailored to the layered anatomy are essential to optimize outcomes when reintervention is contemplated.

## Diagnostic Workup and Imaging for Redo‑TAVI/TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

Redo transcatheter aortic valve procedures—particularly TAV‑in‑TAV and TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV—require a structured diagnostic workup that addresses three separate but interrelated objectives: (1) determine the mechanism of prosthetic failure, (2) exclude active prosthetic valve infection, and (3) perform comprehensive CT‑based procedural planning to anticipate and mitigate anatomic risks, foremost among them coronary obstruction and severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch. The case presented illustrates how these objectives are pursued in practice: transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography established the haemodynamic severity and excluded obvious paravalvular complications; microbiology and imaging excluded active endocarditis after a completed course of treatment; and gated multislice CT (MSCT) defined the inner landing zone, coronary relationships and implant strategy that permitted a third transcatheter valve to be implanted safely.

Establishing the mechanism of valve failure begins with careful clinical assessment and echocardiography. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) provides rapid appraisal of transvalvular gradients (peak and mean), leaflets’ mobility, the presence and degree of transvalvular and paravalvular regurgitation, and global ventricular function. Because reinterventions commonly stem from stenosis, regurgitation, or a mixed picture, quantification should use multiple concordant parameters: mean gradient, continuity‑equation valve area, and the Doppler velocity index (DVI). In prosthetic valves, DVI <0.25 and an indexed effective orifice area (indexed AVA) ≤0.65 cm2/m2 are thresholds that suggest severe obstruction or clinically important prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM). Doppler measurements in the setting of layered stent frames and eccentric flow jets can be misleading, so they must be interpreted together with clinical status and anatomic imaging. In the case described, TTE documented a very high gradient and a small calculated AVA that prompted further anatomic evaluation.

Excluding active prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is mandatory before undertaking another prosthetic implant. This exclusion rests on integration of clinical, microbiological and imaging data. Clinically, lack of fever, subsiding inflammatory markers and resolution of systemic signs are reassuring but insufficient. Blood cultures should be obtained after withholding antibiotics when feasible (at least two sets, preferably three, drawn from separate venipunctures and spaced appropriately) to document microbiological clearance; negative cultures off antimicrobials increase confidence that infection is controlled. In prosthetic valve assessments, transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) remains the reference test to detect vegetations, leaflet perforation, dehiscence or abscess. Complementary ECG‑gated contrast MSCT provides superior spatial resolution for periannular complications—pseudoaneurysms, abscess cavities, fistulae or prosthesis dehiscence—particularly when echocardiography is indeterminate. In the presented patient, TEE and gated CT showed no vegetations or periannular pathology and blood cultures were negative after completed therapy, which, together with infectious disease input, supported proceeding with reintervention.

High‑quality MSCT is the cornerstone of procedural planning for redo‑TAVI and triple‑frame anatomies. The imaging protocol should be ECG gated with thin slices and multiphase reconstructions to allow accurate multiplanar reformatting along the valve axis. Several CT measurements are specifically important in the reintervention context. First, the inner landing zone must be defined by the true minimal internal diameter and cross‑sectional area within the existing prosthetic frame(s) at the level where the new valve leaflets will function. Because valve frames, native leaflets and previously implanted prosthetic leaflets reduce the effective lumen relative to the labelled valve size, measurement of the minimal inner diameter (MID) and its cross‑sectional area on the CT dataset is essential for valve sizing and for anticipating residual gradients and PPM. Non‑circular geometry is common; therefore, perpendicular minimum and maximum diameters and area measurements are preferred to a single nominal diameter. In the reported case, MSCT demonstrated an MID of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame — a critical datum that guided selection of a valve with favorable supra‑annular performance and the decision to pursue high implantation to optimise orifice area.

Second, assessment of coronary obstruction risk must be meticulous. CT provides the data needed to calculate several predictive parameters: coronary ostial height from the annular plane, sinus of Valsalva (SoV) diameters, valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance and valve‑to‑sinotubular junction (VTSTJ) distance. Established thresholds help stratify risk: coronary heights ≤10–12 mm and SoV diameters <30 mm are associated with higher obstruction risk. The VTC distance—measured from the anticipated displaced leaflet or neo‑skirt to the coronary ostium after virtual valve deployment—is particularly useful: VTC <4 mm denotes high risk, 4–6 mm is intermediate, and >6 mm is low risk. VTSTJ measurements are also valuable because a short distance or low sinotubular junction relative to the new and existing frames predisposes to sinus sequestration and impaired coronary filling. In multi‑frame anatomies, the neo‑skirt may be formed by native or prosthetic leaflets pinned outward against an inner frame, so direct measurement of leaflet length relative to coronary ostia and analysis of commissural posts and frame cell geometry are necessary to predict whether coronary orifices will be covered.

Virtual valve simulation and three‑dimensional modelling are powerful extensions of standard CT workup. Overlaying a virtual transcatheter valve of the chosen type and size onto the patient’s CT enables visualization of the anticipated neo‑skirt, projected leaflet displacement, and the spatial relation to the ostia and sinotubular junction. These simulations facilitate decisions about implantation depth (high versus low), valve type (supra‑annular leaflet position, frame height and cell size), and whether adjunctive strategies (BASILICA leaflet laceration, chimney stent protection or upfront coronary wiring) are required. Commissural alignment planning—using CT to determine the rotational orientation that will best position the transcatheter valve posts relative to coronary ostia—becomes more important with each added frame because cumulative mesh burden can compromise future coronary access; careful preprocedural marking of fluoroscopic landmarks aids intraoperative deployment.

Angiography and intra‑procedural balloon testing complement CT planning. A baseline aortogram documents the presence and directionality of regurgitation and provides a live assessment of coronary perfusion. Balloon valvuloplasty within the existing prosthesis under pacing can serve as a dynamic coronary patency test: if balloon inflation reproduces coronary compromise, operators should be prepared to deploy coronary protection (a pre‑positioned, undeployed stent or guidewire) or to use BASILICA if feasible. Coronary protection strategies are often staged decisions—reserved for cases with intermediate or high CT risk or if balloon testing provokes ischemia. In the case in question, pre‑dilation with a 22 mm balloon and simultaneous aortography confirmed adequate coronary flow and supported proceeding without coronary protection.

Besides annular and coronary anatomy, CT angiography of the iliofemoral and aorto‑iliac axes is mandatory in redo procedures, particularly when prior vascular complications exist. Prior access site problems (pseudoaneurysm, surgical repair) or severe peripheral disease may mandate alternative access routes (transapical, transaxillary, or suprasternal) and should influence preprocedural planning. The case patient’s earlier femoral pseudoaneurysm and repair were relevant to access planning and justified detailed peripheral CT angiography.

A practical CT‑to‑cath lab translation is to document, before adding a new frame, the anticipated coronary access paths: which sinus is most accessible, which specific frame cell would permit guide catheter engagement, and the fluoroscopic projection that best demonstrates these relationships. These findings should be recorded and communicated to the implanting team and become part of the procedural plan. Similarly, establishing a pre‑procedure VARC‑3 baseline—documenting gradients, AVA, DVI, degree of regurgitation, ventricular function and the absence/presence of PVE—provides an objective comparator for immediate and longer‑term outcomes.

Finally, the decision to proceed must be multidisciplinary. Infectious disease input to confirm eradication of infection and appropriateness of timing, cardiac surgery consultation to weigh surgical options and potential feasibility of explantation, and discussion with the patient about the limited future options if the new prosthesis fails are all essential. Informed consent should explicitly address the increased technical complexity, higher likelihood of residual PPM, and potential implications for future coronary access and reintervention.

In summary, a systematic diagnostic algorithm for redo‑TAVI/TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV integrates (1) careful clinical and microbiological exclusion of active PVE, (2) comprehensive echocardiographic quantification of prosthetic dysfunction (including DVI and indexed AVA for PPM assessment), (3) high‑resolution ECG‑gated MSCT with measurement of the inner landing zone, coronary heights, SoV dimensions, VTC and VTSTJ distances, and virtual valve simulation for neo‑skirt prediction and commissural alignment planning, and (4) angiographic corroboration and dynamic testing (balloon valvuloplasty) when coronary risk remains uncertain. The case illustrates how these steps translated into a CT‑guided, strategy‑driven third TAVI with preserved coronary flow and durable haemodynamic improvement at one year.

## Procedural Strategy and Technical Considerations for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

When a third transcatheter aortic valve is contemplated, planning must move beyond routine TAVI algorithms and address the compounded anatomic and mechanical consequences of multiple overlapping stent frames. The goal remains the same as for any valve intervention: relieve stenosis or regurgitation, preserve coronary flow and coronary access, and optimise the effective orifice area (EOA) while minimising procedural risk. Achieving those objectives in a triple‑stented anatomy requires meticulous preprocedural imaging and a deliberate, device‑specific implantation strategy.

Imaging and preprocedural planning
Multislice CT (MSCT) is the cornerstone of planning for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. The preprocedural study must quantify the true internal lumen available to a new valve (the minimum internal diameter within the existing prostheses), measure sinus of Valsalva dimensions and coronary ostial heights, and calculate valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distances. Virtual valve implantation and 3D reconstructions are used to predict where a neo‑skirt (the new bioprosthetic sealing cuff and displaced native or prior leaflets) will sit relative to the coronaries. In the case presented, MSCT revealed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut frame; this narrow inner lumen was a central determinant of device selection and of the decision to aim for a very high implantation to limit neo‑skirt height. MSCT also allows assessment of the prior valve frame geometry and the cells through which future coronary access would need to be achieved; identifying large open cells or favourable commissural relationships can make later coronary interventions more feasible.

Valve selection: prioritise EOA and coronary access
Valve choice in re‑intervention must balance hemodynamic performance against frame geometry and height. Supra‑annular leaflet designs generally provide a larger EOA for a given internal diameter than intra‑annular designs and are therefore often preferred in small or narrowed inner diameters to mitigate prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM). In narrow landing zones a valve with supra‑annular leaflets and a relatively low profile or advantageous cell architecture may offer the best tradeoff between gradient reduction and preservation of coronary access. Conversely, very tall frames with dense cell meshes can compromise access to the coronary ostia and make future interventions difficult. In the reported case, an Acurate neo2 (supra‑annular, favorable EOA) was selected consciously to maximize effective orifice area within a small lumen while allowing commissural alignment and minimizing neo‑skirt impingement on the coronaries.

Implantation depth strategy: the rationale for high deployment
Implantation depth is a critical modifiable factor. High implantation—positioning the new valve at or slightly above the level of the prior valve leaflets—serves several purposes in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV scenario. First, it pins the leaflets of the immediately preceding valve outward against the stent frame, preventing inward leaflet folding that would otherwise create a tall neo‑skirt and reduce EOA. Second, a high position moves the sealing zone away from the sinuses and coronary ostia, reducing the risk of coronary obstruction and improving future coronary access. The trade‑off is the potential for increased transvalvular regurgitation or device embolisation if the valve does not seat securely; therefore, accurate fluoroscopic landmarks and preprocedural CT guidance are essential. In the present case a high implantation strategy was deliberately chosen to improve EOA and preserve coronary perfusion, and was supported intra‑procedurally by balloon inflation runs and angiography demonstrating maintained coronary flow.

Commissural alignment and orientation
Commissural alignment has assumed heightened importance as a lifetime‑management consideration. Aligning commissures so that prosthetic posts do not obscure coronary ostia or obstruct likely coronary access pathways reduces the cumulative risk of coronary obstruction and facilitates future coronary angiography or percutaneous coronary intervention. Alignment is device dependent: operators should be familiar with the orientation markers and delivery‑system rotation manoeuvres for each platform. Techniques include using fluoroscopic markers (e.g., delivery nosecone or radiopaque commissural markers), knowledge of the relationship between the delivery system’s orientation at the skin and the implanted valve’s commissural posts, and pre‑procedural CT to identify optimal rotational targets. Even with careful alignment, overlapping meshes from multiple valves may still limit some coronary windows; nonetheless, every attempt should be made to orient the new device so its larger open cells align with the coronaries.

Coronary protection planning: choose a graded strategy
Coronary obstruction risk in TAV‑in‑TAV is higher than for native valve TAVI and may be further magnified by repeated valve layers. Decisions about coronary protection are guided by CT‑derived metrics (coronary height, sinus width, VTC). There are three pragmatic tiers of protection:

- No protection when CT predicts a safe distance (comfortable VTC, roomy sinuses) and simulatory virtual implantation demonstrates clearance.
- Guidewire or catheter standby: place a coronary wire (and ideally a jailed undeployed stent mounted on a balloon) that can be rapidly delivered into the ostium if obstruction occurs. This is a low‑invasiveness, readily reversible precaution.
- Prophylactic chimney stenting or BASILICA: active protection for very high‑risk anatomy. Chimney stenting (stent deployed from coronary ostium into the aorta alongside the new valve) prevents occlusion at the expense of creating a permanent stent protruding into the aorta and may complicate future procedures. BASILICA—intentional leaflet laceration—can prevent leaflet‑related obstruction by splitting the leaflet that would be displaced toward a coronary ostium. In triple‑frame configurations BASILICA’s effectiveness is uncertain because underlying stent frames may restrict traversal or laceration of leaflets, and additional frames may re‑direct leaflet tissue unpredictably. Therefore, BASILICA should be considered only after careful bench and CT assessment and typically in centres experienced with the technique. In the reported case preprocedural modelling showed acceptable coronary clearance and a high implantation strategy combined with intra‑procedural balloon testing allowed the team to proceed without prophylactic coronary stenting, though angiography and aortography were used to confirm coronary flow after balloon dilation.

Balloon strategy: pre‑dilation as a test, post‑dilation as optimisation
Balloon pre‑dilation in this setting has three valuable roles: it can fracture adherent leaflet calcification (if present), confirm the behaviour of the device and leaflets under expansion, and—most importantly—provide a dynamic test of coronary patency by transiently displacing tissue while an aortogram is obtained. A carefully performed balloon inflation with simultaneous aortography can demonstrate whether coronary flow will be compromised after valve expansion. Post‑dilation, performed after valve deployment, can enhance expansion of both the new valve and, to some extent, the prior self‑expanding frames; this can improve leaflet mobility and reduce transvalvular gradients. When post‑dilating, operators should use stepwise, controlled inflations and choose balloon sizes appropriate to the internal dimensions to avoid annular injury, coronary compromise, or valve malapposition. High‑pressure balloons can exert incremental radial force and may partially expand an underlying self‑expanding frame (as likely occurred in this patient), but operators must be aware of device‑specific limits and the risk of aortic root injury.

Bioprosthetic valve fracture/expansion: understand device limits
Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) is a technique developed for certain surgical bioprostheses with rigid rings that can be fractured to accommodate a larger transcatheter valve and increase EOA. BVF is generally not applicable to transcatheter frames that are metallic and designed with continuous stent structures: such frames cannot be “fractured” safely in the same manner. That said, self‑expanding nitinol frames (e.g., some Evolut designs) can sometimes be further dilated by aggressive post‑dilation to increase internal diameter; this is an expansion rather than fracture and is limited by the material properties and anchoring of the device. Attempting high‑pressure dilation beyond tested ranges risks device deformation, vascular or annular injury, and should be undertaken only with full awareness of the device instructions for use and with surgical backup available.

Access management and vascular planning
Repeat large‑bore femoral access increases the risk of vascular complications, particularly in patients with prior access site surgery or peripheral disease. Ultrasound‑guided puncture, preprocedural femoral CT or duplex mapping, and preclosure devices are essential elements of access planning. Consider alternative access (transaxillary, transcarotid, or transcaval) if iliofemoral anatomy is unsuitable. Contralateral radial access is valuable for aortic root angiography and selective coronary engagement for protection or testing; in the case presented, left radial and left femoral access were used to provide both imaging and delivery vectors. Anticipate the need for open repair in patients with prior failed vascular interventions or pseudoaneurysm formation and include vascular surgery on the standby list when appropriate.

Hemodynamic and conduction considerations
Balloon inflations and some deployment steps use rapid ventricular pacing to stabilize the valve and reduce cardiac motion. Given the increased structural burden on the conduction system from multiple frames—particularly with lower implant depths—there is a higher risk of new conduction disturbances. Operators should plan for temporary pacing support and have a low threshold to implant a permanent pacemaker if high‑degree atrioventricular block persists. Preexisting permanent pacemakers reduce the immediate risk of severe bradyarrhythmia, but other conduction abnormalities may still occur and should be monitored.

Radiation and contrast minimisation
Patients undergoing serial structural interventions frequently have reduced renal reserve and have already been exposed to significant radiation. Strategies to limit additional injury include using the preprocedural CT to plan and reduce fluoroscopic time, using image fusion and overlay when available to limit angiographic runs, performing targeted aortography and selective coronary angiography rather than repeated full runs, using low‑osmolar contrast agents with tight volume limits, and employing intraprocedural transoesophageal echocardiography to reduce reliance on angiography. Meticulous peri‑procedural hydration and nephroprotective measures are mandatory in patients with renal risk.

Putting the pieces together: application to the case
In the presented patient the team synthesised MSCT measurements, knowledge of device characteristics and the patient’s frailty to adopt a strategy that maximised EOA and preserved coronary flow. They selected a supra‑annular valve (Acurate neo2) to optimise gradients within a 13.7 mm lumen, aimed for high implantation to pin prior leaflets outward and limit neo‑skirt height, performed balloon pre‑dilation with simultaneous aortography to confirm coronary patency, and used controlled post‑dilation to improve expansion and leaflet motion. Commissural alignment was pursued to preserve coronary access. This combination of imaging‑driven decisions and stepwise intraprocedural testing produced an acceptable haemodynamic result and preserved coronary perfusion, with sustained clinical benefit at 1 year.

Concluding practical principles
A successful third transcatheter valve implantation demands comprehensive CT‑guided planning, device selection that prioritises EOA while respecting future coronary access, deliberate implantation depth and commissural alignment, and an explicit contingency plan for coronary protection. Balloon testing and controlled post‑dilation are powerful intra‑procedural tools to assess and optimise outcomes, but operators must respect the mechanical limits of prior frames. Given the technical complexity and limited long‑term data for serial transcatheter reinterventions, these procedures should be undertaken by experienced heart teams in centres equipped for advanced imaging, coronary protection techniques and prompt surgical conversion when required.

## Postprocedural Care, Antithrombotic Therapy, and Surveillance

Immediate postprocedural assessment after any transcatheter aortic valve procedure — and especially after a technically complex redo or TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV — must be systematic and focused on the elements that determine procedural success and early complications. The Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 (VARC‑3) endpoints remain the practical framework: confirm correct valve position, leaflet mobility, absence or degree of paravalvular leak (PVL), coronary patency, vascular access integrity and the presence or absence of procedure‑related complications. In the catheterization laboratory this is achieved by combined fluoroscopy, aortography and invasive hemodynamics; transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) or transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) may be used immediately to assess leaflet motion and residual regurgitation. In the presented case, immediate aortography and TTE confirmed correct valve position and no regurgitation, satisfying VARC‑3 anatomic success, but the high immediate mean gradient required close attention rather than reflex reintervention.

Interpreting early transvalvular gradients after valve implantation requires understanding several mechanisms. Immediately elevated gradients can result from underexpansion of the newly implanted valve frame (residual constraining by prior frames), incomplete leaflet opening from edema or temporary leaflet restriction, a hyperdynamic circulatory state, or prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) in a small effective orifice area (EOA). In layered stent configurations, the metallic frames can mechanically limit full expansion of the newly implanted prosthesis and create a small lumen immediately after deployment; however, self‑expanding frames and high‑pressure post‑dilatation can increase radial expansion over days to weeks. For these reasons an elevated immediate gradient alone — if the patient is hemodynamically stable and there is no evidence of obstruction from thrombus or malposition — often merits observation and reassessment rather than immediate reintervention. The standard practical approach is routine TTE at discharge or within 30 days to reassess gradient, EOA and Doppler velocity index (DVI) after initial periprocedural remodeling and any delayed frame expansion. The course of the patient in the case— mean gradient 67 mm Hg immediately but improving to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and remaining stable at 1 year — illustrates this phenomenon and supports planned short‑term surveillance rather than an urgent redo.

Quantifying and classifying PPM after TAVI is essential for decision making and prognostication. Indexed EOA thresholds commonly used are: severe PPM when indexed EOA ≤ 0.65 cm²/m² (and ≤ 0.55 cm²/m² for patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) and moderate PPM when indexed EOA ≤ 0.85 cm²/m². These numbers must be interpreted in the clinical context — patient symptoms, LV function, gradients, and complementary metrics such as the DVI. The DVI (ratio of LVOT velocity time integral to transvalvular VTI) is less dependent on LVOT measurement and is useful when LVOT area estimation is uncertain; a low DVI (for example ≤ 0.25–0.35, with values toward the lower bound correlating with more severe obstruction) supports significant prosthetic stenosis. In the presented case a DVI of 0.24 and an indexed AVA in the severe range immediately postprocedure emphasized the severity of transvalvular obstruction, but the subsequent improvement in gradients and AVA after 1 month showed that initial measurements can improve as the frame expands and the leaflets mobilize. Management of true persistent severe PPM (symptomatic patients with sustained high gradients and small indexed EOA) may require consideration of transcatheter options such as balloon post‑dilation, bioprosthetic valve fracture (if the surgical or transcatheter valve is fractureable and safe to do so), or, in selected cases, surgery — decisions that require individualized heart‑team discussion.

A separate, important cause of rising transvalvular gradients after TAVI is subclinical or clinical leaflet thrombosis, manifesting as hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) with or without reduced leaflet motion (RELM). When a rise in gradient is noted without clear mechanical explanation (malposition or paravalvular leak) or if there is new leaflet immobility on echocardiography, a 4D‑contrast gated CT (multiphase CT) is the preferred test to look for HALT/RELM. If HALT is confirmed and the patient has no contraindication to anticoagulation, treatment with therapeutic oral anticoagulation (vitamin K antagonist or direct oral anticoagulant where evidence supports local practice and valve type) has been shown to resolve HALT in many patients and to lower gradients. The decision to anticoagulate must balance the benefit of resolving leaflet thrombosis against the patient’s bleeding risk; trials and registries have shown mixed net benefit in unselected populations, and routine empirical anticoagulation after TAVI is not recommended. In our patient, a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding would have increased the risk of anticoagulation; therefore any decision to start anticoagulation for suspected HALT would require careful multidisciplinary review and, ideally, confirmation with CT.

Antithrombotic therapy after TAVI should be tailored to thromboembolic risk (atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, mechanical valves [not relevant here]) and bleeding risk. Contemporary guideline‑informed practice generally favors single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT; typically aspirin) in patients without another indication for oral anticoagulation, rather than routine dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT). DAPT increases bleeding without clear ischemic benefit in most patients post‑TAVI. In patients with atrial fibrillation or other established indications for long‑term anticoagulation, oral anticoagulant therapy alone (without antiplatelet agents) is preferred in many cases to minimize bleeding, provided the patient’s coronary stents are not in a high‑risk window requiring antiplatelet therapy. If combined antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy is unavoidable (recent PCI with high ischemic risk), the duration should be minimized and individualized. For the patient described, there was no documented atrial fibrillation but he did have a pacemaker and prior coronary disease; his recurrent GI bleeding would push the balance toward minimizing antithrombotic exposure. The most defensible regimen for a similar patient without another indication for anticoagulation would be SAPT with careful secondary prevention measures and close follow‑up; any extension to anticoagulation would require clear indication (e.g., confirmed HALT) and a plan to manage bleeding risk.

Prevention and early detection of infective endocarditis (IE) remain priorities after TAVI, particularly in a patient with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Preventive strategies emphasize meticulous dental hygiene and prompt evaluation of any febrile illness. Current recommendations support antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures in patients with prosthetic heart valves, including transcatheter bioprostheses; other non‑dental interventions generally do not require routine antibiotic prophylaxis unless recommended by local or procedure‑specific guidance. Patients and primary care providers should be counselled to seek immediate medical attention for fever, unexplained malaise or signs of systemic infection; early blood cultures and echocardiography (initially TTE, with low threshold for TEE) must be obtained in such cases given the high morbidity of delayed diagnosis. In this case, the patient’s prior E. faecalis PVE required a prolonged antibiotic course and careful post‑treatment surveillance. After documented eradication of infection, routine long‑term suppressive antibiotics are not indicated unless persistent infection is demonstrable or advised by infectious disease specialists.

A planned surveillance program is essential after complex valve‑in‑valve procedures. Practical recommendations are: baseline TTE before hospital discharge or within 30 days to document valve hemodynamics and establish a postprocedural baseline; repeat imaging at 6–12 months to assess stability, and then annual TTE thereafter in stable, asymptomatic patients. Earlier or more frequent imaging is indicated for new or worsening symptoms, signs of heart failure, or a rising gradient on prior imaging. If there is an abrupt hemodynamic change, clinical suspicion for HALT, cusp immobility, PVL, structural valve deterioration, or coronary obstruction, obtain prompt TTE and consider gated contrast 4D‑CT to define leaflet morphology, frame geometry and the relationship to the coronaries. CT is also useful when planning future coronary access because it delineates the spatial relationship between valve stent cells, commissures and coronary ostia; in fraught anatomies such as triple‑stent configurations CT can be invaluable to guide catheter selection and approach for future coronary angiography or intervention.

Because coronary access may be challenging or impossible in layered transcatheter frames, documentation of the procedural strategy should be explicit and preserved in the medical record: note the final valve model, size and depth of implant relative to the annulus, commissural alignment maneuvers used (and final fluoroscopic angular position of a marker), which sinuses/cells provided the most direct access to the left and right coronary ostia on the procedural angiogram, and the preferred fluoroscopic projections for reaccess. Store and make available the pre‑ and postprocedural CT datasets, including dedicated reformats, to facilitate future planning. In the present case the operators recorded commissural alignment and CT‑based guidance to preserve coronary flow; inclusion of these details in the operative report and imaging archive will aid any future coronary procedures.

Finally, clinical surveillance must integrate patient‑specific comorbidities and life expectancy. For this patient, recurrent GI bleeding raised bleeding risk for any long‑term anticoagulant strategy; prior coronary disease increased the importance of preserving coronary access; and his prior PVE mandated vigilance for infection recurrence. Decisions about antithrombotic therapy, timing of imaging, and thresholds for further intervention must therefore be individualized and revisited at each follow‑up visit by the multidisciplinary team.

Summary practical checklist after complex redo‑TAVI:
- Immediately confirm valve position, leaflet motion, PVL grade and coronary patency (VARC‑3 endpoints).
- If early gradients are high but no clear mechanical cause, observe and reassess with TTE at discharge/within 30 days — delayed improvement is common.
- Use indexed EOA and DVI to quantify PPM; recognize thresholds for moderate and severe PPM and correlate with symptoms.
- If leaflet thrombosis is suspected, obtain 4D‑CT; treat confirmed HALT with anticoagulation if bleeding risk allows.
- Prefer SAPT for patients without another indication for anticoagulation; avoid routine DAPT. Use anticoagulation alone for established indications (eg, AF).
- Emphasize endocarditis prevention: dental hygiene, prompt evaluation of fever, and prophylactic antibiotics for dental procedures.
- Follow a surveillance schedule (discharge/30 days, 6–12 months, then yearly if stable) and use CT for abrupt hemodynamic changes or coronary access planning.
- Document commissural alignment and coronary access strategy clearly in the procedure report and archive CT images for future reference.

Applying these principles to the presented patient led to conservative early management of a high immediate gradient, close imaging follow‑up that documented significant and sustained improvement, minimization of antithrombotic exposure given bleeding history, and continued vigilance for recurrent infection — a pragmatic, individualized approach consistent with contemporary best practice.

## Clinical Pearls, Pitfalls, and Lifetime Management

This case of a successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) crystallises several principles that should guide management of patients who are likely to require serial transcatheter aortic valve interventions. With TAVI offered increasingly to younger and lower‑risk patients and treated patients living longer, operators must adopt a "lifetime management" mindset from the very first valve—anticipating future procedures, preserving coronary access, and planning for the possibility of sequential frame layering. Below are practical lessons distilled from the case, framed to help you apply them to other patients.

Redefined candidacy for redo‑TAVI after PVE
A history of PVE does not automatically preclude redo‑TAVI, but it does change the decision pathway. The first and non‑negotiable step is rigorous exclusion of active infection: multiple negative blood cultures, a thorough transoesophageal echocardiogram showing no vegetations or paravalvular abscess, and cross‑sectional imaging when indicated. Only after a full, guideline‑directed course of antibiotics and confirmation of eradication should reintervention be contemplated. Even then, the local heart team must weigh anatomical feasibility against surgical risk; patients with uncontrolled infection, root abscess, prosthetic dehiscence, or persistent bacteremia remain surgical candidates per current guidelines. In the presented patient, completion of targeted antimicrobial therapy and confirmatory imaging permitted a transcatheter solution in the presence of prohibitive surgical risk.

Preprocedural CT modelling is indispensable for stacked‑frame anatomies
When multiple prosthetic frames are present, the principal imaging task is to predict how a new valve will interact with prior frames and the native coronary ostia. Multislice CT must be used to measure coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and—critically—valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distances and expected neo‑skirt height. In layered valves the neo‑skirt (the effective sealing zone formed by displaced leaflets and new frame) can be higher than after a single valve, and even small reductions in VTC can translate into catastrophic coronary obstruction. Dedicated 3‑D modelling or valve‑in‑valve software is strongly recommended when available; when not, careful manual reconstruction and conservative planning is required. In the case, MSCT demonstrated a severely narrowed inner lumen (minimum 13.7 mm of the prior Evolut), which directly influenced device choice and the decision to avoid routine coronary protection.

Anticipate and mitigate coronary obstruction and access challenges
Multilayer stents magnify the risk of coronary obstruction and make future coronary access harder. Planning must include a clear coronary protection and bailout algorithm before crossing the valve: consider prophylactic coronary wiring, balloon inflation, or readiness for chimney stenting. Leaflet‑modification techniques such as BASILICA (intentional laceration of the bioprosthetic or native aortic leaflet) are proven to reduce obstruction risk in selected valve‑in‑valve cases, but their efficacy and technical feasibility in triple‑stented anatomies are uncertain—mesh density and limited space can prevent safe traversal or controlled laceration. Therefore, have a staged plan: primary strategy to avoid obstruction (valve choice and implantation depth), preplanned coronary protection if VTC is borderline, and an explicit rescue plan (chimney stent placement, urgent conversion) if obstruction occurs. Recognise that even with commissural alignment, the cumulative mesh burden may necessitate complex coronary revascularisation strategies later.

Valve selection, implantation depth and commissural alignment matter more than ever
Device choice should be individualized with lifetime management in mind. Supra‑annular valves often provide superior haemodynamics in small effective diameters by placing leaflets above the annulus, improving effective orifice area (EOA). In narrow lumens—such as the 13–14 mm inner diameter encountered here—this haemodynamic benefit can be decisive. However, supra‑annular designs with tall frames or small upper‑cell geometry may compromise coronary access later. Thus, when anticipating repeat procedures, choose valves with favourable upper‑frame cell design and clear commissural markers that facilitate reproducible commissural alignment. From the index TAVI onward, attempt commissural alignment using available orientation markers to preserve future access; a deliberate aligned deployment is a small upfront investment that yields large downstream benefits.

High implantation and leaflet fixation can preserve EOA but require careful planning
In stacked valves, a strategic high implant of the new valve aligned to the previous leaflets can press the redundant leaflets outward and avoid inward folding that reduces the EOA. This technique can also minimise the neo‑skirt below coronary ostia and conserve coronary flow. Preprocedural CT must confirm that a high implant will not compromise anchoring, will deliver adequate radial force, and will maintain a safe VTC. Post‑dilatation may assist with mechanical expansion of prior frames and improve gradients, but operators must balance this against the theoretical risk of prosthesis damage or annular injury—especially when prior frames are integrated into the aortic wall.

Expect early gradients to evolve; avoid premature reintervention
Immediate post‑implant haemodynamics in complex reinterventions may be misleading. Acute high transvalvular gradients can improve over days to weeks as the new valve expands more fully, prior frames experience some mechanical dilation from ballooning, and the left ventricle remodels. The case demonstrates a marked fall in mean gradient from an early 67 mm Hg to a stable ~26–27 mm Hg by one month and sustained at one year. Therefore, clinical decisions about reintervention should consider symptoms, serial echocardiography, and possibly CT to assess valve expansion rather than relying on a single immediate post‑procedural gradient.

Document everything and make records usable for the future
Meticulous recording of the type, size, model, and implant depth of every device implanted is essential. Photograph/archival fluoroscopic images showing commissural orientation and landmarks, and note the coronary access routes and any protective maneuvers used. These data are invaluable for future planning and for colleagues who may treat the patient years later. Encourage patients to carry a prosthesis card and to have institutional records summarised in a durable format.

Antithrombotic and infection surveillance remain areas of uncertainty
There is no consensus specific to antithrombotic therapy after multiple layered bioprostheses; decisions should be individualized based on thrombotic risk, bleeding risk, and the presence of atrial fibrillation. Similarly, guidance on antibiotic prophylaxis following valve procedures in patients with prior PVE is not standardized—coordinate with infectious disease colleagues. Enlist a low threshold for echocardiographic surveillance and early imaging (TEE/CT) if fever or bacteremia occurs.

Plan for limited future options and involve the patient early
Acknowledge that each additional transcatheter valve reduces future options: surgical explant becomes more complex, and further catheter strategies may be limited. This reality should be discussed with the patient and family at the time of the initial intervention as part of shared decision‑making. When a TAVI approach is chosen for the index procedure, that choice should be informed by the patient's anticipated lifetime trajectory and the desire to preserve coronary access and future therapies.

Contribute to the evidence base and use registries
Serial TAV‑in‑TAV and TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV data are sparse. Wherever possible, enrol patients in registries or institutional databases and consider multicentre collaboration. Key unanswered questions include long‑term durability of stacked valves, optimal antithrombotic regimens, and the best imaging algorithms for 3‑D expansion modelling. Participation in research will inform safer lifetime management strategies.

In summary, redo‑TAVI after treated PVE can be feasible in patients with prohibitive surgical risk, but success hinges on infection eradication, rigorous CT‑guided anatomic planning, explicit strategies to protect coronary flow, deliberate commissural alignment, thoughtful device selection, and careful post‑procedural surveillance. Thinking beyond the immediate procedure—documenting device details, anticipating future access needs, and engaging patients in long‑term planning—will yield the best outcomes as more patients live long enough to require serial valve interventions.
