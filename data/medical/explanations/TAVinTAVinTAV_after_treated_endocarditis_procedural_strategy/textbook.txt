# TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV After Treated Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis: Imaging-Guided Strategy, Hemodynamics, and Lifetime Valve Management

## Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a cornerstone therapy for severe aortic stenosis across the surgical risk spectrum. As TAVI expands into younger and lower-risk populations and device longevity is tested over longer horizons, clinicians increasingly encounter failed transcatheter valves. Redo TAVI (TAV-in-TAV) has emerged as a practical and often preferred strategy in prohibitive or high surgical risk patients. Rarely, sequential reintervention culminates in a third valve within prior transcatheter frames (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), a scenario that concentrates the anatomic, hemodynamic, and procedural challenges of lifetime structural heart disease management.

This chapter uses the case of a man in his late 70s who underwent a Lotus TAVI (2014), TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut (2022), subsequent Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) with treated resolution, and ultimately a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2, 2024) to explore clinical reasoning, pathophysiology, diagnostic strategy, technical planning, and longitudinal care in complex serial TAVI. At 1 year, the patient remains asymptomatic with preserved valve function, providing a unique platform to teach the underlying medicine and decision making.

## Case-Linked Clinical Approach

The index presentation—exertional dyspnea, exertional chest discomfort, dizziness, and fatigue—raises suspicion for recurrent aortic valve dysfunction in a patient with two prior TAVI procedures, reduced exercise tolerance, and known left ventricular (LV) impairment that previously improved after TAVI. The differential diagnosis for symptomatic deterioration after TAVI is broad and includes:

- Structural valve deterioration (SVD): intrinsic leaflet degeneration (calcification, tearing, fibrosis), stent underexpansion, or frame deformation.
- Prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM): small effective orifice area (EOA) relative to body size, often accentuated in valve-in-valve configurations.
- Valve thrombosis: subclinical leaflet thrombosis (hypoattenuated leaflet thickening [HALT] with reduced leaflet motion) or clinically significant obstruction.
- Endocarditis: biofilm-mediated leaflet destruction, vegetations, or paravalvular extension.
- Hemodynamic contributors: paravalvular leak (PVL), LV systolic/diastolic dysfunction, uncontrolled hypertension, anemia.
- Nonvalvular etiologies: progression of coronary artery disease (CAD), arrhythmia, pulmonary disease (notable COPD in this case), or deconditioning.

Findings that narrow the differential include high mean gradients (49 mm Hg) and small calculated aortic valve area (0.5 cm²) with residual intraprosthetic regurgitation, two overlapping frames on imaging, and a CT-defined minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the inner prosthesis—features that favor SVD with superimposed PPM and possibly leaflet restriction related to prior infection. The absence of fever, negative blood cultures, and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)/multislice CT (MSCT) without vegetations or abscesses argue against active endocarditis at the time of reintervention. Coronary ischemia must remain on the radar in a patient with multivessel CAD and prior stents; however, the dominant physiologic abnormality here is valvular.

The clinical problem is thus reframed: how to restore hemodynamics and durability in a triple-stented aortic root while preserving present and future coronary access, minimizing gradients and PPM, and mitigating catastrophic risks such as coronary obstruction—after careful exclusion of active infection.

## Pathophysiology and Disease Mechanisms

Bioprosthetic SVD after TAVI
- Bioprosthetic leaflets, composed of glutaraldehyde-treated bovine or porcine tissue, undergo structural changes over time driven by cumulative mechanical stress, leaflet flexion, and altered calcium-phosphorus metabolism. TAVI-specific factors include crimping-induced collagen fiber disruption, suboptimal circularity (ellipticity) from nonuniform annular calcification, and frame underexpansion in small or rigid annuli—each accelerating asymmetric stress and localized fibrosis/calcification.
- Valve-in-valve (and especially valve-in-valve-in-valve) magnifies these effects: an inner valve must expand within the fixed or underexpanded frame of an outer valve, limiting EOA, increasing leaflet stress, and predisposing to higher residual gradients. Supra-annular leaflet positions (e.g., Evolut, Acurate neo2) generally provide larger EOAs in constrained settings than intra-annular platforms.

Prosthesis-patient mismatch
- PPM occurs when the indexed EOA (iEOA) is too small for the patient’s body size, resulting in high transvalvular gradients despite a normally functioning prosthesis. For the aortic position, severe PPM is often defined by iEOA <0.65 cm²/m², moderate by 0.65–0.85 cm²/m². Doppler velocity index (DVI) <0.25 supports functionally severe obstruction. PPM is more common in small surgical bioprostheses and in TAV-in-valve due to restricted expansion. In serial TAVI, frame layering can generate a “neo-skirt,” raising the leaflet coaptation plane and potentially limiting inflow area, compounding PPM risk.

Thrombosis and HALT
- Subclinical leaflet thrombosis (HALT) with reduced leaflet motion can elevate gradients early after TAVI and may respond to anticoagulation. Risk is higher with smaller EOAs, low-flow states, and valve-in-valve anatomy. Differentiating HALT from SVD relies on CT imaging and clinical response to anticoagulation; in this patient with prior GI bleeding and a long interval of dysfunction, SVD and mechanical restriction outweighed thrombosis in pretest probability.

Endocarditis in TAVI
- PVE after TAVI is typically due to staphylococci, streptococci, or enterococci; E. faecalis is common in older men with genitourinary or gastrointestinal sources. Biofilm formation on prosthetic material fosters persistent infection, leaflet destruction, and paravalvular complications. Active PVE is a relative/absolute contraindication to TAVI; however, after sterilization and resolution of complications, subsequent valve interventions can be considered based on anatomy and risk. This patient completed targeted therapy with demonstration of microbiologic and imaging cure before reintervention.

Coronary obstruction and the neo-skirt
- In valve-in-valve, the prior prosthetic leaflets can be displaced outward toward the sinuses and sinotubular junction (STJ), creating a fixed barrier (neo-skirt) that can cover the coronary ostia. Risk is assessed by CT-derived valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance and valve-to-STJ (VTSTJ) distance; small sinuses, low coronary heights, tall leaflets/frames, and commissural misalignment increase risk. In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, each added frame and leaflet layer raises the coaptation and skirt level, potentially compromising current or future coronary access even if acute obstruction is avoided.

Self-expanding frame biomechanics
- Nitinol frames (Evolut, Acurate neo2) exhibit time-dependent radial expansion, especially after adjunctive balloon dilatation. Early post-implant gradients may be high due to underexpansion, then decline over weeks as frames continue to expand and leaflets achieve better excursion. This explains the marked gradient fall from 67 mm Hg immediately post-implant to ~26–27 mm Hg by 1 month to 1 year in this case.

## Diagnostic Workup and Imaging Strategy

Echocardiography
- Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is first-line to quantify mean gradient, peak velocity, valve area by continuity equation, DVI, and assess regurgitation. Interpretation in prior TAVI requires integration: high gradient with DVI ≤0.25 and small AVA supports severe obstruction; preserved leaflet motion with small EOA suggests PPM; increased acceleration time and rounded jet contour suggest stenosis; holodiastolic flow reversal supports significant regurgitation.
- TEE refines assessment of leaflet motion, PVL, vegetations, and root complications in suspected endocarditis or when TTE windows are limited. In this patient, TEE was essential to exclude ongoing infection before proceeding.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT)
- MSCT is the cornerstone of redo TAVI planning. Key measurements include:
  - Coronary ostial heights and sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameters.
  - VTC and VTSTJ distances using virtual transcatheter valve overlays to estimate the leaflet/neo-skirt position relative to coronaries.
  - Dimensions and geometry of prior frames (inner and outer diameters, ellipticity), minimal internal lumen, and interaction between frames.
  - Annular and LV outflow tract (LVOT) calcification; access vessel diameters and tortuosity.
- In this case, the Evolut inner lumen was only 13.7 mm, signaling severe constraint and high PPM risk if a third device were implanted too low or underexpanded. CT also guided commissural alignment strategy and confirmed that a high implant could preserve coronary clearance, allowing the team to proceed without coronary protection.

Infective endocarditis evaluation
- Diagnosis rests on modified Duke criteria incorporating blood cultures and imaging; in prosthetic valves, adjunctive modalities such as CT angiography and, where available, FDG-PET/CT improve sensitivity for perivalvular complications. Here, negative cultures and imaging after targeted therapy supported cure of PVE before reintervention.

Coronary assessment
- Given extensive CAD, clinicians should consider ischemia evaluation or coronary angiography if chest pain, new LV dysfunction, or high-risk anatomy is suspected. In serial TAVI, future coronary reaccess planning is integral; CT helps anticipate catheter paths through frame cells and the feasibility of engaging the coronaries.

Vascular access planning
- Prior femoral complications (hematoma, pseudoaneurysm) necessitate careful iliofemoral assessment; radial access is useful for aortography and potential coronary protection, while femoral access is usually required for device delivery.

## Procedural Strategy in Serial TAVI

Heart team decision making
- After sterilization of PVE and in the absence of active infection, intervention choice is driven by surgical risk, anatomy, and procedural feasibility. This patient’s prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II 27%, COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding), deep integration of two frames into the aortic wall, and anticipated need for complex aortic root surgery rendered surgical explantation unattractive. CT confirmed that a transcatheter strategy could be executed with coronary preservation.

Device selection and rationale
- A self-expanding, supra-annular prosthesis (Acurate neo2, size S) was selected for favorable hemodynamics in constrained anatomy, a relatively low frame profile, large open cells, and facilitation of commissural alignment. Supra-annular leaflet position offers larger EOAs when inner diameters are small.

High implantation and commissural alignment
- High implant at the level of Evolut leaflets “pins” prior leaflets against the frame, preventing inward leaflet prolapse into the orifice, lowering the effective neo-skirt level, and helping preserve coronary flow and future access. Commissural alignment—guided by CT and fluoroscopic markers—aims to position neocommissures away from coronary ostia, aligning frame cells with coronary takeoffs to ease reaccess.

Balloon strategies
- Predilation with a 22/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing confirmed coronary patency and facilitated expansion in a rigid, triple-frame environment. Postdilation with a 20/40 mm balloon optimized leaflet coaptation and promoted further radial expansion of the nitinol frames without intentional frame fracture. In surgical valve-in-valve, bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) can enlarge the surgical ring; in TAV-in-TAV, frame fracture is typically not applicable, but remodeling with high-pressure postdilation can reduce ellipticity and gradients.

Coronary protection considerations
- When VTC or VTSTJ distances are small, or when commissural alignment cannot reliably preserve ostial clearance, protective strategies include coronary wiring with undeployed “chimney” stents, leaflet laceration techniques (BASILICA) to prevent coronary sequestration, or choosing a device/implant depth that lowers obstruction risk. In this case, CT planning and intra-procedural aortography confirmed safe coronary flow without protection.

Anesthesia and access
- Local anesthesia with left radial and left femoral arterial access minimized hemodynamic swings and facilitated aortography and bailout options. Prior vascular complications underscore the importance of ultrasound-guided puncture and closure device planning.

## Interpreting Early and Late Hemodynamics

Immediate echo after the third valve demonstrated an initially high mean gradient (67 mm Hg), small AVA (0.9 cm², indexed 0.4 cm²/m²), and low DVI (0.24), consistent with severe PPM/underexpansion in the operating room. This scenario is not uncommon in self-expanding valves within constrained rings. Over ensuing weeks:
- Radial creep of the nitinol frames and the effect of postdilation improved circularity and leaflet excursion.
- The mean gradient declined to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and remained ~26 mm Hg at 1 year with AVA ~1.1 cm² and stable LV function (EF ~65%).
- Absence of regurgitation and clinical improvement support an acceptable hemodynamic result in a triple-stented anatomy, recognizing that moderate residual gradients are often the trade-off to avoid catastrophic risks.

Distinguishing PPM from recurrent stenosis or thrombosis during follow-up relies on:
- Stable or improving gradients with good leaflet motion favor PPM/underexpansion over progressive SVD.
- New gradient rise with CT evidence of HALT suggests thrombosis; consider anticoagulation if bleeding risk permits.
- Worsening gradients with leaflet thickening/calcification over time suggests recurrent SVD.

## Postprocedural Management and Longitudinal Care

Antithrombotic therapy
- Contemporary practice after TAVI often favors single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) in the absence of other indications for anticoagulation, to limit bleeding. In valve-in-valve, the risk of leaflet thrombosis is higher; however, in patients with a history of GI bleeding, antithrombotic regimens must be individualized in collaboration with cardiology and gastroenterology. Routine anticoagulation solely to prevent HALT is not universally recommended; targeted anticoagulation may be considered if CT-detected thrombosis emerges.

Endocarditis prevention
- Active IE is a contraindication to TAVI; after cure, vigilance remains key. All TAVI recipients with prosthetic material should receive antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures that manipulate gingival tissue or perforate oral mucosa. Meticulous skin antisepsis for vascular procedures, early recognition of bacteremia sources (urinary, biliary, or GI), and coordinated care with infectious disease specialists reduce recurrent PVE risk.

Imaging surveillance
- Echo before discharge or within 30 days, at 1 year, and annually thereafter (or sooner if symptoms change) tracks gradients, regurgitation, and LV remodeling. CT is reserved for suspected leaflet thrombosis, structural complications, or when planning future interventions. In triple-frame anatomy, low thresholds for advanced imaging are reasonable if coronary access or leaflet motion is uncertain.

Coronary access planning
- Document device types, sizes, commissural alignment, and fluoroscopic/CT landmarks to guide future coronary engagement. Even with alignment, triple-frame mesh can complicate reaccess; preprocedural CT mapping of optimal catheter paths may be invaluable if PCI is later required.

Comorbid disease and HF therapy
- Continue guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure tailored to LV function, blood pressure, renal function, and comorbidities. Optimize COPD management and glycemic control. Address GI bleeding risk factors to maintain safe antithrombotic regimens.

When things go wrong
- Recurrent valve dysfunction after triple TAVI leaves few options. Surgical explant with root replacement is technically formidable and high risk. Catheter techniques such as leaflet laceration (BASILICA) and, in selected settings, high-pressure remodeling may be adjuncts, but their feasibility in triple-stented roots is uncertain and should be undertaken only in centers with extensive expertise.

## Evidence Base and Guidelines Context

Guidelines uniformly recommend surgery for complicated PVE (heart failure, uncontrolled infection, abscess) and consider active infection a contraindication to TAVI. After microbiologic cure and resolution of complications, reintervention should be individualized by a multidisciplinary heart team, integrating surgical risk, anatomic feasibility, and patient preferences. Data on TAV-in-TAV are growing; reports of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV remain scarce, emphasizing the importance of meticulous CT-based planning, commissural alignment, coronary protection strategies when indicated, and realistic hemodynamic expectations. This case adds rare 1-year outcome data demonstrating clinical stability and acceptable gradients after a third TAVI in the context of treated PVE.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

- Think mechanistically in failed TAVI: SVD, PPM, thrombosis, and endocarditis are the dominant causes of recurrent symptoms; Doppler parameters (mean gradient, AVA, DVI), TEE, and CT clarify the diagnosis.
- Active endocarditis is a contraindication to TAVI; after cure, redo TAVI can be considered when surgical risk is prohibitive and CT confirms feasibility.
- CT is the linchpin of redo TAVI planning: measure VTC and VTSTJ, sinus and STJ dimensions, prior frame geometry, and inner lumen; simulate neo-skirt height and commissural alignment to plan implant depth and device choice.
- In serial valve-in-valve, supra-annular, self-expanding platforms often provide better EOAs in constrained anatomy; high implantation can pin prior leaflets, reduce the neo-skirt, and preserve coronaries.
- Expect early high gradients with self-expanding valves in tight frames; nitinol creep and optimized postdilation often lower gradients over weeks. DVI and clinical course help distinguish PPM from progressive obstruction.
- Coronary protection (wiring/chimney stents) and leaflet laceration (BASILICA) are essential tools when VTC or VTSTJ is small; use them selectively based on CT risk.
- Commissural alignment is not cosmetic—it preserves coronary access and may reduce future procedural complexity, a critical consideration in lifetime valve management.
- Antithrombotic therapy after TAVI should be individualized; in valve-in-valve anatomy with high thrombosis risk but prior GI bleeding, balance thrombotic and hemorrhagic risks with multidisciplinary input.
- Document everything: device types, sizes, depths, alignment, and CT maps. Today’s procedural choices shape tomorrow’s options in a lifetime management paradigm.
- Even after treated PVE, reinfection risk persists; enforce prophylaxis for high-risk procedures and maintain vigilance for bacteremia sources.

This case illustrates that a third TAVI can be feasible and effective after treated PVE when driven by rigorous imaging, thoughtful device and strategy selection, and heart team governance—principles that will increasingly anchor the care of patients living longer with transcatheter bioprostheses.
