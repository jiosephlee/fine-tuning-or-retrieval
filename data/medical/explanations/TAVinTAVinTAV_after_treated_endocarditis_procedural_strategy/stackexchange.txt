Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Clinical Pearls] When planning a third transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), which preprocedural CT-derived metrics most strongly predict coronary obstruction risk, and what numerical thresholds (e.g., coronary ostial height, sinus width, valve-to-coronary distance) are considered high risk?

Bottom line
- In repeat TAVR (TAV‑in‑TAV and especially TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) the CT metrics that most strongly predict coronary obstruction are: (1) coronary ostial height from the annular plane, (2) sinus of Valsalva (SOV) dimensions (width and sinus height/STJ relationship), and (3) the CT‑simulated valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC or “virtual transcatheter valve‑to‑coronary” distance) — i.e., the minimal distance between the anticipated final transcatheter valve frame/leaflet plane (neo‑skirt) and the coronary ostium. These should be measured on contrast ECG‑gated MSCT with virtual valve implantation.  

How to think about the measurements (pathophysiology and why they matter)
- Coronary obstruction in valve‑in‑valve (and more so with multiple layered frames) occurs when displaced bioprosthetic leaflets or an implanted transcatheter valve skirt/leaflet assembly physically cover the coronary ostia or when the sinuses cannot accommodate the displaced leaflets, creating a shallow “pouch” that impedes coronary flow. CT lets you (a) measure the absolute vertical clearance of the ostia above the annulus (coronary height), (b) assess sinus room (SOV diameter and the sinotubular junction height/diameter) to accept displaced leaflet bulk, and (c) simulate where the new valve frame/leaflets will sit and therefore how close that structure will come to each coronary (VTC). VTC integrates both ostial height and sinus geometry and is the single best predictor on modern CT simulation.

Numerical thresholds used in clinical practice (practical risk stratification)
- High risk
  - Coronary ostial height ≤10 mm (many groups use ≤10 mm as high risk; ≤12 mm is often considered borderline).
  - Sinus of Valsalva mean diameter <30 mm (narrow sinuses give little room for displaced leaflets).
  - Virtual transcatheter valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance ≤4 mm — this is the most direct high‑risk CT metric for obstruction.
- Intermediate / borderline risk
  - Coronary height 10–12 mm.
  - SOV diameter ≈30–34 mm.
  - VTC 4–6 mm — consider anatomy, leaflet type, and procedural mitigations.
- Low risk
  - Coronary height >12 mm.
  - SOV diameter >34 mm.
  - VTC >6 mm — obstruction unlikely if other features favorable.

Other important CT/echo considerations
- Leaflet anatomy and bioprosthesis type: externally mounted leaflets or long leaflets that extend above the sewing ring (or prior transcatheter leaflets/frames that already sit high) raise risk even when ostial height is borderline.
- STJ height and diameter: a low/narrow STJ can trap displaced leaflets and produce obstruction despite a moderate coronary height.
- Commissural alignment and frame stacking: CT simulation should show where commissures and frame struts will align relative to the coronaries — multiple stacked frames increase “mesh” burden and can worsen coronary access even if overt obstruction does not occur.
- Use of systolic phase reconstruction and virtual valve implantation tools to derive VTC — this is standard for planning of valve‑in‑valve procedures and was the technique used in the presented case.

Application to the case
- The team in this report used MSCT to measure coronary ostial height, SOV dimensions and valve‑to‑coronary distances and to model the anticipated neo‑skirt relative to the ostia. Their CT modelling demonstrated adequate clearance such that they proceeded without routine coronary protection and used a high implantation strategy to avoid creating a neo‑skirt that would impinge on the coronaries. The favorable CT metrics (VTC above the danger threshold and adequate SOV space) directly informed valve choice, implantation depth and the decision not to use prophylactic coronary protection.

Implications for planning and management
- Always perform contrast ECG‑gated MSCT with virtual valve implantation for repeat TAVR, measure coronary height, SOV width/STJ, and compute VTC.  
- If any single high‑risk threshold is met (especially VTC ≤4 mm or coronary height ≤10 mm) treat the case as high risk: plan coronary protection (guidewire ± undeployed stent in target coronary), strongly consider BASILICA if leaflet anatomy suitable, or refer for surgery if feasible.  
- For intermediate risk (VTC 4–6 mm, coronary height 10–12 mm, narrow sinuses), individualized strategy: consider balloon test inflation with simultaneous aortography, coronary protection, and careful choice of valve type and implantation depth (as in this patient).

Teaching pearl
- In repeat and serial TAVR the VTC from CT simulation has become the most actionable metric — it synthesizes ostial height and sinus geometry and correlates best with obstruction risk. Use VTC thresholds (≤4 mm high risk; 4–6 mm intermediate) together with absolute ostial height (≤10–12 mm) and SOV width (<30–34 mm) to guide whether to protect, to perform leaflet laceration (BASILICA), or to consider alternate therapy.

### [Management] After completing treatment for Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, what clinical, microbiologic, and imaging criteria must be met before considering transcatheter reintervention, and how should timing be decided?

Short answer
Before attempting a transcatheter reintervention after Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) you must document eradication of infection clinically, microbiologically and radiographically, and obtain infectious‑disease and heart‑team endorsement. Timing should be deferred until those criteria are met unless life‑threatening haemodynamic deterioration forces earlier surgery/intervention.

What to require before reintervention
1) Clinical stability
- Afebrile, no ongoing signs of sepsis (no persistent leukocytosis, hemodynamic instability, or new embolic phenomena).  
- Clinical improvement on therapy and no need for ongoing suppressive antibiotics unless this was specifically recommended by ID (if suppressive therapy is required, the rationale and risks must be weighed).

2) Microbiologic clearance
- Multiple consecutive negative blood cultures after initiation of targeted therapy (in practice ≥2 sets demonstrating clearance; obtain cultures as directed by ID).  
- Completion of an appropriate, guideline‑recommended antimicrobial course for E. faecalis PVE (the case patient received a 6‑week IV ampicillin‑based regimen with oral follow‑up). Obtain formal ID clearance before reintervention.

3) Imaging exclusion of active infection and complications
- Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE/TOE) demonstrating no vegetations, no new flail leaflets, no paravalvular leak due to infection, and no peri‑annular abscess or fistula. The case team used TEE to confirm absence of active endocarditis prior to reintervention.  
- Cross‑sectional imaging (multislice CT) to exclude periannular extension, pseudoaneurysm, fistula and to document stent/frame integration. MSCT is also essential for procedural planning (coronary heights, valve‑to‑coronary distance, frame interaction) as was performed in the reported patient.  
- Consider 18F‑FDG PET/CT when TEE/MSCT are inconclusive for prosthetic infection (useful adjunct per multidisciplinary discussion).

4) Multidisciplinary endorsement
- Formal ID sign‑off and heart‑team discussion (interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, imaging, anesthesia). The reported decision was made by a multidisciplinary team and involved shared decision‑making.

How to decide timing
- Prefer deferral until infection is controlled and the patient has completed the recommended antimicrobial regimen with documented negative blood cultures and reassuring imaging. In most nonurgent situations this means waiting until end of therapy and ID clearance.  
- Exceptions: urgent intervention (surgical or transcatheter) is warranted if there is uncontrolled infection, prosthetic dehiscence, severe paravalvular leak, large mobile vegetations at high embolic risk, or life‑threatening heart failure—in those scenarios early surgery is standard; transcatheter options are considered only when surgery is prohibitive and infection is controlled or cannot be controlled by medical therapy alone.  
- Practical approach used in the case: the patient completed a full 6‑week IV course, had negative cultures and TEE/MSCT showing no active infection, and only then underwent CT‑guided TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV planning and implantation months later. That sequence — microbiologic and imaging clearance followed by careful CT‑guided planning and heart‑team approval — is the prudent model.

Peri‑procedural considerations
- Continue standard peri‑procedural antibiotic prophylaxis as advised by ID.  
- Maintain close post‑procedural surveillance for recurrent infection (serial clinical assessment, blood cultures if febrile, targeted imaging when indicated).

Bottom line
Do not implant a new transcatheter valve while PVE is active. Proceed only after clinical resolution, documented microbiologic clearance, and imaging exclusion of residual infection/annular complications, with ID and heart‑team clearance and CT‑guided procedural planning — exactly the pathway that permitted the successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV in the reported patient.

### [Clinical Pearls] In a triple-stented aortic root, which valve design features (e.g., supra-annular leaflet position, frame height) and implantation depth strategies help maximize effective orifice area and preserve future coronary access?

Bottom line answer
- To maximize effective orifice area (EOA) and preserve coronary access in a triple‑stented root, choose a valve with supra‑annular leaflets, relatively low frame height and open/larger cell geometry, and deploy it high — at or above the plane of the prior valve leaflets — with meticulous commissural alignment and targeted post‑dilation. These features minimize creation of an additional “neo‑skirt,” pin prior leaflets outward, leave larger windows for catheter passage, and reduce prosthesis–patient mismatch.

Why these features matter (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- Supra‑annular leaflet position places the functioning valve orifice above the annulus and above stacked leaflets of prior valves, so the new leaflets operate in a larger effective flow stream—improving EOA compared with intra‑annular designs that sit inside the narrowed prior frames.
- Low frame height and large/open cells reduce the amount of metal and fabric that overlies the sinuses and coronary ostia. Less frame/skirt material across the sinuses preserves sinus washout, lowers the risk of coronary obstruction, and leaves larger cells for future coronary cannulation.
- High implantation (positioning the new valve at the level of or slightly above the prior valve leaflet plane) pins older leaflets outward against the stent frame rather than letting them fold inward into the orifice (which worsens EOA), and avoids building another skirt beneath the coronaries that would impinge coronary flow or access.
- Commissural alignment orients the new valve posts away from the coronary ostia and the larger cell windows, facilitating future coronary access and reducing the chance that a post or skirt will directly cover a coronary ostium.
- Post‑dilation can expand previously implanted frames (improving central flow and leaflet mobility) and optimize expansion of the new valve, but must be balanced against the risks of annular injury or embolization.

How the case illustrates these principles
- The team selected an Acurate neo2 for the third implant specifically because of its supra‑annular leaflet design, relatively low frame height and favorable haemodynamics in narrow anatomies.
- Preprocedural MSCT modelling guided the strategy: measurements of valve‑to‑coronary distance, sinus dimensions and interaction of frames showed feasibility of a high implantation without coronary protection.
- The valve was implanted high—at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets—intentionally to pin the prior leaflets outward, minimise a neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia and preserve coronary access. Commissural alignment was targeted at deployment using CT and fluoroscopic landmarks.
- Balloon post‑dilation of the stack likely produced partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame and improved valve opening; this contributed to the sustained improvement in gradients over follow‑up.
- The procedure achieved preserved coronary perfusion and acceptable haemodynamics at 1 year, despite an initially high immediate gradient that fell after post‑dilation and early remodeling—illustrating that the combination of device selection, high implantation and post‑dilation can optimize EOA in triple‑stented anatomy.

Practical steps for planning and execution
1. Preprocedure MSCT: quantify coronary heights, sinus dimensions, valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC), prior valve frame geometry/cell size and the plane of prior leaflet nadirs. CT modelling is essential.
2. Device selection: prefer supra‑annular leaflet valves with low frame height and larger cell/window geometry when anatomy allows (to maximize EOA and facilitate reaccess).
3. Implant depth: target a high/supra‑annular implantation—at or slightly above the prior leaflet plane—so prior leaflets are pinned outward and you avoid creating a new skirt below the coronaries.
4. Commissural alignment: use CT planning and device markers to orient the valve so stent posts and skirt are away from coronary ostia.
5. Coronary protection: if VTC or sinus size is borderline, be prepared with coronary protection or BASILICA strategies; avoid assuming safety without CT confirmation.
6. Post‑dilation: consider controlled balloon post‑dilation to improve expansion of both the new and prior frames, accepting trade‑offs and monitoring for complications.

Caveats and trade‑offs
- High implantation must be balanced against risk of valve embolization or paravalvular leak if anchoring is inadequate.
- Even with ideal device choice and depth, triple frames increase mesh burden and may still complicate future coronary interventions; long‑term planning and documentation of commissural orientation are important.
- When CT shows prohibitive VTC or small sinuses, prophylactic coronary protection or leaflet laceration strategies should be considered rather than relying on high implantation alone.

Clinical pearl
- In complex re‑TAVI stacks, the combination of a supra‑annular, low‑frame valve deployed high with planned commissural alignment—and selective post‑dilation guided by CT modelling—is the most reliable strategy to preserve EOA and maintain coronary access, as illustrated by the successful Acurate neo2 implantation and 1‑year outcome in this case.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Immediately after a third TAVI, a patient has a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm², and DVI 0.24 with no regurgitation, which improves to a mean gradient of 27 mm Hg and AVA 1.1 cm² at 1 month. What are the leading causes of an early high postprocedural gradient after TAV-in-TAV, how would you differentiate among them (e.g., underexpansion, PPM, leaflet thrombosis, flow conditions), and what immediate and short-term management steps are appropriate?

Short answer
The immediate differential for a very high mean gradient right after a TAV‑in‑TAV (mean 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm², DVI 0.24) includes (1) mechanical underexpansion/constrainment of the new prosthesis (including incomplete radial expansion of the new frame or inwardly displaced leaflets from prior frames), (2) true prosthesis–patient mismatch (an anatomically small effective orifice after layering of frames), (3) early leaflet thrombosis (HALT with reduced leaflet motion), and (4) reversible flow/measurement factors (low stroke volume, tachycardia, pressure‑recovery or Doppler mismeasurement). In the presented case the most likely explanation was mechanical underexpansion/PPM with subsequent partial mechanical expansion of the frames after implantation (made plausible by use of a self‑expanding Acurate neo2, balloon post‑dilatation and documented reduction of gradient to 27 mm Hg at 1 month).

How to distinguish them at the bedside and with imaging
- First step: confirm the numbers and physiology. Repeat Doppler/TTE measurements (align beam, measure VTI, continuity equation, DVI) and calculate stroke‑volume index (SVi). DVI <0.25 and AVA <1.0 cm² indicate severe obstruction, but interpretation requires SVi: if SVi <35 ml/m² you may have low‑flow physiology that alters gradients.
- Underexpansion / constrained frame:
  - Clues: immediate high gradient after implantation, fluoroscopic evidence of reduced frame diameter or restricted leaflet excursion, small intraluminal diameter on CT/MSCT compared with the expected valve diameter.
  - Diagnostic test: gated contrast CT to measure stent inner diameter and leaflet opening; fluoroscopy or TEE to assess leaflet excursion.
  - Behavior: may improve over days–weeks with self‑expanding frames or after balloon expansion; improvement without anticoagulation favors mechanical expansion rather than thrombosis.
- Prosthesis–patient mismatch (true PPM):
  - Clues: small calculated EOA indexed to BSA (iAVA ≤0.65 cm²/m² moderate‑severe), persistent elevation of gradients despite fully expanded frame on CT and mobile leaflets.
  - Diagnostic test: CT/fluoroscopy confirming full frame expansion and TEE showing normal leaflet motion; clinical persistence of high gradient after wait period.
  - Behavior: unlikely to remit without mechanical intervention.
- Leaflet thrombosis (HALT/RLM):
  - Clues: subacute rise in gradient (days–weeks), reduced leaflet motion on echo/fluoroscopy, CT shows hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening.
  - Diagnostic test: MSCT is the gold standard to detect HALT/reduced leaflet motion. Start therapeutic anticoagulation if suspected and confirm with follow‑up CT/TTE.
  - Behavior: typically improves with anticoagulation over weeks.
- Flow/measurement artefact:
  - Clues: discordant AVA/gradient (low gradient but small AVA), tachycardia, transient hypotension/low flow states, or pressure‑recovery effects.
  - Diagnostic steps: reassess hemodynamics, measure SVi, consider invasive catheter gradient if noninvasive data are equivocal.

Immediate and short‑term management principles
1. Verify and risk‑stratify
   - Recheck TTE Doppler and SVi immediately; review fluoroscopy and procedural notes for whether post‑dilatation was performed (it was, here).
   - If doubts persist, obtain intra‑aortic invasive gradients or TEE in the cath lab.
2. Stabilize hemodynamics and optimize loading conditions
   - Address hypovolemia, anemia or tachycardia that might affect Doppler readings; maintain adequate blood pressure and heart rate.
3. If mechanical underexpansion is suspected and the patient is unstable or gradients remain severe and symptomatic:
   - Consider repeat balloon post‑dilatation with coronary protection ready — but only after CT/planning review because in TAV‑in‑TAV configurations the risk of annular/root injury and coronary compromise is higher.
   - In multi‑stented anatomies this maneuver is higher risk and should be weighed carefully.
4. If leaflet thrombosis is suspected:
   - Start therapeutic anticoagulation (unless contraindicated) and obtain MSCT to document HALT/RLM; repeat imaging at 4–6 weeks to document resolution.
5. If the scenario looks like PPM after confirmed full expansion:
   - Medical optimization and close surveillance if patient stable; definitive options are limited in triple‑stented anatomy (surgical explant is high risk; further transcatheter strategies are complex and case‑specific).
6. Watchful waiting is reasonable when the patient is stable and underexpansion of a self‑expanding valve is plausible
   - Self‑expanding frames frequently continue to expand over days–weeks; this was exactly the course in this case (immediate mean gradient 67 mm Hg fell to 27 mm Hg at 1 month without evidence of HALT or regurgitation).
7. Follow‑up surveillance
   - Repeat TTE at 1 month (earlier if symptomatic) and MSCT if gradients fail to improve or there is suspicion of thrombosis or incomplete expansion.
   - If gradient remains high and symptomatic after a reasonable observation window (and CT confirms full expansion), convene heart team for consideration of reintervention vs. high‑risk surgery.

Why the case behaved as it did
- In this patient the team selected a supra‑annular self‑expanding Acurate neo2, performed balloon post‑dilatation, and documented immediate leaflet mobility without regurgitation. The large immediate gradient most likely reflected mechanical constrainment/PPM in a triple‑stented, small intraluminal lumen; subsequent progressive expansion of the new and prior frames and relief of inward leaflet restriction produced the marked hemodynamic improvement at 1 month (mean 27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²). There was no clinical, echocardiographic or management signal (anticoagulation and CT findings) suggesting leaflet thrombosis in this report.

Take‑home pearls
- Immediate high gradients after TAV‑in‑TAV commonly reflect mechanical underexpansion or PPM; thrombosis is an important but usually subacute cause that should be sought with MSCT.
- Differentiate causes with careful Doppler assessment (including SVi and DVI), fluoroscopy/TEE, and gated CT; management options (observe, anticoagulate, balloon post‑dilate, or re‑intervene) depend on the diagnosis, patient stability and anatomical risks.
- In self‑expanding valves constrained by prior frames, a period of observation is often justified because passive expansion can markedly improve gradients, as demonstrated by this patient.

### [Clinical Pearls] Define prosthesis–patient mismatch after TAVI: What threshold values for indexed effective orifice area and Doppler velocity index indicate severe mismatch, and how do these thresholds influence planning for redo-TAVI to avoid recurrent PPM?

Clinical pearl — definition and thresholds
- Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) occurs when the prosthetic effective orifice area (EOA) is too small for the patient’s body size, producing abnormally high transvalvular gradients despite a well‑seated valve. The diagnostic standard is the EOA indexed to body‑surface area (iEOA).
- Conventional thresholds: moderate PPM = iEOA 0.65–0.85 cm²/m²; severe PPM = iEOA ≤0.65 cm²/m². (Some authors apply slightly lower cut‑offs in obese patients or in valve‑in‑valve contexts, but ≤0.65 cm²/m² is the widely accepted benchmark.)
- Doppler velocity (dimensionless) index (DVI = VTI_LVOT/VTI_AV or peak LVOT velocity/peak transvalvular velocity) is a useful corroborative measure when EOA is uncertain. A low DVI indicates obstruction: values <0.35 suggest clinically important obstruction and values ≲0.25–0.30 correlate with severe prosthetic stenosis/PPM. (In the presented case a DVI of 0.24 and an iEOA of 0.4 cm²/m² were interpreted as severe PPM.)

Why these numbers matter (pathophysiology)
- PPM is simply a mismatch between orifice area and flow demand. A small iEOA produces higher velocities and gradients, promotes adverse left‑ventricular remodeling and symptoms, and adversely affects long‑term outcome after valve replacement. In redo‑TAVI (TAV‑in‑TAV or more), stacked frames and constrained internal diameters make achieving an adequate iEOA especially challenging.

How thresholds influence planning for redo‑TAVI — practical principles
1. Predict iEOA before you commit
   - Use MSCT to measure the true internal diameter of the existing prosthesis and apply valve‑in‑valve (ViV) prediction tools (manufacturer EOA tables, ViV app, published iEOA estimates) to forecast the post‑implant iEOA and DVI.
   - If predicted iEOA ≤0.65 cm²/m² (or predicted DVI in the low range), you must assume severe PPM risk and alter strategy.

2. Select valve and implantation technique to maximize EOA
   - Prefer devices with supra‑annular leaflet position (tend to give larger EOA for a given internal diameter).
   - Choose the largest valve that safely fits the internal frame and coronary anatomy.
   - Plan commissural alignment and a high implant to avoid pushing old leaflets inward and creating an additional neo‑skirt that reduces EOA (as in this case: high implantation and alignment to preserve orifice).

3. Use adjunctive maneuvers when needed
   - Consider bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) where the old frame is fracture‑amenable and coronary obstruction risk is acceptable — BVF can increase internal diameter and projected iEOA.
   - Aggressive balloon post‑dilatation can expand prior frames (as happened here) and improve gradients, but balance against annular injury and coronary risk.
   - If predicted residual iEOA remains unacceptably low despite these options, discuss surgical explantation rather than proceeding with a redo‑TAVI that will produce severe, symptomatic PPM.

4. Balance PPM risk against coronary and infection risks
   - In complex anatomies (multiple frames, prior PVE), preserving coronary access and avoiding obstruction may limit your ability to up‑size or fracture frames. Multidisciplinary decision‑making is essential.

Application to the case
- The team anticipated the PPM risk and selected a supra‑annular Acurate neo2, performed high implantation, commissural alignment and post‑dilatation to maximize EOA. Immediate post‑implant hemodynamics showed severe PPM (iEOA 0.4 cm²/m², DVI 0.24), but further mechanical expansion and early remodeling produced marked improvement (mean gradient ~26–27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²) by 1 month and sustained at 1 year. This illustrates both the importance of predicting PPM and the practical steps (valve choice, implant height, post‑dilatation, and CT‑driven planning) used to avoid or mitigate recurrent severe PPM in redo‑TAVI.

### [Management] For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis in an elderly TAVI recipient with renal vulnerability, what antibiotic regimens and durations are guideline-concordant, and in what situations is long-term suppressive therapy indicated or not?

Bottom line
- For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) in an elderly patient with renal vulnerability, guideline‑concordant therapy is 6 weeks of bactericidal, synergy‑based therapy; the preferred regimen in older or renally vulnerable patients is ampicillin plus ceftriaxone (both IV) for 6 weeks rather than an aminoglycoside‑containing regimen. Aminoglycoside (eg, gentamicin) can provide synergy but is generally avoided or limited to the first 1–2 weeks in patients at risk for nephrotoxicity. Long‑term suppressive antibiotics are reserved only for exceptional situations (persistent infection or when infection cannot be eradicated); they are not indicated when a full course of IV therapy has sterilized the blood, clinical/imaging follow‑up shows resolution, and the patient is clinically well — as in the presented case.

Why these regimens
- Enterococcus faecalis is relatively tolerant to beta‑lactams alone; killing depends on paired mechanisms that achieve synergy. Two accepted, guideline‑supported approaches for ampicillin‑susceptible E. faecalis PVE:
  - Ampicillin (eg, 2 g IV q4h or equivalent continuous infusion) plus ceftriaxone (eg, 2 g IV q12h) for 6 weeks. This dual‑beta‑lactam combination gives reliable synergistic activity without the nephrotoxicity of aminoglycosides and is the regimen of choice in elderly patients and those with renal impairment.
  - Ampicillin (same dosing) plus gentamicin (synergy) can be used, but because gentamicin causes nephrotoxicity it is generally avoided in frail/renally vulnerable patients; if used it is usually limited to the first 1–2 weeks only, while ampicillin is continued for 6 weeks.
- If the isolate is ampicillin‑resistant or the patient is severely beta‑lactam‑allergic, alternatives depend on susceptibility: vancomycin (with dosing/monitoring) or for vancomycin‑resistant enterococci (VRE) agents such as linezolid or high‑dose daptomycin ± combination therapy guided by ID and susceptibility testing.

Duration and monitoring
- Prosthetic‑valve infection requires a prolonged course: at least 6 weeks of definitive IV therapy is standard for enterococcal PVE.
- Monitor clinical status, serial blood cultures until negative, renal function, and repeat echocardiography (TEE preferred) and/or CT when indicated to document resolution and exclude paravalvular complications.

When to consider long‑term (chronic) suppressive therapy
- Indications to consider indefinite (or prolonged) oral suppressive therapy are limited and include:
  - Infection that cannot be definitively eradicated (eg, retained infected prosthetic material that cannot be removed and persistent or relapsing bacteremia despite appropriate therapy),
  - Recurrent/progressive infection in a patient who is not a surgical candidate,
  - Persistent cultures or imaging evidence of ongoing infection after an appropriate course.
- Suppressive therapy is not routine when: the patient has completed a guideline‑directed 6‑week IV course, blood cultures are sterile, symptoms have resolved, and TEE/CT show no active infection — in that situation chronic suppression confers little benefit and exposes the patient to toxicity, resistance, and drug interactions.

How this applies to the case
- The patient in the report received a 6‑week IV ampicillin‑based course for E. faecalis PVE, followed by oral therapy per ID; at the time of reintervention there were negative follow‑up blood cultures, TEE and MSCT without vegetations or paravalvular complications and no ongoing suppressive antibiotics. That approach is consistent with guideline principles: a full IV course with documented clinical and microbiologic resolution obviates routine long‑term suppression. Given his age and renal vulnerability, avoidance of prolonged aminoglycoside exposure (and choosing ampicillin + ceftriaxone when appropriate) is the safer, guideline‑concordant strategy.

Practical points for the team
- Discuss antibiotic strategy with ID early; obtain susceptibilities including high‑level aminoglycoside resistance.
- For elderly/renal‑vulnerable patients favour ampicillin + ceftriaxone over ampicillin + gentamicin.
- Ensure blood cultures are repeatedly negative and TEE (or CT) demonstrates resolution before forgoing suppressive therapy.
- Reserve lifelong suppressive antibiotics for true inability to eradicate prosthetic infection or recurrent/persistent disease despite optimal therapy.

### [Management] When planning coronary protection during TAV-in-TAV (e.g., guidewire protection, chimney stenting, BASILICA), which anatomic constellations mandate protection, and how does the presence of a prior supra-annular self-expanding frame alter the risk-benefit assessment?

Direct answer
The anatomic features that mandate active coronary protection during TAV‑in‑TAV are the same principals used for valve‑in‑valve and TAVR planning—but the thresholds should be treated more conservatively because a prior transcatheter valve creates a “neo‑skirt” and additional frame layers. In practice you should plan protection when any of the following are present on gated CT/3‑D modelling:

- Low coronary ostial height (commonly cited cutoff <10–12 mm)
- Small sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameter (high risk when SOV <30–32 mm)
- Small virtual valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance after modelling (high risk when VTC <4 mm; intermediate risk 4–6 mm)
- Short or tall prior prosthetic leaflets that extend at or above the coronary ostia, or leaflet length > coronary height
- High frame height of the existing valve(s), or frame/leaflet overlap that projects material directly in front of the ostium
- Narrow sinotubular junction (STJ) or evidence that the STJ will not accommodate displaced leaflets/frame (small STJ height/diameter)
- Poor or unpredictable commissural alignment such that leaflets or frame cells will likely sit in front of the coronary takeoff

How a prior supra‑annular self‑expanding frame (eg, Evolut) changes the assessment
A supra‑annular, self‑expanding frame substantially raises the risk and complicates the choice of protection for three reasons:

1) Greater obstruction risk: self‑expanding supra‑annular valves have taller frames and leaflet tissue positioned above the native annulus. When a new valve is implanted inside them the old leaflets are pushed upward and can form a high neo‑skirt that is more likely to impinge on coronaries and the STJ than leaflets from intra‑annular or low‑frame valves. That effectively lowers your safety margins (VTC and coronary height thresholds become more “fragile”).

2) Reduced effectiveness/ease of leaflet laceration (BASILICA): the presence of an upstream nitinol frame and multiple leaflets may make traversal and controlled laceration more technically difficult. Laceration may not achieve adequate leaflet splay if the leaflet is constrained against a rigid frame, and in multi‑frame scenarios “double” laceration or complex maneuvers may be needed; technical success and durability are less certain than in native valve or single bioprosthesis BASILICA series.

3) Tradeoffs for chimney stenting and wiring: pre‑positioning a guidewire and chimney stent remains a reliable bailout, but stent expansion and long‑term patency can be compromised by external compression from multiple metal frames; chronic coronary access can be further impaired in layered stents. Guidewire‑only protection (wire + jailed catheter) is fast and low‑risk but only provides a salvage option rather than prevention.

Putting this into practice (recommended approach)
- Always perform high‑resolution CT with virtual valve‑to‑coronary modelling before TAV‑in‑TAV. If VTC <4 mm, coronary height <10–12 mm, SOV <30–32 mm, or leaflet anatomy predicts obstruction → consider upfront active protection.
- If anatomy and CT suggest leaflets are laceratable and there is an experienced operator/team → BASILICA (or bioprosthetic BASILICA) is preferred to prevent obstruction and preserve long‑term coronary access because it creates a predictable opening rather than leaving a permanently jailed stent in the sinus.
- If BASILICA is judged unlikely to succeed (supra‑annular frame constraining the leaflet, inability to traverse leaflet, or multi‑frame geometry) → plan chimney stent(s) or upfront coronary wiring with stent preloaded to permit immediate deployment. Be mindful of stent compression risk and future access issues.
- For intermediate risk (VTC 4–6 mm, borderline SOV), consider guidewire protection only (wire + guiding catheter or balloon) so you can rapidly stent if needed—this is what many teams do when CT is reassuring but not perfect.
- If CT modelling shows adequate clearance (VTC comfortably >6 mm, good coronary height, roomy SOV/STJ), a well‑planned valve choice, high implantation and commissural alignment may allow you to proceed without protection—as in the presented case where MSCT modelling, selection of a relatively low frame Acurate neo2 and high implant strategy preserved coronary flow and the operators performed the procedure without prophylactic protection.

Case tie‑in
In this report the team used CT modelling to document acceptable valve‑to‑coronary distances and SOV/STJ anatomy despite two prior valves (Lotus then a supra‑annular Evolut). They intentionally chose a low‑frame, supra‑annular‑favourable prosthesis (Acurate neo2), aimed for a high implant and attempted commissural alignment to minimise a neo‑skirt below the coronaries. Given those favourable CT findings and the expected technical challenges lacerating leaflets behind an Evolut frame, they proceeded without routine prophylactic BASILICA or chimney—an approach justified by the imaging and by the known limitations of BASILICA/chimney in multi‑frame anatomy—and achieved preserved coronary flow and good clinical outcome.

Bottom line
Use CT virtual modelling to identify low coronary height, small SOV, and VTC <4 mm as anatomy that mandates protection. A prior supra‑annular self‑expanding frame raises the likelihood of obstruction and makes BASILICA and chimney strategies more technically challenging; therefore you should lower your threshold to protect coronaries, favour CT‑guided upfront planning, and choose the protection strategy (BASILICA if feasible, chimney or pre‑positioned stent if not, wire‑only for intermediate risk) that best matches the anatomy and local expertise.

### [Pathophysiology] How can prior infective endocarditis accelerate structural valve deterioration in transcatheter bioprostheses, and what pathophysiologic effects of multiple overlapping frames contribute to late stenosis and prosthesis–patient mismatch in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV?

Direct answer
Prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) accelerates structural valve deterioration (SVD) in transcatheter bioprostheses by causing direct leaflet injury and a vigorous inflammatory/healing response that produces fibrosis, retraction and mechanical restriction of cusps. Multiple overlapping transcatheter frames (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) then compound that injury by mechanically crowding the valve complex, limiting expansion and orifice area, altering flow dynamics and promoting progressive stenosis with prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

Why PVE accelerates SVD (pathophysiology and linkage to the case)
- Infective injury is not just transient infection: organisms (here Enterococcus faecalis) and the host inflammatory response can cause cusp destruction (vegetations, perforation), endothelial denudation and matrix degradation. Even after microbiologic cure, healed lesions commonly show leaflet thickening, fibrosis, scarring and retraction that reduce leaflet mobility and effective orifice area.
- Inflammatory proteases and cytokines accelerate breakdown of collagen/elastin and promote calcification and pannus formation; these processes shorten the functional life of bioprosthetic tissue compared with purely degenerative wear.
- Clinically this was apparent in the case: 3 months after the Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV the patient developed E. faecalis PVE with severe restenosis, and subsequent imaging/pathologic inference cited leaflet fibrosis and mechanical restriction as contributors to failure despite resolved infection.

How multiple overlapping frames produce late stenosis and PPM
- Mechanical crowding and reduced lumen: each additional stent frame occupies annular cross‑section and can prevent full radial expansion of a subsequent valve, reducing the minimum lumen (in this case MSCT showed a 13.7 mm lumen within the Evolut), thereby reducing effective orifice area and increasing gradients.
- Leaflet immobilization and inward folding: prior valve leaflets can be pinned against underlying frames or folded inward by the new frame (creating a “double/neo‑skirt”), which narrows the flow orifice and restricts native/surgical leaflet motion. The team’s strategy of high implantation to fix previous leaflets against the frame illustrates recognition of this mechanism.
- Increased radial stiffness and frame–frame interaction: stacked metallic frames are less compliant, so post‑implant expansion is constrained; this can be partially relieved by aggressive post‑dilation (as in this case), which improved gradients by further expanding the Evolut frame.
- Adverse hemodynamics: smaller orifices produce higher velocities and shear stress, promoting turbulence, cusp thrombosis and pannus deposition — all feed‑forward processes that worsen stenosis.
- Commissural misalignment and mesh burden: cumulative mesh across the sinus can create flow separation, impair valve opening symmetry and complicate coronary access, and can indirectly worsen functional orifice and PPM.
- Clinical correlate: immediately after the third valve implantation the patient had very high gradients (mean 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm2, DVI 0.24) consistent with severe PPM from crowding and restricted expansion; after targeted post‑dilation and optimization the gradient fell to ~26–27 mm Hg and AVA improved to 1.1 cm2, demonstrating that partial relief of frame constraint and optimized implantation can materially improve haemodynamics.

Clinical implications (teaching pearl)
PVE can leave a bioprosthesis structurally compromised even after cure; when planning redo TAVI in a multiply stented aortic root, anticipate reduced EOA from both biological scarring and mechanical crowding. Preprocedural MSCT, commissural alignment, considered implantation depth (eg, high implant to pin leaflets), and planned post‑dilation or adjunct techniques (valve fracture or leaflet laceration in selected cases) are essential to minimise residual stenosis and PPM and to preserve coronary access.

### [Management] In a patient with prior gastrointestinal bleeding and no other indication for anticoagulation undergoing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, what is the preferred postprocedural antithrombotic regimen, and what clinical scenarios would prompt deviation from that plan?

Bottom line answer
- In this patient — prior recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, no other indication for anticoagulation, and a successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV with no active infection — the preferred antithrombotic strategy is single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT), typically low‑dose aspirin (75–100 mg daily) started immediately and continued long‑term, together with gastrointestinal protection (proton‑pump inhibitor) and targeted evaluation/treatment of the bleeding source.
- Deviations from SAPT are prompted by clear clinical indications for oral anticoagulation (eg, new atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, documented valve thrombosis or left‑ventricular thrombus), by recent coronary stenting that mandates dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), by proven transcatheter valve thrombosis or by uncontrolled thrombotic risk despite SAPT. Conversely, in a patient with ongoing high‑risk bleeding unresponsive to therapy, stopping antiplatelet therapy or using an extremely abbreviated course may be appropriate after multidisciplinary discussion.

Why SAPT here
- Randomized and guideline‑directed practice after TAVR has moved away from routine DAPT because DAPT increases clinically important bleeding without clear ischemic benefit in most patients. When there is no independent indication for anticoagulation, SAPT is the default, and in patients with a strong bleeding history the balance favors minimizing antithrombotic intensity.
- In this case the patient has recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding (since 2022) and no ongoing infective endocarditis or other indication for anticoagulation; the team therefore should minimise bleeding risk while protecting against prosthetic‑related thrombosis — SAPT plus PPI achieves that balance.

When to change the plan (clinical scenarios and preferred alternative)
- New, durable indication for anticoagulation (eg, atrial fibrillation, recent VTE, LV thrombus): start oral anticoagulation and generally stop SAPT unless a specific short‑term combination is required. Choice of agent (VKA versus DOAC) should follow the usual indication‑specific guidance; for suspected/confirmed bioprosthetic valve thrombosis many teams prefer VKA (INR 2.0–3.0) though DOACs are used depending on indication and local practice.
- Documented or strongly suspected transcatheter valve thrombosis or hypoattenuated leaflet thickening with clinical sequelae: treat with therapeutic anticoagulation (often VKA) rather than antiplatelet therapy.
- Recent coronary stent (especially within the past months) requiring DAPT: use the shortest effective DAPT duration (co‑managed with interventional cardiology), then step down to SAPT; if bleeding risk is high, strategies such as clopidogrel‑only or abbreviated DAPT may be considered case‑by‑case.
- Extremely high bleeding risk with recurrent uncontrolled GI bleeding: consider withholding antiplatelet therapy after multidisciplinary review (cardiology, GI, hematology), focus on definitive GI interventions (endoscopy, PPI, H. pylori testing, interventional therapies) and close surveillance.
- Persistent or relapsing infective endocarditis or when long‑term suppressive antibiotics are planned: antithrombotic decisions should be individualized in consultation with infectious diseases; active infection may alter timing of initiation but does not automatically mandate anticoagulation.

Practical points for this patient
- Continue aspirin monotherapy long‑term rather than routine DAPT.
- Start/continue a proton‑pump inhibitor and arrange GI input to address the recurrent bleeding source (optimize H. pylori testing, endoscopic therapy if indicated, evaluate antiplatelet necessity).
- Document the absence of atrial fibrillation/other OAC indications and re‑assess rhythm surveillance during follow‑up.
- Educate the patient about signs of bleeding and plan early echocardiographic surveillance; if clinical concern for valve thrombosis arises, obtain diagnostic imaging (TTE ± 4D CT or transesophageal echo) and escalate to anticoagulation if confirmed.
- Decisions that deviate from SAPT should be made in a multidisciplinary heart‑team context, balancing thrombotic versus bleeding risks and patient preferences.

In short: minimize antithrombotic intensity in this bleeding‑prone patient — lifelong aspirin plus GI protection — and reserve anticoagulation or DAPT for clear, guideline‑based indications or proven valve thrombosis after multidisciplinary discussion.

### [Morning Report] For a patient with two prior transcatheter valves and treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, what factors favor surgical explantation with root replacement versus a third TAVI (e.g., infection status, EuroSCORE II, coronary anatomy, risk of coronary obstruction, feasibility of achieving acceptable hemodynamics)?

Short answer
The decision hinges on three domains: infection status, surgical risk and life‑expectancy, and anatomic/haemodynamic feasibility for a safe, durable transcatheter solution. Active or root‑extending prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), paravalvular destruction/abscess or recurrent uncontrolled infection generally push toward surgical explantation and root replacement. By contrast, eradicated infection, prohibitive surgical risk, CT‑demonstrated coronary safety and a reasonable expectation of acceptable effective orifice area (EOA) after valve‑in‑valve permit consideration of a third TAVI.

Clinical reasoning and key factors (what to weigh at heart‑team discussion)

1) Infection status — the overriding first principle
- Surgery favored: active infection (positive blood cultures, ongoing fevers), evidence of root or annular abscess, prosthetic dehiscence, fistula, or uncontrolled sepsis. ESC guidance still prioritises surgery in PVE when there is heart failure or uncontrolled infection. Surgical explantation allows radical debridement and root repair/replacement.
- TAVI may be considered only after proven eradication of infection with no imaging or microbiologic evidence of residual disease and no need for long‑term suppressive therapy. In the reported case the patient completed targeted antibiotics, had negative cultures and TEE/MSCT with no active endocarditis — a necessary precondition for proceeding with another TAVI.

2) Operative risk and global patient context
- Surgery favored when acceptable operative risk, reasonable life expectancy and the morbidity of a complex root/ascending aorta replacement is justified to obtain a durable, definitive result.
- TAVI favored when surgical risk is prohibitive or extremely high. In the case report the EuroSCORE II was 27.36% and the patient had multiple comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, recurrent GI bleeding, prior chest instrumentation and deep stent integration) making open explantation technically hazardous and high‑mortality — a major driver toward repeat TAVI.

3) Anatomy and coronary obstruction risk (preprocedural CT is essential)
- Surgery favored when CT shows root/annular destruction that cannot be managed percutaneously, or when coronary obstruction risk is prohibitive: very low coronary heights (commonly <10–12 mm), small sinus of Valsalva, very short valve‑to‑coronary distances (often <4 mm), bulky leaflet material or when predicted neo‑skirt will cover the coronaries despite protective strategies.
- TAVI favored when CT modelling demonstrates adequate valve‑to‑coronary clearance, sinuses big enough to accommodate the displaced leaflets, and when protective strategies (BASILICA, coronary wiring/protection) are feasible. The case used MSCT to confirm sufficient valve‑to‑coronary distances and elected no prophylactic coronary protection.

4) Feasibility of acceptable haemodynamics / prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM)
- Surgery favored when predicted post‑ViV EOA will be unacceptably small (indexed EOA <0.65 cm²/m² severe PPM), especially in younger patients or those with long life expectancy, as repeated ViV layers progressively reduce lumen and raise gradients.
- TAVI favored when imaging and device selection strategies (supra‑annular design, high implantation, commissural alignment, post‑dilation or planned fracture/expansion of prior frame) can reasonably achieve acceptable gradients. In the report the team chose a supra‑annular Acurate neo2, planned high implantation and post‑dilation; although immediate mean gradient was high, gradients improved at 1 month and remained acceptable at 1 year.

5) Coronary access and future coronary needs
- Surgery favored if future coronary access or concomitant CABG is likely and cannot be achieved after additional frames (extensive CAD with planned surgical grafting).
- TAVI favored if coronary access can be preserved (commissural alignment, favourable frame geometry) and percutaneous coronary therapy remains feasible. The patient had prior multivessel PCI and the team prioritized commissural alignment to preserve future access.

6) Technical considerations specific to multiple prior TAVIs
- Explantation after multiple well‑ingrown frames often requires root and ascending aortic replacement and can carry very high morbidity. This technical difficulty may tip the balance toward percutaneous therapy when infection is cured and anatomy is acceptable.
- Measures to optimize ViV haemodynamics (choice of valve with supra‑annular leaflets, intentional high position, balloon post‑dilation, bioprosthetic valve fracture where applicable) should be planned preprocedurally.

Putting it together for this patient (why third TAVI was chosen)
- Infection: treated and documented resolution (negative cultures, TEE/MSCT without vegetations) — allowed consideration of TAVI.
- Surgical risk: EuroSCORE II 27% and multiple comorbidities made explantation/root replacement prohibitive.
- Anatomy/CT: detailed MSCT showed acceptable valve‑to‑coronary relationships and feasibility of high implantation/commissural alignment to preserve coronaries.
- Expected haemodynamics: choice of a supra‑annular valve (Acurate neo2), high implantation and post‑dilatation made acceptable gradients achievable — and this was borne out clinically (improvement to mean gradient ~26–27 mm Hg, AVA ~1.1 cm² at 1 year).
- Technical difficulty of explantation (deeply integrated frames) meant surgery would likely be more hazardous and less attractive.

Clinical pearl
Always start with infection control: active PVE with root/annular destruction → surgery. If infection is cured, assemble a multidisciplinary heart team, obtain high‑resolution MSCT to quantify coronary height, sinus size and valve‑to‑coronary distances, estimate post‑ViV indexed EOA, and weigh these against surgical risk and life expectancy. When CT predicts coronary safety and acceptable post‑ViV haemodynamics but surgery is prohibitive (as in the reported case), a carefully planned third TAVI with strategies to preserve coronary flow and maximise EOA is a reasonable, guideline‑concordant option.
