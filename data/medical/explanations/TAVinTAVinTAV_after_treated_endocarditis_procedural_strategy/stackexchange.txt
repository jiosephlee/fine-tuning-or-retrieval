Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Clinical Pearls] In an older adult with prior TAVI, new exertional dyspnea, and a mean transaortic gradient of 49 mm Hg with an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm² on transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), what are the echocardiographic criteria that define severe prosthetic aortic valve stenosis?

Short answer: severe prosthetic aortic stenosis is diagnosed by the same Doppler thresholds used for native valves, but interpreted in the context of prosthesis-specific measurement pitfalls and flow status. In practice you use a combination of haemodynamic parameters rather than a single number.

A prosthetic valve is considered severely stenotic if it shows a mean transvalvular gradient ≥40 mm Hg or a peak aortic jet velocity ≥4.0 m/s together with a small effective orifice area (AVA ≤1.0 cm²). Because AVA calculations can be error‑prone with prostheses, supportive measurements are important: a Doppler velocity index (DVI or ratio of LVOT VTI to aortic VTI) ≤0.25 favors severe obstruction, and an indexed AVA (EOA/BSA) markedly reduced (severe prosthesis–patient mismatch is usually defined as indexed EOA ≤0.65 cm²/m²) strengthens the diagnosis. Always assess flow: stroke volume index <35 mL/m² or low‑flow states can produce low‑gradient severe obstruction and require further evaluation (eg, dobutamine stress or corroborating imaging).

Clinical reasoning and caveats from this case: the patient’s mean gradient of 49 mm Hg and AVA 0.5 cm² meet conventional criteria for severe prosthetic stenosis, and the DVI of ~0.24 and an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² confirm severe obstruction/PPM. Because prosthetic measurements are flow‑dependent and LVOT measurement errors are common after TAVI (multiple frames, supra‑annular leaflets), use DVI and indexed EOA to reduce reliance on a single continuity‑equation number, and correlate with symptoms, LV function, and CT imaging to define mechanism (leaflet restriction, frame underexpansion, thrombosis, pannus, or PVE) and plan management.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient with two prior transcatheter aortic valves presents with fever and worsening heart failure; blood cultures grow Enterococcus faecalis and TTE suggests prosthetic valve dysfunction. What components of the modified Duke criteria and adjunct imaging would you use to confirm prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) in this setting?

The diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) in a patient with prior TAVI (and here two prior transcatheter valves) rests on applying the modified Duke criteria while recognising the lower sensitivity of transthoracic imaging for prosthetic devices and the value of adjunct multimodality imaging.

Clinically you would count the following Duke elements first. A sustained bacteremia with Enterococcus faecalis from multiple separate blood culture sets fulfils a major microbiologic criterion. Fever and a predisposing prosthetic valve are supportive minor criteria. If transthoracic echo (TTE) shows prosthetic valve dysfunction only, that is suggestive but not definitive because TTE is insensitive for prosthetic vegetations, paravalvular abscesses and dehiscence.

To confirm PVE in this setting you therefore need one of the following combinations: (1) a major microbiologic criterion (positive cultures) plus a major imaging criterion (definite evidence of endocardial involvement), or (2) the microbiologic major criterion plus enough minor criteria to reach “definite” by Duke (less common). Practically, obtain the imaging that defines a major imaging criterion:

1) Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) — first-line for prosthetic valves. TEE is far more sensitive than TTE for detecting vegetations, prosthetic dehiscence, paravalvular abscess/pseudoaneurysm or new regurgitation; demonstration of any of these is a major Duke imaging criterion and together with the positive Enterococcus blood cultures establishes definite PVE.

2) If TEE is non-diagnostic or equivocal (common with multiple stent frames), use adjunct advanced imaging. CT angiography / multislice CT (MSCT) of the aortic root can identify abscesses, pseudoaneurysm, prosthetic dehiscence and structural complications that TEE may miss and supports the imaging major criterion when concordant. 18F-FDG PET/CT (or radiolabelled leukocyte SPECT if PET unavailable) is recommended for prosthetic valves when TEE is inconclusive or to detect extracardiac septic foci; abnormal focal FDG uptake around the prosthetic sewing ring or frame is now considered a major criterion for PVE in prosthetic devices and, combined with positive blood cultures, converts a probable to a definite diagnosis. PET is particularly useful months to years after implantation where nonspecific postoperative uptake is unlikely.

3) Repeat blood cultures and clinical correlation. Persistent bacteremia despite therapy, new embolic or vascular phenomena, and rising inflammatory markers provide supportive minor criteria and help judge active infection versus treated past infection.

Apply this to the case: the Enterococcus faecalis positive blood cultures already provide a major microbiologic criterion. TTE evidence of prosthetic dysfunction is suggestive but insufficient. You must perform TEE to seek vegetations, paravalvular leak, abscess or dehiscence—if present, that plus the positive cultures makes definite PVE. If TEE is negative or equivocal (likely given layered TAVI frames), obtain MSCT of the aortic root and 18F-FDG PET/CT; focal abnormal uptake or CT-proven abscess/pseudoaneurysm constitutes a major imaging criterion and, with the positive cultures, confirms PVE. Finally, correlate with serial blood cultures, clinical course and inflammatory markers to distinguish active infection from resolved, treated PVE as was done in this patient before reintervention.

### [Management] After completing 6 weeks of targeted therapy for Enterococcus faecalis PVE and demonstrating negative blood cultures and no vegetations on transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), when is it reasonable to consider a transcatheter reintervention for valve dysfunction?

You should only re-intervene once you are confident the infection is controlled — not merely treated. In practical terms that means the patient has completed the full recommended antimicrobial course, blood cultures have remained persistently negative, serial inflammatory markers and clinical signs have stabilized, and repeat imaging (TEE ± MSCT) shows no residual vegetations, abscess or paravalvular complication. In most routine PVE cases this will be at the end of the prescribed antibiotic course (commonly 4–6 weeks for Enterococcus faecalis) and after an observation interval with negative cultures and stable exams; that timing was followed in this case.

Put another way: do the valve intervention only after microbiologic cure and imaging evidence of quiescent infection — unless there is an urgent indication (refractory heart failure, uncontrolled sepsis, embolic phenomena or rapidly progressive paravalvular destruction) that mandates earlier surgery. Even when infection is apparently eradicated, the decision to proceed should be made by the multidisciplinary heart team with input from infectious disease and imaging specialists, with CT/TEE used to confirm absence of active endocarditis and to plan the transcatheter strategy (coronary risk, frame interactions, feasibility of satisfactory haemodynamics).

### [Clinical Pearls] What CT-based measurements are most critical to estimate the risk of coronary obstruction in a TAV-in-TAV or TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, and what threshold values suggest high risk?

The single most important CT-based concept is not a lone number but the predicted spatial relationship between the coronary ostia and the “neo‑skirt” that will be created by the implanted valve(s). Practically, that is best assessed by a small set of CT measurements plus virtual valve implantation/simulation. Measure and report these key parameters and compare them to the summed heights of existing valve leaflets/frames and the planned prosthesis skirt:

1) Coronary ostial height (from annular plane to ostium). Low ostial heights leave less room for displaced leaflets and are high risk. In re‑interventions, a height ≤10–12 mm is concerning; the lower end (≤10 mm) carries the greatest risk.

2) Valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC) or “virtual transcatheter valve to coronary” distance measured on CT after virtual valve implantation (distance from anticipated prosthetic leaflet/skirt plane to the coronary ostium). VTC ≤4 mm is generally considered high risk for obstruction; 4–6 mm is intermediate risk and prompts additional planning (coronary protection, BASILICA), >6 mm is low risk.

3) Sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameter and depth. Small, shallow sinuses provide no room for displaced leaflets. SOV diameter <30–32 mm (and shallow sinus depth) increases obstruction risk.

4) Sinotubular junction (STJ) diameter and height. A low/ narrow STJ can trap leaflets at the coronary level and is a risk factor when STJ diameter is small relative to SOV/annulus.

5) Thickness/height of the existing valve leaflets, stent frame height and skirt position. In valve‑in‑valve and especially TAV‑in‑TAV procedures, the cumulative leaflet/skirt height (and whether prior valve leaflets will be pinned outward toward the ostia) determine whether the ostium will be covered even if ostial height alone looks acceptable.

6) Annulus size/landing zone morphology and coronary takeoff angulation. Severe leaflet calcification, horizontal takeoff, and commissural misalignment that will position a neo‑skirt directly in front of an ostium increase risk.

Clinical thresholds to trigger high‑risk planning: coronary height ≤10–12 mm, VTC ≤4 mm, SOV diameter <30–32 mm (or very shallow sinus depth), or any predicted coverage of the ostium on virtual valve implantation. If any of these are met, treat the case as high risk: plan coronary protection (wires, balloons), consider BASILICA where feasible, or rethink valve choice/implant strategy. Always perform CT-based virtual implantation/3‑D simulation in TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios because ostial compromise often results from frame and leaflet interactions that static single measurements cannot fully capture.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the concept of the 'neo-skirt' in sequential transcatheter aortic valve implantations and how it contributes to coronary obstruction or sinus sequestration.

The “neo‑skirt” is a practical anatomic concept used to predict what happens to the old bioprosthetic leaflets when you implant another transcatheter valve inside them. Instead of being removed, the leaflets of the existing valve are displaced and drape along — and often are pinned to — the new prosthesis frame. That draped leaflet material, together with overlapping stent frames, behaves like a newly formed “skirt” or curtain that can separate the sinuses of Valsalva from the aortic lumen.

Why that matters clinically is straightforward: if that neo‑skirt sits at or above the level of a coronary ostium or if it effectively seals a sinus against the aortic wall, it can obstruct coronary flow or create a sealed pocket with no egress — the so‑called sinus sequestration. The mechanism is mechanical and anatomic, not embolic: displaced leaflet tissue (often thickened or calcified from degeneration or prior infection) and multiple metal frames create a low‑compliance barrier; when the neo‑skirt blocks the native outflow from a sinus, blood cannot replenish the sinus or reach the coronary ostium, producing acute or delayed myocardial ischemia. The risk is amplified when coronary heights are low, sinuses are small, valve‑to‑coronary distances are short, and when multiple valve frames increase the thickness and rigidity of the curtain — exactly the situation encountered with sequential TAV‑in‑TAV (and more so with TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV).

This explains both immediate coronary obstruction at deployment (the displaced leaflets and skirt overlap the ostium) and delayed presentations (progressive sealing of a sinus during cardiac cycles or after frame expansion). It also explains why valve design, implantation depth, and commissural alignment matter: selecting a valve with supra‑annular leaflets or a lower frame height, implanting high to tuck the pinned leaflets below the coronary level, and aligning commissures to avoid covering an ostium all reduce the chance the neo‑skirt will interfere with coronary flow. Preprocedural CT modelling specifically examines where the anticipated neo‑skirt will lie relative to the sinotubular junction and coronary ostia so you can plan strategies such as coronary protection or leaflet laceration (BASILICA) if necessary.

In this case the team explicitly assessed the anticipated neo‑skirt on CT and chose a high implantation with commissural alignment and an Acurate neo2 with favorable frame geometry to minimize a neo‑skirt across the coronary ostia. That CT‑guided strategy is exactly how you mitigate the unique coronary obstruction and sinus sequestration risks that accumulate with each additional stented valve.

### [Morning Report] In a patient with severe prosthetic valve stenosis after TAVI and prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II ~27%), what factors favor TAV-in-TAV reintervention over surgical explantation?

The decision to pursue TAV‑in‑TAV rather than surgical explantation in a patient like this one rests on a balance of procedural feasibility, expected benefit, and the patient’s operative risk. In practical terms, several factors in this case favored a transcatheter re‑intervention over surgery.

First, the patient’s calculated operative risk was prohibitive (EuroSCORE II ≈27%) and he had multiple comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding, prior CABG/PCI and pacemaker). High surgical mortality and the very real likelihood of an extensive, technically demanding operation (explantation of multiple integrated stent frames with possible aortic root/ascending aorta replacement) strongly bias toward a less invasive option when it can reasonably be expected to work.

Second, active infection had been eradicated. Persistent or uncontrolled prosthetic valve endocarditis, paravalvular abscess, valve dehiscence or large residual vegetations would mandate surgery; by contrast, documented resolution of infection on TEE, negative blood cultures and completion of tailored antibiotic therapy made repeat TAVI ethically and clinically acceptable.

Third, detailed anatomic imaging demonstrated procedural feasibility and an acceptable coronary obstruction risk. Multislice CT quantified the residual lumen, valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions and frame relationships and allowed planning for high implantation, commissural alignment and post‑dilatation strategies. When CT shows adequate coronary clearance and the ability to position a new valve to preserve coronary flow and optimise effective orifice area, TAV‑in‑TAV becomes a viable choice.

Fourth, technical considerations suggested a reasonable chance of improved haemodynamics without intolerable patient‑prosthesis mismatch. In this case the team could choose a supra‑annular valve with low frame profile (Acurate neo2), plan commissural alignment and use post‑dilatation to partially expand the prior frame—strategies that together increased the likelihood of acceptable gradients. If imaging suggests that further layering will leave a critically small effective orifice area or irreversible coronary compromise, surgery would be favored; here CT and modelling supported a good haemodynamic outcome.

Fifth, prior interventions had resulted in deep integration of stent frames into the aortic wall. That makes surgical explantation not only higher risk for mortality but also technically complex, with potential need for root reconstruction. This anatomic reality often tips the balance toward a percutaneous approach when infection is controlled and CT planning deems it feasible.

Finally, shared decision‑making and multidisciplinary heart‑team review weighed the marginal procedural risk of a carefully planned TAV‑in‑TAV against the substantial immediate and long‑term risks of open surgery in this patient. The patient’s values and the expectation of symptomatic and LV functional benefit also supported transcatheter reintervention.

In short: prohibitive surgical risk and comorbidity, eradication of infection, CT‑demonstrated anatomic feasibility with low coronary obstruction risk, the ability to select a valve and implantation strategy to preserve or optimise effective orifice area and coronary access, and the technical hazards of explanting multiple integrated frames all favor TAV‑in‑TAV in this scenario. Conversely, ongoing infection, root abscess, prosthetic dehiscence or CT features predicting untenable PPM or coronary compromise would instead direct toward surgery.

### [Management] What is the recommended first-line antimicrobial regimen and typical duration for Enterococcus faecalis PVE in an older adult, and when might ampicillin–ceftriaxone be preferred over ampicillin–gentamicin?

For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic‑valve endocarditis the accepted first‑line antimicrobial approach is combination, bactericidal therapy rather than a single beta‑lactam alone. In practice the two recommended regimens are intravenous ampicillin (or penicillin G if susceptible) plus an aminoglycoside (gentamicin) or, alternatively, dual β‑lactam therapy with ampicillin plus ceftriaxone. The total duration for PVE is 6 weeks of intravenous therapy.

Why two options? Enterococci are relatively tolerant to β‑lactams and require synergistic killing. Historically, ampicillin + gentamicin provided reliable synergy and is effective when the isolate is susceptible and the patient can tolerate an aminoglycoside. However, gentamicin causes dose‑dependent nephro‑ and ototoxicity and many elderly patients have baseline renal impairment or other contraindications. Randomized and observational data and guideline recommendations therefore support ampicillin + ceftriaxone as an equally effective, less nephrotoxic alternative for ampicillin‑susceptible E. faecalis infecting prosthetic valves.

Choose ampicillin–ceftriaxone over ampicillin–gentamicin when any of the following apply: the strain has high‑level aminoglycoside resistance (HLAR), the patient is elderly or has reduced renal function or is at high risk for aminoglycoside toxicity (pre‑existing renal disease, hearing impairment), or prolonged aminoglycoside exposure is undesirable. Also prefer dual β‑lactam if therapeutic gentamicin monitoring cannot be reliably performed. When gentamicin is used for PVE it is generally given for the full 6 weeks (unlike some native‑valve regimens that shorten aminoglycoside duration); if aminoglycoside toxicity develops, switch to ampicillin–ceftriaxone.

Practical points: confirm organism susceptibility to ampicillin and ceftriaxone before choosing dual β‑lactam, monitor renal function and drug levels as indicated, obtain repeat blood cultures to document clearance, and coordinate with surgery/infectious‑disease for duration and any need for suppressive therapy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Immediately after a third TAVI, TTE shows a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 0.9 cm² (indexed 0.4 cm²/m²), and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 without regurgitation. What diagnoses should be considered for a high immediate post-implant gradient, and which findings favor prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM)?

When you see a very high mean gradient immediately after TAVI (67 mm Hg here) the differential is centered on acute mechanical problems versus a true small effective orifice (PPM). The immediate possibilities to consider are: acute device underexpansion or malposition (including implantation too high or too low), interference or obstruction of the new leaflets by the previous prosthetic leaflets or frame (leaflet entrapment or leaflet restriction), an acute transvalvular obstruction from trapped tissue/vegetation or thrombus (less common immediately but possible), functional measurement error (incorrect LVOT diameter or Doppler misalignment), and true prosthesis–patient mismatch because the final effective orifice is too small for the patient’s body size.

Distinguishing features that favour prosthesis–patient mismatch in this case
The findings that point toward PPM rather than an isolated acute technical failure are the markedly reduced valve area by continuity equation (AVA 0.9 cm2, indexed AVA 0.4 cm2/m2), the very low Doppler velocity index (DVI 0.24 — values <0.35 indicate severe obstruction and <0.25 strong evidence of severe PPM/obstruction), the absence of regurgitation (rules against a significant paravalvular leak altering gradients), and the known anatomy: multiple overlapping frames with a small residual lumen (MSCT minimum lumen 13.7 mm) that mechanically limit the achievable effective orifice. Those features together make a fixed small EOA the most likely explanation.

What argues against PPM and toward a reversible mechanical cause
Immediate improvement after post‑dilation or over the first weeks (as occurred here, gradients falling to 27 mm Hg by 1 month) suggests that acute underexpansion or radial expansion of prior frames contributed to the high gradient. Evidence of restricted leaflet motion on fluoroscopy/TEE or a visible malposition would point to a technical/positional problem rather than intrinsic PPM. Acute thrombus or trapped tissue would usually be suggested by focal reduced leaflet excursion and might respond to anticoagulation.

Practical approach
Confirm measurements (LVOT diameter and VTI alignment), inspect valve frame and leaflet motion on fluoroscopy/TEE, review procedural record for implantation depth and ballooning, and correlate with CT if available. If underexpansion or malposition is suspected, careful post‑dilation can improve gradients; if fixed PPM from an irreducibly small EOA caused by multiple frames is the dominant mechanism, expect persistent elevated gradients and manage according to symptoms and haemodynamics. In this patient the combination of low indexed AVA, low DVI, no regurgitation and a triple‑stented small lumen strongly supports PPM as the principal diagnosis, while the later gradient improvement indicates a component of immediate underexpansion that was at least partially relieved by ballooning and mechanical expansion of prior frames.

### [Clinical Pearls] List the echocardiographic thresholds that support severe PPM after TAVI and explain how DVI helps distinguish obstruction severity.

Think of prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) after TAVI as a physiologic obstruction: you diagnose it by combining gradients, valve area (indexed), and a flow-independent Doppler ratio. Relying on any single number is risky after TAVI because LVOT sizing is often inaccurate when multiple frames are present.

Echocardiographic thresholds that support severe PPM after TAVI
- Indexed effective orifice area (iEOA) by continuity equation ≤0.65 cm2/m2 is broadly used to define severe PPM (moderate 0.65–0.85 cm2/m2). Some groups use ≤0.60 cm2/m2 for obese patients, but ≤0.65 is standard.  
- Absolute AVA: ≤0.8–0.9 cm2 is consistent with severe obstruction (useful when indexing is not available).  
- Mean transvalvular gradient: a mean gradient >40 mm Hg generally indicates severe prosthetic stenosis; gradients in the 20–40 mm Hg range require integration with iEOA and clinical context.  
- Doppler Velocity Index (DVI): ≤0.25 indicates severe obstruction (0.25–0.35 moderate; >0.35 more consistent with normal prosthetic function or mild obstruction).

How DVI helps distinguish obstruction severity (and why it is helpful after TAVI)
The DVI (velocity time integral or peak velocity ratio of LVOT to transvalvular jet) is essentially independent of anatomic LVOT area because it is a ratio of velocities. After TAVI—especially in valve‑in‑valve or multi‑frame situations where measuring LVOT diameter is unreliable—continuity‑equation AVA can be falsely low. A low DVI (≤0.25) confirms true hemodynamic obstruction irrespective of LVOT measurement error and thus reinforces a diagnosis of severe PPM or prosthetic stenosis. Conversely, if transvalvular gradients are high but DVI is preserved (>0.35), consider high flow states, hypertension, or measurement error rather than true fixed obstruction.

Apply this to the case
This patient’s immediate post‑implant TTE showed mean gradient 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm2 with indexed AVA 0.4 cm2/m2 and DVI 0.24 — a concordant set of findings (high gradient, very low iEOA, DVI ≤0.25) that indicate severe prosthesis obstruction/PPM. Over follow‑up the gradient fell and AVA/iEOA improved, consistent with partial mechanical expansion and improved flow after post‑dilation — but the original immediate post‑implant numbers met accepted thresholds for severe PPM.

### [Pathophysiology] How can high implantation of a third transcatheter valve 'pin' the leaflets of the previously implanted valve and improve the effective orifice area in a triple-stented anatomy?

Think of the failing double-stented valve as three concentric components: a rigid or semi-rigid outer frame, the leaflets of the older prosthesis that can be stiffened, folded or prolapsed inward, and the second-frame scaffolding that constrains geometry of the orifice. In this case the single biggest physiological problem was inward leaflet restriction of the previously implanted valve(s), which effectively narrowed the flow lumen despite the presence of two frames around it.

High implantation of the new valve changes the geometry in two mechanically important ways. First, by positioning the new stent and its sewing skirt at or slightly above the level of the existing prosthetic leaflets, the new frame physically “pins” those leaflets outward against the inner surface of the older frame. That tethering converts previously flail, folded or immobile leaflets from obstructing tissue into contributions to the new valve’s orifice perimeter, restoring a more central and fixed flow channel. Second, when the new valve is supra‑annular (leaflets sit above the annulus) and is expanded at the leaflet level of the prior device, its radial force and post‑dilation can partially re-expand the constrained inner frame (the Evolut in this case), increasing the minimal lumen diameter and relieving a component of the stenosis.

CT planning and fluoroscopic alignment matter because you need to predict where the old leaflets sit relative to the coronaries and to choose an implantation depth that will pin leaflets without creating a neo‑skirt that covers coronary ostia. Commissural alignment helps orient the new frame so its leaflets open in the largest available flow window and preserve coronary access. Balloon pre‑ and post‑dilation augment this process by: separating fused leaflet tissue, optimizing expansion of the new valve, and transmitting radial force to the prior frame to increase lumen area.

Mechanistically then, the immediate improvement in effective orifice area after a high, supra‑leaflet implant reflects a combination of leaflet immobilization (pinning), restoration of a coaxial central orifice by the new valve’s leaflets, and mechanical partial expansion of the constrained prior frame. Those effects reduce prosthesis‑patient mismatch and improve gradients — provided coronary flow is preserved and the landing zone was correctly planned on CT.

### [Management] What are the anatomic and device design features of a supra-annular, self-expanding prosthesis (e.g., Acurate neo2) that make it attractive in a small, constrained transcatheter valve frame during TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV?

The attractive elements of a supra‑annular, self‑expanding valve such as the Acurate neo2 in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV situation flow from two linked goals: preserve effective orifice area in a critically constrained lumen and preserve coronary access while safely seating a new valve inside layered frames.

Anatomically, supra‑annular leaflet position places the functional valve orifice above the sewing/stent frames. That shifts the flow‑limiting leaflet coaptation point into a larger diameter portion of the implanted stack than an intra‑annular leaflet would occupy, so for any given internal lumen the supra‑annular design tends to yield a larger effective orifice area and lower transvalvular gradients — a critical advantage when the Evolut lumen here was reduced to ~14 mm. A shorter ventricular skirt and lower degree of intrusion into the LVOT also minimises additional obstruction below the annulus and reduces the size of a “neo‑skirt” that can impinge on the coronaries.

From a device‑design standpoint, the self‑expanding nitinol frame provides continuous, moderate radial force that can conform to and partially remodel an existing transcatheter frame without the abrupt outward stress of a balloon‑expandable device. That allows safer expansion within a previously constrained stent and, in practice, can nudge leaflets of the prior valve outward to improve leaflet motion and central flow (as likely happened here after post‑dilation). Many self‑expanding supra‑annular valves are designed with an open cell geometry and defined commissural markers, which facilitates intentional commissural alignment at deployment to preserve coronary ostia and future coronary access — an essential consideration when multiple metal layers lie between the aorta and the coronaries. The lower overall frame height and reduced metal burden of some supra‑annular designs, compared with tall nitinol frames that extend well above the sinuses, also reduce the risk that the new valve’s skirt will cover coronary ostia or that the cumulative mesh will prohibit catheter access later.

Finally, practical procedural features matter: controlled, top‑down deployment and predictable final position allow deliberate high implantation to trap prior leaflets outward and keep the neo‑skirt below the coronary level, and the compatibility of the design with post‑dilation lets operators optimise expansion without excessive trauma. The trade‑off is that any valve in a triple‑stented anatomy still risks prosthesis‑patient mismatch and impaired coronary access; supra‑annular, self‑expanding designs simply maximise the chances of an acceptable haemodynamic result while minimising additional obstruction and preserving access — exactly the objectives guiding valve choice and high implantation in this patient.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which preprocedural imaging steps are essential to preserve future coronary access when planning a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, and how does commissural alignment facilitate this?

The single most important imaging task before a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is to move beyond simple annular sizing and instead perform a coronary-focused, CT‑based “what‑if” simulation that defines the three‑dimensional relationship between the stacked frames, the sinuses and the coronary ostia. Practically, that means using gated multislice CT (with thin slices and preferably contrast coronary phases) to measure ostial heights, sinus of Valsalva diameters, sinotubular junction size and—critically—the valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distances for the anticipated landing zone of the new valve. You must also reconstruct the prior prostheses (frame height, cell geometry and true inner lumen), identify the location of prior commissures and commissural posts on 3‑D reconstructions, and perform virtual valve implantation at planned depths and rotations so you can predict the neo‑skirt location and residual “open” frame cells over each coronary ostium.

From these CT data you decide device type, size and implantation depth (eg, a lower‑height or supra‑annular valve and a higher implant to push previous leaflets outward), whether coronary protection is needed, and whether adjuncts (BASILICA, valve fracture) might be necessary. Complementary imaging steps are coronary CT angiography to define the ostia and prior stent/PCI anatomy, transesophageal echo for active endocarditis exclusion and intra‑procedure angiography to confirm flow during test inflation or balloon dilatation.

Commissural alignment is the mechanical means by which the CT plan is translated into preserved coronary access. By orienting the new valve so its commissural posts sit between, not in front of, the coronary ostia or larger frame cells, you reduce the chance that a continuous neo‑skirt or dense commissural posts will permanently cover an ostium and you maximize the size of accessible frame cells directly above each coronary. That alignment (planned on CT and executed using fluoroscopic markers and known device orientation/rotation) preserves straighter catheter trajectories for future coronary cannulation, minimizes neo‑skirt impingement on sinus flow, and lowers the risk that the cumulative mesh of multiple frames will create an impenetrable barrier. In the presented case, CT‑guided prediction of commissural relationships, choice of a low‑height/supra‑annular valve (Acurate neo2) and a deliberate high implant together avoided neo‑skirt coverage of the coronaries and maintained future reaccess—exactly the principles you must apply when planning any repeat transcatheter valve procedure.

### [Pathophysiology] Define valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance and valve-to-sinotubular junction (VTSTJ) distance on CT. How do these predict the need for coronary protection strategies (e.g., chimney stenting) or leaflet laceration (BASILICA)?

Valve-to‑coronary (VTC) and valve‑to‑sinotubular junction (VTSTJ) distances are CT-derived measurements used to predict whether transcatheter valve implantation (native or valve‑in‑valve) will obstruct coronary flow and to guide prophylactic strategies such as chimney stenting or leaflet laceration (BASILICA).

VTC is the shortest distance from the anticipated neo‑skirt (the final outer margin of the transcatheter valve leaflets / skirt after implantation) to the coronary ostium. On gated contrast CT you simulate the transcatheter valve frame/ skirt at the planned implantation depth and measure from that skirt plane to the coronary ostium in the appropriate oblique plane. Clinically, a small VTC (commonly ≤4 mm; many centres use ≤3–4 mm as the high‑risk cutoff) identifies patients at high risk for direct coronary obstruction from displaced leaflet tissue or a neo‑skirt. The smaller the VTC, the less room there is for coronary flow and for a chimney stent to be delivered and to function long‑term without compromised flow or thrombosis.

VTSTJ is the distance from the anticipated neo‑skirt to the sinotubular junction (STJ). It predicts the ability of the sinus/STJ complex to accommodate displaced native or bioprosthetic leaflets. A low VTSTJ (eg, minimal distance or <4 mm, often <2–3 mm considered particularly concerning) means the leaflet will be pushed up into a narrow sinus/STJ and can completely seal the sinus outlet — a situation termed sinus sequestration. Sinus sequestration carries two implications: (1) the coronary ostium may be occluded not by direct impingement at the ostium but by the leaflet sealing the entire sinus outflow tract, and (2) chimney stenting is often ineffective or unsafe because a stent external to the neo‑skirt cannot restore physiologic sinus washout and is at high risk of thrombosis.

How this guides the choice between prophylactic chimney versus BASILICA (or no protection)
- If VTC is large (>~4 mm), and sinuses and STJ are roomy, the risk of coronary obstruction is low and routine protection is not required — standard CT planning suffices.
- If VTC is small (≤3–4 mm) but the VTSTJ is relatively large (sinus wide, STJ high), the dominant mechanism is direct ostial impingement. In that setting prophylactic coronary protection (wiring and standby or planned chimney stent) is reasonable because a stented channel can preserve ostial patency and allow bail‑out.
- If VTC is small and VTSTJ is also small (predicting sinus sequestration), BASILICA (intentional leaflet laceration) is the preferred preventive strategy because lacerating the leaflet avoids creation of a sealed sinus and preserves native sinus washout; chimney stenting alone is unlikely to restore flow or may leave a thrombogenic blind pouch.
- Additional modifiers: low coronary height (<10–12 mm), small sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and bulky/bicuspid or externally mounted leaflet bioprostheses raise the risk and lower the VTC/VTSTJ thresholds at which you intervene. Commissural alignment and predicted neo‑skirt height (especially after TAV‑in‑TAV where frames stack) must be assessed because layered frames can effectively reduce VTC and VTSTJ compared with native anatomy.

Applying this to the presented case: the team used CT simulation and found adequate clearance between the anticipated neo‑skirt and the coronary ostia and STJ, so the VTC and VTSTJ were not in the high‑risk zone; therefore they proceeded without prophylactic chimney or BASILICA and instead planned high implantation and commissural alignment to maximize coronary clearance — a strategy justified by the CT measurements and borne out by successful coronary perfusion during balloon inflation and final angiography.

In short, VTC assesses how close the neo‑skirt comes to the coronary ostium (direct obstruction risk); VTSTJ assesses whether the sinus/STJ can accommodate displaced leaflets (sinus sequestration risk). Small VTC favors coronary protection; small VTSTJ (sinus sequestration) favors leaflet laceration (BASILICA) over chimney stenting. Always integrate these distances with coronary height, sinus anatomy, valve type, and planned implantation depth when deciding protection.

### [Management] In a patient with prior PVE and recurrent GI bleeding but no atrial fibrillation after TAVI, what antithrombotic regimen is generally preferred and why?

Short-term single antiplatelet therapy — usually aspirin alone — is generally preferred. 

Reasoning: once infective endocarditis has been treated and there is no other indication for oral anticoagulation (no atrial fibrillation, no mechanical valve, no venous thromboembolism), routine long‑term anticoagulation is not indicated after TAVI. Randomized and guideline data now show that dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus clopidogrel) after TAVI increases bleeding without clear ischemic benefit compared with single antiplatelet therapy, and oral anticoagulation solely to prevent valve thrombosis is not routinely recommended in patients at high bleeding risk. In a patient with recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, minimizing antithrombotic intensity reduces the risk of recurrent hemorrhage while providing reasonable thrombotic protection for the bioprosthesis. 

Practical caveats: use aspirin monotherapy if no recent coronary stent or other indication for DAPT/OAC; if there is a compelling need for anticoagulation later (new AF, documented valve thrombosis), weigh stroke risk versus bleeding and involve gastroenterology for bleeding mitigation (eg, PPI, source control).

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the principal elements assessed by Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) to define procedural/device success after TAVI?

VARC‑3 frames “procedural/device success” as a composite assessment focused on three linked domains: survival and absence of major adverse events, correct anatomic deployment, and intended valve performance without device‑related complications. In practice that means (1) no procedure‑related death and no major events during the index procedure or early follow‑up, (2) correct positioning of the transcatheter prosthesis (single valve in the intended location without malposition or need for immediate surgical conversion), and (3) the valve achieving its intended haemodynamic result — substantial reduction in transvalvular obstruction, no more than trivial-to‑mild transvalvular or paravalvular regurgitation, and no device failure necessitating repeat intervention (for example coronary obstruction, valve embolization, or requirement for urgent re‑intervention). Implicit in VARC‑3 is also the absence of major procedure‑related complications (stroke, life‑threatening bleeding, major vascular injury, need for urgent surgery or new device‑related permanent disability such as irreversible conduction issues requiring urgent management).

Clinically, VARC‑3 forces you to combine procedural anatomy (was the valve where you intended it?), haemodynamics (did gradients and regurgitation improve to an acceptable degree?), and safety (were there any major periprocedural complications). That is why, in this case, the team reported device success by documenting correct valve positioning on imaging, resolution of regurgitation, acceptable post‑procedural haemodynamics over serial echos, and absence of procedure‑related complications or recurrent infection.

### [Pathophysiology] Contrast the mechanisms by which infective endocarditis and structural valve degeneration produce prosthetic valve dysfunction, and explain why both stenosis and regurgitation can result.

In prosthetic valves the final common pathway is loss of effective, unidirectional leaflet motion — but the upstream mechanisms differ between infective endocarditis (IE) and structural valve degeneration (SVD), and that difference explains why each can produce either stenosis, regurgitation, or a mixed lesion.

Infective endocarditis injures the valve acutely and focally. Bacterial adherence produces vegetations composed of organisms, fibrin and inflammatory debris that can physically obstruct the orifice (raising transvalvular gradients) or impair leaflet coaptation. More destructive infections cause leaflet perforation, cusp tearing, or annular–perivalvular extension with abscess and dehiscence; those processes produce acute or subacute regurgitation. Because IE is an inflammatory–destructive process, it can produce obstruction when bulky vegetations or healing fibrous scarring reduce leaflet mobility, and it can produce regurgitation when tissue is lacerated, detached, or the valve seating is disrupted. In the reported case, Enterococcus faecalis endocarditis likely produced leaflet injury and fibrosis that contributed to mechanical restriction and mixed dysfunction.

Structural valve degeneration is a progressive, noninfectious process in bioprosthetic valves driven by leaflet calcification, fibrosis, collagen degeneration, and sometimes thrombosis. These changes principally reduce leaflet excursion and effective orifice area, producing stenosis over months to years. Late leaflet tears or suture/frame disruption from wear and tear can produce flail segments and regurgitation; chronic pannus or fibrous ingrowth at the sewing ring can also cause regurgitation by preventing a tight seal. In transcatheter valves there is the added problem of constrained geometry: frame underexpansion, frame-to-frame interaction in valve‑in‑valve scenarios, or accumulation of layered stents can create fixed obstruction (prosthesis‑patient mismatch) and abnormal leaflet apposition that generates central or paravalvular regurgitation. In this patient the combination of prior degeneration, layered frames, and probable infection‑related leaflet injury produced both high gradients (severe stenosis) and moderate–severe regurgitation before reintervention.

The clinical implication is that IE and SVD are not mutually exclusive and often coexist; therefore management hinges on accurate imaging and microbiologic control. Echocardiography and CT distinguish bulky vegetations, leaflet perforation, paravalvular abscess/dehiscence (favoring IE‑related regurgitation) from diffuse leaflet thickening, calcification, restricted opening, or frame underexpansion (favoring SVD‑related stenosis). Treatment priorities differ: active infection must be controlled before elective reintervention, whereas SVD is addressed by redo valve procedures or explant depending on anatomy and risk. Recognizing the mechanism explains why the same prosthesis can present with stenosis, regurgitation, or a mixed lesion and guides both urgency and strategy of intervention, as illustrated by the CT‑guided, staged approach used in this complex TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV case.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Describe the diagnostic approach to differentiate leaflet thrombosis (HALT/RLM) from structural degeneration in a failed transcatheter aortic valve, and how the result influences management.

When a transcatheter aortic valve fails you must decide whether the problem is dynamic and potentially reversible (thrombotic leaflet thickening with restricted leaflet motion — HALT/RLM) or fixed structural degeneration of the bioprosthetic leaflets. The distinction matters because HALT/RLM is frequently treated medically (anticoagulation with intention to reverse leaflet restriction) while structural degeneration usually mandates reintervention (valve-in-valve, surgical explant, or other mechanical solutions) once infection is excluded.

Diagnostic approach — clinical framing and first principles
Begin by integrating timing, symptoms, and risk factors. HALT/RLM most often appears relatively early after implantation (weeks–months) or after a change in prothrombotic milieu; structural degeneration is a late, progressive process (years) often associated with leaflet fibrosis, calcification, tears or pannus. Important modifiers include recent infection, anticoagulation status, and bleeding risk — all influencing whether a trial of anticoagulation is reasonable.

Imaging and hemodynamics — what to obtain and what findings point which way
Obtain a transthoracic echo (with Doppler) and transesophageal echo as needed to quantify gradients, valve area, regurgitation, leaflet mobility and to exclude vegetations or paravalvular complications from endocarditis. Elevated mean gradients and reduced valve area can result from either process; echo alone cannot reliably separate them.

The decisive test is gated multidetector 4D cardiac CT (contrast, retrospective ECG gating) focused on the prosthetic leaflets. HALT appears as hypoattenuated leaflet thickening on CT with corresponding restricted leaflet motion (RLM) on multiphase cine reconstructions. Structural degeneration is suggested by leaflet calcification, retraction, tears/flail, leaflet discontinuity, or gross distortion of leaflet geometry; CT may also show frame deformation or underexpansion from prior procedures. FDG-PET/CT has a role primarily to distinguish active infection from sterile degeneration when endocarditis is a concern; it is not diagnostic for HALT.

Practical diagnostic pathway
1) Exclude active infection first (blood cultures, clinical exam, TEE, PET/CT when equivocal). If endocarditis is active, treat infection before any definitive re-intervention decision.
2) Echo to quantify hemodynamics and look for leaflet motion abnormalities. If echo suggests immobile leaflets or significant stenosis/regurgitation, proceed to 4D-CT.
3) 4D-contrast CT: identify HALT (hypoattenuated leaflet thickening) with/without RLM versus structural features (calcification, tear, retraction). Compare with prior CTs if available.
4) Integrate bleeding risk, anticoagulation status and timing of failure.

How results influence management
If 4D-CT shows HALT/RLM without features of structural damage and no active infection, a therapeutic anticoagulation trial is indicated (usually with a vitamin K antagonist or DOAC per local practice and valve type) unless there are contraindications. Reassess clinically and repeat CT in 4–6 weeks; many patients show resolution of HALT and improvement in gradients. If HALT persists or gradients/symptoms do not improve despite adequate anticoagulation, or if there is recurrent embolism, proceed to reintervention.

If CT shows structural degeneration (calcification, leaflet tears, retraction) or there is persistent high-gradient severe stenosis or severe regurgitation with symptoms, then the disease is unlikely to respond to anticoagulation and the pathway is toward mechanical management: valve-in-valve TAV (or surgical explant if feasible and infection controlled). Preprocedural CT planning should then define frame geometry, valve-to-coronary distances, potential for PPM, and strategies to preserve coronary access — exactly the considerations used in the reported TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV case.

Contextualizing this patient
In the presented case the prosthesis failed years after initial implant, with a late episode of treated endocarditis and progressive leaflet dysfunction leading to severe stenosis and regurgitation. Team evaluation included TEE and CT that showed no active infection but did show severe structural compromise and a critically narrowed lumen. The clinical chronology (late failure, prior infection but now quiescent), CT appearance, and the patient’s high surgical risk made structural degeneration the dominant mechanism and argued against an anticoagulation-first strategy. Given the patient’s history of recurrent GI bleeding, anticoagulation would also have been high-risk and unlikely to be helpful if CT demonstrated fixed leaflet damage. Those considerations appropriately led to CT-guided reintervention (Acurate neo2 TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) rather than a trial of anticoagulation.

Teaching pearl
When a TAV fails, do not rely on gradients alone. Use 4D-CT (and PET-CT when infection is a concern) to differentiate reversible HALT/RLM from irreversible structural degeneration, then choose a short therapeutic anticoagulation trial only when CT shows HALT/RLM, infection is excluded, and bleeding risk is acceptable; proceed directly to reintervention when CT demonstrates structural leaflet damage or when clinical instability or prohibitive symptoms are present.

### [Management] What clinical and imaging findings must be documented to declare resolution of PVE before undertaking a transcatheter valve reintervention?

Before reintervention on a prosthetic valve previously infected, you must document microbiologic, clinical and anatomic resolution of infection — not simply clinical improvement — because placing new prosthetic material into an active infection risks relapse and catastrophic prosthetic seeding.

Specifically, document the following. First, microbiologic cure: the patient should have completed a guideline‑directed, pathogen‑specific course of antimicrobial therapy and be afebrile and clinically well off parenteral antibiotics; ideally there are persistently negative blood cultures (serial cultures negative during therapy and after its completion) and no ongoing need for chronic suppressive antibiotics. Second, systemic resolution: inflammatory indices (CRP, ESR, leukocyte count) should have fallen to baseline or show a sustained downward trend consistent with cure and there should be absence of fever, ongoing bacteraemia or embolic phenomena. Third, imaging exclusion of active endocarditis or perivalvular complications: repeat transesophageal echocardiography must show no mobile vegetations, no new or enlarging paravalvular leak, and no abscess, fistula or dehiscence; cross‑sectional imaging (MSCT) and, when ambiguity remains, 18F‑FDG PET/CT should show no focal increased uptake or evolving perivalvular collection. Fourth, temporal stability: findings should be stable on serial exams (clinical and imaging) after completion of therapy — not a single transient negative study — and reviewed by the multidisciplinary heart team (cardiology, cardiac surgery, infectious disease, imaging) who agree the infection is resolved and reintervention is safe.

In practice this means documented completion of appropriate antibiotics, serially negative blood cultures, normalization/decline of inflammatory markers, absence of fever or embolic signs, negative/unchanged TEE and CT (and negative or non‑localizing PET if used), and multidisciplinary consensus — with an observation interval after therapy appropriate to the organism and clinical context — before proceeding with transcatheter valve reintervention.

### [Clinical Pearls] During a complex redo TAVI, why is rapid ventricular pacing used during balloon pre- or post-dilation, and what hemodynamic effect does it achieve?

Rapid ventricular pacing is used during balloon pre‑ and post‑dilation to transiently and predictably suppress ventricular ejection so that the balloon (or valve) is exposed to minimal transvalvular flow and cardiac motion during the critical inflation/deployment period. By pacing the ventricle very fast (commonly 160–220 bpm; 180 bpm in this case) you drastically shorten diastole, reduce stroke volume and cardiac output, and drop arterial pressure to near‑zero for a few seconds. The hemodynamic effect is a controlled, transient hypotension with minimal forward flow and almost no systolic ejection, which stabilizes the balloon/prosthesis against the aortic annulus, prevents balloon or valve displacement and reduces the opposing transvalvular pressure that would otherwise impede full expansion. In complex redo TAVI (and here, a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV), that brief period of very low flow and motion makes precise positioning and effective radial expansion possible; the trade‑offs are transient systemic hypotension, myocardial ischemia risk and potential arrhythmia, so pacing is applied for only a few seconds with monitoring and a temporary wire in place.

### [Morning Report] In a triple-stented aortic position, what are the main trade-offs between high vs low implantation depth of the new valve with respect to effective orifice area, anchoring, conduction disturbance risk, and coronary access?

Think of implantation depth in a triple‑stented aortic position as a three‑dimensional trade‑off between flow, fixation, rhythm and coronary access; the right choice depends on valve design, the anatomy of the existing frames and the priorities for lifetime management.

High implantation (more aortic/supra‑annular)
- Effective orifice area (EOA): tends to preserve or increase EOA because the new valve’s leaflets sit above and can “pin” the old leaflets outward against the prior frame rather than folding them into the flow orifice. In this case the team intentionally landed the Acurate neo2 high to avoid inward leaflet folding and reduce prosthesis–patient mismatch.
- Anchoring/stability: anchoring is potentially reduced when the frame has less contact with the annulus/LVOT. In single‑valve anatomy this raises embolization risk; in a multi‑frame situation you may still gain secondary fixation from the prior stent(s), but reliance on that is less predictable and requires CT/fluoro confirmation.
- Conduction disturbance: high implants spare the membranous septum and conduction tissue more than deep implants, so they reduce the risk of new AV block and pacemaker implantation—important here because the patient already had a pacemaker.
- Coronary access: high implantation minimizes the neo‑skirt beneath the coronary ostia and preserves coronary access and flow; this is often the dominant reason to aim high in TAV‑in‑TAV situations and was a stated goal in the presented case.

Low implantation (more ventricular/deep)
- Effective orifice area: deeper implants increase the likelihood that the new frame or the constrained leaflets of prior prostheses will encroach on the central orifice, worsening EOA and increasing the risk of PPM—especially problematic when the internal lumen is already small (as with a 13.7 mm Evolut lumen in this patient).
- Anchoring/stability: deeper seating gives more contact with the annulus/LVOT and generally provides more secure mechanical fixation and lower risk of embolization, and may allow better radial expansion of prior frames with post‑dilation.
- Conduction disturbance: deeper implants increase compression of the membranous septum and the His bundle area and therefore markedly raise the risk of new conduction disease and need for pacemaker.
- Coronary access: deeper implants tend to create a larger neo‑skirt above the annulus and can bury commissures, increasing the chance of coronary obstruction now and making future coronary engagement more difficult or impossible.

Putting it together for triple‑stented anatomy
- The cumulative stent mesh and small residual lumen magnify the consequences of whichever depth you choose: a low implant that improves anchoring may salvage immediate stability but often sacrifices EOA and coronary access; a high implant preserves flow and coronary access but may rely on unpredictable secondary fixation from prior frames and increase procedural technical risk (embolization, paravalvular leak if underseated).
- Device selection and commissural alignment become critical: choose a valve with favorable supra‑annular leaflet position and lower frame height if the plan is high, and use CT modelling to ensure valve‑to‑coronary distances and that the prior frame can provide sufficient support.
- Procedural mitigations: detailed MSCT planning, possible coronary protection, careful rapid pacing during deployment, and aggressive post‑dilation (or planned frame expansion/fracture when feasible) are tools to balance EOA and anchoring. In this report the team used CT guidance, commissural alignment, and post‑dilation to achieve acceptable gradients despite a high implant.

Clinical principle: optimize the depth to match the dominant clinical goal—if preserving coronary access and maximizing EOA (to avoid PPM) is paramount in a layered valve, favor a high, commissure‑aligned implant with careful planning; if immediate mechanical fixation is the limiting factor and coronary anatomy is generous, a deeper implant favors stability but raises pacemaker and PPM risks. In every triple‑stented case the decision should be individualized via preprocedural CT and heart‑team discussion.

### [Management] When planning TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, under what circumstances would you favor prophylactic coronary protection (guidewire and undeployed stent) or BASILICA, and when might you proceed without protection?

The decision to deploy prophylactic coronary protection (a coronary guidewire ± undeployed stent ready for chimney) or to perform BASILICA at the time of a TAV-in-TAV — and especially a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV — should be driven by the mechanism of anticipated obstruction on CT modelling, the quantified anatomic risk, and the technical feasibility of leaflet laceration in the setting of multiple prior frames.

Think of coronary obstruction risk as coming from two things: (1) displaced bioprosthetic leaflets (or creation of a “neo‑skirt”) that can seal the coronary ostium, and (2) the physical bulk of stent frames progressively crowding the sinus/ostial area. Important CT predictors are low coronary height, small sinus of Valsalva, and short valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distances after virtual valve implantation. Practical thresholds most teams use are coronary height ≤10–12 mm, VTC <4 mm, and small sinus diameters (and/or narrow sinotubular junction) — any of which moves you toward active protection. Also factor in valve type and leaflet position (supra‑annular self‑expanding valves that create tall skirts, or prior surgical valves with long leaflets increase risk), female sex, small annulus, and whether you anticipate a high residual mesh burden over the ostia.

When I would favour BASILICA
If CT/virtual implantation shows that obstruction will be caused primarily by preserved leaflet tissue that can be lacerated and thereby create a flow channel to the coronary (for example, a bioprosthetic leaflet leaflet that will be splayed directly across the ostium, VTC small, but the leaflet is accessible to catheter traversal), BASILICA is preferred because it addresses the root cause — the leaflet — and avoids an indwelling chimney stent that complicates long‑term outcomes. BASILICA is especially attractive when coronary protection with a chimney would be likely to leave a persistent bulky stent behind several frames or when lifetime coronary access after a chimney would be compromised.

When I would favour prophylactic coronary wiring ± undeployed stent (chimney standby)
If the risk is intermediate or uncertain — e.g., VTC borderline (≈3–6 mm), low coronary height but anatomy or prior frames make BASILICA technically difficult or unsafe, or when the anticipated obstruction could be from both leaflet displacement and unpredictable frame distortion — I prepare a coronary guidewire and position an undeployed stent across the ostium. This gives immediate bailout capability (rapid chimney stent deployment) if flow becomes compromised. I also favour this approach when BASILICA is judged unlikely to be successful (leaflets are trapped behind multiple frames or cannot be traversed reliably), or when operator experience with BASILICA is limited. A wire alone (without a stent) shortens response time but is less definitive; an undeployed stent is preferred if obstruction would be catastrophic.

When I would proceed without prophylactic protection
When high‑resolution CT modelling demonstrates adequate coronary clearance after virtual valve implantation (VTC comfortably >4 mm, coronary heights >12 mm, adequate sinus volume), when you can plan a high implantation and favorable commissural alignment to avoid creating a neo‑skirt, and when the predicted mechanism of failure is unlikely to involve leaflet obstruction of the ostia, it is reasonable to proceed without prophylactic coronary protection. That was the rationale in the presented case: CT modelling showed sufficient clearance, valve selection and planned high implant (and commissural alignment) reduced the expected risk, and aortography during balloon predilation confirmed preserved flow — so the team proceeded without protection while remaining prepared to bail out if needed.

Specific caveats for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV
Multiple frames make CT prediction less reliable and BASILICA technically more challenging because leaflets may be trapped behind earlier frames and laceration may not create an effective channel. Thus a lower threshold for having rapid coronary bailout is reasonable. In triple‑stented anatomies, if CT is ambiguous I favour prophylactic wiring with an undeployed stent (or even upfront stent positioning) because a chimney may be the only immediately deployable rescue. Conversely, if CT shows a clear path and you can implant high with good commissural alignment (as in this case), proceeding without protection is defensible — but only with meticulous planning, on‑site coronary bailout hardware, and an experienced team.

Bottom line: use CT‑based virtual valve implant to define mechanism and quantify VTC/sinus/height. If obstruction is leaflet‑mediated and laceration is technically feasible, choose BASILICA. If risk is intermediate, anatomy renders BASILICA difficult, or prediction is uncertain, do prophylactic wiring with an undeployed stent ready for chimney. If CT clearly demonstrates safe coronary clearance and implantation strategy (high implant, commissural alignment) mitigates risk, you may proceed without protection but only with immediate bailout preparedness and heart‑team consensus.

### [Pathophysiology] How can high-pressure balloon post-dilation after TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV lead to a reduction in gradients over the subsequent weeks, even without intentional frame fracture?

Short answer: Because the layered transcatheter frames and the trapped bioprosthetic leaflets are not a rigid, instantaneous system — they can undergo further mechanical expansion and biological remodelling after a high‑pressure balloon. Immediate post‑dilation often produces partial plastic deformation of nitinol and stainless‑steel frames, allows leaflet re‑positioning, relieves leaflet impingement, and is followed over days–weeks by time‑dependent stent “creep,” resolution of tissue edema/thrombus and endothelial remodelling. The net result is a larger effective orifice area and lower transvalvular gradients even without a deliberate frame fracture.

Clinical reasoning and mechanisms
When you implant a new valve inside one or more pre‑existing stents, the inner valve is constrained by the composite rigidity of the outer frames and by how the old leaflets lie within that space. Balloon post‑dilation applies a high radial force that can do several things simultaneously:

1) Immediate mechanical effects: the balloon can plastically deform or partially expand the inner and some of the outer stent cells, pushing previously folded or pinned leaflets outward and increasing the luminal diameter. Even without reaching the force/geometry threshold for an intentional “fracture,” partial expansion can materially increase orifice area.

2) Time‑dependent stent behaviour: alloys used in transcatheter frames (especially nitinol) show superelasticity and time‑dependent deformation (creep). After a high‑pressure dilatation the frame may continue to relax and expand slightly over hours–weeks as stresses redistribute. This is a mechanical, not biological, process but happens over time.

3) Biologic and hemodynamic remodeling: ballooning and the new flow pattern reduce leaflet crowding and high shear regions, and any tissue edema or small thrombus that limited leaflet motion can resolve over days to weeks. Left ventricular loading conditions also change after valve implantation; as LV function and forward flow normalise, measured gradients can fall. Improvements in ventricular remodeling and stroke volume will change Doppler‑derived gradients as well.

4) Composite effect on effective orifice area: the combination of immediate partial expansion, delayed stent creep, improved leaflet mobility and reduced tissue swelling/thrombus converts an initially small effective orifice into a larger, more physiologic lumen over weeks. This translates into the marked fall in mean gradient seen in the case.

Broader principle and clinical implication
Always remember that prosthetic valve haemodynamics immediately after implantation are not always the final answer. In complex re‑interventions with multiple frames, high‑pressure post‑dilation can be both diagnostic and therapeutic: it may achieve partial frame expansion that continues to evolve, and early high gradients may improve without re‑intervention. Therefore, unless the patient is unstable or there is clear procedural failure (severe regurgitation, obstructed coronaries), it is reasonable to observe and re‑assess with serial echocardiography before committing to further invasive steps. Use CT and serial imaging to distinguish mechanical underexpansion from other causes (thrombus, leaflet tethering, PVE).

### [Morning Report] List the key CT measurements that inform valve sizing and feasibility when the prior transcatheter valve’s internal lumen measures approximately 14 mm, and explain how these data guide selection of the next valve size and type.

The key CT measurements are those that tell you (1) what lumen you actually have to work with, (2) how a new valve will interact with the prior frames and the coronaries, and (3) whether you can reasonably expect an acceptable effective orifice area after implantation. In a scenario where the prior valve’s internal lumen measures ≈14 mm, the CT-derived parameters that drive valve choice and procedural strategy are the following.

First, measure the true internal lumen at the intended landing zone: the minimum (narrowest) inner diameter and the corresponding cross‑sectional area of the existing prosthetic frame and leaflets on thin‑slice, contrast‑enhanced MSCT. This determines the maximal external diameter of any new device that can expand into the stack and therefore predicts residual effective orifice area and the likelihood of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch. An internal diameter around 14 mm is extremely small for most transcatheter valves and predicts high gradients unless you choose a valve with supra‑annular leaflets and optimize expansion.

Second, perform virtual valve implantation (CT simulation) to derive the valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance and the predicted neo‑skirt relation to the coronary ostia. A VTC <4 mm (and coronary heights <10–12 mm or small sinus of Valsalva diameters) identifies high risk for coronary obstruction and mandates protection strategies (wires/stents, BASILICA planning) or changing the plan. In this case the team used CT modelling to confirm adequate clearance and proceeded without coronary protection.

Third, measure the sinus of Valsalva and sinotubular junction diameters and heights, and the annular geometry below the existing frame. These measurements determine whether there is room for a supra‑annular valve frame and whether high implantation (to pin prior leaflets outwards) will leave a safe path for coronary flow. Small sinuses or low sinotubular junctions increase obstruction risk and limit the ability to implant high.

Fourth, quantify the relation of prior valve frames to the coronaries and to each other: frame heights, leaflet heights, leaflet apposition against the stent, and the distance from the inner frame surface to the coronary ostia. This helps predict how much of the new valve will protrude above/below the prior leaflets and whether commissural alignment is feasible. It also informs whether post‑dilation or attempt at frame expansion (or fracture) is possible or likely to help.

Fifth, evaluate access, tortuosity and the aortic root angulation to judge deliverability of a specific valve type and whether controlled high implantation and precise commissural alignment are achievable.

How these data guide valve size and type selection in a 14 mm internal lumen:

- Realize that a nominal label size cannot be used without considering the 14 mm constraint; most balloon‑expandable valves or large frames will not fully expand or will create a very small EOA. Therefore select a valve design that maximizes valve area for a small internal diameter: a supra‑annular leaflet prosthesis with low frame height and good hemodynamics in small annuli (the Acurate neo2 S in this case) is preferable because supra‑annular leaflets place the orifice above the constrained frame and generally deliver larger effective orifice areas for the same inner diameter.

- Favor self‑expanding valves with some conformability and lower profile if you anticipate partial mechanical expansion of the existing nitinol frame will improve function. Self‑expanding devices can also be implanted higher to pin the old leaflets outwards, improving effective orifice area and preserving coronary flow.

- Use the CT‑derived VTC and sinus measurements to decide implantation depth and whether to attempt commissural alignment. If VTC is marginal (<4 mm) or sinuses are small, plan for coronary protection or consider alternative strategies (surgical redo if feasible, BASILICA, or different valve orientation).

- Decide on planned post‑dilation based on predicted residual stenosis and the mechanical properties of the existing frames. CT showing a circular lumen with some room may support aggressive post‑dilation to improve expansion; if prior frames are not fractureable (eg, nitinol Evolut), valve‑in‑valve fracture is not an option, and one must accept higher gradients or choose a valve with supra‑annular leaflets.

- Anticipate prosthesis–patient mismatch: CT predicts that with an internal diameter ≈14 mm you will likely have a small EOA and high gradients unless you use a supra‑annular valve, implant high to avoid leaflet infolding, and optimize expansion. Communicate this risk with the patient and team.

In short, CT gives you the working lumen (≈14 mm), the spatial relationship to coronaries (VTC), and the envelope of expansion available inside stacked frames. Those measurements lead you to choose a small, supra‑annular, low‑profile valve that can be implanted high with commissural alignment and aggressive but safe post‑dilation to maximize EOA while confirming coronary safety. The Acurate neo2 S chosen in this report exemplifies that reasoning: its supra‑annular design and deliverability made it the best option in a constrained 14 mm internal lumen after multidisciplinary CT planning.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the clinical indicators to suspect PVE in a TAVI recipient presenting with low-grade fever and new or worsening heart failure, and which tests should be ordered first-line?

Think of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) whenever a TAVI recipient presents with new low‑grade fever plus new or worsening heart failure — the combination raises concern that prosthetic dysfunction (leaflet infection, dehiscence, obstruction or paravalvular extension) is driving haemodynamic deterioration rather than a purely medical cause of dyspnoea.

Key clinical indicators that should increase your suspicion are a new or changing murmur or new/worsening aortic regurgitation or stenosis on exam or echo; unexplained increase in transvalvular gradients or sudden drop in valve area; persistent or relapsing low‑grade fever; persistent bacteremia or bacteremia with organisms classically associated with PVE (Staphylococcus, Enterococcus, streptococci); new conduction disturbance (new PR prolongation or heart block) suggesting root/annular extension; evidence of embolic phenomena or splenic/renal infarcts; raised inflammatory markers without alternate source; and any antecedent invasive procedure or bloodstream infection. In a TAVI patient the threshold for concern is lower because prosthetic frames and prior endocarditis increase both infection risk and the likelihood that infection will cause structural malfunction.

First‑line tests and the rationale, in order of priority
1) Blood cultures (set of three from separate venipunctures) obtained before antibiotics. Persistent or reproducible bacteremia is a major diagnostic criterion and guides targeted therapy.  
2) Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) immediately to assess valve hemodynamics and look for large vegetations or new regurgitation — rapid, bedside, helpful as first screen.  
3) Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) as soon as possible (ideally within 24 hours) because TEE has far greater sensitivity than TTE for prosthetic valve vegetations, abscesses and paravalvular leaks; use 3‑D TEE when available to improve visualization around stent frames. If TEE is limited by shadowing from multiple frames, note that negative TEE does not exclude PVE.  
4) ECG to detect new conduction abnormalities that point to annular or root extension (a surgical red flag).  
5) Basic labs: CBC, CRP/ESR, renal/hepatic panel to assess inflammatory response and guide antibiotic dosing.  
6) If echocardiography is inconclusive or you need anatomic detail for planning or to detect perivalvular complications not well seen on TEE (common in TAVI with layered frames), perform gated multislice CT (MSCT) to define abscesses, pseudoaneurysm, prosthesis position and relation to coronaries.  
7) 18F‑FDG PET/CT (or radiolabelled leukocyte imaging) when diagnosis remains uncertain after echo and CT, or in late‑PVE (>3 months) — PET adds sensitivity for prosthetic infection and perivalvular inflammation and is incorporated in modern diagnostic algorithms for PVE.  
8) Early infectious‑disease and heart‑team consultation to integrate microbiology, imaging, and consideration of surgical versus transcatheter management.

Clinical principle to keep in mind: obtain blood cultures before antibiotics and use a stepwise imaging approach — TTE → urgent TEE → MSCT and/or FDG‑PET when echo is limited or equivocal — because prosthetic materials and prior TAVI frames frequently reduce echocardiographic sensitivity and perivalvular complications (abscess, pseudoaneurysm, prosthesis dehiscence) are the findings that most often mandate surgical intervention. In the case you reviewed, Enterococcus bacteremia, new prosthetic dysfunction and TEE/MSCT evidence of resolution guided management; the same pathway, inverted for diagnostic urgency, should be followed when infection is suspected.

### [Clinical Pearls] What features of the sinus of Valsalva and coronary ostial height increase the risk of coronary obstruction during TAV-in-TAV, and what numeric cutoffs are commonly used to define higher risk?

Think of coronary obstruction in TAV‑in‑TAV as a mechanical problem: previously implanted leaflets and the new valve’s skirt are displaced outward and can form a “neo‑skirt” that sits across the coronary ostia. Two anatomic features govern whether that displaced tissue will be accommodated or will block flow — the height of the ostium above the annulus and the room available in the sinus of Valsalva (and downstream sinotubular junction) to accept the displaced leaflets.

Clinically important high‑risk findings on CT and the practical cutoffs most groups use are these. First, low coronary ostial height from the annulus increases risk — an ostial height <10 mm is generally considered high risk and 10–12 mm is borderline. The lower the ostium, the more likely an outward‑moved leaflet or skirt will sit across it. Second, small sinus of Valsalva dimensions increase risk because the sinuses can’t accommodate the displaced leaflet tissue; a mean sinus diameter <30 mm (often <28–30 mm used) is associated with higher risk. Third, the valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance — the predicted gap between the anticipated final transcatheter valve frame/neo‑skirt and the coronary ostium — is a more direct CT‑based predictor for TAV‑in‑TAV: a VTC <4 mm (many use <3–4 mm as high risk) is considered unsafe; 4–6 mm is intermediate and >6 mm low risk. Related measurements — valve‑to‑sinotubular junction (VTSTJ) and sinotubular junction diameter — matter because an already narrow STJ can trap leaflets against the ostia even if the sinus is borderline.

Put another way: the highest risk scenario is low coronary ostia (<10 mm), small SOV/STJ (<28–30 mm), and a predicted VTC <3–4 mm. These are not absolute thresholds — they are used to stratify risk and to plan mitigation (coronary protection wiring, prophylactic BASILICA, surgical referral). Always interpret them in the context of valve type and leaflet height (native or prosthetic), CT virtual valve implantation modeling, and the patient’s clinical risk.

### [Management] In a patient with multivessel coronary artery disease and triple-layered transcatheter frames, what are anticipated challenges for future coronary angiography or PCI, and how does commissural alignment mitigate (but not eliminate) these issues?

In a patient like this—three nested transcatheter frames and known multivessel coronary disease—you should expect coronary angiography and any future PCI to be substantially more difficult than in a native valve or single TAVI. The practical problems arise from the anatomy created by the stacked frames rather than from the coronary lesions themselves.

Mechanistically, multiple overlapping stent cages create a mechanical barrier at the coronary ostia: the ostial orifice can be partially or completely covered by frame struts and by a neo‑skirt formed by immobilised leaflets, metal mesh and tissue overgrowth. That produces unpredictable cell sizes and shapes, angulation of the ostium relative to the aortic root, and restricted radial windows through which wires, catheters and devices must pass. The consequences you will encounter are harder selective engagement (loss of standard coaxiality), poor guiding catheter support, difficulty advancing wires or microcatheters through small or tortuous frame cells, impaired contrast opacification and fluoroscopic landmarks (metallic artifact), longer procedure time and higher radiation/contrast use, and a greater risk of technical failure or complications (dissection, embolisation, coronary obstruction during device manipulation).

Commissural alignment is a deliberate strategy to decrease one major component of this problem: by orienting the prosthetic valve posts and leaflet commissures so they avoid the coronary ostia, you reduce the likelihood that a strut or a leaflet will sit directly in front of an ostium. In practical terms alignment increases the probability of a reasonably sized and predictable cell over the coronary, preserves a straighter catheter approach, and frequently allows selective engagement with conventional guide catheters and standard techniques. In this case the team used pre‑procedural CT and fluoroscopic landmarks to align the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut leaflets—an approach aimed squarely at preserving future coronary access.

Important caveats: commissural alignment mitigates but does not eliminate the problems. Even with perfect alignment you still have multiple metal layers, possible neo‑skirt formation and endothelialisation that narrow the working window; radial recoil or asymmetric expansion of previous frames can deform cells; future frames or post‑dilatations may change the relationship later; and imaging artifacts may still obscure ostial anatomy. Therefore you must plan any future coronary work as high‑risk: obtain recent CT to map ostial windows, consider upfront coronary protection (wires, balloons or chimney stent strategies) if intervening on critical territories, prepare specialty equipment (angled and extra‑backup guides, guide extension catheters, microcatheters, low‑profile devices), and discuss the feasibility of alternative strategies (BASILICA or hybrid/surgical options) in the heart team. In short, commissural alignment improves the odds of successful reaccess but does not remove the need for detailed preprocedural imaging, procedural contingency planning, and readiness for complex coronary techniques.

### [Morning Report] What factors in this scenario would make surgical explantation particularly high risk, and what surgical procedures might be required if explantation were attempted?

Surgical explantation in this patient would be high risk for two sets of reasons: patient-related and anatomy/technical‑related—and both are present here.

Patient-related risk: he is an elderly man with severe comorbidity (three‑vessel CAD with prior multiple PCIs, COPD, diabetes, recurrent GI bleeding, prior pacemaker, and a recent major vascular complication after transfemoral TAVI). His calculated operative risk (EuroSCORE II ~27%) and frailty of end‑organs substantially increase morbidity and mortality from a redo sternotomy with prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass, bleeding and infectious complications. Prior vascular surgical repair and ongoing bleeding risk further complicate perioperative management.

Anatomic and technical factors making explantation hazardous: there are two overlapping transcatheter frames fully endothelialised and intimately incorporated into the aortic annulus and root, with stent struts integrated into the aortic wall and sinus tissue. This produces dense interface between prosthetic metal and native aortic tissue, obliteration of normal anatomic planes, and potential adherent leaflet tissue from prior prosthesis degeneration and prior infective endocarditis. These features markedly increase the risk of catastrophic injury during dissection—annular rupture, coronary ostial avulsion or laceration, uncontrolled bleeding from friable or calcified tissue, and need for extensive reconstruction. Prior endocarditis raises the possibility of occult root infection or weakened tissue even if clinically quiescent, making simple valve excision inadequate and increasing the need for radical debridement.

Given those risks, what operations might be required if surgery were attempted? Simple valve replacement is unlikely to be possible. More likely surgical strategies include excision of the layered prostheses with composite aortic root replacement (Bentall-type) or homograft/root replacement. In practice this may require removal of stent frames, radical debridement of infected/fibrotic tissue, replacement of the aortic valve and root with a prosthetic conduit or cryopreserved homograft, and reimplantation or bypassing of the coronary ostia. If the stent frames extend into the ascending aorta, extension of the repair to the ascending aorta (partial or total replacement) and use of hypothermic circulatory arrest for safe dissection may be necessary. Concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting may be required if coronary roots cannot be safely reimplanted or are injured. Management of the permanent pacemaker system and addressing bleeding diathesis would also be part of the operation.

In short, explantation would likely not be a limited valve exchange but a complex root‑replacement with possible ascending aortic replacement, coronary reimplantation or bypass, and prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass and circulatory arrest. Those technical demands, together with the patient’s comorbidities and prior procedures, explain why the heart team judged surgical explantation prohibitive and favoured a CT‑guided transcatheter solution.

### [Management] What is the role of antibiotic prophylaxis for invasive dental procedures in patients with transcatheter aortic valves, particularly those with a history of PVE?

Short answer: Transcatheter aortic valves are managed the same as surgical bioprostheses for endocarditis prevention — patients with prosthetic valves (including TAVI) and those with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis are considered high risk and should receive antibiotic prophylaxis for invasive dental procedures that are likely to produce bacteremia (manipulation of gingiva, the periapical region of teeth, or perforation of the oral mucosa). This recommendation is guideline‑based despite limited trial evidence because the consequences of PVE are severe and the absolute risk from a single prophylactic dose is small.

Clinical reasoning and practical points grounded in the case
This patient has a prosthetic aortic valve complex (three stacked TAVs) and a history of Enterococcus faecalis PVE. That prior PVE places him in the highest‑risk category for recurrent PVE, and although enterococci are more commonly associated with gastrointestinal/urogenital sources, oral procedures can produce transient bacteremia with organisms capable of seeding prosthetic material. Elective invasive dental work should be deferred until infection is fully treated and the patient is stable — in this case antimicrobial therapy was completed and imaging showed no active infection, so proceeding with dental care with prophylaxis is appropriate.

What to give and when
Follow standard prophylaxis regimens. For dental procedures that warrant prophylaxis:
- Amoxicillin 2 g orally 30–60 minutes before the procedure (adult dose).
- If allergic to penicillin: clindamycin 600 mg orally, or azithromycin/clarithromycin 500 mg orally.
No routine post‑procedure antibiotics are recommended.

Other important principles
Prioritize excellent chronic oral hygiene and regular dental care — preventing invasive procedures by reducing periodontal disease likely does more to lower long‑term endocarditis risk than episodic prophylaxis. Avoid giving prophylaxis for routine everyday activities or for noninvasive procedures (nasal, gastrointestinal) unless guidelines specify otherwise. For patients with recent PVE or ongoing signs of infection, coordinate with infectious disease/cardiology and defer elective dental interventions until infection is resolved. Document shared decision‑making and communicate with the dental team about the prosthetic valve and the need for prophylaxis.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Outline a practical algorithm to evaluate a new high gradient after TAVI: which echocardiographic parameters, invasive hemodynamics, and CT findings help distinguish PPM, valve underexpansion, malposition, leaflet thrombosis, and high-flow states.

When confronted with a newly elevated transvalvular gradient after TAVI, treat the problem as you would any acute prosthetic valve dysfunction: confirm the finding, then work through a structured imaging → physiologic → anatomic algorithm aimed at separating true patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM), frame underexpansion or malposition, leaflet thrombosis, and physiologic high‑flow states. The following practical algorithm is what I use in morning report and on the ward.

Step 1 — confirm and quantify with transthoracic Doppler before assuming a mechanical problem
Always start with careful Doppler TTE because measurement error is common. Confirm Vmax, mean gradient, continuity‑equation AVA, Doppler velocity index (DVI = LVOT VTI / aortic VTI), and stroke volume index (SVI). Key thresholds and patterns to remember: severe obstruction yields DVI ≤0.25 and mean gradient elevated; indexed AVA ≤0.85 cm2/m2 suggests PPM (≤0.65 severe). A very low DVI with small calculated AVA and a low SVI suggests true obstruction; conversely, high gradients with preserved DVI and large SVI point toward a high‑flow state or measurement artefact. Compare to prior post‑implant baseline gradients — an abrupt rise over weeks favors thrombosis or structural change; a chronic persistent high gradient since implantation suggests PPM or chronic frame underexpansion.

Step 2 — discriminate flow versus fixed obstruction
Measure SVI, heart rate, blood pressure, hemoglobin, and check for sepsis, AV fistula, or thyrotoxicosis. If SVI is high (>40–45 mL/m2) and DVI is preserved, suspect high‑flow physiology; treat reversible causes and repeat imaging. If low‑flow low‑gradient physiology is possible (low SVI with low gradients but reduced AVA), consider dobutamine evaluation (to differentiate pseudo‑severe from true obstruction) as for native AS.

Step 3 — look for leaflet motion abnormalities with TEE (including 3D) and gated CT
Transesophageal echo (preferably 3D) is useful to inspect leaflet mobility and to detect restricted cusps or vegetations. If TEE suggests immobile leaflets without vegetations, proceed to ECG‑gated contrast MSCT. On CT, two findings are decisive: hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RLM) — HALT/RLM are diagnostic of (sub)acute leaflet thrombosis when present together and correlate with reversible gradients after anticoagulation. By contrast, CT will also show structural frame problems: true frame underexpansion (small inner frame diameter, eccentric expansion, inward leaflet stent collapse) and malposition (valve plane markedly supra‑ or infra‑annular relative to surgical landmarks or to prior valves). PPM, however, is a physiologic diagnosis (small EOA relative to body size): CT will show a fully expanded frame but a small residual effective orifice (small internal diameter and small calculated EOA) consistent with intrinsic mismatch rather than a deformity that can be fixed.

Step 4 — invasive hemodynamics when noninvasive testing is ambiguous or when intervention is contemplated
Obtain simultaneous LV and aortic pressures to confirm transvalvular mean gradient (beat‑to‑beat). Peak‑to‑peak gradients can underestimate obstruction; use mean gradient from simultaneously recorded pressures. Perform pullback to exclude dynamic LVOT obstruction. If underexpansion is suspected and you plan potential therapy, a balloon retrograde catheter test (balloon valvuloplasty) can be diagnostic: a marked immediate fall in gradient with balloon expansion suggests a mechanical underexpansion that may be responsive to post‑dilation or frame fracture. If thrombosis is suspected, catheterization is not diagnostic but helps exclude other causes before committing to anticoagulation or reintervention.

Putting the findings together — distinguishing the five common causes

- Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM)
Clinical reasoning: PPM is an expected physiologic consequence when the prosthesis effective orifice area is too small for the patient’s body size. TTE shows persistently high gradients from the time of implantation, a small indexed EOA (<0.85 moderate, ≤0.65 severe), DVI low, but CT demonstrates a well‑expanded frame and normal leaflet mobility (no HALT/RLM). Invasive pressures confirm a fixed, non‑improvable gradient. Management is supportive/medical or reintervention if symptomatic and severe; ballooning generally will not help because frame is already expanded.

- Frame underexpansion (mechanical constriction)
Clinical reasoning: Gradients may be higher soon after or later if the frame never fully expanded (e.g., heavily calcified annulus or confinement by prior frames). TTE shows elevated gradients; CT shows an inner lumen diameter smaller than expected, asymmetric or eccentric frame shape, and often a minimum lumen diameter well below labelled valve size (as in the case’s Evolut minimum 13.7 mm). Invasive simultaneous pressures or a diagnostic balloon valvuloplasty that acutely reduces gradient points to mechanical underexpansion amenable to post‑dilation (or high‑pressure techniques/valve fracture where applicable). Improvement of gradients after ballooning confirms mechanical underexpansion.

- Malposition (too high or too low, or axial misplacement)
Clinical reasoning: Malposition often causes mixed findings — significant regurgitation (paravalvular) or obstructive gradients if valve sits suboptimally. TTE may show eccentric jets or unusual flow patterns; CT clearly localizes the prosthesis relative to annular plane and coronary ostia and reveals whether the skirt is intruding into the LVOT or obstructing the valve orifice. Hemodynamics may show gradients and abnormal pressure tracing; correction is rarely achieved by simple ballooning if the valve sits incorrectly — reintervention strategy depends on exact anatomy.

- Leaflet thrombosis (HALT/RLM)
Clinical reasoning: Presents subacutely with rising gradient over days–weeks. TTE shows new/worsening gradients and reduced leaflet opening on TEE; CT shows HALT and RLM. The diagnosis is supported if gradients and leaflet motion improve with therapeutic anticoagulation within days–weeks. Invasive hemodynamics confirm obstruction but are not specific. If anticoagulation fails or obstructive thrombosis is severe, consider thrombectomy or reintervention.

- High‑flow states (physiologic)
Clinical reasoning: Systemic high flow raises velocities across any valve. TTE will show high gradients but preserved DVI and an AVA that may be normal by continuity equation; SVI will be high and there will be clinical clues (anemia, sepsis, thyrotoxicosis, arteriovenous fistula). CT and TEE show normal frame and leaflet motion. Correct the precipitant and repeat imaging; no mechanical intervention is needed.

Practical sequence at the bedside
First, repeat high‑quality Doppler TTE with careful LVOT measurement and SVI. Second, review prior post‑implant baseline studies to see whether the gradient is new or chronic. Third, perform TEE (plus gated CT) if TTE is abnormal or unclear — CT gives the decisive anatomic answer for underexpansion, malposition, and HALT/RLM. Fourth, if CT/echo suggest mechanical restriction and reintervention is being considered, perform invasive simultaneous LV–aortic pressure measurement and consider diagnostic balloon dilation to test reversibility. Fifth, if HALT/RLM is found, start anticoagulation as a therapeutic trial and repeat CT/TTE to document resolution.

Application to this case
This patient had a very small CT inner lumen of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame and immediate post‑implant gradients that dropped substantially after post‑dilation and over the first month. That combination (CT demonstrating severe confinement of the prior frame, acute gradient improvement after ballooning, and sustained lower gradients thereafter) best fits mechanical underexpansion of the Evolut frame (with an element of PPM because a triple‑stented orifice will always be relatively small). There was no CT evidence of HALT/RLM to suggest thrombosis, and no high‑flow state. The team’s approach — CT‑guided valve choice, high implantation, and aggressive post‑dilation — is exactly the appropriate strategy when CT identifies frame constriction as the principal driver of high gradients.

Key teaching pearl: always integrate Doppler parameters (DVI, AVA indexed, SVI), dynamic testing (dobutamine or balloon), CT anatomic detail (inner diameter, eccentricity, HALT/RLM), and invasive simultaneous pressures. The pattern of (1) CT‑confirmed constrained frame + immediate gradient fall with ballooning = underexpansion; (2) CT with normal expanded frame + chronically high, unresponsive gradient = PPM; (3) CT showing HALT/RLM + subacute gradient rise = thrombosis amenable to anticoagulation — that triad will solve most dilemmas after TAVI.

### [Clinical Pearls] How can lifetime management principles be applied at the time of the first TAVI to preserve future options, specifically regarding frame height, commissural alignment, coronary access, and the risk of patient–prosthesis mismatch?

When you plan the first TAVI in a patient who will likely need valves for life, you must think beyond the immediate procedure and deliberately preserve anatomical and technical options for future reinterventions. The practical principles are simple in concept but require disciplined planning and execution.

Choose a valve and implantation strategy with future access and haemodynamics in mind. Valve frame height and leaflet position matter: shorter frames or those with large open cells (and balloon‑expandable platforms with lower skirts) facilitate coronary access and future procedures, whereas tall, densely meshed frames increase the risk of coronary obstruction and make reaccess or BASILICA-type maneuvers more difficult. Conversely, supra‑annular valves often give superior effective orifice area (EOA) in small annuli and reduce the lifetime risk of patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM). You therefore must weigh coronary reaccess needs against haemodynamic performance for the individual patient.

Make commissural alignment routine. Pre‑procedure CT should define the orientation of the coronary ostia and provide fluoroscopic landmarks. During deployment, align the prosthesis so its commissures do not sit in front of the coronary ostia (use manufacturer marker guidance and rotation maneuvers). Good commissural alignment preserves coronary access, reduces the cumulative obstruction when a second valve is implanted, and simplifies any future coronary intervention.

Protect coronary access proactively. Measure coronary heights, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and valve‑to‑coronary distances on CT. If margins are small, prefer valve choices and implantation depths that minimize neo‑skirt formation at the ostia; consider prophylactic strategies (guidewire protection, chimney stenting, or planning for BASILICA if appropriate) when anatomy is at risk. Document valve orientation fluoroscopically and in the CT repository so future operators know where commissures and open cells are.

Minimise long‑term PPM from the start. In patients with small annuli or younger patients, preferentially choose a valve with the largest expected EOA (often supra‑annular designs) and avoid undersizing. If surgical root enlargement is an option at the outset, discuss it with the patient. When TAVI is chosen, aim for full expansion (careful sizing, balloon post‑dilation where safe) and plan implants that leave room for future valve expansion or deliberate bioprosthetic valve fracture if that is part of the lifetime plan.

Tie these elements together with imaging and documentation. High‑quality pre‑TAVI CT, intent to achieve commissural alignment, explicit choice of valve based on the trade‑offs between EOA and frame geometry, and clear operative notes (marker orientation, implant depth, any post‑dilation pressures used) all preserve options. In the case you presented, CT‑guided high implantation, deliberate commissural alignment and selection of a low‑height supra‑annular valve balanced the competing goals: maintained coronary flow and access while maximising EOA in a constrained triple‑stented anatomy—exactly the lifetime‑minded decisions you want at the first TAVI.

In short: plan for the next procedure at the first procedure—select valve platform with future access and haemodynamic needs in mind, align commissures, optimise implant depth to protect coronaries, and choose sizing/technique to minimise PPM. Document everything so future teams can execute those plans.
