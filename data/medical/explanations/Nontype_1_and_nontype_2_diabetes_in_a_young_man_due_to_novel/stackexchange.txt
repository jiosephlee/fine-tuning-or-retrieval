### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C-peptide, and a multigenerational history of early-onset diabetes, which diagnosis should be strongly considered and what is its typical inheritance pattern?

Think of maturity‑onset diabetes of the young — specifically HNF1A‑MODY (MODY3). The clinical pattern — young age at presentation, marked hyperglycaemia with glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved (stimulated) C‑peptide, and a multigenerational history of early‑onset diabetes — points away from autoimmune type 1 and from classic insulin‑resistant type 2 disease and toward a monogenic β‑cell defect. HNF1A mutations classically reduce β‑cell insulin secretion (and lower renal SGLT2 expression, explaining glycosuria), are the commonest MODY subtype, and are inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion; confirming diagnosis by targeted genetic testing alters therapy, prognosis and family counselling.

### [Morning Report] Which clinical features in this case most strongly argue against classic type 1 diabetes at presentation?

The features that most strongly argue against classic type 1 diabetes in this case are the combination of negative islet autoantibodies, demonstrable endogenous insulin secretion, and a clear autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes.

Clinically, classic type 1 diabetes at presentation is usually (though not always) accompanied by positive autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8) and rapid autoimmune β‑cell loss producing very low or absent C‑peptide and a propensity to present with ketosis/DKA. This patient had negative GAD and IA‑2 antibodies, no DKA despite marked hyperglycaemia, and a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (above usual cutoffs used to define preserved β‑cell function). Those findings point away from active autoimmune β‑cell destruction and toward a non‑autoimmune cause of insulin deficiency. The pedigree of multiple first‑degree relatives with early‑onset diabetes strongly favors a monogenic (MODY) process rather than sporadic type 1 disease.

Put together, negative autoantibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide + autosomal‑dominant early‑onset family history are the most discriminating features arguing against classic type 1 diabetes in this patient.

### [Clinical Pearls] What fasting and stimulated C-peptide thresholds support preserved endogenous insulin secretion and argue against absolute insulin deficiency (e.g., after a glucagon stimulation test)?

Clinical pearl: preserved endogenous insulin secretion is best demonstrated by C‑peptide, and thresholds differ for fasting versus stimulated testing because stimulation unmasks reserve that fasting levels can miss. A fasting (or random, with concurrent glucose) C‑peptide above ~0.2 nmol/L (≈0.6 ng/mL) generally indicates endogenous insulin production and makes absolute insulin deficiency unlikely. After a physiological or pharmacologic stimulus (mixed‑meal tolerance test or glucagon stimulation), a peak/stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (≈1.8 ng/mL) is widely used to define clinically meaningful residual β‑cell function and argues strongly against complete insulin deficiency (classic type 1 presentation).

The clinical reasoning is that modest fasting C‑peptide can be suppressed by hyperglycaemia, recent hypoglycaemia, or insulin therapy, so a stimulated test (glucagon at 6 minutes or an MMTT at 90–120 minutes) better reveals β‑cell reserve. Interpret values in the context of the glucose at the time of sampling, assay units (1 ng/mL ≈ 0.331 nmol/L), timing of the stimulation, and clinical context (autoantibodies, duration of disease). Values below these cutoffs are supportive—but not absolute proof—of severe β‑cell failure; borderline results warrant repeat or alternative stimulated testing and integration with autoantibody testing and clinical picture (age, family history) before labeling someone insulin‑deficient.

### [Pathophysiology] Why does HNF1A-MODY often present with glycosuria at relatively lower plasma glucose levels, and which renal transporter is directly implicated?

HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in the liver, pancreas and renal proximal tubule. One of its direct transcriptional targets in the kidney is SGLT2, the sodium–glucose cotransporter located in the early proximal tubule that mediates the bulk (~90%) of filtered glucose reabsorption. Loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression, lowering the kidney’s maximal reabsorptive capacity (Tm) and thus the renal threshold for glucose. Clinically this means patients with HNF1A‑MODY begin spilling glucose in the urine at plasma glucose concentrations that would be below the usual threshold — hence glycosuria at relatively lower plasma glucose levels. This same mechanism explains the caution around using SGLT2 inhibitors in these patients, because further reduction of SGLT2 activity can provoke excessive glycosuria, dehydration and risk of euglycaemic ketoacidosis.

### [Morning Report] Which autoantibodies are most informative when evaluating for autoimmune type 1 diabetes in a young adult, and how do negative results influence the diagnostic workup?

When you’re trying to distinguish autoimmune type 1 diabetes from other causes of early‑onset hyperglycaemia, the most informative autoantibodies to order are glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma‑associated‑2 antibodies (IA‑2A), zinc transporter 8 antibodies (ZnT8), and—if the patient has not yet received exogenous insulin—insulin autoantibodies (IAA). Historically islet cell cytoplasmic antibodies (ICA) were used, but they add little beyond the above assays.

Why these matter: GADA is the most sensitive single marker in adults with new‑onset type 1 diabetes; IA‑2A and ZnT8 increase sensitivity and specificity when combined, and IAA is useful early in disease in children or before insulin exposure. A profile with two or more positive high‑titer antibodies is strongly supportive of autoimmune type 1 disease; a single low‑titer antibody is less definitive and needs clinical correlation.

How negatives change your diagnostic reasoning: negative GADA/IA‑2A/ZnT8/IAA substantially reduces the probability of autoimmune type 1 diabetes but does not rule it out entirely. False‑negative results can occur if testing is done long after disease onset (antibodies can wane), if assays vary in sensitivity, or with atypical immune phenotypes. Therefore negative antibodies should prompt you to integrate other data: measure C‑peptide (fasting and stimulated) to assess endogenous insulin secretion; review tempo of hyperglycaemia, presence/absence of ketoacidosis, BMI and features of insulin resistance, and detailed family history. If C‑peptide is preserved or only modestly reduced, antibodies are repeatedly negative, and there is a strong autosomal dominant family history or atypical course, pursue genetic testing for monogenic diabetes (MODY). Conversely, rapidly falling C‑peptide or development of DKA despite negative antibodies should keep autoimmune type 1 high on your differential and warrant repeat antibody testing and close follow‑up.

Apply this to the case: the patient had negative GADA and IA‑2A, a stimulated C‑peptide indicating residual β‑cell function, and a striking family history of early‑onset diabetes. The negative antibody panel lowered the likelihood of classic autoimmune type 1 disease and, along with preserved C‑peptide and pedigree, correctly led to targeted genetic testing that confirmed HNF1A‑MODY.

### [Clinical Pearls] When should genetic testing for MODY be pursued in young-onset diabetes, and what pretest clinical criteria increase the likelihood of a positive result?

Think of MODY when the diabetes story doesn’t fit type 1 or type 2 — and then test, because confirmation changes therapy, prognosis and family counselling.

Pursue genetic testing when diabetes begins young and the phenotype is atypical for type 1 or type 2. The highest yield is in patients with onset in adolescence or early adulthood (classically <25 years, though some use <35), a strong multigenerational pattern of diabetes, and clinical features that argue against autoimmune type 1 or classic insulin‑resistant type 2 disease. In practice you should move to molecular testing when negative islet autoantibodies and preserved β‑cell function make type 1 unlikely and there is suspicion for a monogenic cause—especially if identifying a mutation would change therapy (for example, the likelihood that sulfonylureas or a GLP‑1 receptor agonist will suffice and insulin can be stopped).

Clinical features that meaningfully increase the pretest probability and therefore support testing are a family history of diabetes in an autosomal dominant pattern (diabetes in ≥2 successive generations, early onset in relatives), diabetes diagnosed at a young age (adolescence to early adulthood), absent or low‑titer islet autoantibodies, and evidence of endogenous insulin secretion out of proportion to type 1 disease (stimulated C‑peptide usually preserved — often quoted thresholds >0.2–0.6 nmol/L depending on the assay and clinical context). Additional supportive clues are marked postprandial hyperglycaemia with glycosuria (suggestive of HNF1A), low insulin requirements long after diagnosis (not requiring insulin or requiring low doses), lack of obesity or other features of insulin resistance (though rising obesity blunts this), absence of ketoacidosis at presentation, and extrapancreatic findings when present (for example renal glycosuria, developmental renal cysts or specific renal/liver features for some genes).

Use clinical tools (for example the Exeter MODY probability calculator) as an adjunct but not a substitute for judgement; these tools were derived in predominantly European populations and can under‑ or over‑estimate risk in other ethnic groups. If the pretest probability is moderate to high (many groups use thresholds such as >10–25% or a calculator estimate above the locally validated cut‑off), proceed to targeted NGS panels for common MODY genes rather than single‑gene testing. Finally, remember the practical principle illustrated by this case: targeted genetic diagnosis allows precision therapy (often discontinuing insulin in HNF1A‑MODY), directs surveillance and enables cascade testing of relatives.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the role of the HNF1A transcription factor in pancreatic beta cells and how pathogenic variants lead to progressive beta-cell dysfunction.

HNF1A is a lineage‑defining transcription factor expressed in pancreatic β cells (and liver and kidney) that coordinates a program of genes required for mature β‑cell glucose sensing, stimulus–secretion coupling and metabolic competence. Mechanistically, HNF1A binds promoter regions and activates transcription of key targets including the insulin gene, the facilitative glucose transporter GLUT2, enzymes of glycolysis (eg, pyruvate kinase), and other genes involved in β‑cell metabolism and ion‑channel regulation. The net effect is to set the β cell’s ability to detect rises in blood glucose and convert that signal into appropriate insulin secretion.

Heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variants (most commonly loss‑of‑function or dominant‑negative changes, often in the DNA‑binding domain) reduce expression of these target genes. Early on this produces a relatively selective defect in glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion: fasting insulin may be preserved but postprandial insulin responses are blunted, so patients first develop marked postprandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. Over time two processes drive progressive β‑cell failure. First, the chronic reduction of glucose sensing and metabolic flux lowers acute insulin secretory capacity at each encounter with glucose. Second, impaired expression of genes that support β‑cell health leads to functional decompensation and, in some cases, diminished β‑cell mass. The result is a slowly progressive decline from compensated impaired glucose tolerance toward overt insulin deficiency.

This pathophysiology explains several clinical pearls: HNF1A‑MODY often presents in adolescence or young adulthood with prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia and low renal glucose threshold (glycosuria), is typically antibody‑negative with measurable C‑peptide early on, and is unusually sensitive to insulin secretagogues (sulfonylureas) while later requiring therapies that preserve or replace β‑cell function (GLP‑1 receptor agonists or insulin). Genotype matters—mutations in the DNA‑binding domain commonly produce clearer loss of transcriptional activity and more typical MODY phenotypes—so genetic diagnosis both clarifies mechanism and guides precision therapy.

### [Pathophysiology] Which beta-cell genes and pathways are downregulated in HNF1A-MODY (e.g., insulin, GLUT2, glycolytic enzymes), and how does this affect glucose-stimulated insulin secretion?

In HNF1A-MODY the primary defect is loss of a β‑cell transcription factor, so the phenotype reflects reduced expression of a set of genes that together implement glucose sensing, metabolism and insulin production. Key downregulated targets include the insulin gene itself (reduced insulin biosynthesis), the principal β‑cell facilitative glucose transporter GLUT2, and enzymes of the proximal glycolytic/energy‑generating pathway such as glucokinase and downstream kinases (reports also note lower pyruvate kinase activity). HNF1A also regulates other genes that support stimulus‑secretion coupling and β‑cell health (for example genes involved in mitochondrial metabolism and components of the secretory apparatus), and in the kidney it regulates SGLT2 explaining glycosuria in these patients.

Functionally, these changes converge to blunt glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion. Lower GLUT2 and/or glucokinase expression reduces glucose entry and phosphorylation, so glycolytic flux and ATP production in response to glucose are attenuated. The ATP/ADP rise that normally closes KATP channels is therefore smaller, membrane depolarization is inadequate, voltage‑dependent Ca2+ channel opening is reduced, and insulin granule exocytosis is diminished. Concurrently, reduced insulin gene transcription lowers stored insulin available for release. Clinically this produces prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia early in the disease, progressive loss of β‑cell secretory capacity, and preserved (but insufficient) fasting C‑peptide in early stages.

This pathophysiology explains therapeutic responses: sulfonylureas bypass impaired glucose sensing by directly closing KATP channels to trigger insulin release, whereas incretin agonists augment cAMP‑dependent exocytosis and can improve secretion without provoking the same hypoglycaemia risk. Recognizing the transcriptional basis of the defect is therefore crucial for targeted treatment and prognostication, as illustrated in this case.

### [Clinical Pearls] What oral glucose tolerance test pattern is commonly seen in HNF1A-MODY, and why might fasting glucose be less abnormal early in the disease?

In HNF1A‑MODY the classic OGTT pattern is relatively preserved fasting glucose with marked post‑load hyperglycaemia — a large incremental rise after the 75‑g load and a very high 2‑hour glucose. Mechanistically this reflects an early, selective defect in glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion: first‑phase insulin (and early C‑peptide) responses are blunted, so the pancreas cannot contain the postprandial excursion, whereas basal (tonic) insulin secretion that maintains overnight/fasting glucose is often relatively preserved early in the disease. In addition, HNF1A mutations impair glucose sensing (reduced GLUT2 expression and downstream glycolytic flux), so β‑cells fail when challenged by a glucose load even though fasting glycaemia may remain near normal. Clinically, therefore, an OGTT or postprandial glucose measurement is more sensitive than a single fasting glucose to detect HNF1A‑MODY in early stages.

### [Management] What is the recommended first-line pharmacologic therapy for HNF1A-MODY and the physiological rationale for its efficacy?

First‑line pharmacologic therapy for HNF1A‑MODY is low‑dose oral sulfonylurea therapy (eg, low‑dose gliclazide or glimepiride). The choice is driven by the specific pathophysiology: HNF1A mutations cause a primary, progressive β‑cell secretory defect with impaired glucose sensing (reduced expression of insulin, GLUT2 and enzymes needed for glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion), so the problem is inadequate insulin release rather than insulin resistance. Sulfonylureas close the β‑cell KATP channel directly and trigger insulin exocytosis independent of the impaired glucose sensing pathway, restoring insulin secretion and often producing marked glycaemic responses at lower doses than used in typical type 2 diabetes. Clinically this manifests as robust reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose and often permits discontinuation of insulin in affected patients and relatives.

Practical caveats: use lower doses because HNF1A patients are particularly sensitive to sulfonylureas and therefore at increased risk of hypoglycaemia; monitor β‑cell function over time because progressive decline may require switching therapy. As β‑cell function wanes or when hypoglycaemia risk is high, GLP‑1 receptor agonists (or other incretin‑based agents) are reasonable alternatives because they enhance glucose‑dependent insulin secretion with lower hypoglycaemia risk and may preserve weight and β‑cell function. Avoid empirical use of agents that target insulin resistance (eg, sole metformin) as first‑line unless there is coexisting insulin resistance, and use SGLT2 inhibitors with caution given altered renal glucose handling in HNF1A‑MODY.

### [Management] In which clinical scenarios might a GLP-1 receptor agonist be preferred over sulfonylureas for HNF1A-MODY, and what are the advantages and trade-offs?

Sulfonylureas have been the classic “precision” therapy for HNF1A‑MODY because the mutant β cell retains exaggerated sensitivity to secretagogues; a low dose often produces large drops in glucose. But in everyday practice there are several clinical situations in which a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (GLP‑1 RA) is the preferred alternative. Use that choice when the patient has one or more of the following: a high baseline HbA1c requiring robust and durable lowering without provoking hypoglycaemia; a history or high risk of hypoglycaemia (older age, erratic meals, prior severe lows); clinically important overweight/obesity or features of metabolic syndrome where weight loss would help; established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or high ASCVD risk for which certain GLP‑1 RAs have outcome data; intolerance or contraindication to sulfonylureas; or when the patient prefers to avoid the weight gain and hypoglycaemia risk of a sulfonylurea. The case you presented fits several of these reasons — marked hyperglycaemia with metabolic abnormalities and concern about preserving β‑cell function — and the patient responded well to dulaglutide.

The advantages of a GLP‑1 RA in HNF1A‑MODY are lower hypoglycaemia risk compared with sulfonylureas, consistent weight loss (or at least avoidance of weight gain), potential cardiovascular benefit with specific agents, and an incretin‑mediated insulinotropic effect that can be effective even as β‑cell function declines. Small crossover trials and case series in HNF1A‑MODY show similar glycaemic efficacy to sulfonylureas with less hypoglycaemia; clinically, GLP‑1 RAs also allow simplification away from insulin in some patients.

The trade‑offs are important and must be discussed. GLP‑1 RAs are more costly and most are injectable, which affects adherence and access. They commonly cause gastrointestinal side effects and are contraindicated or not recommended in pregnancy and in patients with certain personal or family histories (eg, medullary thyroid cancer or MEN2). For patients with very preserved β‑cell function who respond dramatically to low‑dose sulfonylurea, sulfonylureas remain an effective, inexpensive option; sulfonylureas can produce a more rapid initial glucose lowering. Long‑term comparative data in MODY are limited, so individual response should guide therapy. Finally, monitor C‑peptide and glycaemic response after a therapeutic switch — if incretin therapy fails to maintain control, be prepared to add or change agents (including low‑dose sulfonylurea or insulin) while continuing genetic‑informed follow‑up.

In short, reserve GLP‑1 RAs for HNF1A‑MODY patients in whom hypoglycaemia avoidance, weight/Cardio‑metabolic benefit, intolerance to sulfonylureas, or declining β‑cell reserve make sulfonylurea therapy unattractive; weigh those clinical benefits against cost, route of administration, GI adverse effects and pregnancy considerations, and individualize therapy with close monitoring.

### [Management] Why should SGLT2 inhibitors be used with caution or avoided in HNF1A-MODY, and what specific adverse risks are heightened in this genotype?

The key point is that HNF1A mutations reduce expression of the renal SGLT2 transporter, so patients with HNF1A‑MODY already have a lower renal glucose‑reabsorption threshold and baseline glycosuria. Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor therefore has both reduced incremental glycaemic benefit and a higher risk of adverse effects from excessive renal glucose loss and volume depletion.

Mechanism and clinical consequences: HNF1A is a transcription factor that drives SGLT2 expression in proximal tubules. Haploinsufficiency → chronically lower SGLT2 levels → more glycosuria at a given plasma glucose. Pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition on top of that can produce marked osmotic diuresis and electrolyte-free water loss. Clinically this predisposes to severe dehydration, orthostatic hypotension and acute kidney injury, and case reports (and the case series literature) describe frank intravascular volume collapse in HNF1A‑MODY patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors. Because many patients with HNF1A‑MODY have relatively limited β‑cell reserve, the combination of reduced insulin availability and increased glucagon/ketone production seen with SGLT2 inhibitors increases the risk of (often euglycaemic) diabetic ketoacidosis. Genital mycotic infections are also more likely because glycosuria is amplified.

What to do in practice: therefore avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors in genetically confirmed HNF1A‑MODY; if considered in exceptional circumstances, use extreme caution—assess baseline volume status and renal function, counsel and monitor for dehydration and genital infections, instruct on frequent ketone monitoring and stop the drug rapidly when ill or reduced intake. Prefer therapies with proven efficacy and safety in HNF1A‑MODY (low‑dose sulfonylureas early, GLP‑1 receptor agonists, metformin as indicated) guided by the genetic diagnosis.

### [Management] How would you manage initial severe hyperglycaemia in a young adult with suspected MODY before genetic confirmation, and when is it appropriate to taper or discontinue insulin?

When you encounter a young adult with marked hyperglycaemia and clinical features suggesting MODY, treat the metabolic state first and the diagnostic uncertainty second. Severe hyperglycaemia—even without ketoacidosis—causes glucotoxicity that suppresses endogenous insulin secretion and risks dehydration and other complications; brief insulin is a safe, effective way to reverse that while you establish the diagnosis.

Start with insulin for real-world reasons: rapid, titratable glucose lowering, prevention of ketosis/dehydration, and time to perform antibody and C‑peptide testing and arrange genetic testing. A pragmatic approach is low‑to‑moderate doses of basal insulin (eg, the degludec 6–12 U/day range used in the case, adjusted to weight and glucose) ± short‑acting insulin for persistent postprandial excursions until glucose is controlled. Continue metformin if tolerated (often helpful) and avoid agents that could increase risk (eg, SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A with marked glycosuria until genetic status is known).

While insulin is being used, collect diagnostic data that guide when it is safe to wean: pancreatic autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8), fasting and stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon or mixed‑meal), pattern of glycosuria, family history, and—if available—genetic testing. The key clinical signal for considering discontinuation of insulin is evidence of preserved endogenous insulin secretion plus stable or improving glycaemia off high insulin doses.

Operational triggers to taper/discontinue insulin
- No evidence of type‑1 autoimmunity (negative GAD/IA‑2/ZnT8).  
- Preserved C‑peptide: a stimulated C‑peptide in the commonly used range for “preserved” (in many labs >0.6 nmol/L, as in this case) or at least not in the frankly insulin‑deficient range. A fasting C‑peptide >0.2–0.3 nmol/L with a stimulated rise supports residual β‑cell function.  
- Clinical improvement on minimal insulin (falling glucose/HbA1c) and absence of ketosis.  
- Either genetic confirmation of an HNF1A mutation or very high pretest probability (strong family history, typical phenotype).  

How to taper safely
- Prefer a stepwise reduction: stop prandial insulin first if used, reduce basal by 20–30% every 3–7 days while monitoring fasting and premeal glucoses. If glycaemia remains acceptable, continue tapering until basal can be stopped.  
- Use frequent SMBG or CGM during the transition (multiple daily checks for the first 1–2 weeks). Aim for fasting and premeal glucose targets agreed with the patient (eg, fasting <130–140 mg/dL and premeal <180 mg/dL) without recurrent hyperglycaemic symptoms.  
- If glucose rises above target during taper or ketosis/emergency develops, resume or increase insulin and re‑evaluate.  
- Once insulin is discontinued, initiate the planned oral/GLP‑1 therapy promptly—HNF1A‑MODY is typically very sulfonylurea‑sensitive, and GLP‑1 receptor agonists or metformin are reasonable alternatives depending on age, weight, hypoglycaemia risk and patient preference. Monitor HbA1c and C‑peptide over months.

Apply this to the case: initial basal insulin plus metformin was appropriate to correct marked hyperglycaemia. The stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, negative autoantibodies and strong family history supported preserved β‑cell function and high likelihood of HNF1A‑MODY; after genetic confirmation and clinical improvement (HbA1c falling), discontinuing insulin and starting a GLP‑1 agonist (or low‑dose sulfonylurea) was reasonable. The transition should be gradual, closely monitored, and reversible if glycaemia deteriorates.

In short: use insulin short term to reverse glucotoxicity and prevent complications; decide to taper when autoantibodies are negative, C‑peptide shows meaningful endogenous insulin secretion, and glycaemia is improving—ideally after genetic confirmation but sooner if clinical evidence is strong—performing a cautious stepwise taper with close glucose monitoring.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which MODY subtype is characterized by lifelong mild fasting hyperglycaemia, minimal postprandial excursions, and generally requires no pharmacologic therapy?

The description fits glucokinase (GCK)–MODY (also called MODY2). GCK heterozygous loss‑of‑function mutations reset the glucose “set point” of the pancreatic β‑cell sensor, producing lifelong, mild fasting hyperglycaemia with only small postprandial excursions and little progressive β‑cell failure. Patients commonly have modestly raised fasting glucose and HbA1c (often in the non‑diabetic to mild diabetic range), are frequently identified incidentally, and do not usually require pharmacologic therapy because glucose levels are stable and vascular risk from the mild hyperglycaemia is low. Important caveats are recognition in pregnancy (management changes if the fetus does not carry the mutation) and distinguishing GCK‑MODY from progressive forms such as HNF1A‑MODY, which show rising hyperglycaemia and generally benefit from sulfonylureas or other therapy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] List distinguishing clinical features between HNF1A-MODY and HNF1B-MODY that can guide targeted genetic testing.

HNF1A‑MODY and HNF1B‑MODY can both present as young‑onset, non‑autoimmune diabetes, but they differ clinically in ways that should direct targeted genetic testing.

Think first about extrapancreatic findings. HNF1B (the “renal cysts and diabetes” or RCAD syndrome) commonly produces conspicuous renal and genitourinary abnormalities: structural kidney disease on ultrasound (cysts, small or hyperechogenic kidneys), early chronic kidney disease, electrolyte derangements—most classically renal magnesium wasting with hypomagnesemia—and sometimes hyperuricaemia/gout. Genital tract malformations (eg, bicornuate uterus, epididymal or seminal tract anomalies) and pancreatic hypoplasia are frequent. These non‑glycaemic features are the cardinal clue pointing to HNF1B testing.

By contrast, HNF1A‑MODY is primarily a beta‑cell disorder with few consistent structural extra‑organ abnormalities. Patients usually have a clear multigenerational autosomal dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes, progressive insulin secretory failure with preserved C‑peptide for years, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia, and marked glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose because HNF1A regulates SGLT2. Glycosuria and sensitivity to low‑dose sulfonylureas (or excellent response to GLP‑1 agonists/sulfonylureas allowing withdrawal of insulin) favour HNF1A. Typical metabolic syndrome features are usually absent or less prominent than in T2D.

Other practical discriminators: HNF1B mutations are often sporadic (a high rate of de novo mutations), so lack of multigenerational diabetes does not exclude HNF1B. HNF1B patients more frequently have reduced renal function or early CKD and abnormal urinalysis independent of glycaemia; HNF1A patients typically have normal kidneys aside from glycosuria. Autoantibodies are negative in both, so negative GADA/IA‑2 does not distinguish them. Imaging (renal ultrasound, pelvic imaging) and simple labs (serum magnesium, uric acid, creatinine) are therefore high‑yield when deciding to sequence HNF1B. Conversely, a strong family history of autosomal dominant diabetes, prominent glycosuria, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, and marked response to sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 therapy should prioritise HNF1A testing.

In short: pursue HNF1B testing when diabetes is accompanied by renal structural disease, early CKD, hypomagnesemia or genital tract anomalies (even without family history). Pursue HNF1A testing when there is multigenerational early‑onset diabetes, prominent glycosuria with progressive β‑cell failure and good response to low‑dose insulin secretagogues.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the Exeter MODY probability calculator, which clinical inputs drive its estimate, and what are its limitations in non-White populations?

The Exeter MODY probability calculator is a clinical prediction tool developed by the Exeter (UK) group to estimate the probability that a patient with young‑onset diabetes has monogenic MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes. It is intended as a screening aid to prioritize who should be offered genetic testing, not as a diagnostic test.

The calculator combines routinely available clinical data into a logistic model: the patient’s age at diagnosis of diabetes, body mass index (BMI), current or recent HbA1c, whether the patient required insulin soon after diagnosis (insulin within 6 months), parental history of diabetes (affected parent), and islet autoantibody status (GAD antibody positive or negative). Those inputs are weighted because they reflect the underlying pathophysiology of common diabetes types: earlier age at onset, absence of insulin requirement at presentation and negative autoantibodies increase the likelihood of MODY; lower BMI and strong parental history also favor monogenic disease.

Clinical reasoning for each input: age at diagnosis captures the typical young onset of MODY; BMI and HbA1c help distinguish insulin‑resistant T2D from primary β‑cell failure; early and persistent insulin requirement and positive GAD point toward autoimmune type 1 disease; and a parent with diabetes raises the prior probability because most MODY subtypes are autosomal dominant.

Limitations in non‑White populations
The model was derived and validated mainly in White European cohorts, so its calibration and the relative weights of predictors may not generalize. In many non‑White populations the prevalence, age‑structure, BMI distribution and family patterns of type 1 and type 2 diabetes differ, and rates of obesity and early‑onset T2D are often higher—factors that can make the calculator overestimate the probability of MODY (or alter sensitivity and specificity). Autoantibody testing performance, background prevalence of parental diabetes, and the spectrum/frequency of monogenic variants also differ across ethnic groups. Practically, that means a high Exeter score in a non‑White patient should prompt consideration of genetic testing but not be taken as definitive; conversely a low score should not exclude testing when clinical suspicion is strong (clear autosomal dominant family history, non‑obese young patient with glycosuria or preserved C‑peptide). In short, use the calculator as a guide, interpret it in the ethnic and clinical context of the patient, and have a low threshold for genetic testing when precision diagnosis would change management.

### [Pathophysiology] Which circulating biomarker is often lower in HNF1A-MODY than in type 1 or type 2 diabetes and can support the diagnosis when genetic testing is not immediately available?

The circulating biomarker is high‑sensitivity C‑reactive protein (hs‑CRP). HNF1A is a hepatic transcription factor that directly regulates CRP synthesis; pathogenic HNF1A variants reduce hepatic CRP expression, so patients with HNF1A‑MODY tend to have lower baseline hs‑CRP than people with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

Clinically this is useful as a screening aid when genetic testing is not immediately available: markedly low hs‑CRP in a young patient with non‑autoimmune diabetes and a suggestive family history raises the pretest probability of HNF1A‑MODY and prioritises sequencing. Typical proposed cut points vary by study, but an hs‑CRP in the very low range (for example <0.5 mg/L, with some studies using <0.75 mg/L or <0.2 mg/L to increase specificity) is more consistent with HNF1A‑MODY than with common diabetes. Important caveats are that acute inflammation, infection, smoking, obesity, statin use and other hepatic conditions alter hs‑CRP and can confound interpretation; a single low value is supportive but not diagnostic, and normal or only mildly reduced hs‑CRP does not exclude HNF1A‑MODY. Genetic testing remains the gold standard.

### [Management] What is the inheritance risk for offspring when a pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified in a proband, and how should this inform genetic counselling?

When a pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified in a proband, the key inheritance message is simple and actionable: HNF1A-MODY is transmitted in an autosomal dominant manner, so each biological child of the proband has an approximately 50% chance of inheriting the variant.

Why that matters for counselling: autosomal dominant means a single heterozygous pathogenic allele is sufficient to cause disease risk, but expression is age-dependent and variable. HNF1A has relatively high but incomplete and age-related penetrance — many carriers develop clinically manifest diabetes by young or middle adulthood, but onset and severity vary between families and even between affected members. Therefore a positive test predicts substantially increased lifetime risk and commonly earlier onset diabetes, whereas a negative test in a child or relative effectively rules out HNF1A-MODY as the explanation for early-onset diabetes in that family and allows usual population screening/care.

Practical counselling points to communicate to the family
- Recurrence risk: each child of an affected parent has a ~50% chance of inheriting the familial HNF1A pathogenic variant. If the proband’s variant is confirmed de novo (no parental carrier), recurrence in siblings is low but each offspring of the proband remains at 50%.
- Testing strategy: offer targeted (cascade) genetic testing to first‑degree relatives (parents, siblings, children). Testing is most useful when it would change management or surveillance; because HNF1A disease typically manifests in adolescence or early adulthood, consider testing minors when results would alter clinical care (some centres offer testing in childhood if glycaemic surveillance or medication strategies would be affected); otherwise many teams defer until adolescence or when the family prefers earlier knowledge.
- Clinical follow-up for carriers: if a relative is found to carry the variant, institute earlier and more frequent glucose surveillance (fasting glucose, HbA1c, or OGTT as indicated), counsel about lifestyle, and tailor pharmacologic choices (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 agonists often effective; avoid misclassification as type 1). If negative, no special MODY-related follow-up is required beyond routine diabetes screening.
- Reproductive options: explain reproductive choices for carriers who wish to avoid transmission — prenatal diagnosis or preimplantation genetic testing are available when the familial pathogenic variant is known.
- Psychosocial and legal considerations: discuss the emotional impact, potential insurance or employment implications that depend on local law, and refer to genetic counselling for informed consent and detailed discussion.

Finally, coordinate cascade testing through a genetics service (or an experienced endocrine genetics clinic) so family members get pre‑ and post‑test counselling, proper interpretation (including discussion of penetrance and variable expressivity), and a plan linking genetic results to surveillance and treatment decisions.

### [Morning Report] Using ACMG criteria, what lines of evidence support classifying a novel missense HNF1A variant as likely pathogenic (e.g., location in a functional domain, segregation, in silico prediction, absence from population databases)?

Teaching answer:

Interpretation of a novel missense HNF1A change as “likely pathogenic” requires combining multiple independent ACMG lines of evidence rather than reliance on any single datum. In this case the following ACMG criteria are met and together support a likely pathogenic classification.

First, the variant lies in the HNF1A DNA‑binding domain, a well‑established functional region enriched for disease‑causing missense changes (ACMG PM1, moderate). Pathogenic HNF1A mutations concentrate in this domain and alterations there have a biologically plausible effect on transcriptional activity relevant to beta‑cell function.

Second, the substitution is absent from large population databases (gnomAD/ExAC/1000G), supporting rarity incompatible with a common benign allele (ACMG PM2, moderate). A true MODY‑causing allele should not be present at appreciable frequency in population reference sets.

Third, the variant segregates with early‑onset diabetes in the pedigree (affected mother, maternal grandmother, affected brother), providing familial cosegregation evidence (ACMG PP1, supporting). Even modest segregation in multiple affected relatives increases the posterior probability of pathogenicity for an autosomal dominant condition like HNF1A‑MODY.

Fourth, computational pathogenicity predictors give strong deleterious signal (high AlphaMissense score 0.995 and concordant in‑silico tools), supplying supporting in‑silico evidence (ACMG PP3, supporting).

Fifth, the residue is adjacent to or at a site where other pathogenic missense changes have been reported, permitting application of PM5 (moderate) or PM5_supporting depending on the exact context (novel amino‑acid change at/near a known pathogenic residue increases the probability that the residue/region is functionally constrained).

Finally, the clinical phenotype is highly consistent with HNF1A‑MODY (young onset, negative islet autoantibodies, glycosuria, progressive β‑cell dysfunction, family history, treatment response), which meets ACMG PP4 (supporting) because the observed phenotype is specific for defects in this gene.

Putting these together — two or three moderate criteria (PM1, PM2, PM5) plus several supporting criteria (PP1, PP3, PP4) — reaches the ACMG threshold for likely pathogenic. Functional assay data (PS3, strong) are lacking, so a definitive “pathogenic” call would require additional strong evidence (well‑validated functional studies or stronger segregation). In practice, this composite genetic plus clinical evidence justifies reporting the variant as likely pathogenic and using it to guide diagnosis, cascade testing, and precision therapy for HNF1A‑MODY.

### [Pathophysiology] Where are most pathogenic HNF1A mutations located within the protein, and why is a variant in the DNA-binding domain biologically plausible as disease-causing?

Most pathogenic HNF1A mutations cluster in the N‑terminal DNA‑binding region of the protein rather than the C‑terminal transactivation domain. That clustering is clinically important because the DNA‑binding domain is the part of HNF1A that recognizes and binds promoter sequences of key target genes (insulin, GLUT2, SGLT2 and others) and it mediates proper nuclear localization and formation of functional dimers. Missense changes in that region therefore directly impair the transcription factor’s ability to engage chromatin and drive gene expression in pancreatic β cells and renal tubule cells.

A novel missense change in the DNA‑binding domain is biologically plausible as disease‑causing for several reasons. First, heterozygous loss‑of‑function of HNF1A produces the classical autosomal‑dominant, progressive β‑cell secretory defect of HNF1A‑MODY; disrupting DNA binding phenocopies that loss. Second, amino‑acid substitutions in the DNA‑binding surface can reduce or abolish sequence‑specific binding, interfere with dimerization or nuclear import, or produce dominant‑negative effects — all mechanisms that reduce transcriptional activation of insulin and glucose‑handling genes and explain early-onset hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. Third, clinical and genetic data support pathogenicity: pathogenic missense variants are enriched in this domain, they segregate with disease in families, and in‑silico and functional assays (when available) commonly show impaired DNA binding/transactivation. Together, those mechanistic and genetic lines of evidence make a DNA‑binding domain variant a credible cause of HNF1A‑MODY.

### [Management] After confirming HNF1A-MODY in a proband, what cascade testing and clinical screening should be recommended for family members?

Once you have a confirmed pathogenic HNF1A variant in a proband, your next steps are deliberate cascade genetic testing combined with targeted clinical screening — because HNF1A-MODY is autosomal dominant, has high but age-dependent penetrance, and the result changes management.

First, offer genetic counselling and targeted HNF1A testing to first‑degree relatives (parents, siblings, children). Explain the ~50% transmission risk, the implications of a positive result for therapy and surveillance, and that a negative result in a relative excludes that familial MODY risk (but not ordinary type 2 risk). Extend testing to second‑degree relatives with early‑onset diabetes or suggest testing through the affected branch of the family; priority is relatives with diabetes diagnosed at young ages.

For relatives who test positive for the familial HNF1A variant, institute formal diabetes screening even if asymptomatic. Baseline evaluation should include fasting glucose and HbA1c; if these are normal but suspicion remains or for higher sensitivity in younger or early disease, perform a 75‑g OGTT to detect postprandial hyperglycaemia. Repeat testing at regular intervals (for adults at least annually; for adolescents or those approaching typical age of onset consider every 6–12 months) because onset is often in adolescence or early adulthood and penetrance increases with age. For children and adolescents, consider genetic testing in mid‑childhood/adolescence (practical thresholds vary, often from age 10–16) so management can be adjusted promptly when hyperglycaemia appears; perform clinical glucose surveillance more frequently if not tested genetically.

For variant carriers who meet diagnostic criteria for diabetes, tailor therapy to HNF1A-MODY: low‑dose sulfonylureas are first‑line historically, with GLP‑1 receptor agonists as a reasonable alternative to avoid hypoglycaemia and preserve β‑cell function; avoid reflexive insulin if not required. Counsel about SGLT2 inhibitors with caution — HNF1A carriers often have reduced renal glucose reabsorption and may be at higher risk of marked glycosuria, volume depletion and euglycemic DKA. Share these treatment implications during counselling.

Screening for complications mirrors routine diabetes care once diabetes is present: urine albumin-to‑creatinine ratio, serum creatinine and eGFR, blood pressure, fasting lipid profile, and annual retinal examination per diabetes guidelines. Start microvascular screening at diabetes diagnosis or within the usual guideline timeframes for the age group because hyperglycaemia can precede symptoms. For unaffected carriers, routine complication screening is not indicated until diabetes develops.

Address reproductive implications: offer testing and counselling to women of childbearing potential because HNF1A-MODY affects pregnancy management (higher likelihood of overt hyperglycaemia/GDM; treatment choice matters). Discuss implications for prenatal or preconception planning if desired.

Finally, document the family mutation in the medical record, provide a family letter explaining who should be tested, and coordinate testing through a genetics service when available. This focused cascade approach identifies at‑risk relatives, permits earlier detection and precise treatment, and prevents misclassification of MODY as type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

### [Management] How do microvascular complication risks in HNF1A-MODY compare with other forms of diabetes, and what screening schedule should be followed once the diagnosis is made?

Clinical pearl: microvascular complications in HNF1A‑MODY are driven by glycaemic exposure and disease duration rather than a fundamentally different microangiopathic biology; when matched for HbA1c and duration of hyperglycaemia, risks of retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy are similar to other forms of diabetes. In practice this means that early genetic diagnosis and prompt achievement of good glycaemic control (the goal in this case: HbA1c <7%, often <6.5% if safe) both reduce future microvascular risk. Some series suggest HNF1A‑MODY patients have lower prevalences of metabolic syndrome and macrovascular disease than typical T2DM cohorts, but this does not eliminate the need for routine microvascular surveillance — and individual features (for example the glycosuria and possible renal SGLT2 effects of HNF1A mutations) require attention when choosing therapies.

How to screen once HNF1A‑MODY is confirmed (practical, guideline‑oriented approach tailored to this case)
1) Glycaemic monitoring: measure HbA1c every 3 months until at target/stable, then every 6 months. Tight control early after diagnosis reduces cumulative exposure and lowers microvascular risk.

2) Eyes: perform a baseline dilated retinal examination (or validated fundus photography) at diagnosis because many MODY patients present after years of hyperglycaemia. If no retinopathy and glycaemia controlled, repeat annually; if any retinopathy, follow retinal specialist recommendations (often every 3–12 months depending on severity).

3) Kidneys: obtain baseline eGFR and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) at diagnosis. Repeat UACR and eGFR at least annually. If microalbuminuria (UACR 30–300 mg/g) appears, confirm on two of three samples, optimize glucose and BP control and start an ACE inhibitor or ARB as indicated. Be aware of the lower renal tubular glucose threshold in HNF1A—monitor volume status and caution with SGLT2 inhibitors.

4) Neuropathy: perform a baseline peripheral neuropathy screen (monofilament, vibration) and a focused autonomic symptom review. Given this patient’s documented sudomotor dysfunction, refer for formal autonomic testing and monitor more closely for orthostatic intolerance, gastroparesis and silent ischemia. Repeat peripheral neuropathy screening annually (sooner if symptoms).

5) Foot exam: at least annual comprehensive foot inspection and education; more frequently if neuropathy or vascular disease is present.

6) Cardiovascular risk factors: measure blood pressure and fasting lipids at baseline; manage to guideline targets (BP control, statin therapy as appropriate) because microvascular outcomes improve with good BP and lipid control.

7) Reproductive counseling: in women of childbearing potential, address preconception glycaemic optimization and retinal and renal assessments before pregnancy.

Rationale in one sentence: treat surveillance the same way you would for other chronic diabetes but start at diagnosis, because many MODY patients have prolonged asymptomatic hyperglycaemia, and individualize screening frequency when complications (for example autonomic neuropathy seen here) are already present.

### [Management] Describe the typical sensitivity of HNF1A-MODY to low-dose sulfonylureas and the main adverse effect to monitor when prescribing them.

Patients with HNF1A‑MODY are classically highly sensitive to low‑dose sulfonylureas. Because the defect is primarily a progressive beta‑cell secretory failure rather than insulin resistance, sulfonylureas—by closing KATP channels and amplifying residual insulin secretion—often produce a rapid and marked fall in glucose and HbA1c even at doses lower than used for typical type 2 diabetes; this is why many patients can be moved off insulin onto a single low‑dose sulfonylurea.

The principal adverse effect to monitor is hypoglycaemia. Given the exaggerated secretory response, even standard or modest doses can precipitate symptomatic or severe hypoglycaemia (and weight gain). Clinically, start very low and titrate slowly, educate patients about hypoglycaemia recognition and treatment, ensure frequent self‑monitoring of glucose during dose changes, and be particularly cautious in those with renal impairment, irregular oral intake, or concomitant medications that increase hypoglycaemia risk.

### [Clinical Pearls] How do rising obesity and metabolic syndrome prevalence impact the clinical suspicion for MODY, and why can BMI be misleading in modern cohorts?

In a single sentence: rising population rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome progressively erode the historical clinical “gap” that helped distinguish MODY from type 2 diabetes, so body‑mass index alone has become an unreliable gatekeeper for genetic testing.

Why this happens — clinical reasoning tied to the case: MODY is a primary β‑cell defect that traditionally presented in lean, young patients with a strong autosomal dominant family history and little evidence of insulin resistance. Those phenotypic clues guided clinicians to pursue antibody testing, C‑peptide assessment and, when suspected, genetic testing. As obesity and metabolic syndrome become more common, young people with monogenic diabetes can also carry excess weight, dyslipidaemia or features of insulin resistance (as in this patient with an elevated triglyceride level). Superimposed adiposity can accelerate hyperglycaemia, raise HbA1c, and generate the phenotypic overlap that leads to misclassification as type 2 diabetes.

Why BMI is misleading in modern cohorts: BMI is a crude, population‑level marker of adiposity that does not distinguish fat distribution, lean mass, or the relative contributions of insulin resistance versus impaired insulin secretion. Secular increases in BMI shift the baseline: many young people developing diabetes today will have higher BMIs than historical cohorts, so a “normal” BMI threshold that once argued against MODY no longer has the same negative predictive value. Ethnic differences in body composition and lower thresholds for metabolic risk further complicate interpretation. Finally, some MODY genotypes (HNF1A included) can coexist with lifestyle‑related insulin resistance; obesity may modify penetrance and phenotype without changing the underlying monogenic cause.

Clinical implications and pearl: do not exclude MODY solely because a patient is overweight or has features of metabolic syndrome. A combination of early age at onset, strong multi‑generation family history of early diabetes, absence of persistent autoantibodies, evidence of relative β‑cell failure (preserved but inadequate C‑peptide), marked postprandial glycaemia or unexplained glycosuria should lower the threshold for genetic testing despite an elevated BMI. Use probability tools (eg, Exeter calculator) with caution in non‑White populations and where obesity is prevalent; when clinical suspicion persists, proceed to targeted genetic testing because the diagnosis alters therapy, prognosis and family counselling.

### [Morning Report] How do you interpret a glucagon-stimulated C-peptide of approximately 0.8 nmol/L in a young adult with negative autoantibodies, and how does this inform treatment choices?

A stimulated C‑peptide of ~0.8 nmol/L in this context should be read as evidence of substantial residual endogenous insulin secretion — enough to argue strongly against classic, insulin‑dependent type 1 diabetes and to support a non‑autoimmune β‑cell disorder such as MODY. Clinically useful interpretation rests on three linked points.

First, the number. Most clinical series use a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide cutoff of roughly 0.6 nmol/L (or about 0.2–0.3 ng/mL depending on units) to indicate preserved β‑cell function. A peak of 0.83 nmol/L therefore indicates preserved but inadequate insulin secretion: the pancreas is still making insulin, but not enough to maintain euglycaemia.

Second, the antibodies and phenotype. Negative GAD/IA‑2/ZnT8 plus a strong family history and an early age at onset make autoimmune T1D unlikely and raise pretest probability for monogenic diabetes. The preserved stimulated C‑peptide thus becomes a supportive biomarker for MODY rather than an argument for withholding genetic testing.

Third, how this guides therapy. For HNF1A‑MODY the preserved β‑cell reserve predicts a good response to insulin secretagogues. Low‑dose sulfonylureas have classically been first‑line because they directly augment the defective insulin secretion and often allow withdrawal of insulin. In modern practice, GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a reasonable and sometimes preferable alternative: they improve glycaemia with less hypoglycaemia and can preserve β‑cell function and promote weight loss when appropriate. Metformin is reasonable when there are features of insulin resistance or as adjunctive therapy. Insulin can be used short term for severe hyperglycaemia but can often be discontinued once genetic diagnosis is made and non‑insulin agents titrated. SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution in HNF1A‑MODY because of pronounced glycosuria and theoretical dehydration/DKA risk in this group.

Finally, remember the natural history: HNF1A mutations cause progressive β‑cell decline, so serial C‑peptide and glycaemic monitoring are necessary and treatment may need escalation over time. Genetic confirmation not only clarifies current management but directs screening and family counselling. In this case, the stimulated C‑peptide of ~0.8 nmol/L appropriately supported transition off insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (and later oral agents), consistent with the successful outcome described.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which features in this case could have led to misclassification as type 2 diabetes at presentation, and what key steps help avoid that error?

This patient had several features that make HNF1A-MODY easy to mistake for type 2 diabetes at first glance. He was a young adult with markedly elevated HbA1c and fasting glucose but without ketoacidosis, had a body mass index in the overweight range and dyslipidaemia (high triglycerides, low HDL) suggestive of metabolic syndrome, and he had preserved (albeit insufficient) stimulated C‑peptide and negative islet autoantibodies — all findings that are compatible with early type 2 diabetes. A family history of “early‑onset diabetes” can also be misinterpreted as common familial type 2 disease rather than the autosomal dominant pattern of MODY, especially as obesity and T2D prevalence rise and blur traditional distinctions.

To avoid that misclassification you need to combine careful clinical pattern‑recognition with targeted testing. First, seek a three‑generation pedigree and ask about age at diagnosis and treatment response in affected relatives — a clear autosomal dominant, early‑onset pattern raises pretest probability for MODY. Second, use antibody testing and measurement of endogenous insulin secretion (fasting and/or stimulated C‑peptide) to exclude classic type 1 disease; preserved C‑peptide with negative autoantibodies supports non‑autoimmune diabetes. Third, pay attention to discordant features for T2D (absence of acanthosis, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria out of proportion to plasma glucose suggesting a low renal glucose threshold) and to an unexpectedly strong response to low‑dose insulin secretagogues. Fourth, apply diagnostic aids (eg, the MODY probability calculator) but interpret them in the clinical and ethnic context. Finally, confirm the diagnosis with molecular testing (targeted NGS MODY panel) when the phenotype suggests monogenic diabetes, because the result changes therapy, prognosis and family counselling.

In short: don’t let BMI, dyslipidaemia or absence of DKA alone anchor you to a T2D diagnosis in a young patient. A focused family history, autoantibody/C‑peptide testing and early genetic screening when clinical suspicion is high will prevent misclassification and enable precision therapy.

### [Pathophysiology] Why might some HNF1A variants not be sufficient to cause MODY yet still increase the risk of type 2 diabetes at a population level?

Think of HNF1A variants as lying on a spectrum of functional effect rather than a binary “pathogenic/not‑pathogenic” switch. Classic HNF1A‑MODY arises from heterozygous variants that substantially impair protein function (loss‑of‑function or dominant‑negative) and produce a highly penetrant, autosomal‑dominant β‑cell secretory defect. By contrast, many other HNF1A alleles yield only partial reduction in activity. Those partially functional alleles do not, by themselves, produce the early, progressive β‑cell failure characteristic of MODY, but they lower β‑cell reserve and shift the individual’s glucose homeostasis closer to the threshold for diabetes.

Two linked principles explain how a “mild” HNF1A variant can increase population risk for type 2 diabetes without causing MODY in a single carrier. First, effect size and penetrance matter: a variant that reduces HNF1A transcriptional activity modestly produces variable, age‑ and environment‑dependent phenotypes. In a lean adolescent with no metabolic stress such a variant may never cause overt diabetes; in an adult exposed to obesity, insulin resistance, aging, or other genetic risk alleles the same modest deficit in insulin secretory capacity can tip the balance toward T2D. Second, diabetes at the population level is polygenic and multifactorial: common or low‑penetrance HNF1A variants increase baseline risk across many carriers (raising population attributable risk), even though most carriers will not show Mendelian inheritance or an early severe phenotype.

Mechanistically this reflects where and how the variant acts: changes in the DNA‑binding domain or transactivation domain that only partially impair target gene expression (insulin, GLUT2, SGLT2, pyruvate kinase) will reduce stimulated insulin output but leave enough function to avoid classic MODY. Modifier genes, epigenetic differences, and environmental stressors (weight gain, toxins, inflammation) interact with that reduced reserve to determine clinical outcome. From a clinical perspective this explains why genetic databases contain many HNF1A variants of uncertain or intermediate effect, why functional assays and segregation data are often required to classify pathogenicity, and why identification of a mild HNF1A variant should prompt assessment of family history, longitudinal β‑cell function, and counseling about modifiable risks rather than assuming inevitable MODY.

In short: some HNF1A variants are hypomorphic — they lower β‑cell functional reserve without producing the dominant, early‑onset phenotype alone. Those hypomorphic alleles increase population risk for T2D through gene–environment and polygenic interactions, even though they lack the high penetrance required to cause MODY in a single carrier.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How does the risk and mechanism of ketoacidosis at presentation differ between HNF1A-MODY and autoimmune type 1 diabetes, and how do SGLT2 inhibitors modify that risk in HNF1A-MODY?

Type 1 diabetes and HNF1A‑MODY present very differently with respect to ketoacidosis because the underlying pathophysiology of insulin deficiency is different.

In autoimmune type 1 diabetes the beta‑cell mass is acutely and usually almost completely destroyed by autoimmunity. At presentation patients commonly have an absolute insulin deficiency, an increased counter‑regulatory hormone state (glucagon, catecholamines), unopposed lipolysis and rapid hepatic ketogenesis. That combination produces marked hyperglycaemia, osmotic diuresis, profound volume depletion and a high risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) at or soon after presentation.

By contrast, HNF1A‑MODY is a monogenic, non‑autoimmune, progressive beta‑cell secretory defect. Most patients retain substantial endogenous insulin secretion for a long time, so frank DKA at presentation is uncommon. Hyperglycaemia and glycosuria are prominent (HNF1A directly regulates renal SGLT2 expression, lowering the renal glucose threshold) but residual insulin usually suppresses excessive lipolysis and ketogenesis, so patients more typically present with hyperglycaemia, weight loss and polyuria rather than DKA.

SGLT2 inhibitors change that risk profile in HNF1A‑MODY. Mechanistically, SGLT2 inhibition increases urinary glucose loss and lowers plasma glucose, which often leads clinicians or patients to reduce insulin doses or endogenous insulin secretion to match lower glucose levels. The drug class also raises glucagon and increases hepatic ketogenesis, and the accompanying osmotic diuresis can provoke volume depletion. The net effect—reduced insulin action (absolute or relative), increased counter‑regulatory drive and dehydration—favours ketone production and can precipitate euglycaemic DKA. In HNF1A‑MODY this risk may be amplified because these patients already have baseline glycosuria from reduced SGLT2 expression and often limited beta‑cell reserve; further pharmacologic inhibition of renal glucose reabsorption increases osmotic losses and can tip marginal insulin secretion into insufficiency for suppressing ketogenesis.

Clinical implications: DKA is much more likely in type 1 at baseline; in HNF1A‑MODY it is uncommon unless additional stresses or therapies (notably SGLT2 inhibitors) reduce effective insulin action or cause dehydration. Therefore avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with HNF1A‑MODY unless you have a compelling indication and a plan to mitigate risk (close ketone monitoring, patient education about stopping during illness or reduced intake, avoid aggressive insulin dose reduction, ensure euvolemia). For HNF1A‑MODY, first‑line targeted options remain low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists and metformin as illustrated in this case, which achieve glycaemic control without the increased DKA risk seen with SGLT2 inhibitors.

### [Morning Report] What bedside or clinic test can assess small-fiber autonomic function in diabetes, and what does severely reduced sudomotor conductance signify in a newly diagnosed patient?

The simplest clinic bedside test for small‑fiber autonomic function used in many diabetes clinics is sudomotor assessment by electrochemical skin conductance (eg, Sudoscan). This noninvasive device measures sweat gland (sudomotor) activity by recording electrochemical skin conductance in the palms and soles and provides an index of small unmyelinated C‑fiber sympathetic function. Other ways to test small‑fiber/ autonomic function include the quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART), thermoregulatory sweat testing, skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve‑fiber density, and components of a formal autonomic reflex screen (heart‑rate variability, Valsalva, tilt-table), but Sudoscan is the rapid, clinic‑friendly option.

Severely reduced sudomotor conductance in a newly diagnosed diabetic indicates clinically significant small‑fiber autonomic neuropathy involving sympathetic cholinergic fibers. Clinically, this means the patient has early autonomic involvement and is at higher risk for orthostatic intolerance, gastroparesis, bladder dysfunction, impaired thermoregulation, silent myocardial ischemia and impaired wound healing in the feet. In a young patient with apparently recent diagnosis (as in this case of HNF1A‑MODY), severe reduction most likely reflects prolonged or marked hyperglycaemia prior to diagnosis (or an unusually aggressive neuropathic process) rather than measurement artifact; it should prompt a systematic search for other autonomic and small‑fiber complications, optimization of glycemic control, education about foot care and hydration/heat intolerance, and consideration of referral for formal autonomic testing and neurology/endocrine follow‑up.

### [Management] Once genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY is obtained and glycaemic control improves, what medication adjustments are reasonable, and what principles guide de-intensification?

Once HNF1A‑MODY is confirmed and glycaemia has improved, the goal is to simplify therapy to the least intensive regimen that safely maintains target glucose while preserving remaining β‑cell function and minimising hypoglycaemia. The choices and timing of de‑intensification are determined by three clinical measurements—current HbA1c/glucose trends, evidence of endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C‑peptide), and the patient’s hypoglycaemia risk and preferences.

If stimulated C‑peptide is preserved (many centres use the glucagon‑stimulated threshold >0.6 nmol/L as evidence of meaningful β‑cell reserve) and the patient is not insulin‑dependent, it is reasonable to discontinue exogenous insulin and move to oral/GLP‑1–based therapy with close follow‑up. Historically low‑dose sulfonylureas are the canonical first‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY because they directly amplify the defective insulin secretory response; start low, titrate to effect, and counsel about hypoglycaemia. GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a modern, attractive alternative when weight, cardiovascular risk or hypoglycaemia concerns favour them — they improve glycaemia with less hypoglycaemia and may preserve β‑cell function. Metformin can be continued when there is clinical evidence of insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome; it is not disease‑specific but is often helpful adjunctive therapy. DPP‑4 inhibitors have less evidence but may be used if GLP‑1RA are contraindicated or not tolerated. Avoid routine use of SGLT‑2 inhibitors as first‑line in HNF1A‑MODY because HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression and these agents can increase glycosuria and, rarely, precipitate dehydration or euglycaemic ketoacidosis in patients with limited insulin reserve.

Practical de‑intensification approach: confirm genetic result and reassess C‑peptide; if reserve is adequate and glucose improving, gradually taper insulin rather than abrupt cessation in patients on higher doses—stop basal first if fasting glucose is near target, monitor capillary glucoses closely (daily), and plan review within 1–2 weeks. Start or switch to low‑dose sulfonylurea (or GLP‑1RA) when insulin stopped; continue metformin if indicated. Reassess HbA1c at 3 months and C‑peptide periodically if clinical status changes. Educate the patient about hypoglycaemia recognition, sick‑day rules, and symptoms of ketosis; provide written plan for when to restart insulin if control deteriorates.

Finally, individualise: older patients, pregnancy plans, very low C‑peptide, or histories of severe hyperglycaemia may require continued insulin. Genetic diagnosis allows precision tailoring, but safe de‑intensification hinges on objective β‑cell reserve, careful glucose monitoring, and early follow‑up.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How should the occasional presence of islet autoantibodies in patients with MODY be interpreted, and which clinical features retain primacy in distinguishing MODY from type 1 diabetes?

Short answer: an occasional positive islet autoantibody does not reliably exclude MODY. The significance of any antibody result must be judged by its number, titre and persistence, and — most importantly — by the clinical phenotype. High‑titre or multiple positive autoantibodies that appear early and are followed by rapid loss of C‑peptide point to autoimmune (type 1) diabetes; an isolated, low‑titer antibody (especially a single positive GAD) can be incidental/false positive and should not by itself preclude genetic testing for MODY when the clinical picture fits.

Clinical reasoning and practical approach: autoantibodies are biomarkers of autoimmune β‑cell destruction, not direct proof of monogenic disease. In population studies a small fraction of patients with genetically proven MODY have detectable islet antibodies — usually single, low‑titer, non‑persistent results. Assay variability and low positive predictive value for single antibodies mean that isolated positivity can mislead. By contrast, type 1 diabetes typically shows two or more autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8, IAA), often at higher titres, and a predictable clinical course of progressive insulin deficiency with early requirement for insulin and risk of DKA.

Which clinical features retain primacy when distinguishing MODY from type 1:
- Family history of early‑onset, autosomal‑dominant diabetes across successive generations (strong clue to MODY).
- Pattern and tempo of β‑cell failure: preserved or only slowly declining C‑peptide (stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L beyond the honeymoon period) argues for MODY; brisk loss of C‑peptide supports type 1.
- Age at onset and presentation: classic MODY often presents in adolescence/young adulthood without DKA; type 1 presents at any age but with acute symptomatic hyperglycaemia and frequent DKA.
- Insulin requirement: low long‑term insulin needs or stable control on oral agents favors MODY; early, sustained insulin dependence favors type 1.
- Associated features: glycosuria at modest glucose levels (reduced renal threshold) and prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia are typical of HNF1A‑MODY; signs of insulin resistance/obesity point away from classical MODY but do not exclude it in today’s population.
- Autoantibody profile: multiple, high‑titer antibodies carry primacy for type 1; a single, low‑titer or transient antibody should be interpreted cautiously.

Clinical application: do not exclude MODY solely because of a single positive antibody. If the phenotype (strong autosomal family history, preserved C‑peptide, absence of DKA, glycosuria, typical treatment response) suggests MODY, repeat antibody testing, document C‑peptide, and proceed to genetic testing when warranted. Conversely, multiple high‑titer autoantibodies with rapid beta‑cell loss should steer management and counseling toward type 1 diabetes.
