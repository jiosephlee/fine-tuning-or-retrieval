Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

### [Morning Report] Early in HNF1A-MODY, postprandial hyperglycaemia may predominate. How does a 2-hour OGTT value ≥11.1 mmol/L with lesser fasting elevation inform the diagnosis and raise suspicion for HNF1A-MODY despite absent ketosis and negative autoantibodies?

Bottom line: A marked 2‑hour OGTT glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L with only modest fasting elevation in a young adult—especially with a strong family history, negative islet autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide—should increase suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY because it reflects an early, predominantly postprandial β‑cell secretory defect rather than autoimmune insulin deficiency.

Why this pattern points to HNF1A‑MODY (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- HNF1A mutations preferentially impair glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion (loss of first‑phase and meal‑stimulated release) and reduce expression of glucose‑sensing genes (eg, GLUT2, insulin), so early disease produces striking postprandial excursions while fasting glucose may be only mildly or intermittently elevated.  
- With time β‑cell failure progresses and fasting hyperglycaemia develops; but in the early stage an isolated or disproportionately high 2‑hour OGTT result is a characteristic signal of a primary β‑cell defect rather than insulin resistance or autoimmune destruction.
- HNF1A also lowers renal SGLT2 expression → glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels, a supportive clinical clue that can accompany postprandial glycaemia.

How the case illustrates this
- The patient’s 2‑hour OGTT was 19.8 mmol/L (well above the diabetes threshold of ≥11.1 mmol/L) while fasting glucose was less markedly elevated (12.4 mmol/L) — a disproportionate postprandial rise consistent with impaired stimulated insulin secretion.  
- Autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2) were negative and stimulated C‑peptide was moderately preserved (0.83 nmol/L), arguing against classic type 1 autoimmune diabetes and supporting a non‑autoimmune β‑cell defect. The strong family history of early‑onset diabetes further supports a monogenic MODY picture; genetic testing confirmed an HNF1A pathogenic variant.

Diagnostic implication and action
- A 2‑hour OGTT ≥11.1 mmol/L in a young patient with the above features meets the biochemical criterion for diabetes but, when disproportionate to fasting glucose and accompanied by negative autoantibodies/preserved C‑peptide and autosomal dominant family history, should prompt consideration of MODY and referral for genetic testing (NGS panel or targeted HNF1A sequencing).  
- Recognising this pattern matters therapeutically: HNF1A‑MODY patients frequently respond to oral secretagogues (low‑dose sulfonylureas) or GLP‑1 receptor agonists and can often avoid long‑term insulin if correctly diagnosed.

Practical teaching point
- Don’t rely solely on fasting glucose or assume young-onset diabetes = type 1. In young patients with a high 2‑hour OGTT relative to fasting glucose, negative autoantibodies and familial diabetes, think HNF1A‑MODY and pursue genetic confirmation—this changes prognosis, family counselling and treatment.
