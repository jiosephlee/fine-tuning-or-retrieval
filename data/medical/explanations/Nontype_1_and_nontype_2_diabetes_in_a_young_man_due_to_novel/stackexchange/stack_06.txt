### [Clinical Pearls] When should genetic testing for MODY be pursued in young-onset diabetes, and what pretest clinical criteria increase the likelihood of a positive result?

Think of MODY when the diabetes story doesn’t fit type 1 or type 2 — and then test, because confirmation changes therapy, prognosis and family counselling.

Pursue genetic testing when diabetes begins young and the phenotype is atypical for type 1 or type 2. The highest yield is in patients with onset in adolescence or early adulthood (classically <25 years, though some use <35), a strong multigenerational pattern of diabetes, and clinical features that argue against autoimmune type 1 or classic insulin‑resistant type 2 disease. In practice you should move to molecular testing when negative islet autoantibodies and preserved β‑cell function make type 1 unlikely and there is suspicion for a monogenic cause—especially if identifying a mutation would change therapy (for example, the likelihood that sulfonylureas or a GLP‑1 receptor agonist will suffice and insulin can be stopped).

Clinical features that meaningfully increase the pretest probability and therefore support testing are a family history of diabetes in an autosomal dominant pattern (diabetes in ≥2 successive generations, early onset in relatives), diabetes diagnosed at a young age (adolescence to early adulthood), absent or low‑titer islet autoantibodies, and evidence of endogenous insulin secretion out of proportion to type 1 disease (stimulated C‑peptide usually preserved — often quoted thresholds >0.2–0.6 nmol/L depending on the assay and clinical context). Additional supportive clues are marked postprandial hyperglycaemia with glycosuria (suggestive of HNF1A), low insulin requirements long after diagnosis (not requiring insulin or requiring low doses), lack of obesity or other features of insulin resistance (though rising obesity blunts this), absence of ketoacidosis at presentation, and extrapancreatic findings when present (for example renal glycosuria, developmental renal cysts or specific renal/liver features for some genes).

Use clinical tools (for example the Exeter MODY probability calculator) as an adjunct but not a substitute for judgement; these tools were derived in predominantly European populations and can under‑ or over‑estimate risk in other ethnic groups. If the pretest probability is moderate to high (many groups use thresholds such as >10–25% or a calculator estimate above the locally validated cut‑off), proceed to targeted NGS panels for common MODY genes rather than single‑gene testing. Finally, remember the practical principle illustrated by this case: targeted genetic diagnosis allows precision therapy (often discontinuing insulin in HNF1A‑MODY), directs surveillance and enables cascade testing of relatives.
