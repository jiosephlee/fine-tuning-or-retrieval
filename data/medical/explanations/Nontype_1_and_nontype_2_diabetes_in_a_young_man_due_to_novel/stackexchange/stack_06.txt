Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

### [Differential Diagnosis] At the bedside, how would you differentiate among common MODY subtypes—HNF1A-, HNF4A-, HNF1B-, and GCK-MODY—based on characteristic clinical clues (for example, neonatal history, renal/structural abnormalities, severity and pattern of hyperglycaemia), to prioritize gene testing?

Direct answer
At the bedside you can often prioritize which MODY gene to test by looking for a small set of clinical “red flags”: the pattern and severity of hyperglycaemia (stable mild vs progressive marked), presence of neonatal problems (macrosomia or hypoglycaemia), renal/urogenital or pancreatic structural disease, glycosuria, and the family history. Use these features to choose a single-gene test when the phenotype is classic, or a multi‑gene NGS panel when features overlap.

How the four common MODY subtypes differ (practical bedside clues)

- GCK‑MODY (MODY2)
  - Typical bedside picture: lifelong, mild fasting hyperglycaemia that is stable over years (fasting glucose mildly ↑; HbA1c usually only mildly raised, e.g. ~5.5–7.5%).
  - OGTT: only a small increment above baseline; patients often asymptomatic.
  - Neonatal history: no severe neonatal hypoglycaemia; glucose set-point is higher from birth.
  - Complications/treatment: minimal microvascular risk; usually no pharmacologic treatment outside pregnancy.
  - When to prioritise testing: incidentally discovered, mild, non‑progressive hyperglycaemia with family history of similar mild elevations.

- HNF1A‑MODY
  - Typical bedside picture: adolescent/young‑adult onset with progressively worsening β‑cell dysfunction; prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia and often marked rises on OGTT.
  - Distinctive clues: glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose because of lowered renal glucose reabsorption (reduced SGLT2 expression); strong autosomal‑dominant family history of early diabetes; good initial response to low‑dose sulfonylureas (and GLP‑1 agents are effective).
  - Autoantibodies: usually negative; C‑peptide often preserved early.
  - When to prioritise testing: symptomatic young adult with marked postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, negative autoantibodies and dominant family history — exactly the phenotype in this case. The team’s clinical suspicion of HNF1A was appropriate and was confirmed by NGS (novel HNF1A p.N270S).

- HNF4A‑MODY
  - Typical bedside picture: resembles HNF1A in adolescence/young adulthood (progressive insulin secretory defect).
  - Distinctive clues: a history of neonatal macrosomia and transient neonatal hypoglycaemia (hyperinsulinaemia) is characteristic and should prompt HNF4A testing.
  - When to prioritise testing: presence of neonatal hypoglycaemia or large birthweight in the proband or parent plus later-onset familial diabetes.

- HNF1B‑MODY (also called renal cysts and diabetes syndrome)
  - Typical bedside picture: diabetes can be variable; often accompanied by renal structural disease (cysts, hypoplasia), early CKD, abnormal urinalysis, electrolyte abnormalities, genital tract malformations, and sometimes pancreatic hypoplasia.
  - Distinctive clues: renal/urogenital anomalies on imaging, unexplained early kidney disease, or pancreatic exocrine insufficiency – these point to HNF1B rather than an HNF1A/HNF4A phenotype. Many HNF1B cases are de novo, so a negative family history does not exclude it.
  - When to prioritise testing: diabetes plus any renal structural abnormality or early CKD.

Putting it into practice (a simple bedside algorithm)
1. Mild, lifelong, stable fasting hyperglycaemia → test GCK first.
2. Neonatal macrosomia + transient neonatal hypoglycaemia → test HNF4A.
3. Progressive postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, strong autosomal‑dominant family history, negative autoantibodies → test HNF1A first (as in this case).
4. Diabetes with renal/urogenital malformations, early CKD, electrolyte abnormalities or pancreatic hypoplasia → test HNF1B.
5. If features overlap or are not classic, perform a multi‑gene NGS MODY panel.

Clinical relevance illustrated by the case
This patient had marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (OGTT 2‑hr 19.8 mmol/L), glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but insufficient stimulated C‑peptide, and a clear dominant family history — the classic bedside pattern that prioritizes HNF1A testing. Genetic confirmation changed management (discontinuation of insulin, effective use of GLP‑1 receptor agonist and oral agents, and counseling about sulfonylurea sensitivity and SGLT2 inhibitor cautions).

Bottom line
Use the pattern of hyperglycaemia (stable mild vs progressive severe), neonatal history, and presence of renal/structural anomalies to guide targeted testing: GCK for mild stable fasting hyperglycaemia, HNF4A when neonatal macrosomia/hypoglycaemia is present, HNF1A for progressive postprandial disease with glycosuria and strong family history, and HNF1B when renal/urogenital abnormalities accompany diabetes. If uncertainty exists, order a MODY gene panel.
