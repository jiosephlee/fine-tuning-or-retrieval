### [Clinical Pearls] What fasting and stimulated C-peptide thresholds support preserved endogenous insulin secretion and argue against absolute insulin deficiency (e.g., after a glucagon stimulation test)?

Clinical pearl: preserved endogenous insulin secretion is best demonstrated by C‑peptide, and thresholds differ for fasting versus stimulated testing because stimulation unmasks reserve that fasting levels can miss. A fasting (or random, with concurrent glucose) C‑peptide above ~0.2 nmol/L (≈0.6 ng/mL) generally indicates endogenous insulin production and makes absolute insulin deficiency unlikely. After a physiological or pharmacologic stimulus (mixed‑meal tolerance test or glucagon stimulation), a peak/stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (≈1.8 ng/mL) is widely used to define clinically meaningful residual β‑cell function and argues strongly against complete insulin deficiency (classic type 1 presentation).

The clinical reasoning is that modest fasting C‑peptide can be suppressed by hyperglycaemia, recent hypoglycaemia, or insulin therapy, so a stimulated test (glucagon at 6 minutes or an MMTT at 90–120 minutes) better reveals β‑cell reserve. Interpret values in the context of the glucose at the time of sampling, assay units (1 ng/mL ≈ 0.331 nmol/L), timing of the stimulation, and clinical context (autoantibodies, duration of disease). Values below these cutoffs are supportive—but not absolute proof—of severe β‑cell failure; borderline results warrant repeat or alternative stimulated testing and integration with autoantibody testing and clinical picture (age, family history) before labeling someone insulin‑deficient.
