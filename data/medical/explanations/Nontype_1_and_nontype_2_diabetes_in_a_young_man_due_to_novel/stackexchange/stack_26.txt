### [Differential Diagnosis] Which features in this case could have led to misclassification as type 2 diabetes at presentation, and what key steps help avoid that error?

This patient had several features that make HNF1A-MODY easy to mistake for type 2 diabetes at first glance. He was a young adult with markedly elevated HbA1c and fasting glucose but without ketoacidosis, had a body mass index in the overweight range and dyslipidaemia (high triglycerides, low HDL) suggestive of metabolic syndrome, and he had preserved (albeit insufficient) stimulated C‑peptide and negative islet autoantibodies — all findings that are compatible with early type 2 diabetes. A family history of “early‑onset diabetes” can also be misinterpreted as common familial type 2 disease rather than the autosomal dominant pattern of MODY, especially as obesity and T2D prevalence rise and blur traditional distinctions.

To avoid that misclassification you need to combine careful clinical pattern‑recognition with targeted testing. First, seek a three‑generation pedigree and ask about age at diagnosis and treatment response in affected relatives — a clear autosomal dominant, early‑onset pattern raises pretest probability for MODY. Second, use antibody testing and measurement of endogenous insulin secretion (fasting and/or stimulated C‑peptide) to exclude classic type 1 disease; preserved C‑peptide with negative autoantibodies supports non‑autoimmune diabetes. Third, pay attention to discordant features for T2D (absence of acanthosis, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria out of proportion to plasma glucose suggesting a low renal glucose threshold) and to an unexpectedly strong response to low‑dose insulin secretagogues. Fourth, apply diagnostic aids (eg, the MODY probability calculator) but interpret them in the clinical and ethnic context. Finally, confirm the diagnosis with molecular testing (targeted NGS MODY panel) when the phenotype suggests monogenic diabetes, because the result changes therapy, prognosis and family counselling.

In short: don’t let BMI, dyslipidaemia or absence of DKA alone anchor you to a T2D diagnosis in a young patient. A focused family history, autoantibody/C‑peptide testing and early genetic screening when clinical suspicion is high will prevent misclassification and enable precision therapy.
