### [Clinical Pearls] What is the Exeter MODY probability calculator, which clinical inputs drive its estimate, and what are its limitations in non-White populations?

The Exeter MODY probability calculator is a clinical prediction tool developed by the Exeter (UK) group to estimate the probability that a patient with young‑onset diabetes has monogenic MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes. It is intended as a screening aid to prioritize who should be offered genetic testing, not as a diagnostic test.

The calculator combines routinely available clinical data into a logistic model: the patient’s age at diagnosis of diabetes, body mass index (BMI), current or recent HbA1c, whether the patient required insulin soon after diagnosis (insulin within 6 months), parental history of diabetes (affected parent), and islet autoantibody status (GAD antibody positive or negative). Those inputs are weighted because they reflect the underlying pathophysiology of common diabetes types: earlier age at onset, absence of insulin requirement at presentation and negative autoantibodies increase the likelihood of MODY; lower BMI and strong parental history also favor monogenic disease.

Clinical reasoning for each input: age at diagnosis captures the typical young onset of MODY; BMI and HbA1c help distinguish insulin‑resistant T2D from primary β‑cell failure; early and persistent insulin requirement and positive GAD point toward autoimmune type 1 disease; and a parent with diabetes raises the prior probability because most MODY subtypes are autosomal dominant.

Limitations in non‑White populations
The model was derived and validated mainly in White European cohorts, so its calibration and the relative weights of predictors may not generalize. In many non‑White populations the prevalence, age‑structure, BMI distribution and family patterns of type 1 and type 2 diabetes differ, and rates of obesity and early‑onset T2D are often higher—factors that can make the calculator overestimate the probability of MODY (or alter sensitivity and specificity). Autoantibody testing performance, background prevalence of parental diabetes, and the spectrum/frequency of monogenic variants also differ across ethnic groups. Practically, that means a high Exeter score in a non‑White patient should prompt consideration of genetic testing but not be taken as definitive; conversely a low score should not exclude testing when clinical suspicion is strong (clear autosomal dominant family history, non‑obese young patient with glycosuria or preserved C‑peptide). In short, use the calculator as a guide, interpret it in the ethnic and clinical context of the patient, and have a low threshold for genetic testing when precision diagnosis would change management.
