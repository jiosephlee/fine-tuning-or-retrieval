### [Management] Why should SGLT2 inhibitors be used with caution or avoided in HNF1A-MODY, and what specific adverse risks are heightened in this genotype?

The key point is that HNF1A mutations reduce expression of the renal SGLT2 transporter, so patients with HNF1A‑MODY already have a lower renal glucose‑reabsorption threshold and baseline glycosuria. Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor therefore has both reduced incremental glycaemic benefit and a higher risk of adverse effects from excessive renal glucose loss and volume depletion.

Mechanism and clinical consequences: HNF1A is a transcription factor that drives SGLT2 expression in proximal tubules. Haploinsufficiency → chronically lower SGLT2 levels → more glycosuria at a given plasma glucose. Pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition on top of that can produce marked osmotic diuresis and electrolyte-free water loss. Clinically this predisposes to severe dehydration, orthostatic hypotension and acute kidney injury, and case reports (and the case series literature) describe frank intravascular volume collapse in HNF1A‑MODY patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors. Because many patients with HNF1A‑MODY have relatively limited β‑cell reserve, the combination of reduced insulin availability and increased glucagon/ketone production seen with SGLT2 inhibitors increases the risk of (often euglycaemic) diabetic ketoacidosis. Genital mycotic infections are also more likely because glycosuria is amplified.

What to do in practice: therefore avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors in genetically confirmed HNF1A‑MODY; if considered in exceptional circumstances, use extreme caution—assess baseline volume status and renal function, counsel and monitor for dehydration and genital infections, instruct on frequent ketone monitoring and stop the drug rapidly when ill or reduced intake. Prefer therapies with proven efficacy and safety in HNF1A‑MODY (low‑dose sulfonylureas early, GLP‑1 receptor agonists, metformin as indicated) guided by the genetic diagnosis.
