Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

### [Management] In which clinical scenarios might a GLP-1 receptor agonist be preferred over sulfonylureas for HNF1A-MODY, and what are the advantages and trade-offs?

Sulfonylureas have been the classic “precision” therapy for HNF1A‑MODY because the mutant β cell retains exaggerated sensitivity to secretagogues; a low dose often produces large drops in glucose. But in everyday practice there are several clinical situations in which a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (GLP‑1 RA) is the preferred alternative. Use that choice when the patient has one or more of the following: a high baseline HbA1c requiring robust and durable lowering without provoking hypoglycaemia; a history or high risk of hypoglycaemia (older age, erratic meals, prior severe lows); clinically important overweight/obesity or features of metabolic syndrome where weight loss would help; established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or high ASCVD risk for which certain GLP‑1 RAs have outcome data; intolerance or contraindication to sulfonylureas; or when the patient prefers to avoid the weight gain and hypoglycaemia risk of a sulfonylurea. The case you presented fits several of these reasons — marked hyperglycaemia with metabolic abnormalities and concern about preserving β‑cell function — and the patient responded well to dulaglutide.

The advantages of a GLP‑1 RA in HNF1A‑MODY are lower hypoglycaemia risk compared with sulfonylureas, consistent weight loss (or at least avoidance of weight gain), potential cardiovascular benefit with specific agents, and an incretin‑mediated insulinotropic effect that can be effective even as β‑cell function declines. Small crossover trials and case series in HNF1A‑MODY show similar glycaemic efficacy to sulfonylureas with less hypoglycaemia; clinically, GLP‑1 RAs also allow simplification away from insulin in some patients.

The trade‑offs are important and must be discussed. GLP‑1 RAs are more costly and most are injectable, which affects adherence and access. They commonly cause gastrointestinal side effects and are contraindicated or not recommended in pregnancy and in patients with certain personal or family histories (eg, medullary thyroid cancer or MEN2). For patients with very preserved β‑cell function who respond dramatically to low‑dose sulfonylurea, sulfonylureas remain an effective, inexpensive option; sulfonylureas can produce a more rapid initial glucose lowering. Long‑term comparative data in MODY are limited, so individual response should guide therapy. Finally, monitor C‑peptide and glycaemic response after a therapeutic switch — if incretin therapy fails to maintain control, be prepared to add or change agents (including low‑dose sulfonylurea or insulin) while continuing genetic‑informed follow‑up.

In short, reserve GLP‑1 RAs for HNF1A‑MODY patients in whom hypoglycaemia avoidance, weight/Cardio‑metabolic benefit, intolerance to sulfonylureas, or declining β‑cell reserve make sulfonylurea therapy unattractive; weigh those clinical benefits against cost, route of administration, GI adverse effects and pregnancy considerations, and individualize therapy with close monitoring.
