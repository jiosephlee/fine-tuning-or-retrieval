### [Morning Report] How do you interpret a glucagon-stimulated C-peptide of approximately 0.8 nmol/L in a young adult with negative autoantibodies, and how does this inform treatment choices?

A stimulated C‑peptide of ~0.8 nmol/L in this context should be read as evidence of substantial residual endogenous insulin secretion — enough to argue strongly against classic, insulin‑dependent type 1 diabetes and to support a non‑autoimmune β‑cell disorder such as MODY. Clinically useful interpretation rests on three linked points.

First, the number. Most clinical series use a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide cutoff of roughly 0.6 nmol/L (or about 0.2–0.3 ng/mL depending on units) to indicate preserved β‑cell function. A peak of 0.83 nmol/L therefore indicates preserved but inadequate insulin secretion: the pancreas is still making insulin, but not enough to maintain euglycaemia.

Second, the antibodies and phenotype. Negative GAD/IA‑2/ZnT8 plus a strong family history and an early age at onset make autoimmune T1D unlikely and raise pretest probability for monogenic diabetes. The preserved stimulated C‑peptide thus becomes a supportive biomarker for MODY rather than an argument for withholding genetic testing.

Third, how this guides therapy. For HNF1A‑MODY the preserved β‑cell reserve predicts a good response to insulin secretagogues. Low‑dose sulfonylureas have classically been first‑line because they directly augment the defective insulin secretion and often allow withdrawal of insulin. In modern practice, GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a reasonable and sometimes preferable alternative: they improve glycaemia with less hypoglycaemia and can preserve β‑cell function and promote weight loss when appropriate. Metformin is reasonable when there are features of insulin resistance or as adjunctive therapy. Insulin can be used short term for severe hyperglycaemia but can often be discontinued once genetic diagnosis is made and non‑insulin agents titrated. SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution in HNF1A‑MODY because of pronounced glycosuria and theoretical dehydration/DKA risk in this group.

Finally, remember the natural history: HNF1A mutations cause progressive β‑cell decline, so serial C‑peptide and glycaemic monitoring are necessary and treatment may need escalation over time. Genetic confirmation not only clarifies current management but directs screening and family counselling. In this case, the stimulated C‑peptide of ~0.8 nmol/L appropriately supported transition off insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (and later oral agents), consistent with the successful outcome described.
