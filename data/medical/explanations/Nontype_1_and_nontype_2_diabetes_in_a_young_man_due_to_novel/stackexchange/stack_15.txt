### [Differential Diagnosis] List distinguishing clinical features between HNF1A-MODY and HNF1B-MODY that can guide targeted genetic testing.

HNF1A‑MODY and HNF1B‑MODY can both present as young‑onset, non‑autoimmune diabetes, but they differ clinically in ways that should direct targeted genetic testing.

Think first about extrapancreatic findings. HNF1B (the “renal cysts and diabetes” or RCAD syndrome) commonly produces conspicuous renal and genitourinary abnormalities: structural kidney disease on ultrasound (cysts, small or hyperechogenic kidneys), early chronic kidney disease, electrolyte derangements—most classically renal magnesium wasting with hypomagnesemia—and sometimes hyperuricaemia/gout. Genital tract malformations (eg, bicornuate uterus, epididymal or seminal tract anomalies) and pancreatic hypoplasia are frequent. These non‑glycaemic features are the cardinal clue pointing to HNF1B testing.

By contrast, HNF1A‑MODY is primarily a beta‑cell disorder with few consistent structural extra‑organ abnormalities. Patients usually have a clear multigenerational autosomal dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes, progressive insulin secretory failure with preserved C‑peptide for years, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia, and marked glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose because HNF1A regulates SGLT2. Glycosuria and sensitivity to low‑dose sulfonylureas (or excellent response to GLP‑1 agonists/sulfonylureas allowing withdrawal of insulin) favour HNF1A. Typical metabolic syndrome features are usually absent or less prominent than in T2D.

Other practical discriminators: HNF1B mutations are often sporadic (a high rate of de novo mutations), so lack of multigenerational diabetes does not exclude HNF1B. HNF1B patients more frequently have reduced renal function or early CKD and abnormal urinalysis independent of glycaemia; HNF1A patients typically have normal kidneys aside from glycosuria. Autoantibodies are negative in both, so negative GADA/IA‑2 does not distinguish them. Imaging (renal ultrasound, pelvic imaging) and simple labs (serum magnesium, uric acid, creatinine) are therefore high‑yield when deciding to sequence HNF1B. Conversely, a strong family history of autosomal dominant diabetes, prominent glycosuria, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, and marked response to sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 therapy should prioritise HNF1A testing.

In short: pursue HNF1B testing when diabetes is accompanied by renal structural disease, early CKD, hypomagnesemia or genital tract anomalies (even without family history). Pursue HNF1A testing when there is multigenerational early‑onset diabetes, prominent glycosuria with progressive β‑cell failure and good response to low‑dose insulin secretagogues.
