# Not All Early-Onset Diabetes Is T1 or T2: Spotting HNF1A‑MODY at the Front Door

What makes this case worth your attention? A young man walks in with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria, some weight loss, no ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, and a preserved—but insufficient—stimulated C‑peptide. He has a striking three‑generation history of early‑onset diabetes. Genetic testing ultimately revealed a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant and the management changed from insulin to targeted, non‑insulin therapy with excellent glycaemic response. This is the kind of case that reminds us: early‑onset glucose dysregulation is not automatically type 1 or type 2.

## Why HNF1A‑MODY can be missed
MODY (monogenic diabetes) is uncommon compared with T1D/T2D, but when present it has big implications for treatment, prognosis and family screening. HNF1A mutations (the most common MODY subtype) produce progressive β‑cell dysfunction, reduced expression of glucose‑sensing genes and a lower renal glucose threshold. Clinically this produces:

- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia out of proportion to BMI or insulin resistance features.
- Glycosuria at relatively modest plasma glucose (because SGLT2 expression is decreased).
- A family history of autosomal dominant early‑onset diabetes (often 2–3 consecutive generations).
- Negative pancreatic autoantibodies and measurable endogenous insulin secretion—at least early on.

Because of overlap with T1D and T2D (weight may be normal/overweight, symptoms non‑specific), MODY often gets misclassified.

## Bedside and clinic clues from this case
This patient illustrates practical, immediately usable signals that should raise your suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY:

- Glycosuria despite plasma glucose levels that might not explain massive osmotic losses. In HNF1A‑MODY, renal threshold for glucose is lower.
- Negative GAD and IA‑2 antibodies in a young person with hyperglycaemia — this makes T1D less likely but doesn’t prove MODY; it should prompt further evaluation.
- Preserved stimulated C‑peptide (here 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon) indicates residual β‑cell function. That argues against complete autoimmune β‑cell destruction.
- Prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia (2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L) — HNF1A often affects postprandial glucose first.
- Strong multigenerational family history of early‑onset diabetes (mother, grandmother, sibling) — classic autosomal dominant pattern.
- BMI can mislead: a BMI in the overweight range does not exclude MODY in modern populations where obesity is common.

Also useful in this case: the patient had evidence of autonomic dysfunction on sudomotor testing, reminding us that complications/neuropathy can occur and surveillance is still required.

## How this changed management
Initial management for severely elevated glucose often includes insulin for safety. But the genetic diagnosis changed the therapeutic plan:

- Insulin was stopped once genetic HNF1A‑MODY was confirmed and the patient was transitioned to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) plus metformin with excellent HbA1c response (10.2% → 6.0% at 6 months).
- Sulfonylureas have traditionally been first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY (they boost insulin secretion), but GLP‑1RAs are attractive alternatives as β‑cell function wanes and to avoid hypoglycaemia.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors: given the low renal glucose threshold and glycosuria in HNF1A‑MODY, SGLT2 inhibitors may increase dehydration, risk of DKA or severe genital infections; weigh risks carefully.
- Genetic confirmation allowed for de‑escalation of insulin and informed family counselling and cascade testing.

## Practical diagnostic approach at the front door
If you see early‑onset diabetes, think beyond the T1/T2 binary. Use these pragmatic steps:

- Ask explicitly about a three‑generation history of early‑onset diabetes. If present, MODY probability rises.
- Send islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8 where available). Negative autoantibodies are supportive (but not definitive).
- Measure stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal test) if available—preserved C‑peptide suggests non‑autoimmune disease.
- Consider an OGTT or postprandial glucose testing; HNF1A commonly shows exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia.
- Use MODY probability calculators as adjuncts, but interpret cautiously in non‑White populations (they can over/underestimate risk).
- If clinical suspicion is moderate‑high (family history, negative antibodies, preserved C‑peptide, atypical phenotype), arrange genetic testing (preferably an NGS panel for monogenic diabetes).

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t be reassured by an “overweight” BMI — obesity doesn’t exclude MODY in today’s world.
- Glycosuria in someone with only moderate hyperglycaemia should prompt thinking about HNF1A (low renal glucose threshold).
- Negative islet autoantibodies + preserved C‑peptide in a young adult = get genetic testing if family history or atypical features exist.
- A genetic diagnosis changes therapy (often allows stopping insulin) and enables targeted treatment and cascade family screening.
- Avoid reflexive SGLT2 inhibitor use in HNF1A until you’ve considered the risk of exaggerated glycosuria and volume depletion.

## Take‑home messages
- Early‑onset diabetes is not always type 1 or type 2—look for red flags: multigenerational early diabetes, glycosuria out of proportion to glucose level, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C‑peptide.
- Postprandial testing (OGTT) is valuable: HNF1A often causes prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia.
- BMI is a poor discriminator for MODY in modern populations; family history and physiology matter more.
- Genetic testing is the definitive step—when suspicion is high it directs therapy, reduces unnecessary insulin, and enables family counselling.
- In HNF1A‑MODY, sulfonylureas and GLP‑1RAs are reasonable choices; be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors.

This patient’s case is a tidy example of precision medicine in diabetes: recognizing the phenotype at the bedside led to genetic confirmation and a targeted, safer, and effective treatment strategy. Keep MODY on your differential—your diagnosis can materially change a young person’s life.



# The Glycosuria Tell: Low Renal Glucose Threshold as a Clue to HNF1A‑MODY

What makes this case interesting? A young man with marked hyperglycaemia and lots of glycosuria, but no ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, a family history of early‑onset diabetes and a BMI that doesn’t scream insulin resistance. Genetic testing revealed a novel HNF1A mutation. The unusual prominence of glycosuria — sometimes out of proportion to symptoms — can be a subtle but powerful clue pointing away from type 1 or typical type 2 diabetes and toward HNF1A‑MODY.

## Quick physiology refresher: HNF1A, SGLT2 and the renal glucose threshold
- HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in liver, pancreas and kidney. Among its renal targets is the SGLT2 gene (SLC5A2), the major proximal tubule sodium–glucose cotransporter responsible for reabsorbing filtered glucose.
- Loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression. The clinical consequence: a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. Patients spill glucose into the urine at lower plasma glucose levels than people with intact SGLT2.
- Result: glycosuria can be prominent early in the course of disease even before severe symptomatic hyperglycaemia (and without the insulin deficiency and ketosis typical of type 1 diabetes).

## Why that matters clinically
- Glycosuria is often interpreted as simply a sign of hyperglycaemia, but when it’s marked relative to the clinical picture (eg, obvious urinary glucose at moderately high or fluctuating glucose values, dehydration without ketones), think HNF1A‑MODY.
- HNF1A‑MODY is a β‑cell disorder — progressive insulin secretory defect — not autoimmune β‑cell destruction. Expect:
  - Negative GAD/IA‑2/ZnT8 autoantibodies in most cases (but autoantibodies are not an absolute exclusion).
  - Preserved but insufficient C‑peptide early on.
  - Strong autosomal dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes (but family history can be misleading when T2D is common).
- The renal phenotype (glycosuria) can precede severe β‑cell failure and is mechanistically distinct from insulin resistance–related glycosuria.

## How to leverage the sign in practice
- Treat disproportionate glycosuria as a red flag prompting reconsideration of diagnosis:
  - Recheck autoantibodies and C‑peptide (basal and/or stimulated).
  - Take a careful three‑generation family history for early‑onset diabetes.
  - Consider OGTT to document exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia typical of HNF1A defects.
  - Use a MODY probability tool where available — but be cautious: calculators are derived from European cohorts and may under/over‑estimate risk in other ethnic groups.
  - If suspicion is high, order genetic testing (targeted MODY panel or NGS). A genetic diagnosis changes therapy and family counselling.
- Treatment implications:
  - HNF1A‑MODY classically responds well to low‑dose sulfonylureas early on. As β‑cell function declines, alternatives such as GLP‑1 receptor agonists are attractive (good efficacy, lower hypoglycaemia risk).
  - Insulin is avoidable in many cases after diagnosis if glycaemia is controlled with oral/GLP‑1 therapies.
  - Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY: theoretical and reported concerns include exaggerated glycosuria, volume depletion and increased DKA risk because residual SGLT2 activity is already low. Use only with careful consideration and monitoring.

## Practical pitfalls and pearls
- Don’t assume all young, non‑obese patients with glycosuria have type 1 diabetes — check autoantibodies and stimulated C‑peptide.
- A normal or only mildly reduced BMI doesn’t exclude HNF1A‑MODY. Rising population obesity blunts the “classic” phenotype.
- Glycosuria in someone with tight control could reflect a low renal threshold, not poor glycaemic control.
- The absence of a previously reported variant in ClinVar or other databases doesn’t exclude pathogenicity — look for segregation in the family, affected domain (eg, DNA‑binding), in silico support and clinical correlation per ACMG criteria.
- Early genetic diagnosis avoids unnecessary lifelong insulin, informs best drug choices, and prompts cascade testing of relatives.

## Take‑home messages
- Marked glycosuria out of proportion to the clinical picture is a useful clinical clue for HNF1A‑MODY because HNF1A mutations lower SGLT2 expression and the renal glucose threshold.
- Combine this clue with negative autoimmune markers, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, and a family history of early‑onset diabetes to trigger genetic testing.
- Genetic confirmation affects treatment: many HNF1A‑MODY patients can be managed with sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists rather than lifelong insulin; avoid reflexive use of SGLT2 inhibitors without careful risk–benefit consideration.
- Think kidney physiology as well as pancreatic β‑cell physiology when a diabetes presentation doesn’t fit the common types — the urine can tell you more than you expect.



# C-Peptide and Antibodies Won’t Save You: Diagnostic Pitfalls in MODY

What’s interesting here? A young man with HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, frank glycosuria, negative GAD/IA‑2, a preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L), and a strong family history of early‑onset diabetes. The working diagnosis initially swung toward type 2 diabetes (and he was started on insulin), but genetic testing found a novel HNF1A pathogenic variant. Within months his insulin was stopped and GLP‑1 agonist + metformin controlled his glucose to HbA1c 6.0%.

This case shows a common clinic trap: relying on single snapshots of c‑peptide and autoimmune markers to "rule in" type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Those tests tilt the odds — but they don’t close the case.

## Why c‑peptide and antibodies can mislead

- C‑peptide is a dynamic marker of endogenous insulin secretion, but it’s highly influenced by timing, recent glycaemia, insulin therapy, and the test used.
  - A single fasting C‑peptide may be low in early type 1, or preserved in early stages. Stimulated tests (glucagon or mixed‑meal) are better, but thresholds aren’t absolute. In this case a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (>0.60 nmol/L is often considered "preserved") suggested residual β‑cell function — supportive of non‑type 1 diabetes but not definitive.
  - Acute hyperglycaemia can mask true β‑cell capacity; conversely, transient honeymoon periods in type 1 can give preserved c‑peptide.
  - Be careful with units and cutoffs (nmol/L vs ng/mL) and with lab reference ranges when interpreting results.

- Autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8) are highly specific for autoimmune type 1 diabetes, but not perfectly sensitive or exclusive.
  - Most MODY patients are antibody‑negative, but there are reports of autoantibody positivity in a minority of genetically proven MODY patients. Autoantibodies can be transient, low titre, or even incidental.
  - Negative antibodies lower the probability of type 1 diabetes, but do not confirm MODY.

- Clinical context matters: age at onset, family history, phenotype (absence of ketoacidosis, lack of insulin resistance stigmata), and biochemical pattern (marked postprandial hyperglycaemia / abnormal OGTT, glycosuria despite not profound hyperglycaemia) are critical clues.
  - HNF1A‑MODY classically has prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia, progressive β‑cell dysfunction, and increased renal glycosuria due to reduced SGLT2 expression — exactly what we saw with this patient’s OGTT (2‑hr 19.8 mmol/L) and glycosuria.

## How to integrate tests in practice

Think probabilistically, not binary. Use c‑peptide and antibodies to change pre‑test probability — then decide if genetic testing is warranted.

- If young onset (<25–35 years), strong autosomal dominant family history of early diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide — think MODY and consider genetic testing even if BMI is not low.
- If antibodies are positive and titres high → type 1 is likely. If antibody negative but c‑peptide very low on stimulation → type 1 remains possible (especially early), and serial testing may help.
- If obesity, insulin resistance features, very high triglycerides/low HDL, and progressive insulin requirement → type 2 more likely, but remember increasing obesity blurs the distinction.
- Use OGTT or postprandial testing: HNF1A‑MODY often shows disproportionate postprandial glucose excursions and glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels.

## Practical tips (timing, assays, and follow‑up)

- Prefer stimulated over fasting c‑peptide (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal). Document the assay, units, reference ranges, and the timing relative to insulin doses.
- Repeat c‑peptide if the clinical picture is ambiguous: early type 1 can show preserved c‑peptide initially; progressive decline supports autoimmune destruction.
- Don’t overinterpret a single negative GAD/IA‑2: if clinical suspicion of autoimmune disease is high, repeat or add ZnT8 antibodies.
- Use probability tools (for example the Exeter MODY calculator) as an aid — but remember population differences (tools derived from European cohorts may misestimate risk in other ethnic groups).
- When MODY is suspected, prioritize genetic testing (targeted NGS panels) — it’s definitive and can change therapy (sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 rather than lifelong insulin in many HNF1A cases).

## Treatment implications: why it matters

Genetic confirmation changes therapy and prognosis. HNF1A‑MODY patients often respond to low‑dose sulfonylureas; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive alternative as β‑cell function declines (lower hypoglycaemia risk). SGLT‑2 inhibitors deserve caution because intrinsic SGLT2 downregulation in HNF1A‑MODY can amplify glycosuria and dehydration risk. In this case, genetic diagnosis allowed stopping insulin and maintaining control with GLP‑1 agonist + metformin.

## Clinical pearls

- C‑peptide and antibodies are probabilistic tools — they shift odds but don’t provide absolute answers.
- Stimulated c‑peptide > common cutoffs suggests preserved secretion but does not exclude early type 1 or MODY.
- Autoantibody negativity increases the likelihood of non‑autoimmune diabetes but is not pathognomonic for MODY; occasional MODY patients can have positive antibodies.
- Look for disproportionate postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, and strong autosomal dominant family history — these increase pre‑test probability for HNF1A‑MODY.
- When suspicion is moderate to high, order genetic testing; it’s the test that actually changes management.

Take‑home messages

- Don’t let a single c‑peptide or autoantibody result dictate the diagnosis.
- Integrate clinical phenotype, OGTT/postprandial glucose, family history, and dynamic c‑peptide testing.
- If clinical suspicion for MODY is real, proceed to genetic testing — it’s precision medicine that alters therapy and prognosis.



# When to Order MODY Genetics: A Pragmatic Pathway (and Why Calculators Can Mislead)

What makes this case interesting: a young man with markedly elevated HbA1c (10.2%), glycosuria, a multi-generation history of early-onset diabetes and negative islet autoantibodies was found to have a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S). He avoided lifelong insulin, was transitioned off basal insulin to GLP‑1 agonist + metformin, and achieved HbA1c 6.0% within months. This is a classic reminder that identifying MODY changes treatment, prognosis and family counseling — but only if you think to test for it.

If you’re in the ED or clinic and see “young diabetic,” here’s a concise, pragmatic pathway for when to pull the trigger on genetic testing.

## The pragmatic pathway — think MODY when you see a cluster of features

No single feature is diagnostic. Use a combination approach. The more elements present, the stronger the case for genetic testing.

- Age at onset
  - Strong flag: diagnosis of diabetes before age 25.
  - Consider testing up to age 35 if other features are present (family history, atypical phenotype).
- Family history
  - Autosomal dominant pattern (multiple successive generations with early-onset diabetes) strongly increases pre-test probability.
  - Absence of family history does not exclude MODY (de novo variants, mis- or under-diagnosed relatives).
- Autoantibodies
  - Negative GAD65, IA‑2, ZnT8 is supportive (not definitive). Positive antibodies make type 1 much more likely.
- Endogenous insulin secretion (C‑peptide)
  - Preserved fasting or stimulated C‑peptide (e.g., stimulated >0.6 nmol/L) beyond the period expected for T1DM supports non-autoimmune diabetes.
  - Low but not absent stimulated C‑peptide (as in this case: 0.83 nmol/L) can still be compatible with MODY.
- Pattern of glucose abnormality
  - Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia with relatively preserved fasting glucose early on — HNF1A often shows prominent post‑prandial rises.
  - Glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose (reduced renal glucose threshold via SGLT2 expression changes) is a classic HNF1A clue.
- Absence of typical type 2 features
  - No acanthosis nigricans, lack of severe obesity, low/normal insulin requirements—these increase suspicion. Rising obesity prevalence weakens this discriminator.
- Treatment response / insulin requirements
  - Low-dose sulfonylurea responsiveness is a classic HNF1A feature; avoidance of insulin or early insulin independence after diagnosis argues against classic T1DM.
- Extra clues
  - Family members with early-onset diabetes who also lack autoantibodies or have preserved C‑peptide.
  - Young adults with diabetes who have autonomic findings (e.g., sudomotor dysfunction was noted in this case) — don’t over-interpret, but consider as part of the phenotype.

If you have several of these features, proceed to genetic testing rather than prolonged empiric insulin.

## How to practically apply thresholds in clinic (quick checklist)

Order genetic testing when:
- Diabetes onset <25 years plus at least one of:
  - First-degree family history of diabetes in multiple generations, OR
  - Negative islet autoantibodies, OR
  - Preserved C‑peptide beyond expected for T1DM, OR
  - Glycosuria disproportionate to glucose level / prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia.

Consider testing up to age 35 when several supporting features are present (e.g., strong family history + negative autoantibodies + atypical body habitus or low insulin requirements).

## The Exeter MODY probability calculator — useful but imperfect

Why it’s popular:
- Combines age of diagnosis, sex, BMI, family history, treatment, time to insulin to give a probabilistic estimate.
- Helps triage who to test, and is validated in many European cohorts.

Limitations you need to know:
- Developed and calibrated primarily in White European populations. Its performance may differ in Asian, African, Hispanic and other populations. It can overestimate MODY probability in some non‑European groups and underperform when obesity prevalence differs.
- BMI and metabolic features are changing globally; rising obesity blunts BMI’s discriminatory power.
- It uses historical treatment variables (e.g., time to insulin) that may be confounded by local practice patterns or by initial misdiagnosis and empiric therapy.
- It’s probabilistic, not diagnostic. A low score doesn’t absolutely exclude MODY if the clinical picture is convincing; conversely, a high score doesn’t replace genetic confirmation.

How to use it sensibly:
- Use the calculator as a supporting tool — not a gatekeeper.
- In a patient with classic red flags (negative antibodies, strong dominant family history, preserved C‑peptide, early onset), proceed to genetic testing even if the calculator is equivocal or was derived from a different population.
- In populations underrepresented in the calculator’s derivation cohort, interpret its score cautiously; consider lowering the threshold for testing if clinical suspicion is high.

The current case: entering the patient into the Exeter tool gave a 62.4% probability — helpful as a corroborating datapoint, but genetic testing was clinically warranted regardless because of negative autoantibodies, strong family history and preserved stimulated C‑peptide.

## Practical testing strategy and downstream actions

- First-line:
  - Negative autoantibodies and strong clinical suspicion → order a MODY panel (NGS panel covering common MODY genes including HNF1A, HNF4A, GCK, HNF1B).
- If panel finds a pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant:
  - Change management according to gene: e.g., HNF1A — consider low‑dose sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA; avoid reflexive long‑term insulin if not required.
  - Cascade testing of at-risk family members.
- If variant of uncertain significance (VUS):
  - Interpret in clinical context; consider segregation testing in family, functional data if available, and reclassification over time.
- If negative panel despite strong suspicion:
  - Consider whole-exome/genome sequencing in specialized centers, or repeat review over time as new variants are reported.

## Clinical pearls from this case

- Don’t assume young = type 1 and older = type 2. Multi‑generation early-onset diabetes with negative autoantibodies is a red flag for MODY.
- Glycosuria in HNF1A can be out of proportion to plasma glucose due to SGLT2 downregulation; SGLT2 inhibitors may carry atypical risks in these patients.
- Genetic diagnosis changes therapy: many HNF1A patients can be taken off insulin and do well on sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists; precision medicine matters.
- Databases (ClinVar, VarSome) are useful, but novel variants can be pathogenic — interpret with ACMG criteria and family segregation.
- Stimulated C‑peptide is a helpful supportive test but is not definitive; trend and clinical context matter.

## Take-home messages

- Consider MODY in young-onset diabetes with autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide and disproportionate postprandial hyperglycaemia or glycosuria.
- Use the Exeter MODY calculator as an adjunct, not a substitute for clinical judgment — especially in non‑European populations where calibration may differ.
- Genetic testing (NGS panels) should be pursued when clinical suspicion is moderate-to-high; a positive result alters treatment, prognosis and prompts family testing.
- In suspected HNF1A-MODY, think about sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA strategies and be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors until you know the genotype.

If you see a young patient whose diabetes “doesn’t fit,” take a focused family history, check autoantibodies and C‑peptide, and lower your threshold for genetic testing — because the result may let you stop insulin, simplify therapy, and give the family answers.



# Insulin Off-Ramp: De-escalating After MODY Confirmation

What makes this case grab attention is its commonness disguised as something else: a young man with significant hyperglycaemia who started on basal insulin in the ED/clinic and — after rapid genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY — was safely taken off insulin and transitioned to non-insulin therapy with excellent glycaemic control. That “insulin-first, genetics-later” approach is not rare in real-world practice. The practical question is: when and how can you safely take someone with suspected MODY off insulin? This case gives us a tidy real-world algorithm — and some pitfalls to watch for.

## Why you should care
- Misdiagnosis happens. Young-onset diabetes without autoantibodies and with strong family history often means MODY, not type 1 or classic type 2. The treatment implications are big: many HNF1A-MODY patients do well on oral/smart injectable therapy and can avoid lifelong insulin.
- Timing matters. This patient started basal insulin for rapid control, had genetic confirmation a month later, and insulin was stopped once there was evidence of preserved β-cell function and partial biochemical response (HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 9.1%). Transition to dulaglutide + metformin led to HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months.
- Precision medicine here is actionable: a genetic diagnosis changed management (insulin withdrawal), drug choice and follow-up strategy.

## Key clinical considerations before you touch the insulin pen
Before de-escalating insulin, be sure you have the right clinical picture:

- Confirm clinical suspicion for MODY
  - Age of onset, autosomal dominant family history, lack of features of insulin resistance (though BMI can be higher now), prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia or glycosuria.
  - Negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2, ZnT8 where available).
- Assess residual β-cell function
  - Stimulated C-peptide is the best objective measure you’ll usually have. In this case a glucagon stimulation test produced a 6‑min C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (≈2.5 ng/mL), supporting preserved but insufficient insulin secretion.
  - As a rule of thumb: stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (≈1.8 ng/mL) suggests endogenous insulin production sufficient to consider non-insulin options; values are supportive, not absolute.
- Early genetic testing is essential
  - NGS panels rapidly confirming HNF1A mutation were decisive here. If you strongly suspect MODY, pursue genetic testing early — results guide drug choice and family counselling.
- Clinical response to initial therapy
  - An improving HbA1c (even partial) after short insulin exposure indicates capacity for glucose control without continued insulin support.

## Choosing non-insulin agents in HNF1A-MODY
Classic teaching favors low-dose sulfonylureas for HNF1A-MODY because they stimulate insulin secretion. That works, but newer options can be better tailored to the patient.

- Sulfonylureas
  - Pros: established efficacy in many HNF1A patients, low cost.
  - Cons: hypoglycaemia risk, weight gain, durability concerns.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA)
  - Pros: potent glucose lowering, weight benefit, lower hypoglycaemia risk vs sulfonylureas, potential β-cell trophic effects; worked well in this case (dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly → HbA1c 6.0%).
  - Cons: cost, GI side effects; long-acting GLP‑1 RAs require thinking about tolerability before stopping insulin.
- Metformin
  - Pros: sensible first-line adjunct for insulin-sparing effect and weight/metabolic benefits. In this case metformin (1500 mg/day) was continued.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors
  - Pros: modest efficacy, good tolerability; used here as long-term maintenance with metformin.
- SGLT‑2 inhibitors
  - Use with caution in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression, and marked glycosuria plus high SGLT2 inhibition can increase risk of dehydration and euglycaemic DKA. Avoid routinely unless clear benefit and careful monitoring.
- Practical approach
  - If C‑peptide is preserved and antibodies negative, consider moving from basal insulin to GLP‑1 RA + metformin (or SU if cost/availability) once you have genetic confirmation or very high clinical certainty.
  - If HbA1c is very high and risk of catabolic state exists, stabilize with short-term insulin first, then re-assess.

## Stepwise insulin off-ramp — a practical checklist
1. Stabilize acute hyperglycaemia (short-term basal insulin acceptable) and address symptoms/ketosis risk.
2. Obtain autoimmune markers and stimulated C‑peptide. Send a MODY genetic panel early.
3. Reassess 2–6 weeks:
   - Has HbA1c or glucose improved on initial therapy?
   - Is stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L?
   - Are autoantibodies negative?
   - Is genetic test positive for an actionable MODY variant (eg, HNF1A)?
4. If above supportive, plan the off-ramp:
   - Stop prandial insulin first (if any), keep low-dose basal for 48–72 h while initiating alternative agent (eg, start GLP‑1 RA at recommended initiation dose and metformin).
   - After 48–72 h of tolerated non-insulin therapy, consider stopping basal insulin if fasting glucose and home glucose patterns stable.
   - Arrange frequent follow-up and glucose monitoring (see below).
5. If any signs of metabolic decompensation during wean (rising fasting glucoses, ketonuria, symptomatic hyperglycaemia), resume insulin and reassess.

## Monitoring during and after transition
- Self-monitoring:
  - Home capillary glucose checks: fasting and postprandial twice daily initially, more often if symptomatic.
  - Test for ketones if glucose >15–20 mmol/L (270–360 mg/dL) or if unwell.
- Clinic/phone follow-up:
  - Within 48–72 hours of insulin cessation and within 1–2 weeks thereafter; adjust therapy based on glucose logs.
  - If on GLP‑1 RA, counsel on GI side effects and timing; ensure patient can tolerate before stopping insulin entirely.
- Labs:
  - Recheck HbA1c at 3 months after transition.
  - Consider repeat stimulated C‑peptide if clinical course changes.
- Complication surveillance:
  - Screen for microvascular disease as per guidelines; keep long-term follow-up.

## Red flags — when to stop the off-ramp or return to insulin
- Rising fasting glucose or progressive hyperglycaemia despite maximal tolerated non-insulin therapy.
- Presence of ketones or any DKA.
- Symptoms of catabolism (weight loss, polyuria, polydipsia) with high glucose.
- Evidence of insulin autoimmunity developing or C‑peptide falling substantially.
- Severe intercurrent illness or steroid treatment that overwhelms β-cell reserve.
If any of these occur, reinstitute insulin temporarily and reassess β-cell function and diagnosis.

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t let initial insulin therapy prevent you from pursuing MODY testing in young patients with atypical features — genetics can change everything.
- Stimulated C‑peptide is more informative than fasting C‑peptide when planning insulin withdrawal.
- GLP‑1 RAs are a pragmatic, often safer alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY patients who need insulin de-escalation.
- Be cautious with SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY due to increased glycosuria and theoretical DKA/dehydration risk.
- An improving HbA1c while on a brief course of insulin is a good sign that de-escalation may succeed.

## Take-home messages
- In young adults with suspected HNF1A-MODY, pursue early genetic testing — results are often practice-changing.
- Before stopping insulin, confirm negative islet autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide; treat short-term insulin to stabilize if needed.
- Transition strategy: introduce non-insulin agent (GLP‑1 RA ± metformin or SU) while tapering insulin over days, with close glucose and ketone monitoring.
- Watch for red flags (ketones, rising glucose, catabolism) and be ready to restart insulin temporarily.
- Precision diagnosis in MODY allows safer insulin withdrawal and individualized long-term therapy — as illustrated by this case where de-escalation led to HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months.

This case is a reminder that an insulin prescription is not always permanent — get the genetics right, check C‑peptide, and design a safe off-ramp.



# Sulfonylurea or GLP-1 RA for HNF1A‑MODY? Balancing physiology, evidence, and patient priorities

What makes this case interesting: a young man with a novel HNF1A pathogenic variant had dramatic glycaemic improvement after precision‑diagnosed HNF1A‑MODY and a switch off insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) plus metformin, with HbA1c falling from 10.2% to 6.0% in months. That outcome forces the clinical question we face increasingly in practice: classically HNF1A‑MODY is “sulfonylurea‑sensitive” — does that still mean SU first for everyone? Or is a GLP‑1 RA often a better fit?

Below I unpack the physiology, the limited randomized data, pragmatic pros/cons, and a patient‑centered approach to choosing an agent.

## Why HNF1A‑MODY is sulfonylurea responsive (the physiology)
HNF1A mutations cause progressive β‑cell dysfunction with reduced insulin secretory response to glucose, but preserved KATP channel function. Sulfonylureas close KATP channels downstream of glucose sensing and can therefore restore insulin secretion effectively — often at low doses — making them historically the cornerstone therapy for many HNF1A patients. Expect good initial glucose lowering and the possibility of stopping insulin in many patients after genetic confirmation.

## Why sulfonylureas are not always ideal
- Hypoglycaemia: SUs increase insulin release irrespective of ambient glucose. In young active patients, hypoglycaemia risk can be meaningful.
- Weight gain: SUs are weight‑neutral to weight‑gaining, which may be undesirable, especially in patients with cardiovascular or metabolic risk.
- Durability: As β‑cell function declines over years, SU efficacy may wane and hypoglycaemia risk may rise.
- Patient preference: Injections, weight goals, hypoglycaemia fear, and comorbidities alter the calculus.

## What the randomized data say about GLP‑1 RAs vs sulfonylureas in HNF1A
High‑quality randomized data are sparse. The main randomized, double‑blind, crossover trial in genetically confirmed HNF1A patients compared liraglutide with glimepiride and found:
- Similar reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose between liraglutide and glimepiride.
- GLP‑1 RA had a lower risk of mild hypoglycaemia vs sulfonylurea.
- GLP‑1 RAs bring additional benefits (weight loss or neutrality, favourable CV signals in high‑risk T2D populations, slower decline of β‑cell stress hypothesized).

This is not a large, long‑term cardiovascular or preservation‑of‑β‑cell function trial, but it suggests comparable glycaemic efficacy with lower hypoglycaemia for GLP‑1 RAs in HNF1A‑MODY.

## Practical pros and cons — side‑by‑side

Sulfonylureas
- Pros: Oral, inexpensive (often), potent glucose lowering in HNF1A at low doses, well established.
- Cons: Hypoglycaemia risk, weight gain, may lose efficacy as β‑cell function declines; less attractive for people with erratic schedules.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists
- Pros: Excellent glycaemic efficacy in many patients, weight loss or neutrality, low hypoglycaemia risk, favourable cardiometabolic profile in some high‑risk patients, may be injectable weekly (adherence benefit).
- Cons: Cost, side effects (nausea), limited HNF1A‑specific long‑term data, injections (though oral semaglutide exists), regulatory/coverage issues in some systems.

Other agents
- DPP‑4 inhibitors: modest glucose lowering; used as add‑ons (as in the presented case) but less potent than GLP‑1 RA.
- SGLT‑2 inhibitors: physiologically tempting but be cautious — HNF1A variants lower renal SGLT2 expression and can cause glycosuria; case reports warn of dehydration and DKA risk. Use with caution and close monitoring.
- Insulin: needed in severe hyperglycaemia or ketosis at presentation; may be withdrawable after diagnosis and targeted therapy.

## How I approach agent selection in practice (patient‑centered algorithm)
1. Confirm the diagnosis:
   - Genetic confirmation of HNF1A changes the plan. Negative islet autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide support the case, but sequencing is decisive.
2. Assess current glycaemia and β‑cell reserve:
   - Severe hyperglycaemia or catabolism may need initial insulin. Reassess after glucotoxicity resolves.
3. Elicit patient priorities:
   - Fear of hypoglycaemia? Desire to avoid weight gain? Cost concerns? Willingness for injections?
4. Shared decision making:
   - If patient prioritizes minimizing hypoglycaemia and weight control and can afford/accept GLP‑1 RA → reasonable to choose GLP‑1 RA (as in the case where dulaglutide worked well).
   - If patient prefers oral therapy, has limited resources, and accepts hypoglycaemia risk → low‑dose sulfonylurea is still a highly evidence‑based first choice.
5. Dose strategy and monitoring:
   - Sulfonylurea: start low (eg, gliclazide or glimepiride low dose), counsel on hypoglycaemia, frequent glucose checks early on.
   - GLP‑1 RA: start per usual titration, expect GI side effects early; monitor weight, glucose, and injection tolerability.
6. Reassess and plan for long term:
   - Monitor HbA1c, hypoglycaemia events, weight, renal function, and microvascular complications.
   - Be prepared to change therapy as β‑cell function declines — combination therapy or insulin may ultimately be needed.

## Applying this to the presented case
That young man had:
- Strong family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved but insufficient C‑peptide, and novel pathogenic HNF1A variant.
- He presented initially on insulin + metformin + DPP‑4 but after genetic confirmation insulin was stopped and dulaglutide initiated. This aligns with a patient‑centered decision to avoid SU‑related hypoglycaemia and to leverage GLP‑1 benefits — and it worked exceptionally well (HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0%).

This illustrates that precision diagnosis enables personalized therapy: sulfonylurea sensitivity is a guideline, not a mandate. GLP‑1 RAs are a defensible first‑line alternative in many patients, especially where hypoglycaemia, weight, or patient preference matter.

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t assume young‑onset, non‑obese diabetes is type 1: family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide should prompt consideration of MODY genetic testing.
- HNF1A‑MODY often responds well to low‑dose sulfonylureas, but consider GLP‑1 RA when hypoglycaemia risk, weight, or cardiometabolic goals are important.
- GLP‑1 RAs have randomized crossover data in HNF1A showing similar glycaemic control to sulfonylureas and less hypoglycaemia — small studies, but clinically meaningful.
- Be cautious with SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY because of baseline glycosuria and case reports of dehydration/DKA.
- Use shared decision‑making: cost, route (oral vs injection), adverse effects, and lifestyle must guide agent choice.

Key take‑home messages
- Genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY should change your therapeutic thinking — precision diagnosis matters.
- Sulfonylureas remain a low‑cost, effective option and are often first‑line, but they carry hypoglycaemia and weight penalties.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a reasonable, evidence‑supported alternative in HNF1A‑MODY when hypoglycaemia avoidance, weight management, or patient preference favor them.
- Individualize therapy using current betacell reserve, severity of hyperglycaemia, and patient priorities; reassess and adapt as disease progresses.



## SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY: proceed with caution

Here’s the clinical twist that should make you pause when you see “MODY” in a chart: HNF1A mutations don’t just mess with β‑cells — they transcriptionally downregulate SGLT2 in the proximal tubule. That commonly causes baseline glycosuria and a lowered renal threshold for glucose. Add a pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibitor on top of genetically reduced SGLT2 activity and you can get supraphysiologic glycosuria, excessive osmotic diuresis, dehydration — and, in susceptible patients, euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (euDKA). The case above is a perfect illustration: a young adult with HNF1A‑MODY presented with marked glycosuria and, as the literature warns, there are reports of severe dehydration and DKA linked to excessive SGLT2 inhibition in this population.

Let’s walk through the why, the how worried you should be, and practical guidance if you’re considering SGLT2 therapy for cardiorenal indications in a patient with (or suspected of having) HNF1A‑MODY.

Mechanism — why HNF1A matters for SGLT2 drugs
- HNF1A is a transcription factor that upregulates SGLT2 (SLC5A2) expression in the proximal tubule. Loss‑of‑function HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression → lower renal glucose reabsorption → baseline glycosuria and a lower renal threshold.
- Pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibitors block residual transporter activity. In HNF1A‑MODY this can produce exaggerated glycosuria and osmotic diuresis compared with typical T2D patients.
- Excessive fluid losses can lead to hypovolaemia, prerenal AKI, and trigger counter‑regulatory responses (↑glucagon, ↓insulin), predisposing to ketogenesis and euDKA. Because blood glucose may be relatively low (due to glycosuria), euDKA can be missed until advanced.

How worried should you be?
- The absolute risk is not well quantified — HNF1A‑MODY is uncommon and the evidence is mostly case reports and physiologic plausibility. But the signal is strong enough that multiple authors caution against routine SGLT2 use in confirmed HNF1A‑MODY.
- Consider context: cardiorenal benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors are compelling in many patients, but in a young person with preserved renal function and few cardiorenal risks, the marginal benefit is small and the risk (volume depletion, DKA) may outweigh it. In older patients with strong cardiorenal indication, risk/benefit becomes individualized.

Practical guidance — what to do in clinic
- If the patient has known HNF1A‑MODY:
  - Prefer alternatives first: GLP‑1 receptor agonists (for weight/cardio benefit), metformin, or sulfonylureas for glycaemic control in classic HNF1A‑MODY. These align with the pathophysiology and case outcomes (GLP‑1 RA + metformin worked well above).
  - Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors as routine therapy for glucose lowering. If there’s a compelling cardiorenal indication (e.g., HFrEF, albuminuric CKD) and you still consider an SGLT2, proceed only after careful shared decision‑making and specialist input.
  - If you proceed, make a mitigation plan: baseline hydration status and renal function; teach sick‑day rules; check baseline ketones; frequent follow‑up; low threshold to stop the drug during intercurrent illness, reduced oral intake, or dehydration; monitor for orthostatic symptoms and AKI.
- If MODY is suspected but not yet confirmed (young onset, strong autosomal dominant family history, negative GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8, preserved-ish C‑peptide, pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria):
  - Consider deferring SGLT2 initiation until genetic testing returns, especially in younger patients without strong cardiorenal indications.
  - Use MODY probability tools and consult endocrinology for testing (NGS panels are increasingly accessible).
- If a patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor presents with unexplained nausea, malaise, abdominal pain, or signs of dehydration — check serum ketones (β‑hydroxybutyrate), acid/base, and creatinine early. euDKA can have only mildly elevated glucose.
- Documentation and consent: if you initiate an SGLT2 in any patient with suggestive features of HNF1A‑MODY, document the discussion of potential increased risk of dehydration/euDKA and the plan for monitoring and sick‑day management.

Clinical pearls
- HNF1A mutations frequently produce glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels — that finding should trigger suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY in young adults with non‑autoimmune diabetes.
- SGLT2 inhibitors can potentiate a genetic reduction in SGLT2 function — “double blocking” the kidney’s glucose reabsorption and increasing osmotic diuresis.
- euDKA risk with SGLT2 inhibitors is mechanistically plausible in HNF1A‑MODY: dehydration + increased glucagon-to‑insulin ratio + ongoing glycosuria that masks hyperglycaemia.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive alternative in HNF1A‑MODY when you want weight loss, cardiovascular benefit and lower hypoglycaemia risk.

Take‑home messages
- HNF1A‑MODY reduces SGLT2 expression and commonly causes baseline glycosuria — adding an SGLT2 inhibitor can amplify osmotic diuresis and raise the risk of dehydration, AKI and euDKA.
- Avoid routine SGLT2 use for glucose lowering in confirmed HNF1A‑MODY; if there is a strong cardiorenal indication, refer to endocrinology/nephrology and use shared decision‑making with strict safety monitoring.
- In young, non‑autoimmune diabetes with family history and glycosuria, consider MODY genetic testing before starting SGLT2 inhibitors.
- If you do start an SGLT2 in a suspected or confirmed HNF1A patient, ensure clear sick‑day rules, ketone education, hydration advice, and low threshold to stop the drug during illness.

Bottom line: SGLT2 inhibitors are powerful tools — but in HNF1A‑MODY the physiology that makes them attractive in T2D may become a liability. Know the diagnosis or at least suspect it before you “block” the kidney’s last remaining glucose reabsorption.



# Postprandial Spikes Without DKA: The OGTT as a Diagnostic Lever

What caught my eye in this case wasn’t the fasting glucose or the absence of ketoacidosis — it was the jaw-dropping 2-hour OGTT of 19.8 mmol/L (about 357 mg/dL) in a young man with glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes. His fasting was high (12.4 mmol/L / ~223 mg/dL) and HbA1c was 10.2%, but it was the exaggerated postprandial excursion on the OGTT that best signalled a primary beta-cell secretory problem consistent with HNF1A-MODY. Genetic testing later confirmed a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant, and treatment was successfully refined away from insulin to a GLP-1 RA + metformin strategy.

If you’re in the business of sorting young-onset hyperglycaemia, the OGTT is an underused diagnostic lever — especially when you suspect monogenic diabetes.

## Why the OGTT matters in beta-cell disorders like HNF1A-MODY
- HNF1A mutations produce a progressive defect in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. Basal insulin may be preserved early, but the beta cell fails when challenged with a glucose load. That’s exactly the physiology the OGTT interrogates.
- Postprandial hyperglycaemia can be disproportionate to fasting levels. A normal or near-normal fasting glucose does not exclude clinically meaningful beta-cell dysfunction.
- HNF1A also affects GLUT2 expression and renal SGLT2 transcription. That combination predisposes to marked postprandial spikes and glycosuria at lower plasma glucose thresholds — a clinical clue when you see glycosuria out of proportion to BMI or insulin resistance markers.
- In this case the glucagon-stimulated C-peptide was modestly low (peak 0.83 nmol/L), antibodies were negative, family history was classic — but the OGTT crystalised the phenotype and supported targeted genetic testing.

## When to deploy the OGTT in busy clinical practice
Think OGTT when you see any of the following in a young patient (<35–40 years) with hyperglycaemia:
- Early-onset hyperglycaemia with strong multigenerational family history of diabetes.
- Glycosuria disproportionate to fasting glucose, BMI or insulin resistance signs.
- Negative diabetes autoantibodies but equivocal fasting glucose / C-peptide.
- Symptoms that suggest postprandial hyperglycaemia (post-meal polyuria, pronounced thirst after meals) despite only modest fasting abnormalities.
- Diagnostic uncertainty between type 1, type 2 and monogenic diabetes — especially before committing to lifelong insulin.

Avoid OGTT when the patient is acutely ill, on steroids, or has recent large dietary changes; standardize preparation (overnight fast, avoid heavy exercise/alcohol beforehand).

## Practical interpretation — be pragmatic
- Use the 75 g OGTT with plasma glucose at 0 and 2 hours. Remember cutoffs: 2-hr ≥11.1 mmol/L (≥200 mg/dL) diagnostic of diabetes; but the magnitude of the 2-hr rise (and relative mismatch vs fasting) is the informative piece in suspected MODY.
- Look for a pronounced 2-hr increment (large delta between fasting and 2-hr) rather than just the absolute numbers. In beta-cell disorders, you’ll often see sharp post-load spikes.
- Consider paired C-peptide (basal and stimulated) and islet autoantibodies alongside the OGTT. Together they give the physiology (insulin reserve), aetiology (autoimmune vs genetic) and the load-response.

## What the OGTT result changes for management
- It’s not just diagnostic theatre — OGTT results can shift therapy. Identifying a beta-cell secretory defect should raise suspicion for HNF1A-MODY; that has implications:
  - Sulfonylureas are a classic first-line for HNF1A-MODY (low-dose SU often works), but GLP-1 RAs can be used to preserve beta-cell function and lower hypoglycaemia risk.
  - SGLT2 inhibitors may be problematic: HNF1A-driven downregulation of SGLT2 can exaggerate glycosuria and dehydration risk; use with caution.
  - Genetic confirmation opens precision medicine pathways and family screening.
- In the case here, insulin was de-escalated after genetic confirmation; a GLP-1 RA + metformin achieved HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months.

## Pitfalls and limitations
- OGTT is resource- and time-intensive; you won’t do it on everyone. Use pretest probability — age, family history, autoantibodies, C-peptide — to select candidates.
- Obesity and rising T2D prevalence blur phenotypes. MODY can co-exist with obesity; don’t exclude genetic causes solely based on BMI.
- OGTT can be affected by recent diet, meds and illness — standardize prep and repeat if results are inconsistent with clinical picture.
- A single OGTT won’t replace genetic testing — it helps triage who needs sequencing.

## Clinical pearls
- A huge post-load spike with relative preserved or only mildly reduced fasting insulin/C-peptide should make you think beta-cell secretory defects (MODY), not classic insulin resistance.
- Glycosuria in a young non-ketotic patient is a red flag for HNF1A involvement (think reduced renal glucose threshold via SGLT2 downregulation).
- Use OGTT selectively to unmask impaired glucose tolerance patterns that fasting samples miss — especially when genetic testing resources are limited and you need to pick candidates wisely.

Take-home messages
- The OGTT is a high-yield test for detecting exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia from primary beta-cell defects such as HNF1A-MODY.
- Consider OGTT in young patients with early-onset diabetes, strong family history, unexplained glycosuria, negative autoantibodies or discordant fasting vs clinical picture.
- A dramatic 2-hour rise supports targeted genetic testing; results can meaningfully change therapy (e.g., move away from insulin to SU/GLP-1RA and enable family screening).
- OGTT is a tool to triage, not to replace, genetic confirmation — but used wisely it can be the lever that unlocks a precision diagnosis.



# Novel Variant, Real Disease: How to Interpret HNF1A Results Beyond ClinVar

What grabbed me about this case was the familiar mismatch we see too often: a young adult with marked hyperglycaemia, negative autoantibodies, a strong multigenerational diabetes history — yet the genetics report came back with an HNF1A variant that wasn’t in ClinVar or VarSome. The temptation is to shrug and call it “variant of uncertain significance” or — worse — to downplay genetics because a database “doesn’t have it.” This case shows why that’s the wrong move: a newly reported HNF1A p.N270S (c.809A>G) change behaved exactly like HNF1A-MODY clinically, and careful application of ACMG reasoning plus phenotypic data made the variant clinically meaningful.

If you see a novel HNF1A variant, you don’t stop at “not in ClinVar.” You walk a diagnostic pathway: clinical phenotype → biochemical support → family segregation → variant-level evidence (location, conservation, in silico, functional data if available) → integrated ACMG classification. Below I’ll walk through how the team in this report made that assessment and how you can apply the same thinking at the bedside or in the clinic.

Why this case is instructive
- Classic red flags for MODY: young age at presentation, strong autosomal-dominant family history of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but insufficient C-peptide, glycosuria without DKA.
- Variant was novel (absent from ClinVar/VarSome) yet clustered with disease in the family and sat in the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A — a hotspot for pathogenic variants.
- Treatment changed meaningfully after diagnosis: insulin stopped, transitioned to GLP-1 RA + metformin with excellent glycaemic response (HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0% over months), illustrating clinical utility of genetic diagnosis.

Applying ACMG-style reasoning to a novel HNF1A variant — the logical steps

1) Start with the phenotype
Clinical phenotype is not background noise — it is evidence. HNF1A-MODY typically presents with:
- Young onset (often before 25–35), progressive β-cell dysfunction, strong autosomal dominant pattern.
- Glycosuria due to reduced SGLT2 expression (a useful clinical hint).
- Negative T1D autoantibodies and measurable C-peptide early on.
In this case the phenotype fits HNF1A-MODY strongly — treat this as supportive evidence when weighing variant pathogenicity.

2) Segregation in the family
Cosegregation is high-value evidence. The reported family had several first-degree/second-degree relatives with early-onset diabetes (mother, maternal grandmother, brother). Demonstrating that the variant is present in affected relatives (and absent in unaffected where available) upgrades confidence. In practice:
- Every informative meiosis where variant segregates with disease adds weight (ACMG’s PP1 tiered approach).
- Even limited segregation in a clear autosomal dominant pedigree is compelling for MODY genes with high penetrance like HNF1A.

3) Variant-level features: domain and residue
Not all variants are equal. HNF1A has functional domains: DNA-binding domain is a mutation hotspot. Key points:
- A novel missense in the DNA-binding domain (as here) carries more suspicion than one in a region poorly conserved or outside functional domains.
- Proximity to known pathogenic residues increases likelihood of a deleterious effect — adjacent or same functional motif matters.
So p.N270S being in the DNA-binding domain and near known pathogenic sites is moderate-to-strong evidence toward pathogenicity.

4) In silico and quantitative predictors
Use evolutionary conservation and missense impact tools intelligently:
- High scores from multiple models (AlphaMissense, CADD, REVEL etc.) add supporting evidence (ACMG PP3).
- No single prediction proves pathogenicity — use them as part of the ensemble.
This case reported a very high AlphaMissense score (0.995) — supportive but not standalone proof.

5) Population frequency matters
Check gnomAD and local population databases:
- A truly pathogenic HNF1A-MODY variant should not be present at appreciable frequency in the general population.
- Absence from population databases supports rarity (PM2 evidence).
In this report the variant was absent from ClinVar and public catalogs — that absence supports, rather than refutes, pathogenicity.

6) Functional data
When available, functional assays are gold-standard. Often they’re not available for a novel variant. In their absence:
- Domain, conservation, in silico, and segregation evidence must be integrated more heavily.
- If clinical response to targeted therapy (e.g., sulfonylurea/GLP-1 RA enabling insulin cessation) is observed, that is pragmatic supportive evidence of gene-level causality.

7) Integrate all evidence with ACMG framework
No single datapoint rules the day. ACMG-style classification is cumulative:
- PM2 (absent from controls)
- PM1 (located in a mutational hot spot/critical domain)
- PP1 (segregation with disease in multiple affected family members — strength depends on number of meioses)
- PP3 (multiple in silico predictors support deleterious effect)
- PP4 (patient’s phenotype highly specific for gene)
Combined, these can push a novel variant into likely pathogenic or pathogenic even if ClinVar is silent.

Clinical implications — why it matters
- Treatment: HNF1A-MODY patients often respond well to low-dose sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists and may not need long-term insulin. In this case, discontinuation of insulin and good control on GLP-1 RA + metformin was both diagnostic and therapeutic.
- Counseling and cascade testing: A likely pathogenic result triggers cascade testing of relatives — early identification changes monitoring and therapy and helps reproductive counselling.
- Surveillance and drug choices: Knowledge of HNF1A status affects decisions (e.g., caution with SGLT2 inhibitors given baseline glycosuria and theoretical DKA/dehydration risks in severe SGLT2 deficiency).

Practical tips for clinicians receiving a novel HNF1A report
- Don’t throw hands up at “novel” or “not in ClinVar” — ask for the raw variant annotation, domain location, conservation, in silico scores and any segregation data the lab has.
- Correlate tightly with phenotype: age at onset, family history, antibody status, C-peptide, glycosuria.
- If family members are available, arrange targeted testing for segregation — even one or two informative relatives can change classification.
- Talk to the genetics lab: many labs will reclassify variants if you provide segregation or functional data.
- When reporting back to patients, emphasize uncertainty levels and the plan: cascade testing, tailored therapy, and periodic re-evaluation as new evidence emerges.

Clinical pearls
- “Not in ClinVar” ≠ “benign.” Absence from databases is expected for novel pathogenic variants.
- Domain matters: missense changes in HNF1A’s DNA-binding domain deserve extra scrutiny.
- Phenotype-first reasoning is powerful — in suspected MODY, the clinical picture is evidence in variant classification.
- Early genetic diagnosis can change treatment (discontinue insulin, choose sulfonylurea/GLP-1 RA) and prompt cascade testing that benefits family members.

Take-home messages
- Evaluate novel HNF1A variants using an integrated approach: phenotype, segregation, domain/location, population frequency, in silico predictions, and (if available) functional data.
- Absence from ClinVar or population databases supports rarity and does not exclude pathogenicity.
- Even limited cosegregation plus supportive clinical features can be sufficient to call a variant likely pathogenic and change management.
- Arrange cascade testing and genetic counselling — a single HNF1A diagnosis can identify multiple affected family members and enable precision therapy.

This case is a neat reminder: precision medicine isn’t just about fancy sequencing — it’s about integrating genetic data with good clinical thinking. When you see a young patient with diabetes who doesn’t fit type 1 or type 2 neatly, push for the right testing and, when a novel variant shows up, do the work to interpret it — because the result can be dramatically actionable.



# MODY in the Metabolic Syndrome Era: Don’t Let BMI Blind You

What’s interesting here? A young man with biochemical features that could fit metabolic syndrome—elevated triglycerides, low HDL, symptoms of hyperglycaemia—turns out to have HNF1A-MODY from a novel HNF1A mutation. The case is a neat reminder that rising obesity and metabolic syndrome are eroding the clinical heuristics we’ve relied on to pick out monogenic diabetes.

Why this matters now
- Historically we used “young, lean, strong family history, no autoantibodies” as our mental trigger for MODY testing. That heuristic was reasonable when obesity was less prevalent. It’s failing now: MODY occurs in patients with higher BMI and overlapping metabolic features.
- Mislabeling HNF1A-MODY as type 1 or type 2 diabetes has real consequences: unnecessary lifelong insulin, missed opportunity for sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 therapy, incorrect family counselling, and missed cascade testing.

How to think about MODY today (practical, bedside-focused)
- Age cut-offs are shifting from rigid “<25” to a probabilistic view: MODY can present into the 30s and beyond. Don’t dismiss it based on age alone.
- BMI alone is unreliable. As population BMI increases, the negative predictive value of “lean = MODY” drops.
- Look for “discordant” features: a young patient with marked hyperglycaemia but negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, strong multigenerational diabetes history, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia or glycosuria out of proportion to insulin resistance — these should raise the index of suspicion regardless of BMI.
- Use biochemical clues: marked postprandial glucose rise on OGTT, glycosuria with normal/near-normal glycaemic exposure (suggesting low renal threshold), preserved stimulated C‑peptide (>0.6 nmol/L supportive), and negative beta‑cell autoantibodies.
- Probability tools (eg, Exeter MODY calculator) help but were calibrated in mainly White European cohorts and can misestimate risk in other ethnic groups. Treat their output as one input, not a gatekeeper.

A pragmatic trigger strategy to keep sensitivity high without swamping genetics services
(aim: identify patients who should get targeted NGS panel testing)
Consider genetic testing when a patient with diabetes meets at least two of the following:
- Diabetes onset before age 35 (or strong family history of early-onset diabetes across ≥2 generations)
- Absent pancreatic autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8) and preserved stimulated C‑peptide (>0.6 nmol/L) beyond the honeymoon period
- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (eg, 2‑hour OGTT ≥11.1 mmol/L) or disproportionate glycosuria
- Low insulin requirement (eg, <0.5 U/kg/day) beyond the initial management window or ability to discontinue insulin after glycaemic stabilization
- Multigenerational autosomal dominant pattern of diabetes or early-onset diabetes in multiple first-degree relatives

Why these cut-points? They balance sensitivity (catch atypical presentations that include higher-BMI patients) and specificity (avoid mass-testing everyone with young-onset T2D). Local resource realities and pre-test counselling should guide how aggressively you test.

Therapeutic implications worth remembering
- HNF1A-MODY often responds well to low‑dose sulfonylureas; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive alternative as beta cell function declines (less hypoglycaemia, weight benefits).
- Precision diagnosis often permits stopping insulin safely if residual endogenous insulin exists.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY: reduced SGLT2 expression and baseline glycosuria could increase dehydration/DKA risk in some patients — weigh risks vs benefits carefully.
- Cascade testing: identifying an index case allows targeted family testing and earlier diagnosis/treatment for relatives.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t use BMI as a screening stop sign. A modestly elevated BMI or components of metabolic syndrome do not exclude MODY.
- Negative autoantibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide in a young patient should trigger genetic consideration even when the phenotype looks “metabolic.”
- Glycosuria out of proportion to glycaemia (or early renal glycosuria) in a young person is a red flag for HNF1A dysfunction.
- Genetic testing now often uses NGS panels — novel variants will be found; pathogenicity is judged by ACMG criteria, segregation, functional prediction, and phenotype.
- Precision diagnosis changes management: stop unnecessary insulin, pick targeted oral agents or GLP‑1 therapy, and offer family counselling.

Take-home messages
- Rising obesity blunts the classic “lean MODY” heuristic — don’t let BMI alone rule out monogenic diabetes.
- Consider MODY genetic testing when young patients with diabetes have negative autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, a strong multigenerational family history, disproportionate glycosuria or marked postprandial hyperglycaemia.
- Genetic diagnosis alters therapy and prognosis: most HNF1A patients can be managed without insulin and may benefit from sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists; exercise caution with SGLT2 inhibitors.
- Use probability tools and local resources, but keep clinical judgment central — we need sensitivity in the metabolic syndrome era to catch MODY cases like this one.



# Early Neuropathy in MODY? Making Sense of Sudomotor Dysfunction at Diagnosis

What grabs your attention in this HNF1A-MODY case is not the excellent treatment response — that’s increasingly familiar — but the finding you wouldn’t expect in a newly diagnosed young adult: severely reduced hand sudomotor conductance on baseline testing. Small‑fiber and autonomic neuropathies are usually late complications of chronic hyperglycaemia. So how do we interpret severe sudomotor impairment at presentation in someone with a novel HNF1A variant?

Put simply: it’s real enough to merit investigation, but don’t rush to label this “MODY neuropathy” without thinking through alternatives, testing limitations, and practical implications for follow‑up.

Why this is surprising
- Autonomic/small‑fiber neuropathy typically occurs after years of diabetes exposure. Severe sudomotor dysfunction at diagnosis in a young adult suggests either (a) accelerated nerve injury from substantial hyperglycaemia before presentation, (b) a non‑glycaemic cause, or (c) a MODY‑specific effect — for which data are sparse.
- HNF1A-MODY is mainly a β‑cell transcription factor disorder; there are no well‑established mechanisms linking HNF1A mutations to primary small‑fiber degeneration. Published data on early autonomic involvement in MODY are limited and inconsistent.

What could explain the abnormal Sudoscan result?
- True small‑fiber/autonomic neuropathy from hyperglycaemia: This patient had very high glucose/HbA1c at presentation (HbA1c 10.2%). Acute and subacute hyperglycaemia can impair small‑fiber function, and some neuropathic changes may be apparent earlier than we classically expect.
- Pre‑existing neuropathy from another cause: hereditary small‑fiber neuropathies, toxic exposures, nutritional deficiencies, autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy, or prior infections can present in young adults and coexist with diabetes.
- Testing artefact or confounders:
  - Sudoscan and other electrochemical sudomotor tests are convenient but not diagnostic. Results can be influenced by skin condition, hydration status, recent smoking, medications, limb temperature and technical factors.
  - Glycosuria and dehydration can alter skin conductance measurements. The case noted glycosuria; volume depletion could have influenced results.
- Coincident autonomic dysfunction related to a different systemic process, though the case has no other clear red flags.

How to work this up sensibly
- Don’t assume this is irreversible diabetic neuropathy. Confirm and phenotype.
  1. Repeat testing after early glycaemic control: if the abnormality improves with normalization of glucose, that supports a metabolic effect.
  2. Formal autonomic testing: heart rate variability, Valsalva, tilt table testing and quantitative sudomotor axon reflex testing (QSART) provide complementary information. QSART is more specific for postganglionic sudomotor function.
  3. Small‑fiber structural testing if symptoms or objective dysfunction persist: skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve fibre density (IENFD) is the structural gold standard; corneal confocal microscopy is emerging.
  4. Screen for alternative causes: B12/folate, thyroid, HIV, serum protein electrophoresis, toxins/meds, and family history for hereditary neuropathies.
  5. Consider neurophysiology (nerve conduction studies) to exclude large‑fiber disease — keeping in mind NCS are insensitive for small‑fiber neuropathy.

Clinical implications and follow‑up
- Symptom screening: actively ask about orthostatic symptoms, GI dysmotility, erectile dysfunction, urinary dysfunction, sweating abnormalities and distal pain/paraesthesia. Many autonomic problems are under‑reported unless you ask.
- Foot care: even without large‑fiber disease, reduced sudomotor function can alter skin integrity and increase risk of fissures/infection. Advise daily foot inspection and prompt attention to skin changes.
- Frequency of microvascular screening: continue guideline‑directed retinopathy and albuminuria surveillance. Early neuropathic findings don’t mandate changing retinopathy screening intervals immediately, but they should heighten vigilance and reinforce tight glycaemic control.
- Treatment: optimize glycaemic control (this case responded dramatically to GLP‑1 agonist + metformin). Symptomatic therapies for neuropathic pain and autonomic symptoms as needed. Address dehydration and modifiable contributors.
- Family implications: because HNF1A‑MODY is autosomal dominant, relatives should be screened both for dysglycaemia and, if sudomotor/autonomic symptoms are present, for neuropathy workup. Genetic counselling remains central.

What we don’t know — and what to watch for
- There’s scant data linking HNF1A mutations directly to primary small‑fiber or autonomic neuropathy. Most literature focuses on pancreatic, hepatic and renal consequences; peripheral nerve involvement is not a well‑described phenotype of HNF1A‑MODY.
- Longitudinal follow‑up of this patient and his affected relatives could be informative: does sudomotor dysfunction improve with glycaemic control, remain stable, or progress despite good control? Publishing serial objective measures would advance understanding.

Clinical pearls / take‑home messages
- Severe sudomotor dysfunction at diabetes diagnosis is unexpected — confirm it before attributing it to MODY.
- Repeat sudomotor testing after glycaemic optimization; pursue QSART and formal autonomic testing if abnormalities persist or symptoms are present.
- Consider alternative causes of small‑fiber/autonomic neuropathy; don’t anchor on the genetic diagnosis alone.
- Continue routine microvascular surveillance and emphasize foot care and symptomatic management.
- Document and follow objective autonomic measures over time — they may distinguish transient metabolic effects from a persistent or genetic small‑fiber process.

Bottom line: abnormal sudomotor testing in a young person with HNF1A‑MODY is a red flag worth workup, not a definitive manifestation of MODY. Be methodical: confirm, phenotype, exclude other causes, optimize glucose, and follow longitudinally. That approach best serves the patient and builds the evidence base about whether early autonomic involvement is part of the MODY story or an unfortunate co‑incidence.



# Turning One Diagnosis Into Many: Family Cascade Testing and Precision Therapy in HNF1A‑MODY

What grabbed my attention in this case was how a seemingly routine discovery of hyperglycaemia in a young man — HbA1c 10.2%, glycosuria on annual check — unmasked a dominantly inherited, treatable form of diabetes. A previously unreported HNF1A variant (p.N270S) explained the early-onset diabetes in multiple generations and directly changed management: insulin stopped, GLP‑1 receptor agonist and metformin achieved HbA1c 6.0% within months. That’s precision medicine in real time — one genetic test did more than label disease; it redirected therapy and triggered family-wide opportunity for diagnosis and prevention.

Why this matters
- HNF1A‑MODY is often mislabelled as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Misclassification can lead to unnecessary insulin, missed chances for oral agents that work notably well (eg, sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 RAs), and a failure to offer targeted testing to relatives.
- Identification of a pathogenic HNF1A variant has implications beyond the proband: autosomal dominant inheritance means a 50% prior probability for first‑degree relatives, and earlier detection can prevent prolonged hyperglycaemia and overtreatment.
- This case also illustrates that novel variants (absent from ClinVar/VarSome) can be pathogenic based on segregation, in‑silico prediction and domain location — and these findings can and should guide clinical care when interpreted carefully.

From one proband to a family strategy: practical cascade testing steps
- Start with a clear phenotype and documentation: age at onset, BMI, symptoms, autoantibody status, C‑peptide (basal and stimulated), glycosuria, and family pedigree. In this case: early onset, negative GADA/IA‑2, stimulated C‑peptide ~0.83 nmol/L (moderately preserved), multigenerational diabetes — fertile ground for MODY testing.
- Confirm the proband’s genetic diagnosis (NGS panel or targeted sequencing). Once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified, offer targeted testing to first‑degree relatives for that specific variant — cheaper, faster, and unambiguous compared with broad panels.
- Prioritize testing order: symptomatic relatives and those of reproductive age first, then other first‑degree relatives. Use cascade results to guide screening intervals for mutation‑negative relatives (treat as population risk) versus carriers (early surveillance).
- Pre‑ and post‑test counseling matters: discuss inheritance (50% risk), variable expressivity and penetrance, implications for management (medication choices, pregnancy planning), and psychosocial aspects. Obtain informed consent and document discussions.
- Record and communicate results to primary care and the family: a positive genetic result should trigger medication review, monitoring (retinopathy/microalbuminuria screening), and education about symptoms and lifestyle.

Aligning genotype with therapy — clinical pragmatism
- Use residual beta‑cell function and clinical severity to individualize therapy. HNF1A‑MODY typically causes progressive β‑cell dysfunction but often preserves insulin secretory capacity longer than autoimmune type 1 disease. In the case, stimulated C‑peptide was modestly preserved, supporting a trial off insulin.
- First‑line options historically: low‑dose sulfonylureas are effective in many HNF1A patients because of marked insulin secretory responsiveness. But weigh hypoglycaemia risk, patient preference and lifestyle.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists (used here as dulaglutide) are an attractive alternative — effective glycaemic lowering, weight neutrality or loss, and lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas. This patient achieved rapid and sustained improvement (HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0% at 6 months) after switching to GLP‑1 RA + metformin.
- Insulin can often be withdrawn if C‑peptide shows preserved endogenous insulin and autoantibodies are negative — but do this under close supervision with glucose monitoring and follow‑up HbA1c checks.
- Avoid SGLT‑2 inhibitors without careful consideration: reduced SGLT2 expression in HNF1A‑MODY can produce prominent glycosuria and there are case reports of severe dehydration/DKA risk; exercise caution and individualise decisions.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors were part of the long‑term plan for this patient (saxagliptin with metformin) after stabilization — a reasonable maintenance strategy when glycaemia is controlled and hypoglycaemia risk is a concern.

Counselling and long‑term surveillance
- Emphasize that genetic diagnosis changes screening and prognostic conversations. HNF1A carriers still need standard microvascular risk surveillance (annual retinal and renal screening), and cardiovascular risk should be addressed per guidelines.
- Discuss reproductive implications: each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the variant; consider offering testing to adolescents and young adults with appropriate counselling.
- For relatives who decline genetic testing, consider regular metabolic screening (fasting glucose/HbA1c, urine dipstick for glycosuria) given family history.

Pitfalls and realistic expectations
- Not all “MODY” phenotypes will have an identifiable pathogenic variant; conversely, not all variants flagged by in‑silico tools are disease causing. Variant classification requires integrating family segregation, clinical phenotype and ACMG criteria.
- Absence of autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide are supportive but not definitive — repeat testing and longitudinal assessment can be informative.
- Be mindful of mixed phenotypes: rising obesity and T2D prevalence can coexist with monogenic disease, complicating interpretation of BMI and metabolic syndrome features.

Clinical pearls
- Always ask about multigenerational early‑onset diabetes: that pedigree (grandmother in 30s, mother late 20s, brother at 20) should trigger MODY consideration.
- Negative islet autoantibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide + strong family history = high yield for genetic testing.
- Genetic diagnosis of HNF1A can permit stopping insulin and switching to oral/GLP‑1 therapy if β‑cell function allows — with rapid HbA1c and quality‑of‑life gains.
- Cascade testing is cost‑effective: targeted testing for a known familial variant is far cheaper than de novo panel testing and has immediate clinical utility.

Take‑home messages
- Consider MODY (especially HNF1A) in young adults with non‑insulin autoimmune diabetes, preserved C‑peptide and strong family history; genetic testing changes treatment.
- After identifying a pathogenic HNF1A variant in a proband, perform cascade testing for the specific variant in first‑degree relatives with appropriate counselling.
- Use residual β‑cell function to guide therapy: many HNF1A carriers respond to sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 RAs and can often discontinue insulin under close follow‑up.
- Avoid blanket use of SGLT‑2 inhibitors without assessing risks in HNF1A patients; individualized choice is key.
- A single genetic diagnosis can unlock precision care across a family — don’t miss the opportunity.

This case is a reminder that a simple annual check can lead to a genetic diagnosis that changes medications, reduces hypoglycaemia risk, and identifies at‑risk relatives — the very definition of precision, family‑focused medicine.
