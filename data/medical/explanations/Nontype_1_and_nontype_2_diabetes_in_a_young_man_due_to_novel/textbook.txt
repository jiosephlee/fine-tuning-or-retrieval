## Clinical Approach to Young-Onset, Nonautoimmune Diabetes

Young-onset diabetes is usually thought of as either type 1 diabetes (autoimmune β-cell destruction) or increasingly as early type 2 diabetes related to obesity and insulin resistance. However, the case presented — a young man with marked hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, a preserved but insufficient stimulated C‑peptide, and a multigenerational history of early-onset diabetes — illustrates an important third possibility: monogenic diabetes, most commonly maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY). Recognizing MODY at the bedside changes diagnostic reasoning, directs appropriate laboratory testing, triggers genetic confirmation and family cascade testing, and frequently alters therapy. The remainder of this section outlines a pragmatic clinical approach to suspecting and evaluating MODY, with emphasis on HNF1A-MODY features highlighted by the case.

When to suspect MODY
MODY should be considered in any patient with diabetes diagnosed at a young age (classically before age 25, but many clinicians extend consideration to ages 25–35) who lacks typical features of autoimmune type 1 diabetes and in whom type 2 physiology is not convincing. Helpful clinical signals include an autosomal dominant pattern of diabetes (affected individuals in successive generations), nonketotic hyperglycaemia at presentation, and absence of clinical insulin resistance (no acanthosis nigricans, modest or normal BMI). In the case, the patient’s BMI was 24.6 kg/m2, there was no acanthosis, and three consecutive maternal relatives had early-onset diabetes — a pattern that should prompt consideration of MODY. Age cutoffs and definitions are not absolute; rising rates of obesity and variable penetrance mean MODY can present outside classic age ranges and with varying body habitus.

Clinical features that point toward HNF1A-MODY
Within the MODY spectrum, HNF1A mutations are among the most common causes and have distinctive clinical hallmarks. Hyperglycaemia is often characterized by exaggerated postprandial glucose excursions (frequently revealed by an OGTT), progressive β‑cell failure rather than insulin resistance, and a propensity to develop glycosuria at relatively lower plasma glucose concentrations because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the kidney. The presented patient had a striking 2‑hour OGTT glucose of 19.8 mmol/L and glycosuria on screening, features that fit HNF1A biology. Clinically, HNF1A-MODY patients often lack severe obesity and have few or no signs of metabolic syndrome early on, although overlap with type 2 features becomes more common as obesity increases population-wide.

Initial bedside and laboratory evaluation
A careful family history is essential: ask about age at diagnosis and treatment of diabetes in first‑ and second‑degree relatives, noting early onset across generations. Physical exam should seek signs of insulin resistance and features that would argue for alternative diagnoses. Initial labs to distinguish autoimmune from nonautoimmune diabetes include measurements of fasting glucose/HbA1c, islet autoantibodies (GAD65, IA‑2, and ZnT8 when available), and assessment of endogenous insulin secretion by C‑peptide. In the case, both GAD and IA‑2 antibodies were negative and a glucagon stimulation test produced a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L — evidence of preserved endogenous insulin secretion despite hyperglycaemia, supporting a non–type 1 aetiology. A stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L is conventionally taken to indicate residual β‑cell function, whereas very low or undetectable levels point toward type 1 diabetes; remember, however, that early in type 1 disease C‑peptide can be preserved, so serial testing or clinical context is important.

Interpreting discordant or borderline results
No single laboratory test is definitive. Autoantibody negativity supports (but does not prove) nonautoimmune diabetes; rare MODY patients may have positive autoantibodies and conversely some individuals diagnosed clinically with type 1 diabetes will be autoantibody‑negative. Similarly, C‑peptide interpretation depends on timing and glycaemic status — a fasting or stimulated C‑peptide may be low because of acute hyperglycaemia or glucotoxicity and can recover with improved control. Thus, the combination of clinical history (especially autosomal dominant inheritance), absence of autoantibodies, and preserved C‑peptide is the most compelling bedside evidence to justify molecular testing.

When to order genetic testing
Genetic testing for MODY should be considered when the pretest probability is appreciable: young onset, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, strong family history in an autosomal dominant pattern, atypical features for type 2 disease (eg, lack of obesity or severe insulin resistance), or unusual clinical clues such as disproportionate glycosuria. Clinical prediction tools such as the Exeter MODY calculator can help prioritize testing — the calculator estimated a 62% probability in this patient — but these algorithms were developed in European cohorts and can overestimate risk in non‑White populations; they should inform rather than dictate testing decisions. Practical constraints (cost, availability) sometimes necessitate targeting testing to patients with multiple supportive features rather than all young‑onset diabetes.

Choice of genetic test and interpretation
Next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panels covering the common MODY genes (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.) are the usual first‑line molecular approach because they are efficient and detect single nucleotide variants and small indels across multiple genes. Once a variant is identified, interpretation follows standards such as the ACMG guidelines: pathogenicity is assessed by population frequency, segregation with disease in the family, functional data, in silico prediction scores, and published reports. The patient in the case had a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S) which was absent from databases yet met multiple ACMG criteria — segregation with disease in the family, proximity to known pathogenic sites, and strong in silico predictions — supporting a likely pathogenic classification. Genetic counselling should accompany testing for informed consent, discussion of implications for treatment, and planning of cascade testing in relatives.

Therapeutic and prognostic implications of genetic diagnosis
A genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY has immediate clinical consequences. Patients with HNF1A‑MODY typically retain significant insulin secretory capacity early in the disease and are exquisitely sensitive to secretagogues such as low‑dose sulfonylureas; many treated patients can be transferred off insulin to oral agents. The case illustrates a common clinical pathway: initial insulin use to rapidly control severe hyperglycaemia, followed by withdrawal of insulin after genetic confirmation and transition to non‑insulin therapies (in this patient, a GLP‑1 receptor agonist with metformin, later simplified to metformin plus a DPP‑4 inhibitor) with excellent glycaemic response. GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive option because they augment insulin secretion in a glucose‑dependent manner and carry lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas; randomized crossover trials have shown similar glycaemic efficacy between liraglutide and sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY but fewer hypoglycaemic events with GLP‑1 agonists. Conversely, use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY requires caution: reduced SGLT2 expression in these patients contributes to baseline glycosuria and theoretically increases the risk of volume depletion, genital infections, and euglycemic ketoacidosis when SGLT2 inhibitors are added.

Practical algorithm for the bedside clinician
A pragmatic evaluation might proceed as follows: for any patient with diabetes diagnosed under ~35 years, take a careful three‑generation family history and perform a focused exam for insulin resistance. Obtain HbA1c, fasting glucose, lipid profile, islet autoantibodies, and measure fasting or stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon stimulation test if available). If autoantibodies are negative, C‑peptide is preserved, and family history or clinical features are suggestive, discuss targeted genetic testing with the patient and refer for genetic counselling. If a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is found, change therapy as appropriate, inform and offer cascade testing to at‑risk relatives, and institute routine diabetes complication surveillance tailored to expected disease course.

Pitfalls and limitations
Do not exclude MODY simply because a patient is overweight or because there is some family history of type 2 diabetes — obesity is increasingly common in all populations and does not rule out monogenic causes. Likewise, remember that autoantibodies can be transient, and C‑peptide can be misleading early in the course of autoimmune disease; serial assessment and clinical judgement are important. Finally, variant interpretation can be challenging — many variants are classified as of uncertain significance — and pathogenicity often rests on segregation and functional data that may not be immediately available. Collaboration with clinical geneticists and reference laboratories is therefore valuable.

Conclusion
MODY, particularly HNF1A-MODY, is an underdiagnosed cause of young‑onset, nonautoimmune diabetes. The key bedside clues are early age of onset, multigenerational autosomal dominant family history, glycosuria disproportionate to glycaemia, absence of marked insulin resistance, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved endogenous insulin secretion. These features should prompt genetic testing because a confirmed molecular diagnosis enables precision medicine — guides choice of therapy, spares unnecessary insulin in many cases, and identifies at‑risk family members for targeted screening and early treatment. The presented case exemplifies how careful clinical assessment combined with modern genetic testing can transform management and prognosis for a patient with monogenic diabetes.

## Pathophysiology and Genetics of HNF1A‑MODY

HNF1A-MODY is a paradigmatic example of how a single transcription factor defect produces a characteristic clinical syndrome by perturbing gene networks in pancreatic β-cells and in extra‑pancreatic tissues. HNF1A (hepatocyte nuclear factor 1‑alpha) is encoded on chromosome 12q24, spans 10 exons and produces a 631–amino‑acid protein with three functional modules: an N‑terminal dimerization domain that allows HNF1A to form homo‑ and heterodimers, a central POU‑like DNA‑binding domain that mediates sequence‑specific recognition of target promoters, and a C‑terminal transactivation domain that recruits the transcriptional machinery. HNF1A is highly expressed in pancreatic β‑cells, hepatocytes and renal tubular epithelium, and through transcriptional control of multiple downstream genes it coordinates glucose sensing, metabolism and renal glucose handling.

At the β‑cell level, most pathogenic HNF1A variants produce loss of function—either by reducing the amount of functional protein (haploinsufficiency) or, less commonly, through dominant‑negative effects—resulting in progressive β‑cell dysfunction rather than primary β‑cell destruction. HNF1A regulates a suite of genes required for glucose sensing and metabolic coupling: SLC2A2 (GLUT2), enzymes of glycolysis such as pyruvate kinase, and elements of insulin gene expression. Reduced expression of GLUT2 and metabolic enzymes lowers glucose entry and glycolytic flux in β‑cells, decreases ATP generation in response to glucose, and thereby blunts ATP‑dependent closure of KATP channels and glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion. The clinical consequence is a progressive impairment of insulin secretion manifest first as exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia and, over time, frank fasting hyperglycaemia. This pathophysiology explains why patients with HNF1A‑MODY typically have sufficient residual C‑peptide to be distinguished from autoimmune type 1 diabetes for several years after diagnosis, yet they develop gradually worsening insulin deficiency.

HNF1A actions outside the pancreas account for several characteristic non‑glycaemic features of HNF1A‑MODY and provide diagnostic clues. In the kidney, HNF1A transcriptionally regulates SLC5A2, the gene encoding the sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in proximal tubules. Reduced SGLT2 expression lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, so patients with HNF1A variants commonly develop glycosuria at relatively modest plasma glucose levels. This feature — glycosuria out of proportion to hyperglycaemia — is clinically useful when present, and it also explains why SGLT2 inhibitor use in some HNF1A‑MODY patients may unmask profound glucosuric fluid losses and predispose to dehydration or euglycaemic ketoacidosis in susceptible individuals.

Hepatic effects of HNF1A loss further inform diagnostics and risk stratification. HNF1A positively regulates acute‑phase proteins including CRP; accordingly, baseline high‑sensitivity CRP concentrations tend to be lower in people with HNF1A‑MODY than in individuals with typical type 2 diabetes. While low hs‑CRP is not diagnostic by itself, it is an inexpensive extrapancreatic biomarker that can add supportive evidence when combined with clinical context (early onset, autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide). HNF1A also modulates hepatic lipid and drug‑metabolizing pathways, which may affect the broader metabolic phenotype.

The genetic landscape of HNF1A‑MODY is heterogeneous but instructive. Hundreds of sequence variants have been described; pathogenic changes are most commonly single‑nucleotide substitutions or small indels that disrupt the DNA‑binding domain in the central portion of the protein. Variants in the DNA‑binding domain frequently have strong functional effects and are over‑represented among pathogenic alleles. Mutations in the transactivation domain occur less often and sometimes have milder phenotypes. There is also an isoform effect: HNF1A produces multiple transcript isoforms, and mutations that affect exons utilized in all isoforms tend to yield earlier onset or more severe disease than mutations limited to exons expressed in a subset of isoforms.

Interpreting a novel HNF1A variant for clinical care requires integration of genetic and clinical evidence according to established standards (ACMG/AMP). Useful lines of evidence include: absence or extreme rarity of the variant in population databases (supporting pathogenicity), cosegregation with diabetes in multiple affected family members (supporting), localization within a functionally critical domain such as the DNA‑binding region (supporting), conservation of the altered residue across species and concordant deleterious predictions from multiple in silico algorithms (supporting but not definitive), and direct functional studies demonstrating altered transcriptional activity (strong evidence when available). Clinical concordance—early onset, negative islet autoantibodies, glycosuria at modest glucose levels, preserved but inadequate stimulated C‑peptide—provides crucial phenotype support. By combining these elements, a novel variant can often be classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), or benign; this classification should be explicitly reported and periodically revisited as new data accrue.

The case described illustrates these principles. The novel heterozygous substitution c.809A>G (p.Asn270Ser, often written p.N270S) lies in exon 4 within the DNA‑binding domain — a region where pathogenic mutations are concentrated — and segregated with early‑onset diabetes in multiple family members. It was absent from public population databases and received a high deleteriousness score by computational tools; together with the clinical phenotype (early onset, autoantibody negativity, glycosuria and preserved stimulated C‑peptide), these data supported a classification of likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria. That molecular diagnosis had immediate clinical utility: insulin was safely withdrawn and therapy adjusted to agents (e.g., sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists) whose efficacy is predictable in HNF1A‑MODY, and cascade testing for at‑risk relatives could be offered.

From a laboratory perspective, next‑generation sequencing (NGS) multigene panels have become the pragmatic standard for suspected monogenic diabetes because they permit simultaneous interrogation of all MODY genes, detect single‑nucleotide and small indel variants efficiently, and can be combined with methods to detect copy number changes. Reports should state zygosity (heterozygous for HNF1A‑MODY), precise nucleotide and protein nomenclature, exon and protein domain localization, ACMG classification with the evidence lines used, and recommended clinical actions such as cascade testing and phenotype‑driven follow‑up. Clinicians must recognize that many novel variants will initially be VUS and that collaboration with molecular geneticists, functional laboratories and family studies is often required to move from uncertainty to actionable precision care.

In summary, HNF1A‑MODY arises from loss‑of‑function variants in a transcription factor that coordinates β‑cell glucose sensing and renal glucose reabsorption. The resulting syndrome — progressive insulin secretory failure with renal glycosuria and extracutaneous biochemical signatures such as low hs‑CRP — has distinct therapeutic and family‑testing implications. Careful application of ACMG‑guided variant interpretation, combined with targeted phenotyping, enables precision medicine: a genetic diagnosis changes prognosis, directs choice of glucose‑lowering therapy and identifies relatives at risk.

## Differential Diagnosis of Early-Onset Diabetes

Early-onset hyperglycaemia presents a diagnostic challenge because the cardinal diabetes syndromes — type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes (T2D), latent autoimmune diabetes of adults (LADA), secondary causes, mitochondrial diabetes, and the various forms of maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) — overlap in age of onset and some clinical features. The case above illustrates this diagnostic crossroads: a young adult with marked hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, a strong family history of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide. These elements point away from classic T1D and toward a monogenic aetiology (HNF1A-MODY in this patient), but each alternative diagnosis must be explicitly considered and excluded because the management and prognosis differ substantially.

Type 1 diabetes remains the most important mimic in young adults because it is common and can present with marked hyperglycaemia. Typical features that favour T1D are rapid symptom onset, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), and persistent or progressive insulin deficiency with low or undetectable C‑peptide after the honeymoon phase. Circulating islet autoantibodies — most usefully GAD65, IA‑2 and ZnT8 — are present in the majority of new-onset T1D cases and are a strong discriminator. Practically, a positive GAD65/IA‑2/ZnT8 markedly increases the likelihood of autoimmune diabetes and makes MODY much less likely, but it does not absolutely exclude it: low‑titer antibodies have been reported in a small number of monogenic diabetes patients. C‑peptide testing (either fasting or stimulated, for example after glucagon) helps quantify β‑cell function; a stimulated C‑peptide above roughly 0.6 nmol/L (as in this case, 0.83 nmol/L) indicates substantial residual insulin secretion and should prompt consideration of non‑autoimmune diabetes when antibodies are negative. Importantly, in very early T1D the C‑peptide may still be preserved, so serial measurements and clinical context are essential.

Youth‑onset T2D has become more common and is an increasingly frequent differential diagnosis. T2D in young people is characterised by features of insulin resistance such as obesity, central adiposity, acanthosis nigricans and an adverse lipid profile (high triglycerides, low HDL). The decline in β‑cell function is generally more gradual than in T1D, and islet autoantibodies are absent. Family histories are often positive but reflect polygenic risk and common adult-onset diabetes in relatives, rather than clear dominant transmission across generations. Rising rates of overweight and metabolic syndrome mean that some MODY patients will have elevated BMI or dyslipidaemia and may be mislabelled as T2D; in the presented patient the BMI was modest and acanthosis was absent, findings that were not typical for insulin‑resistant T2D and prompted further evaluation for monogenic diabetes.

Within the MODY spectrum, several genotypes have characteristic clinical signatures that guide testing and therapy. Glucokinase‑MODY (GCK‑MODY, MODY2) is the easiest to recognise: it causes a stable, lifelong, mild fasting hyperglycaemia typically in the range of ~5.5–8.5 mmol/L (100–153 mg/dL) with minimal postprandial excursions and a very low risk of microvascular complications. Patients with GCK‑MODY usually do not benefit from pharmacologic glucose‑lowering outside of pregnancy, so identifying this diagnosis spares patients unnecessary therapy and anxiety. By contrast, HNF1A‑MODY (MODY3) produces progressive β‑cell dysfunction with disproportionately large postprandial hyperglycaemia and frequent glycosuria due to a lower renal glucose threshold (reduced SGLT2 expression). HNF1A patients typically present later than GCK-MODY with higher HbA1c and greater therapeutic need; they are classically very sensitive to low‑dose sulfonylureas, although GLP‑1 receptor agonists are increasingly used as effective and safer alternatives when sulfonylurea‑related hypoglycaemia is a concern. The case patient’s high post‑OGTT glucose, marked HbA1c elevation and glycosuria were characteristic of HNF1A dysfunction rather than GCK‑MODY.

HNF4A‑MODY (MODY1) resembles HNF1A clinically in producing progressive insulin secretory failure and sensitivity to sulfonylureas, but it has additional clues: a history of neonatal hypoglycaemia or macrosomic birth weight in the proband or offspring is common because HNF4A variants can transiently increase fetal insulin secretion. If such perinatal features are present, HNF4A should be prioritised.

HNF1B‑associated diabetes (MODY5) is a phenocopy that must be considered because non‑glycaemic features are prominent and alter management. HNF1B variants commonly cause structural renal disease (cysts, dysplasia), genitourinary tract malformations, hypomagnesaemia, and pancreatic hypoplasia; diabetes may present early and often progresses to require insulin. The presence of renal impairment, hypomagnesaemia, abnormal liver enzymes or genital tract anomalies in a young person with diabetes strongly suggests HNF1B and should prompt renal imaging and genetic testing directed at HNF1B rather than HNF1A.

Mitochondrial diabetes, for example from the m.3243A>G mutation, shows a maternal‑only inheritance pattern and is associated with extra‑pancreatic features such as sensorineural hearing loss, progressive myopathy, encephalopathy or stroke‑like episodes. Because mitochondrial heteroplasmy produces variable phenotypes, diabetes can be the presenting feature; when a pedigree shows diabetes transmitted exclusively by mothers or the patient has hearing loss, mitochondrial testing is indicated.

Other causes that should be considered and excluded include LADA (latent autoimmune diabetes of adults), which is essentially autoimmune diabetes with a slower course — older age at onset, presence of autoantibodies and gradual progression to insulin dependence characterise LADA. Secondary diabetes from pancreatic exocrine disease (pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, haemochromatosis), endocrine disorders (Cushing’s, acromegaly), or drug‑induced causes (glucocorticoids, some antipsychotics) must also be considered when the history or examination points to these processes.

Putting this into a practical diagnostic approach: begin with a careful pedigree (look for autosomal dominant transmission across successive generations and whether the pattern is paternal, maternal or mixed), thorough phenotyping (age at onset, acuity of symptoms, DKA history, growth and birth history, renal or genital anomalies, hearing loss, BMI and signs of insulin resistance), and targeted laboratory testing. Key tests that help triage further investigation are islet autoantibodies (GAD65, IA‑2, ZnT8), fasting and stimulated C‑peptide, fasting and postprandial glucose or a 2‑hour OGTT, basic metabolic panel including magnesium, and urinalysis for glycosuria. A strongly positive antibody panel or very low C‑peptide supports autoimmune diabetes. Mild, stable fasting hyperglycaemia with little postprandial rise points to GCK‑MODY and can justify targeted GCK sequencing first. Features such as renal malformations or hypomagnesaemia direct testing toward HNF1B. A maternal‑only pedigree with hearing loss suggests mitochondrial testing. When clinical features point to an autosomal dominant, non‑autoimmune presentation but do not fit a single gene phenotype, a MODY gene panel by next‑generation sequencing is now the most efficient diagnostic strategy and, when positive, directly affects management and cascade testing in relatives.

Finally, the therapeutic and prognostic implications of distinguishing these entities are profound. Identifying HNF1A‑MODY in our patient enabled withdrawal of insulin and selection of sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 based therapy with excellent glycaemic response, avoided unnecessary long‑term insulin use, identified at‑risk family members for cascade testing, and informed counselling about potential complications (eg, progressive β‑cell failure, renal glucose handling). Conversely, recognising GCK‑MODY could have spared this patient pharmacotherapy entirely, while finding HNF1B or mitochondrial disease would have prompted multidisciplinary surveillance for renal or neurologic complications. In every young person with diabetes, integrating careful history, focused examination and targeted biochemical testing will identify the clues that direct appropriate genetic or specialised investigations — and, as the case shows, enable precision treatment that improves outcomes.

## Diagnostic Workup and Testing Algorithm

Confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY requires a systematic approach that integrates routine metabolic testing, evaluation of endogenous insulin secretion, focused autoantibody testing to exclude type 1 diabetes, assessment for characteristic glycaemic patterns, and, when indicated, targeted genetic testing with careful variant interpretation and family cascade testing. The clinical case above illustrates a typical pathway: a young adult with marked hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, a multigenerational history of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide—features that together raised clinical suspicion and prompted molecular testing that confirmed HNF1A-MODY.

Initial laboratory panel and clinical evaluation
Begin with the basic diabetes workup in any patient with new hyperglycaemia: fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c to quantify chronic and fasting control; a basic metabolic panel to evaluate electrolytes, renal function and bicarbonate (to exclude DKA when clinically indicated); a lipid profile because dyslipidaemia may coexist and influences cardiovascular risk management; and urinalysis to document glycosuria and exclude significant proteinuria. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria is often present at lower plasma glucose levels than expected because HNF1A dysfunction reduces SGLT2 expression and lowers the renal threshold for glucose. In practice, documenting glycosuria in someone with modestly elevated plasma glucose is a supportive clinical clue.

Autoantibody testing to exclude type 1 diabetes
Because differentiating MODY from autoimmune type 1 diabetes is essential, test for diabetes-associated autoantibodies—GAD65 (GADA), IA‑2 and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) when available. The absence of these antibodies increases the pretest probability of a nonautoimmune etiology such as MODY, while their presence makes type 1 diabetes more likely. However, antibody status is not absolute: a minority of patients with genetically proven MODY may have positive autoantibodies, and conversely, some patients with autoimmune diabetes are antibody-negative at diagnosis. Therefore antibody results must be interpreted in the context of clinical features and C‑peptide.

Assess endogenous insulin secretion (fasting and stimulated C‑peptide)
Measurement of endogenous insulin secretion is a key discriminator. A fasting C‑peptide can be informative but is less sensitive than stimulated testing. A glucagon stimulation test (baseline and 6‑minute post‑glucagon C‑peptide) or a mixed‑meal tolerance test is commonly used. Stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (as in the reported case where the 6‑min value was 0.83 nmol/L) generally indicates preserved β‑cell function and argues against absolute insulin deficiency typical of longstanding type 1 diabetes. Values below this threshold suggest significant β‑cell failure and make MODY less likely, although early type 1 can present with preserved C‑peptide, so serial testing and the full clinical picture matter.

Dynamic glycaemic testing and clinical clues
An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) often accentuates the diagnostic pattern in HNF1A-MODY: relatively modest fasting hyperglycaemia with exaggerated postprandial glucose excursions (2‑hour glucose frequently ≥11.1 mmol/L). In practice, an OGTT or postprandial glucose measurements can reveal disproportionately high postprandial values for the fasting glycaemia observed. Lack of marked insulin resistance signs (absence of acanthosis nigricans, normal or only mildly elevated BMI) supports MODY, but these features are not definitive—rising obesity prevalence blurs clinical distinction. Ancillary tests such as high‑sensitivity C‑reactive protein (hsCRP) can be helpful: hsCRP tends to be low in HNF1A‑MODY compared with type 2 diabetes, providing supportive but not diagnostic evidence.

When to order genetic testing
Genetic testing should be considered when a patient has:
- Young‑onset diabetes (classically diagnosed before 25 years, though many HNF1A patients present later) AND
- A nonautoimmune phenotype (negative GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8) with preserved endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L), and/or
- A strong autosomal dominant family history of early-onset diabetes (multigenerational affected relatives), or
- Clinical features typical for MODY such as prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia with glycosuria at relatively low glucose levels.

In practice, if multiple of these elements are present—as in our patient with negative autoantibodies, stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, glycosuria and a clear maternal inheritance pattern—genetic testing should be pursued promptly. Use of clinical tools such as the Exeter MODY probability calculator can help triage testing, but interpret their output cautiously in non‑European ancestries because performance declines outside the derivation population.

Choice of genetic test and laboratory considerations
Use a targeted MODY next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panel that includes HNF1A and other common MODY genes (GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.). Advantages of a multigene NGS panel include broad coverage and cost‑effectiveness. Ensure the laboratory interrogates single nucleotide variants and small indels and uses methods to detect copy number variants (deletions/duplications), either by in‑panel read‑depth analysis or by reflex MLPA (multiplex ligation‑dependent probe amplification), because large exonic deletions can be missed by standard NGS. Confirmatory Sanger sequencing is often performed for novel single‑base variants depending on the laboratory’s quality control practices. Pre‑test genetic counseling is recommended to explain potential outcomes, insurance implications and the possibility of uncertain results.

Interpreting genetic results: applying ACMG criteria
Variant interpretation must follow accepted standards (e.g., ACMG/AMP guidelines). Combine multiple lines of evidence:
- Population frequency: pathogenic MODY variants are typically absent or extremely rare in population databases (gnomAD). A common variant is unlikely pathogenic.
- Segregation: cosegregation with diabetes in affected family members strongly supports pathogenicity. In our case report the variant segregated in multiple affected relatives, strengthening the finding.
- Computational prediction and in‑silico scores: tools (and more recently models like AlphaMissense) provide supportive evidence but are not definitive.
- Protein domain and functional relevance: variants in the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A are more likely to be pathogenic; functional data demonstrating impaired protein activity is high‑weight evidence.
- Phenotype concordance: match between the patient’s clinical phenotype (age of onset, glycosuria, preserved C‑peptide, dominant inheritance) and the expected features of HNF1A‑MODY supports causality.
Classify the variant as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign or benign, and include a clear rationale in the laboratory report. For novel variants, absence from databases does not equal pathogenicity; segregation and functional data are often decisive.

Practical management after results
- If a pathogenic/likely pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified: this confirms the diagnosis and should prompt reconsideration of therapy (for example, low‑dose sulfonylureas are a traditional first‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an effective and often safer alternative for those with declining β‑cell reserve or hypoglycaemia risk; insulin may be withdrawn if glycaemia can be controlled). Avoid empirical therapeutic changes based solely on a VUS.
- If the result is a VUS: do not base major treatment changes on the variant alone. Pursue family segregation testing and, when feasible, functional studies or periodic re‑analysis as databases grow.
- If no pathogenic variant is found: continue to manage based on phenotype, consider broader genetic testing if suspicion remains high, and re‑evaluate over time.

Cascade testing and family involvement
Once a P/LP variant is identified, offer cascade genetic testing to first‑degree relatives and other at‑risk family members. Testing affected relatives provides segregation data that can strengthen the variant classification; testing unaffected relatives can determine who needs surveillance and who can avoid unnecessary treatment. Genetic counseling is essential to discuss implications for prognosis, therapy (e.g., potential sulfonylurea sensitivity and SGLT2 inhibitor cautions), reproductive planning, and psychosocial issues. For minors, consider predictive testing when results would alter medical management during childhood or adolescence; otherwise many centers defer testing until the individual can consent.

Pitfalls and limitations to be aware of
- BMI, acanthosis and metabolic syndrome features are imperfect discriminators. Rising obesity prevalence blunts the distinction between MODY and young‑onset type 2 diabetes, so do not exclude MODY based solely on higher BMI.
- Autoantibody positivity does not absolutely exclude monogenic diabetes—rare overlap exists—but positive antibodies increase the likelihood of autoimmune diabetes and should lower the threshold for pursuing serial C‑peptide testing and type 1 management pathways.
- Clinical prediction tools (e.g., Exeter calculator) can aid decision‑making but may overestimate MODY probability in non‑European populations; use them alongside clinical judgment.
- NGS panels can miss certain variant types unless copy‑number analysis is included. Laboratories vary in coverage and interpretation frameworks; use accredited labs and request explicit statements on CNV detection.
- Variant reclassification over time is common. A VUS today may be reclassified as P/LP or benign; establish a mechanism to re‑contact patients and relatives when interpretations change.

Implementation in the presented case
Applying this algorithm to the patient described: routine labs showed fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L and HbA1c 10.2%; urinalysis documented glycosuria; GADA and IA‑2 were negative; stimulated C‑peptide was 0.83 nmol/L—supporting preserved β‑cell function. A strong maternal inheritance pattern increased pretest probability. These features met several indications for targeted NGS panel testing, which identified a novel HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S) variant located in the DNA‑binding domain. The laboratory applied ACMG criteria, integrating rarity in population databases, domain location, computational evidence and segregation in the family to classify the variant as pathogenic/likely pathogenic. The genetic diagnosis enabled precision therapy (transition off insulin to GLP‑1 receptor agonist and metformin, with later maintenance on oral therapy) and initiation of cascade testing and counseling for family members.

Summary algorithm (practical steps)
1. Initial evaluation: fasting glucose, HbA1c, BMP, lipids, urinalysis. Look for glycosuria and absence of DKA.
2. Exclude autoimmune diabetes: GAD65, IA‑2, ZnT8 antibodies.
3. Assess endogenous insulin: fasting and stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon or mixed‑meal); stimulated >0.6 nmol/L suggests preserved secretion.
4. Evaluate glycaemic pattern: OGTT or postprandial glucose to document exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia.
5. Consider supportive biomarkers (hsCRP) and family history; use prediction tools with caution.
6. If clinical suspicion is high, order a targeted MODY NGS panel with CNV analysis and pre‑test counseling.
7. Interpret variants using ACMG criteria; if P/LP, confirm diagnosis, adapt therapy accordingly and offer cascade testing; if VUS, pursue segregation studies and avoid major therapy changes based solely on the variant.
8. Provide genetic counseling, document findings in the medical record, arrange family testing and establish long‑term surveillance for complications.

Following this stepwise workup helps avoid misclassification of monogenic diabetes, enables precision therapy, and facilitates appropriate family counseling and cascade testing—improving outcomes for patients with HNF1A‑MODY and their relatives.

## Management and Prognosis of HNF1A‑MODY

Therapeutic decisions in HNF1A‑MODY are guided by two central principles: the primary defect is progressive β‑cell dysfunction (not insulin resistance), and affected patients typically retain greater sensitivity to insulin secretagogues than people with type 2 diabetes. Recognizing the diagnosis therefore changes both the choice of agents and the pace at which they are escalated. The case just presented illustrates these principles: the patient initially required insulin because of very high glucose and an HbA1c of 10.2%, but after genetic confirmation and demonstration of residual C‑peptide he was safely transitioned off insulin to incretin‑based therapy and metformin with excellent long‑term control. The paragraphs below outline evidence‑based pharmacologic and non‑pharmacologic approaches, practical strategies for transitions off insulin, surveillance for complications, and special‑population considerations.

Initial management and when to use insulin
Patients who present with severe hyperglycemia, metabolic decompensation or catabolic symptoms (weight loss, dehydration, ketonuria/ketosis) require prompt glucose lowering, and insulin is the appropriate initial therapy. Insulin stabilizes the patient and protects against acute complications while other assessments (C‑peptide, autoantibodies, genetic testing) are completed. Once hyperglycaemia is controlled and a diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY is made (or strongly suspected in the context of negative autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide), many patients can be transitioned from insulin to oral agents or GLP‑1 receptor agonists; this should be done gradually with close glucose monitoring. In practice, ensure β‑cell reserve (for example a stimulated C‑peptide in the range typically used in clinical practice—values well above assay‑specific thresholds that indicate endogenous secretion) and clinical stability before attempting withdrawal of insulin, and plan for frequent self‑monitoring during the transition.

First‑line oral agent: sulfonylureas
Classic teaching and multiple case series support low‑dose sulfonylureas as first‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY. Patients show pronounced sensitivity to sulfonylureas because the primary defect is in insulin secretion; small doses can produce substantial reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose. Typical clinical practice is to start with a low dose (eg, the lower end of commonly used doses for glimepiride, gliclazide, or glipizide) and titrate carefully to glycaemic response while monitoring for hypoglycaemia. Over time progressive β‑cell decline may necessitate dose escalation, and higher doses increase the risk of hypoglycaemia; some patients who were initially controlled on sulfonylureas will ultimately require alternative agents or insulin. Sulfonylureas should be used with particular caution in those who have irregular meals, renal impairment, or other hypoglycaemia risk factors.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists and DPP‑4 inhibitors
Incretin‑based therapies are attractive options in HNF1A‑MODY. Small randomized and crossover studies have shown that GLP‑1 receptor agonists (eg, liraglutide) can lower glucose to an extent similar to sulfonylureas while producing less hypoglycaemia and often promoting weight loss. In practice a GLP‑1 receptor agonist is a reasonable alternative when sulfonylureas are poorly tolerated, when hypoglycaemia risk is a concern, or when weight loss is desirable. The case patient responded well to weekly dulaglutide together with metformin, demonstrating the potential for GLP‑1 agents to maintain good control while avoiding insulin and hypoglycaemia. DPP‑4 inhibitors produce more modest HbA1c reductions than GLP‑1 RAs, but they have a favorable safety profile and can be useful as add‑on maintenance therapy or when injectable therapy is not acceptable.

Metformin and agents addressing insulin resistance
Metformin does not correct the primary β‑cell defect in HNF1A‑MODY but is reasonable adjunctive therapy in patients who are overweight, have features of insulin resistance, or require additional glycaemic lowering. It is commonly used in combination with a sulfonylurea or incretin‑based agent. Other agents commonly used in type 2 diabetes (thiazolidinediones, insulin) may be used when indicated by comorbidity or progressive β‑cell failure, but choice should reflect the predominant pathophysiology (secretory failure rather than insulin resistance).

SGLT2 inhibitors: caution advised
Because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in renal proximal tubules, patients with HNF1A‑MODY often have a lower renal threshold for glucose and baseline glycosuria. The addition of an SGLT2 inhibitor can therefore produce pronounced glucosuria and volume losses. Case reports and series have raised concerns about severe dehydration and ketoacidosis in HNF1A‑MODY patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors. For these reasons SGLT2 inhibitors should be used with caution, if at all, in HNF1A‑MODY and only after careful risk–benefit discussion and monitoring; they should be avoided in patients with tendency to volume depletion or who are at risk for ketoacidosis. The safer initial strategy is to use established therapies (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 RAs, metformin) and reserve SGLT2 inhibitors for select circumstances with specialist input.

Practical approach to transitioning off insulin
A practical stepwise approach is helpful: 1) stabilize glucose on insulin and treat any intercurrent illness; 2) perform diagnostic testing (autoantibodies, stimulated C‑peptide) and obtain genetic confirmation when feasible; 3) if C‑peptide indicates residual insulin secretion and autoantibodies are negative, lower basal insulin progressively while initiating a sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA (start low and titrate); 4) provide close glucose monitoring (home glucose checks several times daily) and adjust therapy based on fasting and postprandial readings; 5) stop insulin when stable glycaemia on oral/injectable non‑insulin therapy is demonstrated. Communication with the patient about hypoglycaemia signs and stopping insulin is essential. In the case described, the team discontinued insulin after genetic confirmation and maintained excellent control on dulaglutide plus metformin; this pathway exemplifies the value of targeted therapy once the molecular diagnosis is known.

Lifestyle and non‑pharmacologic management
As with all diabetes types, lifestyle remains foundational. Individualized medical nutrition therapy targeted at weight optimization and carbohydrate distribution, regular physical activity to improve insulin sensitivity and cardiovascular health, and avoidance of smoking are all part of long‑term management. Because many HNF1A‑MODY patients do not have marked insulin resistance, lifestyle goals should be tailored to the individual’s BMI and cardiometabolic risk rather than universally assuming the metabolic syndrome phenotype.

Monitoring and screening for complications
Microvascular and macrovascular risk in HNF1A‑MODY relates predominantly to the degree and duration of hyperglycaemia, so the usual diabetes screening and risk factor management apply. Recommended elements include:

- Glycaemic monitoring: target HbA1c should be individualized (commonly <7.0% for many nonpregnant adults); measure every 3 months until stable, then every 6 months. Intensify monitoring around medication changes or transitions off insulin.
- Retinopathy: ophthalmologic evaluation at diagnosis (if diabetes duration is unclear) or within the first year and then at least annually or as recommended based on findings.
- Nephropathy: urine albumin‑to‑creatinine ratio annually and serum creatinine/eGFR at least annually; aggressive blood‑pressure control if albuminuria is present.
- Neuropathy: annual foot exam, assessment of peripheral neuropathy with monofilament/vibration testing, and screening for autonomic neuropathy (orthostatic symptoms, gastroparesis, erectile dysfunction). The case highlights the importance of autonomic assessment — severely reduced sudomotor function was detected and warrants ongoing surveillance and symptom management.
- Cardiovascular risk: manage lipids, blood pressure and smoking cessation per standard guidelines; many HNF1A patients have typical cardiovascular risk determined by age and other factors rather than a unique genetic cardiac risk.
- Other surveillance: because HNF1A is expressed outside the pancreas (eg, liver), clinicians should be alert to reports in the literature linking HNF1A variants to hepatic adenomas in rare settings; routine screening is not indicated but unexplained hepatic abnormalities merit further evaluation.

Special situations

Pregnancy and preconception
Pregnancy requires special consideration. Tight glycaemic control before and during pregnancy is critical for fetal outcomes. Insulin is the preferred agent in pregnancy because it does not cross the placenta in clinically relevant amounts and can be titrated to achieve tight control; sulfonylureas cross the placenta and are generally avoided in pregnancy (some agents have been used in certain regions but are not first‑line). GLP‑1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors are contraindicated or not recommended in pregnancy. Women with HNF1A‑MODY should receive preconception counseling, early pregnancy care with frequent glucose measurement, and prompt transition to insulin if needed. Genetic counseling regarding fetal inheritance (50% chance if one parent is affected) should be offered.

Children and adolescents
In pediatric patients with confirmed HNF1A‑MODY, low‑dose sulfonylureas are effective but require careful dosing and hypoglycaemia education. Pediatric endocrinology involvement is valuable for dosing, monitoring growth and psychosocial support. Transition to adult care should include reinforcement of self‑management skills.

Family screening and genetic counseling
HNF1A‑MODY is autosomal dominant; each first‑degree relative has a 50% chance of carrying the pathogenic variant. Once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is identified, targeted cascade testing of relatives is recommended so that therapy can be individualized early and unnecessary insulin therapy avoided. Family members with the variant should receive glucose screening and counseling about future risk. Genetic counseling should address inheritance, reproductive options, and implications for life and health insurance where applicable.

Prognosis
Overall prognosis in HNF1A‑MODY is favorable when the diagnosis is made early and treatment is tailored. Long‑term outcomes are closely linked to glycaemic control: appropriate therapy can prevent or delay microvascular complications. Many individuals maintain good control for years on sulfonylureas or incretin‑based therapy; others experience progressive β‑cell failure over decades and require combination therapy or insulin. Cardiovascular risk is determined by usual risk factors, so aggressive control of blood pressure and lipids remains important. Early recognition and precision treatment — as in this case, where genetic diagnosis allowed insulin withdrawal and maintenance of HbA1c below 6.5% — can substantially alter the clinical course and quality of life.

In summary, management of HNF1A‑MODY combines an understanding of a primary secretory defect with careful selection of therapies that maximize glucose control while minimizing hypoglycaemia and adverse effects. Low‑dose sulfonylureas remain the historical first line, but GLP‑1 receptor agonists are effective and often better tolerated; metformin and DPP‑4 inhibitors have adjunctive roles, and SGLT2 inhibitors should generally be used with caution. Regular complication surveillance, family cascade testing and individualized care in special situations (pregnancy, pediatric patients) are essential components of optimal long‑term outcomes.

## Precision Medicine, Counseling, and Clinical Pearls

This patient’s course illustrates how a single genetic result can reframe diagnosis, treatment and family care. HNF1A-MODY is not an academic label: it is a molecular diagnosis that changes the expected natural history of disease, opens specific therapeutic options, identifies people at 50% risk in first‑degree relatives, and directs reproductive counselling. Thinking of diabetes as monogenic when onset is young, antibodies are negative and insulin secretion is relatively preserved transforms management from “type 1 versus type 2” heuristics into precision medicine.

Therapeutic implications are among the most immediate clinical consequences of a confirmed HNF1A pathogenic variant. Historically, low‑dose sulfonylureas have been the most effective oral agents for many patients with HNF1A‑MODY because they directly increase insulin release from the remaining β‑cell mass. In practice, sulfonylureas frequently allow successful withdrawal of insulin if endogenous secretion is adequate. More recently, incretin‑based therapies (GLP‑1 receptor agonists and, to a lesser extent, DPP‑4 inhibitors) have emerged as useful alternatives. GLP‑1 receptor agonists combine glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, postprandial glucose control and weight benefits while carrying a lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas—features attractive in HNF1A patients who can be very sensitive to insulin secretagogues. The case demonstrates this principle: after genetic confirmation, insulin was de‑escalated and a GLP‑1 receptor agonist plus metformin achieved durable glycaemic control with reduced hypoglycaemia risk. Conversely, agents that further increase glycosuria (SGLT2 inhibitors) should be used cautiously because HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression and baseline glycosuria is common; the theoretical risk of dehydration and euglycaemic ketoacidosis may be higher in some patients.

Once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified, cascade testing of at‑risk relatives should be offered. Cascade testing is high‑value medicine: a single proband’s result allows targeted, less expensive testing of relatives who can then be spared diagnostic odysseys, unnecessary insulin therapy, or delayed appropriate oral therapy. Pre‑test and post‑test genetic counselling are essential to discuss inheritance (autosomal dominant, ~50% transmission), age‑dependent penetrance and the options for predictive testing in adults. Testing children raises ethical questions: testing asymptomatic minors is generally deferred unless results will change management during childhood, but in some families early diagnosis may allow surveillance and early lifestyle interventions. Reproductive counselling should include the 50% transmission risk, discussion of prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing options, and the potential implications for pregnancy management.

Accurate documentation of the variant is a practical necessity. The clinic note and problem list should record the exact nucleotide change, protein change, exon and affected domain (for example, c.809A>G, p.N270S, exon 4, DNA‑binding domain), the laboratory that performed testing, and the ACMG classification (pathogenic/likely pathogenic/variant of uncertain significance). Include the evidence used to support the classification (segregation, population frequency, in silico predictions, functional data if available). Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) must be managed as indeterminate: do not use a VUS to change therapy or recommend cascade predictive testing. Because public databases and functional data evolve, plan regular re‑evaluation of VUS (for example annually or if new family data emerges) and notify families of reclassification; many clinical laboratories will re‑note reclassifications, but clinicians should also maintain an active follow‑up process.

Expect variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance within affected families. Even with the same pathogenic HNF1A variant, age at onset, severity of hyperglycaemia and coexisting metabolic features differ among relatives. Rising population obesity means that an HNF1A pathogenic variant can coexist with obesity and insulin resistance, which blurs phenotypic distinctions from type 2 diabetes. Do not exclude MODY simply because BMI is above the historical “lean” picture. Conversely, some carriers may have minimal hyperglycaemia for years; surveillance rather than immediate treatment may be appropriate in such cases.

A pragmatic approach to separating type 1 diabetes from MODY in clinic will reduce missed diagnoses. Measure islet autoantibodies (GAD65, IA‑2, ZnT8) at presentation; their presence makes autoimmune type 1 diabetes far more likely. Assess endogenous insulin secretion with either fasting or stimulated C‑peptide testing (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal tolerance test). In practice, persistence of detectable C‑peptide and negative autoantibodies beyond the initial presentation—and especially beyond 3–5 years after diagnosis—should prompt reconsideration for MODY testing. (By contrast, most classic type 1 patients become C‑peptide‑negative within several years.) Interpret C‑peptide with the assay units used locally; in many centers a stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (≈1.8 ng/mL) is considered evidence of residual β‑cell function, but local thresholds vary.

There are several high‑yield clinical clues that increase the pre‑test probability of HNF1A‑MODY. Disproportionate glycosuria—glycosuria at moderate plasma glucose levels—reflects a reduced renal glucose threshold related to lower SGLT2 expression under HNF1A control and should raise suspicion. Low high‑sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) relative to typical type 2 diabetes has been reported in HNF1A cases and can be a supportive (not definitive) biomarker. A clear autosomal dominant pedigree with several generations of early‑onset diabetes, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia on OGTT, and absence of features of severe insulin resistance (acanthosis, extreme dyslipidaemia) are further pointers. Use these features to select patients most likely to benefit from genetic testing rather than testing unselected populations.

From a systems and cost‑effectiveness perspective, targeted testing strategies yield the most value. For patients with classic phenotypes you may choose a single‑gene test (for example, GCK if mild fasting hyperglycaemia is found, HNF1A if prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia with family history), which can be less expensive and faster. When the phenotype is less specific—or when multiple monogenic forms are plausible—multi‑gene NGS panels are efficient because they reduce the need for serial testing and have become affordable. Preauthorization and knowledgeable genetic counselling improve reimbursement success. Cascade testing of relatives is generally inexpensive compared with full panel testing and should be offered whenever a pathogenic variant is confirmed in a proband.

Operational implementation improves case detection. Embed decision support in young‑onset diabetes pathways (for example, automatic flags in the electronic medical record for diabetes diagnosed before age 35, negative autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide) and establish referral protocols with regional genetic services. Create a standard “MODY letter” for families that includes the molecular result, clinical implications, recommended relatives for testing and contact details for genetic counselling; this facilitates cascade testing and avoids loss to follow‑up. Partnering with clinical genetics teams streamlines informed consent, testing logistics and family counselling, and ensures appropriate interpretation of VUS and reclassification workflows.

In short, the practical clinical pearls are: treat the genetic diagnosis as actionable (it often permits insulin withdrawal or a switch to sulfonylurea or incretin‑based therapy); offer cascade testing to identify at‑risk relatives and direct surveillance; document variant details and classification and re‑assess VUS over time; do not be reassured by obesity alone—phenotypic overlap is common; and use a combination of negative autoantibodies and persistent C‑peptide (especially beyond 3–5 years) to trigger MODY evaluation. Embedding simple screening prompts and close collaboration with genetic services will increase diagnostic yield and convert genetic knowledge into better, safer, and more personalized care for patients and their families.
