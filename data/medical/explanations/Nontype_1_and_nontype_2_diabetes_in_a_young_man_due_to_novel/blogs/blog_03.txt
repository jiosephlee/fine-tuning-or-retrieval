Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# C-Peptide and Antibodies Won’t Save You: Diagnostic Pitfalls in MODY

What’s interesting here? A young man with HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, frank glycosuria, negative GAD/IA‑2, a preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L), and a strong family history of early‑onset diabetes. The working diagnosis initially swung toward type 2 diabetes (and he was started on insulin), but genetic testing found a novel HNF1A pathogenic variant. Within months his insulin was stopped and GLP‑1 agonist + metformin controlled his glucose to HbA1c 6.0%.

This case shows a common clinic trap: relying on single snapshots of c‑peptide and autoimmune markers to "rule in" type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Those tests tilt the odds — but they don’t close the case.

## Why c‑peptide and antibodies can mislead

- C‑peptide is a dynamic marker of endogenous insulin secretion, but it’s highly influenced by timing, recent glycaemia, insulin therapy, and the test used.
  - A single fasting C‑peptide may be low in early type 1, or preserved in early stages. Stimulated tests (glucagon or mixed‑meal) are better, but thresholds aren’t absolute. In this case a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (>0.60 nmol/L is often considered "preserved") suggested residual β‑cell function — supportive of non‑type 1 diabetes but not definitive.
  - Acute hyperglycaemia can mask true β‑cell capacity; conversely, transient honeymoon periods in type 1 can give preserved c‑peptide.
  - Be careful with units and cutoffs (nmol/L vs ng/mL) and with lab reference ranges when interpreting results.

- Autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8) are highly specific for autoimmune type 1 diabetes, but not perfectly sensitive or exclusive.
  - Most MODY patients are antibody‑negative, but there are reports of autoantibody positivity in a minority of genetically proven MODY patients. Autoantibodies can be transient, low titre, or even incidental.
  - Negative antibodies lower the probability of type 1 diabetes, but do not confirm MODY.

- Clinical context matters: age at onset, family history, phenotype (absence of ketoacidosis, lack of insulin resistance stigmata), and biochemical pattern (marked postprandial hyperglycaemia / abnormal OGTT, glycosuria despite not profound hyperglycaemia) are critical clues.
  - HNF1A‑MODY classically has prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia, progressive β‑cell dysfunction, and increased renal glycosuria due to reduced SGLT2 expression — exactly what we saw with this patient’s OGTT (2‑hr 19.8 mmol/L) and glycosuria.

## How to integrate tests in practice

Think probabilistically, not binary. Use c‑peptide and antibodies to change pre‑test probability — then decide if genetic testing is warranted.

- If young onset (<25–35 years), strong autosomal dominant family history of early diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide — think MODY and consider genetic testing even if BMI is not low.
- If antibodies are positive and titres high → type 1 is likely. If antibody negative but c‑peptide very low on stimulation → type 1 remains possible (especially early), and serial testing may help.
- If obesity, insulin resistance features, very high triglycerides/low HDL, and progressive insulin requirement → type 2 more likely, but remember increasing obesity blurs the distinction.
- Use OGTT or postprandial testing: HNF1A‑MODY often shows disproportionate postprandial glucose excursions and glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels.

## Practical tips (timing, assays, and follow‑up)

- Prefer stimulated over fasting c‑peptide (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal). Document the assay, units, reference ranges, and the timing relative to insulin doses.
- Repeat c‑peptide if the clinical picture is ambiguous: early type 1 can show preserved c‑peptide initially; progressive decline supports autoimmune destruction.
- Don’t overinterpret a single negative GAD/IA‑2: if clinical suspicion of autoimmune disease is high, repeat or add ZnT8 antibodies.
- Use probability tools (for example the Exeter MODY calculator) as an aid — but remember population differences (tools derived from European cohorts may misestimate risk in other ethnic groups).
- When MODY is suspected, prioritize genetic testing (targeted NGS panels) — it’s definitive and can change therapy (sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 rather than lifelong insulin in many HNF1A cases).

## Treatment implications: why it matters

Genetic confirmation changes therapy and prognosis. HNF1A‑MODY patients often respond to low‑dose sulfonylureas; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive alternative as β‑cell function declines (lower hypoglycaemia risk). SGLT‑2 inhibitors deserve caution because intrinsic SGLT2 downregulation in HNF1A‑MODY can amplify glycosuria and dehydration risk. In this case, genetic diagnosis allowed stopping insulin and maintaining control with GLP‑1 agonist + metformin.

## Clinical pearls

- C‑peptide and antibodies are probabilistic tools — they shift odds but don’t provide absolute answers.
- Stimulated c‑peptide > common cutoffs suggests preserved secretion but does not exclude early type 1 or MODY.
- Autoantibody negativity increases the likelihood of non‑autoimmune diabetes but is not pathognomonic for MODY; occasional MODY patients can have positive antibodies.
- Look for disproportionate postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, and strong autosomal dominant family history — these increase pre‑test probability for HNF1A‑MODY.
- When suspicion is moderate to high, order genetic testing; it’s the test that actually changes management.

Take‑home messages

- Don’t let a single c‑peptide or autoantibody result dictate the diagnosis.
- Integrate clinical phenotype, OGTT/postprandial glucose, family history, and dynamic c‑peptide testing.
- If clinical suspicion for MODY is real, proceed to genetic testing — it’s precision medicine that alters therapy and prognosis.
