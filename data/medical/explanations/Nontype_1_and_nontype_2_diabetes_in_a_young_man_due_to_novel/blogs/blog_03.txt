Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# Treating HNF1A‑MODY in 2026: Sulfonylurea Tradition vs GLP‑1 Pragmatism

What makes this case worth reading? A young man with severe hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%) and glycosuria, negative diabetes autoantibodies, a strong family history of early‑onset diabetes — and a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant that changed management. He started on insulin for rapid control but, after genetic confirmation and evidence of some residual β‑cell function, was successfully de‑intensified to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) plus metformin and ultimately maintained on metformin + a DPP‑4 inhibitor with HbA1c down to 6.0%. This case captures the modern tension in HNF1A‑MODY care: stick with the historical “use low‑dose sulfonylurea” teaching — or pragmatically prefer GLP‑1 RAs for durability, weight and hypoglycaemia profile?

Below I walk through the physiology that matters, the evidence and the practical algorithm I use in clinic when genetics turns the light on.

## Why HNF1A changes everything (brief refresher)
HNF1A mutations produce progressive β‑cell dysfunction rather than autoimmune destruction. Clinically you’ll often see:
- Young onset, autosomal dominant family history
- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria (reduced SGLT2 expression in the kidney)
- Negative islet autoantibodies and measurable C‑peptide for longer than classic type 1

That biology explains treatment responsiveness: sulfonylureas often produce dramatic early responses because the β‑cell can be stimulated, but β‑cell function is progressive and patients may later fail oral secretagogues.

## The therapeutic crossroads: SU tradition vs GLP‑1 pragmatism
Classic teaching: HNF1A‑MODY → low‑dose sulfonylurea. Why? Potent insulin secretagogue effect, oral, inexpensive, and historically driven by case series showing marked glucose reductions.

But in 2026 we have additional considerations:
- Hypoglycaemia risk and weight gain with sulfonylureas — real downsides in young patients who are otherwise healthy.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists (injectable or oral) stimulate glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, reduce appetite/weight and carry a lower hypoglycaemia risk. Small randomized crossover data in HNF1A patients showed liraglutide and glimepiride achieved similar glycaemic control, but GLP‑1 therapy had less hypoglycaemia.
- GLP‑1 RAs have class cardiovascular and renal benefits in selected populations (e.g., dulaglutide and liraglutide have proven CV benefit in at‑risk cohorts). For a young, low‑CV‑risk person this is less decisive, but it matters for older patients or those with established disease.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors are weight‑neutral, oral and lower risk for hypoglycaemia — but generally less potent than GLP‑1 RAs and limited trial data in HNF1A‑MODY.
- Metformin still has a role for insulin resistance components and as backbone therapy unless contraindicated.

So choice isn’t binary: it’s individualized.

## How this played out in the case
- Presentation: HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, OGTT 2‑hr 19.8 mmol/L, symptomatic but no DKA. Autoantibodies negative. Stimulated C‑peptide after glucagon = 0.83 nmol/L (supporting residual insulin secretion).
- Practical emergency step: start insulin (degludec) + metformin + saxagliptin to quickly bring hyperglycaemia under control.
- After genetic confirmation (novel pathogenic HNF1A variant) and demonstration of residual β‑cell function, clinicians discontinued insulin and started dulaglutide + metformin. HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months and regimen was simplified to metformin + saxagliptin for maintenance.

This is a model of safe de‑intensification: use insulin to manage acute severe hyperglycaemia, then step down guided by genotype and physiology.

## Practical decision framework (how I choose therapy for HNF1A‑MODY)

1. Initial assessment
   - Confirm family history, age at onset, negative autoantibodies.
   - Measure stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon or mixed‑meal) to assess β‑cell reserve.
   - Consider urgent insulin if severe hyperglycaemia, catabolism or symptoms (as in this case).

2. Genetics
   - Arrange targeted MODY genetic testing early when suspicion is high. A positive HNF1A result changes long‑term strategy.

3. Pick therapy after genetic confirmation and stabilization
   - If stimulated C‑peptide is low (clear insulin deficiency): continue insulin.
   - If C‑peptide shows meaningful reserve (case used >0.6 nmol/L as supportive): consider switching off long‑term insulin.

4. Drug selection considerations
   - Sulfonylurea (low dose) — good if:
     - Mild–moderate hyperglycaemia (A1c modest)
     - Cost/simplicity paramount
     - Patient accepts hypoglycaemia risk and potential weight gain
     - No strong contraindications (e.g., high-risk occupation)
   - GLP‑1 receptor agonist — prefer when:
     - Hypoglycaemia avoidance important
     - Weight loss or weight neutrality desired
     - Patient prefers lower hypoglycaemia risk and is willing to use injectables (or oral semaglutide)
     - A higher upfront cost is acceptable or covered
   - DPP‑4 inhibitor — consider if:
     - Patient wants oral therapy and lower side‑effect profile
     - Glycaemic targets are modest and GLP‑1 RA not tolerated/acceptable
     - Note: less robust evidence for potency in HNF1A‑MODY
   - Metformin — keep as adjunct when insulin resistance features exist or per general diabetes care
   - SGLT2 inhibitors — use caution:
     - HNF1A mutation reduces renal SGLT2 expression and increases baseline glycosuria; adding an SGLT2 inhibitor may increase dehydration/DKA risk. Avoid unless compelling reason, with close monitoring.

5. Dosing and monitoring
   - Sulfonylureas: start low and titrate cautiously; counsel on hypoglycaemia recognition.
   - GLP‑1 RAs: choose agent based on access, tolerability and patient preference (daily vs weekly; oral vs injectable). Monitor GI side effects and HbA1c response.
   - Reassess β‑cell function and glycaemic control at 3 months; adjust therapy if progressive failure.

## Specific clinical pearls from this case
- Starting insulin for rapid control of severe hyperglycaemia is reasonable even when MODY is suspected; do not delay acute stabilization while awaiting genetic tests.
- Negative autoantibodies plus measurable stimulated C‑peptide should prompt MODY evaluation in young patients with family history.
- Genetic confirmation (even of a novel HNF1A variant) enables safe de‑intensification and more tailored long‑term therapy.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a pragmatic alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A‑MODY when hypoglycaemia risk, weight or preference are considerations — supported by small randomized crossover data showing similar glycaemic efficacy but less hypoglycaemia with GLP‑1 therapy.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY because of baseline reduced SGLT2 expression and potential for excessive glycosuria/dehydration and DKA.

## Take‑home messages
- Genetic testing matters: confirming HNF1A‑MODY can and will change therapy.
- In severe hyperglycaemia, stabilize with insulin if needed; then reassess and de‑intensify when genotype and β‑cell reserve allow.
- Sulfonylureas remain an effective, low‑cost option for many HNF1A patients, but carry hypoglycaemia and weight penalties.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a compelling, pragmatic alternative — lower hypoglycaemia risk, weight benefit and good efficacy; consider as first‑line after insulin de‑intensification when cost/route are acceptable.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors can be used as adjuncts for oral maintenance but are generally less potent than GLP‑1 RAs.
- Avoid reflexive use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A without careful thought.

This case exemplifies precision medicine in everyday endocrinology: genotype plus physiology (C‑peptide) yields individualized, safer, and often simpler care.
