# Novel Variant, Real Disease: How to Interpret HNF1A Results Beyond ClinVar

What grabbed me about this case was the familiar mismatch we see too often: a young adult with marked hyperglycaemia, negative autoantibodies, a strong multigenerational diabetes history — yet the genetics report came back with an HNF1A variant that wasn’t in ClinVar or VarSome. The temptation is to shrug and call it “variant of uncertain significance” or — worse — to downplay genetics because a database “doesn’t have it.” This case shows why that’s the wrong move: a newly reported HNF1A p.N270S (c.809A>G) change behaved exactly like HNF1A-MODY clinically, and careful application of ACMG reasoning plus phenotypic data made the variant clinically meaningful.

If you see a novel HNF1A variant, you don’t stop at “not in ClinVar.” You walk a diagnostic pathway: clinical phenotype → biochemical support → family segregation → variant-level evidence (location, conservation, in silico, functional data if available) → integrated ACMG classification. Below I’ll walk through how the team in this report made that assessment and how you can apply the same thinking at the bedside or in the clinic.

Why this case is instructive
- Classic red flags for MODY: young age at presentation, strong autosomal-dominant family history of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but insufficient C-peptide, glycosuria without DKA.
- Variant was novel (absent from ClinVar/VarSome) yet clustered with disease in the family and sat in the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A — a hotspot for pathogenic variants.
- Treatment changed meaningfully after diagnosis: insulin stopped, transitioned to GLP-1 RA + metformin with excellent glycaemic response (HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0% over months), illustrating clinical utility of genetic diagnosis.

Applying ACMG-style reasoning to a novel HNF1A variant — the logical steps

1) Start with the phenotype
Clinical phenotype is not background noise — it is evidence. HNF1A-MODY typically presents with:
- Young onset (often before 25–35), progressive β-cell dysfunction, strong autosomal dominant pattern.
- Glycosuria due to reduced SGLT2 expression (a useful clinical hint).
- Negative T1D autoantibodies and measurable C-peptide early on.
In this case the phenotype fits HNF1A-MODY strongly — treat this as supportive evidence when weighing variant pathogenicity.

2) Segregation in the family
Cosegregation is high-value evidence. The reported family had several first-degree/second-degree relatives with early-onset diabetes (mother, maternal grandmother, brother). Demonstrating that the variant is present in affected relatives (and absent in unaffected where available) upgrades confidence. In practice:
- Every informative meiosis where variant segregates with disease adds weight (ACMG’s PP1 tiered approach).
- Even limited segregation in a clear autosomal dominant pedigree is compelling for MODY genes with high penetrance like HNF1A.

3) Variant-level features: domain and residue
Not all variants are equal. HNF1A has functional domains: DNA-binding domain is a mutation hotspot. Key points:
- A novel missense in the DNA-binding domain (as here) carries more suspicion than one in a region poorly conserved or outside functional domains.
- Proximity to known pathogenic residues increases likelihood of a deleterious effect — adjacent or same functional motif matters.
So p.N270S being in the DNA-binding domain and near known pathogenic sites is moderate-to-strong evidence toward pathogenicity.

4) In silico and quantitative predictors
Use evolutionary conservation and missense impact tools intelligently:
- High scores from multiple models (AlphaMissense, CADD, REVEL etc.) add supporting evidence (ACMG PP3).
- No single prediction proves pathogenicity — use them as part of the ensemble.
This case reported a very high AlphaMissense score (0.995) — supportive but not standalone proof.

5) Population frequency matters
Check gnomAD and local population databases:
- A truly pathogenic HNF1A-MODY variant should not be present at appreciable frequency in the general population.
- Absence from population databases supports rarity (PM2 evidence).
In this report the variant was absent from ClinVar and public catalogs — that absence supports, rather than refutes, pathogenicity.

6) Functional data
When available, functional assays are gold-standard. Often they’re not available for a novel variant. In their absence:
- Domain, conservation, in silico, and segregation evidence must be integrated more heavily.
- If clinical response to targeted therapy (e.g., sulfonylurea/GLP-1 RA enabling insulin cessation) is observed, that is pragmatic supportive evidence of gene-level causality.

7) Integrate all evidence with ACMG framework
No single datapoint rules the day. ACMG-style classification is cumulative:
- PM2 (absent from controls)
- PM1 (located in a mutational hot spot/critical domain)
- PP1 (segregation with disease in multiple affected family members — strength depends on number of meioses)
- PP3 (multiple in silico predictors support deleterious effect)
- PP4 (patient’s phenotype highly specific for gene)
Combined, these can push a novel variant into likely pathogenic or pathogenic even if ClinVar is silent.

Clinical implications — why it matters
- Treatment: HNF1A-MODY patients often respond well to low-dose sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists and may not need long-term insulin. In this case, discontinuation of insulin and good control on GLP-1 RA + metformin was both diagnostic and therapeutic.
- Counseling and cascade testing: A likely pathogenic result triggers cascade testing of relatives — early identification changes monitoring and therapy and helps reproductive counselling.
- Surveillance and drug choices: Knowledge of HNF1A status affects decisions (e.g., caution with SGLT2 inhibitors given baseline glycosuria and theoretical DKA/dehydration risks in severe SGLT2 deficiency).

Practical tips for clinicians receiving a novel HNF1A report
- Don’t throw hands up at “novel” or “not in ClinVar” — ask for the raw variant annotation, domain location, conservation, in silico scores and any segregation data the lab has.
- Correlate tightly with phenotype: age at onset, family history, antibody status, C-peptide, glycosuria.
- If family members are available, arrange targeted testing for segregation — even one or two informative relatives can change classification.
- Talk to the genetics lab: many labs will reclassify variants if you provide segregation or functional data.
- When reporting back to patients, emphasize uncertainty levels and the plan: cascade testing, tailored therapy, and periodic re-evaluation as new evidence emerges.

Clinical pearls
- “Not in ClinVar” ≠ “benign.” Absence from databases is expected for novel pathogenic variants.
- Domain matters: missense changes in HNF1A’s DNA-binding domain deserve extra scrutiny.
- Phenotype-first reasoning is powerful — in suspected MODY, the clinical picture is evidence in variant classification.
- Early genetic diagnosis can change treatment (discontinue insulin, choose sulfonylurea/GLP-1 RA) and prompt cascade testing that benefits family members.

Take-home messages
- Evaluate novel HNF1A variants using an integrated approach: phenotype, segregation, domain/location, population frequency, in silico predictions, and (if available) functional data.
- Absence from ClinVar or population databases supports rarity and does not exclude pathogenicity.
- Even limited cosegregation plus supportive clinical features can be sufficient to call a variant likely pathogenic and change management.
- Arrange cascade testing and genetic counselling — a single HNF1A diagnosis can identify multiple affected family members and enable precision therapy.

This case is a neat reminder: precision medicine isn’t just about fancy sequencing — it’s about integrating genetic data with good clinical thinking. When you see a young patient with diabetes who doesn’t fit type 1 or type 2 neatly, push for the right testing and, when a novel variant shows up, do the work to interpret it — because the result can be dramatically actionable.
