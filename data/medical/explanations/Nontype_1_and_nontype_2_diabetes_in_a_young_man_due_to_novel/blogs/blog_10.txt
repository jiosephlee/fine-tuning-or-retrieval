Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# Early Neuropathy in MODY? Making Sense of Sudomotor Dysfunction at Diagnosis

What grabs your attention in this HNF1A-MODY case is not the excellent treatment response — that’s increasingly familiar — but the finding you wouldn’t expect in a newly diagnosed young adult: severely reduced hand sudomotor conductance on baseline testing. Small‑fiber and autonomic neuropathies are usually late complications of chronic hyperglycaemia. So how do we interpret severe sudomotor impairment at presentation in someone with a novel HNF1A variant?

Put simply: it’s real enough to merit investigation, but don’t rush to label this “MODY neuropathy” without thinking through alternatives, testing limitations, and practical implications for follow‑up.

Why this is surprising
- Autonomic/small‑fiber neuropathy typically occurs after years of diabetes exposure. Severe sudomotor dysfunction at diagnosis in a young adult suggests either (a) accelerated nerve injury from substantial hyperglycaemia before presentation, (b) a non‑glycaemic cause, or (c) a MODY‑specific effect — for which data are sparse.
- HNF1A-MODY is mainly a β‑cell transcription factor disorder; there are no well‑established mechanisms linking HNF1A mutations to primary small‑fiber degeneration. Published data on early autonomic involvement in MODY are limited and inconsistent.

What could explain the abnormal Sudoscan result?
- True small‑fiber/autonomic neuropathy from hyperglycaemia: This patient had very high glucose/HbA1c at presentation (HbA1c 10.2%). Acute and subacute hyperglycaemia can impair small‑fiber function, and some neuropathic changes may be apparent earlier than we classically expect.
- Pre‑existing neuropathy from another cause: hereditary small‑fiber neuropathies, toxic exposures, nutritional deficiencies, autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy, or prior infections can present in young adults and coexist with diabetes.
- Testing artefact or confounders:
  - Sudoscan and other electrochemical sudomotor tests are convenient but not diagnostic. Results can be influenced by skin condition, hydration status, recent smoking, medications, limb temperature and technical factors.
  - Glycosuria and dehydration can alter skin conductance measurements. The case noted glycosuria; volume depletion could have influenced results.
- Coincident autonomic dysfunction related to a different systemic process, though the case has no other clear red flags.

How to work this up sensibly
- Don’t assume this is irreversible diabetic neuropathy. Confirm and phenotype.
  1. Repeat testing after early glycaemic control: if the abnormality improves with normalization of glucose, that supports a metabolic effect.
  2. Formal autonomic testing: heart rate variability, Valsalva, tilt table testing and quantitative sudomotor axon reflex testing (QSART) provide complementary information. QSART is more specific for postganglionic sudomotor function.
  3. Small‑fiber structural testing if symptoms or objective dysfunction persist: skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve fibre density (IENFD) is the structural gold standard; corneal confocal microscopy is emerging.
  4. Screen for alternative causes: B12/folate, thyroid, HIV, serum protein electrophoresis, toxins/meds, and family history for hereditary neuropathies.
  5. Consider neurophysiology (nerve conduction studies) to exclude large‑fiber disease — keeping in mind NCS are insensitive for small‑fiber neuropathy.

Clinical implications and follow‑up
- Symptom screening: actively ask about orthostatic symptoms, GI dysmotility, erectile dysfunction, urinary dysfunction, sweating abnormalities and distal pain/paraesthesia. Many autonomic problems are under‑reported unless you ask.
- Foot care: even without large‑fiber disease, reduced sudomotor function can alter skin integrity and increase risk of fissures/infection. Advise daily foot inspection and prompt attention to skin changes.
- Frequency of microvascular screening: continue guideline‑directed retinopathy and albuminuria surveillance. Early neuropathic findings don’t mandate changing retinopathy screening intervals immediately, but they should heighten vigilance and reinforce tight glycaemic control.
- Treatment: optimize glycaemic control (this case responded dramatically to GLP‑1 agonist + metformin). Symptomatic therapies for neuropathic pain and autonomic symptoms as needed. Address dehydration and modifiable contributors.
- Family implications: because HNF1A‑MODY is autosomal dominant, relatives should be screened both for dysglycaemia and, if sudomotor/autonomic symptoms are present, for neuropathy workup. Genetic counselling remains central.

What we don’t know — and what to watch for
- There’s scant data linking HNF1A mutations directly to primary small‑fiber or autonomic neuropathy. Most literature focuses on pancreatic, hepatic and renal consequences; peripheral nerve involvement is not a well‑described phenotype of HNF1A‑MODY.
- Longitudinal follow‑up of this patient and his affected relatives could be informative: does sudomotor dysfunction improve with glycaemic control, remain stable, or progress despite good control? Publishing serial objective measures would advance understanding.

Clinical pearls / take‑home messages
- Severe sudomotor dysfunction at diabetes diagnosis is unexpected — confirm it before attributing it to MODY.
- Repeat sudomotor testing after glycaemic optimization; pursue QSART and formal autonomic testing if abnormalities persist or symptoms are present.
- Consider alternative causes of small‑fiber/autonomic neuropathy; don’t anchor on the genetic diagnosis alone.
- Continue routine microvascular surveillance and emphasize foot care and symptomatic management.
- Document and follow objective autonomic measures over time — they may distinguish transient metabolic effects from a persistent or genetic small‑fiber process.

Bottom line: abnormal sudomotor testing in a young person with HNF1A‑MODY is a red flag worth workup, not a definitive manifestation of MODY. Be methodical: confirm, phenotype, exclude other causes, optimize glucose, and follow longitudinally. That approach best serves the patient and builds the evidence base about whether early autonomic involvement is part of the MODY story or an unfortunate co‑incidence.
