Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# The Glycosuria Tell: Low Renal Glucose Threshold as a Clue to HNF1A‑MODY

What makes this case interesting? A young man with marked hyperglycaemia and lots of glycosuria, but no ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, a family history of early‑onset diabetes and a BMI that doesn’t scream insulin resistance. Genetic testing revealed a novel HNF1A mutation. The unusual prominence of glycosuria — sometimes out of proportion to symptoms — can be a subtle but powerful clue pointing away from type 1 or typical type 2 diabetes and toward HNF1A‑MODY.

## Quick physiology refresher: HNF1A, SGLT2 and the renal glucose threshold
- HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in liver, pancreas and kidney. Among its renal targets is the SGLT2 gene (SLC5A2), the major proximal tubule sodium–glucose cotransporter responsible for reabsorbing filtered glucose.
- Loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression. The clinical consequence: a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. Patients spill glucose into the urine at lower plasma glucose levels than people with intact SGLT2.
- Result: glycosuria can be prominent early in the course of disease even before severe symptomatic hyperglycaemia (and without the insulin deficiency and ketosis typical of type 1 diabetes).

## Why that matters clinically
- Glycosuria is often interpreted as simply a sign of hyperglycaemia, but when it’s marked relative to the clinical picture (eg, obvious urinary glucose at moderately high or fluctuating glucose values, dehydration without ketones), think HNF1A‑MODY.
- HNF1A‑MODY is a β‑cell disorder — progressive insulin secretory defect — not autoimmune β‑cell destruction. Expect:
  - Negative GAD/IA‑2/ZnT8 autoantibodies in most cases (but autoantibodies are not an absolute exclusion).
  - Preserved but insufficient C‑peptide early on.
  - Strong autosomal dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes (but family history can be misleading when T2D is common).
- The renal phenotype (glycosuria) can precede severe β‑cell failure and is mechanistically distinct from insulin resistance–related glycosuria.

## How to leverage the sign in practice
- Treat disproportionate glycosuria as a red flag prompting reconsideration of diagnosis:
  - Recheck autoantibodies and C‑peptide (basal and/or stimulated).
  - Take a careful three‑generation family history for early‑onset diabetes.
  - Consider OGTT to document exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia typical of HNF1A defects.
  - Use a MODY probability tool where available — but be cautious: calculators are derived from European cohorts and may under/over‑estimate risk in other ethnic groups.
  - If suspicion is high, order genetic testing (targeted MODY panel or NGS). A genetic diagnosis changes therapy and family counselling.
- Treatment implications:
  - HNF1A‑MODY classically responds well to low‑dose sulfonylureas early on. As β‑cell function declines, alternatives such as GLP‑1 receptor agonists are attractive (good efficacy, lower hypoglycaemia risk).
  - Insulin is avoidable in many cases after diagnosis if glycaemia is controlled with oral/GLP‑1 therapies.
  - Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY: theoretical and reported concerns include exaggerated glycosuria, volume depletion and increased DKA risk because residual SGLT2 activity is already low. Use only with careful consideration and monitoring.

## Practical pitfalls and pearls
- Don’t assume all young, non‑obese patients with glycosuria have type 1 diabetes — check autoantibodies and stimulated C‑peptide.
- A normal or only mildly reduced BMI doesn’t exclude HNF1A‑MODY. Rising population obesity blunts the “classic” phenotype.
- Glycosuria in someone with tight control could reflect a low renal threshold, not poor glycaemic control.
- The absence of a previously reported variant in ClinVar or other databases doesn’t exclude pathogenicity — look for segregation in the family, affected domain (eg, DNA‑binding), in silico support and clinical correlation per ACMG criteria.
- Early genetic diagnosis avoids unnecessary lifelong insulin, informs best drug choices, and prompts cascade testing of relatives.

## Take‑home messages
- Marked glycosuria out of proportion to the clinical picture is a useful clinical clue for HNF1A‑MODY because HNF1A mutations lower SGLT2 expression and the renal glucose threshold.
- Combine this clue with negative autoimmune markers, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, and a family history of early‑onset diabetes to trigger genetic testing.
- Genetic confirmation affects treatment: many HNF1A‑MODY patients can be managed with sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists rather than lifelong insulin; avoid reflexive use of SGLT2 inhibitors without careful risk–benefit consideration.
- Think kidney physiology as well as pancreatic β‑cell physiology when a diabetes presentation doesn’t fit the common types — the urine can tell you more than you expect.
