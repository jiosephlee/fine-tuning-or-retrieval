Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# Stimulated C‑peptide in the MODY workup: supportive, not definitive

What grabbed my attention in this case was how a single stimulated C‑peptide value (0.83 nmol/L) almost became the clincher for "non‑type 1" — yet the team didn’t stop there. A young adult with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria, a strong multigenerational early‑onset diabetes history, negative islet autoantibodies and a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide that was modestly preserved — all of which culminated in genetic confirmation of a novel HNF1A mutation and a rapid de‑escalation off insulin onto GLP‑1RA + metformin. That outcome illustrates the right way to use C‑peptide in practice: as a helpful piece of the puzzle, never the whole picture.

## Why we check C‑peptide at all
C‑peptide is the byproduct of endogenous insulin production. It’s useful because:
- It distinguishes endogenous insulin secretion from exogenous insulin (no cross‑reactivity with most insulin assays),
- It helps separate absolute insulinopenia (typical of long‑standing type 1 diabetes) from preserved β‑cell function (more consistent with T2D or some forms of MODY),
- It can guide treatment decisions (can we safely stop insulin? will sulfonylureas/GLP‑1RA work?).

But caveat: C‑peptide is an imperfect test. Many factors alter the result — assay, timing, glycaemic state, renal function and the stage of disease.

## Fasting vs stimulated C‑peptide — what each tells you
- Fasting (or random) C‑peptide: easy, outpatient, but less sensitive. A very low fasting C‑peptide supports severe insulin deficiency. A normal fasting value doesn’t exclude evolving type 1 diabetes.
- Stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon or mixed‑meal tolerance test [MMTT]): more sensitive. Glucagon stimulation is practical in clinic (measure baseline, give 1 mg IV glucagon, measure at 6 minutes as in this case). MMTT is more physiologic but takes longer (peaks ~90–120 min).
- Practical thresholds vary by lab/assay and population. In this report the authors use stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L as “preserved.” That’s consistent with many centres where stimulated values >0.6 nmol/L (≈1.8–2 ng/mL) argue against absolute insulin deficiency. The patient’s 0.83 nmol/L (≈2.5 ng/mL) suggested residual β‑cell function — supportive of non‑type 1 aetiology.

Important: always interpret against your lab’s reference ranges and units (nmol/L vs ng/mL). Conversion ~1 nmol/L ≈ 3.02 ng/mL.

## Assay and pre‑analytic caveats you must remember
- Different immunoassays are not interchangeable. Check the method and local cutoffs.
- Hyperglycaemia can transiently increase C‑peptide; conversely, prolonged hyperglycaemia and glucotoxicity can blunt β‑cell responsiveness.
- Renal dysfunction raises C‑peptide (reduced clearance) — can give false reassurance.
- Exogenous insulin doesn’t affect C‑peptide directly, but recent high‑dose insulin can suppress endogenous secretion.
- Cross‑reactivity: some assays detect proinsulin fragments differently — know your lab.
- Urine C‑peptide/creatinine ratio (UCPCR) is a convenient outpatient alternative; published thresholds exist but are lab‑dependent.

Bottom line: the number alone is only as good as the assay and the context.

## Timing matters — when a “normal” C‑peptide can be misleading
Early in autoimmune (type 1) diabetes there is often a “honeymoon” window with preserved C‑peptide. Testing too soon after symptom onset can give a false sense of security. Conversely, late testing in T1D will show very low C‑peptide.

So:
- If clinical suspicion for autoimmune diabetes is high (classic presentation, DKA, progressive insulin requirement, positive autoantibodies), repeat testing or short‑interval clinical reassessment is warranted.
- If the C‑peptide is borderline and the antibody panel is negative, combine with family history, BMI, age of onset, glycosuria and consider genetics rather than assuming non‑T1 disease.

The reported patient had negative GADA and IA‑2A, a family history of early‑onset diabetes and a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L — a constellation that made a MODY workup reasonable rather than diagnostic.

## False reassurance: don’t let one lab value rule your decision‑making
Some patients with MODY have positive autoantibodies; some with type 1 diabetes are antibody negative early on. A preserved C‑peptide in the first months after presentation does not exclude type 1 disease. Practice tip: if a patient is antibody‑positive or has a clinical course typical for T1D, manage as T1D until proven otherwise. If antibody‑negative with preserved C‑peptide and strong family history, pursue genetic testing.

## Who should you consider for MODY genetic testing?
Think MODY (and consider testing) when:
- Diabetes onset <25 years in a non‑obese individual OR strong autosomal dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes across generations,
- Negative islet autoantibodies,
- Preserved C‑peptide inconsistent with insulin‑dependence,
- Absence of features of insulin resistance despite hyperglycaemia (but be aware obesity blurs the line),
- Atypical course for T1D or T2D (e.g., early sulfonylurea sensitivity, glycosuria disproportionate to glucose levels).

Genetic testing is definitive — and directly impacts therapy (sulfonylureas for many HNF1A cases, avoidance of unnecessary lifelong insulin, risk counseling). In this case the combination of family pattern + negative antibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide prompted NGS and found a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant.

## The Exeter MODY calculator — useful but not universal
The Exeter MODY calculator is a validated clinical tool to estimate probability of MODY and can help triage testing. But know its limits:
- It was derived largely from White European cohorts and may miscalibrate in other ethnic groups (it can over‑ or under‑estimate probability).
- It’s best used as one input — alongside C‑peptide, antibody status, family history and clinical judgment.
- A “low” Exeter score shouldn’t close the door if compelling family history or other red flags are present — and a “high” score shouldn’t replace antibody and C‑peptide assessment.

In this case the Exeter tool returned ~62% probability — helpful but not the final arbiter.

## Putting it together — the practical approach
- Use fasting and/or stimulated C‑peptide as supportive data, not definitive proof.
- Always interpret against the assay and the clinical timeline. If in early disease, repeat testing or use serial clinical follow‑up.
- Combine antibody testing, C‑peptide, phenotype and family history to decide on genetic testing.
- When genetic testing is positive for HNF1A (or other MODY genes), change management accordingly — as in this case where insulin was stopped and GLP‑1RA/metformin proved effective.

## Clinical pearls / take‑home points
- Stimulated C‑peptide is more sensitive than fasting C‑peptide but remains supportive, not diagnostic, for MODY vs type 1 diabetes.
- Always check assay units and lab‑specific cutoffs; 1 nmol/L ≈ 3.02 ng/mL.
- Timing matters: preserved C‑peptide early after presentation does not exclude evolving autoimmune diabetes.
- Use C‑peptide + antibody testing + family history + phenotype to triage genetic testing — don’t rely on a single result or the Exeter calculator alone, particularly in non‑European populations.
- When clinical suspicion is high, pursue genetic testing — it changes therapy, prognosis and family counselling (as the HNF1A case nicely demonstrates).

If you’re going to remember just one thing from this case: a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L is strong supportive evidence of residual β‑cell function, but it alone should not be the last word. Use it to build a case — then confirm with genetics when the clinical picture fits.
