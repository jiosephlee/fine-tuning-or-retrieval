Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# Calling a Novel Variant Pathogenic: From ACMG Criteria to Clinical Action

Why this case grabbed my attention: a young man with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes—classic red flags for MODY—turned out to carry a previously unreported HNF1A missense change (c.809A>G, p.N270S). The team used molecular evidence, domain context, in‑silico prediction and family segregation to classify the variant as likely pathogenic and changed management: insulin was stopped, dulaglutide + metformin controlled glucose (HbA1c down to 6.0% at 6 months). This is a tidy example of how variant interpretation translates to precision medicine—but it also highlights the caution required when moving from sequence to bedside.

## The clinician’s problem: novel variant, real patient decisions
When you get a novel missense change, questions you must answer quickly include: is this change really disease causing, or a rare benign polymorphism? Will I change therapy (stop insulin? switch agents?) or order cascade testing for the family? The ACMG/AMP framework gives a structured way to weigh the evidence, but you need to apply it in the clinical context.

Here’s how the team approached p.N270S and how you might think about similar cases.

## Applying ACMG criteria to HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S) — the evidence stack
The case report describes multiple independent lines of evidence used to support a likely pathogenic call. The key elements:

- Domain context (PM1 — moderate): p.N270S lies in HNF1A’s DNA-binding domain, a region where pathogenic missense variants cluster. Variants in this domain are more likely to disrupt transcriptional activity and cause HNF1A-MODY.

- Absence from population databases (PM2 — moderate): the variant was not reported in ClinVar, gnomAD or other major population resources, arguing against a common benign allele.

- Segregation with disease (PP1 — supporting): the variant co-segregates with early-onset diabetes within the patient’s family (maternal grandmother, mother and brother had early-onset diabetes). While the report doesn’t give exhaustive genotype/phenotype counts, reported familial segregation supports pathogenicity.

- Computational evidence (PP3 — supporting): high deleteriousness scores (AlphaMissense 0.995 and conservation of the residue) provide additional supportive in‑silico evidence that the missense change affects protein function.

- Contextual/adjacent residue evidence (could contribute PM5_supporting): the variant affects a residue adjacent to other known pathogenic residues in the DNA-binding domain—this strengthens the plausibility even if it isn’t standalone functional proof.

Combining two moderate (PM1 + PM2) and two supporting (PP1 + PP3) criteria fits well within the ACMG pathway to a Likely Pathogenic classification (two moderate + ≥2 supporting). The team therefore designated p.N270S as likely pathogenic and acted on it.

A couple of practical notes about the mapping:
- Don’t invent strong/very strong claims (e.g., PS1/PS3) unless you have direct functional assay data or a previously established pathogenic amino acid change at the exact residue.
- Co‑segregation strength depends on family size and whether genotypes were obtained; a few affected relatives can be “supporting,” larger pedigrees can upgrade to moderate evidence.
- Computational predictions are supportive, not determinative; they help tip the balance but don’t substitute for experimental or robust segregation data.

## From classification to bedside: why it mattered here
This wasn’t just an academic exercise. The molecular call changed care in several concrete ways in this patient:

- Therapeutic de‑intensification: insulin was stopped after genetic confirmation and preserved (though reduced) β‑cell function—switching to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) + metformin led to durable glycaemic control (HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months). Sulfonylureas are historically first-line for HNF1A-MODY, but GLP‑1RA was chosen here as a safer alternative with good efficacy and less hypoglycaemia risk as β-cell function declines.

- Avoidance/precaution around SGLT2 inhibitors: HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression and HNF1A-MODY patients can have a lower renal threshold for glucose (glycosuria). There are case reports of dehydration and DKA in HNF1A-MODY with exaggerated glycosuria; that risk informed therapy choices.

- Cascade testing and counseling: a likely pathogenic call supports offering targeted testing to at-risk relatives for early diagnosis and tailored therapy. It also provides a rationale for genetic counseling about inheritance, testing options, and reproductive implications.

## Communicating uncertainty — the critical conversation
Lab calls are not gospel forever. You must be explicit with patients about what “likely pathogenic” means, and plan for re-evaluation:

- Explain the classification in plain language: this variant is very likely to be the cause of the family’s early-onset diabetes based on multiple lines of evidence, but it is not yet proven by direct functional experiments.

- Document the rationale in the chart and give the patient a copy of the genetic report. Note that reclassification is possible as new data arrive; commit to notifying them if that happens.

- Offer referral to genetics/genetic counseling before cascade testing. Discuss implications for insurability and psychosocial impact as appropriate for your healthcare setting.

- Submit / encourage variant submission to public databases (ClinVar) so the global knowledge base can grow. Many labs do this automatically, but clinicians can help by providing phenotype details.

## Pitfalls: don’t over-call, don’t under-call
- Over-calling (labeling VUS as pathogenic) risks inappropriate therapy changes and unnecessary anxiety or invasive screening in relatives. Never base irreversible decisions solely on a VUS.

- Under-calling (leaving a true pathogenic variant as a VUS) denies patients the benefits of precision therapy and cascade testing. If your clinical picture is strongly suggestive (negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, strong family history, compatible phenotype), push for a formal variant review or second opinion, and consider family segregation testing if available.

A pragmatic approach is to combine variant evidence with robust clinical data (autoantibody status, stimulated C‑peptide, OGTT, family history). In this case the phenotype and genetics aligned and the classification supported a reasonable therapeutic de‑escalation that improved outcomes.

## Practical tips for clinicians ordering/interpreting MODY testing
- Trigger testing when you see: diabetes onset <25–30 years, strong autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, atypical phenotypes for T1D/T2D, or unexplained glycosuria.

- Use an NGS MODY panel that includes HNF1A and other common genes; confirm findings with appropriate lab standards and ensure the lab follows ACMG criteria for reporting.

- If a novel missense change is reported, ask the lab for the ACMG criterion mapping they used and for any segregation/functional data they have. Ask if they will submit the variant to ClinVar.

- Prioritize genetic counseling before family cascade testing. A likely pathogenic or pathogenic HNF1A result usually justifies predictive testing in adult family members, and in adolescents if results would change management.

- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY patients due to glycosuria and potential dehydration/DKA risk.

## Closing thought
Variant interpretation is part genetics, part detective work, and part clinical judgment. The ACMG framework gives you the structure, but the clinical phenotype and family context are often what tip the scales from “maybe” to “likely.” In this case, disciplined application of ACMG criteria to a novel HNF1A missense change—combined with a congruent clinical picture—allowed a move from insulin dependence to GLP‑1RA + metformin and enabled meaningful cascade testing. That’s precision medicine done well: sequence-to-bedside with appropriate humility and safeguards.

## Clinical pearls / take-home points
- Suspect MODY when diabetes presents young with negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide and autosomal dominant family history.
- Use ACMG criteria systematically: domain/context (PM1) + absence from population (PM2) + segregation (PP1) + computational support (PP3) can combine to a Likely Pathogenic call for novel missense variants.
- “Likely pathogenic” is actionable but not absolute—communicate uncertainty, document rationale, offer genetic counseling and cascade testing.
- A genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY can justify de‑intensifying insulin and guiding therapy (sulfonylurea historically, GLP‑1RA a safe/effective option), and raises caution about SGLT2 inhibitors due to glycosuria-related risks.
- Submit and share variant data (ClinVar) and re-evaluate variants as new evidence accumulates.
