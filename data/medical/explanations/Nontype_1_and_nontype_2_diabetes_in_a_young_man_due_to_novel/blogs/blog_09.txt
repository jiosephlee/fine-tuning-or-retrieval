Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# MODY in the Metabolic Syndrome Era: Don’t Let BMI Blind You

What’s interesting here? A young man with biochemical features that could fit metabolic syndrome—elevated triglycerides, low HDL, symptoms of hyperglycaemia—turns out to have HNF1A-MODY from a novel HNF1A mutation. He lacked autoantibodies, had preserved (but insufficient) stimulated C‑peptide, glycosuria, a strong family history of early-onset diabetes, and a dramatic glycaemic response after precision therapy (GLP‑1 agonist + metformin → HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0% in 6 months). The case is a neat reminder that rising obesity and metabolic syndrome are eroding the clinical heuristics we’ve relied on to pick out monogenic diabetes.

Why this matters now
- Historically we used “young, lean, strong family history, no autoantibodies” as our mental trigger for MODY testing. That heuristic was reasonable when obesity was less prevalent. It’s failing now: MODY occurs in patients with higher BMI and overlapping metabolic features.
- Mislabeling HNF1A-MODY as type 1 or type 2 diabetes has real consequences: unnecessary lifelong insulin, missed opportunity for sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 therapy, incorrect family counselling, and missed cascade testing.
- Genetic diagnosis mattered here — it allowed stopping insulin and moving to GLP‑1 + metformin with sustained control. It also expanded the HNF1A variant catalog (novel p.N270S) and supports precision therapy.

How to think about MODY today (practical, bedside-focused)
- Age cut-offs are shifting from rigid “<25” to a probabilistic view: MODY can present into the 30s and beyond. Don’t dismiss it based on age alone.
- BMI alone is unreliable. As population BMI increases, the negative predictive value of “lean = MODY” drops.
- Look for “discordant” features: a young patient with marked hyperglycaemia but negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, strong multigenerational diabetes history, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia or glycosuria out of proportion to insulin resistance — these should raise the index of suspicion regardless of BMI.
- Use biochemical clues: marked postprandial glucose rise on OGTT, glycosuria with normal/near-normal glycaemic exposure (suggesting low renal threshold), preserved stimulated C‑peptide (>0.6 nmol/L supportive), and negative beta‑cell autoantibodies.
- Probability tools (eg, Exeter MODY calculator) help but were calibrated in mainly White European cohorts and can misestimate risk in other ethnic groups. Treat their output as one input, not a gatekeeper.

A pragmatic trigger strategy to keep sensitivity high without swamping genetics services
(aim: identify patients who should get targeted NGS panel testing)
Consider genetic testing when a patient with diabetes meets at least two of the following:
- Diabetes onset before age 35 (or strong family history of early-onset diabetes across ≥2 generations)
- Absent pancreatic autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8) and preserved stimulated C‑peptide (>0.6 nmol/L) beyond the honeymoon period
- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (eg, 2‑hour OGTT ≥11.1 mmol/L) or disproportionate glycosuria
- Low insulin requirement (eg, <0.5 U/kg/day) beyond the initial management window or ability to discontinue insulin after glycaemic stabilization
- Multigenerational autosomal dominant pattern of diabetes or early-onset diabetes in multiple first-degree relatives

Why these cut-points? They balance sensitivity (catch atypical presentations that include higher-BMI patients) and specificity (avoid mass-testing everyone with young-onset T2D). Local resource realities and pre-test counselling should guide how aggressively you test.

Therapeutic implications worth remembering
- HNF1A-MODY often responds well to low‑dose sulfonylureas; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive alternative as beta cell function declines (less hypoglycaemia, weight benefits).
- Precision diagnosis often permits stopping insulin safely if residual endogenous insulin exists.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY: reduced SGLT2 expression and baseline glycosuria could increase dehydration/DKA risk in some patients — weigh risks vs benefits carefully.
- Cascade testing: identifying an index case allows targeted family testing and earlier diagnosis/treatment for relatives.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t use BMI as a screening stop sign. A modestly elevated BMI or components of metabolic syndrome do not exclude MODY.
- Negative autoantibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide in a young patient should trigger genetic consideration even when the phenotype looks “metabolic.”
- Glycosuria out of proportion to glycaemia (or early renal glycosuria) in a young person is a red flag for HNF1A dysfunction.
- Genetic testing now often uses NGS panels — novel variants will be found; pathogenicity is judged by ACMG criteria, segregation, functional prediction, and phenotype.
- Precision diagnosis changes management: stop unnecessary insulin, pick targeted oral agents or GLP‑1 therapy, and offer family counselling.

Take-home messages
- Rising obesity blunts the classic “lean MODY” heuristic — don’t let BMI alone rule out monogenic diabetes.
- Consider MODY genetic testing when young patients with diabetes have negative autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, a strong multigenerational family history, disproportionate glycosuria or marked postprandial hyperglycaemia.
- Genetic diagnosis alters therapy and prognosis: most HNF1A patients can be managed without insulin and may benefit from sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists; exercise caution with SGLT2 inhibitors.
- Use probability tools and local resources, but keep clinical judgment central — we need sensitivity in the metabolic syndrome era to catch MODY cases like this one.
