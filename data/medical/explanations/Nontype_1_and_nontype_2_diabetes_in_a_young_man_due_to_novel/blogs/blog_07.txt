# Postprandial Spikes Without DKA: The OGTT as a Diagnostic Lever

What caught my eye in this case wasn’t the fasting glucose or the absence of ketoacidosis — it was the jaw-dropping 2-hour OGTT of 19.8 mmol/L (about 357 mg/dL) in a young man with glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes. His fasting was high (12.4 mmol/L / ~223 mg/dL) and HbA1c was 10.2%, but it was the exaggerated postprandial excursion on the OGTT that best signalled a primary beta-cell secretory problem consistent with HNF1A-MODY. Genetic testing later confirmed a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant, and treatment was successfully refined away from insulin to a GLP-1 RA + metformin strategy.

If you’re in the business of sorting young-onset hyperglycaemia, the OGTT is an underused diagnostic lever — especially when you suspect monogenic diabetes.

## Why the OGTT matters in beta-cell disorders like HNF1A-MODY
- HNF1A mutations produce a progressive defect in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. Basal insulin may be preserved early, but the beta cell fails when challenged with a glucose load. That’s exactly the physiology the OGTT interrogates.
- Postprandial hyperglycaemia can be disproportionate to fasting levels. A normal or near-normal fasting glucose does not exclude clinically meaningful beta-cell dysfunction.
- HNF1A also affects GLUT2 expression and renal SGLT2 transcription. That combination predisposes to marked postprandial spikes and glycosuria at lower plasma glucose thresholds — a clinical clue when you see glycosuria out of proportion to BMI or insulin resistance markers.
- In this case the glucagon-stimulated C-peptide was modestly low (peak 0.83 nmol/L), antibodies were negative, family history was classic — but the OGTT crystalised the phenotype and supported targeted genetic testing.

## When to deploy the OGTT in busy clinical practice
Think OGTT when you see any of the following in a young patient (<35–40 years) with hyperglycaemia:
- Early-onset hyperglycaemia with strong multigenerational family history of diabetes.
- Glycosuria disproportionate to fasting glucose, BMI or insulin resistance signs.
- Negative diabetes autoantibodies but equivocal fasting glucose / C-peptide.
- Symptoms that suggest postprandial hyperglycaemia (post-meal polyuria, pronounced thirst after meals) despite only modest fasting abnormalities.
- Diagnostic uncertainty between type 1, type 2 and monogenic diabetes — especially before committing to lifelong insulin.

Avoid OGTT when the patient is acutely ill, on steroids, or has recent large dietary changes; standardize preparation (overnight fast, avoid heavy exercise/alcohol beforehand).

## Practical interpretation — be pragmatic
- Use the 75 g OGTT with plasma glucose at 0 and 2 hours. Remember cutoffs: 2-hr ≥11.1 mmol/L (≥200 mg/dL) diagnostic of diabetes; but the magnitude of the 2-hr rise (and relative mismatch vs fasting) is the informative piece in suspected MODY.
- Look for a pronounced 2-hr increment (large delta between fasting and 2-hr) rather than just the absolute numbers. In beta-cell disorders, you’ll often see sharp post-load spikes.
- Consider paired C-peptide (basal and stimulated) and islet autoantibodies alongside the OGTT. Together they give the physiology (insulin reserve), aetiology (autoimmune vs genetic) and the load-response.

## What the OGTT result changes for management
- It’s not just diagnostic theatre — OGTT results can shift therapy. Identifying a beta-cell secretory defect should raise suspicion for HNF1A-MODY; that has implications:
  - Sulfonylureas are a classic first-line for HNF1A-MODY (low-dose SU often works), but GLP-1 RAs can be used to preserve beta-cell function and lower hypoglycaemia risk.
  - SGLT2 inhibitors may be problematic: HNF1A-driven downregulation of SGLT2 can exaggerate glycosuria and dehydration risk; use with caution.
  - Genetic confirmation opens precision medicine pathways and family screening.
- In the case here, insulin was de-escalated after genetic confirmation; a GLP-1 RA + metformin achieved HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months.

## Pitfalls and limitations
- OGTT is resource- and time-intensive; you won’t do it on everyone. Use pretest probability — age, family history, autoantibodies, C-peptide — to select candidates.
- Obesity and rising T2D prevalence blur phenotypes. MODY can co-exist with obesity; don’t exclude genetic causes solely based on BMI.
- OGTT can be affected by recent diet, meds and illness — standardize prep and repeat if results are inconsistent with clinical picture.
- A single OGTT won’t replace genetic testing — it helps triage who needs sequencing.

## Clinical pearls
- A huge post-load spike with relative preserved or only mildly reduced fasting insulin/C-peptide should make you think beta-cell secretory defects (MODY), not classic insulin resistance.
- Glycosuria in a young non-ketotic patient is a red flag for HNF1A involvement (think reduced renal glucose threshold via SGLT2 downregulation).
- Use OGTT selectively to unmask impaired glucose tolerance patterns that fasting samples miss — especially when genetic testing resources are limited and you need to pick candidates wisely.

Take-home messages
- The OGTT is a high-yield test for detecting exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia from primary beta-cell defects such as HNF1A-MODY.
- Consider OGTT in young patients with early-onset diabetes, strong family history, unexplained glycosuria, negative autoantibodies or discordant fasting vs clinical picture.
- A dramatic 2-hour rise supports targeted genetic testing; results can meaningfully change therapy (e.g., move away from insulin to SU/GLP-1RA and enable family screening).
- OGTT is a tool to triage, not to replace, genetic confirmation — but used wisely it can be the lever that unlocks a precision diagnosis.
