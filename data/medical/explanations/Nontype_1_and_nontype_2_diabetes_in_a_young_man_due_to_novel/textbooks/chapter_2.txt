Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis of Early-Onset Diabetes

Early-onset hyperglycaemia presents a diagnostic challenge because the cardinal diabetes syndromes — type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes (T2D), latent autoimmune diabetes of adults (LADA), secondary causes, mitochondrial diabetes, and the various forms of maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) — overlap in age of onset and some clinical features. The case above illustrates this diagnostic crossroads: a young adult with marked hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, a strong family history of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide. These elements point away from classic T1D and toward a monogenic aetiology (HNF1A-MODY in this patient), but each alternative diagnosis must be explicitly considered and excluded because the management and prognosis differ substantially.

Type 1 diabetes remains the most important mimic in young adults because it is common and can present with marked hyperglycaemia. Typical features that favour T1D are rapid symptom onset, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), and persistent or progressive insulin deficiency with low or undetectable C‑peptide after the honeymoon phase. Circulating islet autoantibodies — most usefully GAD65, IA‑2 and ZnT8 — are present in the majority of new-onset T1D cases and are a strong discriminator. Practically, a positive GAD65/IA‑2/ZnT8 markedly increases the likelihood of autoimmune diabetes and makes MODY much less likely, but it does not absolutely exclude it: low‑titer antibodies have been reported in a small number of monogenic diabetes patients. C‑peptide testing (either fasting or stimulated, for example after glucagon) helps quantify β‑cell function; a stimulated C‑peptide above roughly 0.6 nmol/L (as in this case, 0.83 nmol/L) indicates substantial residual insulin secretion and should prompt consideration of non‑autoimmune diabetes when antibodies are negative. Importantly, in very early T1D the C‑peptide may still be preserved, so serial measurements and clinical context are essential.

Youth‑onset T2D has become more common and is an increasingly frequent differential diagnosis. T2D in young people is characterised by features of insulin resistance such as obesity, central adiposity, acanthosis nigricans and an adverse lipid profile (high triglycerides, low HDL). The decline in β‑cell function is generally more gradual than in T1D, and islet autoantibodies are absent. Family histories are often positive but reflect polygenic risk and common adult-onset diabetes in relatives, rather than clear dominant transmission across generations. Rising rates of overweight and metabolic syndrome mean that some MODY patients will have elevated BMI or dyslipidaemia and may be mislabelled as T2D; in the presented patient the BMI was modest and acanthosis was absent, findings that were not typical for insulin‑resistant T2D and prompted further evaluation for monogenic diabetes.

Within the MODY spectrum, several genotypes have characteristic clinical signatures that guide testing and therapy. Glucokinase‑MODY (GCK‑MODY, MODY2) is the easiest to recognise: it causes a stable, lifelong, mild fasting hyperglycaemia typically in the range of ~5.5–8.5 mmol/L (100–153 mg/dL) with minimal postprandial excursions and a very low risk of microvascular complications. Patients with GCK‑MODY usually do not benefit from pharmacologic glucose‑lowering outside of pregnancy, so identifying this diagnosis spares patients unnecessary therapy and anxiety. By contrast, HNF1A‑MODY (MODY3) produces progressive β‑cell dysfunction with disproportionately large postprandial hyperglycaemia and frequent glycosuria due to a lower renal glucose threshold (reduced SGLT2 expression). HNF1A patients typically present later than GCK-MODY with higher HbA1c and greater therapeutic need; they are classically very sensitive to low‑dose sulfonylureas, although GLP‑1 receptor agonists are increasingly used as effective and safer alternatives when sulfonylurea‑related hypoglycaemia is a concern. The case patient’s high post‑OGTT glucose, marked HbA1c elevation and glycosuria were characteristic of HNF1A dysfunction rather than GCK‑MODY.

HNF4A‑MODY (MODY1) resembles HNF1A clinically in producing progressive insulin secretory failure and sensitivity to sulfonylureas, but it has additional clues: a history of neonatal hypoglycaemia or macrosomic birth weight in the proband or offspring is common because HNF4A variants can transiently increase fetal insulin secretion. If such perinatal features are present, HNF4A should be prioritised.

HNF1B‑associated diabetes (MODY5) is a phenocopy that must be considered because non‑glycaemic features are prominent and alter management. HNF1B variants commonly cause structural renal disease (cysts, dysplasia), genitourinary tract malformations, hypomagnesaemia, and pancreatic hypoplasia; diabetes may present early and often progresses to require insulin. The presence of renal impairment, hypomagnesaemia, abnormal liver enzymes or genital tract anomalies in a young person with diabetes strongly suggests HNF1B and should prompt renal imaging and genetic testing directed at HNF1B rather than HNF1A.

Mitochondrial diabetes, for example from the m.3243A>G mutation, shows a maternal‑only inheritance pattern and is associated with extra‑pancreatic features such as sensorineural hearing loss, progressive myopathy, encephalopathy or stroke‑like episodes. Because mitochondrial heteroplasmy produces variable phenotypes, diabetes can be the presenting feature; when a pedigree shows diabetes transmitted exclusively by mothers or the patient has hearing loss, mitochondrial testing is indicated.

Other causes that should be considered and excluded include LADA (latent autoimmune diabetes of adults), which is essentially autoimmune diabetes with a slower course — older age at onset, presence of autoantibodies and gradual progression to insulin dependence characterise LADA. Secondary diabetes from pancreatic exocrine disease (pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, haemochromatosis), endocrine disorders (Cushing’s, acromegaly), or drug‑induced causes (glucocorticoids, some antipsychotics) must also be considered when the history or examination points to these processes.

Putting this into a practical diagnostic approach: begin with a careful pedigree (look for autosomal dominant transmission across successive generations and whether the pattern is paternal, maternal or mixed), thorough phenotyping (age at onset, acuity of symptoms, DKA history, growth and birth history, renal or genital anomalies, hearing loss, BMI and signs of insulin resistance), and targeted laboratory testing. Key tests that help triage further investigation are islet autoantibodies (GAD65, IA‑2, ZnT8), fasting and stimulated C‑peptide, fasting and postprandial glucose or a 2‑hour OGTT, basic metabolic panel including magnesium, and urinalysis for glycosuria. A strongly positive antibody panel or very low C‑peptide supports autoimmune diabetes. Mild, stable fasting hyperglycaemia with little postprandial rise points to GCK‑MODY and can justify targeted GCK sequencing first. Features such as renal malformations or hypomagnesaemia direct testing toward HNF1B. A maternal‑only pedigree with hearing loss suggests mitochondrial testing. When clinical features point to an autosomal dominant, non‑autoimmune presentation but do not fit a single gene phenotype, a MODY gene panel by next‑generation sequencing is now the most efficient diagnostic strategy and, when positive, directly affects management and cascade testing in relatives.

Finally, the therapeutic and prognostic implications of distinguishing these entities are profound. Identifying HNF1A‑MODY in our patient enabled withdrawal of insulin and selection of sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 based therapy with excellent glycaemic response, avoided unnecessary long‑term insulin use, identified at‑risk family members for cascade testing, and informed counselling about potential complications (eg, progressive β‑cell failure, renal glucose handling). Conversely, recognising GCK‑MODY could have spared this patient pharmacotherapy entirely, while finding HNF1B or mitochondrial disease would have prompted multidisciplinary surveillance for renal or neurologic complications. In every young person with diabetes, integrating careful history, focused examination and targeted biochemical testing will identify the clues that direct appropriate genetic or specialised investigations — and, as the case shows, enable precision treatment that improves outcomes.
