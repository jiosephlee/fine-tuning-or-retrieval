Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Testing Algorithm

Confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY requires a systematic approach that integrates routine metabolic testing, evaluation of endogenous insulin secretion, focused autoantibody testing to exclude type 1 diabetes, assessment for characteristic glycaemic patterns, and, when indicated, targeted genetic testing with careful variant interpretation and family cascade testing. The clinical case above illustrates a typical pathway: a young adult with marked hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, a multigenerational history of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide—features that together raised clinical suspicion and prompted molecular testing that confirmed HNF1A-MODY.

Initial laboratory panel and clinical evaluation
Begin with the basic diabetes workup in any patient with new hyperglycaemia: fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c to quantify chronic and fasting control; a basic metabolic panel to evaluate electrolytes, renal function and bicarbonate (to exclude DKA when clinically indicated); a lipid profile because dyslipidaemia may coexist and influences cardiovascular risk management; and urinalysis to document glycosuria and exclude significant proteinuria. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria is often present at lower plasma glucose levels than expected because HNF1A dysfunction reduces SGLT2 expression and lowers the renal threshold for glucose. In practice, documenting glycosuria in someone with modestly elevated plasma glucose is a supportive clinical clue.

Autoantibody testing to exclude type 1 diabetes
Because differentiating MODY from autoimmune type 1 diabetes is essential, test for diabetes-associated autoantibodies—GAD65 (GADA), IA‑2 and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) when available. The absence of these antibodies increases the pretest probability of a nonautoimmune etiology such as MODY, while their presence makes type 1 diabetes more likely. However, antibody status is not absolute: a minority of patients with genetically proven MODY may have positive autoantibodies, and conversely, some patients with autoimmune diabetes are antibody-negative at diagnosis. Therefore antibody results must be interpreted in the context of clinical features and C‑peptide.

Assess endogenous insulin secretion (fasting and stimulated C‑peptide)
Measurement of endogenous insulin secretion is a key discriminator. A fasting C‑peptide can be informative but is less sensitive than stimulated testing. A glucagon stimulation test (baseline and 6‑minute post‑glucagon C‑peptide) or a mixed‑meal tolerance test is commonly used. Stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (as in the reported case where the 6‑min value was 0.83 nmol/L) generally indicates preserved β‑cell function and argues against absolute insulin deficiency typical of longstanding type 1 diabetes. Values below this threshold suggest significant β‑cell failure and make MODY less likely, although early type 1 can present with preserved C‑peptide, so serial testing and the full clinical picture matter.

Dynamic glycaemic testing and clinical clues
An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) often accentuates the diagnostic pattern in HNF1A-MODY: relatively modest fasting hyperglycaemia with exaggerated postprandial glucose excursions (2‑hour glucose frequently ≥11.1 mmol/L). In practice, an OGTT or postprandial glucose measurements can reveal disproportionately high postprandial values for the fasting glycaemia observed. Lack of marked insulin resistance signs (absence of acanthosis nigricans, normal or only mildly elevated BMI) supports MODY, but these features are not definitive—rising obesity prevalence blurs clinical distinction. Ancillary tests such as high‑sensitivity C‑reactive protein (hsCRP) can be helpful: hsCRP tends to be low in HNF1A‑MODY compared with type 2 diabetes, providing supportive but not diagnostic evidence.

When to order genetic testing
Genetic testing should be considered when a patient has:
- Young‑onset diabetes (classically diagnosed before 25 years, though many HNF1A patients present later) AND
- A nonautoimmune phenotype (negative GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8) with preserved endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L), and/or
- A strong autosomal dominant family history of early-onset diabetes (multigenerational affected relatives), or
- Clinical features typical for MODY such as prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia with glycosuria at relatively low glucose levels.

In practice, if multiple of these elements are present—as in our patient with negative autoantibodies, stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, glycosuria and a clear maternal inheritance pattern—genetic testing should be pursued promptly. Use of clinical tools such as the Exeter MODY probability calculator can help triage testing, but interpret their output cautiously in non‑European ancestries because performance declines outside the derivation population.

Choice of genetic test and laboratory considerations
Use a targeted MODY next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panel that includes HNF1A and other common MODY genes (GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.). Advantages of a multigene NGS panel include broad coverage and cost‑effectiveness. Ensure the laboratory interrogates single nucleotide variants and small indels and uses methods to detect copy number variants (deletions/duplications), either by in‑panel read‑depth analysis or by reflex MLPA (multiplex ligation‑dependent probe amplification), because large exonic deletions can be missed by standard NGS. Confirmatory Sanger sequencing is often performed for novel single‑base variants depending on the laboratory’s quality control practices. Pre‑test genetic counseling is recommended to explain potential outcomes, insurance implications and the possibility of uncertain results.

Interpreting genetic results: applying ACMG criteria
Variant interpretation must follow accepted standards (e.g., ACMG/AMP guidelines). Combine multiple lines of evidence:
- Population frequency: pathogenic MODY variants are typically absent or extremely rare in population databases (gnomAD). A common variant is unlikely pathogenic.
- Segregation: cosegregation with diabetes in affected family members strongly supports pathogenicity. In our case report the variant segregated in multiple affected relatives, strengthening the finding.
- Computational prediction and in‑silico scores: tools (and more recently models like AlphaMissense) provide supportive evidence but are not definitive.
- Protein domain and functional relevance: variants in the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A are more likely to be pathogenic; functional data demonstrating impaired protein activity is high‑weight evidence.
- Phenotype concordance: match between the patient’s clinical phenotype (age of onset, glycosuria, preserved C‑peptide, dominant inheritance) and the expected features of HNF1A‑MODY supports causality.
Classify the variant as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign or benign, and include a clear rationale in the laboratory report. For novel variants, absence from databases does not equal pathogenicity; segregation and functional data are often decisive.

Practical management after results
- If a pathogenic/likely pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified: this confirms the diagnosis and should prompt reconsideration of therapy (for example, low‑dose sulfonylureas are a traditional first‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an effective and often safer alternative for those with declining β‑cell reserve or hypoglycaemia risk; insulin may be withdrawn if glycaemia can be controlled). Avoid empirical therapeutic changes based solely on a VUS.
- If the result is a VUS: do not base major treatment changes on the variant alone. Pursue family segregation testing and, when feasible, functional studies or periodic re‑analysis as databases grow.
- If no pathogenic variant is found: continue to manage based on phenotype, consider broader genetic testing if suspicion remains high, and re‑evaluate over time.

Cascade testing and family involvement
Once a P/LP variant is identified, offer cascade genetic testing to first‑degree relatives and other at‑risk family members. Testing affected relatives provides segregation data that can strengthen the variant classification; testing unaffected relatives can determine who needs surveillance and who can avoid unnecessary treatment. Genetic counseling is essential to discuss implications for prognosis, therapy (e.g., potential sulfonylurea sensitivity and SGLT2 inhibitor cautions), reproductive planning, and psychosocial issues. For minors, consider predictive testing when results would alter medical management during childhood or adolescence; otherwise many centers defer testing until the individual can consent.

Pitfalls and limitations to be aware of
- BMI, acanthosis and metabolic syndrome features are imperfect discriminators. Rising obesity prevalence blunts the distinction between MODY and young‑onset type 2 diabetes, so do not exclude MODY based solely on higher BMI.
- Autoantibody positivity does not absolutely exclude monogenic diabetes—rare overlap exists—but positive antibodies increase the likelihood of autoimmune diabetes and should lower the threshold for pursuing serial C‑peptide testing and type 1 management pathways.
- Clinical prediction tools (e.g., Exeter calculator) can aid decision‑making but may overestimate MODY probability in non‑European populations; use them alongside clinical judgment.
- NGS panels can miss certain variant types unless copy‑number analysis is included. Laboratories vary in coverage and interpretation frameworks; use accredited labs and request explicit statements on CNV detection.
- Variant reclassification over time is common. A VUS today may be reclassified as P/LP or benign; establish a mechanism to re‑contact patients and relatives when interpretations change.

Implementation in the presented case
Applying this algorithm to the patient described: routine labs showed fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L and HbA1c 10.2%; urinalysis documented glycosuria; GADA and IA‑2 were negative; stimulated C‑peptide was 0.83 nmol/L—supporting preserved β‑cell function. A strong maternal inheritance pattern increased pretest probability. These features met several indications for targeted NGS panel testing, which identified a novel HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S) variant located in the DNA‑binding domain. The laboratory applied ACMG criteria, integrating rarity in population databases, domain location, computational evidence and segregation in the family to classify the variant as pathogenic/likely pathogenic. The genetic diagnosis enabled precision therapy (transition off insulin to GLP‑1 receptor agonist and metformin, with later maintenance on oral therapy) and initiation of cascade testing and counseling for family members.

Summary algorithm (practical steps)
1. Initial evaluation: fasting glucose, HbA1c, BMP, lipids, urinalysis. Look for glycosuria and absence of DKA.
2. Exclude autoimmune diabetes: GAD65, IA‑2, ZnT8 antibodies.
3. Assess endogenous insulin: fasting and stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon or mixed‑meal); stimulated >0.6 nmol/L suggests preserved secretion.
4. Evaluate glycaemic pattern: OGTT or postprandial glucose to document exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia.
5. Consider supportive biomarkers (hsCRP) and family history; use prediction tools with caution.
6. If clinical suspicion is high, order a targeted MODY NGS panel with CNV analysis and pre‑test counseling.
7. Interpret variants using ACMG criteria; if P/LP, confirm diagnosis, adapt therapy accordingly and offer cascade testing; if VUS, pursue segregation studies and avoid major therapy changes based solely on the variant.
8. Provide genetic counseling, document findings in the medical record, arrange family testing and establish long‑term surveillance for complications.

Following this stepwise workup helps avoid misclassification of monogenic diabetes, enables precision therapy, and facilitates appropriate family counseling and cascade testing—improving outcomes for patients with HNF1A‑MODY and their relatives.

## Management and Prognosis of HNF1A‑MODY

Therapeutic decisions in HNF1A‑MODY are guided by two central principles: the primary defect is progressive β‑cell dysfunction (not insulin resistance), and affected patients typically retain greater sensitivity to insulin secretagogues than people with type 2 diabetes. Recognizing the diagnosis therefore changes both the choice of agents and the pace at which they are escalated. The case just presented illustrates these principles: the patient initially required insulin because of very high glucose and an HbA1c of 10.2%, but after genetic confirmation and demonstration of residual C‑peptide he was safely transitioned off insulin to incretin‑based therapy and metformin with excellent long‑term control. The paragraphs below outline evidence‑based pharmacologic and non‑pharmacologic approaches, practical strategies for transitions off insulin, surveillance for complications, and special‑population considerations.

Initial management and when to use insulin
Patients who present with severe hyperglycemia, metabolic decompensation or catabolic symptoms (weight loss, dehydration, ketonuria/ketosis) require prompt glucose lowering, and insulin is the appropriate initial therapy. Insulin stabilizes the patient and protects against acute complications while other assessments (C‑peptide, autoantibodies, genetic testing) are completed. Once hyperglycaemia is controlled and a diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY is made (or strongly suspected in the context of negative autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide), many patients can be transitioned from insulin to oral agents or GLP‑1 receptor agonists; this should be done gradually with close glucose monitoring. In practice, ensure β‑cell reserve (for example a stimulated C‑peptide in the range typically used in clinical practice—values well above assay‑specific thresholds that indicate endogenous secretion) and clinical stability before attempting withdrawal of insulin, and plan for frequent self‑monitoring during the transition.

First‑line oral agent: sulfonylureas
Classic teaching and multiple case series support low‑dose sulfonylureas as first‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY. Patients show pronounced sensitivity to sulfonylureas because the primary defect is in insulin secretion; small doses can produce substantial reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose. Typical clinical practice is to start with a low dose (eg, the lower end of commonly used doses for glimepiride, gliclazide, or glipizide) and titrate carefully to glycaemic response while monitoring for hypoglycaemia. Over time progressive β‑cell decline may necessitate dose escalation, and higher doses increase the risk of hypoglycaemia; some patients who were initially controlled on sulfonylureas will ultimately require alternative agents or insulin. Sulfonylureas should be used with particular caution in those who have irregular meals, renal impairment, or other hypoglycaemia risk factors.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists and DPP‑4 inhibitors
Incretin‑based therapies are attractive options in HNF1A‑MODY. Small randomized and crossover studies have shown that GLP‑1 receptor agonists (eg, liraglutide) can lower glucose to an extent similar to sulfonylureas while producing less hypoglycaemia and often promoting weight loss. In practice a GLP‑1 receptor agonist is a reasonable alternative when sulfonylureas are poorly tolerated, when hypoglycaemia risk is a concern, or when weight loss is desirable. The case patient responded well to weekly dulaglutide together with metformin, demonstrating the potential for GLP‑1 agents to maintain good control while avoiding insulin and hypoglycaemia. DPP‑4 inhibitors produce more modest HbA1c reductions than GLP‑1 RAs, but they have a favorable safety profile and can be useful as add‑on maintenance therapy or when injectable therapy is not acceptable.

Metformin and agents addressing insulin resistance
Metformin does not correct the primary β‑cell defect in HNF1A‑MODY but is reasonable adjunctive therapy in patients who are overweight, have features of insulin resistance, or require additional glycaemic lowering. It is commonly used in combination with a sulfonylurea or incretin‑based agent. Other agents commonly used in type 2 diabetes (thiazolidinediones, insulin) may be used when indicated by comorbidity or progressive β‑cell failure, but choice should reflect the predominant pathophysiology (secretory failure rather than insulin resistance).

SGLT2 inhibitors: caution advised
Because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in renal proximal tubules, patients with HNF1A‑MODY often have a lower renal threshold for glucose and baseline glycosuria. The addition of an SGLT2 inhibitor can therefore produce pronounced glucosuria and volume losses. Case reports and series have raised concerns about severe dehydration and ketoacidosis in HNF1A‑MODY patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors. For these reasons SGLT2 inhibitors should be used with caution, if at all, in HNF1A‑MODY and only after careful risk–benefit discussion and monitoring; they should be avoided in patients with tendency to volume depletion or who are at risk for ketoacidosis. The safer initial strategy is to use established therapies (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 RAs, metformin) and reserve SGLT2 inhibitors for select circumstances with specialist input.

Practical approach to transitioning off insulin
A practical stepwise approach is helpful: 1) stabilize glucose on insulin and treat any intercurrent illness; 2) perform diagnostic testing (autoantibodies, stimulated C‑peptide) and obtain genetic confirmation when feasible; 3) if C‑peptide indicates residual insulin secretion and autoantibodies are negative, lower basal insulin progressively while initiating a sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA (start low and titrate); 4) provide close glucose monitoring (home glucose checks several times daily) and adjust therapy based on fasting and postprandial readings; 5) stop insulin when stable glycaemia on oral/injectable non‑insulin therapy is demonstrated. Communication with the patient about hypoglycaemia signs and stopping insulin is essential. In the case described, the team discontinued insulin after genetic confirmation and maintained excellent control on dulaglutide plus metformin; this pathway exemplifies the value of targeted therapy once the molecular diagnosis is known.

Lifestyle and non‑pharmacologic management
As with all diabetes types, lifestyle remains foundational. Individualized medical nutrition therapy targeted at weight optimization and carbohydrate distribution, regular physical activity to improve insulin sensitivity and cardiovascular health, and avoidance of smoking are all part of long‑term management. Because many HNF1A‑MODY patients do not have marked insulin resistance, lifestyle goals should be tailored to the individual’s BMI and cardiometabolic risk rather than universally assuming the metabolic syndrome phenotype.

Monitoring and screening for complications
Microvascular and macrovascular risk in HNF1A‑MODY relates predominantly to the degree and duration of hyperglycaemia, so the usual diabetes screening and risk factor management apply. Recommended elements include:

- Glycaemic monitoring: target HbA1c should be individualized (commonly <7.0% for many nonpregnant adults); measure every 3 months until stable, then every 6 months. Intensify monitoring around medication changes or transitions off insulin.
- Retinopathy: ophthalmologic evaluation at diagnosis (if diabetes duration is unclear) or within the first year and then at least annually or as recommended based on findings.
- Nephropathy: urine albumin‑to‑creatinine ratio annually and serum creatinine/eGFR at least annually; aggressive blood‑pressure control if albuminuria is present.
- Neuropathy: annual foot exam, assessment of peripheral neuropathy with monofilament/vibration testing, and screening for autonomic neuropathy (orthostatic symptoms, gastroparesis, erectile dysfunction). The case highlights the importance of autonomic assessment — severely reduced sudomotor function was detected and warrants ongoing surveillance and symptom management.
- Cardiovascular risk: manage lipids, blood pressure and smoking cessation per standard guidelines; many HNF1A patients have typical cardiovascular risk determined by age and other factors rather than a unique genetic cardiac risk.
- Other surveillance: because HNF1A is expressed outside the pancreas (eg, liver), clinicians should be alert to reports in the literature linking HNF1A variants to hepatic adenomas in rare settings; routine screening is not indicated but unexplained hepatic abnormalities merit further evaluation.

Special situations

Pregnancy and preconception
Pregnancy requires special consideration. Tight glycaemic control before and during pregnancy is critical for fetal outcomes. Insulin is the preferred agent in pregnancy because it does not cross the placenta in clinically relevant amounts and can be titrated to achieve tight control; sulfonylureas cross the placenta and are generally avoided in pregnancy (some agents have been used in certain regions but are not first‑line). GLP‑1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors are contraindicated or not recommended in pregnancy. Women with HNF1A‑MODY should receive preconception counseling, early pregnancy care with frequent glucose measurement, and prompt transition to insulin if needed. Genetic counseling regarding fetal inheritance (50% chance if one parent is affected) should be offered.

Children and adolescents
In pediatric patients with confirmed HNF1A‑MODY, low‑dose sulfonylureas are effective but require careful dosing and hypoglycaemia education. Pediatric endocrinology involvement is valuable for dosing, monitoring growth and psychosocial support. Transition to adult care should include reinforcement of self‑management skills.

Family screening and genetic counseling
HNF1A‑MODY is autosomal dominant; each first‑degree relative has a 50% chance of carrying the pathogenic variant. Once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is identified, targeted cascade testing of relatives is recommended so that therapy can be individualized early and unnecessary insulin therapy avoided. Family members with the variant should receive glucose screening and counseling about future risk. Genetic counseling should address inheritance, reproductive options, and implications for life and health insurance where applicable.

Prognosis
Overall prognosis in HNF1A‑MODY is favorable when the diagnosis is made early and treatment is tailored. Long‑term outcomes are closely linked to glycaemic control: appropriate therapy can prevent or delay microvascular complications. Many individuals maintain good control for years on sulfonylureas or incretin‑based therapy; others experience progressive β‑cell failure over decades and require combination therapy or insulin. Cardiovascular risk is determined by usual risk factors, so aggressive control of blood pressure and lipids remains important. Early recognition and precision treatment — as in this case, where genetic diagnosis allowed insulin withdrawal and maintenance of HbA1c below 6.5% — can substantially alter the clinical course and quality of life.

In summary, management of HNF1A‑MODY combines an understanding of a primary secretory defect with careful selection of therapies that maximize glucose control while minimizing hypoglycaemia and adverse effects. Low‑dose sulfonylureas remain the historical first line, but GLP‑1 receptor agonists are effective and often better tolerated; metformin and DPP‑4 inhibitors have adjunctive roles, and SGLT2 inhibitors should generally be used with caution. Regular complication surveillance, family cascade testing and individualized care in special situations (pregnancy, pediatric patients) are essential components of optimal long‑term outcomes.
