Chapter 1: ## Clinical Approach to Young-Onset, Nonautoimmune Diabetes

Young-onset diabetes is usually thought of as either type 1 diabetes (autoimmune β-cell destruction) or increasingly as early type 2 diabetes related to obesity and insulin resistance. However, the case presented — a young man with marked hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, a preserved but insufficient stimulated C‑peptide, and a multigenerational history of early-onset diabetes — illustrates an important third possibility: monogenic diabetes, most commonly maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY). Recognizing MODY at the bedside changes diagnostic reasoning, directs appropriate laboratory testing, triggers genetic confirmation and family cascade testing, and frequently alters therapy. The remainder of this section outlines a pragmatic clinical approach to suspecting and evaluating MODY, with emphasis on HNF1A-MODY features highlighted by the case.

When to suspect MODY
MODY should be considered in any patient with diabetes diagnosed at a young age (classically before age 25, but many clinicians extend consideration to ages 25–35) who lacks typical features of autoimmune type 1 diabetes and in whom type 2 physiology is not convincing. Helpful clinical signals include an autosomal dominant pattern of diabetes (affected individuals in successive generations), nonketotic hyperglycaemia at presentation, and absence of clinical insulin resistance (no acanthosis nigricans, modest or normal BMI). In the case, the patient’s BMI was 24.6 kg/m2, there was no acanthosis, and three consecutive maternal relatives had early-onset diabetes — a pattern that should prompt consideration of MODY. Age cutoffs and definitions are not absolute; rising rates of obesity and variable penetrance mean MODY can present outside classic age ranges and with varying body habitus.

Clinical features that point toward HNF1A-MODY
Within the MODY spectrum, HNF1A mutations are among the most common causes and have distinctive clinical hallmarks. Hyperglycaemia is often characterized by exaggerated postprandial glucose excursions (frequently revealed by an OGTT), progressive β‑cell failure rather than insulin resistance, and a propensity to develop glycosuria at relatively lower plasma glucose concentrations because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the kidney. The presented patient had a striking 2‑hour OGTT glucose of 19.8 mmol/L and glycosuria on screening, features that fit HNF1A biology. Clinically, HNF1A-MODY patients often lack severe obesity and have few or no signs of metabolic syndrome early on, although overlap with type 2 features becomes more common as obesity increases population-wide.

Initial bedside and laboratory evaluation
A careful family history is essential: ask about age at diagnosis and treatment of diabetes in first‑ and second‑degree relatives, noting early onset across generations. Physical exam should seek signs of insulin resistance and features that would argue for alternative diagnoses. Initial labs to distinguish autoimmune from nonautoimmune diabetes include measurements of fasting glucose/HbA1c, islet autoantibodies (GAD65, IA‑2, and ZnT8 when available), and assessment of endogenous insulin secretion by C‑peptide. In the case, both GAD and IA‑2 antibodies were negative and a glucagon stimulation test produced a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L — evidence of preserved endogenous insulin secretion despite hyperglycaemia, supporting a non–type 1 aetiology. A stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L is conventionally taken to indicate residual β‑cell function, whereas very low or undetectable levels point toward type 1 diabetes; remember, however, that early in type 1 disease C‑peptide can be preserved, so serial testing or clinical context is important.

Interpreting discordant or borderline results
No single laboratory test is definitive. Autoantibody negativity supports (but does not prove) nonautoimmune diabetes; rare MODY patients may have positive autoantibodies and conversely some individuals diagnosed clinically with type 1 diabetes will be autoantibody‑negative. Similarly, C‑peptide interpretation depends on timing and glycaemic status — a fasting or stimulated C‑peptide may be low because of acute hyperglycaemia or glucotoxicity and can recover with improved control. Thus, the combination of clinical history (especially autosomal dominant inheritance), absence of autoantibodies, and preserved C‑peptide is the most compelling bedside evidence to justify molecular testing.

When to order genetic testing
Genetic testing for MODY should be considered when the pretest probability is appreciable: young onset, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, strong family history in an autosomal dominant pattern, atypical features for type 2 disease (eg, lack of obesity or severe insulin resistance), or unusual clinical clues such as disproportionate glycosuria. Clinical prediction tools such as the Exeter MODY calculator can help prioritize testing — the calculator estimated a 62% probability in this patient — but these algorithms were developed in European cohorts and can overestimate risk in non‑White populations; they should inform rather than dictate testing decisions. Practical constraints (cost, availability) sometimes necessitate targeting testing to patients with multiple supportive features rather than all young‑onset diabetes.

Choice of genetic test and interpretation
Next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panels covering the common MODY genes (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.) are the usual first‑line molecular approach because they are efficient and detect single nucleotide variants and small indels across multiple genes. Once a variant is identified, interpretation follows standards such as the ACMG guidelines: pathogenicity is assessed by population frequency, segregation with disease in the family, functional data, in silico prediction scores, and published reports. The patient in the case had a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S) which was absent from databases yet met multiple ACMG criteria — segregation with disease in the family, proximity to known pathogenic sites, and strong in silico predictions — supporting a likely pathogenic classification. Genetic counselling should accompany testing for informed consent, discussion of implications for treatment, and planning of cascade testing in relatives.

Therapeutic and prognostic implications of genetic diagnosis
A genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY has immediate clinical consequences. Patients with HNF1A‑MODY typically retain significant insulin secretory capacity early in the disease and are exquisitely sensitive to secretagogues such as low‑dose sulfonylureas; many treated patients can be transferred off insulin to oral agents. The case illustrates a common clinical pathway: initial insulin use to rapidly control severe hyperglycaemia, followed by withdrawal of insulin after genetic confirmation and transition to non‑insulin therapies (in this patient, a GLP‑1 receptor agonist with metformin, later simplified to metformin plus a DPP‑4 inhibitor) with excellent glycaemic response. GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive option because they augment insulin secretion in a glucose‑dependent manner and carry lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas; randomized crossover trials have shown similar glycaemic efficacy between liraglutide and sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY but fewer hypoglycaemic events with GLP‑1 agonists. Conversely, use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY requires caution: reduced SGLT2 expression in these patients contributes to baseline glycosuria and theoretically increases the risk of volume depletion, genital infections, and euglycemic ketoacidosis when SGLT2 inhibitors are added.

Practical algorithm for the bedside clinician
A pragmatic evaluation might proceed as follows: for any patient with diabetes diagnosed under ~35 years, take a careful three‑generation family history and perform a focused exam for insulin resistance. Obtain HbA1c, fasting glucose, lipid profile, islet autoantibodies, and measure fasting or stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon stimulation test if available). If autoantibodies are negative, C‑peptide is preserved, and family history or clinical features are suggestive, discuss targeted genetic testing with the patient and refer for genetic counselling. If a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is found, change therapy as appropriate, inform and offer cascade testing to at‑risk relatives, and institute routine diabetes complication surveillance tailored to expected disease course.

Pitfalls and limitations
Do not exclude MODY simply because a patient is overweight or because there is some family history of type 2 diabetes — obesity is increasingly common in all populations and does not rule out monogenic causes. Likewise, remember that autoantibodies can be transient, and C‑peptide can be misleading early in the course of autoimmune disease; serial assessment and clinical judgement are important. Finally, variant interpretation can be challenging — many variants are classified as of uncertain significance — and pathogenicity often rests on segregation and functional data that may not be immediately available. Collaboration with clinical geneticists and reference laboratories is therefore valuable.

Conclusion
MODY, particularly HNF1A-MODY, is an underdiagnosed cause of young‑onset, nonautoimmune diabetes. The key bedside clues are early age of onset, multigenerational autosomal dominant family history, glycosuria disproportionate to glycaemia, absence of marked insulin resistance, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved endogenous insulin secretion. These features should prompt genetic testing because a confirmed molecular diagnosis enables precision medicine — guides choice of therapy, spares unnecessary insulin in many cases, and identifies at‑risk family members for targeted screening and early treatment. The presented case exemplifies how careful clinical assessment combined with modern genetic testing can transform management and prognosis for a patient with monogenic diabetes.

## Pathophysiology and Genetics of HNF1A‑MODY

HNF1A-MODY is a paradigmatic example of how a single transcription factor defect produces a characteristic clinical syndrome by perturbing gene networks in pancreatic β-cells and in extra‑pancreatic tissues. HNF1A (hepatocyte nuclear factor 1‑alpha) is encoded on chromosome 12q24, spans 10 exons and produces a 631–amino‑acid protein with three functional modules: an N‑terminal dimerization domain that allows HNF1A to form homo‑ and heterodimers, a central POU‑like DNA‑binding domain that mediates sequence‑specific recognition of target promoters, and a C‑terminal transactivation domain that recruits the transcriptional machinery. HNF1A is highly expressed in pancreatic β‑cells, hepatocytes and renal tubular epithelium, and through transcriptional control of multiple downstream genes it coordinates glucose sensing, metabolism and renal glucose handling.

At the β‑cell level, most pathogenic HNF1A variants produce loss of function—either by reducing the amount of functional protein (haploinsufficiency) or, less commonly, through dominant‑negative effects—resulting in progressive β‑cell dysfunction rather than primary β‑cell destruction. HNF1A regulates a suite of genes required for glucose sensing and metabolic coupling: SLC2A2 (GLUT2), enzymes of glycolysis such as pyruvate kinase, and elements of insulin gene expression. Reduced expression of GLUT2 and metabolic enzymes lowers glucose entry and glycolytic flux in β‑cells, decreases ATP generation in response to glucose, and thereby blunts ATP‑dependent closure of KATP channels and glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion. The clinical consequence is a progressive impairment of insulin secretion manifest first as exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia and, over time, frank fasting hyperglycaemia. This pathophysiology explains why patients with HNF1A‑MODY typically have sufficient residual C‑peptide to be distinguished from autoimmune type 1 diabetes for several years after diagnosis, yet they develop gradually worsening insulin deficiency.

HNF1A actions outside the pancreas account for several characteristic non‑glycaemic features of HNF1A‑MODY and provide diagnostic clues. In the kidney, HNF1A transcriptionally regulates SLC5A2, the gene encoding the sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in proximal tubules. Reduced SGLT2 expression lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, so patients with HNF1A variants commonly develop glycosuria at relatively modest plasma glucose levels. This feature — glycosuria out of proportion to hyperglycaemia — is clinically useful when present, and it also explains why SGLT2 inhibitor use in some HNF1A‑MODY patients may unmask profound glucosuric fluid losses and predispose to dehydration or euglycaemic ketoacidosis in susceptible individuals.

Hepatic effects of HNF1A loss further inform diagnostics and risk stratification. HNF1A positively regulates acute‑phase proteins including CRP; accordingly, baseline high‑sensitivity CRP concentrations tend to be lower in people with HNF1A‑MODY than in individuals with typical type 2 diabetes. While low hs‑CRP is not diagnostic by itself, it is an inexpensive extrapancreatic biomarker that can add supportive evidence when combined with clinical context (early onset, autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide). HNF1A also modulates hepatic lipid and drug‑metabolizing pathways, which may affect the broader metabolic phenotype.

The genetic landscape of HNF1A‑MODY is heterogeneous but instructive. Hundreds of sequence variants have been described; pathogenic changes are most commonly single‑nucleotide substitutions or small indels that disrupt the DNA‑binding domain in the central portion of the protein. Variants in the DNA‑binding domain frequently have strong functional effects and are over‑represented among pathogenic alleles. Mutations in the transactivation domain occur less often and sometimes have milder phenotypes. There is also an isoform effect: HNF1A produces multiple transcript isoforms, and mutations that affect exons utilized in all isoforms tend to yield earlier onset or more severe disease than mutations limited to exons expressed in a subset of isoforms.

Interpreting a novel HNF1A variant for clinical care requires integration of genetic and clinical evidence according to established standards (ACMG/AMP). Useful lines of evidence include: absence or extreme rarity of the variant in population databases (supporting pathogenicity), cosegregation with diabetes in multiple affected family members (supporting), localization within a functionally critical domain such as the DNA‑binding region (supporting), conservation of the altered residue across species and concordant deleterious predictions from multiple in silico algorithms (supporting but not definitive), and direct functional studies demonstrating altered transcriptional activity (strong evidence when available). Clinical concordance—early onset, negative islet autoantibodies, glycosuria at modest glucose levels, preserved but inadequate stimulated C‑peptide—provides crucial phenotype support. By combining these elements, a novel variant can often be classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), or benign; this classification should be explicitly reported and periodically revisited as new data accrue.

The case described illustrates these principles. The novel heterozygous substitution c.809A>G (p.Asn270Ser, often written p.N270S) lies in exon 4 within the DNA‑binding domain — a region where pathogenic mutations are concentrated — and segregated with early‑onset diabetes in multiple family members. It was absent from public population databases and received a high deleteriousness score by computational tools; together with the clinical phenotype (early onset, autoantibody negativity, glycosuria and preserved stimulated C‑peptide), these data supported a classification of likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria. That molecular diagnosis had immediate clinical utility: insulin was safely withdrawn and therapy adjusted to agents (e.g., sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists) whose efficacy is predictable in HNF1A‑MODY, and cascade testing for at‑risk relatives could be offered.

From a laboratory perspective, next‑generation sequencing (NGS) multigene panels have become the pragmatic standard for suspected monogenic diabetes because they permit simultaneous interrogation of all MODY genes, detect single‑nucleotide and small indel variants efficiently, and can be combined with methods to detect copy number changes. Reports should state zygosity (heterozygous for HNF1A‑MODY), precise nucleotide and protein nomenclature, exon and protein domain localization, ACMG classification with the evidence lines used, and recommended clinical actions such as cascade testing and phenotype‑driven follow‑up. Clinicians must recognize that many novel variants will initially be VUS and that collaboration with molecular geneticists, functional laboratories and family studies is often required to move from uncertainty to actionable precision care.

In summary, HNF1A‑MODY arises from loss‑of‑function variants in a transcription factor that coordinates β‑cell glucose sensing and renal glucose reabsorption. The resulting syndrome — progressive insulin secretory failure with renal glycosuria and extracutaneous biochemical signatures such as low hs‑CRP — has distinct therapeutic and family‑testing implications. Careful application of ACMG‑guided variant interpretation, combined with targeted phenotyping, enables precision medicine: a genetic diagnosis changes prognosis, directs choice of glucose‑lowering therapy and identifies relatives at risk.
