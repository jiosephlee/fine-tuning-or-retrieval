Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Chapter 4: ## Precision Therapy in HNF1A-MODY: From Initial Control to Long-term Management

Patients with HNF1A-MODY require a treatment approach guided by the degree of hyperglycaemia at presentation, residual β‑cell function, and the natural history of progressive insulin secretory failure. In this case the patient presented with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L) but had negative islet autoantibodies and a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L—evidence of significant glucose toxicity with partially preserved endogenous insulin secretion. These features illustrate two therapeutic principles: (1) rapidly correcting severe hyperglycaemia to relieve glucotoxicity and preserve β‑cell function, and (2) once the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY is established, shifting to therapies that exploit the pathophysiology of insulin secretory failure while minimising hypoglycaemia and unnecessary insulin exposure.

Initial management of marked hyperglycaemia
When hyperglycaemia is severe (markedly elevated glucose or HbA1c, symptomatic polyuria/polydipsia, weight loss) short‑term insulin is commonly used to achieve prompt glycaemic control and reverse glucotoxicity. In monogenic diabetes this is a temporary measure unless absolute insulin deficiency is present. Practical indications for starting basal insulin include inability to control fasting glucose with oral agents, significant symptoms, or very high A1c that warrants rapid lowering. De‑escalation is appropriate when glycaemia has improved, there is no ketosis, and objective evidence of β‑cell reserve exists—typically a stimulated C‑peptide above thresholds used clinically (in this report a stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L is considered preserved). In the presented patient, basal insulin degludec was started for rapid control and then discontinued after one month once HbA1c improved (to 9.1%), clinical suspicion of HNF1A‑MODY was supported by genetic testing, and endogenous insulin secretion was judged sufficient to attempt non‑insulin therapy. De‑escalation should be gradual, with frequent capillary glucose monitoring (or CGM) during dose reduction to detect fasting and postprandial hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia.

Sulfonylureas as traditional first‑line in HNF1A‑MODY
HNF1A mutations cause progressive β‑cell secretory defects but leave the machinery for stimulus‑secretion coupling intact; consequently, insulin secretagogues—especially sulfonylureas—have long been the classical pharmacotherapy for HNF1A‑MODY. Patients with HNF1A‑MODY are often very sensitive to sulfonylureas, and low starting doses are recommended (for example glimepiride 0.5–1 mg or equivalent gliclazide MR lower than typical T2D starting doses), with cautious titration. Sulfonylureas can produce rapid improvements in fasting and postprandial glucose because they close KATP channels on β‑cells to augment insulin release. The principal downside is hypoglycaemia risk, which can be substantial in this genetically predisposed population; long‑acting agents and high doses should be avoided when possible. Sulfonylureas remain an important option, particularly in younger patients with clear HNF1A pathogenic variants and retained β‑cell function, but choice must balance efficacy against hypoglycaemia and weight gain.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists versus sulfonylureas: evidence and rationale in this case
Recent controlled data comparing incretin‑based therapy and sulfonylureas in HNF1A‑MODY provide useful context. A small randomized, double‑blind, crossover study (as noted in the case discussion) found that liraglutide and glimepiride produced comparable reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose in HNF1A‑MODY, but GLP‑1 receptor agonists had a more favourable hypoglycaemia profile. GLP‑1 RAs additionally offer weight‑lowering or neutral effects, potential cardiovascular benefit, and a lower risk of hypoglycaemia because their insulinotropic action is glucose‑dependent.

In the reported patient, these considerations supported switching from insulin to a weekly GLP‑1 RA (dulaglutide 1.5 mg) after genetic confirmation. Advantages specific to this decision included once‑weekly dosing (improving adherence), effective HbA1c lowering in patients with residual β‑cell function, and a lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas—an important safety consideration given the patient’s young age and lifestyle. The clinical outcome—HbA1c falling from 9.1% at one month to 6.0% at six months after initiating GLP‑1 RA plus metformin—illustrates that GLP‑1 RAs can be a powerful alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A‑MODY, particularly when preservation of β‑cell function and hypoglycaemia avoidance are priorities.

Roles of metformin and DPP‑4 inhibitors as adjuncts
Metformin is not disease‑specific for MODY but remains a useful adjunct when there is evidence of insulin resistance (higher BMI, adverse lipid profile) or to provide modest, weight‑neutral glycaemic lowering and hepatic glucose reduction. In this case metformin was continued throughout as part of combination therapy addressing possible insulin resistance (BMI 24.6 kg/m2 and elevated triglycerides).

DPP‑4 inhibitors (used here initially as saxagliptin) are less potent than GLP‑1 RAs but can be used when GLP‑1 RAs are contraindicated, not tolerated, or when only modest HbA1c lowering is required. Their glucose‑dependent mechanism means low hypoglycaemia risk, but their efficacy depends on residual incretin responsiveness and β‑cell capacity; they are therefore generally adjunctive rather than first‑line agents in HNF1A‑MODY. In this patient a strategy of GLP‑1 RA induction followed by simplification to metformin plus a DPP‑4 inhibitor maintained glycaemic targets, demonstrating step‑down therapy can work when β‑cell function and previous response are favourable.

SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY: mechanistic concerns and caution
HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the proximal tubule, and HNF1A‑MODY is associated with a lower renal threshold for glucose and baseline glycosuria. Theoretical and reported concerns exist about adding SGLT2 inhibitors in this context: augmented glycosuria may provoke dehydration, hypotension or precipitate euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis, and genital infections risk is increased. Given these mechanistic considerations and reported cases of severe dehydration/DKA with SGLT2 inhibition in HNF1A‑MODY, clinicians should be cautious and generally avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with marked baseline glycosuria, low insulin reserve, or risk factors for DKA. If considered (for example, when cardiovascular or renal indications strongly favour them), careful patient selection, education, and close monitoring are essential.

Targets, monitoring, and lifestyle
Glycaemic targets in HNF1A‑MODY are individualized: for most young, otherwise healthy patients seeking durable complication prevention, an HbA1c target <7.0% (and often <6.5% if achievable without hypoglycaemia) is reasonable. Because hypoglycaemia risk differs across agents, monitoring should include regular SMBG or CGM, particularly during therapy transitions (insulin withdrawal, initiation of sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA) to detect hypoglycaemia, fasting/postprandial excursions, and to guide titration. Routine screening for microvascular complications (annual urine albumin, periodic retinal exams) is indicated as in other diabetes types. Lifestyle measures—individualised nutrition counselling, regular physical activity, and weight management—remain foundational; however, when intensifying insulin secretagogues, dietary plans should be paired with education on hypoglycaemia recognition and management.

When to reinstitute insulin and long‑term considerations
Insulin may be reinstated temporally for intercurrent illness, surgery, pregnancy, or permanently if progressive β‑cell failure produces inadequate glycaemic control despite optimal non‑insulin therapy. Regular reassessment of C‑peptide and clinical response helps anticipate this transition. Finally, the case demonstrates the value of genetic diagnosis in guiding therapy: identifying HNF1A‑MODY allowed safe discontinuation of insulin, selection of a GLP‑1 RA to preserve function and reduce hypoglycaemia risk, and later simplification to oral therapy with sustained HbA1c control—an example of precision medicine enabling better‑targeted, individualized long‑term management.
