Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Art of Exclusion: Navigating the Differential for Fever, Headache, and Hyponatremia

We’ve all seen the "triad" that makes a clinician pause: a patient presenting with fever, headache, and profound hyponatremia. It is a diagnostic labyrinth where the wrong turn can lead to missing a life-threatening infection, a hidden malignancy, or a rare systemic inflammatory flare. In many cases, the diagnosis is found not by finding the "smoking gun" immediately, but by systematically stripping away the possibilities until only the truth remains.

In a recent case, a man in his 60s presented with this exact triad, complicated by persistent hiccups and a "folliculitis-like" rash. While his labs showed a striking sodium level of 124 mmol/L and an elevated CRP, the initial challenge was determining whether we were looking at a standard "medical" hyponatremia or something much more sinister involving the central nervous system (CNS).

## The Systematic Strip-Down

When you see CNS symptoms combined with systemic inflammation, the "rule-out" list must be aggressive. The first priority is always infectious. Given the headache and fever, we look for the usual suspects: HSV, VZV, and bacterial meningitis. In this case, the CSF analysis was the primary tool of exclusion—showing a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis but remaining negative for viral antigens, bacterial cultures, and mycobacterial markers. 

Once the "germs" are off the table, the focus shifts to the "hidden" killers: malignancy and autoimmune disease. This is where the "art" of the differential comes in. The patient had a mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R). While often a red flag for hematologic malignancy, it can be a non-specific marker of general inflammation. When paired with a clean brain MRI and normal CSF cytology, it allows us to pivot away from lymphoma and toward systemic inflammatory disorders. Similarly, while sarcoidosis is a high-priority differential for CNS inflammation, normal ACE and lysozyme levels helped narrow the field, even if they aren't perfectly sensitive.

## The Rare Reveal: Neuro-Behçet’s and the SIADH Connection

After ruling out the common and the catastrophic, we were left with a rare clinical scenario: acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) presenting concurrently with systemic BD. Typically, neuro-BD develops years after the initial diagnosis of mucocutaneous lesions, but here, the oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and skin rashes appeared simultaneously with the neurological symptoms.

One of the most interesting nuances of this case is the presence of SIADH. While we often associate SIADH with medications (like cyclophosphamide) or specific tumors, this case highlights a potential direct link between neuro-inflammatory states—specifically neuro-BD—and the dysregulation of antidiuretic hormone. The mechanism may be driven by proinflammatory cytokines like IL-6, which are known to trigger ADH release. It serves as a reminder that hyponatremia in a patient with CNS inflammation shouldn't just be managed as an electrolyte imbalance; it must be managed as a symptom of the underlying inflammatory process.

## Clinical Pearls and Management

This case offers several high-yield takeaways for the ED and the clinic:

*   **The "Hiccup" Clue:** Persistent hiccups can be a subtle but significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation (like meningitis). 
*   **The Rhabdomyolysis Connection:** Severe hyponatremia can cause muscle damage. In this patient, a CK of 1349 U/L was identified—a complication of profound hyponatremia that is frequently overlooked in the initial workup of a febrile patient.
*   **Imaging Limits:** A normal brain MRI does *not* rule out acute neuro-BD. In the early stages, the disease can present as aseptic meningitis without significant parenchymal lesions.
*   **Treatment Nuance:** While corticosteroids are the standard for neuro-BD, this patient responded remarkably well to colchicine monotherapy. While colchicine is usually considered insufficient for acute neuro-BD, this case suggests it may be a viable option for managing the mucocutaneous and SIADH components when steroids are undesirable.

**Take-Home Messages:**

*   In a patient with fever, headache, and hyponatremia, systematically exclude infection and malignancy before settling on rare inflammatory diseases.
*   Consider neuro-Behçet’s disease in patients with mucocutaneous lesions who present with non-specific CNS symptoms, even if neuro-involvement is concurrent with the initial diagnosis.
*   Be aware that SIADH can be a direct manifestation of neuro-inflammation via cytokine-mediated pathways.
*   Look for "extra" symptoms like hiccups or elevated CK as potential red flags for the severity of an underlying electrolyte disturbance.
