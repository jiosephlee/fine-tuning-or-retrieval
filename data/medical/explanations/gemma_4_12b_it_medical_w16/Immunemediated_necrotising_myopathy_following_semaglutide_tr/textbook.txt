## Pathophysiology and Background of Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM)

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is a severe form of inflammatory myopathy characterized by progressive, symmetric proximal muscle weakness. Unlike other inflammatory myopathies, such as polymyositis or dermatomyositis, which are characterized by significant lymphocytic infiltration into the muscle tissue, IMNM is defined by the presence of prominent myofiber necrosis with a relative paucity of inflammatory cells. In clinical practice, this often presents as a rapid decline in functional status, affecting the large muscle groups of the hips and shoulders, and in severe cases, can involve the muscles of mastication and deglutition, leading to dysphagia and dysphonia—as seen in this patient.

The pathogenesis of IMNM involves a targeted immune attack on the skeletal muscle fibers. Histologically, this manifests as dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers. A hallmark of this process is "myophagocytosis," where macrophages (marked by CD68) infiltrate the muscle to clear the debris of dead fibers. This inflammatory response is often accompanied by a membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on the myofibers, indicating a complement-mediated component of the tissue damage. While the specific triggers can vary, the disease is frequently associated with specific autoantibodies that target proteins involved in muscle metabolism and protein synthesis.

Two primary autoantibodies are highly associated with IMNM: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). The HMGCR antibody is particularly relevant in patients with metabolic syndrome, as it is frequently triggered by the use of statins, which are standard treatments for hyperlipidemia. However, approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are "seronegative," meaning they do not possess these known biomarkers. This subset poses a significant diagnostic challenge, as the lack of specific antibodies suggests that other, yet-to-be-identified autoantibodies or triggers may be responsible for the necrotizing process.

Understanding the patient's clinical course requires situating it within the context of modern metabolic therapies, specifically glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists like semaglutide. These medications are increasingly used to manage obesity and type 2 diabetes by mimicking incretin hormones to stimulate insulin secretion and slow gastric emptying. While weight loss and reduced muscle mass are known potential side effects, the medication's relationship with acute muscle injury is an emerging area of concern. Clinicians must distinguish between intended weight loss, drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, and an autoimmune-mediated response like IMNM.

Distinguishing drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from an autoimmune myopathy like IMNM is critical for determining management. Rhabdomyolysis typically presents as an acute, massive elevation of creatine kinase (CK) that resolves rapidly (often within days) upon cessation of the offending agent and initiation of aggressive hydration. In contrast, IMNM is characterized by a more progressive clinical course where CK levels remain elevated or rise significantly over weeks or months, necessitating immunosuppressive therapy such as high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). In this case, the 12-fold rise in CK over four months and the requirement for IVIG and steroids strongly point toward a necrotizing myopathy rather than a simple acute toxic reaction to the medication.

## Clinical Presentation: Acute Reaction and Progressive Myopathy

The patient’s clinical profile is characterized by significant metabolic comorbidities, including obesity, hypertension, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). These conditions provided the clinical rationale for initiating semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist commonly used to manage weight and metabolic dysfunction. Understanding this baseline is crucial, as metabolic disorders are frequently associated with other medications—such as statins—that are known triggers for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

The patient’s clinical course began with an immediate, acute adverse reaction following a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she experienced severe gastrointestinal distress, including nausea and vomiting. This led to a secondary hospital admission two days later for severe dehydration, poor oral intake, dyspnea, and acute renal failure accompanied by severe metabolic acidosis. While these symptoms were managed successfully with aggressive intravenous hydration and bicarbonate, the initial laboratory work-up showed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 337 U/L. This mild elevation was notably insufficient to suggest acute rhabdomyolysis, which typically presents with much higher CK concentrations and more severe muscle breakdown.

Following the resolution of her acute gastrointestinal issues, the patient underwent a significant period of clinical stability before a delayed progression of symptoms began. Over a four-month period, she developed a steady decline in physical function, characterized by progressive proximal muscle weakness. Critically, she also began to experience dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and dysphonia (difficulty with voice production). These symptoms are clinical "red flags" in the context of myopathy, as they indicate involvement of the striated muscles of the upper esophagus and the larynx, respectively, which can lead to significant morbidity or aspiration risks.

The temporal relationship between the medication exposure and the onset of myopathy is a key diagnostic differentiator in this case. In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, symptoms and CK elevations typically peak rapidly and begin to resolve shortly after the drug is discontinued. In contrast, this patient’s deterioration occurred over four months following a single dose. This delayed progression suggests that the semaglutide may have acted as a trigger for an underlying immune-mediated process—specifically IMNM—rather than causing a direct, acute toxic effect on the muscle fibers.

Upon physical examination during the peak of her symptoms, the patient exhibited severe weakness primarily localized to the proximal muscle groups. The examination was notable for the absence of systemic inflammatory signs, such as rashes, synovitis, or pulmonary crackles. The focus on proximal weakness (such as the quadriceps and hip flexors) is a hallmark of myopathic processes, distinguishing it from primary neurological disorders which might present with more distal weakness or diminished deep tendon reflexes. This presentation, combined with the clinical history, necessitated a thorough investigation into inflammatory and necrotising myopathies.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning

The diagnostic evaluation of a patient presenting with progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and elevated creatine kinase (CK) requires a systematic approach to differentiate between acute toxicities, metabolic disturbances, and primary inflammatory myopathies. In this case, the clinical reasoning was anchored in the temporal relationship between the administration of semaglutide and the onset of symptoms, alongside a multi-modal workup involving laboratory trends, advanced imaging, and histopathology.

Laboratory markers provided the initial framework for suspicion. While the patient had a mildly elevated CK (337 U/L) immediately following the first dose of semaglutide, her subsequent clinical decline over four months—culminating in a CK of 3992 U/L—was a critical diagnostic pivot. In cases of pure drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, one typically expects a rapid, acute spike in CK followed by a swift resolution upon cessation of the offending agent. The 12-fold increase in CK over several months suggested a more persistent, aggressive inflammatory process rather than a simple toxic effect. Furthermore, the patient’s persistent elevation of transaminases (AST and ALT) required careful interpretation; since she had a biopsy-proven history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), these markers were likely reflective of her underlying hepatic condition rather than acute muscle breakdown.

Autoantibody profiling served to narrow the differential diagnosis of inflammatory myopathies. While immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is frequently associated with anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies, approximately one-third of patients are seronegative. In this patient, the myositis panel was entirely negative, including the hallmark HMGCR and SRP antibodies. The presence of a low-titer positive SS-B antibody in the absence of anti-SSA or anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) was an outlier, but it did not provide a definitive link to a specific connective tissue disease. This "seronegative" status highlights the diagnostic challenge of IMNM, where the absence of specific markers necessitates a heavier reliance on clinical progression and histopathology.

Diagnostic imaging provided supportive evidence of active muscle involvement. MRI of the bilateral femurs revealed symmetric muscular edema, specifically in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps. Muscle MRI is highly sensitive for detecting inflammation and edema, helping to localize the primary site of myopathic activity. Additionally, cardiac imaging (echocardiogram and cardiac MRI) was utilized to rule out primary cardiac causes of weakness or to assess for secondary effects. While the cardiac MRI showed mild hypokinesis and myocardial edema, these findings were interpreted as potentially related to stress-induced cardiomyopathy or early myocarditis rather than the primary driver of the patient's profound proximal muscle weakness.

The definitive diagnosis was established through histopathology. A muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis revealed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, a hallmark of necrotising myopathy. Crucially, immunohistochemistry demonstrated myophagocytosis—where macrophages (CD68+ cells) are seen "consuming" necrotic fibers—and deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC) on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers. These findings are pathognomonic for IMNM. The paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates is a key differentiator from polymyositis, which typically presents with dense inflammatory cell clusters. These histological features confirmed an aggressive, immune-mediated necrotising process.

Finally, the clinical team synthesized these findings to establish a causal link between semaglutide and the IMNM. To do this, they utilized the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) criteria, which assess causality based on a reasonable time relationship, the lack of alternative explanations, and a positive clinical response to the withdrawal of the suspected agent. Because the patient’s symptoms and CK levels showed marked improvement following the cessation of semaglutide and the initiation of a short course of corticosteroids and IVIG, the case was classified as "probable causality." This distinction is vital: while drug-induced rhabdomyolysis is a known side effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists, this case suggests a more complex, immune-mediated secondary reaction that requires specific immunosuppressive intervention.

## Management Strategies and Clinical Outcomes

The management of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) focuses on rapid suppression of the underlying inflammatory process to prevent further muscle necrosis and functional decline. In this case, the severity of the patient’s presentation—characterized by significant proximal muscle weakness and dysphagia—necessitated aggressive initial intervention. The patient was started on pulse methylprednisolone, receiving 1000 mg intravenously daily for five days. High-dose corticosteroid "pulses" are a standard clinical strategy for severe inflammatory myopathies, aimed at achieving a rapid reduction in systemic inflammation and improving muscle strength more quickly than oral steroids alone might allow. Following the pulse therapy, the patient was transitioned to a maintenance dose of oral prednisone (1 mg/kg daily) to sustain the immunosuppressive effect.

Because the patient exhibited persistent weakness and severe clinical features despite initial steroid treatment, adjunctive therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was initiated. IVIG is frequently employed in the management of severe myopathies and inflammatory disorders to modulate the immune response, potentially by neutralizing pathogenic autoantibodies or interfering with complement activation. In this instance, the patient received 2 g/kg of IVIG over four days. The combination of high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG is a standard approach for patients who do not show an immediate, robust response to steroids alone or those who present with life-threatening complications, such as the dysphagia seen in this case.

A critical component of managing any steroid-dependent myopathy is the gradual tapering of the medication to minimize long-term side effects while ensuring the disease remains in remission. In this report, the patient demonstrated a favorable response to the initial treatment, evidenced by a marked reduction in creatine kinase (CK) levels within two weeks of starting therapy. Because the CK levels remained normal and her functional status improved significantly, she was able to be weaned off prednisone rapidly over a two-month period. The ability to taper steroids quickly is a positive prognostic indicator, suggesting that the inflammatory process was effectively suppressed and that the condition may have been a drug-induced inflammatory event rather than a chronic, progressive idiopathic myopathy.

The ultimate goal of treatment in IMNM is the restoration of baseline function and the normalization of biochemical markers. This patient achieved a successful clinical outcome, regaining her baseline functional status and remaining off all immunosuppressive medications for eight months. Furthermore, the normalization of her CK levels provided biochemical evidence of halted muscle necrosis. The resolution of imaging abnormalities, specifically the normalization of myocardial edema and hypokinesis on cardiac MRI and echocardiogram, further confirms the resolution of the systemic inflammatory process. This case highlights the importance of a multi-modal approach—combining aggressive initial immunosuppression, monitoring of biochemical markers like CK, and longitudinal follow-up to ensure the resolution of both muscular and secondary systemic involvement.

## Clinical Significance and Pharmacovigilance

Understanding the distinction between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is critical when managing patients on new metabolic therapies. While both conditions can present with elevated creatine kinase (CK) and muscle weakness, their clinical trajectories differ significantly. Rhabdomyolysis typically presents as an acute, toxic response to a medication, characterized by a rapid rise in CK that improves within days of drug discontinuation. In contrast, this case illustrates IMNM, which followed a more protracted, subacute course over four months. The hallmark of IMNM in this clinical context is the requirement for intensive immunosuppression, such as high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), to achieve functional recovery, rather than simple hydration and drug cessation alone.

When a patient presents with myopathy but lacks the characteristic autoantibodies (such as anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP), clinicians must remain vigilant. Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative, making laboratory tests alone insufficient for diagnosis. In this case, the muscle biopsy served as the definitive diagnostic tool, revealing necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage infiltration (CD68+), and membrane attack complex (MAC) positivity. These histological findings confirmed an inflammatory myopathy despite a negative myositis panel, highlighting the necessity of tissue biopsy in diagnosing subacute muscle weakness when serology is inconclusive.

The emergence of this case underscores the importance of pharmacovigilance in the rapidly expanding use of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists like semaglutide. While these drugs are highly effective for obesity and type 2 diabetes, they represent a new frontier for potential autoimmune triggers. By applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality criteria, this case demonstrates a "probable" link between semaglutide and IMNM based on the temporal relationship between the first dose and the onset of symptoms, the lack of alternative explanations, and the clinical response to both drug withdrawal and immunosuppression.

For clinical practice, these findings necessitate a proactive approach to patient counseling. Patients initiated on GLP-1 receptor agonists should be educated on the early warning signs of myopathy, specifically progressive proximal muscle weakness and, crucially, "red flag" symptoms like dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) or dysphonia (difficulty speaking), which may indicate involvement of the pharyngeal muscles. While the specific factors predisposing this patient to an immune-mediated reaction to semaglutide remain unknown, future clinical research must focus on identifying these genetic or environmental triggers to improve the safety profile of these widely used metabolic medications.
