Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the three key clinical hallmarks that should prompt a clinician to consider Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) in a patient presenting with new-onset diabetes?

The clinical suspicion for Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) should be high in patients presenting with new-onset diabetes who exhibit three primary hallmarks: **early age of onset** (typically before the age of 25), a **strong family history** of early-onset diabetes, and **negative autoantibodies** for type 1 diabetes.

These hallmarks are essential for differentiating MODY—a group of autosomal dominant, monogenic disorders caused by primary defects in pancreatic $\beta$-cell development and function—from other forms of diabetes. Unlike type 1 diabetes, which involves autoimmune destruction of $\beta$-cells, or type 2 diabetes, which is characterized by insulin resistance, MODY follows a Mendelian inheritance pattern. In this case, the patient’s family history provided a classic clue: his mother was diagnosed in her late 20s, his younger brother at age 20, and his maternal grandmother in her 30s, illustrating the autosomal dominant transmission typical of *HNF1A*-MODY.

Furthermore, the absence of pancreatic autoantibodies—specifically glutamic acid decarboxylase (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—is a critical diagnostic step to rule out type 1 diabetes. In this report, the combination of the patient's young age, multi-generational family history, and negative antibody status served as the primary indicators for MODY, eventually leading to the identification of a novel heterozygous *HNF1A* mutation. Recognizing these hallmarks is vital because an accurate diagnosis of MODY can fundamentally change management, potentially allowing patients to transition from insulin therapy to oral agents or GLP-1 receptor agonists, as demonstrated in this patient’s successful clinical outcome.

### [Pathophysiology] In the context of HNF1A-MODY, what is the specific pathophysiological mechanism by which mutations in the HNF1A gene lead to impaired insulin secretion?

HNF1A-MODY is the most common form of maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), characterized by an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern and a primary defect in pancreatic $\beta$-cell function. The pathophysiological mechanism of HNF1A-MODY centers on the role of the *HNF1A* gene as a transcription factor.

The *HNF1A* gene encodes a protein that is essential for the normal development and function of $\beta$-cells. Mutations in this gene—such as the novel p.N270S variant identified in this case—lead to a progressive decline in $\beta$-cell function by impairing the transcriptional regulation of several key genes. Specifically, *HNF1A* deficiency leads to:

1.  **Reduced Expression of Insulin and GLUT2:** The *HNF1A* transcription factor is required to maintain the expression of the insulin gene and the *GLUT2* gene. Because GLUT2 is the primary glucose transporter in $\beta$-cells, its diminished expression impairs the cell's ability to sense extracellular glucose.
2.  **Decreased Pyruvate Kinase Activity:** *HNF1A* mutations also result in reduced activity of pyruvate kinase, a critical enzyme in the glycolytic pathway. 

Together, these molecular defects disrupt the metabolic coupling between glucose sensing and insulin release. If the $\beta$-cell cannot efficiently transport glucose (via GLUT2) or process it through glycolysis (via pyruvate kinase), it cannot generate the necessary ATP to trigger insulin secretion. This results in the characteristic hyperglycemia and glucose intolerance observed in this patient. 

Furthermore, the report highlights a secondary but important pathophysiological consequence: the reduced expression of the *SGLT2* gene in the renal tubules. This leads to a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, which explains why patients with HNF1A-MODY often present with glycosuria even when blood glucose levels are not excessively high.

### [Pathophysiology] Why do many patients with HNF1A-MODY present with glycosuria even when their blood glucose levels are relatively low?

The clinical presentation of glycosuria in the setting of relatively low blood glucose is a hallmark of HNF1A-MODY and serves as an important diagnostic clue. This phenomenon occurs because the *HNF1A* gene encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha, a critical transcription factor expressed in several tissues, including the pancreas, liver, and kidneys.

In the kidneys, HNF1A is responsible for the transcriptional regulation of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2), which is the primary transporter responsible for reabsorbing glucose from the glomerular filtrate in the proximal tubules. Mutations in the *HNF1A* gene result in a reduced expression of these SGLT2 transporters. Consequently, the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption is significantly lowered. Because the kidneys are less efficient at reclaiming glucose from the filtrate, glucose "spills" into the urine even when plasma glucose concentrations remain below the levels typically required to cause glycosuria in patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

Recognizing this mechanism is clinically significant not only for diagnosis but also for management. As noted in this case, because the residual SGLT2 activity is already compromised, these patients may be at an increased risk for severe dehydration or metabolic complications if SGLT2 inhibitors are used, as the medication could further inhibit the limited transport capacity of the renal tubules.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating a young patient with diabetes, what are the primary biochemical findings that help differentiate MODY from Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus?

When evaluating a young patient with diabetes, the differentiation between Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) and Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) relies primarily on the assessment of islet autoantibodies and the evaluation of endogenous insulin secretion via C-peptide levels.

T1DM is an autoimmune disease characterized by the progressive destruction of pancreatic $\beta$-cells. Consequently, the presence of islet autoantibodies—specifically glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) antibodies—is a hallmark of T1DM. In contrast, MODY is a monogenic, non-autoimmune disorder resulting from a primary defect in $\beta$-cell development or function. Therefore, the absence of these autoantibodies in a patient with early-onset diabetes is a critical biochemical finding that points away from T1DM and toward a non-autoimmune etiology like MODY.

Furthermore, C-peptide levels serve as a proxy for endogenous insulin production. In T1DM, $\beta$-cell destruction typically leads to low or undetectable C-peptide. Patients with MODY, however, often maintain moderately preserved $\beta$-cell function, especially in the earlier stages of the disease. As demonstrated in this case, a glucagon stimulation test revealed a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L; since levels above 0.60 nmol/L generally indicate preserved endogenous secretion, this finding helped distinguish the patient’s condition from the profound insulin deficiency seen in T1DM. 

While these markers are foundational, they should be interpreted in the context of the clinical triad characteristic of MODY: onset before age 25, a strong autosomal dominant family history of diabetes, and the absence of significant insulin resistance. In the specific case of HNF1A-MODY, the presence of glycosuria can also be a biochemical clue, as it reflects a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption caused by the impaired expression of SGLT2.

### [Management] Why are sulfonylureas often considered a first-line pharmacological treatment for HNF1A-MODY, and how does their mechanism of action align with the underlying pathology?

Sulfonylureas are frequently employed as a first-line pharmacological treatment for HNF1A-MODY because the underlying pathology of this condition is primarily a defect in insulin secretion rather than a complete loss of $\beta$-cell mass or a primary state of insulin resistance.

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor essential for the expression of genes involved in $\beta$-cell function, specifically the insulin and GLUT2 genes, as well as those regulating pyruvate kinase activity. Mutations in HNF1A lead to a progressive decline in $\beta$-cell function, which manifests as a diminished capacity to secrete insulin in response to glucose. This is reflected in the clinical case provided, where the patient exhibited "moderately preserved yet insufficient" insulin secretion on a glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L).

The mechanism of action of sulfonylureas aligns directly with this secretory defect. Sulfonylureas act by binding to and closing potassium-sensitive ATP channels ($K_{ATP}$) on the $\beta$-cell membrane. This closure induces membrane depolarization, which opens voltage-gated calcium channels, leading to an influx of calcium and the subsequent exocytosis of insulin granules. By directly stimulating the secretory machinery of the $\beta$-cells, sulfonylureas effectively compensate for the impaired transcriptional regulation caused by the HNF1A mutation. 

While the case report highlights that GLP-1 receptor agonists can be a safer and effective alternative—particularly as $\beta$-cell function declines over time or in patients prone to hypoglycemia—sulfonylureas remain a cornerstone of therapy because they target the specific physiological "bottleneck" of HNF1A-MODY: the impaired secretory response of the pancreas.

### [Management] What is the specific clinical concern regarding the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors in patients diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY?

The specific clinical concern regarding the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors in patients with HNF1A-MODY is the risk of severe dehydration, vascular collapse, and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), which may outweigh the potential therapeutic benefits.

The underlying pathophysiology involves the role of HNF1A as a transcription factor. The HNF1A gene is responsible for the transcriptional control of several genes, including SGLT2 in the renal tubules. In patients with HNF1A-MODY, there is a primary reduction in the expression of SGLT2, which leads to a characteristically low renal threshold for glucose reabsorption and subsequent glycosuria. 

Because these patients already have a baseline reduction in SGLT2 expression, the introduction of an SGLT-2 inhibitor can lead to excessive inhibition of the remaining transporter activity. This can precipitate significant clinical complications, including severe dehydration, genital infections, and a heightened risk of metabolic decompensation like DKA. Therefore, clinicians should exercise extreme caution or avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in this specific subset of monogenic diabetes.

### [Clinical Pearls] In this case, the patient exhibited severely reduced sudomotor function. What does this finding suggest about the systemic manifestations of HNF1A-MODY beyond simple hyperglycemia?

The finding of severely reduced sudomotor function in this patient, as evidenced by electrochemical skin conductance testing, indicates significant **autonomic dysfunction**, specifically involving the small nerve fibers. 

From a clinical perspective, this finding is significant because it suggests that the systemic manifestations of *HNF1A*-MODY may extend beyond the primary pancreatic $\beta$-cell defect. While *HNF1A* is well-known for its role in regulating insulin secretion and renal glucose reabsorption (the latter explaining the glycosuria observed in this patient), it is a transcription factor expressed in various tissues, including the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. The presence of early-onset autonomic neuropathy in this patient underscores the possibility that the underlying genetic mutation may have pleiotropic effects on other organ systems or that the underlying pathophysiology leads to an earlier onset of small-fiber involvement than typically expected in non-monogenic diabetes.

For the clinician, this serves as a reminder that patients with monogenic diabetes should be evaluated for systemic complications—including autonomic and peripheral neuropathies—even in the early stages of the disease. It highlights that *HNF1A*-MODY should be conceptualized as a systemic genetic disorder rather than an isolated metabolic derangement of the pancreas.

### [Morning Report] When a novel genetic variant (not listed in ClinVar or VarSome) is identified in a patient with suspected MODY, what clinical and in silico factors should be used to determine its pathogenicity according to ACMG guidelines?

When a novel genetic variant—such as the p.N270S mutation in *HNF1A* identified in this case—is not documented in established repositories like ClinVar or VarSome, determining pathogenicity requires a multi-modal synthesis of *in silico* predictions, structural genomics, and clinical segregation analysis as outlined by ACMG guidelines.

First, **in silico computational scores** provide a high-confidence prediction of the variant’s impact on protein structure and function. In this case, a high AlphaMissense score (0.995) serves as a significant indicator of a deleterious effect on the protein's stability or biochemical activity. 

Second, **genomic and structural context** is critical. The *HNF1A* gene is a transcription factor, and its functional importance is highly localized; research indicates that the majority of pathogenic mutations are clustered within the DNA-binding domain. A novel variant situated within this domain, or adjacent to known pathogenic residues, provides strong structural evidence for its relevance to the disease phenotype.

Finally, and most importantly, **clinical segregation** serves as a cornerstone of the diagnostic process. The observation that the variant co-segregates with the autosomal dominant transmission of early-onset diabetes within the patient's family provides the definitive clinical evidence needed to support a "Likely Pathogenic" or "Pathogenic" classification.

In summary, while databases are invaluable, the classification of a novel variant in MODY relies on the convergence of high-confidence computational evidence, the variant's location in a known functional hotspot, and its demonstrated co-segregation with the clinical phenotype in a pedigree. These factors allow for a precise diagnosis that can guide targeted management, such as the transition from insulin to GLP-1 receptor agonists or sulfonylureas observed in this patient.
