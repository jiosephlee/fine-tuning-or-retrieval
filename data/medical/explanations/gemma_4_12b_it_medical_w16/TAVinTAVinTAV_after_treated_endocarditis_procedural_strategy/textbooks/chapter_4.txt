Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 4: ## Procedural Strategy and Technical Execution of the Triple Valve Implantation

The technical execution of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure requires meticulous planning to navigate the extreme anatomical constraints imposed by multiple overlapping stent frames. In this case, the interventional team utilized a dual-access strategy, employing both left radial and left femoral arterial access. This approach provides redundancy and allows for stable positioning of the pigtail catheter. Aortography performed via the pigtail catheter is a critical diagnostic step, as it allows the clinician to confirm the severity of the aortic regurgitation and, crucially, to verify that coronary flow remains unobstructed during the early stages of the procedure.

Before the implantation of the third valve, balloon pre-dilation was performed using a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing (RVP) at 180 beats per minute. RVP is a standard technique in transcatheter aortic valve procedures used to minimize cardiac motion, thereby providing a stable environment for accurate sizing and ensuring that the balloon can exert uniform pressure against the previous valve frames. In a triple-stented anatomy, pre-dilation is essential to create sufficient space for the new prosthesis and to reduce the resistance encountered during deployment.

Valve selection is a pivotal decision in sequential reinterventions. For this patient, the Acurate neo2 (size S) was selected due to its specific design characteristics: supra-annular leaflet positioning, a relatively low frame height, and favorable performance in narrow, complex anatomies. These features are particularly advantageous in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios where the available lumen diameter—measured at 13.7 mm in this case—is significantly restricted by previous frames. The supra-annular design helps maximize the effective orifice area (EOA) while the low frame height reduces the risk of obstructing the coronary ostia.

A cornerstone of the procedural strategy in this case was the "high implantation" technique. Because each successive valve adds a "neo-skirt" (a sealing mechanism at the base of the valve), there is a cumulative risk that these skirts will eventually overlap with and obstruct the coronary arteries. By deliberately implanting the third valve higher—at the level of the leaflets of the previous Evolut valve—the team achieved two goals: it fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the current stent frame to prevent them from folding inward and causing further stenosis, and it ensured the neo-skirt remained well below the coronary ostia. This strategy was essential for preserving coronary flow and maintaining a corridor for future coronary access.

Furthermore, the team prioritized commissural alignment, matching the leaflets of the new prosthesis with those of the preceding valves. Commissural alignment is intended to optimize the EOA by ensuring a symmetrical expansion of the leaflets and to minimize the mechanical burden of the multiple overlapping meshes on future coronary interventions. Following the deployment of the Acurate neo2, post-dilation was performed with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing. While intentional frame fracture was not performed, the high-pressure post-dilation provided a secondary benefit by inducing partial radial expansion of the nested Evolut frame. This maneuver was instrumental in improving the hemodynamics, as evidenced by the significant reduction in the transvalvular mean gradient from 67 mm Hg immediately post-procedure to 27 mm Hg at the one-month follow-up.
