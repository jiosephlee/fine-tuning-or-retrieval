## Fundamentals of Transcatheter Aortic Valve Intervention and Structural Deterioration

The rapid expansion of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) as a primary treatment for severe aortic stenosis has significantly improved outcomes for patients with high surgical risk. As these patients survive longer, clinical practice is increasingly shifting toward the management of "lifetime" structural valve disease. This transition introduces the challenge of structural valve deterioration (SVD), where a previously functional prosthesis begins to fail. In the context of this case, the patient’s journey from an initial 2014 TAVI to a subsequent 2022 TAV-in-TAV intervention reflects the growing clinical necessity of managing multi-stage prosthetic failure in patients who are poor candidates for open-heart surgery.

Structural valve deterioration typically occurs through mechanisms of calcification, leaflet fibrosis, or mechanical restriction. In bioprosthetic valves, these processes gradually reduce the effective orifice area (EOA) and increase transvalvular gradients, eventually leading to symptomatic stenosis. A significant and complicating driver of SVD is prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). PVE involves the colonization of a prosthetic valve by microorganisms—in this case, *Enterococcus faecalis*—forming a biofilm that can lead to leaflet destruction, abscess formation, and accelerated mechanical failure. Even after successful antimicrobial clearance, the resulting leaflet scarring and fibrosis can cause persistent hemodynamic compromise, necessitating a secondary or tertiary intervention.

To address these failures without the risks of repeated open surgery, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) technology has emerged. This procedure involves deploying a new transcatheter valve within the frame of a previously implanted bioprosthesis. The new valve frame provides structural support and a new set of leaflets, while the original prosthesis acts as a "housing." While effective, each successive TAV-in-TAV procedure increases the complexity of the anatomy, creating a "nested" configuration where multiple stent frames occupy the same space within the aortic root.

Serial reinterventions, particularly a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, present two critical technical hurdles: coronary artery obstruction and patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). Because each new valve frame adds bulk to the aortic annulus, there is a heightened risk that the valve’s "neo-skirt" (the sealing mechanism at the base of the valve) or the frame itself could occlude the coronary ostia. This risk is compounded by the "stacking" effect of multiple frames, making precise preprocedural imaging via multislice computed tomography (MSCT) essential to measure coronary-to-valve distances. Simultaneously, every additional valve reduces the available EOA. If the remaining opening is too small for the patient's body surface area, it results in PPM, which can lead to poor hemodynamic performance and persistent symptoms despite a successful technical deployment.

Ultimately, the management of such complex cases requires a strategy-driven approach. As seen in this report, the transition from a single TAVI to a triple-stented anatomy necessitates meticulous planning regarding commissural alignment—ensuring the new valve leaflets align with the previous ones to maximize flow—and high-level implantation to preserve coronary access. Understanding these fundamentals is vital for interpreting how the clinical team navigated the narrow anatomical margins and previous infectious complications to achieve a functional result for this patient.

## Clinical Progression: From Initial TAVI to Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis

The clinical course of this patient illustrates the challenges of managing structural valve disease in a high-risk patient with multiple comorbidities. The patient’s journey began in 2014 with a diagnosis of severe aortic stenosis. Given his significant medical history—including three-vessel coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)—he was deemed a high-risk candidate for traditional open-heart surgery. Consequently, he underwent a transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) using a 27 mm Lotus valve. This highlights a fundamental principle in contemporary cardiology: TAVI is often the preferred primary intervention for patients whose surgical risk profile makes conventional valve replacement prohibitive.

By late 2021, the patient experienced a significant hemodynamic decline characterized by worsening exertional dyspnea. Echocardiographic evaluation revealed severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis, with the mean transvalvular gradient escalating to 83 mm Hg and a concurrent drop in left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) to 42%. This deterioration necessitated a "redo" procedure. In May 2022, the patient underwent a TAV-in-TAV procedure, where a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis was implanted within the original Lotus frame. While the procedure was hemodynamically successful—improving the EF to 54%—it was complicated by a vascular emergency (femoral pseudoaneurysm) requiring open vascular surgery, further complicating his clinical profile.

Only three months after the second intervention, the patient’s condition shifted from mechanical failure to infectious complication. He presented with persistent fever and escalating heart failure symptoms. Clinical investigation revealed a positive blood culture for *Enterococcus faecalis*, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed severe restenosis of the prosthesis (mean gradient 35 mm Hg). These findings confirmed a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). PVE is a serious complication where bacteria colonize the prosthetic material, often leading to rapid leaflet destruction, abscess formation, or embolic events. The patient required a rigorous six-week course of intravenous ampicillin-based antimicrobial therapy, followed by oral treatment. Crucially, for the subsequent management steps, the clinical team had to confirm the complete resolution of the infection—verified by negative blood cultures and clear imaging—before considering further mechanical interventions.

Following the successful treatment of the infection, the patient entered a period of observation. However, by January 2024, he again presented with worsening prosthetic valve dysfunction. This period demonstrates a critical clinical distinction: the transition from an active infectious process to chronic structural deterioration. Although the infection was resolved, the previous episode of PVE likely contributed to permanent leaflet fibrosis and mechanical restriction. TTE revealed a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg and a markedly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm². 

When assessing such cases, it is vital to correlate clinical symptoms with objective measurements. While the patient reported subjective sensations of fatigue and dyspnea, the definitive evidence of severe prosthetic valve dysfunction was found in the significant gradient and the tiny AVA calculated via the continuity equation. This discrepancy between "feeling" symptomatic and "measuring" severe stenosis is a cornerstone of cardiac diagnostics; the goal of the subsequent TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure was to address this specific hemodynamic compromise while navigating the complex anatomy of two previously implanted frames.

## Preprocedural Planning and Diagnostic Reasoning for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) requires a rigorous shift from standard treatment protocols toward a highly individualized, strategy-driven approach. In this case, the primary diagnostic reasoning was dictated by the patient’s prohibitive surgical risk and the complex anatomical "nesting" of two previously implanted transcatheter valves. With a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and significant comorbidities—including COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding—the patient was deemed a high-risk candidate for open surgery. Furthermore, the deep integration of the existing valve frames into the aortic wall meant that any surgical intervention would likely have necessitated a high-risk total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. Consequently, the clinical team had to determine if a third transcatheter intervention was anatomically feasible and how to mitigate the inherent risks of triple-stented anatomy.

Multislice Computed Tomography (MSCT) serves as the cornerstone of preprocedural planning in complex multi-stented cases. Unlike standard TAVI planning, which focuses on the native anatomy, TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV requires a detailed assessment of the "lumen within a lumen." In this patient, MSCT was utilized to measure the minimum lumen diameter of the second prosthesis (the Evolut valve), which was found to be 13.7 mm. This measurement is critical because it defines the maximum allowable size of the third valve and helps predict the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). MSCT also allows the clinician to visualize the spatial relationship between the existing frames, the dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, and the specific height of the coronary ostia.

A significant challenge in sequential TAVIs is the cumulative "mesh burden" of overlapping frames, which can lead to two primary complications: a reduced effective orifice area (EOA) and iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction. To address these, the clinical team employed 3D modeling to assess the interaction between the frames. The goal was to ensure that the third valve would not only fit within the second but would also be positioned to maximize the EOA. This involves planning for commissural alignment—matching the leaflets of the new valve with the existing structures to optimize blood flow dynamics and minimize the risk of the new valve's fabric (the neo-skirt) obstructing the coronary openings.

The final procedural strategy was centered on determining the optimal implantation depth. Because the patient already had two overlapping frames, there was a high risk that a standard implantation would place the third valve’s neo-skirt too low, potentially occluding the coronary arteries. The team opted for a "high implantation" strategy, aiming to seat the third valve at the level of the second valve's leaflets. This approach served a dual purpose: it prevented the leaflets of the second valve from folding inward—which would have further constricted the opening—and ensured that the neo-skirt of the third valve remained above the coronary ostia. By combining these anatomical measurements and 3D modeling, the team was able to select a specific prosthesis (the Acurate neo2) and a deployment depth that balanced the need for hemodynamic improvement with the necessity of preserving future coronary access.

## Procedural Strategy and Technical Execution of the Triple Valve Implantation

The technical execution of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure requires meticulous planning to navigate the extreme anatomical constraints imposed by multiple overlapping stent frames. In this case, the interventional team utilized a dual-access strategy, employing both left radial and left femoral arterial access. This approach provides redundancy and allows for stable positioning of the pigtail catheter. Aortography performed via the pigtail catheter is a critical diagnostic step, as it allows the clinician to confirm the severity of the aortic regurgitation and, crucially, to verify that coronary flow remains unobstructed during the early stages of the procedure.

Before the implantation of the third valve, balloon pre-dilation was performed using a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing (RVP) at 180 beats per minute. RVP is a standard technique in transcatheter aortic valve procedures used to minimize cardiac motion, thereby providing a stable environment for accurate sizing and ensuring that the balloon can exert uniform pressure against the previous valve frames. In a triple-stented anatomy, pre-dilation is essential to create sufficient space for the new prosthesis and to reduce the resistance encountered during deployment.

Valve selection is a pivotal decision in sequential reinterventions. For this patient, the Acurate neo2 (size S) was selected due to its specific design characteristics: supra-annular leaflet positioning, a relatively low frame height, and favorable performance in narrow, complex anatomies. These features are particularly advantageous in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios where the available lumen diameter—measured at 13.7 mm in this case—is significantly restricted by previous frames. The supra-annular design helps maximize the effective orifice area (EOA) while the low frame height reduces the risk of obstructing the coronary ostia.

A cornerstone of the procedural strategy in this case was the "high implantation" technique. Because each successive valve adds a "neo-skirt" (a sealing mechanism at the base of the valve), there is a cumulative risk that these skirts will eventually overlap with and obstruct the coronary arteries. By deliberately implanting the third valve higher—at the level of the leaflets of the previous Evolut valve—the team achieved two goals: it fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the current stent frame to prevent them from folding inward and causing further stenosis, and it ensured the neo-skirt remained well below the coronary ostia. This strategy was essential for preserving coronary flow and maintaining a corridor for future coronary access.

Furthermore, the team prioritized commissural alignment, matching the leaflets of the new prosthesis with those of the preceding valves. Commissural alignment is intended to optimize the EOA by ensuring a symmetrical expansion of the leaflets and to minimize the mechanical burden of the multiple overlapping meshes on future coronary interventions. Following the deployment of the Acurate neo2, post-dilation was performed with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing. While intentional frame fracture was not performed, the high-pressure post-dilation provided a secondary benefit by inducing partial radial expansion of the nested Evolut frame. This maneuver was instrumental in improving the hemodynamics, as evidenced by the significant reduction in the transvalvular mean gradient from 67 mm Hg immediately post-procedure to 27 mm Hg at the one-month follow-up.

## Postoperative Outcomes, Surveillance, and Lifetime Management

The success of a complex transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) procedure is first evaluated through immediate hemodynamic assessment. In this case, the procedure was judged successful according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria, which are the standard for evaluating TAVI outcomes. While the immediate post-procedural echocardiogram confirmed the absence of aortic regurgitation (Grade 0) and showed mobile leaflets, the initial hemodynamics were challenging, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg. This high gradient indicated that while the valve was physically deployed, the three layers of overlapping frames significantly restricted the effective orifice area (EOA).

A critical consideration in multi-valve interventions is Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM). PPM occurs when the effective orifice area of the implanted prosthesis is too small relative to the patient’s body surface area, potentially leading to persistent symptoms of aortic stenosis. In this patient, the initial AVA of 0.9 cm² represented severe PPM. However, the clinical course demonstrated a favorable "settling" period; by one month, the mean gradient improved to 27 mm Hg and the AVA increased to 1.1 cm². This improvement, alongside observed left ventricular (LV) remodeling—characterized by a reduction in chamber volumes and an improved ejection fraction—suggests that while the triple-stented anatomy limits the maximum possible orifice area, the procedural strategy (high implantation and commissural alignment) successfully optimized the remaining space to achieve functional stability.

Long-term surveillance is paramount, particularly in a patient with a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). At the one-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with a stable mean gradient of 26 mm Hg and an AVA of 1.1 cm². For the clinician, the primary goals of long-term monitoring in this context are the identification of structural valve deterioration (SVD) and the detection of recurrent infection. Because the patient had completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy for *Enterococcus faecalis* and showed no clinical signs of active endocarditis on imaging, the focus shifted toward monitoring the mechanical integrity and hemodynamics of the triple-stented valve.

This case illustrates the emerging paradigm of "lifetime management" for structural valve disease. As TAVI becomes a standard of care for a wider range of patients, clinicians must move beyond thinking of the procedure as a single isolated event. Instead, each intervention must be viewed as a step in a potential series of reinterventions. This requires "procedural foresight," where the first valve choice considers the possibility of a second, and the second considers the possibility of a third. Factors such as frame height, coronary access preservation, and the expected "mesh burden" of overlapping stents must be weighed early to ensure that future interventions remain technically feasible.

Looking toward the future, the clinical team must consider the limitations of sequential TAVI. If the third valve were to fail, the risk of open surgical explantation would be prohibitively high due to the deep fixation of the prior frames into the aortic wall. This may necessitate the adoption of emerging catheter-based solutions for terminal valve failure. One such concept is the BASILICA (bioprosthetic aortic scallop intentional laceration) technique, which involves intentional leaflet laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction. While still under investigation and not yet standard for triple-stented configurations, such maneuvers represent the next frontier in managing the "end-of-the-line" complications of multi-prosthetic valve failure.
