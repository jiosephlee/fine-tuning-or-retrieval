Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the anatomical pathway by which pancreatic fluid from a posterior pancreatic injury typically migrates into the pleural cavity.

The anatomical pathway of pancreatic fluid migration in a posterior pancreatic injury involves a sequential movement from the abdominal cavity into the thoracic cavity. When a rupture occurs in the posterior portion of the pancreas—whether due to trauma, as seen in this case, or from chronic pancreatitis—the pancreatic fluid (or a pseudocyst) leaks into the **retroperitoneal space**.

Because the retroperitoneum is continuous with the mediastinum, the fluid follows the path of least resistance upward. It tracks superiorly and enters the mediastinum by passing through the diaphragm, typically via the **aortic or oesophageal hiatuses**. Once the fluid reaches the mediastinum, it breaches the pleural space to accumulate within the pleural cavity, resulting in a recurrent pleural effusion.

This pathway explains why patients with pancreatopleural fistulas (PPF) often present with "thoracic" symptoms such as respiratory distress, chest pain, and pleural effusions—a phenomenon referred to in this case as a "pulmonary mask." This clinical presentation can frequently lead to a diagnostic delay, as clinicians may focus on pulmonary pathologies (such as tuberculosis or malignancy) before considering a retroperitoneal source. Identifying the hallmark finding of significantly elevated pleural fluid amylase (typically ≥1000 IU/L) is crucial for distinguishing this condition from other causes of hemorrhagic or amylase-rich effusions.
