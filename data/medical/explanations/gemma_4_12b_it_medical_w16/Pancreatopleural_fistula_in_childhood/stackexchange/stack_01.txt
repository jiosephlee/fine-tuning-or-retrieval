Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the 'pulmonary mask' mentioned in the case report, and how does it contribute to the diagnostic delay of a pancreatopleural fistula?

The "pulmonary mask" refers to the clinical phenomenon where a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) presents primarily with thoracic symptoms—such as respiratory distress, chest pain, and recurrent pleural effusions—which overshadow the underlying pancreatic pathology. Because the patient's chief complaints are respiratory, the clinical focus is naturally directed toward identifying a pulmonary or pleural disease, effectively "masking" the source of the fluid in the abdomen.

This mask contributes to diagnostic delay by misleading the clinician toward common thoracic diagnoses. In this case, for example, the patient's recurrent effusions and family history of pulmonary tuberculosis led to an empirical course of antitubercular therapy. The underlying pathology—a fistula tracking from the tail of the pancreas into the pleural cavity—was only identified after a more detailed history revealed a prior history of blunt abdominal trauma and MRCP visualized the specific communication between the pancreatic duct and the pleural space.

To avoid this diagnostic pitfall, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for PPF in patients with persistent, recurrent, or blood-stained pleural effusions. A pleural fluid amylase level ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF and should prompt an investigation into the pancreas, even when the primary presentation is pulmonary.
