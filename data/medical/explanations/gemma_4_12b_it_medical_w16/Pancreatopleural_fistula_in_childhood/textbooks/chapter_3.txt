Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Imaging Modalities

The diagnostic journey in cases of suspected pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is often complicated by a "pulmonary mask," where the primary pathology in the abdomen produces significant thoracic symptoms. Because the patient presents with respiratory distress and pleural effusion, the initial clinical instinct is to search for a primary pulmonary cause, such as tuberculosis, malignancy, or empyema. However, a systematic approach involving biochemical analysis of the pleural fluid and multi-modal imaging is essential to shift the focus from the chest to the pancreas.

The first critical step in the diagnostic workup is the analysis of the pleural fluid. In this case, the aspiration of "dark red" fluid provided a significant clinical clue, but the biochemical markers were definitive. A pleural fluid amylase level $\geq$1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. In this patient, the amylase was markedly elevated at 6118 U/L, with a corresponding elevation in lipase (7211 U/L). When faced with high amylase levels, clinicians must differentiate between pancreatic and salivary amylase. While conditions like Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) can also result in amylase-rich pleural fluid, they typically present with salivary-type amylase. The presence of pancreatic-type amylase strongly points toward a communication with the pancreatic ductal system.

Initial imaging usually focuses on the thorax to characterize the effusion. In this report, a chest X-ray and bedside lung ultrasound were used to identify a right-sided hydropneumothorax and a large volume of pleural fluid (approximately one liter). While these modalities confirm the presence and severity of the respiratory compromise, they do not identify the etiology. To bridge the gap between a thoracic symptom and an abdominal cause, contrast-enhanced CT of the chest is the next logical step. In this case, the CT was pivotal; it revealed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas. This "tracking" finding is a classic radiological sign of a fistula, suggesting that fluid is migrating from a retroperitoneal source into the pleural cavity.

To confirm the specific anatomy of the fistula and the status of the pancreatic duct, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the preferred non-invasive imaging modality. MRCP provides high-resolution visualization of the biliary and pancreatic ducts. In this case, MRCP confirmed the diagnosis by showing a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas that communicated directly with the pancreatic duct. It also identified the specific path of the fistula through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, as well as a secondary communication with the left pleural cavity.

While MRCP is excellent for diagnosis, Endoscopic Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for both confirming the fistula and planning therapeutic intervention. Although more invasive, ERCP allows for the visualization of the ductal anatomy and the potential placement of trans-papillary stents. In pediatric cases, ERCP has shown high success rates for closing fistulas by reducing intraductal pressure. By moving systematically from pleural fluid biochemistry to advanced cross-sectional imaging, clinicians can peel back the "pulmonary mask" to identify the underlying pancreatic pathology.
