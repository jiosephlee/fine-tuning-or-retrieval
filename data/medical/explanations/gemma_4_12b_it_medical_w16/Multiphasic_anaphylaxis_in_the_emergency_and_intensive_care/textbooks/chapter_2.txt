Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Emergency Stabilization

The patient, a woman in her 60s with a history of mild allergic reactions, presented to the emergency department (ED) with acute symptoms following the ingestion of her first dose of oral clarithromycin. Her presentation highlights a critical clinical challenge: the transition from a known history of minor allergies—such as facial swelling from amoxicillin or local reactions to wasp stings—to a life-threatening systemic reaction. The sudden onset of pruritus, flushing, increased transpiration, and a sensation of throat swelling within one hour of drug ingestion is highly suggestive of an IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction. In such cases, the immediate priority is to recognize the rapid progression of symptoms and initiate life-saving interventions before the patient's condition deteriorates further.

Upon arrival at the ED, the patient exhibited the classic hallmarks of anaphylactic shock, a severe form of anaphylaxis characterized by systemic circulatory collapse. Her vital signs revealed significant hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxaemia (88% on room air), indicating that the systemic release of mediators—such as histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins—had caused profound vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, and potential airway compromise. The clinical examination further corroborated these findings with generalized erythema, urticaria, and facial swelling. Recognizing these signs as a medical emergency is paramount; the objective in the initial phase is not to determine the specific allergen but to stabilize the patient's airway, breathing, and circulation (the ABCs).

The immediate management of anaphylaxis centers on the rapid administration of intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. In this case, the patient received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via an auto-injector. Epinephrine is the first-line treatment because it acts as an alpha-1 agonist (causing vasoconstriction to increase blood pressure), a beta-1 agonist (increasing heart rate and contractility), and a beta-2 agonist (promoting bronchodilation and stabilizing mast cells). Supporting these measures, the patient was provided with high-flow supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and an initial bolus of 500 mL of normal saline to address hypoxaemia and volume depletion caused by capillary leak. While antihistamines like clemastine were also administered to manage cutaneous symptoms, they are secondary to epinephrine and do not treat the life-threatening components of anaphylactic shock.

A rapid assessment of the clinical response to these interventions is necessary to determine the need for escalating care. In this report, the patient showed a "good response," evidenced by the normalization of her blood pressure and the visible reduction of facial swelling and erythema. This stabilization confirms the effectiveness of the initial epinephrine dose and allows for a transition to a period of observation. Crucially, the patient’s clarithromycin was discontinued immediately. Removing the offending allergen is a fundamental principle of management; while the acute stabilization addresses the current physiological crisis, preventing further exposure is essential to stop the ongoing inflammatory cascade and prevent further episodes of anaphylaxis.
