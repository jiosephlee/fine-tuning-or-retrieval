Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] In an adult patient weighing over 50 kg, what is the recommended dose of intramuscular epinephrine for anaphylaxis, and what is the clinical concern regarding the widespread use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors?

For an adult patient weighing over 50 kg, the recommended dose of intramuscular (IM) epinephrine for the treatment of anaphylaxis is 0.5 mg (based on the standard guideline of 0.01 mg/kg, capped at a maximum of 0.5 mg). 

The clinical concern regarding the widespread use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—which are frequently available in the preclinical setting—is that they can lead to a potentially critical underdosing of epinephrine in adults over 50 kg. While these auto-injectors are valuable for reducing treatment hesitancy and providing rapid access to medication in non-clinical environments, the 0.3 mg dose may be insufficient to achieve a full clinical response in larger patients. Evidence suggests that patients initially receiving 0.3 mg are significantly more likely to require escalation of care, such as additional epinephrine injections, compared to those who receive the recommended 0.5 mg dose.

In this case, the 104 kg patient initially received 0.3 mg via auto-injector. While this provided a prompt response during her first episode, the subsequent multiphasic nature of her reaction highlighted the complexities of managing severe anaphylaxis, where ensuring adequate initial dosing is paramount to preventing the need for repeated interventions.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed pathophysiological mechanism for biphasic anaphylaxis, and how does the timing of these reactions typically differ from the initial exposure?

Biphasic anaphylaxis is characterized by a recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms following an initial reaction that has reached apparent resolution, notably occurring in the absence of further exposure to the inciting allergen. The proposed pathophysiological mechanism involves a delayed, secondary release of inflammatory mediators—such as histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins—from sensitized mast cells and basophils, or a sustained systemic inflammatory response following the initial insult. The case report highlights the importance of underlying mast cell disorders in these complex presentations; the patient’s potential systemic mastocytosis, which involves an abnormal increase in mast cells and excessive mediator release, serves as a clinical example of a condition that can predispose individuals to severe, multiphasic systemic reactions.

Regarding timing, biphasic reactions typically occur within the first 24 hours of the initial allergen exposure, with approximately half of these reactions manifesting within 6 to 12 hours after the initial event. However, as noted in the case report, clinical presentation can be highly variable, with some instances of symptom recurrence documented as late as 78 hours after the initial exposure. These variables necessitate a period of clinical observation following the resolution of initial symptoms, as the timing of a relapse can be unpredictable.

### [Clinical Pearls] Despite its traditional use in treating anaphylaxis, what does current evidence suggest regarding the efficacy of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic reactions?

Despite the long-standing tradition of administering corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions in anaphylaxis, current evidence does not support their efficacy for this purpose. A systematic review of 22 studies found that only one study supported the use of corticosteroids to reduce the risk of biphasic reactions, while 21 studies showed no such benefit.

Furthermore, some evidence suggests that corticosteroids may have adverse effects, including a potential increase in the likelihood of requiring intensive care or hospital admission following prehospital treatment. Consequently, most contemporary guidelines now advise against the routine administration of corticosteroids in the management of anaphylaxis. While they may still be utilized in some clinical settings due to established tradition, they should not be relied upon to prevent recurrence. The cornerstone of management remains the prompt administration of intramuscular epinephrine and appropriate supportive care.

### [Morning Report] This patient's severe multiphasic course and elevated tryptase levels point toward systemic mastocytosis. What are the key clinical and laboratory features that define this condition, and how does it predispose patients to severe allergic reactions?

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is a rare hematologic disorder characterized by the abnormal accumulation of mast cells in various tissues and organs, leading to the excessive release of inflammatory mediators. In the context of this case, the patient’s multiphasic anaphylaxis and elevated tryptase levels are hallmark indicators of an underlying mast cell disorder.

**Clinical and Laboratory Features**
The clinical presentation of systemic mastocytosis is heterogeneous and can be categorized into several domains:
*   **Cutaneous Manifestations:** Patients often experience flushing, pruritus, and urticaria, frequently triggered by physical factors (heat, pressure) or chemical triggers (medications, foods).
*   **Systemic and Organ Involvement:** Mast cell infiltration can lead to hepatomegaly or splenomegaly (as seen in this patient’s abdominal sonography) and gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain or diarrhea.
*   **Neuropsychiatric Symptoms:** Patients may present with depression, anxiety, or cognitive changes, such as the memory problems noted in this clinical report.
*   **Laboratory Markers:** The primary laboratory hallmark is an elevated serum tryptase level. While a baseline elevation (typically >20 ng/mL) suggests mast cell burden, a significant rise during an acute reaction—as observed in this patient—confirms mast cell degranulation. Diagnosis is formally established by identifying mast cell aggregates in the bone marrow or other organs, though this patient's diagnosis was limited by insufficient biopsy material.

**Pathophysiology and Predisposition to Anaphylaxis**
Systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe and complex allergic reactions through two primary mechanisms:
1.  **Increased Mast Cell Burden:** A higher total number of mast cells in the skin, mucosa, and internal organs provides a larger "reservoir" of inflammatory cells ready to respond to a trigger.
2.  **Lowered Degranulation Threshold:** In SM, mast cells are often hyper-responsive. Even a minor exposure to an allergen (such as the single dose of clarithromycin in this case) can trigger a massive, coordinated release of pre-formed mediators—including histamine, heparin, and tryptase—as well as newly synthesized leukotrienes and prostaglandins.

This hyper-responsiveness explains the **multiphasic course** observed here. Because of the high mast cell burden, the initial anaphylactic event may only partially deplete the available mediators, or the ongoing inflammatory milieu may facilitate recurrent degranulation episodes. Consequently, these patients are at high risk for severe, refractory, and recurrent anaphylaxis, necessitating a high index of clinical suspicion and prolonged observation.

### [Management] When evaluating a patient for anaphylaxis, what is the optimal timing for obtaining serial tryptase levels to accurately establish a baseline and identify peak levels during a reaction?

To accurately evaluate anaphylaxis and screen for underlying mast cell disorders, guidelines recommend obtaining at least three serial tryptase samples at specific intervals. The optimal timing is as follows:

1.  **Initial Sample:** This should be collected as soon as feasible without delaying life-saving treatment (such as the administration of epinephrine).
2.  **Peak Sample:** A second sample should be obtained 1–2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) after the onset of symptoms. This timing is critical because serum tryptase levels typically peak during this window following mast cell degranulation.
3.  **Baseline Sample:** A third sample must be taken a minimum of 24 hours after full clinical resolution of the anaphylactic episode to establish the patient's baseline tryptase level.

The clinical reasoning for this serial testing is to differentiate between acute mast cell activation and underlying mast cell pathology. By comparing the peak level to the 24-hour baseline, clinicians can better characterize the severity of the reaction. Furthermore, a significantly elevated baseline tryptase level (as seen in the patient in this case report) is a key indicator of systemic mastocytosis. 

In the presented case, the failure to adhere to these recommended intervals during the initial anaphylactic episodes hindered the early identification of the patient's potential systemic mastocytosis. For clinicians, maintaining this strict sampling schedule is essential for both the diagnostic workup of the acute event and the long-term management of the patient's underlying condition.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How does a clinician differentiate between 'biphasic anaphylaxis' and 'sustained anaphylaxis' caused by ongoing allergen absorption (e.g., from a slow-release medication or residual food)?

To differentiate between biphasic anaphylaxis and sustained anaphylaxis caused by ongoing allergen absorption, the clinician must evaluate two primary factors: the presence of an asymptomatic interval and the status of the allergen source.

**Biphasic anaphylaxis** is defined by a period of "apparent symptom resolution"—a window where the patient is clinically stable and asymptomatic—followed by a recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms in the absence of further allergen exposure. Pathophysiologically, this is thought to represent a delayed release of inflammatory mediators or a secondary wave of mast cell and basophil degranulation. In the provided case, while the patient experienced a multiphasic course, the initial trigger was a single oral dose of clarithromycin, which complicates the distinction but highlights the challenge of managing recurrence when the primary exposure has already occurred.

In contrast, **sustained anaphylaxis** (or recurrent anaphylaxis due to ongoing absorption) occurs when symptoms persist or recur without a clear period of clinical resolution. This is typically driven by the continuous entry of the allergen into the systemic circulation. Examples include the ingestion of slow-release medications, residual food remaining in the gastrointestinal tract, or a continuous intravenous infusion of a triggering agent. 

**Clinical Reasoning for Differentiation:**
1.  **Clinical Course:** If the patient remains symptomatic or shows progressive worsening despite epinephrine, the clinician should first rule out and mitigate ongoing exposure (e.g., stopping a continuous infusion or ensuring no further ingestion). If the patient achieves a full clinical response, remains stable for several hours, and then suddenly relapses, biphasic anaphylaxis is the more likely diagnosis.
2.  **Source Identification:** The clinician must actively investigate the "source" of the allergen. In the case report, the patient took only one dose of clarithromycin, which points away from ongoing absorption of the primary drug and toward a multiphasic/biphasic mechanism. 
3.  **Monitoring Implications:** For sustained reactions, management focuses on the removal of the trigger. For biphasic reactions, because the trigger is no longer present, management focuses on prolonged observation (traditionally 4–6 hours, though this varies) and risk-stratification, as there are currently no reliable predictors to determine which patients will experience a relapse.
