Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Imaging and Differential Diagnosis

The diagnostic evaluation of large abdominal masses during pregnancy is complicated by the physiological expansion of the uterus and the relative limitations on imaging modalities. In this case, the clinical presentation of acute pain and dyspnoea necessitated a rapid but cautious diagnostic pathway. Ultrasound (US) served as the primary imaging tool, revealing two large unilocular cystic masses. A critical diagnostic challenge in such cases is differentiating between adnexal masses, such as ovarian cysts or endometriomas, and uterine leiomyomas that have undergone cystic degeneration. While the upper mass (18 cm) appeared somewhat independent, the lower mass (9.5 cm) was notably adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. The presence of cystic degeneration within a myoma can mimic the appearance of an adnexal cyst on ultrasound, as both can present as fluid-filled structures with regular margins and peripheral vascularization.

When ultrasound provides insufficient clarity regarding the exact origin or relationship of a mass to the uterus, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is an essential next step. In pregnancy, MRI is a safe and highly effective modality provided it is performed without gadolinium contrast, which is generally avoided due to potential risks to the fetus. In this patient, non-contrast MRI was utilized to better localize the lesions. The MRI revealed thick walls (1.4 cm) and focal hyperintense solid components, which are important markers for characterizing the complexity of the masses. Crucially, MRI helped determine the continuity of the caudal mass with the posterior leiomyoma. However, the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus and the sheer size of the masses hindered the identification of a connecting stalk for the upper mass, leaving its exact origin—whether ovarian or uterine—ambiguous.

The interpretation of serum tumor markers, specifically CA-125, requires a nuanced understanding of pregnancy physiology. CA-125 levels typically exhibit a "bimodal" trend, rising during the first and third trimesters and remaining lower during the second trimester. In this case, the patient’s CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL was higher than the expected median for the second trimester. While isolated measurements are often non-diagnostic due to these physiological fluctuations, a rising trend in the second trimester can provide a clinical hint toward pathological processes, such as endometrioma or, less commonly, malignancy. In this patient, the slight elevation favored an endometrioma for the upper cyst but did not provide a definitive diagnosis, necessitating a correlation with imaging and clinical findings.

The final differential diagnosis was narrowed down to decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The upper mass’s appearance and the slight CA-125 elevation pointed toward a decidualised endometrioma. Conversely, the lower mass's anatomical continuity with a known posterior myoma strongly suggested a myomatous origin. The diagnostic reasoning in this case relied on integrating these conflicting cues: the physical symptoms (dyspnoea caused by mechanical compression), the imaging characteristics (thick walls and solid components), and the anatomical limitations imposed by the enlarging uterus. This highlights a common clinical dilemma in obstetric surgery where the goal is to differentiate between an adnexal pathology and a complex uterine mass to determine the safest surgical approach.
