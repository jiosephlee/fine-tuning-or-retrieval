Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A young adult presents with a constellation of progressive pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and pancytopenia. After excluding common autoimmune, infectious, and nutritional etiologies, which category of rare genetic disorders should be strongly considered in the differential diagnosis?

In a young adult presenting with a multisystemic constellation of progressive pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, pancytopenia, and signs of premature aging—especially after excluding common autoimmune, infectious, and nutritional etiologies—**telomere biology disorders (TBDs)** should be strongly considered in the differential diagnosis.

TBDs are a group of rare genetic syndromes caused by Mendelian defects in genes responsible for maintaining telomere length, such as *TERT*, *TERC*, *DKC1*, and *PARN*. Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences that cap the ends of chromosomes; they protect genomic integrity by preventing degradation. In TBDs, defective telomere maintenance leads to premature telomere attrition, which triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis in rapidly dividing cell populations. This pathophysiology explains the hallmark involvement of the bone marrow (leading to pancytopenia or bone marrow failure), the liver, and the lungs (leading to fibrosis), as well as systemic features of accelerated aging.

This case illustrates a "cryptic" presentation of a TBD. While dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is the prototypical syndrome—classically defined by a mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—TBDs can manifest in adults without these primary features. Instead, they may present with isolated organ-specific involvement, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or hepatic fibrosis. The identification of a monoallelic *PARN* mutation in this patient highlights the emerging association between *PARN* variants and pulmonary fibrosis, as well as their role in the broader spectrum of telomere-related diseases.

When clinical suspicion is raised by these multisystem findings, the diagnostic gold standard involves genetic testing (such as whole-exome sequencing) and flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to measure telomere length. Early recognition is critical, as it guides genetic counseling and may influence management strategies, including the consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for progressive bone marrow failure.
