Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] What is the clinical rationale for using plasma exchange (PEX) as a 'bridging' therapy in a patient diagnosed with CM-HUS before they can begin eculizumab treatment?

The clinical rationale for using plasma exchange (PEX) as a "bridging" therapy in complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) is to provide rapid, temporary stabilization of the patient's clinical status while the definitive treatment—complement blockade with eculizumab—is initiated.

In the setting of acute thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), patients can present with life-threatening complications, including severe microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), profound thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI). While PEX is the primary treatment for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) due to its ability to remove large molecules like von Willebrand factor and ADAMTS13 inhibitors, its role in CM-HUS is secondary. In CM-HUS, PEX works by physically removing circulating complement fragments (such as C5a), inflammatory cytokines, and potentially autoantibodies that drive the acute inflammatory cascade and endothelial damage. 

In this case, the patient exhibited a severe clinical course characterized by sudden isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia. The initiation of PEX allowed for immediate management of these symptoms. This is evidenced by the patient's return of spontaneous diuresis following the final PEX session, which provided a critical window of clinical stabilization.

Furthermore, "bridging" with PEX may be necessitated by the clinical management of the underlying trigger. In this patient, the diagnosis of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia presented a therapeutic dilemma: while eculizumab is the gold standard for CM-HUS, its use during active bacteraemia carries a significant risk of uncontrolled infection due to the inhibition of terminal complement components. PEX provides a vital bridge, stabilizing the patient's haematology and renal function while clinicians ensure the infection is controlled (e.g., awaiting negative blood cultures) before safely commencing eculizumab induction. 

Ultimately, while PEX provides transient relief by clearing circulating mediators, it does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation. As noted in the report, sustained haematological and renal recovery in this patient was only achieved once eculizumab was initiated to provide targeted blockade of the complement pathway.
