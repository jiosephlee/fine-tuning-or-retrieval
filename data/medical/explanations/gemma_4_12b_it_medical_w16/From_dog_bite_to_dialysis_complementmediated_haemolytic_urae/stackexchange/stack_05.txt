Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] What is the primary mechanism of action of eculizumab, and what specific infectious risk must clinicians monitor for when initiating this therapy in a patient with a history of encapsulated bacterial infections?

Eculizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that acts by binding to the complement component C5. By inhibiting the cleavage of C5 into its active fragments, C5a (a potent anaphylatoxin) and C5b, eculizumab prevents the assembly of the terminal membrane attack complex (MAC). In the context of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), this blockade is essential to arrest the uncontrolled complement-mediated endothelial damage and subsequent thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) that leads to acute kidney injury and haemolysis.

When initiating eculizumab, clinicians must specifically monitor for life-threatening infections caused by encapsulated bacteria, most notably *Neisseria meningitidis*. Because the terminal complement pathway is a critical component of the host defense required to opsonize and clear encapsulated organisms, inhibiting C5 significantly increases a patient's susceptibility to overwhelming systemic infection. 

In this case, the patient’s presentation with *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—an encapsulated gram-negative rod—highlights the clinical paradox of complement dysregulation: while the patient's own complement activation triggered the TMA, the same pathway is necessary to clear the underlying infection. To mitigate the risk of eculizumab-associated sepsis, it is standard practice to ensure patients are up-to-date on meningococcal vaccinations (as seen with the administration of Menveo and Bexsero in this case) and, in some settings, to provide antimicrobial prophylaxis before and during complement blockade.
