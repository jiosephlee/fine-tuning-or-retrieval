Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Pathophysiology] In a patient presenting with sepsis, what are the primary clinical and laboratory features that distinguish Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) from Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA)?

In the clinical setting of sepsis, distinguishing between Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) and Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) is critical, as their underlying pathophysiologies and management strategies differ significantly. While both conditions involve microvascular thrombosis, they can be differentiated by specific clinical and laboratory hallmarks.

**Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)**
DIC is characterized by systemic activation of the coagulation cascade, leading to widespread fibrin deposition and the subsequent consumption of clotting factors and platelets. In the early phase of this patient's presentation, her condition was consistent with DIC secondary to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. The primary clinical features of DIC include systemic coagulopathy and a high risk of hemorrhage. In this case, the patient exhibited "diffuse skin bleeding with ecchymoses" and "multiple haematomas," which are hallmark signs of the factor consumption and consumption coagulopathy inherent to DIC. Laboratory findings in DIC typically show prolonged prothrombin time (PT) and partial thromboplastin time (PTT), as well as low fibrinogen levels.

**Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA)**
TMA is characterized by the formation of microthrombi within small vessels, leading to mechanical destruction of erythrocytes as they pass through the obstructed vasculature. The hallmark of TMA is **Microangiopathic Haemolytic Anaemia (MAHA)**. While the patient initially presented with a coagulopathy consistent with DIC, her clinical picture shifted as the sepsis began to improve. She developed a "sudden isolated drop in haemoglobin" despite no evidence of bleeding, "persistently low platelets," and "undetectable haptoglobin." 

The definitive laboratory differentiator in this case was the peripheral blood smear, which revealed **15%–20% schistocytes**. Schistocytes (fragmented red blood cells) are the diagnostic cornerstone of TMA and are not typically the primary feature of DIC unless there is concomitant severe microangiopathic hemolysis. Furthermore, the renal biopsy provided definitive evidence of TMA, showing "ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescent formation," distinguishing it from the more generalized coagulopathy of DIC.

**Clinical Reasoning Summary**
To distinguish these in a septic patient:
1.  **Clinical Presentation:** DIC often presents with signs of active bleeding (ecchymoses, haematomas) due to factor consumption. TMA presents with the triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI).
2.  **Laboratory Markers:** DIC is diagnosed via coagulation parameters (PT, PTT, fibrinogen). TMA is diagnosed by evidence of mechanical hemolysis—specifically schistocytes on a peripheral smear, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and low haptoglobin—often with near-normal coagulation parameters once the initial septic insult has stabilized.
3.  **Differential Diagnosis:** In the setting of TMA, it is essential to rule out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) by measuring ADAMTS13 activity (which was normal in this patient) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS) via stool PCR.
