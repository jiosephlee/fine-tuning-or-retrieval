Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## The Zoonotic Trigger: Capnocytophaga canimorsus

The clinical journey of this patient began with a common domestic incident—a dog bite—which served as the portal of entry for *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. To understand the patient’s rapid progression from a localized injury to systemic collapse, it is necessary to understand the unique biology of this pathogen. *C. canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that typically resides as part of the normal oral flora in dogs and cats. While it is generally commensal in these animals, it can become an opportunistic and highly aggressive pathogen in humans. Human infection is relatively rare but is well-documented following bites, scratches, or even close physical contact with infected animals.

The clinical spectrum of *C. canimorsus* infection in humans is remarkably broad, which can complicate early diagnosis. While some patients may present with relatively isolated symptoms such as meningitis, others experience fulminant sepsis. The patient in this case experienced the latter extreme: a rapid progression toward septic shock, characterized by high fever, malaise, and systemic inflammation. In severe cases, the pathogen can trigger multi-organ failure and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). The patient’s initial presentation—including mottled extremities, lactic acidosis, and severe coagulopathy—is a classic manifestation of the profound systemic inflammatory response and microvascular compromise induced by *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia.

The pathophysiology of *C. canimorsus*-induced sepsis involves a combination of direct tissue invasion and a systemic host response. The bacteria can trigger a cascade of inflammatory mediators that lead to the activation of the coagulation system. This explains the patient's early development of DIC, where widespread activation of coagulation leads to the formation of microthrombi, contributing to her initial organ dysfunction and mottled skin changes. It is also important to note that while *C. canimorsus* can cause severe systemic illness, its mortality rate can approach 30%, underscoring the need for aggressive early intervention with broad-spectrum antibiotics.

A significant challenge in managing this infection is the laboratory diagnosis. *C. canimorsus* is a fastidious organism, meaning it has complex nutritional requirements and grows very slowly in standard culture media. This can lead to significant delays in identification, often allowing the patient's condition to deteriorate before the specific pathogen is confirmed. In this case, the patient was initially treated with broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam to cover suspected dog-associated pathogens while awaiting cultures. The eventual confirmation of *C. canimorsus* seven days after admission highlights the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for zoonotic pathogens in patients with rapid sepsis following animal contact, and emphasizes the growing role of molecular methods, such as real-time nanopore sequencing, in overcoming the limitations of traditional culture.

## Clinical Evolution: Distinguishing Sepsis-Related DIC from TMA

The initial presentation of this patient provides a classic example of how severe sepsis can create a "noisy" clinical picture that may obscure underlying secondary complications. Upon admission, the patient exhibited the hallmarks of septic shock: profound systemic inflammation, metabolic acidosis with elevated lactate, refractory hypoglycemia, and multi-organ dysfunction including acute kidney injury (AKI) and pancytopenia. In the context of a suspected bacterial infection—exacerbated by a dog bite—the presence of coagulopathy and low platelet counts strongly points toward disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). DIC is a systemic process where widespread activation of the coagulation cascade leads to the simultaneous formation of fibrin clots throughout the vasculature and the consumption of platelets and clotting factors. In the early stages of this case, the clinical focus was appropriately directed at stabilizing the patient's hemodynamics and treating the primary infection with broad-spectrum antibiotics.

A critical component of clinical reasoning in complex cases is identifying the "pivot"—the point where a patient’s clinical course deviates from the expected trajectory of the primary disease. In this case, the pivot occurred after the patient showed initial clinical improvement from antibiotic therapy. While the systemic inflammatory markers and coagulopathy began to stabilize, she suddenly developed isolated, progressive hemolysis and persistent, refractory thrombocytopenia. This discordance is a major red flag: if the primary sepsis were the only driver of the hematological derangements, one would expect the platelet count and hemoglobin levels to improve in tandem with the resolution of the infection. Instead, the emergence of worsening hemolysis in a stabilizing septic patient suggests a secondary, superimposed pathology, specifically a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

To confirm the transition from sepsis-induced DIC to a TMA like haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS), clinicians must look for specific laboratory indicators of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). Unlike the generalized consumption seen in DIC, MAHA is characterized by the mechanical destruction of red blood cells as they pass through small vessels obstructed by microthrombi. This is evidenced by a triad of findings: a significant drop in hemoglobin without an increase in reticulocytes, a rise in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and a drop in haptoglobin (a protein that binds free hemoglobin). The definitive "gold standard" for identifying MAHA on a laboratory level is the presence of schistocytes—fragmented red blood cell pieces—on a peripheral blood smear. In this patient, the identification of 15%–20% schistocytes provided the necessary evidence to shift the diagnostic focus toward TMA.

The complexity of this case lies in how severe sepsis can "mask" the early development of complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). Because sepsis itself causes AKI, thrombocytopenia, and elevated LDH, these symptoms can overlap almost perfectly with the hallmarks of TMA. In the early days of hospitalization, the presence of pancytopenia and renal failure was easily attributed to the "noise" of septic shock and DIC. It was only when the septic "noise" began to quieten that the specific "signal" of CM-HUS became visible. For the clinician, this highlights the importance of continuous reassessment; when a patient fails to achieve a full haematological recovery despite successful treatment of the primary infection, a secondary microvascular process must be high on the differential diagnosis.

## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Diagnosis of TMA

To understand the clinical progression of this case, it is first necessary to define the overarching syndrome: thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). TMA is a clinical entity characterized by the formation of microvascular thrombi, which leads to a triad of hallmark findings: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and multi-organ dysfunction—most frequently manifesting as acute kidney injury (AKI). Because several distinct pathologies can present with this triad, the diagnostic workup must systematically exclude other common causes of TMA to arrive at the correct diagnosis.

The primary goal in an adult presenting with TMA is to differentiate between thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin-producing *Escherichia coli* hemolytic uraemic syndrome (STEC-HUS), and complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). In this case, TTP was the first major differential. TTP is caused by a severe deficiency in the enzyme ADAMTS13, which is responsible for cleaving von Willebrand factor. A diagnostic threshold of less than 10% ADAMTS13 activity is typically required to confirm TTP. This patient’s activity was measured at 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP and shifting the diagnostic focus toward other forms of TMA. Similarly, STEC-HUS—often triggered by specific enteric bacteria—was considered but excluded through a negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin and the absence of a prodromal gastrointestinal illness.

During the diagnostic process, laboratory nuances such as the Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) can complicate the clinical picture. The patient’s initial DAT was positive for IgG, which might suggest autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA). However, in the context of a patient with schistocytes (fragmented red blood cells) on a peripheral smear, the presence of MAHA is much more likely. Furthermore, the DAT can yield false positives due to recent medication use (such as the penicillin administered for the dog bite) or prior blood transfusions. When the repeat DAT was negative, it reinforced that the hemolysis was driven by mechanical destruction in the microvasculature (TMA) rather than an antibody-mediated process.

Pathological confirmation provided the definitive evidence of TMA. A renal biopsy is a critical tool when the clinical diagnosis remains uncertain. In this patient, the biopsy revealed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli, and fibrin thrombi, along with scattered crescent formation. These findings are classic histological markers of TMA, indicating that the kidney damage was caused by microvascular occlusion and subsequent ischaemia rather than primary glomerulonephritis or simple sepsis-induced tubular injury.

Finally, interpreting complement studies in the context of CM-HUS requires careful clinical reasoning. A common misconception is that CM-HUS must present with low complement levels (C3 or C4). However, in many patients, these levels may remain within the normal range even during an acute episode of TMA. In this case, the normal C3 and C4 levels did not rule out CM-HUS; instead, the diagnosis was supported by the rapid clinical response to eculizumab (a terminal complement inhibitor) and the exclusion of other TMAs. The later finding of reduced alternative pathway activity was interpreted as a likely consequence of treatment rather than a baseline diagnostic finding. Together, these steps—ruling out TTP and STEC-HUS, confirming TMA via biopsy, and observing response to complement blockade—form the diagnostic backbone of the case.

## Pathophysiology of Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS)

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), historically referred to as atypical HUS (aHUS), is a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) characterized by the systemic overactivation of the alternative complement pathway (ACP). Unlike Shiga toxin-associated HUS (STEC-HUS), which is driven by bacterial toxins, or thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is caused by a protease deficiency (ADAMTS13), CM-HUS is fundamentally a disorder of complement regulation. In the clinical context of this case, the patient presented with the hallmarks of TMA—microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI)—following a *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection, which served as the catalyst for this systemic dysregulation.

The alternative complement pathway is designed to provide a rapid, "always-on" surveillance system to identify and neutralize pathogens. Under normal physiological conditions, the ACP maintains a low level of continuous activity, known as "tick-over," where C3 is spontaneously hydrolyzed. This process is strictly governed by a suite of regulatory proteins that prevent the pathway from attacking host cells. These regulators include Factor H (the primary fluid-phase regulator), Factor I, Factor B, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46). When these regulatory "brakes" are defective—either due to genetic mutations or acquired factors—the C3 convertase enzyme remains active, leading to the continuous cleavage of C3 and the subsequent formation of the C5 convertase. This results in the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) on host endothelial cells, particularly in the renal microvasculature, leading to inflammation, thrombosis, and tissue damage.

The development of CM-HUS often follows the "second hit" theory of disease. Many individuals may carry subclinical genetic variants or predispositions in complement regulatory genes (such as Factor H) that do not manifest as disease in daily life. However, an external physiological stressor—the "second hit"—can push the system beyond its compensatory capacity. In this case, the *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia acted as the trigger. The immune system's attempt to clear the encapsulated bacteria activated the complement system; in a host with insufficient regulatory capacity, this activation became uncontrolled, transitioning from a protective defense to a destructive systemic process that caused the patient's rapid progression to anuria and TMA.

This creates a significant clinical paradox: the complement system is essential for host defense against pathogens like *C. canimorsus*, yet its dysregulation is the direct cause of the patient’s multi-organ failure. This explains why the patient’s clinical course was so complex; while antibiotics successfully addressed the primary infection, the "fire" of complement activation continued to damage the endothelium. Furthermore, CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion. Because the alternative pathway is independent of the classical pathway, standard laboratory tests for C3 and C4 levels are often normal during an acute episode, as seen in this patient. This necessitates a high index of suspicion for CM-HUS when a patient presents with TMA and fails to respond to standard treatments for sepsis or other TMA-inducing conditions.

Distinguishing between hereditary complement deficiencies and infection-triggered CM-HUS is vital for long-term management. Hereditary forms typically involve high-penetrance mutations and often manifest earlier in life. However, as demonstrated in this case, CM-HUS can occur in immunocompetent adults without detectable pathogenic genetic variants, suggesting that triggers can occasionally overwhelm even a "normal" complement system. The management principle highlighted here is that early identification of this pathway dysregulation—evidenced by the rapid response to eculizumab, a C5 inhibitor—is the most critical factor in preventing irreversible renal damage and achieving haematological remission.

## Therapeutic Management and Clinical Dilemmas

The management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) requires a rapid transition from general supportive care to specific, targeted immunomodulation. In the early stages of this patient’s presentation, the clinical priority was the management of septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). This involved aggressive fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam), and blood product support. However, as the clinical picture evolved to include persistent microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and anuria despite resolving sepsis, the diagnostic focus shifted toward thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Once the diagnosis of CM-HUS was suspected, the therapeutic goal moved toward inhibiting the dysregulated alternative complement pathway.

In cases of suspected TMA, plasma exchange (PEX) is often initiated as a "bridging therapy" while awaiting definitive results for ADAMTS13 activity or specialized complement assays. While PEX is the cornerstone of treatment for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), its role in CM-HUS is secondary; it works by removing circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators, providing transient stabilization rather than correcting the underlying complement dysfunction. In this case, PEX provided a period of clinical stabilization, evidenced by the return of spontaneous diuresis, but it was the subsequent initiation of eculizumab that facilitated sustained haematological remission and renal recovery.

Eculizumab is the standard of care for CM-HUS, functioning as a monoclonal antibody that binds to and inhibits terminal complement component 5 (C5). By blocking C5, eculizumab prevents the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), thereby halting the complement-mediated destruction of the vascular endothelium. For patients with CM-HUS, the timing of eculizumab initiation is the primary determinant of prognosis. Early intervention is critical because irreversible renal damage, such as severe acute tubular necrosis and glomerular scarring, can occur rapidly. The patient in this report achieved partial renal recovery because the blockade was initiated while there was still viable tissue to salvage, highlighting the importance of a low threshold for starting complement inhibition in the setting of TMA.

A significant clinical dilemma arises when managing CM-HUS in the context of active infection, as seen with this *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. Because terminal complement components are essential for the host's defense against encapsulated bacteria (such as *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*), administering eculizumab can theoretically increase the risk of life-threatening infections. To navigate this, clinicians must balance the risk of end-organ failure from TMA against the risk of overwhelming sepsis. In this case, the team minimized this risk by delaying eculizumab induction until the blood cultures were negative, ensuring the primary infection was controlled before neutralizing the complement system.

To further mitigate the risk of opportunistic infection during complement blockade, rigorous prophylactic measures are mandatory. This patient received meningococcal vaccinations (Menveo and Bexsero) and a course of prophylactic antibiotics (Apocillin). These measures are standard protocols to protect the patient from encapsulated pathogens while their primary immune defense is suppressed. This case underscores that while CM-HUS treatment is highly effective, it requires a sophisticated, multi-layered approach that integrates rapid diagnostic reasoning, precise timing of targeted therapy, and proactive measures to manage the inherent risks of immunosuppression.
