Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Clinical Presentation and Chronicity Analysis

This case study illustrates the diagnostic challenge posed by Chiari malformation type I (CMI) when the initial presentation is dominated by non-specific neurological and respiratory symptoms over a protracted period. The patient, a woman in her early 40s, presented with a history spanning 14 years, which resulted in a delayed diagnosis despite invasive investigations latically. Understanding this timeline is critical for interpreting the secondary symptoms, specifically how a traumatic event may have unmasked a structural anomaly rather than caused it directly. The initial onset of symptoms occurred 14 years prior to this evaluation, coinciding with a minor motor vehicle accident, yet the development of neurological deficits was progressive rather than immediate, highlighting the spectrum of symptomatic CMI manifestation.

The headache characteristics are classic for posterior fossa compression but often lead to misdiagnosis. The patient reported a 14-year history of constant, pressure-like occipital and vertex headaches localised to a coin-sized area. A distinctive clinical feature identified was scalp hypersensitivity or allodynia, where the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. The patient noted that cutting her hair short alleviated pain from 6/10 to 3/10, a finding that, while subjective, correlates with the compression of the greater occipital nerve secondary to brainstem expansion or softening changes in the posterior fossa. Clinically, while the headache was not explicitly exacerbated by Valsalva manoeuvres, the allodynia serves as a significant neurological red flag pointing toward the vertex and occipital nerve territories, which are often involved in Chiari syndromes due to the tethering of the posterior fossa contents.

Concurrent with the headache was a chronic recovery of choking episodes that began 18 months prior to the accident and continued for 18 years. These episodes affected both solids and liquids equally, indicating a central or bulbar neuropathy rather than a mechanical obstruction of the larynx or oesophagus. Fundamental to the diagnostic reasoning here is the failure of standard endoscopy and laryngoscopy, which were reported as normal. Normally, swallowing difficulties (dysphagia) should prompt immediate investigation, yet the normal physical findings suggest that the pathology is peripheral to the upper aerodigestive tract and likely central. The impact on quality of life was profound, as the choking episodes "interrupting meals" and the fear of choking led to significant social withdrawal and avoidance of restaurants or gatherings.

The respiratory component was initially dismissed as anxiety but was objectively corroborated through home monitoring. The patient documented asymptomatic oxygen desaturation ranging from 70% to 80% during sleep and when distracted. This occurrence of "silent" hypoventilation is a critical diagnostic clue for CMI. In typical acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, patients often experience dyspnoea. In this case, the lack of sensation (airs hunger) during hypoxaemia suggests a specific brainstem dysfunction where the afferent interoceptive pathways to the cortex—often running through damaged or compressed fibres—are interrupted. Consequently, the drive to breathe in response to hypoxia is absent without the conscious awareness of distress. This physiology explains the lack of spontaneous effort to recover from hypoventilation episodes, distinguishing it from primary pulmonary pathology or cardiac events.

Although the trajectory linked the symptoms to the motor vehicle accident, the latency period suggests a complex etiology. The 14-year span implies that the Chiari malformation was likely a congenital or pre-existing condition that became symptomatic after the trauma or when compensatory CSF dynamics shifted. Chronicity analysis reveals that for over a decade, the patient endured neurological morbidity attributed to "nummular headache" or "anxiety" before the rare inclusion of central respiratory manifestations forced a neurological reconsideration. This case underscores the necessity of reviewing historical imaging in patients with latency between trauma onset and symptom diagnosis, as previously overlooked structural deficits can account for progressive neurological decline. Additionally, the psychological burden of living with undiagnosed chronic respiratory and motility disorders highlights the need for comprehensive history taking that includes psychosocial dynamics when physical symptoms appear intractable to medical interventions.

## Neurophysiology of Respiratory Dysfunction in Chiari Malformation

Understanding the pathophysiology of respiratory dysfunction in Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) requires a synthesis of structural neurological deficits and brainstem functional disruption, as demonstrated in this patient with significant structural asymmetry. Clinically, CMI is defined by the caudal displacement of cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum, but the disease spectrum involves the host response to a volumetric mismatch between fossa volume and neural structures. In this case, a detailed re-review of a previously normal head CT revealed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction. This hind-point obstruction is critical because the posterior fossa has limited compliance; when the cerebellar tonsils herniate (noted as 11 mm descent in the current MRI), they occupy space that should be reserved for the 4th ventricle and spinal CSF outflow channels. This crowding results in tunneled compression of the foramen magnum, mechanically impeding CSF flow during the respiratory cycle, which is when the head moves in the posterior direction and intracranial pressure fluctuates. This dynamic obstruction is what precipitates symptoms, rather than the 11 mm descent alone, and sets the stage for medullary compression.

The mechanism of acquired central hypoventilation, often termed 'Ondine’s curse' in this context, involves direct compression of brainstem respiratory centers. While CMI is frequently associated with congenital tunneling, this patient’s history of motor vehicle accident and subsequent unmasking symptoms over 14 years suggests an acquired or exacerbated secondary component. The brainstem medulla and pons, where automatic respiratory drive is modulated, are susceptible to distortion from this posterior fossa crowding. In a patient with significant desaturations to 70–80% without distress, the pathophysiology implies that the automatic control mechanisms (emerging from the ventral respiratory group) are suppressed or disrupted, while the sympathetic drive to dilate airways or initiate peripheral compensation is absent. This differentiates the acquired central apnea pattern from primary pulmonary disease, as pulmonary imaging and function tests were normal in this case. The core neurophysiological insult is the interference with paraxial cardiorespiratory reflexes, often mediated through disruption of trigeminal and glossopharyngeal afferents that modulate brainstem output.

A distinctive and often confusing feature of this condition, illustrated vividly by this patient, is the dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing. Patients with CMI typically preserve the cortical pathways mediating coughing, swallowing, and the conscious act of breathing when awake and calm. However, the automatic inspiratory drive during sleep or unconscious states is compromised by the brainstem compression. In the absence of this drive, desaturation can occur in 6.7% or more of populations, but pure central sleep apnoea is rare unless the compression is severe. The profound absence of dyspnea sensation (air hunger) during these hypoxic episodes is a crucial diagnostic clue. Breathlessness is a sensation of interoception dependent on afferent signals (nociceptive and chemoreceptive) transmitted to the cortical insular cortex. In this condition, the brainstem dysfunction interrupts these afferent pathways before their arrival at the cortex, meaning the patient is physiologically hypoxic without any conscious sensory awareness. This explains how a patient can experience violent coughing upon waking without recognizing the duration of hypoxia occurring during sleep, as observed with the multiple choking episodes that ended in the airway but no dyspnoea.

Finally, the bulbar symptoms of choking are attributed to lower cranial nerve (IX, X, and XII) involvement rather than a peripheral airway pathology. The case presented a history of interference with laryngeal sensation, pharyngeal closure mechanisms, and the coordination of the soft palate. Normal endoscopies and laryngoscopies were performed because the pathology is central: the bulbar dysfunction arises from pressure on the motor nuclei of the lower cranial nerves and associated nerve pathways within the pontomedullary junction. This compression impairs the cricopharyngeal muscle function (cricopharyngeal achalasia) and the motor phrenic nerve output that coordinates breathing. In approximately 15–26% of CMI patients, these cranial nerve palsies lead to drooling or choking episodes upon swallowing. The specific presentation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, with normal GI endoscopic findings, strongly points to a neurogenic cause, as distinct from mechanical obstruction or gastro-esophageal reflux. Recognizing that the symptoms stem from central brainstem compression, rather than an upper airway lesion, is vital for determining that the only potential cure lies in posterior fossa decompression to resume normal CSF dynamics and relieve the cranial nerve burden.

## Diagnostic Approach and Imaging Interpretation

The diagnostic pathway for this patient illustrates a critical principle in clinical reasoning: when visceral symptoms persist despite negative organ-specific evaluations, anatomical defects in the central nervous system must be considered. In the case of the woman presenting with recurrent choking and neurological headaches, the initial diagnostic thrust correctly followed standard protocols for bulbar dysfunction by systematically excluding gastrointestinal and structural airway pathology. Recognizing the value of historical imaging review is essential, as the re-analysis of a routine trauma scan twelve years prior revealed the anatomical defect that had been unmasked only during her current symptomatic evaluation. Furthermore, interpreting sleep studies in the context of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) requires understanding the distinction between central and obstructive events and recognizing the limitations of polysomnography when performed with reduced parameters.

The initial workup began with an extensive evaluation of the respiratory tract, aiming primarily to rule out gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) as the cause of her choking symptoms. While laryngopharyngeal reflux can cause aspiration of liquids, the patient's normal results during multiple flexible laryngoscopies and upper gastrointestinal endoscopies provided evidence against structural mechanical defects or significant esophageal obstruction. Similarly, the absence of diurnal variation and the normal response to maximal acid suppression effectively excluded GERD-driven aspiration. These exclusionary findings, particularly the normal pH studies and endoscopies, allowed the medical team to pivot away from a metabolic or gastroenterological diagnosis and toward a central neurological etiology. This is a key teaching point for future practitioners: when anatomical obstruction is repeatedly disproven, the function of adjacent cranial nerves (particularly IX and X) should be scrutinized for signs of compression rather than emphasized solely for baseline health.

Comparable pulmonary evaluation further isolated the pathology away from primary lung disease. The forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) were preserved at 98% and 96% predicted respectively, indicating that the patient's capacity for gas exchange is normal when breathing under resting conditions. Normal arterial blood gas analysis (pH 7.41, PaCO2 42 mm Hg) on room air further confirmed the absence of hypoxemia or hypercapnia during waking hours. Consequently, standard chest imaging and pulmonary function testing ruled out pneumonia, interstitial lung disease, or restrictive lung diseases, which would present with concurrent desaturation and hypoxia. This significant step in the diagnostic algorithm serves to highlight that the patient's physiological reserves are intact, making the symptomatic episodes—characterized by profound desaturation to 70%–80%—an anomaly of controlled breathing rather than a compromise of pulmonary mechanics. The normal spirometry results essentially confirmed that the mystery lay not in the lungs, sino in the command to breathe.

The pivotal discovery in this patient's diagnostic journey was the retrospective re-evaluation of historical imaging. While the 14-year-old non-contrast CT of the head was initially interpreted as normal during the original workup for unrelated trauma, a detailed recount reveals a crowded foramen magnum and loss of CSF space. This finding is critical because it often requires a secondary look at archived data to uncover structural subtleties. In many cases, the diagnosis of CMI relies on the presence of crowding at the craniocervical junction that may not be easily visualized without precise imaging orientation. This demonstrates the importance of reviewing longitudinal imaging whenever a new neurological syndrome emerges, as historically normal scans may now provide the necessary anatomical pathway to linking chronic headaches with intermittent desaturation episodes. Without this historical arrow evidence, the MRI findings might have been viewed as incidental.

Polygraphy interpretation presents another layer of diagnostic reasoning. Type III sleep study results demonstrated a total of 11 events comprising a total apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) of 6.0, which is technically in the mild range. However, the predominance of central events at 72% was the most significant finding, distinguishing this case from the classic primary central sleep apnoea or oxygen desaturations seen in neuromuscular disorders. The clinical correlation between these central events and the extensive desaturation episodes documented over home monitoring was the key to recognizing acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine's curse). It is crucial to note the methodological limitations of the Type III study, which lacks EEG monitoring. This absence necessitated reliance on respiratory effort, airflow, and heart rate, leading to a potential calculation error where total recording time may have included periods of wakefulness rather than true sleep, potentially underestimating the severity. Despite the numerical AHI of 6.0, the presence of profound blood gas desaturations without corresponding dyspnoea indicated a failure of automatic brainstem control rather than a failure of airflow mechanics. The predominance of central events validated the hypothesis that the brainstem's respiratory centers were under pressure, prompting further high-contrast imaging.

Definitive diagnosis was confirmed through current MRI of the brain and cervical spine, demonstrating a clear 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent through the foramen magnum. This measurement exceeds the 5 mm diagnostic threshold for symptomatic Chiari Malformation Type I. This descent was associated with compression of the lower cranial nerve nuclei and the respiratory centers within the medulla oblongata. The interaction between the tonsillar herniation and the brainstem explains the physical impossibility of sustained breathing control in certain states. The MRI results validated the historical CT findings and provided the anatomical localization necessary to explain the patient's clinical presentation. The presence of an 11 mm displacement confirms that the structural variant was not merely an asymptomatic anatomical abnormality but a pathological compression affecting the rostroventral medulla. This structural diagnosis justifies the differential markers discussed in the literature, validating the clinical decision to monitor for potential surgical intervention should symptoms deteriorate. Thus, the diagnostic logic flowed from peripheral exclusions (normal lungs, throat) to historical radiological discovery (old CT) to detailed mapping of the pathology (MRI), culminating in a unified understanding of CMI presenting as acquired central hypoventilation.

## Management Strategies and Surgical Considerations

The management of symptomatic Chiari malformation type I (CMI) in this patient involved a clinical decision-making process that balanced definitive surgical correction against conservative monitoring, driven by the complex neuroprotective risks of intervention and the patient's personal autonomy. The initial recommendation for surgical intervention was posterior fossa decompression, a procedure designed to correct the volumetric mismatch between the neural structures and the available space in the posterior fossa and restore normal cerebrospinal fluid outflow dynamics. This surgical route is considered definitive for symptomatic CMI as it may reverse respiratory symptoms and reduce brainstem compression, potentially resolving the acquired central hypoventilation pattern observed in the patient's sleep study and hypoxic episodes.

Despite this recommendation, treatment selection required negotiation with the patient's goals and risk profile. The patient ultimately opted for conservative management while contemplating surgical options, implementing specific lifestyle changes to mitigate symptoms without immediate operative exposure. This approach involved dietary modifications to address choking episodes and, significantly, continuing the practice of cutting her hair short, which she observed provided relief for the vertex allodynia. These coping strategies highlight that immediate surgical decision-making is not always necessary, particularly when other symptoms are manageable or the risk of surgical morbidity outweighs the need for immediate correction, provided the condition remains stable.

Given the patient's choice to defer surgery, a structured monitoring protocol was established to track the natural history of the condition and identify indications for future intervention. This plan included monthly clinical assessments to evaluate neurological status, quarterly overnight oximetry to monitor oxygenation trends, and annual MRI surveillance to assess cerebellar tonsillar position. The clinical team acknowledged that clinical deterioration at any point could necessitate surgical intervention, thereby maintaining a safety net while respecting the patient's current stability. This case illustrates the importance of individualised decision-making in managing neurological hyperscycling, where the long-term probability of surgical success must be weighed against the short-term decline of patient quality of life and anxiety regarding neurosurgeon intervention.

## Clinical Lessons and Long-term Prognosis

This case demonstrates the critical importance of prioritizing a comprehensive neurological workup when presenting symptoms do not align with standard cardiovascular or pulmonary etiologies. The diagnostic journey in this patient highlights a significant pitfall in modern clinical practice: the tendency to attribute recurrent unexplained respiratory distress and hypoxia to anxiety or social factors without sufficient structural imaging review. In cases of chronic vertical headache and bulbar dysfunction, clinicians must actively search for structural anomalies in the craniocervical junction. As illustrated in this report, the revisiting of historical imaging, such as a CT scan from the patient's remote past medical record, often provides the key diagnostic clue that previous interpretations missed. Failure to correlate current symptoms with potential aetiological triggers, such as prior head trauma, can lead to prolonged delays in diagnosis. Consequently, unexplained dysphagia or choking that affects both solids and liquids equally, particularly when associated with nocturnal events, mandates consideration of neurological causes before settling on a gastroenterological or otolaryngological diagnosis.

The distinction between Chiari malformation type I (CMI) and acquired central hypoventilation syndrome must be approached through understanding the anatomical versus genetic mechanism of the hypoxia. While congenital central hypoventilation syndrome is associated with genetic mutations like PHOX2B presenting with persistent lack of automatic drive from birth, CMI presents as a secondary form acquired through structural compression. In this case, the underlying pathology was not intrinsic to the respiratory drive's genetic programming but secondary to brainstem compression caused by cerebellar tonsillar descent. Patients with CMI typically preserve voluntary breathing mediated by cortical pathways, but automatic breathing controlled by medullary centres fails during sleep or distraction. This specific phenotype explains the asymptomatic oxygen desaturation observed in the patient during an airport flight when external distractions removed the need for voluntary compensation, effectively distinguishing the acquired pathological nature from congenital idiopathic forms.

A structured, long-term surveillance strategy is essential for patients with suspected CMI who are managed conservatively or undergoing preoperative optimization. Given the patient’s choice to defer surgical intervention, a rigorous monitoring protocol prevents rapid deterioration and future airway compromise. This involves monthly clinical assessments to track neurological stability, specifically monitoring for signs of worsening lower cranial nerve function, alongside quarterly overnight oximetry to capture desaturation events missed by daytime evaluations. Crucially, annual magnetic resonance imaging surveillance is required to assess the progression of tonsillar descent relative to the foramen magnum and to monitor for the development of syringomyelia. This structured approach ensures that the definitive treatment, posterior fossa decompression, is offered timely if the patient experiences clinical deterioration or heralding signs of neurological decline.

Vertex symptoms, specifically scalp allodynia and the relief of pain associated with cutting hair, serve as a significant but often underutilized clinical sign in the evaluation of CMI. The patient experienced intense pressure and discomfort in the vertex, localised to a coin-sized area, which was notably alleviated by shortening hair. This phenomenon aligns with the concept of occipital neuralgia secondary to brainstem compression or the 'burning vertex syndrome' described in spinal literature. The clinical lesson here is that dermatological hypersensitivity and trigeminal/visceral pain syndromes should prompt neurological imaging review in the context of chronic headaches, even when dermatological patterns traditionally suggest tension-type or migraine disorders. Recognizing this specific pain colloquium allows for earlier identification of CMI in patients with atypical headache presentations where standard vascular imaging may be unrevealing.

Limitations in sleep diagnostic technology can lead to underestimation of the severity of sleep-disordered breathing in Chiari cases. In this scenario, the forcing of a Type III sleep study was compromised by the lack of EEG monitoring, which skews the apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI) calculation by including wakefulness in the denominator. This methodological limitation meant the index was likely lower than the true severity of sleep disturbance during deep sleep cycles, potentially masking the gravity of the central hypoventilation. Future clinical decision-making requires clinicians to demand Type I polysomnography with video recording and end-tidal capnography when structural brainstem pathology is suspected. Additionally, prospective studies should utilize validated instrumentation to quantitate these symptoms in CMI populations, preventing the reliance on preliminary data that may suggest mild results while patients remain critically symptomatic in their daily activities.
