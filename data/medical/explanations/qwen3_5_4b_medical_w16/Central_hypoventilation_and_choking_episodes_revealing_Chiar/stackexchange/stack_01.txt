Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Morning Report] A patient presents with recurrent choking episodes and unexplained hypoxia with normal pulmonary function tests. What is the most likely unifying neurological diagnosis in this context, and why would laryngoscopy and esophagoscopy have been non-diagnostic?

The most likely unifying neurological diagnosis is **Chiari malformation type I (CMI)**. In this context, the recurrent choking and unexplained hypoxia represent **neurogenic dysfunction** secondary to brainstem compression, rather than structural airway or esophageal pathology. Laryngoscopy and esophagoscopy remain non-diagnostic because the lower cranial nerve nuclei (IX, X, and XII) and the medullary respiratory centers are compressed by tonsillar descent, impairing the neuromuscular coordination of swallowing and automatic respiration without causing anatomical narrowing visible on direct visualization.

In the provided case, the patient presented with a 14-year history of occipital headaches and a 7-year history of bulbar symptoms, alongside documented oxygen desaturations up to 70%–80% without respiratory distress. The key to diagnosis was the retrospective review of a historical CT scan from 14 years post-accident, which revealed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space, confirmed later by MRI with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent. Here, the pathology is central: the brainstem compression disrupts phrenic and bulbar inputs, leading to oropharyngeal dysphagia and acquired central hypoventilation (Ondine’s curse). Consequently, normal pulmonary function tests and normal endoscopy should not rule out CMI in a patient with unexplained choking or hypoxia; instead, assessing for structural brainstem compression via neuroimaging is the critical diagnostic standard.
