Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 2: ## Neurophysiology of Respiratory Dysfunction in Chiari Malformation

Understanding the pathophysiology of respiratory dysfunction in Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) requires a synthesis of structural neurological deficits and brainstem functional disruption, as demonstrated in this patient with significant structural asymmetry. Clinically, CMI is defined by the caudal displacement of cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum, but the disease spectrum involves the host response to a volumetric mismatch between fossa volume and neural structures. In this case, a detailed re-review of a previously normal head CT revealed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction. This hind-point obstruction is critical because the posterior fossa has limited compliance; when the cerebellar tonsils herniate (noted as 11 mm descent in the current MRI), they occupy space that should be reserved for the 4th ventricle and spinal CSF outflow channels. This crowding results in tunneled compression of the foramen magnum, mechanically impeding CSF flow during the respiratory cycle, which is when the head moves in the posterior direction and intracranial pressure fluctuates. This dynamic obstruction is what precipitates symptoms, rather than the 11 mm descent alone, and sets the stage for medullary compression.

The mechanism of acquired central hypoventilation, often termed 'Ondine’s curse' in this context, involves direct compression of brainstem respiratory centers. While CMI is frequently associated with congenital tunneling, this patient’s history of motor vehicle accident and subsequent unmasking symptoms over 14 years suggests an acquired or exacerbated secondary component. The brainstem medulla and pons, where automatic respiratory drive is modulated, are susceptible to distortion from this posterior fossa crowding. In a patient with significant desaturations to 70–80% without distress, the pathophysiology implies that the automatic control mechanisms (emerging from the ventral respiratory group) are suppressed or disrupted, while the sympathetic drive to dilate airways or initiate peripheral compensation is absent. This differentiates the acquired central apnea pattern from primary pulmonary disease, as pulmonary imaging and function tests were normal in this case. The core neurophysiological insult is the interference with paraxial cardiorespiratory reflexes, often mediated through disruption of trigeminal and glossopharyngeal afferents that modulate brainstem output.

A distinctive and often confusing feature of this condition, illustrated vividly by this patient, is the dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing. Patients with CMI typically preserve the cortical pathways mediating coughing, swallowing, and the conscious act of breathing when awake and calm. However, the automatic inspiratory drive during sleep or unconscious states is compromised by the brainstem compression. In the absence of this drive, desaturation can occur in 6.7% or more of populations, but pure central sleep apnoea is rare unless the compression is severe. The profound absence of dyspnea sensation (air hunger) during these hypoxic episodes is a crucial diagnostic clue. Breathlessness is a sensation of interoception dependent on afferent signals (nociceptive and chemoreceptive) transmitted to the cortical insular cortex. In this condition, the brainstem dysfunction interrupts these afferent pathways before their arrival at the cortex, meaning the patient is physiologically hypoxic without any conscious sensory awareness. This explains how a patient can experience violent coughing upon waking without recognizing the duration of hypoxia occurring during sleep, as observed with the multiple choking episodes that ended in the airway but no dyspnoea.

Finally, the bulbar symptoms of choking are attributed to lower cranial nerve (IX, X, and XII) involvement rather than a peripheral airway pathology. The case presented a history of interference with laryngeal sensation, pharyngeal closure mechanisms, and the coordination of the soft palate. Normal endoscopies and laryngoscopies were performed because the pathology is central: the bulbar dysfunction arises from pressure on the motor nuclei of the lower cranial nerves and associated nerve pathways within the pontomedullary junction. This compression impairs the cricopharyngeal muscle function (cricopharyngeal achalasia) and the motor phrenic nerve output that coordinates breathing. In approximately 15–26% of CMI patients, these cranial nerve palsies lead to drooling or choking episodes upon swallowing. The specific presentation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, with normal GI endoscopic findings, strongly points to a neurogenic cause, as distinct from mechanical obstruction or gastro-esophageal reflux. Recognizing that the symptoms stem from central brainstem compression, rather than an upper airway lesion, is vital for determining that the only potential cure lies in posterior fossa decompression to resume normal CSF dynamics and relieve the cranial nerve burden.
