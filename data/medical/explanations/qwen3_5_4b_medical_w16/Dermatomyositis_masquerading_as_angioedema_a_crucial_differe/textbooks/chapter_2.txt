Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Reasoning: Differentiating Angioedema from Inflammatory Neck Swelling

The clinical challenge of distinguishing inflammatory neck swelling from angioedema was central to the diagnostic reasoning in this case, requiring a careful synthesis of imaging, history, and exclusionary testing. The patient's initial presentation in the Emergency Department featured severe neck swelling and a consult focus on Ludwig stroke (Ludwig’s angina), driven by the CT finding of diffuse neck oedema involving strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. However, the clinical picture lacked the systemic signs of severe infection; the patient was afebrile, not tachypnoeic, and oxygen saturation remained stable despite the significant CT abnormalities. This lack of sepsis markers, combined with the afebrile state, initially diverted attention away from infectious causes but compounded the diagnostic difficulty when the patient was transferred for further evaluation. The initial impression of a primary otolaryngological disorder was not settled until the patient was referred to a quaternary center with ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgery services. This transfer was necessary because the localized nature of the oedema, unresponsive to initial resuscitation logic, and the presence of proximal muscle weakness required a broader differential assessment that general acute care constraints often preclude. The diagnostic path moved logically from the ED to the ENT, then to clinical immunology, where the lack of structural or infective causes persisted, leading to the suspicious "idiopathic angioedema" label despite the patient denying ACE inhibitors or similar medications.

Clinically, several "red flags" for inflammatory myopathy outweighed the suspicion of histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. Beyond the classic presentation of neck swelling, the patient presented with dysphagia, which was corroborated by the flexible nasendoscopy note of increased secretions and coughing, as well as subsequent aspiration pneumonitis identified on CT. This airway muscle involvement is a critical differentiator, as idiopathic angioedema does not typically produce the profound proximal muscle weakness (4/5 hip flexor power) found in this individual. Furthermore, subtle cutaneous findings that often escape the initial glance were critical clues. During examination at the immunology review, the clinician noted subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema. Crucially, nailfold capillaroscopy performed at presentation identified proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages along with avascular areas. These subtle vascular changes serve as early indicators of connective tissue inflammation and, in this context, signaled that the muscle and skin involvement were not merely allergic but inflammatory. The patient’s denied history of angioedema and the absence of structural lung issues (specifically vocal cord abnormalities noted endoscopically) further painted a picture of an acquired inflammatory process rather than an allergic mediator response.

The systematic exclusion of alternative inflammatory and infectious causes provided the foundation for pivoting toward rheumatology. Initially, the concern for infection—such as cellulitis or deep neck space infection—was addressed through the lack of fever, lack of leukocytosis (white cell count was 9.1×109/L), and negative aspiration findings. In the context of suspected angioedema, the exclusion of hereditary and acquired forms was paramount. Standard workup prior to the rheumatologic diagnosis included normal C3 and C4 complement levels and normal C1 esterase function and quantification, effectively ruling out hereditary angioedema or acquired angioedema associated with complement deficiencies. Drug-induced angioedema was also excluded as the patient denied recent prescriptions of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors. The logical pivot occurred when a comprehensive clinical profile revealed physical findings inconsistent with pure angioedema and inconsistent with infection. While the inflammatory markers (CrP 55.6 mg/L) remained high, the presence of these normal complement levels and the absence of drug triggers necessitated an investigation into autoimmune etiology, specifically prompting the request for a creatine kinase level.

The pivot to rheumatology was confirmed by the unexpected serology and laboratory abnormalities that directly explained the swelling. The request for creatine kinase (CK) was the most expedient and high-yield maneuver, revealing an elevation of 16,184 U/L. This massive elevation immediately flagged rhabdomyolysis or myopathy, which, given the muscular weakness and neck oedema, suggested myositis rather than simple oedema. Subsequent investigations including ANA (1:1280 speckled pattern), ANCA testing, and a specific myositis antibody panel strongly pinpointed the diagnosis. The presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies was diagnostic, confirming the inflammatory etiology of the "angioedema" was actually intense muscle inflammation affecting the superficial cervical soft tissues. The muscle biopsy provided the final ex-vivo confirmation, showing perifascicular atrophy and complement deposition in capillaries. This diagnostic reasoning highlighted that the "swelling" was not exudative fluid accumulation from vascular leak (angioedema) but rather the physical expansion of inflamed, edematous muscle tissue (myositis), a distinction that dictated the urgent initiation of high-dose immunosuppression rather than antihistamines.
