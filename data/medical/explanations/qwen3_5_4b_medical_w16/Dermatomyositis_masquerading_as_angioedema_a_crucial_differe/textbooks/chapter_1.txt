Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Pathophysiology and Clinical Spectrum of Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) represent a heterogeneous group of rare autoimmune disorders characterized by immune-mediated necrotizing inflammation of skeletal muscle, encompassing dermatomyositis (DM) and polymyositis. Dermatomyositis, with an estimated incidence of 10 cases per million population, is distinguished by the involvement of cutaneous markers alongside proximal muscle weakness, with a bimodal distribution of onset notably in children and adults aged 45 to 64. Pathophysiology involves the production of autoantibodies against various nuclear antigens that lead to endothelial damage and complement-mediated myocyte necrosis, a process confirmed by the deposition of the membrane attack complex C5b-9 in the endomysial capillaries during specific biopsies. In the clinical scenario provided by Chan et al., the profound elevation in creatine kinase to 16,184 U/L at presentation indicates significant muscle breakdown, a hallmark of myositis. While these antibodies typically cause muscle weakness, the presence of cutaneous inflammation suggests a broader autoimmune attack on muscle-rich fascial groups, which can lead to bulky soft tissue inflammation even when frank dystrophy is not immediately apparent.

A critical distinction in understanding the spectrum of dermatomyositis is the presence of specific autoantibodies, with anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) being associated with a distinct clinical phenotype compared to anti-Mi-2 or anti-Jo-1. Unlike other myositis antibodies, SAE1 positivity has been linked to increased risks of interstitial lung disease, dysphagia, and malignancy, although it does not always manifest with erythematous eruption patterns like Haller's purple. In this case, the patient displayed a strongly positive SAE1 antibody titre (signal intensity 173) against a background of normal immunoglobulin levels and negative myositis specific antibodies other than SAE1. This specific immunoprofile correlates with severe tissue inflammation and dysphagia, yet the standard cutaneous signs of dermatomyositis, such as Gottron’s papules or heliotrope eruption, were initially absent. The clinical presentation often mirrors a systemic inflammatory cascade where the muscle inflammation drives the cutaneous findings, leading to a diagnosis of overlap or early presentation where skin changes may develop after the onset of internal inflammatory processes.

One of the most pedagogically significant aspects of this case involves the misdiagnosis of severe neck swelling as angioedema. True angioedema is primarily mediated by histamines or bradykinins, whereas the swelling observed in dermatomyositis is largely driven by localized tissue edema secondary to fascial inflammation and muscle involvement. In the patient described, the inflammatory myositis resulted in edema extending to the retropharyngeal space and involving the strap muscles, mimicking a deep neck space infection or angioedema. This "pseudo-angioedema" is not mediated by mast cells or vascular permeability changes in the same manner as allergic or hereditary angioedema. Consequently, standard diagnostic workups for histaminergic causes, such as C1 esterase quantification and complement levels, may remain non-diagnostic, necessitating the consideration of inflammatory myopathies when neck swelling is refractory to standard treatment or accompanied by subtle myopathic signs on capillaroscopy.

Beyond the muscle and skin, the clinical spectrum of SAE1-associated dermatomyositis frequently involves systemic complications that dictate further management and monitoring. As detailed in the case, the patient exhibited severe pharyngeal dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis and a restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests, reflecting the known high association between dysphagia and interstitial lung disease in this antibody population. Furthermore, the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines consider dysphagia and specific antibody profiles (such as SAE1) as high-risk factors for underlying malignancy, necessitating extensive screening including CT imaging and gastrointestinal evaluation. The initial presentation of hip flexor weakness and myalgia in this 40-year-old male served as a clue to the systemic nature of the disease, prompting a transition from infection and allergy management to systemic immunosuppression. Ultimately, recognizing that soft tissue swelling in this context arises from muscle inflammation rather than histamine-mediated permeability is essential for timely diagnosis, preventing unnecessary radiation or antibiotic therapy for infections, and initiating the targeted immunosuppression required to restore muscle function and prevent respiratory compromise.
