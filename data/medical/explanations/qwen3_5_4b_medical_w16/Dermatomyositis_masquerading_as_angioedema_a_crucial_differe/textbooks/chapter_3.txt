Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 3: ## Confirmatory Workup: Serology, Imaging, and Perifascicular Biopsy

In the clinical evaluation of dermatomyositis, confirming the diagnosis requires a hierarchical testing strategy that differentiates inflammatory myopathy from non-immune etiology and other autoimmune conditions. While the presentation of neck swelling and proximal weakness suggested a significant systemic process, distinguishing between histaminergic-mediated angioedema, infection, and inflammatory myopathy necessitated specific diagnostic modalities. The confirmation of the patient’s SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis relied on integrating blood-based markers, radiographic imaging, and histopathological features.

The initial tier of serological screening always involves assessing the status of muscle enzymes, specifically creatine kinase (CK). In inflammatory myopathies, increased CK levels result from the exposure of intracellular enzymes due to muscle fiber necrosis and membrane permeability shifts during acute inflammation. For this patient, the elevated CK of 16,184 U/L was highly specific for significant muscle injury and served as a critical pivot point in the diagnostic reasoning. Such profound elevation is typically inconsistent with simple dermatological edema or infection alone, immediately elevating the suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy. Although the urinary myoglobin screen was negative and renal function remained normal, ruling out renal impairment, the CK level necessitated a push for further immunological characterization beyond initial metabolic workup.

Following the initial marker of muscle injury, serology was directed toward identifying the causative autoantibody. In this case, a myositis antibody panel was the definitive tool. The patient tested positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, a highly specific marker for a subset of inflammatory myositis. This distinguishes the pathology from other common subsets such as anti-Mi-2 dermatomyositis or anti-MDA-5 disease, which have distinct clinical correlates such as gastrointestinal telangiectasias or rare cutaneous vasculitis, respectively. While a diagnosis of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy could still be considered SAE1-negative, the patient’s significantly positive signal intensity (173, above the normal range of <35) strongly correlated with a Myositis Association Assessment. Conversely, the panel was negative for anti-TIF1-γ antibodies, which are characteristic of anti-synthetase syndrome and are often more strongly associated with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary hypomontage. Thus, the serology confirmed the specific molecular pathology while helping to exclude differential diagnoses such as myositis associated with malignancy or drug-induced myopathy.

Imaging was essential not only to assess muscle involvement but also to evaluate the airway and pulmonary tract, which were at risk due to extrathoracic muscle involvement. A CT of the chest identified multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules and peribronchial consolidation, consistent with aspiration pneumonitis likely secondary to the patient’s dysphagia. This risk factor prompted early consideration of malignancy screening, as dermatomyositis is an "oncologic marker" that increases cancer risk. Furthermore, Flurodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) was crucial in the differential diagnosis. It demonstrated diffuse FDG accumulation within multiple muscle groups, confirming active inflammation. The PET findings helped rule out malignancy by showing that metabolically active disease was localized to the soft tissues and muscles rather than a systemic nodal or primary tumor burden, thereby supporting a primary inflammatory etiology.

Histopathology remains the gold standard for diagnosing dermatomyositis. The patient underwent a thigh muscle biopsy, which provided the definitive histological features necessary to confirm the diagnosis. Histology revealed foci of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate, myophagocytosed myofibres with a perifascicular distribution. Perifascicular atrophy is particularly characteristic of dermatomyositis and results from progressive ischemia at the angular vacuoles, which compromises capillary function. Confirmatory findings included immunohistochemistry showing deposition of the membrane attack complex component C5b-9 in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This complement deposition is a hallmark of the hypersensitive vascular damage in dermatomyositis and is virtually pathognomonic in the absence of other factors like diabetes or renal disease, contrasting with the perifascicular sparing seen in other myopathies. Together, the elevated CK, specific anti-SAE1 serology, PET-positive muscle activity, and characteristic histopathology provided a comprehensive, multi-modal confirmation of dermatomyositis, guiding immediate aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.
