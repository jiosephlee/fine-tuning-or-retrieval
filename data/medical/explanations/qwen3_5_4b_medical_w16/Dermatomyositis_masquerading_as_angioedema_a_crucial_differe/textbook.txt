Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Pathophysiology and Clinical Spectrum of Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) represent a heterogeneous group of rare autoimmune disorders characterized by immune-mediated necrotizing inflammation of skeletal muscle, encompassing dermatomyositis (DM) and polymyositis. Dermatomyositis, with an estimated incidence of 10 cases per million population, is distinguished by the involvement of cutaneous markers alongside proximal muscle weakness, with a bimodal distribution of onset notably in children and adults aged 45 to 64. Pathophysiology involves the production of autoantibodies against various nuclear antigens that lead to endothelial damage and complement-mediated myocyte necrosis, a process confirmed by the deposition of the membrane attack complex C5b-9 in the endomysial capillaries during specific biopsies. In the clinical scenario provided by Chan et al., the profound elevation in creatine kinase to 16,184 U/L at presentation indicates significant muscle breakdown, a hallmark of myositis. While these antibodies typically cause muscle weakness, the presence of cutaneous inflammation suggests a broader autoimmune attack on muscle-rich fascial groups, which can lead to bulky soft tissue inflammation even when frank dystrophy is not immediately apparent.

A critical distinction in understanding the spectrum of dermatomyositis is the presence of specific autoantibodies, with anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) being associated with a distinct clinical phenotype compared to anti-Mi-2 or anti-Jo-1. Unlike other myositis antibodies, SAE1 positivity has been linked to increased risks of interstitial lung disease, dysphagia, and malignancy, although it does not always manifest with erythematous eruption patterns like Haller's purple. In this case, the patient displayed a strongly positive SAE1 antibody titre (signal intensity 173) against a background of normal immunoglobulin levels and negative myositis specific antibodies other than SAE1. This specific immunoprofile correlates with severe tissue inflammation and dysphagia, yet the standard cutaneous signs of dermatomyositis, such as Gottron’s papules or heliotrope eruption, were initially absent. The clinical presentation often mirrors a systemic inflammatory cascade where the muscle inflammation drives the cutaneous findings, leading to a diagnosis of overlap or early presentation where skin changes may develop after the onset of internal inflammatory processes.

One of the most pedagogically significant aspects of this case involves the misdiagnosis of severe neck swelling as angioedema. True angioedema is primarily mediated by histamines or bradykinins, whereas the swelling observed in dermatomyositis is largely driven by localized tissue edema secondary to fascial inflammation and muscle involvement. In the patient described, the inflammatory myositis resulted in edema extending to the retropharyngeal space and involving the strap muscles, mimicking a deep neck space infection or angioedema. This "pseudo-angioedema" is not mediated by mast cells or vascular permeability changes in the same manner as allergic or hereditary angioedema. Consequently, standard diagnostic workups for histaminergic causes, such as C1 esterase quantification and complement levels, may remain non-diagnostic, necessitating the consideration of inflammatory myopathies when neck swelling is refractory to standard treatment or accompanied by subtle myopathic signs on capillaroscopy.

Beyond the muscle and skin, the clinical spectrum of SAE1-associated dermatomyositis frequently involves systemic complications that dictate further management and monitoring. As detailed in the case, the patient exhibited severe pharyngeal dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis and a restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests, reflecting the known high association between dysphagia and interstitial lung disease in this antibody population. Furthermore, the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines consider dysphagia and specific antibody profiles (such as SAE1) as high-risk factors for underlying malignancy, necessitating extensive screening including CT imaging and gastrointestinal evaluation. The initial presentation of hip flexor weakness and myalgia in this 40-year-old male served as a clue to the systemic nature of the disease, prompting a transition from infection and allergy management to systemic immunosuppression. Ultimately, recognizing that soft tissue swelling in this context arises from muscle inflammation rather than histamine-mediated permeability is essential for timely diagnosis, preventing unnecessary radiation or antibiotic therapy for infections, and initiating the targeted immunosuppression required to restore muscle function and prevent respiratory compromise.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Differentiating Angioedema from Inflammatory Neck Swelling

The clinical challenge of distinguishing inflammatory neck swelling from angioedema was central to the diagnostic reasoning in this case, requiring a careful synthesis of imaging, history, and exclusionary testing. The patient's initial presentation in the Emergency Department featured severe neck swelling and a consult focus on Ludwig stroke (Ludwig’s angina), driven by the CT finding of diffuse neck oedema involving strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. However, the clinical picture lacked the systemic signs of severe infection; the patient was afebrile, not tachypnoeic, and oxygen saturation remained stable despite the significant CT abnormalities. This lack of sepsis markers, combined with the afebrile state, initially diverted attention away from infectious causes but compounded the diagnostic difficulty when the patient was transferred for further evaluation. The initial impression of a primary otolaryngological disorder was not settled until the patient was referred to a quaternary center with ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgery services. This transfer was necessary because the localized nature of the oedema, unresponsive to initial resuscitation logic, and the presence of proximal muscle weakness required a broader differential assessment that general acute care constraints often preclude. The diagnostic path moved logically from the ED to the ENT, then to clinical immunology, where the lack of structural or infective causes persisted, leading to the suspicious "idiopathic angioedema" label despite the patient denying ACE inhibitors or similar medications.

Clinically, several "red flags" for inflammatory myopathy outweighed the suspicion of histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. Beyond the classic presentation of neck swelling, the patient presented with dysphagia, which was corroborated by the flexible nasendoscopy note of increased secretions and coughing, as well as subsequent aspiration pneumonitis identified on CT. This airway muscle involvement is a critical differentiator, as idiopathic angioedema does not typically produce the profound proximal muscle weakness (4/5 hip flexor power) found in this individual. Furthermore, subtle cutaneous findings that often escape the initial glance were critical clues. During examination at the immunology review, the clinician noted subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema. Crucially, nailfold capillaroscopy performed at presentation identified proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages along with avascular areas. These subtle vascular changes serve as early indicators of connective tissue inflammation and, in this context, signaled that the muscle and skin involvement were not merely allergic but inflammatory. The patient’s denied history of angioedema and the absence of structural lung issues (specifically vocal cord abnormalities noted endoscopically) further painted a picture of an acquired inflammatory process rather than an allergic mediator response.

The systematic exclusion of alternative inflammatory and infectious causes provided the foundation for pivoting toward rheumatology. Initially, the concern for infection—such as cellulitis or deep neck space infection—was addressed through the lack of fever, lack of leukocytosis (white cell count was 9.1×109/L), and negative aspiration findings. In the context of suspected angioedema, the exclusion of hereditary and acquired forms was paramount. Standard workup prior to the rheumatologic diagnosis included normal C3 and C4 complement levels and normal C1 esterase function and quantification, effectively ruling out hereditary angioedema or acquired angioedema associated with complement deficiencies. Drug-induced angioedema was also excluded as the patient denied recent prescriptions of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors. The logical pivot occurred when a comprehensive clinical profile revealed physical findings inconsistent with pure angioedema and inconsistent with infection. While the inflammatory markers (CrP 55.6 mg/L) remained high, the presence of these normal complement levels and the absence of drug triggers necessitated an investigation into autoimmune etiology, specifically prompting the request for a creatine kinase level.

The pivot to rheumatology was confirmed by the unexpected serology and laboratory abnormalities that directly explained the swelling. The request for creatine kinase (CK) was the most expedient and high-yield maneuver, revealing an elevation of 16,184 U/L. This massive elevation immediately flagged rhabdomyolysis or myopathy, which, given the muscular weakness and neck oedema, suggested myositis rather than simple oedema. Subsequent investigations including ANA (1:1280 speckled pattern), ANCA testing, and a specific myositis antibody panel strongly pinpointed the diagnosis. The presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies was diagnostic, confirming the inflammatory etiology of the "angioedema" was actually intense muscle inflammation affecting the superficial cervical soft tissues. The muscle biopsy provided the final ex-vivo confirmation, showing perifascicular atrophy and complement deposition in capillaries. This diagnostic reasoning highlighted that the "swelling" was not exudative fluid accumulation from vascular leak (angioedema) but rather the physical expansion of inflamed, edematous muscle tissue (myositis), a distinction that dictated the urgent initiation of high-dose immunosuppression rather than antihistamines.

## Confirmatory Workup: Serology, Imaging, and Perifascicular Biopsy

In the clinical evaluation of dermatomyositis, confirming the diagnosis requires a hierarchical testing strategy that differentiates inflammatory myopathy from non-immune etiology and other autoimmune conditions. While the presentation of neck swelling and proximal weakness suggested a significant systemic process, distinguishing between histaminergic-mediated angioedema, infection, and inflammatory myopathy necessitated specific diagnostic modalities. The confirmation of the patient’s SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis relied on integrating blood-based markers, radiographic imaging, and histopathological features.

The initial tier of serological screening always involves assessing the status of muscle enzymes, specifically creatine kinase (CK). In inflammatory myopathies, increased CK levels result from the exposure of intracellular enzymes due to muscle fiber necrosis and membrane permeability shifts during acute inflammation. For this patient, the elevated CK of 16,184 U/L was highly specific for significant muscle injury and served as a critical pivot point in the diagnostic reasoning. Such profound elevation is typically inconsistent with simple dermatological edema or infection alone, immediately elevating the suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy. Although the urinary myoglobin screen was negative and renal function remained normal, ruling out renal impairment, the CK level necessitated a push for further immunological characterization beyond initial metabolic workup.

Following the initial marker of muscle injury, serology was directed toward identifying the causative autoantibody. In this case, a myositis antibody panel was the definitive tool. The patient tested positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, a highly specific marker for a subset of inflammatory myositis. This distinguishes the pathology from other common subsets such as anti-Mi-2 dermatomyositis or anti-MDA-5 disease, which have distinct clinical correlates such as gastrointestinal telangiectasias or rare cutaneous vasculitis, respectively. While a diagnosis of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy could still be considered SAE1-negative, the patient’s significantly positive signal intensity (173, above the normal range of <35) strongly correlated with a Myositis Association Assessment. Conversely, the panel was negative for anti-TIF1-γ antibodies, which are characteristic of anti-synthetase syndrome and are often more strongly associated with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary hypomontage. Thus, the serology confirmed the specific molecular pathology while helping to exclude differential diagnoses such as myositis associated with malignancy or drug-induced myopathy.

Imaging was essential not only to assess muscle involvement but also to evaluate the airway and pulmonary tract, which were at risk due to extrathoracic muscle involvement. A CT of the chest identified multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules and peribronchial consolidation, consistent with aspiration pneumonitis likely secondary to the patient’s dysphagia. This risk factor prompted early consideration of malignancy screening, as dermatomyositis is an "oncologic marker" that increases cancer risk. Furthermore, Flurodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) was crucial in the differential diagnosis. It demonstrated diffuse FDG accumulation within multiple muscle groups, confirming active inflammation. The PET findings helped rule out malignancy by showing that metabolically active disease was localized to the soft tissues and muscles rather than a systemic nodal or primary tumor burden, thereby supporting a primary inflammatory etiology.

Histopathology remains the gold standard for diagnosing dermatomyositis. The patient underwent a thigh muscle biopsy, which provided the definitive histological features necessary to confirm the diagnosis. Histology revealed foci of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate, myophagocytosed myofibres with a perifascicular distribution. Perifascicular atrophy is particularly characteristic of dermatomyositis and results from progressive ischemia at the angular vacuoles, which compromises capillary function. Confirmatory findings included immunohistochemistry showing deposition of the membrane attack complex component C5b-9 in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This complement deposition is a hallmark of the hypersensitive vascular damage in dermatomyositis and is virtually pathognomonic in the absence of other factors like diabetes or renal disease, contrasting with the perifascicular sparing seen in other myopathies. Together, the elevated CK, specific anti-SAE1 serology, PET-positive muscle activity, and characteristic histopathology provided a comprehensive, multi-modal confirmation of dermatomyositis, guiding immediate aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

## Therapeutic Management: Immunosuppression Strategies in Myositis

Effective management of autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, particularly in the acute setting where multiorgan involvement is present, relies on aggressive immunosuppression to halt disease progression and preserve respiratory muscle function. In this case, the patient presented with severe cervical soft tissue swelling and dysphagia suggestive of airway compromise driven by myositis. Consequently, the initial therapeutic strategy prioritized rapid reduction of inflammation. The first line of intervention involved high-dose intravenous corticosteroids. An initial protocol of intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 3 days was utilized to acutely suppress the inflammatory response within the neck muscles and associated soft tissues. This "pulse" therapy serves as a standard induction approach to stabilize airway patency and reduce systemic cytokine storms, transitioning the patient to oral corticosteroids once adequate inflammatory control was achieved. The patient was then stepped down to hydrocortisone (prednisone) tapering regimen starting at 60 mg daily to maintain remission whilst minimizing long-term systemic toxicity.

Following the induction phase, the treatment plan evolved into a maintenance and combination regimen designed to sustain disease control and spare long-term steroid toxicity. Given the patient's condition—specifically the presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies and features consistent with inflammatory lung involvement—monotherapy with corticosteroids was deemed insufficient for durable remission. A combination approach was adopted, incorporating mycophenolate mofetil (1 g twice daily), rituximab (1000 mg two doses, administered 2 weeks apart), and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg. This tri-phasic regimen targets the underlying autoimmune process through distinct mechanisms: mycophenolate mofetil inhibits purine synthesis to halt lymphocyte proliferation, rituximab depletes B-cells implicated in autoantibody production, and IVIG modulates immune function and neutralization of circulating factors. While treatment outcomes for dermatomyositis-associated oedema are variable, the combination of these agents allowed for significant clinical improvement, resolving the inflammatory neck swelling and normalizing creatine kinase levels.

A critical decision-making point in the chemoprophylaxis was the avoidance of methotrexate. Although widely utilized as a steroid-sparing agent in dermatomyositis, methotrexate was contraindicated in this specific patient due to the risk of exacerbating pulmonary complications. Upon presentation, the patient exhibited abnormal lung function tests (restrictive pattern) and CT findings suggesting peribronchial consolidation and pulmonary nodules, which were indicative of an ILD-prone inflammatory state. Clinical guidelines suggest that in patients with anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis risk factors for interstitial lung disease (ILD) and those who already demonstrate respiratory compromise, methotrexate-associated fibrotic changes pose a significant safety hazard. Therefore, the decision to avoid methotrexate and rely on the combination of mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab was a strategic choice to prioritize lung preservation while managing the inflammation.

Finally, the management required careful consideration of prophylaxis for opportunistic infections, specifically Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP), alongside vigilant monitoring for hematologic toxicity. Standard prophylaxis involves sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim; however, in this patient, that regimen was discontinued due to the development of haemolytic anaemia. This occurred despite the absence of documented glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, highlighting the importance of monitoring red blood cell indices during steroid and immunosuppressant therapy. To manage the PJP risk without compromising red cell health, the patient was subsequently switched to nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks. This modification allowed for the continuation of necessary infection prophylaxis while maintaining hematologic stability throughout the treatment course. Ultimately, this comprehensive regimen facilitated a successful transition to partial steroid taper and mucocutaneous resolution, allowing for the eventual removal of thoracic assistance and removal of the nasogastric tube.

## Malignancy Risk Stratification and Long-Term Follow-up

The management of initial inflammatory myopathies, including dermatomyositis (DM), requires rigorous assessment for an underlying neoplastic etiology, as malignancy occurs more frequently in anti-SAE1–positive patients compared to other antibody profiles. In the context of this case, the patient presented with a high risk of occult malignancy despite a negative malignancy screen at diagnosis. This risk is stratified using the International guidelines for Malignancy Screening (IMACS) by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group. Under the IMACS framework, dermatomyositis itself constitutes a high-risk factor. Furthermore, the presence of dysphagia, SAE1抗体 positivity, and the patient's male sex in this demographic contribute additional high-risk and intermediate-risk points. Because anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis is associated with dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, which are warning signs for malignancy, the low threshold for cancer screening is critical. In this instance, the IMACS criteria identified two high-risk factors and two intermediate-risk factors, classifying the patient as having a very high cancer risk profile. Consequently, the initial clinical management prioritized a comprehensive malignancy workup to rule out paraneoplastic triggers or metastatic burden, ensuring that immunosuppressive therapy was not initiated prior to the exclusion of a malignancy, especially given the patient's significant airway and muscular involvement.

Malignancy surveillance in patients with anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis was guided by these IMACS risk criteria, necessitating multimodal imaging and laboratory testing immediately at the diagnosis. The patient underwent detailed Chest CT imaging to assess for interstitial lung disease (ILD) and potential metastases, with subsequent findings showing patchy consolidation likely due to aspiration pneumonitis rather than malignancy. While CT alone has limitations, the addition of a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)–positron emission tomography (PET) scan provided a functional metabolic map, revealing no metabolically active malignancy elsewhere in the body. This finding is pedagogically significant as it demonstrates that functional imaging can be superior to structural imaging in seronegative or paucisymptomatic periods, though it is not a standalone substitute for tissue diagnosis. Additionally, tumor markers—including CEA, CA 19-9, CA-125, and PSA—were part of the baseline screening panel to establish a reference point for future comparison, despite normal results in this patient. Gastroscopy and colonoscopy were identified as essential screenings to exclude colorectal and upper gastrointestinal malignancies, a plan that remains a cornerstone of long-term monitoring to detect occult cancers that may not be immediately symptomatic. These procedures were integrated into the multidisciplinary plan to ensure that the profound immunosuppression required for the dermatomyositis does not occur in the setting of an undiagnosed cancer.

Recovery trajectories in anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis differ significantly depending on the severity of the initial inflammatory burden and the promptness of immunosuppressive therapy. In this case, the aggressive initial dosing of intravenous methylprednisolone and the subsequent infusion of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) correlated with a rapid resolution of the proximal muscle weakness. Muscle biopsy findings and clinical strength assessments showed that the creatine kinase level normalized to baseline within three weeks, and proximal muscle strength fully recovered by discharge. However, extramuscular manifestations such as cervical soft tissue swelling, while initially dramatic, demonstrated a slower resolution timeline. By three months post-discharge, the cervical swelling had completely resolved, coinciding with the tapering of corticosteroids, whereas the facial erythema persisting longer responded more gradually to the combination of immunosuppressants. This discrepancy highlights that inflammatory edema in the subcutaneous tissues, while a hallmark of SAE1 dermatomyositis (as seen here), may persist longer than muscle inflammation requires for remission. Furthermore, the preservation of pulmonary function and the resolution of aspiration-related consolidations indicate that addressing the pharyngeal dysphagia via enteral feeding and immunosuppression was required to mitigate secondary intrapulmonary complications, demonstrating that the neuromuscular component of the disease often requires the most active management during the acute phase.

Ongoing monitoring for DM involves a balanced strategy of tailoring steroid-sparing agents while maintaining long-term efficacy for sustained remission. The patient on discharge demonstrated the need for cessation of oral corticosteroids in light of side effects, adhering to a standard taper schedule from 60 mg/day down to a discontinuation at seven months post-discharge. To prevent relapse, the patient was maintained on mycophenolate mofetil and monthly IVIG, with methotrexate withheld due to the pre-existing restrictive lung pattern identified on pulmonary function tests. Long-term follow-up protocols focus on maintaining efficacy while minimizing the cost of hospitalization and the risk of secondary malignancies from chronic immunosuppression. In this specific case, the patient remained on mycophenolate and IVIG, with compliance depending on clinical progress. The continuation of total IVIG and mycophenolate tapering plans, alongside ongoing respiratory function testing and complex imaging surveillance, supports the management paradigm where aggressive anti-inflammatory control must proceed without the pressure of indefinite immunosuppression on tumor screening or organ health. Ultimately, this case underscores that while dermatomyositis is often manageable with sustained remission, the initial risk of malignancy and the varying timelines of cutaneous and muscular recovery dictate a multidisciplinary, longitudinal surveillance approach.
