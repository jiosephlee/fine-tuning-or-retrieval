# The Angioedema Mimic: Recognizing Neck Swelling in Dermatomyositis

Imagine a patient walking into the emergency department with a swollen neck. For the vast majority of clinicians, this screams infection—Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space abscess, or potentially a retropharyngeal mass. It triggers an immediate cascade: CT scan, antibiotics, ENT consultation. But sometimes, the tissue swelling isn't bacterial or histaminic. It's myositis masquerading as angioedema. This case of anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis is a stark reminder that when a patient's white count is normal, they are afebrile, and the CT shows diffuse neck edema without the classic mandibular fusion of Ludwig’s, you must be willing to ask the rheumatology question before ordering the next biopsy.

The patient was a male in his 40s with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling. He had no fever and was afebrile on presentation, which immediately bumps the probability of necrotizing fasciitis or deep space infection down. He was initially frustrated that he was cleared by ENT, only to be sent back under the differential of acquired angioedema. But angioedema workup was negative—no history of ACE inhibitors, normal complement levels, and no family history. The pivot point in this case wasn't the neck; it was the muscles. The patient presented with hip flexor weakness and proximal myalgia alongside the swelling. In a standard patient, these symptoms might have been dismissed secondary to pain or referred sensation, but here, they were the key to unlocking the correct metabolic profile. When the creatine kinase level finally resulted as a standout 16,184 U/L, the clinical picture shifted from soft tissue edema to systemic inflammatory myopathy.

The significance of SAE1 (Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier Activating Enzyme) antibody positivity cannot be overstated in the context of this presentation. While dermatomyositis typically presents with the classic sun-exposed rash or Gottron’s papules, the cutaneous markers can be subtle. This patient had generalized facial erythema but notably lacked the overt Gottron’s papules or Heliotrope rash initially caught in progress. Instead, nailfold capillaroscopy revealed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, which are early, sensitive indicators for dermatomyositis. It is easy to miss these vascular changes in a patient who is preoccupied with their swollen neck. However, the association of proximal weakness with significant inflammatory markers like CK and AST is rare enough to pause standard dermatologic algorithms. When the diagnosis is dermatomyositis, the swelling is not caused by histamine or bradykinin; it is a result of active muscle inflammation extending into the soft tissue. The high hook here is emphasis upon the fact that the "edema" in the neck was myositis-related tissue inflammation that was driving the dysphagia and aspiration risk.

The treatment course highlights the severity of airway involvement in this specific phenotype. Because the swelling was driven by deep muscle inflammation rather than superficial allergic reaction, respiratory support remained a priority. The patient required aggressive immunosuppression including high-dose steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. While the response to therapy was excellent, the initial airway compromise from the neck swelling required careful monitoring. The outcome shows that early recognition of this soft tissue presentation prevents unnecessary ENT procedures and avoids the morbidity associated with missed airway obstruction from alternative diagnoses. The key lesson is that patient's weakness is often the harbinger, not just the "mystery swelling."

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Observe for muscle signs:** Always assess for proximal muscle weakness and myalgia in patients with unexplained neck swelling.
*   **Check the CK early:** A significantly elevated creatine kinase is a rapid, low-cost pivot when soft tissue edema mimics infection or angioedema.
*   **Look for the subtle rash:** Examine for nailfold microhaemorrhages and subtle erythema even if classic Ericsson or Gottron's papules are absent.
*   **Rule out angioedendolo first:** Positive autoimmune workup (ANA, specific antibodies) can override angioedema workup when infection is excluded.
*   **Monitor the airway:** A negative CT does not rule out airway involvement; active myositis affects the pharyngeal muscles and can cause dysphagia and aspiration.

## Final Thoughts

As clinical providers, we are taught to trust CT imaging. However, imaging reports reading "retropharyngeal inflammation" without a clear infectious source warrant a thought-experiment. If the patient has no fever and no acute inflammatory risk factors, ask if that swelling is inflammatory myopathy. Ordering a creatine kinase and a myositis antibody panel in these ambiguous cases saves patients from the complications of unnecessary steroids of one kind or another and correct treatment of a rare autoimmune hemorrhage that threatens the airway. The next time you encounter a swollen neck, remember that this swelling might not be in the throat, but in the muscle underneath.



## The Creatine Kinase Clue: Diagnosing Myositis in Unexplained Edema

Do you remember the case where the ENT thought it was Ludwig’s angina, the ED thought it was ACE-inhibitor induced angioedema, and nobody considered a rare inflammatory myopathy until a single blood test changed the trajectory? That’s the takeaway from dermatomyositis masquerading as unexplained neck swelling.

We often see patients present with diffuse soft tissue swelling around the face and neck that looks like a/UI or destructive infection. The algorithm in the ER is usually clear: Check creatinine, check angio factors, order antibiotics. But sometimes, the swelling isn't mediated by histamine or bradykinin—it's coming from inflamed muscle pushing through the subcutaneous tissue.

When I reviewed this case, the CK level of 16,184 U/L sounded like a typo. It didn’t just move the differential needle; it lifted the stratosphere. My patients did get clear imaging and no response to antibiotics, but the pivot was admitting the possibility of muscle involvement when the history didn't explain the physical exam.

In this patient, we went from a suspected neck space infection to a diagnosis of Ant–Small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme (SAE1) antibody–positive dermatomyositis over just a few days. This is a high-yield reminder for Emergency Physicians, Immunologists, and Rheumatologists: **Angioedema should indeed be in the top differential for facial swelling AND dermatomyositis.**

Here is the breakdown of why I believe keeping the card tight on CK is the critical pivot point in undiagnosed edema.
Check the Clinical Pearls below.

## The Unexplained Neck Swelling Dilemma

The patient, a male in his 40s, presented with a 3-week history of progressive neck swelling. This wasn't a simple allergy. The CT of the neck showed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles extending to the retropharyngeal space. In the absence of fever, tachypnea, or oxygen desaturation, the differential immediately swung toward Ludwig’s angina or spontaneous angioedema.

However, a few clues stood out early on that didn't fit the standard angioedema mold:
*   **Physical Exam:** He had no history of ACE inhibitors or NSAIDs to cause ACE-I induced edema.
*   **Skin:** There was generalized erythema over the face (nasolabial folds) and a rash on his thigh.
*   **Nailfold Changes:** Proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages were found on capillaroscopy.
*   **Weakness:** He reported proximal hip flexor weakness (4/5 power initially).

We ruled out structural ENT causes with a nasendoscopy. The secretariat system didn't show significant anatomical abnormalities, but there were increased secretions and coughing during the exam. This set up the discussion with clinical immunology for suspected angioedema, but the clinical profile was atypical for both acquired or hereditary angioedema (normal C3, C4, C1 function). The key, as noted, is often the combination of features seen on nailfold capillaroscopy.

## The Creatine Kinase Clue

This is the pearl we’ve seen time and time again. In clients suspected of having idiopathic angioedema, the CK is usually normal. In this case, the prompt was: *"What if the swelling isn't in the dermis, but in the muscle?"*

A CK level was ordered due to the atypical neck oedema and skin/nail changes. The result was 16,184 U/L. Ranging from 37 U/L (normal) to over 1000 U/L is usually not associated with non-angioedema sources. This was followed by:
*   Elevated Liver Enzymes (ALT 143, AST 396).
*   Elevated Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH 692).
*   Urine myoglobin screen negative (suggesting renal function preservation despite rhabdomyolysis risk).

This high CK indicated significant muscle breakdown and is a red flag for inflammatory myopathies. It didn't just rule out hypokalemia or anxiety; it redirected the entire care pathway. It prompted immediate之急 (urgent) evaluation for immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy or dermatomyositis rather than antibiotics.

## Treatment and Resolution

Management was aggressive because he was at risk for airway compromise due to dysphagia. Plan:
1.  **Corticosteroids:** Intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for a short course, followed by oral prednisone taper.
2.  **Biologics:** Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) 2 g/kg loads, split across 2-3 weeks.
3.  **Immunomodulators:** Mycophenolate mofetil and Rituximab introduced alongside corticosteroids.

Despite the severity, the patient demonstrated marked improvement. Within 3 weeks of hospitalization, there was significant muscle recovery and resolution of cervical soft tissue swelling. By 3 months post-discharge, dysphagia resolved and the drug regimen was successfully tapered.

## Key Take-aways

*   **Consider Dermatomyositis** in unexplained neck swelling that mimics angiogenesis or deep neck space infection.
*   **Elevated Creatine Kinase** > 1000 U/L is the pivot point; if CK > 1000 U/L is normal in the setting of neck swelling with rash, immediate biopsy and immunosuppression is required.
*   **Airway Risk** is real: Dysphagia and severe muscle weakness can lead to aspiration pneumonitis, making prompt diagnosis essential.
*   **Negative Angio Workup** should not delay a search for myositis: Normal C1-Esterase, C1-Quantification, and C3/C4 support myositis over hereditary forms.
*   **Screen for Malignancy:** Dermatomyositis, especially with SAE1 positivity and neck swelling, requires rigorous surveillance for malignancy as indicated by IMACS guidelines.

This case serves as a reminder that the CK level is often the critical pivot point and that sometimes the first CT scan is going to be the wrong diagnosis. If you see a patient with rapidly evolving subcutaneous oedema, a rash, and weakness, don't stop at antibiotics.



## Don't Miss the Nail: Catching Dermatomyositis Before the Kopron’s Appears

*Updated July 2024 | Kevin Chan Case Report Analysis*

We think of rare inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis (DM) primarily through the lens of classic, textbook presentations: heliotrope rashes around the eyes and Gottron’s papules on the knuckles. But what happens when those hallmark signs are missing? In a case report by Chan et al., a patient presented with massive neck swelling that was initially diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The absence of typical cutaneous signs led the team down a rabbit hole of ENT and immunology referrals, only to be steered toward dermo_myositis by the presence of *subtle* nail changes. This case challenges the assumption that you must see a classic rash before suspecting DM, particularly when a patient has red flags like unexplained neck oedema.

The most surprising aspect of this case is that the swelling in the neck and face was not histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated—it was muscle swelling. The patient was a male in his 40s who came in with a 3-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. His CT scan showed diffuse neck oedema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space, leading to a panic regarding Ludwig’s angina. Once infection was ruled out, the team suspected idiopathic angioedema. However, when the physical exam turned to the nails, the picture shifted. The case explicitly notes: Proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages, along with avascular areas, were found on nailfold capillaroscopy. While they didn't have the classic Gottron’s papules or Heliotrope rash, they did have a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema. The next day, the CK was ordered out of sheer concern and revealed a massive elevation of 16,184 U/L.
The underlying mechanism is often overlooked: Dermatomyositis can cause subcutaneous tissue oedema. In this patient, it arose from muscle inflammation in the neck (myositis), masquerading as angioedema. This is distinct from the common papillary edema or histamine-driven reactions.

**Clinical Pearls: The Subtle Exam**
This case highlights why a thorough physical exam, specifically looking at the feet and hands, is critical when a patient presents with non-histaminergic tissue swelling. A superficial scan might miss the early cutaneous manifestations that confirm the diagnosis.

*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy:** Subtle changes like microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on nailfold capillaroscopy precede overt skin rash in a significant number of myositis patients. Do not rely solely on standard dermatologic views.
*   **Holster Sign:** Recognizing early nailfold signs such as the Holster sign (periungual violaceous changes or telangiectasia) can point to DM even when Gottron’s papules are absent.
*   **Consider the CK Early:** If a patient presents with neck swelling and is non-allergic/non-infectious, an unexplained muscle enzyme level (like CK) is a vital screening tool, even if muscle weakness isn't the primary complaint.
*   **SAE1 Antibody:** In this case, the autoantibody was SAE1 (anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1). This profile carries specific risks for myositis and lung disease (70% complication rate in literature), including dysphagia which further complicates the airway.

The therapy here was aggressive: Methcxol (Methylprednisolone), IVIG, and Rituximab, with Mycophenolate Mofetil as the long-term buffer. The outcome was good, with complete resolution of neck swelling by 3 months post-discharge. However, the initial delay in diagnosis was substantial due to the lack of classic signs.

## Takeaway Points
*   **Dermatomyositis** can present with neck swelling (myositis) that mimics angioedema; it is not always histamine-mediated.
*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy** is high yield for early DM. Proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas appear before classic cutaneous rashes like Heliotrope or Gottron’s papules.
*   **Don't wait for the rash.** If four-fifths of the case presentation has dysphagia, proxyangioedema, and unexplained muscle weakness, check the Creatine Kinase (CK).
*   **Differential:** When neck swelling is unexplained, rule out infection first, then consider idiopathic myositis with anti-SAE1 antibodies as a cause for the oedema and dysphagia.



# Malignancy Surveillance: IMACS Guidelines for Filtering High-Risk Dermatomyositis

## The Neck Swelling That Wasn't Standing Up to Otolaryngology

I remember a case that sent a chill down the spine of the round (literally). A 40-year-old male walked into the ER with diffuse neck swelling and proximal weakness. The team immediately called in ENT for a workup for Ludwig’s angina or idiopathic angioedema. The scan showed oedema in the strap muscles and within the retropharyngeal space, but the patient was afebrile and the nasendoscopy showed no anatomical obstruction. Eventually, a bite was left to skin changes: periungual erythema and nail fold abnormalities. We requested a creatine kinase level, which was a massive 16,184 U/L. The diagnosis for many was dermatomyositis (DM), specifically anti-SAE1 positive, eventually confirmed with biopsies.

The disease course highlighted a critical transition point. While the primary focus of the clinic was clearing the airway and treating the inflammatory swelling, the underlying malignancy risk in dermatomyositis was looming behind the neck mass. At the time, most of the care was focused on the acute inflammatory crisis (steroids, IVIG, rituximab) and the lack of captialization screening.

This case serves as a stark reminder that when you find a dermatomyositis patient—especially one with dysphagia or atypical antibodies—you cannot just close the file.

## The Malignancy Link in Dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis is a rare autoimmune myopathy, but it is notoriously associated with an increased risk of malignancy. While the prevalence of cancer in systemic skin/rash is low in immunosuppression-naive patients, it rises significantly once the disease is diagnosed. In dermatomyositis, this is sometimes referred to as the "way to the cancer."

However, the risk is not evenly distributed. It is age-dependent and inflammation-dependent. A key factor in this equation is the antibody profile. This patient was SAE1 positive. While anti-Jo-1 and anti-MDA-5 are often the high profiles for interstitial lung disease, the anti-SAE1 phenotype often correlates with a distinct progression of inflammatory features like the oedema seen in our patient.

In this specific scenario, the presence of DM itself is a risk factor, but it pairs with other features like dysphagia or specific antibodies to push the probability higher. Ignoring the malignancy screen post-diagnosis leaves patients vulnerable to an entirely nagging or curable issue. Anticipating the cancer risk allows for better prognosis management, ensuring that if a patient improves clinically, the immune system is working, not just suppressing symptoms.

## Risk Stratification and the High-Risk Criteria

As we navigate IMACS guidelines, we arrive at the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) risk stratification tool. This tool helps us categorize patients based on their specific risk profile for cancer.

The key is screening frequency and intensity. High-risk patients require screening at every visit. This includes all risk factors according to the tool. Some examples include PC, malignancy, dyspepsia, and age.

This patient had a high-risk profile based on two high-risk factors: the diagnosis itself (dermatomyositis) and dysphagia (evidenced by aspiration pneumonitis seen on CT). He also had two intermediate risk factors: being male >45 years old and the presence of SAE1 antibodies.

This combination meant that the risk was not negligible. Guidelines dictate that a malignancy screen should include specific imaging and endoscopy for this category of patient. Skipping the scan assumes the patient is low-risk, but that is a clinical error if the risk factors stack up like in this case.

## The Workup: CT, PET, and Endoscopy

When the diagnosis was confirmed in the acute phase, the logic for the screening was straightforward but rigorous. The patient required a complete scan to rule out underlying masses before committing to long-term immunosuppression doses like rituximab.

We utilized a multi-part approach:
*   **CT Chest/Abdomen/Pelvis:** To assess for common primaries.
*   **PET-Fluorodeoxyglucose Scan:** This showed active muscle inflammation but did not reveal metabolically active disease elsewhere suggesting malignancy.
*   **Endoscopy Planning:** We planned for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult gastrointestinal pathology given the high-risk factors.

This aligns with IMACS recommendation 6.16, regarding screening for malignancy in dermatomyositis. While the NCCN guidelines suggest regular surveillance, the specific IMACS approach for high-risk individuals demands that you consider the differential before treatment escalates.

## Malignancy Surveillance In Reminder

It happens when dealing with rare diseases. We are comfortable with the steroids. We are comfortable with the IVIG. But the cancer risk is often missed.

The neck swelling in this patient made no sense to ENT until the CK level arrived. The malignancy risk is often found in the labs that got ignored. To treat the patient, you must treat the airway. To guide the patient, you must treat the cancer risk.

It is critical to filter the case properly. Don't assume the mass lesion is inflammatory when the patient has multiple risk factors. The screening ensures that if a patient does have a mass, it is caught while the systemic process is manageable.

## Take-Home Messages

*   Dermatomyositis requires active malignancy surveillance based on IMACS risk stratification, not routine follow-up alone.
*   Specifically, male patients over 45 and those with SAE1 antibodies should be flagged as high-risk for aggressive screening.
*   Dysphagia increases the high-risk factors and warrants a screen including GI endoscopy.
*   Imaging should include CT, PET, and Endoscopy in high-risk DM cases rather than waiting for symptomatic change.
*   Malignancy should be considered when the clinical profile suggests a differential but imaging is incomplete or missed.



# Aggressive Immunosuppression: Managing Acute Myositis with Airway Involvement

## The Angioedema Trap

Picture the classic alert you've heard a hundred times: a patient comes in with a swollen neck. Your internal monologue goes immediately to "anaphylaxis" or "angioedema." If they took an ACE inhibitor, or if the swelling is part of a known drug reaction, you act quickly with antihistamines or epi. But when you see a "non-histamine, non-bradykinin" swelling without medication history, and you are utterly baffled by the lack of response to standard ENT clearance, what is suddenly the minding the "obvious"? Keep looking for more. Dermatomyositis, a rare autoimmune disease, often masquerades as deep neck infection or angioedema. It is crucial to recognize this differential, especially when neck swelling occurs alongside subtle muscle complaints or nail changes.

This case involved a male in his 40s with three weeks of progressive neck swelling and muscle weakness. Initially, the impressions were Ludwig’s angina and idiopathic angioedema. But the details of the physical exam shifted the tide. He had proximal nailfold changes, specifically microhaemorrhages and avascular areas. As he lay on the procedure table, he had difficulty swallowing and retained fluid in his pyriform fossae during a nasendoscopy.

Look for this: The classic presentation of dermatomyositis is the "shawl sign" or "heliotrope rash." This patient did not have those initially. But the underbelly of the presentation involved deep perimysial inflammation causing subcutaneous oedema. The airway was the first to be compromised, and it was the muscle involvement that mimicked the swelling. Do not let the landlocked nature of dermatomyositis fly blind in the face of what looks like pure airway pathology.

## The Diagnostic Pivot: Creatine Kinase Is Your Best Friend

Once the regulation of peripheral signs is established, focus shifts to the biomarker that answers the "why." The patient had a creatine kinase level of 16,184 U/L. This is a sky-high number, signaling that the myositis is moderate to severe.

It is vital to understand that anti-SAE1 antibodies (Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier 1 Activating Enzyme) are a strong marker for dermatomyositis with a high propensity for dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. When you see an elevated CRP (55.6 mg/L) with neck swelling, ask why your CT scan showed "diffuse edema" involving the strap muscles rather than just airway collapse from fluid or laryngeal stenosis.

Subacute neck swelling without infection, high embolic in CK, normal complement, positive ANA, and nailfold changes is a pattern that screams dermatomyositis. The airway is being invaded by myositis. This distinction is critical because the treatment for idiopathic angioedema (steroids, epi) differs significantly from the suicide protocol for dermatomyositis (high-dose steroids, IVIG, immunosuppressants).

If you suspect DM, you need a muscle biopsy eventually. But a high CK is the green light to start.

## Aggressive Immunosuppression: Setting the Bar High

Once the diagnosis is confirmed, the treatment has to be aggressive. This patient requires a "burnout" approach because of the high risk of airway compromise. The treatment regimen included intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 3 days), followed by a prednisone taper starting at 60 mg.

The gold standard of immunomodulation for SAE1-positive dermatomyositis with airway involvement often requires:
*   **IVIG (Intravenous Immunoglobulin):** The patient received a 2 g/kg dose over 5 days.
*   **Second-line Immunosuppressants:** Mycophenolate Mofetil 1 g BID.
*   **Rituximab:** Two doses at 1000 mg IV, 2 weeks apart.

Why such a heavy blow? Elevated transaminases and CK, plus active inflammation in the head and neck seen on PET scan, suggest a systemic myositis that is not responding to low-dose treatment. The pill bottle of MTX (Methotrexate) was avoided due to association with interstitial lung disease, a risk confirmed by the respiratory findings on presentation.

Do not underestimate the risk of pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP) prophylaxis in this setting. The patient was placed on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim but had to discontinue it due to haemolytic anaemia in the absence of G6PD deficiency. He was switched to nebulised pentamidine. This is a common pivot in real-world cases of high-dose immunosuppression. Monitor hemoglobin and red cell indices closely while on anticholestreremic agents. The goal is treating the immune system without destroying the oxygen-carrying capacity.

## Safety & Follow-Up: Watch for Cancer

One of the most significant lessons from this case is the malignancy link. Anti-SAE1 is unique in the dermatomyositis landscape because it is associated with a 18% malignancy risk. With this patient having dysphagia (intermediate risk) and the antibody positivity (high risk factor per IMACS guidelines), the malignancy screen was aggressive: CT chest, abdomen/pelvis, PET scan, and tumor markers.

The patient had extensive screening but was kept cancer-free. However, this must happen routinely for SAE1-positive dermatomyositis. You also need to monitor the lung carefully; these patients show "patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation" due to aspiration pneumonitis from dysphagia.

The outcome for this patient was excellent because the muscle strength recovered fully and dysphagia resolved enough to clear the NG tube. But keep the follow-up vigilant.

## Key Takeaways

*   **Dermatomyositis can present as neck swelling:** Do not assume spontaneous angioedema if CK is elevated or if swelling is non-histaminic.
*   **Subcutaneous oedema is rare:** Facial or neck swelling is an uncommon feature (6.6% of cohort in studies) but potential for one or two cases.
*   **CK is the diagnostic key:** A CK > 10,000 suggests significant myositis and muscle breakdown. Request it early if the neck swelling looks like angioedema.
*   **Heavy Immunosuppression is often required:** For SAE1+ DM with airway involvement, consider IVIG, Rituximab, and Mycophenolate, plus steroid pulses.
*   **Screen for Malignancy:** Anti-SAE1 associated with high malignancy risk (18%). Endoscopy and imaging are vital.
*   **Manage Pneumocystis Prophylaxis:** Monitor for haemolysis when using sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim; switch to pentamidine or Dapsone as needed.
*   **Assess for aspiration risk:** Dysphagia is a common but invisible complication; treat early to prevent lung consolidation.
