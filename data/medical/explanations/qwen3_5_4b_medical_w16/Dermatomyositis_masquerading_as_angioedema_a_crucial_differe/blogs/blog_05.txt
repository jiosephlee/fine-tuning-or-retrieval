Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Aggressive Immunosuppression: Managing Acute Myositis with Airway Involvement

## The Angioedema Trap

Picture the classic alert you've heard a hundred times: a patient comes in with a swollen neck. Your internal monologue goes immediately to "anaphylaxis" or "angioedema." If they took an ACE inhibitor, or if the swelling is part of a known drug reaction, you act quickly with antihistamines or epi. But when you see a "non-histamine, non-bradykinin" swelling without medication history, and you are utterly baffled by the lack of response to standard ENT clearance, what is suddenly the minding the "obvious"? Keep looking for more. Dermatomyositis, a rare autoimmune disease, often masquerades as deep neck infection or angioedema. It is crucial to recognize this differential, especially when neck swelling occurs alongside subtle muscle complaints or nail changes.

This case involved a male in his 40s with three weeks of progressive neck swelling and muscle weakness. Initially, the impressions were Ludwig’s angina and idiopathic angioedema. But the details of the physical exam shifted the tide. He had proximal nailfold changes, specifically microhaemorrhages and avascular areas. As he lay on the procedure table, he had difficulty swallowing and retained fluid in his pyriform fossae during a nasendoscopy.

Look for this: The classic presentation of dermatomyositis is the "shawl sign" or "heliotrope rash." This patient did not have those initially. But the underbelly of the presentation involved deep perimysial inflammation causing subcutaneous oedema. The airway was the first to be compromised, and it was the muscle involvement that mimicked the swelling. Do not let the landlocked nature of dermatomyositis fly blind in the face of what looks like pure airway pathology.

## The Diagnostic Pivot: Creatine Kinase Is Your Best Friend

Once the regulation of peripheral signs is established, focus shifts to the biomarker that answers the "why." The patient had a creatine kinase level of 16,184 U/L. This is a sky-high number, signaling that the myositis is moderate to severe.

It is vital to understand that anti-SAE1 antibodies (Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier 1 Activating Enzyme) are a strong marker for dermatomyositis with a high propensity for dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. When you see an elevated CRP (55.6 mg/L) with neck swelling, ask why your CT scan showed "diffuse edema" involving the strap muscles rather than just airway collapse from fluid or laryngeal stenosis.

Subacute neck swelling without infection, high embolic in CK, normal complement, positive ANA, and nailfold changes is a pattern that screams dermatomyositis. The airway is being invaded by myositis. This distinction is critical because the treatment for idiopathic angioedema (steroids, epi) differs significantly from the suicide protocol for dermatomyositis (high-dose steroids, IVIG, immunosuppressants).

If you suspect DM, you need a muscle biopsy eventually. But a high CK is the green light to start.

## Aggressive Immunosuppression: Setting the Bar High

Once the diagnosis is confirmed, the treatment has to be aggressive. This patient requires a "burnout" approach because of the high risk of airway compromise. The treatment regimen included intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 3 days), followed by a prednisone taper starting at 60 mg.

The gold standard of immunomodulation for SAE1-positive dermatomyositis with airway involvement often requires:
*   **IVIG (Intravenous Immunoglobulin):** The patient received a 2 g/kg dose over 5 days.
*   **Second-line Immunosuppressants:** Mycophenolate Mofetil 1 g BID.
*   **Rituximab:** Two doses at 1000 mg IV, 2 weeks apart.

Why such a heavy blow? Elevated transaminases and CK, plus active inflammation in the head and neck seen on PET scan, suggest a systemic myositis that is not responding to low-dose treatment. The pill bottle of MTX (Methotrexate) was avoided due to association with interstitial lung disease, a risk confirmed by the respiratory findings on presentation.

Do not underestimate the risk of pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP) prophylaxis in this setting. The patient was placed on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim but had to discontinue it due to haemolytic anaemia in the absence of G6PD deficiency. He was switched to nebulised pentamidine. This is a common pivot in real-world cases of high-dose immunosuppression. Monitor hemoglobin and red cell indices closely while on anticholestreremic agents. The goal is treating the immune system without destroying the oxygen-carrying capacity.

## Safety & Follow-Up: Watch for Cancer

One of the most significant lessons from this case is the malignancy link. Anti-SAE1 is unique in the dermatomyositis landscape because it is associated with a 18% malignancy risk. With this patient having dysphagia (intermediate risk) and the antibody positivity (high risk factor per IMACS guidelines), the malignancy screen was aggressive: CT chest, abdomen/pelvis, PET scan, and tumor markers.

The patient had extensive screening but was kept cancer-free. However, this must happen routinely for SAE1-positive dermatomyositis. You also need to monitor the lung carefully; these patients show "patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation" due to aspiration pneumonitis from dysphagia.

The outcome for this patient was excellent because the muscle strength recovered fully and dysphagia resolved enough to clear the NG tube. But keep the follow-up vigilant.

## Key Takeaways

*   **Dermatomyositis can present as neck swelling:** Do not assume spontaneous angioedema if CK is elevated or if swelling is non-histaminic.
*   **Subcutaneous oedema is rare:** Facial or neck swelling is an uncommon feature (6.6% of cohort in studies) but potential for one or two cases.
*   **CK is the diagnostic key:** A CK > 10,000 suggests significant myositis and muscle breakdown. Request it early if the neck swelling looks like angioedema.
*   **Heavy Immunosuppression is often required:** For SAE1+ DM with airway involvement, consider IVIG, Rituximab, and Mycophenolate, plus steroid pulses.
*   **Screen for Malignancy:** Anti-SAE1 associated with high malignancy risk (18%). Endoscopy and imaging are vital.
*   **Manage Pneumocystis Prophylaxis:** Monitor for haemolysis when using sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim; switch to pentamidine or Dapsone as needed.
*   **Assess for aspiration risk:** Dysphagia is a common but invisible complication; treat early to prevent lung consolidation.
