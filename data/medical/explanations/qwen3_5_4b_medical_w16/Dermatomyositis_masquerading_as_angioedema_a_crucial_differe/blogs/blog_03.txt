Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Don't Miss the Nail: Catching Dermatomyositis Before the Kopron’s Appears

*Updated July 2024 | Kevin Chan Case Report Analysis*

We think of rare inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis (DM) primarily through the lens of classic, textbook presentations: heliotrope rashes around the eyes and Gottron’s papules on the knuckles. But what happens when those hallmark signs are missing? In a case report by Chan et al., a patient presented with massive neck swelling that was initially diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The absence of typical cutaneous signs led the team down a rabbit hole of ENT and immunology referrals, only to be steered toward dermo_myositis by the presence of *subtle* nail changes. This case challenges the assumption that you must see a classic rash before suspecting DM, particularly when a patient has red flags like unexplained neck oedema.

The most surprising aspect of this case is that the swelling in the neck and face was not histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated—it was muscle swelling. The patient was a male in his 40s who came in with a 3-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. His CT scan showed diffuse neck oedema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space, leading to a panic regarding Ludwig’s angina. Once infection was ruled out, the team suspected idiopathic angioedema. However, when the physical exam turned to the nails, the picture shifted. The case explicitly notes: Proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages, along with avascular areas, were found on nailfold capillaroscopy. While they didn't have the classic Gottron’s papules or Heliotrope rash, they did have a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema. The next day, the CK was ordered out of sheer concern and revealed a massive elevation of 16,184 U/L.
The underlying mechanism is often overlooked: Dermatomyositis can cause subcutaneous tissue oedema. In this patient, it arose from muscle inflammation in the neck (myositis), masquerading as angioedema. This is distinct from the common papillary edema or histamine-driven reactions.

**Clinical Pearls: The Subtle Exam**
This case highlights why a thorough physical exam, specifically looking at the feet and hands, is critical when a patient presents with non-histaminergic tissue swelling. A superficial scan might miss the early cutaneous manifestations that confirm the diagnosis.

*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy:** Subtle changes like microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on nailfold capillaroscopy precede overt skin rash in a significant number of myositis patients. Do not rely solely on standard dermatologic views.
*   **Holster Sign:** Recognizing early nailfold signs such as the Holster sign (periungual violaceous changes or telangiectasia) can point to DM even when Gottron’s papules are absent.
*   **Consider the CK Early:** If a patient presents with neck swelling and is non-allergic/non-infectious, an unexplained muscle enzyme level (like CK) is a vital screening tool, even if muscle weakness isn't the primary complaint.
*   **SAE1 Antibody:** In this case, the autoantibody was SAE1 (anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1). This profile carries specific risks for myositis and lung disease (70% complication rate in literature), including dysphagia which further complicates the airway.

The therapy here was aggressive: Methcxol (Methylprednisolone), IVIG, and Rituximab, with Mycophenolate Mofetil as the long-term buffer. The outcome was good, with complete resolution of neck swelling by 3 months post-discharge. However, the initial delay in diagnosis was substantial due to the lack of classic signs.

## Takeaway Points
*   **Dermatomyositis** can present with neck swelling (myositis) that mimics angioedema; it is not always histamine-mediated.
*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy** is high yield for early DM. Proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas appear before classic cutaneous rashes like Heliotrope or Gottron’s papules.
*   **Don't wait for the rash.** If four-fifths of the case presentation has dysphagia, proxyangioedema, and unexplained muscle weakness, check the Creatine Kinase (CK).
*   **Differential:** When neck swelling is unexplained, rule out infection first, then consider idiopathic myositis with anti-SAE1 antibodies as a cause for the oedema and dysphagia.
