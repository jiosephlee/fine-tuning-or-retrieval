Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Angioedema Mimic: Recognizing Neck Swelling in Dermatomyositis

Imagine a patient walking into the emergency department with a swollen neck. For the vast majority of clinicians, this screams infection—Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space abscess, or potentially a retropharyngeal mass. It triggers an immediate cascade: CT scan, antibiotics, ENT consultation. But sometimes, the tissue swelling isn't bacterial or histaminic. It's myositis masquerading as angioedema. This case of anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis is a stark reminder that when a patient's white count is normal, they are afebrile, and the CT shows diffuse neck edema without the classic mandibular fusion of Ludwig’s, you must be willing to ask the rheumatology question before ordering the next biopsy.

The patient was a male in his 40s with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling. He had no fever and was afebrile on presentation, which immediately bumps the probability of necrotizing fasciitis or deep space infection down. He was initially frustrated that he was cleared by ENT, only to be sent back under the differential of acquired angioedema. But angioedema workup was negative—no history of ACE inhibitors, normal complement levels, and no family history. The pivot point in this case wasn't the neck; it was the muscles. The patient presented with hip flexor weakness and proximal myalgia alongside the swelling. In a standard patient, these symptoms might have been dismissed secondary to pain or referred sensation, but here, they were the key to unlocking the correct metabolic profile. When the creatine kinase level finally resulted as a standout 16,184 U/L, the clinical picture shifted from soft tissue edema to systemic inflammatory myopathy.

The significance of SAE1 (Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier Activating Enzyme) antibody positivity cannot be overstated in the context of this presentation. While dermatomyositis typically presents with the classic sun-exposed rash or Gottron’s papules, the cutaneous markers can be subtle. This patient had generalized facial erythema but notably lacked the overt Gottron’s papules or Heliotrope rash initially caught in progress. Instead, nailfold capillaroscopy revealed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, which are early, sensitive indicators for dermatomyositis. It is easy to miss these vascular changes in a patient who is preoccupied with their swollen neck. However, the association of proximal weakness with significant inflammatory markers like CK and AST is rare enough to pause standard dermatologic algorithms. When the diagnosis is dermatomyositis, the swelling is not caused by histamine or bradykinin; it is a result of active muscle inflammation extending into the soft tissue. The high hook here is emphasis upon the fact that the "edema" in the neck was myositis-related tissue inflammation that was driving the dysphagia and aspiration risk.

The treatment course highlights the severity of airway involvement in this specific phenotype. Because the swelling was driven by deep muscle inflammation rather than superficial allergic reaction, respiratory support remained a priority. The patient required aggressive immunosuppression including high-dose steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. While the response to therapy was excellent, the initial airway compromise from the neck swelling required careful monitoring. The outcome shows that early recognition of this soft tissue presentation prevents unnecessary ENT procedures and avoids the morbidity associated with missed airway obstruction from alternative diagnoses. The key lesson is that patient's weakness is often the harbinger, not just the "mystery swelling."

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Observe for muscle signs:** Always assess for proximal muscle weakness and myalgia in patients with unexplained neck swelling.
*   **Check the CK early:** A significantly elevated creatine kinase is a rapid, low-cost pivot when soft tissue edema mimics infection or angioedema.
*   **Look for the subtle rash:** Examine for nailfold microhaemorrhages and subtle erythema even if classic Ericsson or Gottron's papules are absent.
*   **Rule out angioedendolo first:** Positive autoimmune workup (ANA, specific antibodies) can override angioedema workup when infection is excluded.
*   **Monitor the airway:** A negative CT does not rule out airway involvement; active myositis affects the pharyngeal muscles and can cause dysphagia and aspiration.

## Final Thoughts

As clinical providers, we are taught to trust CT imaging. However, imaging reports reading "retropharyngeal inflammation" without a clear infectious source warrant a thought-experiment. If the patient has no fever and no acute inflammatory risk factors, ask if that swelling is inflammatory myopathy. Ordering a creatine kinase and a myositis antibody panel in these ambiguous cases saves patients from the complications of unnecessary steroids of one kind or another and correct treatment of a rare autoimmune hemorrhage that threatens the airway. The next time you encounter a swollen neck, remember that this swelling might not be in the throat, but in the muscle underneath.
