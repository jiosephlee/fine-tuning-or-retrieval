Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient presents with unexplained neck swelling that mimics idiopathic angioedema. How does the pathophysiological mechanism of edema in dermatomyositis differ from typical vasogenic swelling, and what accompanying systemic symptoms should prompt consideration of myopathy over an allergic etiology?

When evaluating a patient with unexplained neck swelling mimicking idiopathic angioedema, it is critical to distinguish between vasogenic mechanisms and inflammatory infiltrative processes. While typical angioedema is a form of vasogenic edema driven by histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated vascular permeability—often precipitated by allergens or ACE inhibitors—the edema seen in dermatomyositis, as demonstrated in this case, arises from an inflammatory myositis. The patient’s swelling represents "pseudo-angioedema" caused by immune complex deposition and inflammatory cell infiltration into the muscle and surrounding soft tissue, rather than acute capillary leak. The anti-SAE1 antibody highlighted in our patient suggests a specific autoimmune mechanism destroying tissue integrity, leading to soft tissue swelling and compression rather than purely vascular dilation. Consequently, the emergence of dysphagia due to pharyngeal muscle spasm or infiltration is a hallmark of dermatomyositis, distinguishing it from allergic swelling which typically resolves without weakness.

Clinically, one must opportunistically look for concomitant myopathic signs to avoid mistaking an autoimmune myopathy for a primary allergic or infectious etiology. In this case, the presence of progressive proximal muscle weakness (first in hip flexors), severe dysphagia with aspiration risk, and cutaneous microangiology (periungual changes) should trigger immediate suspicion. Since the patient was afebrile, not tachypneic, and showed no signs of infection, the absence of these allergic history triggers aids the differential. The most definitive diagnostic step, when angioedema remains unexplained by allergic or hereditary causes, is the measurement of creatine kinase. In our patient, the creatine kinase was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L, a finding that is virtually pathognomonic for inflammatory myopathies and necessitates deep tissue biopsy to confirm perifascicular inflammation.

Given that the edema is not vasogenic, management of the patient as an angioedema emergency (e.g., epinephrine, antihistamines) is inappropriate and ineffective. The treatment paradigm shifts immediately to aggressive immunosuppression to halt the tissue destruction. Early recognition prevents permanent airway compromise and muscle atrophy; in our case, prompt administration of high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone followed by mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab reverse the cervical swelling and restored muscle function. Therefore, in any patient with neck swelling lacking clear allergic triggers or signs of acute infection, history of dysphagia, and proximal weakness, dermatomyositis—including rare etiologies like anti-SAE1 positivity—must remain in the differential diagnosis until proven otherwise.

### [Pathophysiology] Subcutaneous neck swelling can occur in dermatomyositis due to inflammation of the underlying musculature. How does this inflammatory ED to subcutaneous tissue mechanistically arise, distinguishing it from the histamine or bradykinin-mediated pathways associated with classic angioedema?

This morning’s case illustrates a critical diagnostic pitfall where dermatomyositis presents as "pseudoangioedema," masquerading as idiopathic or hereditary angioedema. The key to distinguishing these entities lies in understanding the distinct mechanistic origins of the swelling.

The subcutaneous neck swelling in this patient arose from **immune-mediated muscle inflammation extending into the subcutaneous tissue**, as confirmed by the elevated creatine kinase (16,184 U/L) and muscle biopsy findings of necrotic, vacuolated myofibers with perifascicular inflammation. In contrast, classic angioedema is mediated by **histamine or bradykinin**, which directly increase endothelial capillary permeability without primary muscle necrosis. In this patient, the process was driven by the systemic myositis and positive anti-SAE1 antibodies, resulting in tissue inflammation rather than the acute, vascular-calibrated vascular leak seen in mast cell or complement-mediator dependent angioedema.

Clinically, this differentiation is vital for management. Angioedema treated with epinephrine or bradykinin inhibition, whereas this case required intense immunosuppression (corticosteroids, IVIG, and rituximab). Therefore, any unrecognized antibody or muscle abnormality in the presence of neck swelling should prompt serologic screening for myositis antibodies—such as anti-SAE1—and creatine kinase levels to avoid delaying appropriate immunosuppressive therapy.

### [Management] Given the patient's interstitial lung disease markers and dysphagia history, why was methotrexate explicitly avoided in favor of mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab during the initial immunosuppressive regimen?

In the management of inflammatory myopathies, selecting an immunosuppressive strategy must balance efficacy against pulmonary toxicity risk. While methotrexate (MTX) is a standard first-line corticosteroid-sparing agent for dermatomyositis, it is associated with a risk of inducing or exacerbating interstitial lung disease (ILD).

In this case report, the team explicitly avoided MTX due to this specific pulmonary safety profile. Although the chest CT at initial presentation showed nodules and consolidation suggestive of aspiration (likely secondary to dysphagia) rather than definite fibrosis, the patient presented with abnormal pulmonary function tests indicative of a restrictive pattern. Given that SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis carries a high incidence of ILD and the patient had significant dysphagia predisposing him to aspiration-related lung injury, the MTX-related risk of pulmonary fibrosis was deemed too high. The team documented that MTX was not used "due to the association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, noting that the patient had abnormal lung function tests."

Consequently, the regimen prioritized mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab. These agents provided potent immunosuppression while minimizing the risk of drug-induced pulmonary toxicity. This case underscores a critical principle in rheumatology: when treating multiorgan disease like dermatomyositis, one must prioritize lung-sparing agents for patients with established respiratory compromise or high-risk serological markers to prevent progressive respiratory decline.

### [Management] How did the significantly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level influence the diagnostic workup of a patient with unexplained neck swelling, and why is this specific lab marker critical for distinguishing between a soft tissue infection/allergy and an inflammatory myopathy?

In cases of unexplained neck swelling that mimics angioedema or deep neck space infection, a significantly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level serves as the critical pivot point in the diagnostic algorithm, shifting the focus from eosinophilic allergies or infectious etiologies to inflammatory myopathy.

**Clinical Reasoning and Pathophysiology**
Soft tissue infections, angioedema, and isolated chemical hypersensitivity reactions typically involve vascular permeability or micro-infection without primary muscle necrosis; consequently, serum CK levels remain within or near the normal range. Conversely, inflammatory myopathies, such as dermatomyositis, cause direct destruction of muscle membranes and subsequent myophagocytosis. This necrotic process releases intramuscular enzymes, including a massive spike in CK. Therefore, a CK level in the tens of thousands is incompatible with non-muscle soft tissue pathology and strongly indicates active myositis.

**Case Illustration**
In the case of dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema, the patient presented with severe neck swelling and progressive proximal muscle weakness (hip flexors). Standard ENT and immunology workups for allergic angioidema (C1 esterase, ACE-inhibitor history) were negative. The cavity to initiating therapeutic intervention was profound: the requested CK was found to be **16,184 U/L**. This result did two critical things:
1.  It ruled out histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and primary cellulitis (which would not typically cause such massive enzyme leakage).
2.  It directed diagnostic efforts toward muscle biopsy and autoimmune panels (ultimately revealing the SAE1 antibody and perifascicular findings).

The elevated CK confirmed that the neck swelling was not merely edema or fluid accumulation in soft tissue, but inflammation originating from the sternocleidomastoid, strap, and skeletal muscles (as seen on MRI and PET), leading directly to the provision of prompt immunosuppression rather than antibiotics or antihistamines.

**Management Principle**
When evaluating unexplained head and neck swelling with associated myalgia or dysphagia, a markedly elevated CK level should immediately trigger an inflammatory myopathy workup. Misunderstanding this marker can delay life-saving immunosuppression in cases of severe airway-threatening myositis. In this case, early recognition allowed for the initiation of high-dose steroids, IVIG, and mycophenolate, preventing permanent airway compromise.

The patient required nasogastric (NG) tube feeding initially due to active inflammatory myositis affecting the pharyngeal muscles, which caused severe dysphagia and aspiration risk rather than a mechanical or structural airway obstruction. Although the dermal and subcutaneous edema of the neck resolved rapidly, the myopathic process in the swallowing complex persisted. Crucially, the 11-month follow-up CT chest showed near-complete resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation initially attributed to aspiration pneumonitis. This radiological improvement confirms that sustained immunosuppression was necessary to clear secondary lung injury, validating the protocol despite the rapid resolution of the initial neck swelling.
