# Hiccups as a Medical Red Flag: Recognizing SIADH in Neuroinflammation

There are some symptoms that scream "classic neuro" and others that whisper "inpatient metabolic hazard." A patient presenting with fever, headache, and **persistent hiccups** might immediately be routed for meningitis or a mass lesion. However, in a specific subset of autopsy or clinical cases, that incessant hiccupsing isn't a motor neuron firing problem—it's a desperate compensation for severe electrolyte disturbance. I recently reviewed a fascinating case where a male patient in his 60s presented with diffuse symptoms, but looking closely at the lab values and that specific symptom, a picture of acute neurological Behçet’s disease complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) emerged. This isn't just about the diagnosis; it's about recognizing the subtle neurological signs of profound hyponatremia before the rhabdomyolysis bite.

In this case, the surprise element was not just the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) with concurrent neurological involvement, but the systemic interplay between central inflammation and fluid balance. The patient arrived with fever, oral and genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes. Initially, the hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) was noted as a non-systemic electrolyte imbalance, likely due to SIADH. However, the neurological manifestations—headache, vertigo, and hiccups—were initially suspected to be central的标志. The crucial twist was that the hyponatremia persisted, and the neurological symptoms, including the hiccups, resolved with the correction of the inflammatory state. The key regulatory mechanism here is the detection of sodium suppression leading to increased antidiuretic hormone secretion to conserve water, which in turn led to profound hyponatremia.

## Why Hiccups Matter in the EVS (Early Ventricular Syndrome?? No, Endocrine/Volume Status)
In the realm of critical care and internal medicine, we often categorize SIADH as a "diagnosis of exclusion" following thyroid, adrenal, or renal function workups. However, when the CNS is inflamed, SIADH becomes a direct consequence of the central nervous system. The interpreter must understand that neurological symptoms like hiccups are not isolated phenomena but can signal cerebral salt wasting or SIADH severity.

The case highlights a specific neurological sequela of severe hyponatremia: persistent hiccups. The literature suggests that persistent hiccups may serve as an important clinical clue to severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. In this instance, the patient had been given natriuretic therapy or empiric trials initially, which failed. The solution lay in identifying the underlying etiology: the inflammatory cascade within the cranial vault due to Behçet’s.

Clinical alerts often go silent because the hyponatremia is masked by fever and metabolic cytokine release. But here, the body tried to adjust the water balance through a classic SIADH pathway (elevated ADH 22.4 pg/mL + elevated urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg). The rhabdomyolysis observed (elevated creatine kinase) was a secondary effect of the incredibly severe hyponatremia that was present before the hiccups were noticed by some, and often missed by others. The ability to filter these CSF findings (aseptic meningitis, no malignant cells, no viral antigen) to the lab findings is crucial for the diagnosis.

## Diagnostic Pitfalls in Behçet’s
The case highlights another common pitfall: the reliance on brain imaging in the acute phase. This patient had a confirmed diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s based on mucocutaneous features and CSF analysis. However, the Brain MRI showed **no significant abnormalities**. This is a classic teaching point for clinicians with a high index of suspicion for neuro-BD that MRI has missed.

Acute neuro-BD is characterized by CNS inflammation, which may not manifest radiologically in the malt time of meningoencephalitis. Brain MRI revealed no specific abnormalities at the typical BD lesion sites. Typical BD lesions often occur around 4 years after initial diagnosis, but concurrent diagnosis with systemic disease and neurological symptoms is rarer. In this case, the diagnosis relied heavily on international criteria: two oral aphthae, two genital ulcers, one folliculitis-like rash (totaling 5 points), and exclusion of other causes. It underscores a vital teaching moment: **Availing for investigation MRI is not absolute; a normal MRI does not rule out early neuro-BD.**

The other diagnosis included was sarcoidosis. The measurement of ACE (normal) and sIL-2R (mildly elevated) played a role in ruling this out. However, the case injection is that **limiting imaging to cerebral specific organs is not enough**. Checking for genetic links (HLA-B51 positive) was done, but the guidelines don't require HLA testing. The reliance on clinical features is paramount.

## Treatment and Recovery
Once the diagnosis was solidified, the treatment path presents its own educational value. The standard of care for acute neuro-BD is corticosteroids. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy. Colchicine is widely used for mucocutaneous manifestations in BD. The observation is that colchicine reduced neutrophil migration and activation and suppressed cytokine production, including IL-6 and IL-1β.

In this case, the patient's fever resolved, the hiccups stopped, and the hyponatremia corrected with fluid restriction alone. Hippogenic vessels (hypertonic saline) and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not required. The resolution suggests a possible direct association between the inflammatory resolution and the fluid imbalance resolution. This indicates that sometimes, the choice to avoid steroid-induced side effects early on may be safe, provided the inflammation is the primary driver. It emphasizes that treating the underlying inflammation (via colchicine) can resolve the SIADH as a secondary effect.

## Key Takeaways
*   **Hiccups as a Flag:** Persistent hiccups in the context of fever and headache should trigger a workup for SIADH and its neurological sequelae, especially when hyponatremia is profound.
*   **Normal MRI is not Diagnostic Exclusion:** In early acute neuro-BD, parenchymal lesions may be absent on MRI. Rely solely on clinical symptoms and CSF analysis if infection is ruled out.
*   **Silent SIADH:** Hyponatremia in the context of inflammation can lead to severe complications like rhabdomyolysis. Look for creatine kinase elevations early.
*   **BD Criteria:** Behçet's disease is often underdiagnosed until early systemic signs appear. A diagnosis of "definite" BD relies on medium strength criteria clinical scores. Never rely on HLA-B51 as a sole diagnostic tool.
*   **Treatment Flexibility:** Colchicine monotherapy can be effective in acute neuro-BD and associated SIADH if available, particularly when steroids are contraindicated.

Ultimately, this case is a reminder that "idiopathic" fits often explode into specific inflammatory conditions when you pair clinical history with electrolyte numbers. Don't let the normal MRI convince you of normal neurobiology.



# When MRI is Normal but the Nerves Are Hurting: Diagnosing Acute Neuro-Behçet's

We've all encountered that burn. A patient walks in with fever, headache, and a "normal" MRI, and you feel 99% dominated by the scan. If the radiology report comes back clean, you tend to trust it. But in the world of Behçet's disease (BD), trust is a dangerous malpractice when the neurological symptoms are objective, even if the structures look intact. We recently reviewed a case of acute neuro-Behçet's where the brain MRI revealed nothing, yet the patient was suffering from aseptic meningitis and a rare endocrine complication. This discrepancy is the gold standard for learning to ignore what isn't there and rely on what is.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: When the Brain Looks Normal but Isn't

The classic presentation of Neuro-Behçet's usually involves parenchymal brain lesions, often in the brainstem. It's expected to see signal abnormalities on MRI in this classic form. However, in this case, the patient—a man in his 60s—presents with fever, headache, and global dysfunction. The clinical team ordered a study, and the brain MRIs showed no significant abnormalities.

This is classic distractor. If you stop at the MRI, you might miss the ventilation failure waiting for you. In early neuro-BD, particularly when presenting with aseptic meningitis without parenchymal extension, MRI sensitivity drops. The American Society of Neurology goes to two things: clinical suspicion and CSF analysis. In this case, the CSF was the game changer. Analysis revealed an increased protein level (0.97 g/L) and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL).

This is where the diagnostic path diverges. You have to rely on the chemistry and the inflammation count rather than the visible lesions. With CSF culture and viral tests for HSV, VZV, and Cryptococcus negative, the workup shifts from "Staging" to "Differential chaos." Clinicians need to remember that CNS inflammation doesn't always break a bone or stain white. A normal MRI effectively rules out the massive parenchymal lesions, but it does not rule out the what you suspect is happening.

Wait, there's more: SIADH. The patient presented with striking hyponatremia (124 mmol/L). In the face of fever and headache, SIADH is often the last thing on the local doctor's list before pneumonia or heart failure. Yet, in this case, SIADH was the endocrine manifestation of the SIADH (inflammation). It wasn't a drug effect (cyclophosphamide), it was a direct CNS inflammation response.

## The SIADH Connection and Treatment Surprise

The interplay between acute neuro-BD and SIADH makes this a place where you can't ignore electrolytes. The case highlights that IL-6, a pro-inflammatory cytokine often elevated in neuro-BD, drives ADH release. When neuroinflammation is high, Sodium gets locked out of the system.

This case highlights the paradox of treatment. Standard of care for acute neuro-BD for years has been aggressive immunosuppression—corticosteroids, IVIG, Azathioprine. The patient here responded to Colchicine monotherapy. Talk about a board exam question. Usually, if something is that neuro, steroids need to be thrown upfront to prevent herniation or permanent damage. This patient's fever resolved in two weeks, and the hyponatremia normalized without a single line of steroids or immunomodulators.

Of course, spontaneous resolution or a uniquely responsive patient isn't always the norm. But the takeaway here is that in a board setting or simplified clinical protocol, you don't always need the heavy artillery. Colchicine alone inhibited neutrophil migration and suppressed cytokine production enough to quiet the storm.

The case also brought to light that hiccups are a red flag for CNS lymphocytic pleocytosis and severe hyponatremia. Persistent hiccups, let's be honest, aren't always just "stomach upset." They can signal a central drive in the somatic nervous system.

## Key Takeaways

*   **MRI is not always the end:** In acute neuro-Behçet's, spinal cord or brain imaging can be normal early on. Relying on normal neuroimaging can lead to delayed diagnosis of aseptic meningitis.
*   **CSF analysis is your trump card:** When infectious screens are negative and structural imaging is clean, look for elevated protein and lymphocytic pleocytosis to rule out structural and autoimmune etiologies.
*   **Hyponatremia is a compass:** SIADH in a patient with non-specific CNS symptoms (fever, headache, hiccups) should trigger a search for inflammatory etiologies over metabolic ones.
*   **Colchicine has a moment:** While corticosteroids are standard, colchicine can be the primary treatment in acute neuro-Behçet's, particularly in specific subtypes or where allergies/contraindications prevent heavier immunosuppression.



# Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-Behçet's: A Controversial Approach

Acute neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) is the clinician’s nectar and worry for the top ten percent of patients. While we generally expect steers or cyclophosphamide to calm the storm, we recently reviewed a case where the brain inflammation and the hyponatremia settled down without systemic steroids. It’s a sequence of events that suggests we might need to re-evaluate the therapeutic dogmas when the magnetic resonance is normal but the meniscus symptoms are screaming.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: When MRI is Normal

Neuro-Behçet’s is typically reserved for when you see brain parenchymal lesions on an MRI, often in the brainstem. But imagine a 60-year-old man reporting fever, headache, hiccups, and kit end, off. His MRI comes back normal. The CSF shows aseptic meningitis—mild protein and lymphocytic pleocytosis—but no infection on cultures. The clinical picture screams neuro-BD (definite by International Criteria based on oral/genital ulcers), yet the rules say MRI positivity is expected.

Spot wearing the picture was technically the hyponatremia. A sodium of 124 mmol/L, combined with inappropriately high urine osmolality and elevated serum ADH, confirmed Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH). This particular combination of CNS inflammation and SIADH was complicated by persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase, suggesting rhabdomyolysis.

Here are a few things to keep in mind regarding the presentation:

*   Acute neuro-BD often presents as aseptic meningitis without visible parenchymal lesions on early MRI.
*   SIADH is a recognized, though rare, manifestation of CNS inflammation associated with neuro-BD.
*   Symptoms like persistent hiccups or elevated CK (rhabdomyolysis) can be critical clues to severe hyponatremia in this population.

## The Treatment Curve: Steers or Colchicine?

The gold standard for neuro-BD is typically corticosteroids, sometimes paired with IVIG or azathioprine. For this patient, that was not the protocol. After excluding infection and malignancy, the team initiated colchicine monotherapy. The response was rapid. Within 16 days, the fever cleared, the neurological symptoms resolved, and crucially, the sodium normalized.

This presents a classic "real vs. myth" moment. Standard reviews note that colchicine is usually ineffective for acute neuro-BD and is typically an adjunct, not monotherapy. In this scenario, avoiding systemic immunosuppressants likely minimized risks to the patient, such as infections or myopathy, but it relies on the unique inflammatory mediators present in this specific subset of patients.

The lack of filtered metals (MRI lesions) complicates the prognosis. It means you can’t see the damage, but patients can still be in a severe inflammatory state with a normal radiographic baseline.

## When to Suspect Neuro-BD with Hyponatremia

This report underscores a specific lens: a nervous system patient with fever and headache plus hyponatremia. The SIADH isn't just noise; it’s part of the inflammatory cascade. Elevated IL-6, a driver of neuro-BD inflammation, is linked to increased ADH release. If you see a patient who has oral ulcers and now has a sigma of 124, colchicine might be the answer before you reach for the punch of steroids.

It is important to note that this case is an outlier. As this report suggests, the temporal association in a new case might reflect the intensity of the inflammatory response rather than a universal rule. However, the safety of avoiding steroids in a patient unable or unsuitable for them is a practical lesson.

## Take-Home Messages

*   Norm MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD; aseptic meningitis can be the primary finding.
*   SIADH may parallel acute neuro-BD due to IL-6 mediated inflammation, warranting fluid restriction.
*   Hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be overlooked markers for severe hyponatremia in neuro-BD.
*   In select cases of acute neuro-BD, colchicine monotherapy may resolve symptoms without the need for systemic corticosteroids.



## Febrile Hyponatremia: Navigating the Differential Between Infection and Autoimmune Inflammation

When a patient presents with fever, headache, and hyponatremia, the instinctive reflex is often to reach for the antimicrobial gloves first. But in a patient in his sixties with a constellation of mucocutaneous findings, the calculus shifts rapidly. Here is a rare but instructive case from a patient with concurrent systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), acute neurological involvement, and Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH). The surprise here isn't just the diagnosis; it's the outcome. This patient responded to colchicine monotherapy, resolving neurological symptoms and hyponatremia without the need for high-dose corticosteroids, a therapeutic shift often reserved for more aggressive induction of acute neuro-BD.

The presentation began with a classic triage puzzle. The 60-something male arrived with a temperature of 38.1°C, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia, accompanied by oral aphthae. The hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) was profound enough to explain elevated creatine kinase levels indicative of subtle rhabdomyolysis, a subtle complication of massive water retention often missed without targeted electrolyte monitoring. While the fever and headache scream infection, the concurrent aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like rashes immediately pushed the agent back to a systemic inflammatory diagnosis. However, before cementing the autoimmune label, the workup was executed using a rigorous exclusion strategy to rule out malignancy and infection.

The diagnostic landscape was crowded with differentials. Standard neuroviruses like HSV and VZV were screened via PCR and remained negative. Bacterial meningitis was dismissed due to the lack of neutrophilic pleocytosis, and tuberculosis was excluded with specific cultures and DNA testing. As part of that broad evaluation, which includes testing for lysozyme, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), and soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels, sarcoidosis and hematological malignancy were considered. While sIL-2R was mildly elevated, no diagnostic imaging supported a diagnosis of lymphoma or CNS tumor, and the brain MRI was notably normal.

In neuro-BD, one might expect MRI abnormalities involving the brainstem or hypothalamus. In this rare acute presentation, imaging was unremarkable, yet the cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein and opening pressure. This discrepancy highlights a crucial diagnostic nuance: a normal neuro-BD scan does not rule out the diagnosis of aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal lesions. The absence of an underlying carcinomatous or viral etiology confirmed a probable autoimmune etiology, and the definitive International Behçet’s Disease score was reached based on mucocutaneous findings (5 points total, including genital aphthosis).

SIADH is a well-established consequence of CNS inflammation, but linking it specifically to acute neuro-BD without pharmacological triggers is elusive. The case details demonstrate a crucial association: the patient had elevated ADH and inappropriately concentrated urine despite hypotonic serum. The temporal relationship between the neurological inflammation and the electrolyte disturbance suggests that the IL-6–mediated cytokine storm characteristic of BD inflammation directly drives ADH release. Recognizing persistent hiccups as a potential clue to severe hyponatremia, which can precipitate rhabdomyolysis, is a clinical pearl that requires heightened vigilance in febrile CNS cases.

The treatment pathway was as intriguing as the diagnosis. While corticosteroids are typically the mainstay for acute neuro-BD due to their broad anti-inflammatory effects, this patient required nothing more than colchicine. Oral colchicine exerts anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing cytokine production, specifically IL-1β and IL-6. Over the course of treatment, mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations like vertigo and headache, and serum sodium normalization to 136 mmol/L all resolved simultaneously. The patient maintained good control for four years with this regimen alone, sparing him from the long-term side effects of immunosuppressant therapy.

## The Take-Home Messages

*   **Hyponatremia requires structural investigation:** In any febrile CNS patient with significant electrolyte derangement, persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK) should immediately prompt a check for severe hyponatremia, a phenomenon often linked to SIADH from CNS inflammation.
*   **Exclusion is impossible before the right diagnosis:** When neuro-inflammation is unresolvable, ensure you have exhausted infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune differentials using sIL-2R, CSF pleocytosis patterns, and imaging. A normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro-BD, but CSF findings of aseptic meningitis are supportive.
*   **Therapeutic thresholds are debatable:** While colchicine is standard for mucocutaneous Behçet’s, its use as a monotherapy for acute neuro-BD is rare. This case suggests that if steroids are contraindicated, early colchicine may be a viable strategy to prevent the progression of neurological decline.
*   **SIADH is not always drug-induced:** Be vigilant for SIADH in patients with acute neuro-BD regardless of whether they are on cyclophosphamide or other agents. CNS inflammation itself is a valid trigger for ADH dysregulation in this context.



# The Older Behçet's Patient: Managing Late-Onset Disease Versus Behçet's' Paranoia

Behçet's disease is a fascinating diagnosis but a notorious one for its late-diagnosis delays and systemic variability. When we see a 60-year-old man presenting with fever, aphthous ulcers, and what sounds like side effects of a new drug—but turns out to be an immune dysregulation itself—we get into the weeds. This case report comes from Kakehi et al., focusing on a rare overlap of systemic Behçet's disease (BD), acute neurological involvement (neuro-BD), and a metabolic disaster we might not expect: Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH).

The real kicker here isn't just that the patient is 60—it's the diagnostic outline. Neuro-behem's typically follows an indeterminate period after the initial mucocutaneous diagnosis. This man has concurrent systemic findings at presentation. Furthermore, the neurological complications of his condition didn't show up on a brain MRI, leaving the team to rely entirely on CSF analysis and clinical deduction to reach the diagnosis. And at the end of it all, the treatment didn't involve the heavy immunosuppression we often debate for neurological involvement in BD. It was colchicine.

## Why This Case Is an EM "Frankenstein" Surprise

In our daily practice, we often rely on neuroimaging to validate a neurological workup. When a patient presents with acute encephalopathy or meningoencephalitis signs, we expect to see lesions on the brainstem or diffuse parenchymal changes. Here, MRI was normal. Instead, the authors had to use brain MRI findings to essentially be *normal* to support the diagnosis, alongside cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showing mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein.

This presentation mimics the "paranoia" or functional psychiatric deficits that Behçet's can mimic, but the clinical focus here is on the metabolic partner complication. SIADH is common in CNS inflammation, but reporting it in the absence of malignancy or medication triggers is rare. In this specific case, the elevated serum CRP suggests a systemic inflammatory response, specifically the role of cytokines like IL-6 in the neuroinflammation cascade.

Recognizing that acute neuro-BD can present with aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions is critical. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion when combined with mucocutaneous findings. If you have a 60-year-old with genital ulcers and hyponatremia, don't just reach for a viral panel. Consider the vasculitis.

## The SIADH-Neuro-BD Connection

As the case details move toward the diagnosis of SIADH, the hyponatremia wasn't a passive finding; it was an active marker of the neuroinflammatory state. The patient had significant hiccups and loss of appetite, which are often overlooked clusters of symptoms. These are classic clues to severe hyponatremia associated with the central nervous system inflammation of neuro-BD.

When brain MRI shows no lesions, the pathophysiology often lies in the autoimmune dysregulation of the hypothalamus or the meningeal inflammation itself, rather than a focal tumor or stroke. The hyponatremia persisted and fluctuated even as myocutaneous symptoms were managed, driving the need for fluid restriction and careful electrolyte monitoring. This resonance between the cytokine storm driving the mucocutaneous inflammation and the SIADH suggests a systemic nature to the acute activation, not just localized CNS damage.

The CRP was elevated significantly (180 mg/L), and while IL-6 was not directly measured, the clinical picture points to systemic activation rather than isolated CNS pathology. However, it is important to note that this case highlights the low baseline of SIADH in BD compared to other CNS infections. It makes the subset of BD patients with concurrent neurological and endocrine findings particularly prone to electrolyte mismanagement.

## Colchicine Monotherapy: A Remedy Worth Discussing

Here is the biggest clinical pearl. Most guidelines for acute neuro-BD suggest corticosteroids or high-dose immunosuppressants like cyclophosphamide to induce rapid neurological remission. However, in this patient, corticosteroids were not used. Instead, the patient was treated empirically with colchicine monotherapy. Within a week of colchicine initiation, fever resolved, mucocutaneous symptoms cleared, neurological symptoms (including vertigo and hiccups), and hyponatremia normalized.

This raises a significant point of debate. While colchicine's role in neuro-BD is traditionally considered limited or adjunctive, spontaneous resolution of acute meningoencephalitis presentation is well documented in some contexts. This case suggests that colchicine may offer a viable option, particularly when starting with fluid restriction alone managing SIADH. This is timely given the push for alternative steroid-sparing therapy in the older population to minimize long-term immunosuppression risks.

## Googling the Case: What to Look For

The takeaway here is not just that he had Behçet's, but that he had Behçet's *originally mistaken for infection* and ended up with a metabolic storm.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages

*   **Rarity:** Immediate recognition of neuro-BD in the late-onset phase (>50s) requires a high index of suspicion when accompanied by mucocutaneous signs, as MRI often remains negative in early erosive inflammation.
*   **SIADH Link:** Sympathetic dysfunction in neuro-BD may predispose patients to SIADH; consider electrolyte disturbances in any patient with fever, headache, and non-specific CNS findings who has genital/oral ulcers.
*   **Therapeutic Trial:** Colchicine monotherapy was effective in this case, suggesting that it may be a valuable consideration in patients with acute neuro-BD who do not require heavy immunosuppression.
*   **Hiccups & Rhabdomyolysis:** Monitor for severe hyponatremia complications (hiccups, rhabdomyolysis) in neuro-BD settings, as these symptoms may be under-appreciated indicators of electrolyte dysregulation in the acute phase.
*   **Differential:** Always exclude malignancy before concluding neuro-BD, particularly in older adults where sIL-2R can be mildly elevated but non-specific.
