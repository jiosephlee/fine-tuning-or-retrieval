Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-Behçet's: A Controversial Approach

Acute neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) is the clinician’s nectar and worry for the top ten percent of patients. While we generally expect steers or cyclophosphamide to calm the storm, we recently reviewed a case where the brain inflammation and the hyponatremia settled down without systemic steroids. It’s a sequence of events that suggests we might need to re-evaluate the therapeutic dogmas when the magnetic resonance is normal but the meniscus symptoms are screaming.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: When MRI is Normal

Neuro-Behçet’s is typically reserved for when you see brain parenchymal lesions on an MRI, often in the brainstem. But imagine a 60-year-old man reporting fever, headache, hiccups, and kit end, off. His MRI comes back normal. The CSF shows aseptic meningitis—mild protein and lymphocytic pleocytosis—but no infection on cultures. The clinical picture screams neuro-BD (definite by International Criteria based on oral/genital ulcers), yet the rules say MRI positivity is expected.

Spot wearing the picture was technically the hyponatremia. A sodium of 124 mmol/L, combined with inappropriately high urine osmolality and elevated serum ADH, confirmed Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH). This particular combination of CNS inflammation and SIADH was complicated by persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase, suggesting rhabdomyolysis.

Here are a few things to keep in mind regarding the presentation:

*   Acute neuro-BD often presents as aseptic meningitis without visible parenchymal lesions on early MRI.
*   SIADH is a recognized, though rare, manifestation of CNS inflammation associated with neuro-BD.
*   Symptoms like persistent hiccups or elevated CK (rhabdomyolysis) can be critical clues to severe hyponatremia in this population.

## The Treatment Curve: Steers or Colchicine?

The gold standard for neuro-BD is typically corticosteroids, sometimes paired with IVIG or azathioprine. For this patient, that was not the protocol. After excluding infection and malignancy, the team initiated colchicine monotherapy. The response was rapid. Within 16 days, the fever cleared, the neurological symptoms resolved, and crucially, the sodium normalized.

This presents a classic "real vs. myth" moment. Standard reviews note that colchicine is usually ineffective for acute neuro-BD and is typically an adjunct, not monotherapy. In this scenario, avoiding systemic immunosuppressants likely minimized risks to the patient, such as infections or myopathy, but it relies on the unique inflammatory mediators present in this specific subset of patients.

The lack of filtered metals (MRI lesions) complicates the prognosis. It means you can’t see the damage, but patients can still be in a severe inflammatory state with a normal radiographic baseline.

## When to Suspect Neuro-BD with Hyponatremia

This report underscores a specific lens: a nervous system patient with fever and headache plus hyponatremia. The SIADH isn't just noise; it’s part of the inflammatory cascade. Elevated IL-6, a driver of neuro-BD inflammation, is linked to increased ADH release. If you see a patient who has oral ulcers and now has a sigma of 124, colchicine might be the answer before you reach for the punch of steroids.

It is important to note that this case is an outlier. As this report suggests, the temporal association in a new case might reflect the intensity of the inflammatory response rather than a universal rule. However, the safety of avoiding steroids in a patient unable or unsuitable for them is a practical lesson.

## Take-Home Messages

*   Norm MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD; aseptic meningitis can be the primary finding.
*   SIADH may parallel acute neuro-BD due to IL-6 mediated inflammation, warranting fluid restriction.
*   Hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be overlooked markers for severe hyponatremia in neuro-BD.
*   In select cases of acute neuro-BD, colchicine monotherapy may resolve symptoms without the need for systemic corticosteroids.
