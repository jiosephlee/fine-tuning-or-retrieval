Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When MRI is Normal but the Nerves Are Hurting: Diagnosing Acute Neuro-Behçet's

We've all encountered that burn. A patient walks in with fever, headache, and a "normal" MRI, and you feel 99% dominated by the scan. If the radiology report comes back clean, you tend to trust it. But in the world of Behçet's disease (BD), trust is a dangerous malpractice when the neurological symptoms are objective, even if the structures look intact. We recently reviewed a case of acute neuro-Behçet's where the brain MRI revealed nothing, yet the patient was suffering from aseptic meningitis and a rare endocrine complication. This discrepancy is the gold standard for learning to ignore what isn't there and rely on what is.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: When the Brain Looks Normal but Isn't

The classic presentation of Neuro-Behçet's usually involves parenchymal brain lesions, often in the brainstem. It's expected to see signal abnormalities on MRI in this classic form. However, in this case, the patient—a man in his 60s—presents with fever, headache, and global dysfunction. The clinical team ordered a study, and the brain MRIs showed no significant abnormalities.

This is classic distractor. If you stop at the MRI, you might miss the ventilation failure waiting for you. In early neuro-BD, particularly when presenting with aseptic meningitis without parenchymal extension, MRI sensitivity drops. The American Society of Neurology goes to two things: clinical suspicion and CSF analysis. In this case, the CSF was the game changer. Analysis revealed an increased protein level (0.97 g/L) and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL).

This is where the diagnostic path diverges. You have to rely on the chemistry and the inflammation count rather than the visible lesions. With CSF culture and viral tests for HSV, VZV, and Cryptococcus negative, the workup shifts from "Staging" to "Differential chaos." Clinicians need to remember that CNS inflammation doesn't always break a bone or stain white. A normal MRI effectively rules out the massive parenchymal lesions, but it does not rule out the what you suspect is happening.

Wait, there's more: SIADH. The patient presented with striking hyponatremia (124 mmol/L). In the face of fever and headache, SIADH is often the last thing on the local doctor's list before pneumonia or heart failure. Yet, in this case, SIADH was the endocrine manifestation of the SIADH (inflammation). It wasn't a drug effect (cyclophosphamide), it was a direct CNS inflammation response.

## The SIADH Connection and Treatment Surprise

The interplay between acute neuro-BD and SIADH makes this a place where you can't ignore electrolytes. The case highlights that IL-6, a pro-inflammatory cytokine often elevated in neuro-BD, drives ADH release. When neuroinflammation is high, Sodium gets locked out of the system.

This case highlights the paradox of treatment. Standard of care for acute neuro-BD for years has been aggressive immunosuppression—corticosteroids, IVIG, Azathioprine. The patient here responded to Colchicine monotherapy. Talk about a board exam question. Usually, if something is that neuro, steroids need to be thrown upfront to prevent herniation or permanent damage. This patient's fever resolved in two weeks, and the hyponatremia normalized without a single line of steroids or immunomodulators.

Of course, spontaneous resolution or a uniquely responsive patient isn't always the norm. But the takeaway here is that in a board setting or simplified clinical protocol, you don't always need the heavy artillery. Colchicine alone inhibited neutrophil migration and suppressed cytokine production enough to quiet the storm.

The case also brought to light that hiccups are a red flag for CNS lymphocytic pleocytosis and severe hyponatremia. Persistent hiccups, let's be honest, aren't always just "stomach upset." They can signal a central drive in the somatic nervous system.

## Key Takeaways

*   **MRI is not always the end:** In acute neuro-Behçet's, spinal cord or brain imaging can be normal early on. Relying on normal neuroimaging can lead to delayed diagnosis of aseptic meningitis.
*   **CSF analysis is your trump card:** When infectious screens are negative and structural imaging is clean, look for elevated protein and lymphocytic pleocytosis to rule out structural and autoimmune etiologies.
*   **Hyponatremia is a compass:** SIADH in a patient with non-specific CNS symptoms (fever, headache, hiccups) should trigger a search for inflammatory etiologies over metabolic ones.
*   **Colchicine has a moment:** While corticosteroids are standard, colchicine can be the primary treatment in acute neuro-Behçet's, particularly in specific subtypes or where allergies/contraindications prevent heavier immunosuppression.
