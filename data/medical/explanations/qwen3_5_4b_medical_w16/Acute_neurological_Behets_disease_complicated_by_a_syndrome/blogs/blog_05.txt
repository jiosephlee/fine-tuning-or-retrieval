Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Older Behçet's Patient: Managing Late-Onset Disease Versus Behçet's' Paranoia

Behçet's disease is a fascinating diagnosis but a notorious one for its late-diagnosis delays and systemic variability. When we see a 60-year-old man presenting with fever, aphthous ulcers, and what sounds like side effects of a new drug—but turns out to be an immune dysregulation itself—we get into the weeds. This case report comes from Kakehi et al., focusing on a rare overlap of systemic Behçet's disease (BD), acute neurological involvement (neuro-BD), and a metabolic disaster we might not expect: Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH).

The real kicker here isn't just that the patient is 60—it's the diagnostic outline. Neuro-behem's typically follows an indeterminate period after the initial mucocutaneous diagnosis. This man has concurrent systemic findings at presentation. Furthermore, the neurological complications of his condition didn't show up on a brain MRI, leaving the team to rely entirely on CSF analysis and clinical deduction to reach the diagnosis. And at the end of it all, the treatment didn't involve the heavy immunosuppression we often debate for neurological involvement in BD. It was colchicine.

## Why This Case Is an EM "Frankenstein" Surprise

In our daily practice, we often rely on neuroimaging to validate a neurological workup. When a patient presents with acute encephalopathy or meningoencephalitis signs, we expect to see lesions on the brainstem or diffuse parenchymal changes. Here, MRI was normal. Instead, the authors had to use brain MRI findings to essentially be *normal* to support the diagnosis, alongside cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showing mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein.

This presentation mimics the "paranoia" or functional psychiatric deficits that Behçet's can mimic, but the clinical focus here is on the metabolic partner complication. SIADH is common in CNS inflammation, but reporting it in the absence of malignancy or medication triggers is rare. In this specific case, the elevated serum CRP suggests a systemic inflammatory response, specifically the role of cytokines like IL-6 in the neuroinflammation cascade.

Recognizing that acute neuro-BD can present with aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions is critical. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion when combined with mucocutaneous findings. If you have a 60-year-old with genital ulcers and hyponatremia, don't just reach for a viral panel. Consider the vasculitis.

## The SIADH-Neuro-BD Connection

As the case details move toward the diagnosis of SIADH, the hyponatremia wasn't a passive finding; it was an active marker of the neuroinflammatory state. The patient had significant hiccups and loss of appetite, which are often overlooked clusters of symptoms. These are classic clues to severe hyponatremia associated with the central nervous system inflammation of neuro-BD.

When brain MRI shows no lesions, the pathophysiology often lies in the autoimmune dysregulation of the hypothalamus or the meningeal inflammation itself, rather than a focal tumor or stroke. The hyponatremia persisted and fluctuated even as myocutaneous symptoms were managed, driving the need for fluid restriction and careful electrolyte monitoring. This resonance between the cytokine storm driving the mucocutaneous inflammation and the SIADH suggests a systemic nature to the acute activation, not just localized CNS damage.

The CRP was elevated significantly (180 mg/L), and while IL-6 was not directly measured, the clinical picture points to systemic activation rather than isolated CNS pathology. However, it is important to note that this case highlights the low baseline of SIADH in BD compared to other CNS infections. It makes the subset of BD patients with concurrent neurological and endocrine findings particularly prone to electrolyte mismanagement.

## Colchicine Monotherapy: A Remedy Worth Discussing

Here is the biggest clinical pearl. Most guidelines for acute neuro-BD suggest corticosteroids or high-dose immunosuppressants like cyclophosphamide to induce rapid neurological remission. However, in this patient, corticosteroids were not used. Instead, the patient was treated empirically with colchicine monotherapy. Within a week of colchicine initiation, fever resolved, mucocutaneous symptoms cleared, neurological symptoms (including vertigo and hiccups), and hyponatremia normalized.

This raises a significant point of debate. While colchicine's role in neuro-BD is traditionally considered limited or adjunctive, spontaneous resolution of acute meningoencephalitis presentation is well documented in some contexts. This case suggests that colchicine may offer a viable option, particularly when starting with fluid restriction alone managing SIADH. This is timely given the push for alternative steroid-sparing therapy in the older population to minimize long-term immunosuppression risks.

## Googling the Case: What to Look For

The takeaway here is not just that he had Behçet's, but that he had Behçet's *originally mistaken for infection* and ended up with a metabolic storm.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages

*   **Rarity:** Immediate recognition of neuro-BD in the late-onset phase (>50s) requires a high index of suspicion when accompanied by mucocutaneous signs, as MRI often remains negative in early erosive inflammation.
*   **SIADH Link:** Sympathetic dysfunction in neuro-BD may predispose patients to SIADH; consider electrolyte disturbances in any patient with fever, headache, and non-specific CNS findings who has genital/oral ulcers.
*   **Therapeutic Trial:** Colchicine monotherapy was effective in this case, suggesting that it may be a valuable consideration in patients with acute neuro-BD who do not require heavy immunosuppression.
*   **Hiccups & Rhabdomyolysis:** Monitor for severe hyponatremia complications (hiccups, rhabdomyolysis) in neuro-BD settings, as these symptoms may be under-appreciated indicators of electrolyte dysregulation in the acute phase.
*   **Differential:** Always exclude malignancy before concluding neuro-BD, particularly in older adults where sIL-2R can be mildly elevated but non-specific.
