Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Hiccups as a Medical Red Flag: Recognizing SIADH in Neuroinflammation

There are some symptoms that scream "classic neuro" and others that whisper "inpatient metabolic hazard." A patient presenting with fever, headache, and **persistent hiccups** might immediately be routed for meningitis or a mass lesion. However, in a specific subset of autopsy or clinical cases, that incessant hiccupsing isn't a motor neuron firing problem—it's a desperate compensation for severe electrolyte disturbance. I recently reviewed a fascinating case where a male patient in his 60s presented with diffuse symptoms, but looking closely at the lab values and that specific symptom, a picture of acute neurological Behçet’s disease complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) emerged. This isn't just about the diagnosis; it's about recognizing the subtle neurological signs of profound hyponatremia before the rhabdomyolysis bite.

In this case, the surprise element was not just the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) with concurrent neurological involvement, but the systemic interplay between central inflammation and fluid balance. The patient arrived with fever, oral and genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes. Initially, the hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) was noted as a non-systemic electrolyte imbalance, likely due to SIADH. The crucial twist was that the hyponatremia persisted, and the neurological symptoms, including the hiccups, resolved with the correction of the inflammatory state. The key regulatory mechanism here is the detection of sodium suppression leading to increased antidiuretic hormone secretion to conserve water, which in turn led to profound hyponatremia.

## Why Hiccups Matter in the EVS (Early Ventricular Syndrome?? No, Endocrine/Volume Status)
In the realm of critical care and internal medicine, we often categorize SIADH as a "diagnosis of exclusion" following thyroid, adrenal, or renal function workups. However, when the CNS is inflamed, SIADH becomes a direct consequence of the central nervous system. The interpreter must understand that neurological symptoms like hiccups are not isolated phenomena but can signal cerebral salt wasting or SIADH severity.

The case highlights a specific neurological sequela of severe hyponatremia: persistent hiccups. The literature suggests that persistent hiccups may serve as an important clinical clue to severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. In this instance, the patient had been given natriuretic therapy or empiric trials initially, which failed. The solution lay in identifying the underlying etiology: the inflammatory cascade within the cranial vault due to Behçet’s.

Clinical alerts often go silent because the hyponatremia is masked by fever and metabolic cytokine release. But here, the body tried to adjust the water balance through a classic SIADH pathway (elevated ADH 22.4 pg/mL + elevated urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg). The rhabdomyolysis observed (elevated creatine kinase) was a secondary effect of the incredibly severe hyponatremia that was present before the hiccups were noticed by some, and often missed by others. The ability to filter these CSF findings (aseptic meningitis, no malignant cells, no viral antigen) to the lab findings is crucial for the diagnosis.

## Diagnostic Pitfalls in Behçet’s
The case highlights another common pitfall: the reliance on brain imaging in the acute phase. This patient had a confirmed diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s based on mucocutaneous features and CSF analysis. However, the Brain MRI showed **no significant abnormalities**. This is a classic teaching point for clinicians with a high index of suspicion for neuro-BD that MRI has missed.

Acute neuro-BD is characterized by CNS inflammation, which may not manifest radiologically in the malt time of meningoencephalitis. Brain MRI revealed no specific abnormalities at the typical BD lesion sites. Typical BD lesions often occur around 4 years after initial diagnosis, but concurrent diagnosis with systemic disease and neurological symptoms is rarer. In this case, the diagnosis relied heavily on international criteria: two oral aphthae, two genital ulcers, one folliculitis-like rash (totaling 5 points), and exclusion of other causes. It underscores a vital teaching moment: **Availing for investigation MRI is not absolute; a normal MRI does not rule out early neuro-BD.**

The other diagnosis included was sarcoidosis. The measurement of ACE (normal) and sIL-2R (mildly elevated) played a role in ruling this out. However, the case injection is that **limiting imaging to cerebral specific organs is not enough**. Checking for genetic links (HLA-B51 positive) was done, but the guidelines don't require HLA testing. The reliance on clinical features is paramount.

## Treatment and Recovery
Once the diagnosis was solidified, the treatment path presents its own educational value. The standard of care for acute neuro-BD is corticosteroids. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy. Colchicine is widely used for mucocutaneous manifestations in BD. The observation is that colchicine reduced neutrophil migration and activation and suppressed cytokine production, including IL-6 and IL-1β.

In this case, the patient's fever resolved, the hiccups stopped, and the hyponatremia corrected with fluid restriction alone. Hippogenic vessels (hypertonic saline) and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not required. The resolution suggests a possible direct association between the inflammatory resolution and the fluid imbalance resolution. This indicates that sometimes, the choice to avoid steroid-induced side effects early on may be safe, provided the inflammation is the primary driver. It emphasizes that treating the underlying inflammation (via colchicine) can resolve the SIADH as a secondary effect.

## Key Takeaways
*   **Hiccups as a Flag:** Persistent hiccups in the context of fever and headache should trigger a workup for SIADH and its neurological sequelae, especially when hyponatremia is profound.
*   **Normal MRI is not Diagnostic Exclusion:** In early acute neuro-BD, parenchymal lesions may be absent on MRI. Rely solely on clinical symptoms and CSF analysis if infection is ruled out.
*   **Silent SIADH:** Hyponatremia in the context of inflammation can lead to severe complications like rhabdomyolysis. Look for creatine kinase elevations early.
*   **BD Criteria:** Behçet's disease is often underdiagnosed until early systemic signs appear. A diagnosis of "definite" BD relies on medium strength criteria clinical scores. Never rely on HLA-B51 as a sole diagnostic tool.
*   **Treatment Flexibility:** Colchicine monotherapy can be effective in acute neuro-BD and associated SIADH if available, particularly when steroids are contraindicated.

Ultimately, this case is a reminder that "idiopathic" fits often explode into specific inflammatory conditions when you pair clinical history with electrolyte numbers. Don't let the normal MRI convince you of normal neurobiology.
