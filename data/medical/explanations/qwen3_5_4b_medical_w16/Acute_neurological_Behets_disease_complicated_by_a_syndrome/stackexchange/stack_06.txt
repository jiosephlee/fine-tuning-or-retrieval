Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Management] Why was the correction of serum sodium strictly monitored to avoid rapid correction, and what specific principle of fluid management was prioritized instead of pharmacologic reversal?

**Clinical Teaching Point: Management of SIADH in Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease**

In this patient, the strict monitoring of serum sodium to avoid rapid correction was mandated to prevent **osmotic demyelination syndrome** (central pontine myelinolysis), a perilous complication associated with rapidly correcting chronic hyponatremia. The shift in serum osmolality must be gradual to avoid neuronal injury in the brainstem.

In lieu of pharmacologic reversal (e.g., hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists), the specific principle of fluid management prioritized in this case was **conservative fluid restriction** coupled with vigilant monitoring. As noted in the treatment section of the case report, SIADH was managed conservatively with fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), "careful daily monitoring of serum sodium and avoidance of the rapid correction of sodium levels," with the explicit statement that "Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not required."

The hyponatremia resolved spontaneously (124 mmol/L to 136 mmol/L) following the resolution of the underlying inflammation with colchicine monotherapy. This underscores a key teaching point: in cases of SIADH driven by inflammatory CNS pathology (such as acute neuro-Behçet’s disease), the immediate resolution of the sodium abnormality is tied to the inhibition of the inflammatory cytokines driving ADH release. Therefore, aggressive sodium replacement is reserved for severe symptomatic hyponatremia (e.g., seizures), while strict fluid restriction and disease-specific treatment remain the primary strategies.
