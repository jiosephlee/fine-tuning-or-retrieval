Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Foundational Knowledge: Behçet's Disease and Neuro-Endocrine Interactions

To understand the concurrent diagnosis of Behçet's Disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD presented in this case, one must first establish the criteria and pathophysiology of systemic Behçet's Disease itself. BD is a chronic, multisystem vasculitis characterized by a distinct immunologic profile, most commonly defined by clinical features such as recurrent oral aphthae and genital ulcers. Diagnosis is robustly supported by the International Study Group Criteria, which allows for a classification of definite disease when patients meet specific combination requirements, such as recurrent oral ulceration combined with one or more specific features including genital ulceration or ocular pathology. In the reported case, the patient met the criteria for definite system BD based on oral aphthae and subsequent appearance of genital ulcers, alongside a folliculitis-like rash. While systemic vasculitis is the hallmark, the marrow immune dysregulation associated with BD can extend beyond the mucosa, making a precise understanding of systemic disease criteria essential for distinguishing inflammatory etiologies from infectious or neoplastic mimics like viral encephalitis or sarcoidosis.

The neurological presentation of Behçet's Disease, or neuro-BD, represents a significant subset of the overall disease burden, occurring in approximately 10% to 20% of individuals with the systemic condition. A critical teaching point regarding this specific section of the case context is the temporal relationship between the diagnosis of systemic disease and neurological involvement. Typically, neuro-BD develops several years after mucocutaneous onset, suggesting that significant immunologic tolerance or immune surveillance changes are required. The concomitant diagnosis of acute neuro-BD and systemic disease at first presentation, as seen here, is a rare phenomenon that challenges the classic latency model. Thisypical presentation often includes headache and psychiatric changes; however, when the onset is acute with mucocutaneous features present simultaneously, clinicians must be cognizant that neurological symptoms do not necessarily require a delay in the original systemic diagnosis.

Pathophysiologically, acute neuro-BD often manifests as meningoencephalitis with or without parenchymal brain involvement, a distinction that is vital for diagnostic interpretation. While MRI is often used as a surrogate for detecting parenchymal lesions, particularly in the brainstem, these findings may be absent during the early acute inflammatory phase. This mirrors the findings in the case where the patient presented with headache and vertigo but had a normal brain MRI without hypothalamic involvement. The accurate diagnosis in the absence of parenchymal lesions is frequently supported by Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) analysis, which typically reveals aseptic meningitis with lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, as observed in this patient. Consequently, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for acute neuro-BD when aseptic CSF findings are accompanied by systemic inflammatory markers and characteristic mucocutaneous signs, even if neuroimaging initially appears unremarkable.

In the context of neuro-BD, the development of electrolyte abnormalities such as the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) highlights a specific neuroendocrine interaction. SIADH is characterized by hyponatremia driven by the nephrogenic retention of water, which results from non-osmotic stimulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). When the central nervous system is inflamed, as seen in acute neuro-BD, systemic or local mediators can dysregulate hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axes, leading to inappropriate water retention. In the case report, this manifested as profound hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) with low serum osmolality and inappropriately elevated urine osmolality and ADH levels. While SIADH is often associated with malignancy or medication use, it is a known complication of CNS inflammation. Therefore, understanding the link between meningoencephalitis and SIADH is crucial when interpreting laboratory data in patients with acute neurological Behçet's disease.

Finally, the potential mechanistic link between the systemic inflammation of Behçet's Disease and the endocrine complications is mediated through pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly Interleukin-6 (IL-6). IL-6 is a key regulator of inflammation and has been shown to stimulate ADH release; elevated levels are frequently detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with neuro-BD and correlate with clinical severity. Furthermore, serum inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) in the reported case served as a non-specific surrogate for the underlying IL-6–mediated inflammatory response. The resolution of both neurological symptoms and hyponatremia following colchicine monotherapy supports the role of colchicine in suppressing neutrophil migration and IL-6 production. This underscores the importance of recognizing that metabolic endocrine complications in neuro-BD may be direct consequences of the same inflammatory pathway affecting the vasculature and CNS, rather than independent comorbidities.

## Clinical Phenotype: Presentation and Initial Differential Suspicions

**Symptoms: Fever, Headache, and Fatigue as Indicators of CNS Involvement**

The patient's initial presentation was dominated by a classic triad of systemic inflammation and neurological upset: high-grade fever (38.1°C), severe headache, and general malaise accompanied by loss of appetite. In the acute setting, these three features are the alarm bells for meningoencephalitis or primary infection, particularly encephalitis or vasculitis. For the clinician, this implies that the hypothalamus and adjacent structures are under stress, potentially through direct inflammation or metabolic derangement. In the context of this specific case, these systemic signs were subtle indicators of an underlying vasculitic process affecting the CNS. While neuronal inflammation usually manifests later in Behçet’s disease, the acute onset of meningoencephalitis-like symptoms in a 60-year-old male prompted an urgent search for opportunistic CNS infection. Recognizing that this inflammatory constellation could also signal an autoimmune etiology is critical; it prepares the learner to look beyond infection and consider the potential for non-specific neurological presentations of systemic collagen vascular diseases.

**Mucocutaneous Clues: Oral Aphthae, Genital Ulcers, and Folliculitis-like Rash**

The mucocutaneous examination provided the most specific evidence for the diagnosis that was initially obscured by systemic symptoms. On physical examination, no objective focal neurological deficits were found, but oral aphthae were present at the time of admission, alongside folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. While oral ulcers are the hallmark of Behçet's disease (BD), their presentation in isolation often raises suspicion for recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS), a benign condition that requires careful differentiation from autoimmune vasculitis. The key learning point regarding this patient is the spatiotemporal appearance of lesions. Oral ulcers were present from day one, but genital ulcers did not appear until day 5. This progression highlights the heterogeneity of mucocutaneous BD, where the genital component is the definitive discriminator for the disease score in the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease. The absence of uveitis at the initial exam does not exclude the disease, further emphasizing the disease's multisystemic nature and the necessity of a broad screening for systemic disease markers.

**Red Flags for SIADH: Hyponatremia, Elevated Urine Sodium, and Volume Status**

The metabolic profile presented a second major diagnostic challenge that isolated the endocrine system from effective fluid regulation. Initial laboratory tests revealed marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) with a low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) but a high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg). Crucially, the patient was clinically euvolemic with no signs of peripheral edema or hypotension, which distinguishes this from hypovolemic hyponatremia seen in infection or hyperaldosteronism. The combination of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, high urine sodium (88 mmol/L), and elevated ADH levels confirmed the diagnosis of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH); in this context, the SIADH was a consequence of the CNS inflammation rather than exogenous drug triggers. This section is vital because SIADH is a frequent metabolic complication of inflammatory CNS diseases, often causing confusion in the differential diagnosis. The presence of SIADH complicates the management of hyponatremia because correcting sodium levels requires fluid restriction without causing rapid osmotic demyelination, impacting the clinical approach to the neurological presentation.

**Early Signs of Neuro-Endocrine Interaction: Hiccups and Gait Instability**

Beneath the systemic symptoms, specific neurological signs provided clues regarding the severity of the electrolyte disturbance and brainstem involvement. Persistent hiccups were reported by the patient upon initial presentation, a frequent clinical clue for severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation like meningitis. The pathophysiology involves direct irritation of the brainstem respiratory centers. In this case, hiccups, along with vertigo and arthralgia, served as warning signs of significant electrolyte imbalance. Furthermore, the laboratory finding of markedly elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L) pointed to myocyte necrosis and rhabdomyolysis. This is a rare but recognized complication of profound hyponatremia, distinct from other inflammatory causes, and highlights the systemic reach of the metabolic disturbance. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for rhabdomyolysis in the context of hyponatremia, as prompt correction of sodium levels can reverse muscle enzyme elevation and prevent renal failure.

**Initial Working Diagnosis: Viral Encephalitis vs. Systemic Inflammatory Disorder**

The diagnostic journey followed an exclusionary strategy, initially prioritizing viral infection. The combination of fever, headache, and general malaise typically favors viral encephalitis (e.g., HSV or VZV), for which Acyclovir was administered empirically after day 3. However, the diagnostic criteria for infection were never met due to negative cerebrospinal fluid cultures and the absence of viral antigens. This underscores the importance of when to stop empiric treatment for infection and pivot toward inflammatory etiologies given negative testing. The eventual diagnosis of Definite BD was supported by clinical scoring (≥4 points), where the combination of oral aphthae (2 points) and genital aphthosis (2 points) outweighed the non-specific systemic and endocrine findings. This reinforces the learning that in patients presenting with confusion, fever, and metabolic derangements, exclusion of primary infection must be followed by vigilant investigation for underlying autoimmune vasculitis if infectious markers fail to explain the presentation. The absence of CNS lesions on MRI speaks to the early phase of acute neuro-BD, meaning the clinician must recognize that a normal scan does not rule out severe meningoencephalitis.

## Diagnostic Logic: Exclusion of Mimics and Confirmation of Neuro-BD

The diagnosis of neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) is fundamentally an exclusionary process, particularly when non-specific neurological symptoms such as fever, headache, and encephalopathy are presented. In this case, the diagnostic challenge lay in distinguishing an inflammatory, vasculitic etiology from a primary infection, central nervous system neoplasm, or metabolic disturbance. For medical learners, understanding this diagnostic hierarchy is critical; without systematically ruling out life-threatening mimics such as bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis, and lymphoma, the diagnosis of neuro-BD remains provisional. The specific laboratory and imaging findings presented in this case serve as a practical demonstration of how a clinician moves from a practical suspicion of inflammation to a specific diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease (BD) with neurological involvement.

In terms of neuroimaging, the absence of specific parenchymal lesions on brain MRI does not preclude the diagnosis of neuro-BD, although it complicates the initial workup. While neuro-BD is classically associated with characteristic findings such as parenchymal brain lesions or brainstem hyperintensities, the inflammatory process may be microstructural or confined to the meningeal space in the acute phase, often resulting in normal imaging. In this patient, the MRI showed no significant abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or other regions, which initially shifted the clinical focus away from parenchymal encephalitis toward a meningoencephalitis pattern that might be insensitive to structural changes seen on standard MRI sequences. This underscores the teaching point that clinicians must maintain clinical suspicion for neuro-BD even when neuroimaging is normal, provided there is supportive cerebrospinal fluid analysis and mucocutaneous context. The decision to diagnose acute neuro-BD in the absence of structural brain lesions required careful consideration of the CSF data and the concurrent mucocutaneous disease.

Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was pivotal in narrowing the differential diagnosis toward an inflammatory etiology. The presence of mildly elevated opening pressure, increased protein, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis is consistent with aseptic meningitis. Crucially, CSF cultures and tests for common pathogens such as herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and tuberculosis were all negative. This effectively excluded the two most common and rapidly progressive differential diagnoses in a patient with fever and neurological decline: infectious meningitis and tuberculosis meningitis. By characterizing the meningeal reaction as aseptic, the investigators could reasonably attribute the findings to a non-infectious systemic inflammatory disorder. The absence of malignant cells in the CSF also helped rule out primary CNS lymphoma, which had been considered given the elevated inflammatory markers and neurological symptoms.

Although the primary diagnosis was underlying neuro-BD, the co-existence of hyponatraemia required a specific diagnostic workup to confirm Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH). Hyponatraemia can be a precursor to neurological symptoms, mimicking or exacerbating the clinical picture of encephalitis itself. The diagnostic criteria established in this case included serum osmolality that was low (267 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality that was inappropriately elevated at 900 mOsm/kg despite low serum osmolality, and urine sodium concentration of 88 mmol/L, indicative of impaired water excretion. Furthermore, an elevated plasma ADH level confirmed the presence of ADH secretion inappropriately despite hypo-osmolar serum. This workup confirmed that the hyponatraemia was due to SIADH secondary to the CNS inflammation rather than hypopotasemia, adrenal insufficiency, or renal failure. Recognizing this distinction was vital for managing the patient, as it required fluid restriction rather than the aggressive hydration often required in diuretic-wasting states or thyroid storm-associated dehydration.

To fully confirm the diagnosis of neyro-BD, the systemic workup must also exclude other autoimmune etiologies most commonly associated with meningitis-like presentations. In this case, tests for sarcoidosis, neurosarcoidosis, and hematological malignancy were necessary. Soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) were measured to screen for lymphoma and sarcoidosis, respectively. In this instance, the sIL-2R was only mildly elevated, which is a non-specific finding that can occur in acute inflammatory states and does not confirm lymphoma, while the ACE level was within the normal range. The absence of characteristic radiological findings on MRI further supported the rejection of neurosarcoidosis. These negative results, combined with the exclusion of infectious causes, created the necessary space for diagnosing an idiopathic inflammatory syndrome.

Ultimately, the diagnosis of neuro-BD was solidified by the application of the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, which relies heavily on clinical features rather than serology or specific biomarkers. The diagnostic panoply included recurrent oral aphthae (two points) and genital ulcers (two points) in addition to a folliculitis-like rash (one point), totaling five points, which meets the threshold for definite BD (≥4 points). This mucocutaneous diagnosis provided the "anchor" to interpret the concurrent neurological and endocrine symptoms. The resolution of hyponatraemia and neurological deficits following colchicine monotherapy supported the diagnosis of an inflammatory process responsive to anti-inflammatory agents, distinguishing it from neoplastic or purely infectious etiologies. This case concludes the diagnostic logic by illustrating that definitive BD, neurological involvement, and metabolic complications can be arranged in a unified pathological framework, where mucocutaneous diagnosis and CSF exclusion of mimics work in tandem to validate the systemic and neurological components.

## Therapeutic Intervention: Rationale for Colchicine Monotherapy

Upon admission, standard protocol for a patient presenting with fever, headache, and altered mental status prioritizes ruling out life-threatening infections. The initial management strategy included broad-spectrum empiric antimicrobial therapy with cefepime to address the possibility of bacterial meningitis or encephalitis, given the high fever and elevated inflammatory markers. Concurrently, the evolving neurological symptoms of headache and vertigo triggered an empirical trial with acyclovir to exclude viral etiologies such as herpes simplex or varicella zoster. However, because cerebrospinal fluid analysis and viral antigen testing were negative for HSV and VZV, acyclovir was discontinued. The subsequent exclusion of bacterial, viral, and neoplastic CNS causes via comprehensive workup clarified that the underlying pathology was inflammatory, shifting the therapeutic focus from antimicrobial coverage to immunomodulatory treatment.

The decision to utilize colchicine was based on the concurrent diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neurological involvement. While corticosteroids are typically the mainstay of treatment for acute neuro-BD, the clinical course demonstrated that mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms could resolve rapidly with colchicine monotherapy. Colchicine exerts a broad anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing the production of cytokines associated with inflammation, such as IL-1β and IL-6. Given the severity of the acute inflammatory presentation, initial UK guidelines suggest immunosuppressants are often required for adequate control. In this case, however, colchicine served as the primary agent, potentially reflecting the specific inflammatory profile of this patient or a favorable response to its neutrophil-cycling inhibition pathway without the high steroid burden.

The patient also presented with Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH), a complication treated with a distinct management protocol. Hyponatremia and hiccups are classic signs of hyponatremia; however, persistent hiccups may serve as a specific clinical clue to this condition in the setting of neuroinflammation. Management centered on conservative measures, specifically fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) coupled with daily monitoring of serum sodium. This approach avoided the risks of rapid correction associated with hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists, as the sodium levels and anti-diuretic hormone levels resolved spontaneously as the underlying inflammation subsided. The recognition that profound hyponatremia could precipitate rhabdomyolysis—as noted by the markedly elevated creatine kinase—further underscores the need for careful electrolyte surveillance in neuroinflammatory conditions.

Systemic corticosteroids were deliberately omitted from the initial treatment regimen. While they are a gold standard for aggressive neuro-BD, severe CK elevation and extensive fluid restriction were not immediately required for this case. The absence of cross-syndrome therapeutics suggests that the inflammatory state was manageable without systemic immunosuppression of the first-line steroid class, thereby avoiding long-term adverse effects. Oral aphthous lesions were managed locally with topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and cutaneous folliculitis-like rashes were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. This multimodal approach allowed for systemic control and local symptom relief without relying on heavy immunosuppression initially.

Ultimately, the therapeutic strategy demonstrated the potential efficacy of colchicine monotherapy in specific presentations of neuro-BD. The resolution of neurological manifestations and hyponatremia without systemic steroids highlights that treatment decisions may depend on the acute severity of inflammation and the presence of systemic markers like elevated inflammatory cytokines. This case serves as a cautionary reminder that while traditional preferential treatment includes systemic steroids, alternative pathways like colchicine warrant consideration in mild-to-moderate neurological presentations when infection has been rigorously excluded.

This intervention highlights a critical aspect of clinical decision-making: recognizing the potential overlap between systemic and neuro-inflammatory processes in BD. The shift from empirical antimicrobial coverage to specific immunomodulation after ruling out infection and malignancy illustrates the importance of exclusion-based diagnoses. Furthermore, the management of SIADH through conservative fluid restriction, rather than pharmacological intervention, provides a model for handling plasma sodium imbalances in inflammatory states, provided neurological and volume status are carefully monitored. The successful combination of systemic colchicine and topical agents underscores the need for multi-symptom management in severe Behçet's cases.

## Case Conclusions and Educational Implications

The ultimate outcome of this case demonstrates the efficacy of targeted anti-inflammatory therapy in a rare overlapping presentation of Behçet’s disease (BD), acute neurological involvement, and hyponatremia. The resolution of systemic symptoms, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia following colchicine initiation highlights that the underlying pathophysiology of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) in this context was likely driven by active CNS inflammation rather than independent endocrine failure or medication toxicity. The normalization of serum sodium (136 mmol/L) and the reduction in antidiuretic hormone activity (1.8 pg/mL) occurred without the need for vasopressin receptor antagonists or hypertonic saline, confirming that treating the inflammatory trigger effectively corrected the electrolyte disturbance. This underscores a critical pathophysiological principle in this patient where serum CRP levels served as a marker for the resolution of the cytokine-driven inflammation, which consequently ameliorated SIADH.

Long-term management strategies emphasized in this report suggest a potential for sustained remission through colchicine monotherapy, challenging the historical view that acute neurological BD requires aggressive immunosuppression. The patient maintained disease control for four years using colchicine alone, without recurrence of neurological symptoms or mucocutaneous lesions. This case supports maintaining long-term colchicine therapy as a cornerstone for both mucocutaneous disease and systemic inflammation in neuro-BD, particularly in patients where corticosteroids might be contraindicated or when observing for steroid-sparing efficacy. Furthermore, the incidental discovery of diabetic control issues illustrates the importance of screening for underlying metabolic comorbidities in patients presenting with acute inflammatory states, as poor glycaemic control (HbA1c 8.2%) contributed to the severity of the visceral presentation and subsequent complications.

A significant diagnostic lesson is how normal brain MRI findings can mislead the evaluation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease, specifically when aseptic meningitis is the primary presentation. In this patient, the absence of parenchymal brain lesions on MRI did not exclude the diagnosis, as confirmed by significant cerebrospinal fluid analysis showing lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated opening pressure. Clinicians must understand that while brainstem lesions are common in neuro-BD, up to 10–20% of patients may present with non-parenchymal inflammatory changes or mild meningeal inflammation alone. The diagnostic weight shifted here from radiological findings to the synthesis of the clinical history of oral/genital ulcers combined with exclusion of infectious and neoplastic causes, as mandated by the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (5 points). This reinforces the concept that normal neuroimaging does not rule out neuro-BD in the presence of suspected aseptic meningitis.

Recognizing SIADH as a manifestation of neuro-BD adds another layer of diagnostic complexity, distinguishing drug-induced from inflammation-induced states. While SIADH is a known side effect of medications such as cyclophosphamide used in BD treatment, this patient received no such pharmacological agents prior to presentation. The exacerbation of neurological symptoms preceding the SIADH findings, along with the concurrent inflammatory markers, supports the hypothesis that CNS inflammation alone is sufficient to dysregulate antidiuretic hormone secretion via cytokine pathways, such as interleukin-6. The absence of malignancy markers despite elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor levels further emphasizes that practitioners should not automatically suspect cancer in neuro-BD patients with electrolyte disturbances without correlating with clinical inflammatory markers and imaging.

A vital clinical pearl derived from this case is that persistent hiccups should be regarded as a sentinel sign for severe hyponatremia in the context of acute CNS inflammation. The patient presented with general malaise, headache, and hiccups, which initially suggested neurological dysfunction unrelated to electrolyte imbalance. However, the subsequent rapid correction of sodium with colchicine flattening urine output and reduction in ADH activity reversed these symptoms. This teaching point emphasizes that patients with suspected meningoencephalitis without fever should be maintained at high risk for electrolyte abnormalities, as hiccups may indicate hypothalamic irritation caused by severe hyponatremia. Monitoring clinical findings like hiccups alongside standard CSF analysis can prevent the delay of crucial management for SIADH-associated neurological decline.

Finally, this case necessitates a reevaluation of neuro-BD treatment protocols, specifically regarding the early-phase management of inflammation before establishing a definitive diagnosis. While corticosteroids remain the standard of care in most guidelines, the successful resolution of this patient’s condition with colchicine monotherapy suggests that in cases with early mucocutaneous/Bhallast (Behçet) features and aseptic meningitis, an anti-inflammatory approach may be a valid alternative. However, training should still prioritize the rapid initiation of broad-spectrum antimicrobial coverage and the exclusion of infection with any concurrent headache and focal neurological deficits, as was done in this case. Long-term, while colchicine was effective here, its utility as monotherapy for neuro-BD remains an evolving area of investigation, requiring clinicians to consider the spectrum of disease activity and the specific clinical phenotype of the patient when selecting between immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory modalities.
