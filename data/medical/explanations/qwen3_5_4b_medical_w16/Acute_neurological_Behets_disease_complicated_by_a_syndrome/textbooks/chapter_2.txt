Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Phenotype: Presentation and Initial Differential Suspicions

**Symptoms: Fever, Headache, and Fatigue as Indicators of CNS Involvement**

The patient's initial presentation was dominated by a classic triad of systemic inflammation and neurological upset: high-grade fever (38.1°C), severe headache, and general malaise accompanied by loss of appetite. In the acute setting, these three features are the alarm bells for meningoencephalitis or primary infection, particularly encephalitis or vasculitis. For the clinician, this implies that the hypothalamus and adjacent structures are under stress, potentially through direct inflammation or metabolic derangement. In the context of this specific case, these systemic signs were subtle indicators of an underlying vasculitic process affecting the CNS. While neuronal inflammation usually manifests later in Behçet’s disease, the acute onset of meningoencephalitis-like symptoms in a 60-year-old male prompted an urgent search for opportunistic CNS infection. Recognizing that this inflammatory constellation could also signal an autoimmune etiology is critical; it prepares the learner to look beyond infection and consider the potential for non-specific neurological presentations of systemic collagen vascular diseases.

**Mucocutaneous Clues: Oral Aphthae, Genital Ulcers, and Folliculitis-like Rash**

The mucocutaneous examination provided the most specific evidence for the diagnosis that was initially obscured by systemic symptoms. On physical examination, no objective focal neurological deficits were found, but oral aphthae were present at the time of admission, alongside folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. While oral ulcers are the hallmark of Behçet's disease (BD), their presentation in isolation often raises suspicion for recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS), a benign condition that requires careful differentiation from autoimmune vasculitis. The key learning point regarding this patient is the spatiotemporal appearance of lesions. Oral ulcers were present from day one, but genital ulcers did not appear until day 5. This progression highlights the heterogeneity of mucocutaneous BD, where the genital component is the definitive discriminator for the disease score in the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease. The absence of uveitis at the initial exam does not exclude the disease, further emphasizing the disease's multisystemic nature and the necessity of a broad screening for systemic disease markers.

**Red Flags for SIADH: Hyponatremia, Elevated Urine Sodium, and Volume Status**

The metabolic profile presented a second major diagnostic challenge that isolated the endocrine system from effective fluid regulation. Initial laboratory tests revealed marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) with a low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) but a high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg). Crucially, the patient was clinically euvolemic with no signs of peripheral edema or hypotension, which distinguishes this from hypovolemic hyponatremia seen in infection or hyperaldosteronism. The combination of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, high urine sodium (88 mmol/L), and elevated ADH levels confirmed the diagnosis of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH); in this context, the SIADH was a consequence of the CNS inflammation rather than exogenous drug triggers. This section is vital because SIADH is a frequent metabolic complication of inflammatory CNS diseases, often causing confusion in the differential diagnosis. The presence of SIADH complicates the management of hyponatremia because correcting sodium levels requires fluid restriction without causing rapid osmotic demyelination, impacting the clinical approach to the neurological presentation.

**Early Signs of Neuro-Endocrine Interaction: Hiccups and Gait Instability**

Beneath the systemic symptoms, specific neurological signs provided clues regarding the severity of the electrolyte disturbance and brainstem involvement. Persistent hiccups were reported by the patient upon initial presentation, a frequent clinical clue for severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation like meningitis. The pathophysiology involves direct irritation of the brainstem respiratory centers. In this case, hiccups, along with vertigo and arthralgia, served as warning signs of significant electrolyte imbalance. Furthermore, the laboratory finding of markedly elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L) pointed to myocyte necrosis and rhabdomyolysis. This is a rare but recognized complication of profound hyponatremia, distinct from other inflammatory causes, and highlights the systemic reach of the metabolic disturbance. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for rhabdomyolysis in the context of hyponatremia, as prompt correction of sodium levels can reverse muscle enzyme elevation and prevent renal failure.

**Initial Working Diagnosis: Viral Encephalitis vs. Systemic Inflammatory Disorder**

The diagnostic journey followed an exclusionary strategy, initially prioritizing viral infection. The combination of fever, headache, and general malaise typically favors viral encephalitis (e.g., HSV or VZV), for which Acyclovir was administered empirically after day 3. However, the diagnostic criteria for infection were never met due to negative cerebrospinal fluid cultures and the absence of viral antigens. This underscores the importance of when to stop empiric treatment for infection and pivot toward inflammatory etiologies given negative testing. The eventual diagnosis of Definite BD was supported by clinical scoring (≥4 points), where the combination of oral aphthae (2 points) and genital aphthosis (2 points) outweighed the non-specific systemic and endocrine findings. This reinforces the learning that in patients presenting with confusion, fever, and metabolic derangements, exclusion of primary infection must be followed by vigilant investigation for underlying autoimmune vasculitis if infectious markers fail to explain the presentation. The absence of CNS lesions on MRI speaks to the early phase of acute neuro-BD, meaning the clinician must recognize that a normal scan does not rule out severe meningoencephalitis.
