Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What infectious and neoplastic etiologies were effectively excluded from the differential diagnosis based on cerebrospinal fluid analysis and neuroimaging?

In this case, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis and neuroimaging were critical in systematically excluding infectious and neoplastic etiologies to confirm the diagnosis of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD).

**Infectious etiologies** were ruled out based on specific CSF findings. Viral meningoencephalitis, specifically caused by herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella zoster virus (VZV), was excluded when CSF testing yielded negative results for both viral antigen and antibodies. Bacterial and tuberculous meningitis were also effectively excluded: bacterial infection was negated by the absence of neutrophilic predominance and negative CSF cultures, while tuberculous meningitis was ruled out by negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

**Neoplastic etiologies**, particularly primary CNS malignancy (such as lymphoma), were excluded using neuroimaging and cytological review. Although the patient exhibited symptomatic neurological deficits and a mildly elevated serum interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level, the brain MRI showed no abnormalities or paraneoplastic lesions. Furthermore, CSF cytology did not reveal malignant cells.

This case demonstrates that the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD relies heavily on the exclusion of these common mimics. The combination of mucocutaneous findings, aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis, and a normal brain MRI provided the definitive basis for confirming the vascular inflammatory process of Behçet’s disease in the CNS.

### [Pathophysiology] Which pro-inflammatory cytokine is hypothesized to mediate the association between CNS inflammation in Behçet's disease and the syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone?

Residents of the housestaff, regarding the teaching question: the pro-inflammatory cytokine hypothesized to mediate the association between CNS inflammation in Behçet’s disease (BD) and the syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) is **interleukin-6 (IL-6)**.

In the discussion of this case report (Kakehi et al.), the authors clarify that neuro-BD is characterized by CNS inflammation, which may contribute to dysregulated ADH secretion. Several studies demonstrate that IL-6, a proinflammatory cytokine frequently elevated in neuroinflammatory disorders, is associated with increased ADH release. Specifically, in BD, aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production; elevated IL-6 levels have been detected in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD. The authors suggest that this IL-6–mediated inflammation might contribute to SIADH in this setting, noting that the markedly elevated serum CRP in their patient likely reflects this underlying IL-6–mediated inflammatory response.

It is important to note that IL-6 levels were not measured in the serum or CSF in this specific patient; however, the temporal association between the hyponatremia, neurological symptoms (aseptic meningitis), and the systemic inflammatory profile supports the hypothesis. Furthermore, the therapeutic response to this case—including the resolution of hyperintense symptoms and the normalization of sodium with colchicine monotherapy—is chemically relevant, as colchicine exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6.

The critical clinical pearl is that clinicians should recognize concurrent neurological and endocrine manifestations in patients with newly diagnosed BD. When a patient with BD presents with fever, headache, and hyponatremia, SIADH should be considered a potential complication of acute neuro-BD rather than solely an infectious or malignant etiology, as evidenced by the CSF findings of aseptic meningitis and the absence of radiation therapy or drugs known to cause SIADH.

### [Clinical Pearls] What specific neurological symptom, reported in the patient's history, serves as a clinical red flag for severe hyponatremia in the context of CNS inflammation?

In the context of CNS inflammation and the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), **persistent hiccups** serve as a specific neurological symptom that acts as a clinical red flag for severe hyponatremia. In this case of acute neurological Behçet’s disease, the patient presented with fever, headache, and notably persistent hiccups, alongside marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) and evidence of SIADH. As the authors highlight in the Discussion, persistent hiccups may serve as an important clinical clue to severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. Pathophysiologically, profound hyponatremia associated with inflammatory CNS processes can cause cerebral edema and direct irritation of the brainstem respiratory centers or hypothalamic pathways, eliciting intractable inspiration without consciousness.

Clinically, this symptom should prompt an immediate evaluation of serum sodium levels even in the absence of overt focal neurological deficits, particularly in patients with mucocutaneous signs of Behçet’s disease and non-specific CNS symptoms. In this patient, despite a normal brain MRI in the early phase of the disease, the combination of hiccups and fever necessitated careful evaluation for neurological inflammation. Management in this setting, as demonstrated in the case, involves fluid restriction to manage the hyponatremia and avoidance of rapid sodium correction to prevent neuronal injury, while treating the underlying inflammatory pathology with colchicine. Therefore, the presence of persistent hiccups in a patient with suspected Behçet’s disease should trigger a high index of suspicion for severe hyponatremia and oversee immediate electrolyte management.

### [Clinical Pearls] Upon initial evaluation, what metabolic complication related to severe hyponatremia was identified that is often overlooked in acute neurological presentations?

Clinical Pearl: **Rhabdomyolysis**

Upon initial evaluation of a patient with acute neurological Behçet’s disease presenting with severe hyponatremia, **rhabdomyolysis** was identified as a metabolic complication often overlooked in pure neurological presentations.

In this case, the patient presented with a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L and creatine kinase (CK) of 1349 U/L; the latter finding revealed rhabdomyolysis, a complication frequently recognized yet underappreciated in the context of severe hyponatremia. While the diagnosis of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) accounted for the water balance disturbance, the metabolic sequelae of profound sodium depletion extended beyond the central nervous system to include skeletal muscle integrity.

**Educational Point:**
Clinicians must broaden their differential diagnosis beyond central inflammatory markers when asymptomatic patients present with fever, headache, and hyponatremia. While the workup of acute neuro-BD often prioritizes MRI, CSF analysis, and autoimmune screens to rule out CNS infection and malignancy, severe hyponatremia can induce osmotic injury and cellular swelling that predisposes to myocyte damage independent of direct neurological localization. This case underscores that in patients with acute neuro-BD and SIADH, initial evaluation must include CK to identify hidden metabolic injury, such as rhabdomyolysis, which can otherwise be mistaken for unrelated myositis or unexplained fatigue.

### [Management] What pharmacological monotherapy resulted in the resolution of neurological symptoms and hyponatremia in this case without the use of corticosteroids, contrasting with standard management?

In this case of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), **oral colchicine monotherapy** was the agent that led to the resolution of neurological symptoms and hyponatremia without the use of corticosteroids. While corticosteroids are considered the mainstay of treatment for acute neuro-BD, colchicine resolved the patient’s fever, mucocutaneous lesions, ventricular symptoms, and severe hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) over two weeks, normalizing serum sodium to 136 mmol/L and reducing ADH levels.

The clinical reasoning relies on the understanding that neuro-BD is driven by CNS inflammation mediated by pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically IL-6. The case report suggests that colchicine, which inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses cytokine production (including IL-1β and IL-6), effectively modulated this inflammatory cascade in this specific context. Despite the fact that literature reviews generally classify colchicine as ineffective for acute neuro-BD, this case underscores the importance of individualized management and the association between neuro-BD and SIADH. Clinicians should consider colchicine as a viable monotherapy option for acute neuro-BD, particularly in patients where corticosteroid toxicity is a concern or when rapid recovery of neurological function and fluid balance is observed.

### [Management] Why was the correction of serum sodium strictly monitored to avoid rapid correction, and what specific principle of fluid management was prioritized instead of pharmacologic reversal?

**Clinical Teaching Point: Management of SIADH in Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease**

In this patient, the strict monitoring of serum sodium to avoid rapid correction was mandated to prevent **osmotic demyelination syndrome** (central pontine myelinolysis), a perilous complication associated with rapidly correcting chronic hyponatremia. The shift in serum osmolality must be gradual to avoid neuronal injury in the brainstem.

In lieu of pharmacologic reversal (e.g., hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists), the specific principle of fluid management prioritized in this case was **conservative fluid restriction** coupled with vigilant monitoring. As noted in the treatment section of the case report, SIADH was managed conservatively with fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), "careful daily monitoring of serum sodium and avoidance of the rapid correction of sodium levels," with the explicit statement that "Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not required."

The hyponatremia resolved spontaneously (124 mmol/L to 136 mmol/L) following the resolution of the underlying inflammation with colchicine monotherapy. This underscores a key teaching point: in cases of SIADH driven by inflammatory CNS pathology (such as acute neuro-Behçet’s disease), the immediate resolution of the sodium abnormality is tied to the inhibition of the inflammatory cytokines driving ADH release. Therefore, aggressive sodium replacement is reserved for severe symptomatic hyponatremia (e.g., seizures), while strict fluid restriction and disease-specific treatment remain the primary strategies.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Beyond infection and malignancy, which autoimmune condition was specifically considered for the patient's lymphocytic pleocytosis but subsequently ruled out by specific laboratory results?

Based on this case report, **neurosarcoidosis** was the specific autoimmune condition considered for the patient’s lymphocytic pleocytosis but excluded by specific laboratory results.

In the differential diagnosis section, the authors explicitly included neurosarcoidosis in the listing of possibilities for CNS inflammation, noting that while magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings may be normal in the early stages, case findings could still be misleading. However, subsequent laboratory data made this diagnosis unlikely. Specifically, the report states:

> "Tests for sarcoidosis revealed normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL) and angiotensin-converting enzyme levels... subsequent laboratory data made this diagnosis unlikely."

Although the patient had mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels, this was regarded as a non-specific finding and did not support a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma or sarcoidosis. Once infectious causes, malignancy, and sarcoidosis were ruled out using these objective data points, a diagnosis of acute neurological Behçet’s disease was established in the context of mucocutaneous findings.

### [Pathophysiology] In this case, brain MRI was initially normal despite clinical signs of CNS inflammation. How does this imaging finding alter the differential diagnosis compared to typical neuro-Behçet's disease?

In this context, a normal brain MRI does not exclude the diagnosis of acute neurological Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) and necessitates a shift in diagnostic weighting from structural imaging to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis and clinical exclusion.

While typical neuro-BD often presents with delayed onset (years after mucocutaneous diagnosis) and parenchymal lesions, frequently involving the brainstem, this case represented a rare concurrent onset of acute neuro-BD and systemic disease. Consequently, the absence of MRI abnormalities suggests an early inflammatory phase where axillary and parenchymal changes may not yet be radiologically evident, or that the inflammation is meningeal rather than parenchymal at presentation.

This imaging finding alters the differential diagnosis by compelling clinicians to rely on CSF analysis and systematic exclusion of mimics. As described in the case report, while the MRI was normal, the CSF demonstrated aseptic meningitis (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, increased opening pressure). Without radiation findings, the diagnosis becomes contingent upon ruling out infectious causes (such as HSV/VZV encephalitis or bacterial meningitis) and malignancies. The presence of a normal MRI specifically in the context of severe hyponatraemia (SIADH) in this patient highlights that endocrine complications can arise from CNS inflammation even when structural lesions are not yet visualized. Thus, the diagnostic pathway pivots to satisfying clinical inflammatory criteria supported by CSF evidence and the absence of mimics, ensuring that a normal MRI does not hinder the recognition of early neuro-Behçet's disease.

### [Differential Diagnosis] According to the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, what mucocutaneous features allowed for the diagnosis of definite systemic disease in conjunction with acute neuro-BD diagnosis?

In this case report, the diagnosis of **definite systemic Behçet’s disease (BD)** concurrent with acute neurological involvement (acute neuro-BD) was established based on the cumulative scoring system of the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease. To be classified as definite BD, a patient requires a total score of **≥4 points**.

Based on the patient’s clinical findings described in the report, the mucocutaneous features contributing to this_score were:

1.  **Oral Aphthae:** 2 points
2.  **Genital Aphthosis:** 2 points (presented as a painful scrotal ulcer in this case)
3.  **Folliculitis-like Rash:** 1 point

The total score calculated was **5 points**, fulfilling the threshold for definite systemic disease. While HLA-B51 was positive, the authors note that international criteria rely primarily on clinical features, and HLA genotyping does not solely determine the definitive diagnosis.

A key teaching point derived from this case is that **neurological symptoms and an asymptomatic or normal MRI brain scan** do not rule out BD. This patient demonstrated acute neuro-BD marked by aseptic meningitis (elevated CSF protein and lymphocytes without bacterial pathogens) but with no parenchymal MRI abnormalities. Because neuro-BD typically presents several years after initial mucocutaneous onset, a concurrent diagnosis of acute neuro-BD requires strong end-organ variation scoring in the mucocutaneous domain to satisfy the systemic diagnostic definition. This underscores the necessity of confirming mucocutaneous criteria alongside neurological suspicion to avoid misdiagnosing SD with non-neuroBD etiologies.
