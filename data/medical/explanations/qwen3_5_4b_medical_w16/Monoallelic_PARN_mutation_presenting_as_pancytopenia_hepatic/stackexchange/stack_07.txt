Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] Why is hepatic fibrosis a frequent but often under-recognized manifestation in adult Telomere Biology Disorders, and what mechanism links telomere attrition in hepatocytes to liver stiffness?

### Clinical Teaching: Hepatic Fibrosis in Telomere Biology Disorders

**Question:** Why is hepatic fibrosis a frequent but often under-recognized manifestation in adult Telomere Biology Disorders, and what mechanism links telomere attrition in hepatocytes to liver stiffness?

**Answer:**

Hepatic fibrosis is frequently observed in adult Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) yet is often dismissed as a secondary liver disease or nutritional consequence. This occurs because TBDs frequently present without the classic mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita, such as nail dystrophy or leukoplakia, leading clinicians to default to autoimmune or acquired etiologies for fibrosis in the absence of these signs.

The pathophysiological link between telomere attrition and liver dysfunction is rooted in cellular replicative senescence. As stated in the discussion of this case, telomeres shield chromosome ends and shorten with each cell division, eventually triggering apoptosis or senescence. Actively dividing cells, including hepatocytes, are disproportionately affected. In our patient (Soni et al.), we identified a monoallelic *PARN* mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) within a context of multisystem decline. While monoallelic *PARN* variants classically associate with bone marrow failure and pulmonary fibrosis, the discussion notes that the impact on telomerase and gene expression may extend to hepatocytes, leading to the hepatic dysfunction we observed.

Quantifying this, our patient’s transient elastography revealed a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, consistent with cirrhosis. This physical manifestation aligns with frontier registry data cited in the case background, which noted 88.8% of adult patients with pathogenic *TERC* or *TERT* mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis. The mechanism linking attrition to stiffness involves the accumulation of senescent hepatocytes releasing pro-fibrotic cytokines, creating a microenvironment of chronic inflammation and fibrogenesis that is characteristic of the disease.

Consequently, clinical recognition should be prompted when "plausible" but unexplained fibrosis persists despite nutritional optimization. In this case, severe B12 deficiency was corrected, yet fibrosis and cytopenia remained, shifting suspicion toward TBD. Early hepaetic surveillance using elastography and genetic testing for variants such as *PARN* is essential to distinguish cryptic inherited disorders from common acquired causes.
