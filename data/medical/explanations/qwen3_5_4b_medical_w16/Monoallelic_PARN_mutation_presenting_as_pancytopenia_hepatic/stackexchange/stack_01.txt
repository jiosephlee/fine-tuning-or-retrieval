Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In an adult with pancytopenia and signs of premature aging, what clinical and laboratory features specifically distinguish a Telomere Biology Disorder from severe nutritional deficiencies, even when B12 and folate levels are abnormal?

**Morning Report Pearl: Differentiating Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD) from Nutritional Deficiencies**

**Clinical Scenario**
Consider a young adult presenting with pancytopenia and signs of cellphone aging. While severe Vitamin B12/folate deficiency can explain the marrow failure (pancytopenia, ineffective erythropoiesis), specific features in this case point toward a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD) such as **telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure type 4 (TBD-TBD)**.

**Distinguishing Clinical Features**
In this vignette (Soni et al.), several features distinguish TBD from simple nutritional deficiency, even when lab values confirm B12 deficiency:

*   **Disproportionate Visceral Fibrosis:** The patient exhibited **hepatic cirrhosis** and **pulmonary fibrosis** (ground-glass haziness on CT). While nutritional deficiency explains the cytopenia, severe B12/folate deficiency alone does not typically cause accelerated fibrosis of both the liver and lungs in a young adult.
*   **Premature Mucocutaneous Signs:** Examination revealed **premature greying of hair**, bald tongue (urticaria atrophian), and knuckle hyperpigmentation. These are classic heralds of TBDs but may be absent or subtle in monoallelic variants compared to the classical triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita.
*   **Persistent Systemic Dysfunction:** The key differentiator observed was the **severity of clinical findings appeared disproportionate to nutritional deficiency alone**. Despite intensive correction of Vitamin B12 (IV cyanocobalamin) and folate, the systemic dysfunction (fibrosis, cytopenias, neuropathy) remained persistent and multisystemic.

**Laboratory Considerations**
*   **Morphology vs. Aetiology:** The peripheral smear showed features of ineffective erythropoiesis (dimorphic anemia, Cabot rings). While this is diagnostic of B12 deficiency, the persistence of this pattern after correction suggests an underlying susceptibility (e.g., short telomeres impairing hematopoietic stem cell replication).
*   **Iron Overload:** The patient had elevated serum iron and ferritin consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis, which can complicate the picture of "nutritional anemia."

**Management Principle**
In young adults with pancytopenia and organ fibrosis, the diagnosis of TBD should not be excluded before ruling out malignancy. As noted in the background: "**Monoallelic variants in TERT, TERC, RTEL1 [and] PARN… typically identified in TBDs… presenting as IPF or BMFS.**"

While genetic testing (Next-Generation Sequencing) is the gold standard, the presence of **persistent fibrosis disproportionate to hematologic findings and premature greying** warrants the pharmacogenetic workup. The case highlights that **WES identified the PARN mutation**, confirming that even monoallelic variants are associated with this spectrum. Early recognition is crucial, as curative treatment (eventually hematopoietic stem cell transplantation) may be indicated if marrow failure progresses.

**Take-Home Point:**
In patients with pancytopenia and micronutrient deficiencies, **hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis combined with premature greying** should raise suspicion for a Telomere Biology Disorder. These findings often persist despite nutritional correction, necessitating genetic testing for telomere-related genes like **PARN**.
