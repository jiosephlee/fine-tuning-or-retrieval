Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Genomic Risks and Survivorship: Management of Mendelian Premature Ageing in Patients

Imagine a patient in their late twenties who arrives with a confusing constellation of symptoms. There is fatigue, pancytopenia, and what looks like severe B12 deficiency leading to ineffective erythropoiesis and liver issues. You correct the vitamin and transfuse blood cells, but the patient still has premature greying of the hair, brittle bones, and progressive lung fibrosis. The diagnosis is a riddle without the classic clues. This patient turned out to have a monoallelic variant in the *PARN* gene, presenting with telomere biology disorder features that lacked the textbook mucocutaneous triad. What makes this case particularly instructive isn't just the diagnosis—it is that genetic disorders causing telomere attrition can masquerade as acquired multisystem inflammatory syndromes or nutritional deficiencies in adults.

Now that we have confirmed the genetic pathology with Whole Exome Sequencing, the clinical narrative shifts entirely. You move from an acute diagnostic crisis to the far more complex task of survivorship and genomic management. This is where the reality of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) meets the gray areas of evidence-based medicine. Patients with confirmed variants in telomere-related genes, such as the *PARN* patient described, often present with a unique challenge: how much active intervention is warranted against a progressive, likely incurable genetic process?

**Differentiating Monoallelic vs. Biallelic Risk**
From a clinical management perspective, understanding the inheritance pattern is the first step in counseling. *PARN* demonstrates haploinsufficiency, meaning monoallelic variants (one affected copy) can lead to significant disease, as seen in the *PFBMFT4* phenotype, whereas biallelic variants often present in recessive dyskeratosis congenita. This distinction is vital for survivors. A monoallelic *PARN* carrier, like our patient, faces a syndrome more akin to pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure than the aggressive childhood onset of Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. 

You must tailor the surveillance intensity accordingly. While biallelic disease patients might require aggressive itinerant testing, monoallelic carriers still face a trajectory of declining telomere length. The critical difference in management is the recognition that while somatic repair is impossible, the specific organ systems targeted by the haploinsufficiency can be monitored closely to catch the tipping point where cell death becomes terminal.

**Organ-Specific Survivorship Protocols**
Based on the case complications, the most immediate threats are hepatic and pulmonary, with the hematopoietic niche remaining vulnerable.
*   **Respiratory Surveillance:** The patient presented with ground-glass haziness and subpleural atelectasis on pET-CT. Plans should include regular serial respiratory function tests and high-resolution microscopy. If the fibrosis progresses to require escalation, referral for transplant evaluation is a consideration sooner rather than later.
*   **Hepatic Tracking:** This patient developed cirrhosis without autoimmune markers. In TBD cases, hepatomegaly and hepatic stiffness on transient elastography are frequent even without classic autoimmune hepatitis antibodies. Routine screening for viral markers or autoantibodies is standard, but you must also watch for hepatocellular carcinoma and early decompensation, as fibrosis may progress silently.
*   **Bone Marrow Vigilance:** Pancytopenia in this context is often exacerbated by telomere shortening. The patient had a response to B12 correction, but underlying marrow failure remains a risk. Complete blood counts should be monitored quarterly or more frequently if transfusion dependence persists. A telomere blooddot FISH assay should be available for monitoring telomere dynamics if standard markers are ambiguous.

**The Art of Genetic Understanding**
Counseling is just as critical as clinical follow-up. Many families worry if this is hereditary. In this instance, a de novo mutation was a possibility, and the family history was negative. It is crucial to explain that while the patient overall carries the disease, there are no obvious family threads to confirm inheritance. Patients must understand that passing it on risks having the same disease in their children. Furthermore, the variant status (e.g., "Variant of Uncertain Significance" as initially labeled) can be confusing. Clinicians must emphasize that the genotype provides strong evidence for the phenotype even if the specific protein sequence is not definitively pathogenic in isolation. The gene function (cleaving poly(A) tail length) combined with the clinical score overrides the in-silico prediction in atypical cases.

**The Reality of Treatment Curability**
In TBD management, there is a sobering acceptance that cure is often not feasible. Supportive care—transfusions, supplements, and physiotherapy—has shown symptomatic improvement in our patient without reversing the underlying telomere biology. However, you do not give up on transplant. For hematopoietic melanocyte dysfunction, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only curative option. However, in pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, the prognosis remains guarded. 

It is tempting to consider androgens like danazol to stimulate telomerase. Current data suggests a potential increase in telomere length and symptom improvement, but the safety profile is less than ideal, especially with long-term use. We must discuss the risk of adverse events in a patient with liver fibrosis.
*   **The Bottom Line:** The initial focus is optimizing supportive care, maintaining remission of the B12 and marrow issues, and preventing complications of the fibrosis.
*   **Future Directions:** Telomerase activators are not a standard of care yet; their role is palliative.

## Clinical Take-home Messages

*   Always consider Telomere Biology Disorders when unexplained multiorgan failure presents in young adults, even without the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad.
*   Monitor for organ-specific fibrosis (pulmonary and hepatic) in monoallelic *PARN* variants, as these often mimic acquired autoimmune diseases.
*   Recognize that while hemoglobin reduction and marrow failure can be responsive to nutritional support and transfusion, genetic predisposition necessitates lifelong marrow surveillance.
*   Genetic counseling is essential to clarify inheritance patterns (including potential de novo cases) and managing expectations regarding transplant vs. palliative support.
*   Maintain a multidisciplinary approach involving hematology, pulmonology, and genetic services to manage the length of the disease trajectory.
