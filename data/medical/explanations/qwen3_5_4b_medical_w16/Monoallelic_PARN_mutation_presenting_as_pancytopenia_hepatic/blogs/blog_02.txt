Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## PARN Variants and Pulmonary Fibrosis: The Undervalued Respiratory Sign of Dyskeratosis Congenita

Most of the time when we triage a patient with suspected idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), we expect an older demographic, perhaps paraneoplastic or associated with autoimmune connective tissue disease. When you walk into the clinic with a 28-year-old woman presenting with exertional dyspnea, progressive fatigue, and peripheral fibrosis, your gut says "overwork" or "interstitial lung disease due to environmental exposure." But there is a second, quieter voice listening in the lab: the bone marrow failure, the premature greying of her hair, the liver stiffness like cirrhosis, and the severe cyclic anemia that refused to respond to standard nutritional correction alone.

This is a situation where Initiaip suspicion of IPF drowns out the possibility of a systemic cause. The patient in the case we are discussing came in with classic "telomere biology disorder" (TBD) features that didn't fit the textbook dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad initially. She looked tired, weak, and unhealthy, but she hadn't developed the leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, or reticular skin pigmentation that define the prototypical DC. Yet, investigate her whole genome. She was a PARN mutation carrier (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), classified initially as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), though the pattern strongly suggested a telomere biology defect.

If you don't pause here and think "Telomere," you might spend the rest of her life managing symptoms without addressing the root cause. This case highlights a critical transition point in clinical practice: the intersection of respiratory decline, hematopoietic failure, and aging in young adults.

## Diagnostic Dilemmas in Young Adults

Let’s be clear: IPF is rare in the late 20s, but it does happen. However, when it comes paired with bone marrow failure, think Uber. This combination of cytopenia, premature ageing signs, and organ fibrosis is the signature of a dysregulation in telomere length and maintenance.

The case report highlights several diagnostic pitfalls that mimic acquired disease:

The patient had severe vitamin B12 deficiency, confirmed by low serum levels and elevated Methylmalonic acid. The peripheral smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes, Cabot rings, and ineffective erythropoiesis on marrow exam. Initially, the clinicians treated with quantum B12. But the lung fibrosis, the liver scarring, and the skeletal deficits did not resolve. In the telomere biology world, you are effectively "fueled" but still running out of fuel.

The organ involvement pattern matters: PARN variants are part of a small family of genes (around 20% are tolerant to disruptive variants) where haploinsufficiency is the mechanism. Biallelic variants cause Autosomal Recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6), while monoallelic PARN variants are specifically known to link with Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure (PFBMFT4). Even when the variant status is a "VUS" by ACMG standards (which this was PM1, PM2, PP3 compliant), the clinical phenotype often dictates the management pathway.

Also note the hepatic involvement. We often think of TBDs as primarily mucocutaneous or hematologic. Recent registry data from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry suggests that liver fibrosis/cirrhosis is surprisingly prevalent in adults with TERT or TERC mutations. In this case, the patient had cirrhosis despite being a monoallelic PARN carrier, reinforcing that organ fibrosis extends beyond the respiratory system in these syndromes.

## Management and Surveillance

How do you manage this? When you suspect a genetic origin, and you have a "VUS" but a strong clinical match, follow the phenotype. Supportive care is the key. The patient received correctantidation for B12 and folic acid. She had a stable flow status on blood counts after this. Physiotherapy and supplementation helped her home dysfunction. Genetic counselling was provided.

The gold standard is detection at the molecular level, as per the case discussion. This includes Next-Generation Sequencing. While telomerase length can be measured, genetic testing is primary for differential diagnosis. And for the clinician: keep an eye out for the expanding phenotype.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   Young adults (<50) presenting with pulmonary fibrosis should prompt a workup for telomere biology disorders (TBD) even without classic mucocutaneous features.
*   Severe vitamins deficiencies can mimic ineffective erythropoiesis on MRI or marrow exam, but persistent fibrosis in other organs (liver) suggests an underlying genetic cause.
*   A monollical PARN variant is associated with PFBMFT4 (Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure), often presenting with monoallelic variants even if the VUS status is not fully pathogenic.
*   Hepatic fibrosis is a known component of TBDs, more prevalent in literature than previously appreciated (up to 88% in some adult TBD cohorts).
*   Genetic Counseling is mandatory for families; BOXE and support are necessary measures, as curative measures are limited but bone marrow transplantation is a consideration for some.

## Final Thoughts for the Clinician

This case was a perfect staging ground to discuss the overlap of respiratory and hematologic failure. We cannot diagnose Telomere Deficiency disorder in the "emergency room" setting, but we can see the red flags. If you are treating a patient with unexplained cytopenia and fibrosis, do not discard the genetic hypothesis just because the variant was a VUS. The clinical picture is the strongest clue in 50% of these rare cases.
