Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Atypical Dyskeratosis Congenita: Beyond the Mucocutaneous Triad in Young Adults

When we think of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs), our palette is usually dominated by Dyskeratosis Congenita. We think of the classic rosier triad: reticular skin pigmentation, nail dystrophy, and oral leukoplakia. We think of kids with growth failure and kids who suddenly bump into a petri dish with recovery. We often assume that if a patient presents with "premature aging" and bone marrow failure, we either have a young patient with a genetics board, or we need to get a biopsy for "untreated" hairy leukoplakia. But sometimes, the picture is much quieter. Sometimes, it’s a quiet, grinding fatigue in a 28-year-old woman that looks like aplastic anemia but doesn't quite fit the classic picture.

This is the fascinating case of a woman in her late 20s presenting with a 5-year history of worsening fatigue, generalized weakness, and exertional dyspnea. The constellation was daunting: pancytopenia, severe Vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatosplenomegaly early on, yet the most striking features actually pointed toward something else entirely. She had premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, a "bald tongue" (atrophic lingual papillae), and osteoporosis. She had liver fibrosis and lung fibrosis.

There was an immediate trap to avoid in this case. **Don't just fix the B12 and write "resolved".**

The initial workup was spectacularly thorough, and yet, the clinical picture resisted simple explanations. Peripheral smear showed ineffective erythropoiesis (dyshomery with Cabot rings, basophilic stippling), and morning experiments confirmed pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte response. There was also just enough evidence to suggest a positive immune mechanism, as the DAT was positive for IgG. But the marrow was empty (trilineage backup with no aplasia), the serologies for autoimmune disease were negative, and most critically, correcting the severe Vitamin B12 deficiency did not resolve the deeper systemic phenotype.

If a young patient presents with major hematologic stress and organ-specific fibrosis (like liver or lung scarring) without the classic DC look, you have to really think. This phenotype can mimic acquired conditions but has a cryptic genetic origin.

## The Genetic Culprit: PARN and the Monoallelic Disease

Because the patient didn't look like a textbook cancer case, MRI, endoscopy, and a panel of autoimmune markers were negative for other standard workups. It was a case of the "hard-to-explain." We eventually had to call in the whole-exome sequencing team.

The genetic finding was a monoallelic missense variant in the **PARN gene** (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This variant was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in isolation, but the protein impact and the clinical phenotype matched. PARN is a highly conserved exoribonuclease involved in RNA processing. Crucially, it is essential for the 3'-end maturation of the telomerase R component (TERC).

Here is the kicker regarding the genetics: **This is not just classic Dyskeratosis Congenita.** The case highlights the critical distinction between biallelic and monoallelic mutations in this gene. Biallelic variants in PARN cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), usually with the classic mucocutaneous triad. However, **monoallelic variants** are more often implicated in **Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure (PFBMFT4)**.

This distinction is vital. Monoallelic PARN mutations can underlie the spectrum of TBDs. This has been documented in over 65 cases in the literature, and this specific patient, presenting with pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, fits that specific "monoallelic" disease pathway. It shows how a heterozygous state can manifest as a multisystem disorder in a young adult, often rapidly misdiagnosed as a nutritional deficiency or a standard autoimmune vasculitis until you see the telomeres aren't the issue, the gene is.

## Management and Systemic Surveillance

The patient was treated with supportive care: intravenous cyanocobalamin to correct the B12, folic acid, and physiotherapy. Her symptoms improved significantly, and her blood counts stabilized. This was the critical momentum, but the long term is about **surveillance**.

Since the foundation is telomere attrition, there is no curative medical therapy for the genetic defect itself (androgen therapy is still in clinical trials with unknown long-term effects; HCT is an option for BM failure but carries cure risks). The goal is delaying the disease progression. For a patient with this genetic profile, you must monitor the bone marrow failure risk because the marrow can succumb to stress, such as a sudden flare of erythrocytic stress or even solid organ transplant complications. The liver fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis also require ongoing monitoring for telomere shortening, as liver stiffness and lung fibrosis are seen in **88% of adult patients** with other components of the telomerase machinery (like TERT/TERC), and we found cirrhosis in this patient despite only monoallelic PARN mutation.

**We need to talk about the signaling to the team.** When you've ruled out autoimmune and neoplastic causes, but you still have fibrosis and cytopenia in a young person, ask your genetics lab for PARN. Ask flow cytometry for telomere length if available.

If you see premature greying, osteoporosis, and "fad" anemia in a young person with a negative iron panel and no autoimmune "numbskull" on serologies, do you suspect an underlying genetic etiology? The case above suggests the answer might be yes.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Rested Diagnostic Approach:** Consider Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD) in young adults with multi-organ fibrosis and unexplained cytopenia.
*   **Atypical Phenotype:** TBDs do not require the classic Dyskeratosis Congenita mucocutaneous triad (nail/pigment/leukoplakia).
*   **Premature Aging Signs:** Premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and atrophic lingual papillae should trigger scrutiny for a genetic cause in the context of marrow failure.
*   **PARN Monoallelic Spectrum:** Recognize that monoallelic PARN variants are linked to Pulmonary Fibrosis/Bone Marrow Failure (PFBMFT4) even more than typical RC mutations.
*   **Carry-Spouse Risk:** Once a genetic etiology is suspected, offer genetic counseling and family screening; many TBDs are inherited but can be silent in carriers until triggered by specific stressors.
