Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# B12 and Folate Deficiency: Complicating the Hematologic Diagnosis in Marrow Failure Syndromes

### Don't Let Peripheral Smear Features Blind You to the Underlying Syndrome

There's a classic clinical narrative that plays out frequently in our hematopathology consults: a young adult presents with anemia, fatigue, and a periphery smear full of signs pointing directly to folate or vitamin B12 deficiency. You treat the deficiency, the platelets rebound, you close the chart, and a year later, the patient returns with the same weight, but also new liver fibrosis or relentless neuropathy.

This is the rub. Too often, we treat the obvious and miss the cryptic. Consider a 24-year-old woman who presented with a five-year history of worsening fatigue and weakness. Her peripheral smear told a familiar story: dimorphic anemia, basophilic stippling, and Cabot rings. Standard differential diagnosis for hematologists includes severe nutritional deficiencies. The workup confirmed severe vitamin B12 deficiency. She gets IV cyanocobalamin. Her symptoms improve. He checks... she has a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, pulmonary fibrosis on PET/CT, and progressive osteoporosis.

This exact constellation—pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis markers, yet unexplained fibrosis despite nutritional correction—should have been a yellow flag. The patient ultimately carried a monoallelic *PARN* mutation, confirmed as a variant linked to Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD). She didn't have the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita. Instead, she presented with what we call a "surgical" presentation of telomere attrition: accelerated "aging" signs like premature greying and a multidisciplinary syndrome involving pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis.

### Effective Erythropoiesis: A Sign, Not a Diagnosis

The most common reason we pivot from nutritional deficiency to marrow failure diagnostics in these cases is "ineffective erythropoiesis." When you have dimorphic anemia and basophilic stippling, you have failed to rule out nutritional causes definitively, but you also have to recognize that the bone marrow physiology in telomere biology disorders is fundamentally different from what you might expect from simple malabsorption.

In the case report provided, the patient had a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) with IgG 2+. While this often screams autoimmune hemolysis, the reticulocyte count was 0.39%—too low for immune destruction. Instead, the marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores. This is the polylandry of TBDs: the stem cells don't die immediately, but they stop responding efficiently. The marrow thinks it should iron B12-dependent erythropoiesis, but the telomeres have already signaled senescence.

The patient's liss (anemia) and neutrophil dysplasia (Cabot rings) strongly mimic the classic signs of Vitamin B12 deficiency, e.g., "megaloblastic anemia." However, in the context of pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, you have to pause. That is when you look for thefibrosis (hypothesis). That is when you look for fibrosis.

### The Red Herring of Ineffective Erythropoiesis

B12 deficiency is the "red herring" in some cases because it is treatable. If the marrow failure is purely nutritional, fixing the B12 cures the marrow failure. But if the failure is driven by telomere biology (as in this case), fixing the B12 treats the secondary inflammatory marker, or as the OBs call it, the secondary desease.

The patient's clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant after extensive workups. PARN is an exoribonuclease required for TERC maturation. A pathogenic variant here impairs telomerase function, or rather, the stability of the telomerase complex. This leads to shorter telomeres and shorter-lived stem cells. This explains the foundational marrow failure symptoms. But because B12 deficiency exacerbates DNA damage, the patient's symptoms improved *symptomatically* but did not reverse the telomere erosion physiology.

This is a crucial distinction. In this patient, correcting B12 *did* improve symptoms, but that does not mean the primary pathology was nutritional. It means the nutritional deficiency likely accelerated a pre-existing telomere attrition process.

### Takeaway: Rethinking the Differential for "B12 Failure"

When you are working through a patient with a "failed" nutritional deficiency and persistent cytopenia, ask yourself what organ systems are affected beyond the erythroid line. B12/folate deficiency alone rarely causes pulmonary fibrosis and skeletal abnormalities like osteoporosis without wasting.

1.  **Ineffective Erythropoiesis is the Starting Point, But Not the Diagnosis**: Dimorphic erythrocytes and basophilic stippling can be caused by B12 deficiency, but they also occur in Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs). Persistence of dysplasia despite repletion warrants genetic testing.
2.  **Multi-System Fibrosis Warrants SUV 2+**: In a young adult with pancytopenia, look hard for pulmonary or liver fibrosis. ß18F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on PET/CT can show diffuse marrow uptake in TBDs. Result: Normal autoimmune serologies.
3.  **Genetic Testing is Not Optional in "Atypical" The**: If B12 and folate correction fails to clear the marrow failure completely, or if there are concurrent skin/hair/organ fibrosis, consider Next-Generation Sequencing (WES) for telomere-related genes. Early recognition is crucial for genetic counselling and potential bone marrow transplant consideration.

### The Bottom Line

B12/Folate deficiency is common. Telomere Biology Disorders are rare. When they overlap, it can look like a simple malnutrition case initially. But when you chase the B12 supplementation and the marrow failure doesn't resolve, or when you see fibrosis elsewhere, stop the scroll on the "Nutritional" hypothesis. Look at the telomere. The patient didn't get cured by B12 alone; she got stabilized, confirmed she had a genetic type of TBD, and was put on a genetic counseling track. That is the sigmoidal curve of diagnostic discovery.
