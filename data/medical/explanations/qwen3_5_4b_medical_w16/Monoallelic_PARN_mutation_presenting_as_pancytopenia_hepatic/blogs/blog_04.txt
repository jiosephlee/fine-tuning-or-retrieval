Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Variant of Uncertain Significance in TBD: Where Clinical Phenotype Validates Genomics

## The Clinical Dilemma

In the world of precision medicine, there is a specific kind of diagnostic frustration that occurs when the molecular data doesn't scream "PATHOGENIC" but the clinical picture is screaming "PATHOGENIC." A Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in a gene with high functional importance, particularly in a patient with multisystem organ failure, often leaves clinicians agonizing. The ideal marker is the telomere biology chaos case: a patient who survived long enough to register, but presented with a constellation of symptoms so specific that the VUS actually painted a clearer picture of the disease than the gene說明 would have suggested.

Consider the case of a woman in her late 20s, presenting with a quintessential "old age" in a young body. We aren't talking about journals or dietary habits here; we are talking about wasted amyloid, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and pancytopenia. She had a 5-year history of fatigue that had reduced her functional capacity. When you look at the physical exam, you see her receding hairline, premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a tongue with lingual papillae atrophy. These are the hallmarks of a dyskeratosis congenita (DC)-like phenotype.

In this specific case, the whole-exome sequencing revealed a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). Under standard ACMG/AMP criteria, many clinical geneticists would classify this as a Variant of Uncertain Significance because of the "incomplete penetrance" rules and heterozygous status. However, the data known in the field regarding *PARN* prioritization leans toward haploinsufficiency for multiple alleles. So, the clinical question becomes: do we accept a VUS as a diagnosis for a telomere biology disorder (TBD) when the phenotype is classic for it?

## Why the Variant Was Uncertain

The issue of *PARN* is particularly tricky. The gene encodes a poly(A) PIA polymerase protein that forms a critical complex with TerC (TERC) to maintain the telomere lig. If you mess with that machinery, your telomeres get too short. This causes cellular senescence in erythroid cells eventually.

The patient in our case tested positive for severe vitamin B12 deficiency and ineffective erythropoiesis. Folic acid and cobalamin replacement were standard treatments. There was a positive DAT (direct Coats test) for IgG, but the indirect Coats test was negative. This initially made immune-mediated hemolytic anemia a consideration, but the low reticulocyte count in the face of hemolysis markers grew more concerning for intrinsic bone marrow issues.

The liver showed cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa), but autoimmune etiology was ruled out. There was no evidence of viral infection or cancer on an 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake PET-CT window. So, if you look at your data streams, you get two things. One is a biochemical process that suggests deficiency (B12 deficiency), and the other is a molecular process that suggests telomeric attrition (VUS *PARN*). The severity of the systemic findings outweigh the discrepancy between the genetic label and the clinical reality.

## From Uncertain to Diagnosis

Why this clinical correlation matters is precisely where the "Take Home" points come in. You cannot rely solely on the "Probable" label from the bioinformatics team when the protein structure itself lies in a "critical functional domain" (R3H domain). The case highlights that while *PARN* has been linked to autosomal recessive Tier Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6), monoallelic variants often underlie telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

The patient in our case had a systemic failure pattern involving the bone marrow, liver, lungs, and overall myocyte function. While classical mucocutaneous features like nail dystrophy might have been expected, they were absent, yet the patient had a bald tongue, greying hair, and neurologic involvement. The VUS status did not exclude the disease. Instead, the presence of the variant combined with a classic telomere biology phenotype provided a winning diagnosis.

It also raises the question of why it is monoallelic but present in early adulthood. The case notes that one third of adult patients with additional *TERC* or *TERT* mutations have liver failure, and the *PARN* variant here normalized the telomere structure led to tissue failure in specific organs. The identification of the PARN gene alone was a start, but the clinical phenotype validated the pathogenicity.

## Management and the Future

The management of TBDs is shifting from "supportive" to "diagnostic counseling focused." In this case, the patient was treated aggressively for nutritional deficiencies (IV cyanocobalamin, vitamin D, iron, calcium). She received blood transfusions for anemia.

After the VUS-phenotype correlation was established, the family received genetic counselling. They understood that this could become a "progressive case" over time. The outcome report showed stable blood counts after follow-up. The lesson here is not just to treat the symptoms but to provide a long-range plan for bone marrow surveillance and lung function testing.

Sometimes, a diagnosis isn't enough to explain the prognosis, and knowing the genetic etiology changes the burden of expectation for the family.

## Key Takeaways

*   **VUS is not a diagnosis, but in syndrome-specific contexts, clinical phenotype can validate genetic variants.** When a patient has a classic TBDD phenotype (fibrosis + marrow failure + ageing features), a VUS in a telomere gene is highly suggestive of the disorder.
*   **Telomere Biology Disorders can present without the classic mucocutaneous triad.** Lung fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and bone marrow failure are equally valid presentation pathways for TBDs.
*   **Monoallelic *PARN* variants affect telomere biology.** While PARN mutations in DDR1 usually cause recessive PK, their monoallelic presence corresponds to PFBMFT4 (pulmonary fibrosis/bone marrow failure).
*   **Screening is critical.** Once a suspected VUS is identified, ongoing clinical and haematological monitoring is essential to assess for evolving marrow failure phenotypes.
*   **Don't dismiss the phenotype.** If nutritional or other common causes of pancytopenia don't explain the fibrosis, and a genetic variant in the right pathway exists, it may be genetic enough to consider for definitive diagnosis.
