Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Atypical Dyskeratosis Congenita: Beyond the Mucocutaneous Triad in Young Adults

When we think of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs), our palette is usually dominated by Dyskeratosis Congenita. We think of the classic rosier triad: reticular skin pigmentation, nail dystrophy, and oral leukoplakia. We think of kids with growth failure and kids who suddenly bump into a petri dish with recovery. We often assume that if a patient presents with "premature aging" and bone marrow failure, we either have a young patient with a genetics board, or we need to get a biopsy for "untreated" hairy leukoplakia. But sometimes, the picture is much quieter. Sometimes, it’s a quiet, grinding fatigue in a 28-year-old woman that looks like aplastic anemia but doesn't quite fit the classic picture.

This is the fascinating case of a woman in her late 20s presenting with a 5-year history of worsening fatigue, generalized weakness, and exertional dyspnea. The constellation was daunting: pancytopenia, severe Vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatosplenomegaly early on, yet the most striking features actually pointed toward something else entirely. She had premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, a "bald tongue" (atrophic lingual papillae), and osteoporosis. She had liver fibrosis and lung fibrosis.

There was an immediate trap to avoid in this case. **Don't just fix the B12 and write "resolved".**

The initial workup was spectacularly thorough, and yet, the clinical picture resisted simple explanations. Peripheral smear showed ineffective erythropoiesis (dyshomery with Cabot rings, basophilic stippling), and morning experiments confirmed pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte response. There was also just enough evidence to suggest a positive immune mechanism, as the DAT was positive for IgG. But the marrow was empty (trilineage backup with no aplasia), the serologies for autoimmune disease were negative, and most critically, correcting the severe Vitamin B12 deficiency did not resolve the deeper systemic phenotype.

If a young patient presents with major hematologic stress and organ-specific fibrosis (like liver or lung scarring) without the classic DC look, you have to really think. This phenotype can mimic acquired conditions but has a cryptic genetic origin.

## The Genetic Culprit: PARN and the Monoallelic Disease

Because the patient didn't look like a textbook cancer case, MRI, endoscopy, and a panel of autoimmune markers were negative for other standard workups. It was a case of the "hard-to-explain." We eventually had to call in the whole-exome sequencing team.

The genetic finding was a monoallelic missense variant in the **PARN gene** (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This variant was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in isolation, but the protein impact and the clinical phenotype matched. PARN is a highly conserved exoribonuclease involved in RNA processing. Crucially, it is essential for the 3'-end maturation of the telomerase R component (TERC).

Here is the kicker regarding the genetics: **This is not just classic Dyskeratosis Congenita.** The case highlights the critical distinction between biallelic and monoallelic mutations in this gene. Biallelic variants in PARN cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), usually with the classic mucocutaneous triad. However, **monoallelic variants** are more often implicated in **Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure (PFBMFT4)**.

This distinction is vital. Monoallelic PARN mutations can underlie the spectrum of TBDs. This has been documented in over 65 cases in the literature, and this specific patient, presenting with pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, fits that specific "monoallelic" disease pathway. It shows how a heterozygous state can manifest as a multisystem disorder in a young adult, often rapidly misdiagnosed as a nutritional deficiency or a standard autoimmune vasculitis until you see the telomeres aren't the issue, the gene is.

## Management and Systemic Surveillance

The patient was treated with supportive care: intravenous cyanocobalamin to correct the B12, folic acid, and physiotherapy. Her symptoms improved significantly, and her blood counts stabilized. This was the critical momentum, but the long term is about **surveillance**.

Since the foundation is telomere attrition, there is no curative medical therapy for the genetic defect itself (androgen therapy is still in clinical trials with unknown long-term effects; HCT is an option for BM failure but carries cure risks). The goal is delaying the disease progression. For a patient with this genetic profile, you must monitor the bone marrow failure risk because the marrow can succumb to stress, such as a sudden flare of erythrocytic stress or even solid organ transplant complications. The liver fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis also require ongoing monitoring for telomere shortening, as liver stiffness and lung fibrosis are seen in **88% of adult patients** with other components of the telomerase machinery (like TERT/TERC), and we found cirrhosis in this patient despite only monoallelic PARN mutation.

**We need to talk about the signaling to the team.** When you've ruled out autoimmune and neoplastic causes, but you still have fibrosis and cytopenia in a young person, ask your genetics lab for PARN. Ask flow cytometry for telomere length if available.

If you see premature greying, osteoporosis, and "fad" anemia in a young person with a negative iron panel and no autoimmune "numbskull" on serologies, do you suspect an underlying genetic etiology? The case above suggests the answer might be yes.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Rested Diagnostic Approach:** Consider Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD) in young adults with multi-organ fibrosis and unexplained cytopenia.
*   **Atypical Phenotype:** TBDs do not require the classic Dyskeratosis Congenita mucocutaneous triad (nail/pigment/leukoplakia).
*   **Premature Aging Signs:** Premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and atrophic lingual papillae should trigger scrutiny for a genetic cause in the context of marrow failure.
*   **PARN Monoallelic Spectrum:** Recognize that monoallelic PARN variants are linked to Pulmonary Fibrosis/Bone Marrow Failure (PFBMFT4) even more than typical RC mutations.
*   **Carry-Spouse Risk:** Once a genetic etiology is suspected, offer genetic counseling and family screening; many TBDs are inherited but can be silent in carriers until triggered by specific stressors.



## PARN Variants and Pulmonary Fibrosis: The Undervalued Respiratory Sign of Dyskeratosis Congenita

Most of the time when we triage a patient with suspected idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), we expect an older demographic, perhaps paraneoplastic or associated with autoimmune connective tissue disease. When you walk into the clinic with a 28-year-old woman presenting with exertional dyspnea, progressive fatigue, and peripheral fibrosis, your gut says "overwork" or "interstitial lung disease due to environmental exposure." But there is a second, quieter voice listening in the lab: the bone marrow failure, the premature greying of her hair, the liver stiffness like cirrhosis, and the severe cyclic anemia that refused to respond to standard nutritional correction alone.

This is a situation where Initiaip suspicion of IPF drowns out the possibility of a systemic cause. The patient in the case we are discussing came in with classic "telomere biology disorder" (TBD) features that didn't fit the textbook dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad initially. She looked tired, weak, and unhealthy, but she hadn't developed the leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, or reticular skin pigmentation that define the prototypical DC. Yet, investigate her whole genome. She was a PARN mutation carrier (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), classified initially as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), though the pattern strongly suggested a telomere biology defect.

If you don't pause here and think "Telomere," you might spend the rest of her life managing symptoms without addressing the root cause. This case highlights a critical transition point in clinical practice: the intersection of respiratory decline, hematopoietic failure, and aging in young adults.

## Diagnostic Dilemmas in Young Adults

Let’s be clear: IPF is rare in the late 20s, but it does happen. However, when it comes paired with bone marrow failure, think Uber. This combination of cytopenia, premature ageing signs, and organ fibrosis is the signature of a dysregulation in telomere length and maintenance.

The case report highlights several diagnostic pitfalls that mimic acquired disease:

The patient had severe vitamin B12 deficiency, confirmed by low serum levels and elevated Methylmalonic acid. The peripheral smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes, Cabot rings, and ineffective erythropoiesis on marrow exam. Initially, the clinicians treated with quantum B12. But the lung fibrosis, the liver scarring, and the skeletal deficits did not resolve. In the telomere biology world, you are effectively "fueled" but still running out of fuel.

The organ involvement pattern matters: PARN variants are part of a small family of genes (around 20% are tolerant to disruptive variants) where haploinsufficiency is the mechanism. Biallelic variants cause Autosomal Recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6), while monoallelic PARN variants are specifically known to link with Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure (PFBMFT4). Even when the variant status is a "VUS" by ACMG standards (which this was PM1, PM2, PP3 compliant), the clinical phenotype often dictates the management pathway.

Also note the hepatic involvement. We often think of TBDs as primarily mucocutaneous or hematologic. Recent registry data from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry suggests that liver fibrosis/cirrhosis is surprisingly prevalent in adults with TERT or TERC mutations. In this case, the patient had cirrhosis despite being a monoallelic PARN carrier, reinforcing that organ fibrosis extends beyond the respiratory system in these syndromes.

## Management and Surveillance

How do you manage this? When you suspect a genetic origin, and you have a "VUS" but a strong clinical match, follow the phenotype. Supportive care is the key. The patient received correctantidation for B12 and folic acid. She had a stable flow status on blood counts after this. Physiotherapy and supplementation helped her home dysfunction. Genetic counselling was provided.

The gold standard is detection at the molecular level, as per the case discussion. This includes Next-Generation Sequencing. While telomerase length can be measured, genetic testing is primary for differential diagnosis. And for the clinician: keep an eye out for the expanding phenotype.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   Young adults (<50) presenting with pulmonary fibrosis should prompt a workup for telomere biology disorders (TBD) even without classic mucocutaneous features.
*   Severe vitamins deficiencies can mimic ineffective erythropoiesis on MRI or marrow exam, but persistent fibrosis in other organs (liver) suggests an underlying genetic cause.
*   A monollical PARN variant is associated with PFBMFT4 (Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure), often presenting with monoallelic variants even if the VUS status is not fully pathogenic.
*   Hepatic fibrosis is a known component of TBDs, more prevalent in literature than previously appreciated (up to 88% in some adult TBD cohorts).
*   Genetic Counseling is mandatory for families; BOXE and support are necessary measures, as curative measures are limited but bone marrow transplantation is a consideration for some.

## Final Thoughts for the Clinician

This case was a perfect staging ground to discuss the overlap of respiratory and hematologic failure. We cannot diagnose Telomere Deficiency disorder in the "emergency room" setting, but we can see the red flags. If you are treating a patient with unexplained cytopenia and fibrosis, do not discard the genetic hypothesis just because the variant was a VUS. The clinical picture is the strongest clue in 50% of these rare cases.



# B12 and Folate Deficiency: Complicating the Hematologic Diagnosis in Marrow Failure Syndromes

### Don't Let Peripheral Smear Features Blind You to the Underlying Syndrome

There's a classic clinical narrative that plays out frequently in our hematopathology consults: a young adult presents with anemia, fatigue, and a periphery smear full of signs pointing directly to folate or vitamin B12 deficiency. You treat the deficiency, the platelets rebound, you close the chart, and a year later, the patient returns with the same weight, but also new liver fibrosis or relentless neuropathy.

This is the rub. Too often, we treat the obvious and miss the cryptic. Consider a 24-year-old woman who presented with a five-year history of worsening fatigue and weakness. Her peripheral smear told a familiar story: dimorphic anemia, basophilic stippling, and Cabot rings. Standard differential diagnosis for hematologists includes severe nutritional deficiencies. The workup confirmed severe vitamin B12 deficiency. She gets IV cyanocobalamin. Her symptoms improve. He checks... she has a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, pulmonary fibrosis on PET/CT, and progressive osteoporosis.

This exact constellation—pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis markers, yet unexplained fibrosis despite nutritional correction—should have been a yellow flag. The patient ultimately carried a monoallelic *PARN* mutation, confirmed as a variant linked to Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD). She didn't have the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita. Instead, she presented with what we call a "surgical" presentation of telomere attrition: accelerated "aging" signs like premature greying and a multidisciplinary syndrome involving pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis.

### Effective Erythropoiesis: A Sign, Not a Diagnosis

The most common reason we pivot from nutritional deficiency to marrow failure diagnostics in these cases is "ineffective erythropoiesis." When you have dimorphic anemia and basophilic stippling, you have failed to rule out nutritional causes definitively, but you also have to recognize that the bone marrow physiology in telomere biology disorders is fundamentally different from what you might expect from simple malabsorption.

In the case report provided, the patient had a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) with IgG 2+. While this often screams autoimmune hemolysis, the reticulocyte count was 0.39%—too low for immune destruction. Instead, the marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores. This is the polylandry of TBDs: the stem cells don't die immediately, but they stop responding efficiently. The marrow thinks it should iron B12-dependent erythropoiesis, but the telomeres have already signaled senescence.

The patient's liss (anemia) and neutrophil dysplasia (Cabot rings) strongly mimic the classic signs of Vitamin B12 deficiency, e.g., "megaloblastic anemia." However, in the context of pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, you have to pause. That is when you look for thefibrosis (hypothesis). That is when you look for fibrosis.

### The Red Herring of Ineffective Erythropoiesis

B12 deficiency is the "red herring" in some cases because it is treatable. If the marrow failure is purely nutritional, fixing the B12 cures the marrow failure. But if the failure is driven by telomere biology (as in this case), fixing the B12 treats the secondary inflammatory marker, or as the OBs call it, the secondary desease.

The patient's clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant after extensive workups. PARN is an exoribonuclease required for TERC maturation. A pathogenic variant here impairs telomerase function, or rather, the stability of the telomerase complex. This leads to shorter telomeres and shorter-lived stem cells. This explains the foundational marrow failure symptoms. But because B12 deficiency exacerbates DNA damage, the patient's symptoms improved *symptomatically* but did not reverse the telomere erosion physiology.

This is a crucial distinction. In this patient, correcting B12 *did* improve symptoms, but that does not mean the primary pathology was nutritional. It means the nutritional deficiency likely accelerated a pre-existing telomere attrition process.

### Takeaway: Rethinking the Differential for "B12 Failure"

When you are working through a patient with a "failed" nutritional deficiency and persistent cytopenia, ask yourself what organ systems are affected beyond the erythroid line. B12/folate deficiency alone rarely causes pulmonary fibrosis and skeletal abnormalities like osteoporosis without wasting.

1.  **Ineffective Erythropoiesis is the Starting Point, But Not the Diagnosis**: Dimorphic erythrocytes and basophilic stippling can be caused by B12 deficiency, but they also occur in Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs). Persistence of dysplasia despite repletion warrants genetic testing.
2.  **Multi-System Fibrosis Warrants SUV 2+**: In a young adult with pancytopenia, look hard for pulmonary or liver fibrosis. ß18F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on PET/CT can show diffuse marrow uptake in TBDs. Result: Normal autoimmune serologies.
3.  **Genetic Testing is Not Optional in "Atypical" The**: If B12 and folate correction fails to clear the marrow failure completely, or if there are concurrent skin/hair/organ fibrosis, consider Next-Generation Sequencing (WES) for telomere-related genes. Early recognition is crucial for genetic counselling and potential bone marrow transplant consideration.

### The Bottom Line

B12/Folate deficiency is common. Telomere Biology Disorders are rare. When they overlap, it can look like a simple malnutrition case initially. But when you chase the B12 supplementation and the marrow failure doesn't resolve, or when you see fibrosis elsewhere, stop the scroll on the "Nutritional" hypothesis. Look at the telomere. The patient didn't get cured by B12 alone; she got stabilized, confirmed she had a genetic type of TBD, and was put on a genetic counseling track. That is the sigmoidal curve of diagnostic discovery.



# Variant of Uncertain Significance in TBD: Where Clinical Phenotype Validates Genomics

## The Clinical Dilemma

In the world of precision medicine, there is a specific kind of diagnostic frustration that occurs when the molecular data doesn't scream "PATHOGENIC" but the clinical picture is screaming "PATHOGENIC." A Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in a gene with high functional importance, particularly in a patient with multisystem organ failure, often leaves clinicians agonizing. The ideal marker is the telomere biology chaos case: a patient who survived long enough to register, but presented with a constellation of symptoms so specific that the VUS actually painted a clearer picture of the disease than the gene說明 would have suggested.

Consider the case of a woman in her late 20s, presenting with a quintessential "old age" in a young body. We aren't talking about journals or dietary habits here; we are talking about wasted amyloid, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and pancytopenia. She had a 5-year history of fatigue that had reduced her functional capacity. When you look at the physical exam, you see her receding hairline, premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a tongue with lingual papillae atrophy. These are the hallmarks of a dyskeratosis congenita (DC)-like phenotype.

In this specific case, the whole-exome sequencing revealed a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). Under standard ACMG/AMP criteria, many clinical geneticists would classify this as a Variant of Uncertain Significance because of the "incomplete penetrance" rules and heterozygous status. However, the data known in the field regarding *PARN* prioritization leans toward haploinsufficiency for multiple alleles. So, the clinical question becomes: do we accept a VUS as a diagnosis for a telomere biology disorder (TBD) when the phenotype is classic for it?

## Why the Variant Was Uncertain

The issue of *PARN* is particularly tricky. The gene encodes a poly(A) PIA polymerase protein that forms a critical complex with TerC (TERC) to maintain the telomere lig. If you mess with that machinery, your telomeres get too short. This causes cellular senescence in erythroid cells eventually.

The patient in our case tested positive for severe vitamin B12 deficiency and ineffective erythropoiesis. Folic acid and cobalamin replacement were standard treatments. There was a positive DAT (direct Coats test) for IgG, but the indirect Coats test was negative. This initially made immune-mediated hemolytic anemia a consideration, but the low reticulocyte count in the face of hemolysis markers grew more concerning for intrinsic bone marrow issues.

The liver showed cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa), but autoimmune etiology was ruled out. There was no evidence of viral infection or cancer on an 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake PET-CT window. So, if you look at your data streams, you get two things. One is a biochemical process that suggests deficiency (B12 deficiency), and the other is a molecular process that suggests telomeric attrition (VUS *PARN*). The severity of the systemic findings outweigh the discrepancy between the genetic label and the clinical reality.

## From Uncertain to Diagnosis

Why this clinical correlation matters is precisely where the "Take Home" points come in. You cannot rely solely on the "Probable" label from the bioinformatics team when the protein structure itself lies in a "critical functional domain" (R3H domain). The case highlights that while *PARN* has been linked to autosomal recessive Tier Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6), monoallelic variants often underlie telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

The patient in our case had a systemic failure pattern involving the bone marrow, liver, lungs, and overall myocyte function. While classical mucocutaneous features like nail dystrophy might have been expected, they were absent, yet the patient had a bald tongue, greying hair, and neurologic involvement. The VUS status did not exclude the disease. Instead, the presence of the variant combined with a classic telomere biology phenotype provided a winning diagnosis.

It also raises the question of why it is monoallelic but present in early adulthood. The case notes that one third of adult patients with additional *TERC* or *TERT* mutations have liver failure, and the *PARN* variant here normalized the telomere structure led to tissue failure in specific organs. The identification of the PARN gene alone was a start, but the clinical phenotype validated the pathogenicity.

## Management and the Future

The management of TBDs is shifting from "supportive" to "diagnostic counseling focused." In this case, the patient was treated aggressively for nutritional deficiencies (IV cyanocobalamin, vitamin D, iron, calcium). She received blood transfusions for anemia.

After the VUS-phenotype correlation was established, the family received genetic counselling. They understood that this could become a "progressive case" over time. The outcome report showed stable blood counts after follow-up. The lesson here is not just to treat the symptoms but to provide a long-range plan for bone marrow surveillance and lung function testing.

Sometimes, a diagnosis isn't enough to explain the prognosis, and knowing the genetic etiology changes the burden of expectation for the family.

## Key Takeaways

*   **VUS is not a diagnosis, but in syndrome-specific contexts, clinical phenotype can validate genetic variants.** When a patient has a classic TBDD phenotype (fibrosis + marrow failure + ageing features), a VUS in a telomere gene is highly suggestive of the disorder.
*   **Telomere Biology Disorders can present without the classic mucocutaneous triad.** Lung fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and bone marrow failure are equally valid presentation pathways for TBDs.
*   **Monoallelic *PARN* variants affect telomere biology.** While PARN mutations in DDR1 usually cause recessive PK, their monoallelic presence corresponds to PFBMFT4 (pulmonary fibrosis/bone marrow failure).
*   **Screening is critical.** Once a suspected VUS is identified, ongoing clinical and haematological monitoring is essential to assess for evolving marrow failure phenotypes.
*   **Don't dismiss the phenotype.** If nutritional or other common causes of pancytopenia don't explain the fibrosis, and a genetic variant in the right pathway exists, it may be genetic enough to consider for definitive diagnosis.



## Genomic Risks and Survivorship: Management of Mendelian Premature Ageing in Patients

Imagine a patient in their late twenties who arrives with a confusing constellation of symptoms. There is fatigue, pancytopenia, and what looks like severe B12 deficiency leading to ineffective erythropoiesis and liver issues. You correct the vitamin and transfuse blood cells, but the patient still has premature greying of the hair, brittle bones, and progressive lung fibrosis. The diagnosis is a riddle without the classic clues. This patient turned out to have a monoallelic variant in the *PARN* gene, presenting with telomere biology disorder features that lacked the textbook mucocutaneous triad. What makes this case particularly instructive isn't just the diagnosis—it is that genetic disorders causing telomere attrition can masquerade as acquired multisystem inflammatory syndromes or nutritional deficiencies in adults.

Now that we have confirmed the genetic pathology with Whole Exome Sequencing, the clinical narrative shifts entirely. You move from an acute diagnostic crisis to the far more complex task of survivorship and genomic management. This is where the reality of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) meets the gray areas of evidence-based medicine. Patients with confirmed variants in telomere-related genes, such as the *PARN* patient described, often present with a unique challenge: how much active intervention is warranted against a progressive, likely incurable genetic process?

**Differentiating Monoallelic vs. Biallelic Risk**
From a clinical management perspective, understanding the inheritance pattern is the first step in counseling. *PARN* demonstrates haploinsufficiency, meaning monoallelic variants (one affected copy) can lead to significant disease, as seen in the *PFBMFT4* phenotype, whereas biallelic variants often present in recessive dyskeratosis congenita. This distinction is vital for survivors. A monoallelic *PARN* carrier, like our patient, faces a syndrome more akin to pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure than the aggressive childhood onset of Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. 

You must tailor the surveillance intensity accordingly. While biallelic disease patients might require aggressive itinerant testing, monoallelic carriers still face a trajectory of declining telomere length. The critical difference in management is the recognition that while somatic repair is impossible, the specific organ systems targeted by the haploinsufficiency can be monitored closely to catch the tipping point where cell death becomes terminal.

**Organ-Specific Survivorship Protocols**
Based on the case complications, the most immediate threats are hepatic and pulmonary, with the hematopoietic niche remaining vulnerable.
*   **Respiratory Surveillance:** The patient presented with ground-glass haziness and subpleural atelectasis on pET-CT. Plans should include regular serial respiratory function tests and high-resolution microscopy. If the fibrosis progresses to require escalation, referral for transplant evaluation is a consideration sooner rather than later.
*   **Hepatic Tracking:** This patient developed cirrhosis without autoimmune markers. In TBD cases, hepatomegaly and hepatic stiffness on transient elastography are frequent even without classic autoimmune hepatitis antibodies. Routine screening for viral markers or autoantibodies is standard, but you must also watch for hepatocellular carcinoma and early decompensation, as fibrosis may progress silently.
*   **Bone Marrow Vigilance:** Pancytopenia in this context is often exacerbated by telomere shortening. The patient had a response to B12 correction, but underlying marrow failure remains a risk. Complete blood counts should be monitored quarterly or more frequently if transfusion dependence persists. A telomere blooddot FISH assay should be available for monitoring telomere dynamics if standard markers are ambiguous.

**The Art of Genetic Understanding**
Counseling is just as critical as clinical follow-up. Many families worry if this is hereditary. In this instance, a de novo mutation was a possibility, and the family history was negative. It is crucial to explain that while the patient overall carries the disease, there are no obvious family threads to confirm inheritance. Patients must understand that passing it on risks having the same disease in their children. Furthermore, the variant status (e.g., "Variant of Uncertain Significance" as initially labeled) can be confusing. Clinicians must emphasize that the genotype provides strong evidence for the phenotype even if the specific protein sequence is not definitively pathogenic in isolation. The gene function (cleaving poly(A) tail length) combined with the clinical score overrides the in-silico prediction in atypical cases.

**The Reality of Treatment Curability**
In TBD management, there is a sobering acceptance that cure is often not feasible. Supportive care—transfusions, supplements, and physiotherapy—has shown symptomatic improvement in our patient without reversing the underlying telomere biology. However, you do not give up on transplant. For hematopoietic melanocyte dysfunction, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only curative option. However, in pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, the prognosis remains guarded. 

It is tempting to consider androgens like danazol to stimulate telomerase. Current data suggests a potential increase in telomere length and symptom improvement, but the safety profile is less than ideal, especially with long-term use. We must discuss the risk of adverse events in a patient with liver fibrosis.
*   **The Bottom Line:** The initial focus is optimizing supportive care, maintaining remission of the B12 and marrow issues, and preventing complications of the fibrosis.
*   **Future Directions:** Telomerase activators are not a standard of care yet; their role is palliative.

## Clinical Take-home Messages

*   Always consider Telomere Biology Disorders when unexplained multiorgan failure presents in young adults, even without the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad.
*   Monitor for organ-specific fibrosis (pulmonary and hepatic) in monoallelic *PARN* variants, as these often mimic acquired autoimmune diseases.
*   Recognize that while hemoglobin reduction and marrow failure can be responsive to nutritional support and transfusion, genetic predisposition necessitates lifelong marrow surveillance.
*   Genetic counseling is essential to clarify inheritance patterns (including potential de novo cases) and managing expectations regarding transplant vs. palliative support.
*   Maintain a multidisciplinary approach involving hematology, pulmonology, and genetic services to manage the length of the disease trajectory.
