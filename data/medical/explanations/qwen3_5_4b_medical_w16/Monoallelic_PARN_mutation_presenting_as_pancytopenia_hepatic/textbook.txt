Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Differential Diagnosis

The patient is a woman in her late 20s who presented with a five-year history of progressive generalised weakness and early fatigability. In clinical practice, fatigue in this demographic is often multifactorial, typically attributed to common stressors, anemia, or chronic metabolic disorders. However, the chronicity of her symptoms coupled with a gradual decline in functional capacity, reducing her ability to perform daily activities compared to her peers, signals a systemic pathology rather than an acute, isolated complaint. The 12-month timeline of exacerbation—where weakness progressed to the point of impairing self-care—suggests an underlying stabilisation or progressive disease process that requires investigation beyond standard reversible causes, such as cardiac or thyroid abnormalities initially excluded in general practice settings.

Physical examination provides a critical set of clues pointing toward accelerated ageing and multi-system involvement. Notable findings included premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae, commonly described as a 'bald tongue'. These features approximate to a classic triad of dyskeratosis congenita, though the patient lacked oral leukoplakia. The presence of hepatosplenomegaly (liver 5 cm below the right costal margin) and reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scapular regions indicated concurrent hepatocellular and pulmonary pathology. These physical stigmata—specifically prematurity of greying and mucosal changes—are considered pathognomonic for telomere biology disorders (TBDs), even in the absence of the full mucocutaneous triad. Such features necessitate the differential diagnosis to extend beyond standard hematologic cancer or nutritional issues towards an inherited genetic syndrome.

Laboratory evaluation initially confirmed pancytopenia with a significantly low reticulocyte count (0.39%), indicating bone marrow failure rather than simple peripheral destruction. Peripheral smear supported this, demonstrating dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and Cabot rings, which are hallmarks of ineffective erythropoiesis. Bone marrow examination confirmed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia but an inability to generate functional cells. This distinction is crucial: while the marrow was attempting to produce cells (hyperplasia), they were dying early in their development. This pattern differentiates bone marrow failure syndromes from autoimmune hemolytic anemia, where markers of ineffective erythropoiesis are typically absent, and distinguishes it from nutritional deficiencies where marrow often shows megaloblastic changes without the granular stippling or hyperchromasia seen here.

Despite these genetic markers, the initial workup presented significant red herrings that could divert the diagnostic trajectory. Serum studies confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, which accounts for several clinical features including neuropahty and macrocytosis. Additionally, a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG raised the suspicion of an immune-mediated process, such as autoimmune hemolytic anemia. However, the low reticulocyte response and the finding of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral destruction suggested the positive DAT might be a false marker or secondary to the marrow dysfunction. Furthermore, neurologic and neural signs could have been attributed to B12 deficiency alone, yet the severity of cytopenia and organ fibrosis was disproportionate to the documented nutritional status.

The final trigger for the genetic diagnosis was the persistence of organ fibrosis unresponsive to aggressive nutritional management. Ultrasound and transient elastography confirmed grade II hepatic steatosis and cirrhosis, while chest imaging revealed diffuse ground-glass haziness and fibrotic changes consistent with pulmonary fibrosis. Given that the pancytopenia remained stable despite correction of the vitamin deficiencies, and given the specific pattern of premature aging and fibrotic organ involvement, an acquired auto-immune or nutritional etiology could no longer explain the constellation of multisystem disease. This clinical picture strongly suggested a cryptic genetic disorder affecting telomere biology, a group of rare syndromes that can mimic acquired conditions and present with variable penetrance in young adults. Consequently, the differential diagnosis shifted toward telomere-related disorders to guide further genetic testing and surveillance.

## Telomere Biology Disorders: Pathophysiology and the PARN Mechanism

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a spectrum of rare, early-onset premature ageing phenotypes driven by defects in the maintenance of chromosomal termini. The fundamental pathophysiology rests on the biology of telomeres, those repetitive nucleotide sequences (TTAGGG in humans) capping the ends of linear chromosomes. These structures protect genomic integrity by shielding chromosomes from degradation and preventing end-to-end fusions. As eukaryotic cells divide, telomeres shorten; when they reach a critical minimum length, this cessation of cell division activates the senescence or apoptosis pathways. This phenomenon, known as the Hayflick limit, represents the finite replicative capacity of somatic cells. In mediating this process, the enzyme telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein complex comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—elaborates telomere length through elongation preceded by replication. In clinical practice, this understanding is vital for interpreting the progression of organ failure in the case presented; the patient's bone marrow failure, liver fibrosis, and lung changes reflect the cumulative effect of telomere attrition across rapidly dividing and metabolically active tissues.

Specific molecular dysregulation in this patient was identified within the *PARN* gene, which encodes a protein belonging to the highly conserved family of exoribonucleases. The core function of the PARN protein is the deadenylation of mRNA to shorten poly(A) tails, thereby regulating gene expression and stabilizing nuclear RNA degradation pathways. Crucially for telomere biology, this deadenylation process is essential for the 3′-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Deep sequencing suggests that PARN removes post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails that would otherwise render the TERC complex unstable or insufficient for optimal TERT activity. Pathogenic variants in *PARN* impair this RNA maturation, leading to incomplete TERC assembly. Since the TERT subunit requires a functional TERC RNA template to elongate telomeres, the disruption caused by *PARN* dysfunction results in a secondary failure of the telomerase complex. Consequently, while the primary defect is in the *PARN* protein, the downstream effect is a measurable reduction in functional telomerase activity and accelerated shortening of telomere length in affected tissues.

The clinical complexity of *PARN* disorders is often explained by the concept of haploinsufficiency. Germline pathogenic variants in *PARN* are recognized as causes of autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and telomere-related bone marrow failure and pulmonary fibrosis (PFBMFT4). However, the threshold for haploinsufficiency varies significantly among genes, with *PARN* being among the twenty most intolerant to loss-of-function variation. In the case presented, the patient carried a monoallelic missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This specific variant alters a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain, which is one of a five out of five in silico prediction tools as indicative of a damaging effect. Because many TBDs require bi-allelic mutations for clinical manifestation, monoallelic variants are typically expected to cause milder, condition-specific phenotypes such as pulmonary fibrosis or isolated bone marrow failure. However, *PARN* demonstrates high essentiality, supporting the underlying diagnosis even in the presence of a variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) initially.

When such haploinsufficiency occurs, it creates a field defect in rapidly dividing cells that disproportionately exceeds tissue tolerance limits. The hematopoietic stem cells, bone marrow, skin, and hepatocytes are the primary cell groups affected because they are actively dividing. The case highlights the interaction between telomere biology and other causes of marrow suppression; the patient presented with severe vitamin B12 deficiency, which caused ineffective erythropoiesis and raised suspicion for a primary nutritional cause. However, telomere shortening compromises the replicative potential of marrow precursors, rendering them intrinsically susceptible to stressors like severe B12 deficiency. This creates a "field defect" where the genetic predisposition magnifies the impact of secondary insults. In the context of the patient's fibrosis, the tropism for fibrosis-prone tissues requires an extended understanding of cellular senescence.

The impact on differentiated tissues, particularly the liver and lungs, necessitates differentiating constitutional fibrosis from acquired pathology. The patient presented with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis concomitant with osteoporosis and neuropathy. While chronic inflammation or autoimmune disease (e.g., autoimmune hepatitis) could mimic fibrosis, the absence of specific serologies and exclusion of infectious causes pointed toward a genetic etiology. The key differentiator lies in the persistence of structural accumulation and the lack of acclimatization to therapy. The patient improved symptomatically after correcting B12 deficiency, yet the deep structural changes in the lung and liver persisted on imaging (ground-glass haziness, stiffness). In acquired fibrosis, biomarkers such as liver stiffness and lung CT attenuation often fluctuate with anti-inflammatory treatment, whereas constitutional fibrosis in TBDs is often irreversible due to the irreversible accumulation of cellular senescence. This distinction is crucial for surveillance; in TBDs, the prognosis depends on the rate of progressional fibrosis and the risk of neoplasia, requiring longitudinal monitoring even as acquired metabolic conditions are treated separately. Understanding this mechanism allows clinicians to prioritize genetic testing in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease, even when classical mucocutaneous features are absent, ensuring early recognition for genetic counselling and appropriate surveillance of potential bone marrow transplantation indications.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Genetic Interpretation

In this case, the diagnostic journey illustrates the diagnostic dilemma often encountered when a patient presents with multisystem organ failure in the absence of a clear etiology. The patient manifested progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, and organ fibrosis, features that initially suggested common acquired disorders such as severe nutritional deficiency, autoimmune connective tissue disease, or malignancy. Early investigation confirmed severe vitamin B12 deficiency with evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis and autoimmune hepatitis serologies were negative. However, the persistence of organ dysfunction—specifically hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis—as well as premature greying of hair and osteoporosis, despite the correction of nutritional deficiencies, remained unexplained. This clinical disconnect between the response to supportive treatment and the continued progression of fibrotic and hematologic phenotypes represents a common trigger for expanding diagnostic thresholds beyond biochemical corrections. When standard malignancy and inflammatory workups, including PET-CT and viral serology, were negative, the possibility of an underlying cryptic genetic syndrome became probable, necessitating a shift in diagnostic strategy toward molecular analysis.

Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) proved to be the critical diagnostic tool in identifying this atypical presentation. In patients presenting with telomere biology disorders (TBDs) who lack the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oropharyngeal leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation), WES allows for the identification of Mendelian defects that are masked by the lack of these specific signs. The patient's exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). Initially classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), this result was counterintuitive in isolation but highly supportive clinically. Interpreting VUS requires weighing computational predictions against clinical context. According to the ACMG/AMP standards outlined in the discussion, this variant meets specific supportive criteria: PM1 (substitution in a critical functional R3H domain), PP2 (low rate of benign variation in PARN), and PP3 (in silico predictors of damaging effect). However, a key principle in TBD diagnosis is that clinical-phenotypic concordance can elevate the significance of a variant even when its pathogenicity remains uncertain. The presence of dual organ failure (hematologic and fibrotic) with a rare gene of known telomere biology importance provided sufficient correlational evidence to diagnose a TBD despite the VUS classification at the time of reporting.

Interpreting the genetic data further requires understanding the genotype-phenotype correlation specific to the PARN gene. Variants in this gene demonstrate a distinct spectrum where biallelic variants typically cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita 6 (DKCB6), while monoallelic variants are more frequently associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). This patient presented as a monoallelic carrier but exhibited a phenotype that bridged both categories. The bone marrow failure was crucially highlighted by the pancytopenia and ineffective erythropoiesis, while the hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary involvement were pronounced, suggesting a broader disruption in telomere regulation beyond isolated marrow failure. This observation underscores the necessity of monitoring for progressive bone marrow failure even in monoallelic PARN carriers who present initially with pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis. The heterogeneity is further explained by the pathway of telomere attrition compromising stem cell replicative potential in multiple tissues.

The diagnostic reasoning also highlighted the role of supportive evidence that may not always be part of the initial workup but serves as a confirmation of the genetic suspicion. While clinical correlation drove the diagnosis in this specific case, the standard for TBDS often includes flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays to measure telomere length. Echocardiography and transfusion studies, though supportive of hemodynamic stability, did not fully explain the fibrosis. In broader TBD contexts, the combination of genetic variants with telomere length measurements helps differentiate a VUS from a false-negative variant. For this patient, the progression to normalization of blood counts and stabilization of fibrosis status during the 2-week follow-up is partially attributable to the supportive management of deficiencies, yet the underlying genetic burden remains a critical factor requiring surveillance. Ultimately, the integration of WES findings with the exclusion of secondary causes like autoimmune hepatitis or infectious granulomatous disease demonstrates that cryptic genetic syndromes can be missed by standard protocols. Early recognition is paramount to guide long-term genetic counseling, surveillance for malignancy risk, and potential hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive cases.

## Management Strategies and Long-term Surveillance

The management of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is fundamentally supportive, as no disease-modifying pharmacological therapies exist for the flawed telomere biology itself in most cases. While emerging data suggest that androgens like danazol or telocytes might temporarily extend telomere length, the standard of care for patients like our subject relies on mitigating complications and stabilizing organ function. Early recognition is viewed as the cornerstone of therapy, allowing for the prevention of irreversible fibrosis and the timely consideration of curative interventions such as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) in the context of progressive bone marrow failure (BMFS).

Nutritional management plays a pivotal role in the initial stabilization of TBD patients, particularly when metabolic deficiencies contribute to exacerbations of hematologic manifestations. In this case, the severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies likely acted as unmasking factors or accelerators of the underlying telomere shortening, resulting in ineffective erythropoiesis. Management involves aggressive parenteral or oral correction of specific components, such as intravenous cyanocobalamin and folic acid, to reverse cytopenias driven by nutrient malabsorption. However, because underlying TBD physiology renders hematopoietic stem cells highly sensitive to stress, simple nutritional correction must be vigilantly monitored. Guidelines for managing inefficient erythropoiesis in TBDs recommend maintaining a reticulocyte count above 5-10% and ensuring hemoglobin levels remain stable; transfusion triggers are individualized based on symptoms of severe anemia, with transfusions reserved for cases where red blood cell quality remains poor despite nutritional intervention to prevent iron overload, a risk in patients with ineffective erythropoiesis.

Bone health is another critical domain requiring specific attention given the patient's constitutional and genetic profile. Telomere attrition affects rapidly dividing cells, including osteoblasts, leading to secondary osteoporosis and metabolic bone disease, as exemplified by this patient's thoracic scoliosis and fragility fractures. Management follows a standard protocol of aggressive prevention, including high-dose vitamin D and calcium supplementation, alongside periodic dual-energy X-ray absorpty (DEXA) scanning to monitor bone mineral density. Given the patient’s elevated alkaline phosphatase and secondary hyperparathyroidism, which further compromise bone integrity, monitoring for metabolic bone disease is essential for fracture risk reduction in TBD patients.

Genetic counseling is indispensable for long-term surveillance and family planning. The management plan must address the variant architecture, which typically follows a Mendelian model but presents specific complexities cases this patient. While the identified *PARN* variant was categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in isolation, the clinical-phenotypic correlation supports the hypothesis of a pathogenic monoallelic event, suggesting haploinsufficiency. Couples should be counseled regarding the possibility of de novo mutations, which can occur, as well as the risk of autosomal recessive transmission if the biologic nature of the mutation is ultimately clarified. Families must understand that the disorder is progressive, and genetic carriers of *PARN* variants face elevated risks of telomere-related disease, necessitating pre-natal testing options if clinically appropriate.

Finally, defining the standard for long-term care involves establishing a protocol of inter-disciplinary surveillance to detect organ-specific progression before clinical decompensation occurs. For patients presenting with pancytopenia, regular complete blood counts with morphological assessment are required to differentiate between marrow failure and secondary dysplasia. Simultaneously, monitoring for pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis is vital; the patient required regular imaging to monitor liver stiffness and lung function, as fibrosis progression is a major morbidity factor. HSCT should be considered as a potential curative option primarily for those with active or progressive cytopenias, though eligibility criteria remain restrictive. This patient continued surveillance for hepatic and pulmonary function, maintaining a balance between curative timing risk and the current availability of only symptomatic interventions.

## Clinical Significance and Emerging Phenotypes of PARN Mutations

The case of the forty-seven-year-old patient fundamentally challenges the traditional dogma that Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) and its associated Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD) require the classic mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticulated pigmentation. While this patient exhibited several of these features including knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue, her primary presentation was dominated by bone marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary involvement without the overt skin or mucosal findings seen in autosomal recessive KC/TH. This expansion into tissue-specific syndromes, particularly in adult-onset presentations where the inherited defect is masked by organ failure, necessitates a shift in diagnostic prioritization. In the context of hematological emergencies in young adults, the presence of a monogenic TBD gene variant like PARN must raise suspicion above common acquired causes such as alcoholic liver disease or viral hepatitis, especially when symptoms are disproportionate to laboratory values, as seen in this patient’s pancytopenia and severe fibrosis.

Genotypically, the spectrum of disease caused by *PARN* mutations is defined by the allelic inheritance status. While biallelic variants typically cause Autosomal Recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita Type 6 (DKCB6) with a severe childhood phenotype, this case highlights the distinct natural history of monoallelic variants. This patient presented a monoallelic missense variant (c.613T>C) associated with the Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis/Bone Marrow Failure Type 4 (PFBMFT4) spectrum. However, unlike the classic teaching that monoallelic PARN variants predominantly manifest primarily as Pulmonary Fibrosis (PFBMFT4), this individual demonstrated significant hematological dysfunction alongside fibrotic lung and liver disease. This realization suggests that haploinsufficiency in *PARN* may lead to systemic telomere dysfunction across multiple rapidly dividing cell populations, including hematopoietic stem cells and parenchymal liver cells, even when there is no biallelic defect. This has profound implications for surveillance; clinicians must monitor for pulmonary fibrosis in patients with monoallelic PARN mutations simultaneously with bone marrow failure, rather than viewing them as mutually exclusive organ-specific presentations.

The integration of hepatic fibrosis into the *PARN* associated profile further complicates the diagnostic algorithm. Literature-based associations for TBD with hepatic fibrosis are most robustly linked to mutations in telomere maintenance genes such as *TERT* and *TERC*, where cirrhosis has been identified in a significant percentage of adult patients. Conversely, *PARN* is predominantly an exoribonuclease involved in the processing of the Telomerase RNA Component (TERC), not a core structural component of the enzyme complex itself. Despite this distinction, the development of hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis in this monoallelic *PARN* patient underscores the pleiotropic nature of dyskeratosis-associated DNA. Clinically, this emphasizes that in patients exhibiting evidence of hepatic stiffness and steatosis without typical inflammatory markers, genetic evaluation cannot be postponed until autoimmune or viral etiologies are ruled out. The case serves as a reminder that unexplained coronary fibrosis in early-onset malignancy risks or unexplained cytopenias invites a genetic inquiry that transcends standard liver pathology workups.

Regarding the genetic classification itself, the management in the case highlights the pragmatic application of ACMG/AMP standards to variants of uncertain significance (VUS). The identified variant (p.Cys205Arg) was not definitively pathogenic but was supported by specific criteria including PM1 (conserved in the R3H domain), PM2 (population absence), and PP3/PP2 (computational prediction and gene tolerance of benign variants). For medical students and treating physicians, the lesson drawn from this analysis is that while ACMG standards provide a rigorous framework, they must be applied concurrently with clinical phenotype. A VUS should not rule out a TBD diagnosis when the clinical phenotype is highly suggestive of a telomere biology disorder; rather, these findings require a stratified follow-up strategy. The patient was managed with aggressive surveillance of bone marrow function and organ progression, exercising the same vigilance as if the variant were definitively pathogenic, until further data solidifies the classification.

Future therapeutic landscapes are currently limited in the *PARN*-associated spectrum of TBDs. In the case presented, management relied heavily on supportive care to address reversible deficiencies (such as the severe vitamin B12 deficiency) and to stabilize hemodynamics through transfusion. There is growing interest in telomere elongation agents, such as androgens (e.g., danazol), and small molecule activators, though the long-term efficacy and safety profiles regarding malignancy risk are unknown. The emergence of somatic genetic rescue in *TERC* has encouraged the hypothesis that haploinsufficiency in other genes may similarly respond to such interventions, yet clinical data for *PARN* remains paucity. This case ultimately reinforces that while curative options like hematopoietic stem cell transplantation are potentially life-saving in severe marrow fails, the long-term management of a TBD with multiple system involvement is a lifelong, condition-specific journey.
