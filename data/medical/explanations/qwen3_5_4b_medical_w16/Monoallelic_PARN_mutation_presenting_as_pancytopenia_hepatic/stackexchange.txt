Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In an adult with pancytopenia and signs of premature aging, what clinical and laboratory features specifically distinguish a Telomere Biology Disorder from severe nutritional deficiencies, even when B12 and folate levels are abnormal?

**Morning Report Pearl: Differentiating Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD) from Nutritional Deficiencies**

**Clinical Scenario**
Consider a young adult presenting with pancytopenia and signs of cellphone aging. While severe Vitamin B12/folate deficiency can explain the marrow failure (pancytopenia, ineffective erythropoiesis), specific features in this case point toward a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD) such as **telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure type 4 (TBD-TBD)**.

**Distinguishing Clinical Features**
In this vignette (Soni et al.), several features distinguish TBD from simple nutritional deficiency, even when lab values confirm B12 deficiency:

*   **Disproportionate Visceral Fibrosis:** The patient exhibited **hepatic cirrhosis** and **pulmonary fibrosis** (ground-glass haziness on CT). While nutritional deficiency explains the cytopenia, severe B12/folate deficiency alone does not typically cause accelerated fibrosis of both the liver and lungs in a young adult.
*   **Premature Mucocutaneous Signs:** Examination revealed **premature greying of hair**, bald tongue (urticaria atrophian), and knuckle hyperpigmentation. These are classic heralds of TBDs but may be absent or subtle in monoallelic variants compared to the classical triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita.
*   **Persistent Systemic Dysfunction:** The key differentiator observed was the **severity of clinical findings appeared disproportionate to nutritional deficiency alone**. Despite intensive correction of Vitamin B12 (IV cyanocobalamin) and folate, the systemic dysfunction (fibrosis, cytopenias, neuropathy) remained persistent and multisystemic.

**Laboratory Considerations**
*   **Morphology vs. Aetiology:** The peripheral smear showed features of ineffective erythropoiesis (dimorphic anemia, Cabot rings). While this is diagnostic of B12 deficiency, the persistence of this pattern after correction suggests an underlying susceptibility (e.g., short telomeres impairing hematopoietic stem cell replication).
*   **Iron Overload:** The patient had elevated serum iron and ferritin consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis, which can complicate the picture of "nutritional anemia."

**Management Principle**
In young adults with pancytopenia and organ fibrosis, the diagnosis of TBD should not be excluded before ruling out malignancy. As noted in the background: "**Monoallelic variants in TERT, TERC, RTEL1 [and] PARN… typically identified in TBDs… presenting as IPF or BMFS.**"

While genetic testing (Next-Generation Sequencing) is the gold standard, the presence of **persistent fibrosis disproportionate to hematologic findings and premature greying** warrants the pharmacogenetic workup. The case highlights that **WES identified the PARN mutation**, confirming that even monoallelic variants are associated with this spectrum. Early recognition is crucial, as curative treatment (eventually hematopoietic stem cell transplantation) may be indicated if marrow failure progresses.

**Take-Home Point:**
In patients with pancytopenia and micronutrient deficiencies, **hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis combined with premature greying** should raise suspicion for a Telomere Biology Disorder. These findings often persist despite nutritional correction, necessitating genetic testing for telomere-related genes like **PARN**.

### [Management] Despite a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), how do the findings of low reticulocytes and increased trilineage maturation (with erythroid hyperplasia) on bone marrow biopsy support a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis over peripheral immune-mediated hemolysis?

**Clinical Pearl: Distinguishing Ineffective Erythropoiesis from Immune Hemolysis**

While a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) often suggests an autoimmune etiology for anemia, the bone marrow response provides the definitive differentiator between peripheral immune-mediated hemolysis and ineffective erythropoiesis. Peripheral destruction typically elicits a compensatory marrow response characterized by significant reticulocytosis; conversely, ineffective erythropoiesis involves intramedullary hemolysis of precursors, resulting in an inappropriately low reticulocyte count despite active marrow proliferation.

In this case, the patient presented with an IgG-positive DAT but exhibited a **reticulocyte count of 0.39%** and a bone marrow biopsy showing **erythroid hyperplasia** with preserved trilineage maturation. The combination of robust erythroid hyperplasia without a corresponding rise in reticulocytes indicates that the marrow is attempting to compensate but is failing to release functional erythrocytes, consistent with a marrow insidious compared to peripheral loss. As noted by the authors, "the low reticulocyte response and marrow findings were more consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral immune-mediated destruction." This dissociation between the positive DAT and the lack of reticulocytosis, augmented by the clinical picture of a telomere biology disorder, supports an intramedullary failure mechanism over primary peripheral immune destruction.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the specific molecular function of the PARN protein regarding telomeric RNA stability, and how does a mutation affecting the R3H domain lead to cellular telomere shortening?

This case report highlights the critical molecular dependency of telomere maintenance on the PARN protein. **PARN acts as a highly conserved exoribonuclease essential for the 3-prime-end maturation of the Telomerase RNA Component (TERC).** Specifically, the report notes that PARN is "required for the removal of post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails that target nuclear RNAs for degradation." These tails, if not processed by PARN, ensure the 3-prime-end maturation of TERC, a constitutive step required for the functional assembly of the telomerase complex (TERT-TERC).

**R3H Domain Mutations Compromise Telomere Length**
In this patient, a monoallelic missense variant (p.Cys205Arg) was identified within the **R3H domain (residues 178–245)** of the PARN protein. The case report characterizes substitutions within this domain as occurring in a "critical and well-established functional domain" with no known benign variation. Damage to the R3H domain impairs PARN's deadenylation function, which leads to the failure to properly mature TERC. Consequently, the telomerase complex cannot maintain telomere length. This mechanism of "abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres" underlies the diasedal phenotype of the telomere biology disorder (TBD), resulting in clinical manifestations such as bone marrow failure (pancytopenia) and organ fibrosis observed in the patient.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why is Whole Exome Sequencing the definitive diagnostic tool for this case, and what clinical patterns typically warrant direct consideration of Telomere Biology Disorders as a differential diagnosis?

In this case, whole exome sequencing (WES) serves as the definitive diagnostic tool because it identified the molecular etiology— a monoallelic *PARN* variant—that remained elusive despite a thorough clinical exclusion of common hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune causes. While flow cytometry and telomere length quantification assess the functional consequence of telomere attrition, WES identifies the specific genetic defect driving it. In the context of this patient, WES was essential to link a "variant of uncertain significance" in a telomere-maintenance gene to her progressive multisystem decline, whereas targeted testing likely would have missed the *PARN* alteration (not on the *Dyskeratosis Congenita* prototype panel for *DKC1*).

Regarding telomere biology disorders (TBDs), clinical vigilance should rise when encountering young adults (typically late 20s to early 30s) with unexplained multisystem involvement. Key patterns warranting consideration include:
1.  **Bone Marrow Failure:** Progressive cytopenia or ineffective erythropoiesis that persists despite nutritional correction, suggesting replicative stress on hematopoietic stem cells due to telomere shortening.
2.  **Organ Fibrosis:** Concurrent liver (cirrhosis/hepatocyte dysfunction) and lung (pulmonary fibrosis) involvement, particularly when accompanied by neuropathy and osteoporosis.
3.  **Accelerated Ageing Features:** Premature greying of hair and scleral discoloration, even in the absence of other features.

Crucially as shown in the present case, the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticulate skin pigmentation) must not preemptively dismiss the diagnosis. The monocausal variant in *PARN* drives phenotypes distinct from autosomal recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita, often manifesting as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. Therefore, when young adult patients present with this constellation of failure without clear infectious, autoimmune, or environmental triggers, genetic evaluation via WES is the diagnostic standard.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How does the clinical presentation and prognosis differ between biallelic PARN variants (e.g., autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita) versus monoallelic PARN variants seen in this patient?

In this case, the distinction between biallelic and monoallelic *PARN* variants is fundamental to understanding the phenotypic spectrum of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD), as aptly illustrated in this series. Understanding this distinction is critical for avoiding misdiagnosis and ensuring appropriate genetic counseling.

The primary difference lies in the **dosage effect and resultant phenotype severity**. As noted in the case report, biallelic *PARN* variants typically cause **autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6)**. This presents with the classic mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, often accompanied by pediatric-onset bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS) and rapid malignant transformation. These patients generally have a poorer prognosis with earlier, systemic organ involvement.

In contrast, **monoallelic *PARN* variants** (as seen in our patient) more frequently underlie **telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4)**. The patient in this case (Soni et al.) presented in her late 20s, shortly before the loss of classic mucocutaneous features (like leukoplakia) would be expected in severe biallelic disease. She exhibited signs of premature aging—premature greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—but lacked the aggressive, rapidly progressive marrow failure typical of classic DKCB6 at her age of presentation. Her clinical picture was dominated by hepatopulmonary fibrosis and cytopenia, features that are often less severe initially but progress differently than biallelic disease.

**Pathophysiologic Basis and Management Principles**

The clinical reasoning here rests on the concept of **haploinsufficiency** in *PARN*. *PARN* is an exonuclease required for the 3′-end maturation of the TERC RNA component of telomerase. Because *PARN* is one of the 20% of genes least tolerant to common variants and particularly sensitive to deleterious combinations, loss of one allele (in monoallelic cases) or both (in biallelic cases) leads to accelerated telomere attrition.

For this patient, while the classic mucocutaneous triad was not present initially, the presence of cytopenia, fibrosis, and premature greying raised suspicion for a TBD despite the lack of family history (possibly explaining the variant as a de novo monoallelic event). The diagnostic standard remains NGS and fluorescent in situ hybridization for telomere length, which revealed the underlying mechanism despite the clinical snapshot being atypical.

**Prognostic Implications**

Prognosis differs significantly. Patients with biallelic *PARN* variants face a high risk of early mortality from severe aplastic anemia, liver cirrhosis, and malignancy, necessitating urgent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Conversely, patients with monoallelic *PARN* variants often have a more indolent course, as evidenced by the survival and improvement seen in our patient following anemia management and fibrosis monitoring. However, these patients remain at risk for progressive pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis and evolving marrow failure, requiring lifelong surveillance.

Therefore, for board purposes: When encountering TBD-like phenotypes, always evaluate for biallelic vs. monoallelic *PARN* mechanisms. While biallelic invariably causes classic dyskeratosis congenita, monoallelic mutations can present as isolated pulmonary fibrosis or adult-onset bm failure without the full mucocutaneous triad, making genetic testing essential in asymptomatic or mild domestic cases to prevent misdiagnosis as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or nutritional deficiency.

### [Management] In patients with Telomere Biology Disorders, what is the current role of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation regarding disease-modifying or curative effects in bone marrow failure, and how does the monoallelic nature of this variant influence that decision?

In patients with Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs), hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is currently the only potentially curative intervention for life-threatening hematologic manifestations, as it replaces telomere-depleted stem cells with healthy, reparative marrow. While the standard basis for transplant is unstable bone marrow failure, the specific genetic architecture of the disorder significantly influences the therapeutic decision-making process.

As described in the case report, the *PARN* gene demonstrates a distinct phenotype based on variant status: biallelic variants typically cause autosomal recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita, while monoallelic variants more often underlie telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). This distinction is clinically vital. Monoallelic mutations like the one identified in our patient (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) suggest a specific predisposition to "haploinsufficiency" involving pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis alongside marrow stress.

In our patient, the hematologic profile stabilized following the correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency and supportive management, rendering HSCT unnecessary at this specific juncture. However, the identification of the monoallelic *PARN* variant mandates that clinicians view the hematologic presentation as potentially latent. Even if the initial cytopenias are reversible, the underlying telomere biology defect predisposes the patient to progressive, non-reversible marrow failure and associated fibrotic organ damage. Therefore, the decision to pursue HSCT in patients with monoallelic TBDs is not made solely on the presence of a mutation, but on the evolution of true bone marrow insufficiency. The monolecistic nature serves as a marker for a fibrosis-rich syndrome, meaning transplant consultation must be weighed against the burden of extrahematologic complications, while maintaining a vigilant surveillance protocol for marrow decline to identify the serous window for a potentially curative outcome.

### [Pathophysiology] Why is hepatic fibrosis a frequent but often under-recognized manifestation in adult Telomere Biology Disorders, and what mechanism links telomere attrition in hepatocytes to liver stiffness?

### Clinical Teaching: Hepatic Fibrosis in Telomere Biology Disorders

**Question:** Why is hepatic fibrosis a frequent but often under-recognized manifestation in adult Telomere Biology Disorders, and what mechanism links telomere attrition in hepatocytes to liver stiffness?

**Answer:**

Hepatic fibrosis is frequently observed in adult Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) yet is often dismissed as a secondary liver disease or nutritional consequence. This occurs because TBDs frequently present without the classic mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita, such as nail dystrophy or leukoplakia, leading clinicians to default to autoimmune or acquired etiologies for fibrosis in the absence of these signs.

The pathophysiological link between telomere attrition and liver dysfunction is rooted in cellular replicative senescence. As stated in the discussion of this case, telomeres shield chromosome ends and shorten with each cell division, eventually triggering apoptosis or senescence. Actively dividing cells, including hepatocytes, are disproportionately affected. In our patient (Soni et al.), we identified a monoallelic *PARN* mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) within a context of multisystem decline. While monoallelic *PARN* variants classically associate with bone marrow failure and pulmonary fibrosis, the discussion notes that the impact on telomerase and gene expression may extend to hepatocytes, leading to the hepatic dysfunction we observed.

Quantifying this, our patient’s transient elastography revealed a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, consistent with cirrhosis. This physical manifestation aligns with frontier registry data cited in the case background, which noted 88.8% of adult patients with pathogenic *TERC* or *TERT* mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis. The mechanism linking attrition to stiffness involves the accumulation of senescent hepatocytes releasing pro-fibrotic cytokines, creating a microenvironment of chronic inflammation and fibrogenesis that is characteristic of the disease.

Consequently, clinical recognition should be prompted when "plausible" but unexplained fibrosis persists despite nutritional optimization. In this case, severe B12 deficiency was corrected, yet fibrosis and cytopenia remained, shifting suspicion toward TBD. Early hepaetic surveillance using elastography and genetic testing for variants such as *PARN* is essential to distinguish cryptic inherited disorders from common acquired causes.
