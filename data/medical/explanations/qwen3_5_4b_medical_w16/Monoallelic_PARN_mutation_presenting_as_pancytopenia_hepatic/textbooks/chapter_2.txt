Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 2: ## Telomere Biology Disorders: Pathophysiology and the PARN Mechanism

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a spectrum of rare, early-onset premature ageing phenotypes driven by defects in the maintenance of chromosomal termini. The fundamental pathophysiology rests on the biology of telomeres, those repetitive nucleotide sequences (TTAGGG in humans) capping the ends of linear chromosomes. These structures protect genomic integrity by shielding chromosomes from degradation and preventing end-to-end fusions. As eukaryotic cells divide, telomeres shorten; when they reach a critical minimum length, this cessation of cell division activates the senescence or apoptosis pathways. This phenomenon, known as the Hayflick limit, represents the finite replicative capacity of somatic cells. In mediating this process, the enzyme telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein complex comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—elaborates telomere length through elongation preceded by replication. In clinical practice, this understanding is vital for interpreting the progression of organ failure in the case presented; the patient's bone marrow failure, liver fibrosis, and lung changes reflect the cumulative effect of telomere attrition across rapidly dividing and metabolically active tissues.

Specific molecular dysregulation in this patient was identified within the *PARN* gene, which encodes a protein belonging to the highly conserved family of exoribonucleases. The core function of the PARN protein is the deadenylation of mRNA to shorten poly(A) tails, thereby regulating gene expression and stabilizing nuclear RNA degradation pathways. Crucially for telomere biology, this deadenylation process is essential for the 3′-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Deep sequencing suggests that PARN removes post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails that would otherwise render the TERC complex unstable or insufficient for optimal TERT activity. Pathogenic variants in *PARN* impair this RNA maturation, leading to incomplete TERC assembly. Since the TERT subunit requires a functional TERC RNA template to elongate telomeres, the disruption caused by *PARN* dysfunction results in a secondary failure of the telomerase complex. Consequently, while the primary defect is in the *PARN* protein, the downstream effect is a measurable reduction in functional telomerase activity and accelerated shortening of telomere length in affected tissues.

The clinical complexity of *PARN* disorders is often explained by the concept of haploinsufficiency. Germline pathogenic variants in *PARN* are recognized as causes of autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and telomere-related bone marrow failure and pulmonary fibrosis (PFBMFT4). However, the threshold for haploinsufficiency varies significantly among genes, with *PARN* being among the twenty most intolerant to loss-of-function variation. In the case presented, the patient carried a monoallelic missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This specific variant alters a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain, which is one of a five out of five in silico prediction tools as indicative of a damaging effect. Because many TBDs require bi-allelic mutations for clinical manifestation, monoallelic variants are typically expected to cause milder, condition-specific phenotypes such as pulmonary fibrosis or isolated bone marrow failure. However, *PARN* demonstrates high essentiality, supporting the underlying diagnosis even in the presence of a variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) initially.

When such haploinsufficiency occurs, it creates a field defect in rapidly dividing cells that disproportionately exceeds tissue tolerance limits. The hematopoietic stem cells, bone marrow, skin, and hepatocytes are the primary cell groups affected because they are actively dividing. The case highlights the interaction between telomere biology and other causes of marrow suppression; the patient presented with severe vitamin B12 deficiency, which caused ineffective erythropoiesis and raised suspicion for a primary nutritional cause. However, telomere shortening compromises the replicative potential of marrow precursors, rendering them intrinsically susceptible to stressors like severe B12 deficiency. This creates a "field defect" where the genetic predisposition magnifies the impact of secondary insults. In the context of the patient's fibrosis, the tropism for fibrosis-prone tissues requires an extended understanding of cellular senescence.

The impact on differentiated tissues, particularly the liver and lungs, necessitates differentiating constitutional fibrosis from acquired pathology. The patient presented with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis concomitant with osteoporosis and neuropathy. While chronic inflammation or autoimmune disease (e.g., autoimmune hepatitis) could mimic fibrosis, the absence of specific serologies and exclusion of infectious causes pointed toward a genetic etiology. The key differentiator lies in the persistence of structural accumulation and the lack of acclimatization to therapy. The patient improved symptomatically after correcting B12 deficiency, yet the deep structural changes in the lung and liver persisted on imaging (ground-glass haziness, stiffness). In acquired fibrosis, biomarkers such as liver stiffness and lung CT attenuation often fluctuate with anti-inflammatory treatment, whereas constitutional fibrosis in TBDs is often irreversible due to the irreversible accumulation of cellular senescence. This distinction is crucial for surveillance; in TBDs, the prognosis depends on the rate of progressional fibrosis and the risk of neoplasia, requiring longitudinal monitoring even as acquired metabolic conditions are treated separately. Understanding this mechanism allows clinicians to prioritize genetic testing in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease, even when classical mucocutaneous features are absent, ensuring early recognition for genetic counselling and appropriate surveillance of potential bone marrow transplantation indications.
