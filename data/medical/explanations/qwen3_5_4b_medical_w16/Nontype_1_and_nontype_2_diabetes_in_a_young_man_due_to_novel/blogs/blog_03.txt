Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Beyond the First Line: Why HNF1A-MODY Patients May Thrive on GLP-1 Agonists

We all know that diagnosing young-onset diabetes can feel like walking through a minefield. You have Type 1 Diabete sparing the immune system, and Type 2 with the metabolic obesity and insulin resistance. Then, you have the MODY types—monogenic forms that often look like T1D or T2D until you finish the genetic sequencing. This case report offers a perfect lookbook on why getting that diagnosis right changes the game entirely, specifically highlighting the potential pitfalls of standard "Type 2" insulin protocols and the safety and efficacy profile offered by GLP-1 receptor agonists in HNF1A-MODY.

## The Diagnostic Trap

Let's look at how this patient presented. A young adult walked into the clinic with a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2—so he wasn't the obese patient. Think T2D. Yet, his retrospective family history told a different story: early-onset diabetes in his maternal grandmother at 30s, mother in her late 20s, and brother at age 20. That autosomal dominant clustering is classic MODY.

However, his metabolic labs painted a picture that seasoned clinicians might default to Type 2 diabetes with advanced failure. He had an HbA1c of 10.2% and a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. He was polydipsic, polyphagic, and had lost weight. Crucially, he had glycosuria, which is often blamed on diabetes pickles or renal threshold issues in Type 2 or T1D. But his beta-cell function story was nuanced. A glucagon stimulation test showed a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L, which suggests preserved but insufficient function (typically <0.60 suggests low, >0.60 suggests preserved). Wait, that's weird. If it's 0.83, that's preserved? It's actually *above* the 0.60 cutoff. The key here is the context. He wasn't considered T1 (negative autoantibodies) but wasn't doing well enough on T2 management.

The clincher was the genetic testing. They tested for the HNF1A gene. It hitched a ride on next-generation sequencing (NGS) and found a novel heterozygous substitution p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G). This variant wasn't in ClinVar. This is why genetic screening is so crucial in young-onset diabetes—especially when population-based data can underestimate rates in non-European populations compared to typical guidelines.

## The Genetic Answer: Precision Matters

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor that regulates insulin production and function. When this goes wrong, the beta cells hang on, but they can't pump insulin effectively, leading to hyperglycemia. The presentation is often agonizing: high glucose, high glycosuria, and sometimes signs of autonomic neuropathy early on (like this patient's severely reduced sudomotor function).

Initially, he started on a "Type 2" regimen that mimics his clinical presentation: Metformin 1500 mg/day, Saxagliptin, and Basal Insulin Degludec. You'll note the 2021 US Medical Imaging guideline on Metformin as a separate point. Let's get back to the point. He had a 1 month follow-up HbA1c of 9.1%, which showed some improvement with insulin. But the renal thresholds, the beta-cell dysfunction markers, and the family history screamed "MODY." Replacing "standard" Type 2 management with the specific genetic diagnosis opens doors to specific therapies that are contraindicated in Type 2 but might fit MODY better.

Here's the clincher: sulfonylureas and insulin are often the default first-line therapy for new-onset diabetes. But in HNF1A-MODY, these drugs carry unique risks. While sulfonylureas stimulate insulin release, HNF1A mutations can lead to a progressive decline in beta-cell function that makes them perilous. This patient didn't get into a sulfonylurea trap, so he stayed on the glucose-lowering drugs, but insulin therapy was the initial driver.

## Precision Medicine Tactics: Insulin vs. GLP-1

The metabolic shift in the treatment plan is what makes this case report a gold standard for education. Once MODY was confirmed, the team switched off the insulin and brought on the GLP-1 receptor agonist: Dulaglutide. Why? GLP-1s have a proven safety profile for preserving beta-cell function, whereas insulin and sulfonylureas can accelerate functional decline in this specific genotype. The HNF1A pathway also plays a role in SGLT2 expression. This means that certain SGLT2 inhibitors might actually be risky for patients with HNF1A-MODY due to the risk of dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis (the text mentions a "decomposition of SGLT-2 activity" leading to potential complications).

The results speak for themselves. After starting Dulaglutide, the patient's HbA1c dropped from 9.1% down to 6.0% in just 6 months of GLP-1 support, alongside Metformin. This survival—stabilization without hypoglycemia, a better safety profile than sulfonylureas, and preservation of the beta cell function—is why this regimen is so appealing. The regimen was later simplified to Metformin plus Saxagliptin, but the proof was already in the books.

Many clinicians might have stuck the patient on insulin to reach the 6% target, but the MODY algorithm (insufficient beta-cell function) warns against over-masking the underlying disease without genetic confirmation. It's worth noting the patient also didn't have microalbuminuria or retinopathy, which suggests early detection and tight control mitigated complications.

## Key Takeaways

*   **MODY is a mimic:** Don't label a young patient with diabetes as T1 or T2 without ruling out MODY when family history is clustered.
*   **Genetic testing is diagnostic:** Use NGS panels in young patients with persistent hyperglycemia despite insulin stimulation or negative autoantibodies.
*   **HNF1A-MODY management is distinct:** GLP-1 receptor agonists are safer alternatives to sulfonylureas/insulin in preserving beta-cell function and avoiding hypoglycemia in this specific subtype.
*   **Avoid SGLT2 in HNF1A:** Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY due to the risk of DKA.
*   **Novel mutations matter:** Even if a variant isn't in ClinVar, functional family segregation and bioinformatic scores support pathogenicity.
