Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Call for Diversity: Why Non-White Populations Need Better Representation in MODY Algorithms

## The Sneaky Cousin in Targeted Therapy
Let's talk about a case that highlights how rampant diagnostic reliance on Eurocentric metrics can be. We have a young Asian adult who presents with classic hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%) and significant weight loss. He has family history across the maternal line—mate grandmother at 30s, mom at late 20s, brother at 20. But his initial workup had a major red flag: a novel mutation in the *HNF1A* gene.

The interesting part isn't the mutation itself (there are p.N270 variants, though he had a novel c.809A>G substitution not yet in ClinVar); it's the journey of management. Standard workup ruled out Type 1 (negative IgGADA, IA-2A). Yet, the clinical suspicion for Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) was high because of his BMI and metabolic profile (triglycerides 4.31 mmol/L). He was initially loaded with insulin (degudec), metformin, and saxagliptin. Then came the switch. Once the genetic test confirmed HNF1A-MODY, insulin was dropped, and a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) was introduced. Result: HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% in half a year.

But the headline isn't the drug switch; it's the mathematical guesswork that preceded the genetic lab confirmation. A common tool for assessing MODY likelihood, the “Exeter Calculator,” assigned this patient a 62.4% probability. That sounds like a solid lead, right? But the case report notes that this tool was derived from White European populations and *may overestimate the probability of MODY in non-White groups*. In an Asian population where HNF1A mutations are prevalent but under-studied, relying on a Euro-centric probability algorithm without curiosity can be dangerous.

## The Calculator is Not a Crystal Ball
In clinical education, we teach that algorithms should help, not hinder. But the limitations of these tools are glaring. The calculator gave a *62% MODY probability* based on family history and phenotypic data. The tool was built on data that doesn't represent his underlying genetics.

This touches on a systemic issue. Most genetic databases like ClinVar are Eurocentric. When a patient has a novel variant like the one in this report (p.N270S), it sits unreported. It meets ACMG criteria based on AlphaMissense scores and family segregation, but it won't light up in your standard search.

The clinical pearl here is that a quantitative risk score of 62.4% should never be the final word, especially when the patient's ancestry suggests deviations from Eurocentric prevalence rates. We are seeing higher MODY rates in Asian populations, but the algorithms are lagging. This isn't just about the calculation; it's about the treatment pathway. A patient misdiagnosed as T2D receives insulin. A patient misdiagnosed as Type 1 (if we aren't careful) gets immunosuppression or anti-glutamate drugs. A HNF1A patient gets managed to a specific trajectory where potentially dangerous sulfonylureas might be avoided or where hypoglycemia risk is managed via GLP-1s. Often, the wrong drug is more harmful than the algorithmic error.

## Precision Medicine Requires Inclusion
The bottom line is precise phenotyping. The patient had a glucagon stimulation test with low C-peptide (0.83 nmol/L) and sudomotor testing showing severe autonomic dysfunction. These are valuable. The BLUE spectrum of diabetes is being ignored because we are focused on Euro-centric best practices.

However, this case report notes that SGLT2 inhibitors are risky in HNF1A-MODY due to SGLT2 expression modulation by HNF1A itself. Using them can lead to dehydration and DKA in MODY patients. This specific drug-genic interaction is hard-coded into current forms of medicine because it requires understanding the subtleness of the genotype.

We need more diversity. The Exeter tool averaged a non-White-ability of MODY prevalence, which included failure to identify the cohort. In this case, the final diagnosis was 100% confirmed by NGS. But it took a year of follow-up to confirm the lack of diabetic complications compared to the T2D trajectory. This delay is the cost of our current diagnostic baggage.

## Take-Home Messages
*   **Treat the Genetics, Not Just the HbA1c:** When HbA1c is elevated and family history fits MODY, the Eurocentric probability tools may fail in non-White populations; confirm with NGS if suspicion remains high.
*   **Avoid SGLT2 Avoidance:** Do not prescribe SGLT2 inhibitors to patients with HNF1A mutations without specific precaution, as these can precipitate DKA due to reduced SGLT2 in renal tubules.
*   **GLP-1 is Often Safer than Insulin:** For HNF1A-MODY, transitioning from insulin-based regimens to GLP-1 receptor agonists often provides better beta-cell preservation and lower hypoglycemia risk.
*   **Check Sudomotor Function:** Autonomic dysfunction can be a subtle sign of MODY, particularly in the younger demographic.
*   **Never Trust the Calculator Alone:** Always prioritize direct genetic sequencing over population-based likelihood calculators in diverse ethnical groups with unknown variants.
