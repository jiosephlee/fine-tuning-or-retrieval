Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Precision in the Genomic Era: Interpreting Novel HNF1A Variants Without ClinVar Match

## The Diagnostic Blind Spot

We recently encountered a clinical vignette that shines a light on the limitations of modern genomic databases in real-world diagnosis. In a young adult male, the clinical presentation initially screamed "Type 2 Diabetes" with a historical HbA1c of 10.2%, BMI of 24.6, and HNF1A mutation suspicion. However, the family history told a completely different story: the maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the patient’s brother at age 20. Standard Type 2 algorithms would have ignored the strong autosomal dominant inheritance pattern in favor of lifestyle factors. More importantly, the initial trial of basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin failed to account for the underlying monogenic defect until genetic confirmation was retrieved later in the process.

This case serves as a stark reminder that a negative ClinVar match does not equate to a negative result. The patient harbored a novel heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene. Despite this variant being absent from major databases like ClinVar and VarSome, ACMG guidelines and multiple bioinformatic lines of evidence pointed toward pathogenicity. The critical error that occurred in practice isn't the lack of data, but the premature dismissal of genetic testing in the face of atypical T2D-related features like autonomic dysfunction (severely reduced sudomotor function) and glycosuria in a non-obese patient.

## Decoding a "Unknown" Variant

When we look out for these "unknowns," we are often dealing with Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). However, precision medicine is shifting the paradigm. In this specific case, the pathogenicity of the p.N270S variant was supported by several independent lines of evidence despite the lack of prior reports:

*   **Structural Context:** The amino acid residue is adjacent to known pathogenic residues in the DNA-binding domain.
*   **Bioinformatics Scores:** The AlphaMissense score was exceptionally high at 0.995, suggesting a high probability of dysfunction.
*   **Segregation:** The variant segregated with diabetes in the family, confirming autosomal dominant transmission.
*   **Clinical Phenotype:** The presence of HNF1A-MODY symptoms (early onset, negative autoantibodies, insulin requirement) correlates perfectly with a known pathogenic class.

This case teaches us that we must rely on integration of clinical bio-data rather than solely looking up variants. A lack of historical precedent is a false negative, not a definitive rule. If a patient presents with HNF1A-MODY phenotype and carries a structural VUS with a high functional score, treat as pathogenic until proven otherwise.

## Treatment Implications for MODY

The diagnosis was a game-changer for this patient's management. The clinical progression from initial glycemic control to genetic confirmation directly influenced the therapy. The patient's HbA1c dropped significantly from 10.2% to 6.0% after the regimen shifted.

Crucially, the HNF1A diagnosis altered the medication protocol. Insulin therapy, initially started due to hyperglycemia and negative C-peptide stimulation (0.39 nmol/L at 0 min), was discontinued and replaced with a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide). This shift was effective in reducing HbA1c while sparing residual beta-cell function.

However, there are direct contraindications to note. The discussion section highlights a specific risk profile for HNF1A-MODY regarding SGLT-2 inhibitors. While often used in T2D, there is evidence suggesting that HNF1A-MODY patients may experience severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to high inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity or altered renal thresholds. In this case, the treatment regimen was simplified to metformin and saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor) for long-term maintenance, avoiding the exogenous glucose drag of sulfonylureas and the renal stress of SGLT2 inhibition associated with this specific monogenic defect.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Don't Rule Out MODY early:** A young patient with hyperglycemia, negative autoantibodies, strong family history, and autonomic dysfunction should warrant genetic testing for MODY regardless of BMI.
*   **ACMG Guidelines Override Database Gaps:** A novel variant (e.g., p.N270S) can be classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic based on segregation data, structural domain analysis, and bioinformatic scores even if it is not in ClinVar.
*   **Selection of Agents Matters:** HNF1A-MODY benefits from GLP-1 receptor agonism over sulfonylureas (due to hypoglycemia risk) and requires caution with SGLT-2 inhibitors due to DKA/dehydration risk.
*   **Precision Medicine Saves Insulin:** Accurate genetic diagnosis facilitates the transition off insulin, improving long-term glycaemic targets (HbA1c) and reducing early complications without the catabolic weight loss associated with T2DM.
