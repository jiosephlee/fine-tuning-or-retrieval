Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The SGLT2 Paradox: Why SGLT2 Inhibitors Are a Risk in HNF1A-MODY

Case complexity often lies in the overlap between presentation and physiology. Consider a 20-something male presenting with hyperglycemia, glycosuria, and weight loss. He looks classic for type 2, yet his BMI is only 24.6 kg/m², and he has no acanthosis nigricans. His family history is the real tell: his mother, grandmother, and younger brother all had early-onset diabetes. On the surface, you might prescribe metformin or insulin and treat for type 2 diabetes (T2D). However, if you dig deeper, the metabolic stability is precarious. In this case report, a young man with this exact profile was eventually diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY due to a novel heterozygous mutation (p.N270S). The educational opportunity isn't just in the genetic variant; it is in subsequent management missteps that could have been caught.

While the patient in the case report was successfully managed with metformin and a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide), one specific pharmacologic class poses a genuine—and sometimes paradoxical—danger in HNF1A-MODY that we need to flag firmly: SGLT2 inhibitors. The guideline standard for many years was to treat type 2 diabetes with broad classes including SGLT2 inhibitors, которых (in Georgian context) for traditional diabetes management. In the context of HNF1A-MODY, the underlying pathophysiology often renders these agents contraindicated.

The gene *HNF1A* encodes a transcription factor critical for pancreatic beta-cell function, but it also regulates the expression of the SGLT2 transporter in the kidney. In many cases of HNF1A-MODY, the mutation reduces SGLT2 expression, leading to early spontaneous glycosuria even before significant hyperglycemia develops. While this glycosuria is a hallmark of the disease, adding an SGLT2 inhibitor on top of an already compromised renal glucose reabsorption threshold can be catastrophic. The case report discussion notes that high inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity in this population may increase the risk of severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis. Because these patients often have reduced beta-cell reserve, they cannot compensate for the osmotic shifts driven by the inhibitor, pushing them toward euglycemic DKA or severe dehydration faster than in classic T2D.

Furthermore, the distinction between HNF1A-MODY and T2D becomes blurred as age advances and BMI increases. In the case described, the patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, not obese, yet his HbA1c was 10.2%. If you miss the genetic diagnosis and assume T2D, you might lean towards an SGLT2 inhibitor to gain neoteric weight. This ignores the risk of fungal infections (common in SGLT2-induced glycosuria) and the specific danger of DKA in monogenic diabetes. The patient in the report improved his HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% on GLP-1 + Metformin, proving that targeting beta-cell stimulation (or preservation) via GLP-1 agents can be superior to agents that stress renal glucose handling.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Genetics is safety:** In young-onset diabetes with a discordant BMI or negative PPD (post-prandial post) findings, check for MODY, especially with strong family history.
*   **SGLT2 caution in HNF1A:** The existing low SGLT2 expression in HNF1A-MODY creates a vulnerability to DKA and dehydration when inhibiting SGLT2 further. Avoid these agents unless closely monitored, given the risk of vascular collapse.
*   **GLP-1 is the choice:** GLP-1 receptor agonists support β-cell function and lower HbA1c safely in HNF1A-MODY without the renal dehydration risk of SGLT2 inhibitors.
*   **Antibodies are not enough:** Negative GADA or Ia-2A does not rule out MODY; always confirm with genetic sequencing or functional C-peptide analysis in discordant T2D presents.
*   **Novel mutations matter:** Clinching a diagnosis with ACMG guidelines on new variants (e.g., p.N270S) is as important as the gene name itself for treatment planning.
