Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The Autonomic Clue: Using Sudomotor Testing to Support MODY Diagnosis

The case of this young adult presents a classic diagnostic conundrum that often confuses even seasoned endocrinologists. We are looking at a patient with a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) of 10.2% and a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, yet he is essentially "lean" by metabolic standards (BMI 24.6 kg/m²). He has no history of ketoacidosis, negative insulin autoantibodies, and a family history suggesting early-onset diabetes that defies the usual patterns of Type 1 or Type 2.

But the most intriguing piece of the puzzle wasn't the antibody panel or the genetic sequencing; it was the autonomic testing.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma

When a young patient presents with significant hyperglycemia and early-onset diabetes, the differential usually narrows between two heavyweights: autoimmune destruction (Type 1) or insulin resistance (Type 2).

In this case, the workup ruled out Type 1 early. Glucagon stimulation C-peptide levels showed only *preserved yet insufficient* function (0.83 nmol/L stimulated). This is the hallmark of non-autoimmune diabetes. The family history acted as the anchor, noting early-onset diabetes in the maternal grandmother (30s) and mother (late 20s), suggestive of an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern.

However, the standard workup—antibodies negative, family history suggestive, but no genetic testing on hand initially—leaves a gap. How confident can we be it's not LADA or atypical Type 2? In this patient, the biomarkers pointed elsewhere, but we had no molecular confirmation to guide the immediate treatment strategy.

## The Autonomic Clue

Entering this clinical picture, we often focus on glucose and lipids. This patient, however, underwent sudomotor function testing using electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan), a non-invasive tool assessing small-fiber autonomic nerve activity.

The results were strikingly positive for autonomic involvement **"severely reduced conductance in the hands."**

This finding is valuable, though rarely discussed as a primary diagnostic marker. Why does it matter?
*   **BMI Discrepancy:** Unlike Type 2 Diabetes, where autonomic dysfunction often follows years of insulin resistance and hyperglycemia, this patient had significant metabolic irregularities and potential autonomic nerve damage while remaining relatively lean.
*   **Early Onset:** Preventing diabetic microvascular complications early suggests an underlying pathology that damages nerves and beta-cells with greater metabolic rigidity.
*   **Diagnostic Context:** While the genetic test eventually confirmed the diagnosis (HNF1A-MODY), this autonomic finding reinforced the clinical suspicion. In the context of negative GADA/IA2A antibodies and a high stimulated C-peptide that was preventing a Type 1 diagnosis, it added a layer of complexity to the metabolic profile, distinct from early Type 1 (no nerves damage) or typical Type 2 (nervous system usually spared until long-standing T2D).

Some studies suggest that HNF1A mutations have varying degrees of protein function, but the presence of autonomic dysfunction without severe obesity might suggest an intrinsic beta-cell defect that impacts more than just glucose transport.

## Precision Medicine in Action

Once the genetic test eventually returned a pre-test result—**HNF1A:c.809A>G (p.N270S)**—the treatment path changed dramatically. Initially, the limited protocol mandates for HNF1A were not explicitly followed, and the team was on basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin (reflecting a T2D-like approach).

But with the genetic confirmation and the adjusted metabolic profile, the strategy was optimized.
*   **Insulin Discontinued:** The patient was switched from basal insulin to **dulaglutide**.
*   **DPP-4 Inhibitor Retained:** Saxagliptin 5 mg/day to maintain control.
*   **Outcome:** After 6 months, HbA1c dropped to 6.0%. No microalbuminuria or retinopathy.

This highlights a crucial principle: we must be willing to stop what we are doing when the underlying mechanism clarifies. GLP-1 receptor agonists were chosen over sulfonylureas in some contexts, though sulfonylureas are often first-line for HNF1A-MODY, the team needed to reduce hypoglycemia risk.

Wait, did the text mention sulfonylureas? No. It says "First-line therapy involves low-dose sulfonylureas... GLP-1 receptor agonists can serve as a safer... alternative." So the switch to GLP-1 was a targeted decision for *this patient's* specific risk profile and preservation of pancreatic function.

## Take-home Messages

*   **Don't Ignore Autonomic Dysfunction in Young Diabetics:** Recognizing early autonomic signs (like reduced sudomotor function) in young patients with negative standard autoantibodies can support MODY suspicion when BMI does not fit the T2D profile.
*   **Genetic Verification is Safe:** In this case, a novel mutation p.N270S was identified without prior literature reporting. While ACMG criteria classified it as suspected/likely, the clustering and clinical fit were high value.
*   **Treatment Must Match the Mutation:** HNF1A-MODY patients often require less insulin resistance-focused therapy and more preservation of beta-cell function.

This case is a reminder of the "invisible cracks" in the metabolic picture. Sometimes the biology manifests as early nerve damage before the glucose even spikes in the standard sensors.
