Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Pathophysiology] How does a heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene transcription factor mechanistically lead to the progressive decline in pancreatic beta-cell function and glucose tolerance in HNF1A-MODY?

Based on the mechanism described in this case report, a heterozygous mutation in the *HNF1A* gene disrupts the transcriptional regulation of key pancreatic and hepatic genes, leading to an intrinsic defect in beta-cell physiology.

HNF1A acts as a critical transcription factor for the maintenance of beta-cell function. As detailed in this specific case, the patient possesses a novel heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G) located in the DNA-binding domain. This mutation impairs the transcriptional control of target genes including *INS* (insulin) and *SLC2A2* (GLUT2). Consequently, there is reduced expression of insulin and glucose transporters, directly causing decreased insulin secretion and glucose intolerance as observed in the patient’s hyperglycemia and elevated postprandial glucose.

The dysregulation extends beyond the beta-cell. As noted in the discussion regarding the patient’s glycosuria, HNF1A transcriptional control also affects renal proteins; specifically, reduced expression of SGLT2 (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2) is associated with this phenotype. This molecular explanation underscores why these patients may present with glycosuria without significant thirst or polyuria initially.

Clinically, this mechanism results in a progressive decline in beta-cell function rather than the abrupt destruction seen in Type 1 diabetes or the fixed genetic defect of glucose sensing seen in Glucokinase-MODY. The patient in this report demonstrated preserved but insufficient secretory capacity, evidenced by a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L on glucagon stimulation test, which declined over time as the transcriptional defect persisted. This distinguishes HNF1A-MODY from autoimmune diabetes, explaining the negative autoantibody status (GADA, IA-2A) observed in this case.

Precise genetic diagnosis, as performed using next-generation sequencing (NGS) per ACMG guidelines in this report, is therefore essential to distinguish this mechanism from Type 2 or autoimmune diabetes. Recognizing the transcriptional defect allows clinicians to anticipate a progressive loss of beta-cell reserve and tailor therapy—such as switching from insulin to GLP-1 receptor agonists in this patient—to preserve residual function while avoiding the hypoglycemia risk associated with sulfonylureas.
