Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Pathophysiology and Biology of HNF1A-MODY

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a group of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes disorders characterized by a primary defect in the development and function of pancreatic β-cells, typically presenting before age 25. This genetic etiology explains the strong family history observed in the case report, where the patient, his mother, maternal grandmother, and younger brother were all diagnosed with early-onset diabetes. Unlike type 1 diabetes, which is defined by autoimmunity, or type 2 diabetes by insulin resistance and compromised beta-cell mass, MODY is a single-gene disorder with an autosomal dominant transmission pattern. The initial clinical dilemma in this patient involved distinguishing this condition from type 1 or type 2 diabetes, as the phenotypic presentation included features like fasting hyperglycemia, which can overlap with both. However, the absence of type 1 diabetic-associated autoantibodies, specifically glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A), alongside the strong presence of autosomal dominant family history, prompted a genetic diagnostic approach.

The hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (*HNF1A*) gene is located on the long arm of chromosome 12 (12q24) and encodes a transcription factor consisting of 10 exons and a 631-amino acid protein that regulates gene expression in the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. In HNF1A-MODY, mutations impair the transcription factor's ability to bind DNA and activate target genes necessary for beta-cell function. In the case described, genetic testing identified a novel heterozygous pathogenic variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4. This specific mutation resides in the DNA-binding domain of the protein and is believed to disrupt the protein's structural integrity. Biologically, this defect leads to the dysregulation of downstream genes such as insulin and GLUT2. The resulting decrease in insulin gene expression and GLUT2 protein availability compromises the beta-cell's ability to sense glucose and secrete appropriate amounts, directly correlating with the significant postprandial glucose intolerance seen in the patient, including an OGTT value of 19.8 mmol/L.

Preserved yet insufficient beta-cell function is a hallmark of HNF1A-MODY, supported by the specific biochemical profiles seen in this patient. While type 1 diabetes is characterized by absolute insulin deficiency, HNF1A-MODY typically allows for some baseline endogenous production. The glucagon stimulation test performed on the patient revealed a circulating C-peptide level of 0.39 nmol/L at 0 minutes and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, indicating stimulated secretion that falls below the threshold for preserved function (>0.60 nmol/L) but confirms the diagnosis is not type 1. This profile explains the discrepancy in initial management plans; although the patient was started on basal insulin with normal C-peptide levels suggest beta-cell reserve, the eventual transition to glucose-incretin therapy highlights that residual function is frequently insufficient to maintain glycaemic control long-term without pharmaceutical support.

The unique pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY also significantly impacts renal glucose handling, frequently resulting in glycosuria at blood glucose levels that are not typically associated with such findings in other diabetes types. HNF1A functions as a transcriptional activator of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the proximal renal tubules, driving glucose reabsorption. Impairment of this transcriptional regulation leads to reduced SGLT2 expression, thereby lowering the renal threshold for glucose and causing glycosuria as observed in the patient's presentation. This mechanism has direct therapeutic implications, as SGLT2 inhibitors, which block renal glucose reabsorption, are often effective in type 2 diabetes but pose higher risks in HNF1A-MODY. In patients with MODY, the renal defect is intrinsic, and inhibiting SGLT2 function further may precipitate severe dehydration, diabetic ketoacidosis, and euglycemic hyperosmolar states. Consequently, the treatment strategy for this patient was optimized to avoid this class of medication, favoring a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) and metformin combination, which resulted in significant improvement of HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% over six months.

## Clinical Recognition and Differential Diagnosis

The recognition of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) in a clinical setting begins with identifying diagnostic thresholds that distinguish monogenic diabetes from more common adult-onset forms, such as Type 1 or Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus. In the case report, the patient is a young adult presenting with hyperglycemia and glycosuria, which clinically overlaps significantly with these common etiologies. A critical starting point for the differential diagnosis is the interpretation of family history. This patient presents with a strong family history of early-onset diabetes characterized by vertical transmission: the maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother in his 20s. This inheritance pattern, where multiple generations are affected and the like-for-like transmission suggests an autosomal dominant trait, serves as a profound marker for monogenic insulin分泌 defects. Furthermore, the patient's anthropometric data, including a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and lack of acanthosis nigricans, initially point away from the typical severe obesity phenotype often associated with Type 2 Diabetes, even though lipid profiles suggested early metabolic disturbances. This discrepancy between BMI and presentation emphasizes the diagnostic dilemma where genetic forms of diabetes can infiltrate the clinical spectrum mimicking Type 2, particularly as the prevalence of obesity rises globally.

Once clinical suspicion for a genetic disorder is raised by the family history, the diagnosis relies heavily on ruling out Autoimmune diabetes (Type 1). The standard clinical approach involves a comprehensive panel of autoantibodies, specifically the glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) antibodies. These immunological markers are pivotal because their presence establishes a diagnosis of Type 1 Diabetes, highlighting a primary defect in immune destruction of pancreatic beta-cells. In the present case, both GADA and islet antigen 2 antibodies were negative, which significantly lowers the probability of Type 1 Diabetes despite the clinical presentation of weight loss and polydipsia. The negative status of these antibodies is a cornerstone of the diagnostic algorithm for this report; it effectively excludes the autoimmune mechanism, forcing the clinician to look for alternative explanations such as genetic defects. Therefore, the clinical reasoning shifts from searching for disease causing immunity to searching for gene defects once autoimmunity is ruled out by these specific markers.

Differentiation between autoimmune, Type 2, and MODY forms is often complicated by residual beta-cell function testing. In the case report, a pivotal diagnostic tool was the glucagon stimulation test, which evaluated the patient's endogenous insulin secretion capability. At baseline, the serum C-peptide level was 0.39 nmol/L, indicating low insulin output, while the 6-minute post-stimulation level rose to 0.83 nmol/L. This test is used to distinguish Type 1, where C-peptide levels are typically near zero regardless of stimulation (absolute insulin deficiency), from Type 2 or MODY, where insulin secretion is prodromal or defective but present. The stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L suggests that the patient possesses residual beta-cell function, whereas Type 1 diabetes would likely exhibit failed secretion (<0.60 nmol/L). However, this residual function is insufficient for normal glucose homeostasis. This specific finding supports the diagnostic dilemma, demonstrating that the beta-cells are not fully destroyed (as in Type 1) but are compromised (as in MODY), necessitating further investigation into genetic causes before finalizing the diagnosis.

The final layer of clinical recognition involves analyzing the full spectrum of symptoms and their relationship with modern metabolic markers. Patients with MODY, particularly HNF1A-MODY, may present with classic polydipsia, polyuria, and polyphagia, leading to misdiagnoses of either Type 1 or Type 2 based on these metabolic signs alone. However, the patient's clinical picture in this specific case was complicated by autonomic dysfunction, evidenced by severely reduced sudomotor function over the hands on electrochemical skin conductance testing (Sudoscan). While diabetic autonomic neuropathy is usually a long-term complication observed in Type 1 and Type 2 over years, its presence in a newly diagnosed young adult suggests early exertion-related dysregulation or inherent genetic susceptibility to tissue hypoperfusion. Additionally, the finding of dyslipidemia (HDL 1.01 mmol/L, triglycerides 4.31 mmol/L) suggested possible metabolic syndrome, a condition often used to classify Type 2 Diabetes. This highlights a diagnostic nuance: the classic distinction between a lean Type 1 patient and an obese Type 2 patient is blurring, and distinct metabolic risks must be considered. The combination of negative autoantibodies, preserved yet insufficient C-peptide secretion, early familial onset, and objective signs of autonomic dysfunction collectively points toward a hereditary etiology rather than an environmental or autoimmune one, validating the subsequent decision for genetic screening.

## Genetic Confirmation and Variant Interpretation

The decision to proceed with genetic testing in this case was driven by a combination of clinical phenotypes that did not fit the classic profiles of type 1 or type 2 diabetes, alongside the patient’s strong family history. During the diagnostic workup, while the patient presented with symptoms such as polyuria, polydipsia, and postprandial hyperglycemia, the presence of negative pan-Autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A, ZnT8) strongly suggested a non-autoimmune etiology. Furthermore, the glucagon stimulation test indicated insufficient insulin secretion despite preserved fasting C-peptide levels (0.83 nmol/L), distinguishing the physiology from absolute insulin deficiency associated with type 1 diabetes. While a diabetes probability calculator often assists in identifying MODY candidates, clinicians must remain aware that these tools are derived primarily from White European populations and may overestimate MODY probability in non-White groups. In this manuscript’s context, the patient’s ethnicity and family history (maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, brother at age 20) elevated the pre-test probability, making a targeted genetic panel for monogenic diabetes critically accessible and necessary. This step moves the diagnosis from empirical clinical reasoning to a precision medicine framework.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels represent a primary diagnostic utility for monogenic diabetes, particularly when next-generation sequencing identifies coding variants. Unlike standard blood draws, NGS analyzes specific regions of the genome simultaneously to identify pathogenic mutations that account for clinical symptoms. In this case report, a specific NGS panel was utilized to screen for over 15 known MODY-related genes, with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) being the focus of the investigation. The sequencing revealed a heterozygous substitution at codon N270, resulting in the amino acid exchange of asparagine to serine, denoted as p.N270S. This specific variant corresponds to the nucleotide change c.809A>G in Exon 4 of the HNF1A gene. Importantly, this finding was novel; it had not been previously reported in the Latin and VarSome databases nor in ClinVar, highlighting the constant challenge rare genetic research faces, where thousands of distinct variants exist, many of which remain unidentified in public repositories.

Interpreting such a novel variant requires a rigorous application of the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. Standard operating procedures generally classify variants as benign, likely benign, pathogenic, or pathogenic-likely based on functional data, segregation, and database patterns. For this p.N270S mutation, the classification as a likely pathogenic (LP') variant was supported by multiple independent lines of evidence. First, the variant localized to the DNA-binding domain of the protein, a region known to be functional when disrupted. Second, bioinformatic tools indicated a high likelihood of being deleterious, with the AlphaMissense score projecting at 0.995. Third and most significantly, segregation analysis confirmed the variant's inheritance within the family. The patient’s mother and younger brother also carried the mutation, and the affected status of the grandmother aligned with the dominant transmission pattern, ensuring that the genetic data was not a sporadic mutation unrelated to the diabetes phenotype. Because the summary of reports showed that hundreds of different HNF1A mutations can cause disease, and this specific change was absent from previous literature, the convergence of in vitro prediction, biochemical output, and familial inheritance allowed for the validation of pathogenicity without relying solely on prior case literature.

This accurate genetic interpretation directly influenced the management strategy, ensuring that the patient received the correct therapeutic approach. Initially, treatment prioritized metformin and insulin to manage acute hyperglycemia; however, upon confirming the HNF1A mutation, the management strategy was refined to preserve pancreatic function. Genetic confirmation confirmed that sulfonylureas (which mimic insulin secretion mechanisms) might be effective but that sulfonylureas carry a risk of hypoglycemia, particularly if C-peptide function declines further. Therefore, the team transitioned the patient from insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which provided superior glycaemic control with minimal hypoglycemia, supported by evidence that GLP-1 analogs are superior in MODY due to their action on beta-cell function and reduced risk of weight gain compared to insulin. Furthermore, the identification of this specific variant confirmed the utility of genetic testing in clinical settings, as the patient received therapy that optimized blood glucose levels to 6.0% within three months, preventing long-term complications such as microalbuminuria and renal failure. Ultimately, this case demonstrates that distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from common diabetes forms not only clarifies the prognosis but also guides treatment toward safer, efficacy-focused regimens.

## Long-term Outcomes and Clinical Implications

The therapeutic trajectory observed in this case underscores the critical necessity of aligning diabetes management with underlying pathophysiology rather than adhering to empiric algorithms. Initially, the patient was treated with insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin. This regimen reflected a standard approach to hyperglycemia in a young adult presenting with significant elevation in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), effectively mimicking a Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) presentation given the absence of autoimmune markers. However, the use of basal insulin alone in a young patient is often a curative strategy for Type 1 diabetes or an aggressive insulin failure; in this context, it obscured the patient's preserved yet insufficient endogenous beta-cell reserve. Relying on basal insulin can be a pitfall because it treats the symptom (hyperglycemia) without addressing the mechanism (modulated insulin secretion potential and sensitivity). Switching from insulin to insulin secretagogues or glucose-dependent agents is often the sustainable long-term goal for preservation of beta-cell mass in monogenic forms, a concept missed in the initial T2D-treatment phase.

Following genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, the treatment paradigm shifted away from high-dose insulin dependency toward agents that spare beta-cell function. The selection of a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, specifically dulaglutide, follows specific pathophysiological reasoning for HNF1A-MODY. Because HNF1A mutations lead to a progressive decline in beta-cell function, therapies that rely heavily on high glucose-dependent insulin secretion or suppress glucagon release are preferred to prevent beta-cell exhaustion. The case highlights that while sulfonylureas (e.g., glimepiride) are often first-line for HNF1A-MODY in younger patients, they carry a significant risk of hypoglycemia, especially if residual insulin function is suboptimal. In contrast, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer metabolic profile, reducing hypoglycemia risk while stimulating postprandial glucose control. The patient’s HbA1c improvement from 9.1% to 6.0% shortly after starting dulaglutide confirms that GLP-1 therapy is effective as a first-line agent that balances metabolic control with physiological safety in this genetically defined cohort.

Treatment selection in HNF1A-MODY also necessitates specific negative selection of anticoagulant therapy options due to underlying genetic transcription effects. A crucial aspect of this case is the deliberate avoidance of Sodium-Glucose Linked Transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. The HNF1A gene regulates the transcription of SGLT2 in the renal tubules; patients with HNF1A-MODY already exhibit reduced baseline SGLT2 expression, meaning their renal glucose threshold is naturally lower. Consequently, exogenous pharmacological inhibition of SGLT2 in these patients can precipitate dehydration, volume depletion, and an increased risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). The case treats this as a contraindication, prioritizing safety over glycemic reduction efficacy. Instead, fixed-dose combinations like metformin pairings with DPP-4 inhibitors, such as saxagliptin, were deemed appropriate. This choice avoids the renal and neurological risks associated with SGLT2 inhibition while simplifying the regimen for maintenance.

The long-term outcomes in this case demonstrate that genotype-driven precision medicine can prevent complications while reducing drug burden. After 3 months of intensive treatment, the patient's HbA1c normalized to 6.0%. The decision to simplify the regimen to metformin and saxagliptin for long-term maintenance illustrates a strategic step-down approach. Once glycaemic control is stable, removing basal insulin avoids the risks associated with long-term insulin therapy, such as weight gain and hypoglycemic unawareness. The absence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy during the 6-month to multi-year follow-up further validates the efficacy of this tailored stabilization. This success reinforces that genetic screening in early-onset diabetes is not merely for research but for practical clinical management; accurate diagnosis allows for the avoidance of potentially harmful insulin doses, prevents the use of contraindicated anti-hyperglycemic agents, and facilitates the most cost-effective maintenance regimen possible for the individual patient.
