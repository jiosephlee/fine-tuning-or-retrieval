Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 3: ## Genetic Confirmation and Variant Interpretation

The decision to proceed with genetic testing in this case was driven by a combination of clinical phenotypes that did not fit the classic profiles of type 1 or type 2 diabetes, alongside the patient’s strong family history. During the diagnostic workup, while the patient presented with symptoms such as polyuria, polydipsia, and postprandial hyperglycemia, the presence of negative pan-Autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A, ZnT8) strongly suggested a non-autoimmune etiology. Furthermore, the glucagon stimulation test indicated insufficient insulin secretion despite preserved fasting C-peptide levels (0.83 nmol/L), distinguishing the physiology from absolute insulin deficiency associated with type 1 diabetes. While a diabetes probability calculator often assists in identifying MODY candidates, clinicians must remain aware that these tools are derived primarily from White European populations and may overestimate MODY probability in non-White groups. In this manuscript’s context, the patient’s ethnicity and family history (maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, brother at age 20) elevated the pre-test probability, making a targeted genetic panel for monogenic diabetes critically accessible and necessary. This step moves the diagnosis from empirical clinical reasoning to a precision medicine framework.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels represent a primary diagnostic utility for monogenic diabetes, particularly when next-generation sequencing identifies coding variants. Unlike standard blood draws, NGS analyzes specific regions of the genome simultaneously to identify pathogenic mutations that account for clinical symptoms. In this case report, a specific NGS panel was utilized to screen for over 15 known MODY-related genes, with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) being the focus of the investigation. The sequencing revealed a heterozygous substitution at codon N270, resulting in the amino acid exchange of asparagine to serine, denoted as p.N270S. This specific variant corresponds to the nucleotide change c.809A>G in Exon 4 of the HNF1A gene. Importantly, this finding was novel; it had not been previously reported in the Latin and VarSome databases nor in ClinVar, highlighting the constant challenge rare genetic research faces, where thousands of distinct variants exist, many of which remain unidentified in public repositories.

Interpreting such a novel variant requires a rigorous application of the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. Standard operating procedures generally classify variants as benign, likely benign, pathogenic, or pathogenic-likely based on functional data, segregation, and database patterns. For this p.N270S mutation, the classification as a likely pathogenic (LP') variant was supported by multiple independent lines of evidence. First, the variant localized to the DNA-binding domain of the protein, a region known to be functional when disrupted. Second, bioinformatic tools indicated a high likelihood of being deleterious, with the AlphaMissense score projecting at 0.995. Third and most significantly, segregation analysis confirmed the variant's inheritance within the family. The patient’s mother and younger brother also carried the mutation, and the affected status of the grandmother aligned with the dominant transmission pattern, ensuring that the genetic data was not a sporadic mutation unrelated to the diabetes phenotype. Because the summary of reports showed that hundreds of different HNF1A mutations can cause disease, and this specific change was absent from previous literature, the convergence of in vitro prediction, biochemical output, and familial inheritance allowed for the validation of pathogenicity without relying solely on prior case literature.

This accurate genetic interpretation directly influenced the management strategy, ensuring that the patient received the correct therapeutic approach. Initially, treatment prioritized metformin and insulin to manage acute hyperglycemia; however, upon confirming the HNF1A mutation, the management strategy was refined to preserve pancreatic function. Genetic confirmation confirmed that sulfonylureas (which mimic insulin secretion mechanisms) might be effective but that sulfonylureas carry a risk of hypoglycemia, particularly if C-peptide function declines further. Therefore, the team transitioned the patient from insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which provided superior glycaemic control with minimal hypoglycemia, supported by evidence that GLP-1 analogs are superior in MODY due to their action on beta-cell function and reduced risk of weight gain compared to insulin. Furthermore, the identification of this specific variant confirmed the utility of genetic testing in clinical settings, as the patient received therapy that optimized blood glucose levels to 6.0% within three months, preventing long-term complications such as microalbuminuria and renal failure. Ultimately, this case demonstrates that distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from common diabetes forms not only clarifies the prognosis but also guides treatment toward safer, efficacy-focused regimens.
