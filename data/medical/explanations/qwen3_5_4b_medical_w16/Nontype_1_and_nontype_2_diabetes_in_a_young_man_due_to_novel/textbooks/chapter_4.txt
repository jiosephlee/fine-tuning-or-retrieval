Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 4: ## Long-term Outcomes and Clinical Implications

The therapeutic trajectory observed in this case underscores the critical necessity of aligning diabetes management with underlying pathophysiology rather than adhering to empiric algorithms. Initially, the patient was treated with insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin. This regimen reflected a standard approach to hyperglycemia in a young adult presenting with significant elevation in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), effectively mimicking a Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) presentation given the absence of autoimmune markers. However, the use of basal insulin alone in a young patient is often a curative strategy for Type 1 diabetes or an aggressive insulin failure; in this context, it obscured the patient's preserved yet insufficient endogenous beta-cell reserve. Relying on basal insulin can be a pitfall because it treats the symptom (hyperglycemia) without addressing the mechanism (modulated insulin secretion potential and sensitivity). Switching from insulin to insulin secretagogues or glucose-dependent agents is often the sustainable long-term goal for preservation of beta-cell mass in monogenic forms, a concept missed in the initial T2D-treatment phase.

Following genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, the treatment paradigm shifted away from high-dose insulin dependency toward agents that spare beta-cell function. The selection of a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, specifically dulaglutide, follows specific pathophysiological reasoning for HNF1A-MODY. Because HNF1A mutations lead to a progressive decline in beta-cell function, therapies that rely heavily on high glucose-dependent insulin secretion or suppress glucagon release are preferred to prevent beta-cell exhaustion. The case highlights that while sulfonylureas (e.g., glimepiride) are often first-line for HNF1A-MODY in younger patients, they carry a significant risk of hypoglycemia, especially if residual insulin function is suboptimal. In contrast, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer metabolic profile, reducing hypoglycemia risk while stimulating postprandial glucose control. The patient’s HbA1c improvement from 9.1% to 6.0% shortly after starting dulaglutide confirms that GLP-1 therapy is effective as a first-line agent that balances metabolic control with physiological safety in this genetically defined cohort.

Treatment selection in HNF1A-MODY also necessitates specific negative selection of anticoagulant therapy options due to underlying genetic transcription effects. A crucial aspect of this case is the deliberate avoidance of Sodium-Glucose Linked Transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. The HNF1A gene regulates the transcription of SGLT2 in the renal tubules; patients with HNF1A-MODY already exhibit reduced baseline SGLT2 expression, meaning their renal glucose threshold is naturally lower. Consequently, exogenous pharmacological inhibition of SGLT2 in these patients can precipitate dehydration, volume depletion, and an increased risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). The case treats this as a contraindication, prioritizing safety over glycemic reduction efficacy. Instead, fixed-dose combinations like metformin pairings with DPP-4 inhibitors, such as saxagliptin, were deemed appropriate. This choice avoids the renal and neurological risks associated with SGLT2 inhibition while simplifying the regimen for maintenance.

The long-term outcomes in this case demonstrate that genotype-driven precision medicine can prevent complications while reducing drug burden. After 3 months of intensive treatment, the patient's HbA1c normalized to 6.0%. The decision to simplify the regimen to metformin and saxagliptin for long-term maintenance illustrates a strategic step-down approach. Once glycaemic control is stable, removing basal insulin avoids the risks associated with long-term insulin therapy, such as weight gain and hypoglycemic unawareness. The absence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy during the 6-month to multi-year follow-up further validates the efficacy of this tailored stabilization. This success reinforces that genetic screening in early-onset diabetes is not merely for research but for practical clinical management; accurate diagnosis allows for the avoidance of potentially harmful insulin doses, prevents the use of contraindicated anti-hyperglycemic agents, and facilitates the most cost-effective maintenance regimen possible for the individual patient.
