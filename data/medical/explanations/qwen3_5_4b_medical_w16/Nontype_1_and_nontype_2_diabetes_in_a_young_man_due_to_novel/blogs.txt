# The MODY Gray Zone: Navigating the Diagnostic Tension Between T1DM and T2DM in Young Adults

### The Clinical Puzzle

Case reports often serve as a wake-up call for us because they exist in that uncomfortable gray zone where Type 1 Diabetes (T1DM), Type 2 Diabetes (T2DM), and Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) blur together. You’re triageing a young adult in your endocrine clinic. They brought in for routine screening showing a Glycated Haemoglobin of 10.2% and fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. No ketones, no DKA, but they’re thirsty, hungry, and losing weight. Simple秋水仙 to say T2DM? Not if there’s strong vertical transmission involving the maternal grandmother in her 30s. Do you call it autoimmune T1DM because sugars are high and insulin might be needed? Not if the antibodies are negative.

This is where many of us run in circles. The NAR programme, and others, suggest looking at the family tree first. In this vignette, the family history was the silent alpha there. The maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at 20. You have an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance. It shouldn't necessarily be ignored. However, distinguishing these entities clinically is difficult. Can you tell HNF1A-MODY from T1DM pre-biopsy? No. Without genetic testing, you are flying blind, often chasing the wrong phenotypic labels and sticking the patient on the wrong therapy entirely.

### The Genetic Turnkey

The case of a patient with a novel HNF1A mutation (p.N270S) demonstrates the costs of missing this diagnosis. The initial management of this patient was aggressive but misguided. We started him on Basal Insulin Degludec, Metformin, and Saxagliptin. Why? Because the initial picture suggested Type 2 with hyperinsulinemia resistance, or T1D that defaulted successfully. The insulin dose was eight units daily, assuming T1D or significant insulin deficiency. Initial A1C dropped to 9.1%, which felt like a victory. However, after genetic confirmation, the strategy shifted.

This case highlights the power of the Gene. The patient had a heterozygous HNF1A variant that was not previously reported in literature or databases like ClinVar. This novel mutation highlights the need to look beyond the text version of the gene when interpreting results. While the patient had negative islet antibodies, some HNF1A variants are associated with autoantibodies, but majority don't. So, negative GADA is common. C-peptide was insufficient on glucagon stimulation test (0.83 nmol/L), which is a nuanced marker for residual beta cell function. Even though this preservation was insufficient, the genetic result confirmed MODY with HNF1A.

The treatment implications are profound. The patient successfully transitioned from high-dose basal insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (Dulaglutide) combined with Metformin. After 6 months, HbA1c bottomed out at 6.0%. No insulin was needed long-term. Why does this matter? Because HNF1A-MODY patients retain *some* beta-cell function, making them less prone to DKA compared to T1 and more risk of hypoglycemia with sulfonylureas than with GLP-1 or Metformin. The 60% of patients with HNF1A-MODY often don't fit the “classic” phenotype with severe obesity or ketoacidosis, so treating them as classic T2DM can lead to overtreatment with insulin, or undertreatment with anti-hypertrophic agents that don't engage the correct pathway.

## Key Takeaways

*   **Consider Genetic Testing Early:** Screen for HNF1A and other genes in young-onset diabetes, especially with strong family history or negative autoimmune antibodies.
*   **Interpret C-peptide and Antibodies with Caution:** Negative antibodies and preserved C-peptide support MODY over T1DM, but function can be dynamic over time.
*   **Tailor Therapy to the Pathway:** Avoid lifelong insulin for HNF1A-MODY; GLP-1 agonists and Metformin are often superior and safer to maintain beta-cell function.
*   **Novelty is x-Editing:** Even variants not in ClinVar can be pathogenic if they exhibit high AlphaMissense scores and segregate with the phenotype in families.
*   **Watch Out for the KPIs:** HbA1c drops with early intervention, but the biochemical markers may shift; monitor microalbuminuria and autonomic function to prevent long-term complications.

### Bottom Line

Don't let the clinical picture dictate the treatment without the full pedigree check in place. The case of the young man with HbA1c 10.2% who was saved from years of insulin dependence proves that tapping into the HNF1A gene is not just academic—it’s practical medicine. The pathophysiology of MODY dictates the etiology and treatment. Treat the MODY, not just the diabetes.



# Precision in the Genomic Era: Interpreting Novel HNF1A Variants Without ClinVar Match

## The Diagnostic Blind Spot

We recently encountered a clinical vignette that shines a light on the limitations of modern genomic databases in real-world diagnosis. In a young adult male, the clinical presentation initially screamed "Type 2 Diabetes" with a historical HbA1c of 10.2%, BMI of 24.6, and HNF1A mutation suspicion. However, the family history told a completely different story: the maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the patient’s brother at age 20. Standard Type 2 algorithms would have ignored the strong autosomal dominant inheritance pattern in favor of lifestyle factors. More importantly, the initial trial of basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin failed to account for the underlying monogenic defect until genetic confirmation was retrieved later in the process.

This case serves as a stark reminder that a negative ClinVar match does not equate to a negative result. The patient harbored a novel heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene. Despite this variant being absent from major databases like ClinVar and VarSome, ACMG guidelines and multiple bioinformatic lines of evidence pointed toward pathogenicity. The critical error that occurred in practice isn't the lack of data, but the premature dismissal of genetic testing in the face of atypical T2D-related features like autonomic dysfunction (severely reduced sudomotor function) and glycosuria in a non-obese patient.

## Decoding a "Unknown" Variant

When we look out for these "unknowns," we are often dealing with Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). However, precision medicine is shifting the paradigm. In this specific case, the pathogenicity of the p.N270S variant was supported by several independent lines of evidence despite the lack of prior reports:

*   **Structural Context:** The amino acid residue is adjacent to known pathogenic residues in the DNA-binding domain.
*   **Bioinformatics Scores:** The AlphaMissense score was exceptionally high at 0.995, suggesting a high probability of dysfunction.
*   **Segregation:** The variant segregated with diabetes in the family, confirming autosomal dominant transmission.
*   **Clinical Phenotype:** The presence of HNF1A-MODY symptoms (early onset, negative autoantibodies, insulin requirement) correlates perfectly with a known pathogenic class.

This case teaches us that we must rely on integration of clinical bio-data rather than solely looking up variants. A lack of historical precedent is a false negative, not a definitive rule. If a patient presents with HNF1A-MODY phenotype and carries a structural VUS with a high functional score, treat as pathogenic until proven otherwise.

## Treatment Implications for MODY

The diagnosis was a game-changer for this patient's management. The clinical progression from initial glycemic control to genetic confirmation directly influenced the therapy. The patient's HbA1c dropped significantly from 10.2% to 6.0% after the regimen shifted.

Crucially, the HNF1A diagnosis altered the medication protocol. Insulin therapy, initially started due to hyperglycemia and negative C-peptide stimulation (0.39 nmol/L at 0 min), was discontinued and replaced with a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide). This shift was effective in reducing HbA1c while sparing residual beta-cell function.

However, there are direct contraindications to note. The discussion section highlights a specific risk profile for HNF1A-MODY regarding SGLT-2 inhibitors. While often used in T2D, there is evidence suggesting that HNF1A-MODY patients may experience severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to high inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity or altered renal thresholds. In this case, the treatment regimen was simplified to metformin and saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor) for long-term maintenance, avoiding the exogenous glucose drag of sulfonylureas and the renal stress of SGLT2 inhibition associated with this specific monogenic defect.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Don't Rule Out MODY early:** A young patient with hyperglycemia, negative autoantibodies, strong family history, and autonomic dysfunction should warrant genetic testing for MODY regardless of BMI.
*   **ACMG Guidelines Override Database Gaps:** A novel variant (e.g., p.N270S) can be classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic based on segregation data, structural domain analysis, and bioinformatic scores even if it is not in ClinVar.
*   **Selection of Agents Matters:** HNF1A-MODY benefits from GLP-1 receptor agonism over sulfonylureas (due to hypoglycemia risk) and requires caution with SGLT-2 inhibitors due to DKA/dehydration risk.
*   **Precision Medicine Saves Insulin:** Accurate genetic diagnosis facilitates the transition off insulin, improving long-term glycaemic targets (HbA1c) and reducing early complications without the catabolic weight loss associated with T2DM.



# Beyond the First Line: Why HNF1A-MODY Patients May Thrive on GLP-1 Agonists

We all know that diagnosing young-onset diabetes can feel like walking through a minefield. You have Type 1 Diabete sparing the immune system, and Type 2 with the metabolic obesity and insulin resistance. Then, you have the MODY types—monogenic forms that often look like T1D or T2D until you finish the genetic sequencing. This case report offers a perfect lookbook on why getting that diagnosis right changes the game entirely, specifically highlighting the potential pitfalls of standard "Type 2" insulin protocols and the safety and efficacy profile offered by GLP-1 receptor agonists in HNF1A-MODY.

## The Diagnostic Trap

Let's look at how this patient presented. A young adult walked into the clinic with a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2—so he wasn't the obese patient. Think T2D. Yet, his retrospective family history told a different story: early-onset diabetes in his maternal grandmother at 30s, mother in her late 20s, and brother at age 20. That autosomal dominant clustering is classic MODY.

However, his metabolic labs painted a picture that seasoned clinicians might default to Type 2 diabetes with advanced failure. He had an HbA1c of 10.2% and a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. He was polydipsic, polyphagic, and had lost weight. Crucially, he had glycosuria, which is often blamed on diabetes pickles or renal threshold issues in Type 2 or T1D. But his beta-cell function story was nuanced. A glucagon stimulation test showed a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L, which suggests preserved but insufficient function (typically <0.60 suggests low, >0.60 suggests preserved). Wait, that's weird. If it's 0.83, that's preserved? It's actually *above* the 0.60 cutoff. The key here is the context. He wasn't considered T1 (negative autoantibodies) but wasn't doing well enough on T2 management.

The clincher was the genetic testing. They tested for the HNF1A gene. It hitched a ride on next-generation sequencing (NGS) and found a novel heterozygous substitution p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G). This variant wasn't in ClinVar. This is why genetic screening is so crucial in young-onset diabetes—especially when population-based data can underestimate rates in non-European populations compared to typical guidelines.

## The Genetic Answer: Precision Matters

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor that regulates insulin production and function. When this goes wrong, the beta cells hang on, but they can't pump insulin effectively, leading to hyperglycemia. The presentation is often agonizing: high glucose, high glycosuria, and sometimes signs of autonomic neuropathy early on (like this patient's severely reduced sudomotor function).

Initially, he started on a "Type 2" regimen that mimics his clinical presentation: Metformin 1500 mg/day, Saxagliptin, and Basal Insulin Degludec. You'll note the 2021 US Medical Imaging guideline on Metformin as a separate point. Let's get back to the point. He had a 1 month follow-up HbA1c of 9.1%, which showed some improvement with insulin. But the renal thresholds, the beta-cell dysfunction markers, and the family history screamed "MODY." Replacing "standard" Type 2 management with the specific genetic diagnosis opens doors to specific therapies that are contraindicated in Type 2 but might fit MODY better.

Here's the clincher: sulfonylureas and insulin are often the default first-line therapy for new-onset diabetes. But in HNF1A-MODY, these drugs carry unique risks. While sulfonylureas stimulate insulin release, HNF1A mutations can lead to a progressive decline in beta-cell function that makes them perilous. This patient didn't get into a sulfonylurea trap, so he stayed on the glucose-lowering drugs, but insulin therapy was the initial driver.

## Precision Medicine Tactics: Insulin vs. GLP-1

The metabolic shift in the treatment plan is what makes this case report a gold standard for education. Once MODY was confirmed, the team switched off the insulin and brought on the GLP-1 receptor agonist: Dulaglutide. Why? GLP-1s have a proven safety profile for preserving beta-cell function, whereas insulin and sulfonylureas can accelerate functional decline in this specific genotype. The HNF1A pathway also plays a role in SGLT2 expression. This means that certain SGLT2 inhibitors might actually be risky for patients with HNF1A-MODY due to the risk of dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis (the text mentions a "decomposition of SGLT-2 activity" leading to potential complications).

The results speak for themselves. After starting Dulaglutide, the patient's HbA1c dropped from 9.1% down to 6.0% in just 6 months of GLP-1 support, alongside Metformin. This survival—stabilization without hypoglycemia, a better safety profile than sulfonylureas, and preservation of the beta cell function—is why this regimen is so appealing. The regimen was later simplified to Metformin plus Saxagliptin, but the proof was already in the books.

Many clinicians might have stuck the patient on insulin to reach the 6% target, but the MODY algorithm (insufficient beta-cell function) warns against over-masking the underlying disease without genetic confirmation. It's worth noting the patient also didn't have microalbuminuria or retinopathy, which suggests early detection and tight control mitigated complications.

## Key Takeaways

*   **MODY is a mimic:** Don't label a young patient with diabetes as T1 or T2 without ruling out MODY when family history is clustered.
*   **Genetic testing is diagnostic:** Use NGS panels in young patients with persistent hyperglycemia despite insulin stimulation or negative autoantibodies.
*   **HNF1A-MODY management is distinct:** GLP-1 receptor agonists are safer alternatives to sulfonylureas/insulin in preserving beta-cell function and avoiding hypoglycemia in this specific subtype.
*   **Avoid SGLT2 in HNF1A:** Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY due to the risk of DKA.
*   **Novel mutations matter:** Even if a variant isn't in ClinVar, functional family segregation and bioinformatic scores support pathogenicity.



# The Autonomic Clue: Using Sudomotor Testing to Support MODY Diagnosis

The case of this young adult presents a classic diagnostic conundrum that often confuses even seasoned endocrinologists. We are looking at a patient with a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) of 10.2% and a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, yet he is essentially "lean" by metabolic standards (BMI 24.6 kg/m²). He has no history of ketoacidosis, negative insulin autoantibodies, and a family history suggesting early-onset diabetes that defies the usual patterns of Type 1 or Type 2.

But the most intriguing piece of the puzzle wasn't the antibody panel or the genetic sequencing; it was the autonomic testing.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma

When a young patient presents with significant hyperglycemia and early-onset diabetes, the differential usually narrows between two heavyweights: autoimmune destruction (Type 1) or insulin resistance (Type 2).

In this case, the workup ruled out Type 1 early. Glucagon stimulation C-peptide levels showed only *preserved yet insufficient* function (0.83 nmol/L stimulated). This is the hallmark of non-autoimmune diabetes. The family history acted as the anchor, noting early-onset diabetes in the maternal grandmother (30s) and mother (late 20s), suggestive of an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern.

However, the standard workup—antibodies negative, family history suggestive, but no genetic testing on hand initially—leaves a gap. How confident can we be it's not LADA or atypical Type 2? In this patient, the biomarkers pointed elsewhere, but we had no molecular confirmation to guide the immediate treatment strategy.

## The Autonomic Clue

Entering this clinical picture, we often focus on glucose and lipids. This patient, however, underwent sudomotor function testing using electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan), a non-invasive tool assessing small-fiber autonomic nerve activity.

The results were strikingly positive for autonomic involvement **"severely reduced conductance in the hands."**

This finding is valuable, though rarely discussed as a primary diagnostic marker. Why does it matter?
*   **BMI Discrepancy:** Unlike Type 2 Diabetes, where autonomic dysfunction often follows years of insulin resistance and hyperglycemia, this patient had significant metabolic irregularities and potential autonomic nerve damage while remaining relatively lean.
*   **Early Onset:** Preventing diabetic microvascular complications early suggests an underlying pathology that damages nerves and beta-cells with greater metabolic rigidity.
*   **Diagnostic Context:** While the genetic test eventually confirmed the diagnosis (HNF1A-MODY), this autonomic finding reinforced the clinical suspicion. In the context of negative GADA/IA2A antibodies and a high stimulated C-peptide that was preventing a Type 1 diagnosis, it added a layer of complexity to the metabolic profile, distinct from early Type 1 (no nerves damage) or typical Type 2 (nervous system usually spared until long-standing T2D).

Some studies suggest that HNF1A mutations have varying degrees of protein function, but the presence of autonomic dysfunction without severe obesity might suggest an intrinsic beta-cell defect that impacts more than just glucose transport.

## Precision Medicine in Action

Once the genetic test eventually returned a pre-test result—**HNF1A:c.809A>G (p.N270S)**—the treatment path changed dramatically. Initially, the limited protocol mandates for HNF1A were not explicitly followed, and the team was on basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin (reflecting a T2D-like approach).

But with the genetic confirmation and the adjusted metabolic profile, the strategy was optimized.
*   **Insulin Discontinued:** The patient was switched from basal insulin to **dulaglutide**.
*   **DPP-4 Inhibitor Retained:** Saxagliptin 5 mg/day to maintain control.
*   **Outcome:** After 6 months, HbA1c dropped to 6.0%. No microalbuminuria or retinopathy.

This highlights a crucial principle: we must be willing to stop what we are doing when the underlying mechanism clarifies. GLP-1 receptor agonists were chosen over sulfonylureas in some contexts, though sulfonylureas are often first-line for HNF1A-MODY, the team needed to reduce hypoglycemia risk.

Wait, did the text mention sulfonylureas? No. It says "First-line therapy involves low-dose sulfonylureas... GLP-1 receptor agonists can serve as a safer... alternative." So the switch to GLP-1 was a targeted decision for *this patient's* specific risk profile and preservation of pancreatic function.

## Take-home Messages

*   **Don't Ignore Autonomic Dysfunction in Young Diabetics:** Recognizing early autonomic signs (like reduced sudomotor function) in young patients with negative standard autoantibodies can support MODY suspicion when BMI does not fit the T2D profile.
*   **Genetic Verification is Safe:** In this case, a novel mutation p.N270S was identified without prior literature reporting. While ACMG criteria classified it as suspected/likely, the clustering and clinical fit were high value.

This case is a reminder of the "invisible cracks" in the metabolic picture. Sometimes the biology manifests as early nerve damage before the glucose even spikes in the standard sensors.

# The SGLT2 Paradox: Why SGLT2 Inhibitors Are a Risk in HNF1A-MODY

Case complexity often lies in the overlap between presentation and physiology. Consider a 20-something male presenting with hyperglycemia, glycosuria, and weight loss. He looks classic for type 2, yet his BMI is only 24.6 kg/m², and he has no acanthosis nigricans. His family history is the real tell: his mother, grandmother, and younger brother all had early-onset diabetes. On the surface, you might prescribe metformin or insulin and treat for type 2 diabetes (T2D). However, if you dig deeper, the metabolic stability is precarious. In this case report, a young man with this exact profile was eventually diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY due to a novel heterozygous mutation (p.N270S). The educational opportunity isn't just in the genetic variant; it is in subsequent management missteps that could have been caught.

While the patient in the case report was successfully managed with metformin and a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide), one specific pharmacologic class poses a genuine—and sometimes paradoxical—danger in HNF1A-MODY that we need to flag firmly: SGLT2 inhibitors. The guideline standard for many years was to treat type 2 diabetes with broad classes including SGLT2 inhibitors, которых (in Georgian context) for traditional diabetes management. In the context of HNF1A-MODY, the underlying pathophysiology often renders these agents contraindicated.

The gene *HNF1A* encodes a transcription factor critical for pancreatic beta-cell function, but it also regulates the expression of the SGLT2 transporter in the kidney. In many cases of HNF1A-MODY, the mutation reduces SGLT2 expression, leading to early spontaneous glycosuria even before significant hyperglycemia develops. While this glycosuria is a hallmark of the disease, adding an SGLT2 inhibitor on top of an already compromised renal glucose reabsorption threshold can be catastrophic. The case report discussion notes that high inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity in this population may increase the risk of severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis. Because these patients often have reduced beta-cell reserve, they cannot compensate for the osmotic shifts driven by the inhibitor, pushing them toward euglycemic DKA or severe dehydration faster than in classic T2D.

Furthermore, the distinction between HNF1A-MODY and T2D becomes blurred as age advances and BMI increases. In the case described, the patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, not obese, yet his HbA1c was 10.2%. If you miss the genetic diagnosis and assume T2D, you might lean towards an SGLT2 inhibitor to gain neoteric weight. This ignores the risk of fungal infections (common in SGLT2-induced glycosuria) and the specific danger of DKA in monogenic diabetes. The patient in the report improved his HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% on GLP-1 + Metformin, proving that targeting beta-cell stimulation (or preservation) via GLP-1 agents can be superior to agents that stress renal glucose handling.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Genetics is safety:** In young-onset diabetes with a discordant BMI or negative PPD (post-prandial post) findings, check for MODY, especially with strong family history.
*   **SGLT2 caution in HNF1A:** The existing low SGLT2 expression in HNF1A-MODY creates a vulnerability to DKA and dehydration when inhibiting SGLT2 further. Avoid these agents unless closely monitored, given the risk of vascular collapse.
*   **GLP-1 is the choice:** GLP-1 receptor agonists support β-cell function and lower HbA1c safely in HNF1A-MODY without the renal dehydration risk of SGLT2 inhibitors.
*   **Antibodies are not enough:** Negative GADA or Ia-2A does not rule out MODY; always confirm with genetic sequencing or functional C-peptide analysis in discordant T2D presents.
*   **Novel mutations matter:** Clinching a diagnosis with ACMG guidelines on new variants (e.g., p.N270S) is as important as the gene name itself for treatment planning.
