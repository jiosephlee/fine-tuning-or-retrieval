Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Morning Report] In the initial presentation of this case, how did the family history of early-onset diabetes across multiple generations guide the initial clinical suspicion of a monogenic diagnosis versus common Type 2 Diabetes in a young adult?

In this case, the family history of early-onset diabetes presented a classic autosomal dominant transmission pattern, which served as the primary discriminator between Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) and sporadic Type 2 Diabetes (T2D). While T2D is a polygenic disorder typically associated with obesity and older age, the patient’s maternal lineage demonstrated a consistent, generational manifestation of diabetes in the absence of documented insulin resistance markers. Specifically, the report identified maternal grandmother diagnosis in her 30s, the mother diagnosed in her late 20s, and the younger brother at age 20. This vertical inheritance, where each patient presented with relative fasting and postprandial hyperglycemia in adolescence or young adulthood, strongly suggested a monogenic etiology over sporadic, age-dependent T2D.

The absence of acanthosis nigricans and normal body mass index (24.6 kg/m²) further heightened clinical suspicion for a non-insulin-resistant, genetic defect rather than classic metabolic syndrome associated with T2D. Furthermore, the negative islet autoantibody testing excluded autoimmune Type 1 diabetes, narrowing the differential to why the distinction was typically genetic based family history. Clinically, the recognition of this pattern prompts refusal of empirical insulin resistance management (e.g., basal insulin) initially and transitions patients toward genetic counseling and screening, such as next-generation sequencing for superfections in HNF1A as performed in this patient. In this manner, the severity of the family history provided a sufficient index of suspicion for monogenic diabetes, guiding the discrepancy between initial treatment protocols and the eventual discovery of the novel HNF1A p.N270S variant.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What specific serological markers were tested in this patient to definitively exclude Type 1 Diabetes, and what is the clinical significance of their negative results in the context of suspected Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young?

To definitively exclude Type 1 Diabetes in this patient, the imaging and laboratory workup included serological testing for islet autoantibodies. According to the "Investigations" section of the case report, specific antibody tests included glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A). The **Discussion** further clarifies that the diagnostic process adhered to established MODY criteria which included negative autoantibodies for GADA, IA-2A, and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8). All were reported as negative.

The clinical significance of these negative results lies in their utility for differential diagnosis. Type 1 Diabetes is characterized by autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta-cells, leading to the presence of these autoantibodies. Conversely, Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) is a non-autoimmune, monogenic disorder. Therefore, negative GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8 antibodies strongly support a diagnosis of non-T1DM etiology in a young adult with diabetes.

In the context of this specific case, these negative serological results were pivotal in shifting the diagnostic suspicion from Type 1 or Type 2 Diabetes to HNF1A-MODY. When combined with the patient's strong family history (maternal grandmother, mother, and brother affected), preserved yet insufficient C-peptide levels on glucagon stimulation, and the absence of black-tastic features like acanthosis nigricans found in this patient's initial evaluation, the antibody panel helped identify MODY patients who are otherwise frequently misdiagnosed.

These findings directly influenced the therapeutic strategy; whereas negative antibodies alone often require genetic confirmation before prescribing high potency sulfonylureas or avoiding insulin for T1/D type, the combination of negative autoantibodies, family history, and subsequent genetic testing led to the correct diagnosis of *HNF1A*-MODY. This allowed for the safe discontinuation of basal insulin and the initiation of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which lowered HbA1c to 6.0% while preserving pancreatic function, underscoring that serological exclusion of Type 1 Diabetes is a prerequisite step in accurate MODY diagnosis and precision medicine management.

### [Pathophysiology] How does a heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene transcription factor mechanistically lead to the progressive decline in pancreatic beta-cell function and glucose tolerance in HNF1A-MODY?

Based on the mechanism described in this case report, a heterozygous mutation in the *HNF1A* gene disrupts the transcriptional regulation of key pancreatic and hepatic genes, leading to an intrinsic defect in beta-cell physiology.

HNF1A acts as a critical transcription factor for the maintenance of beta-cell function. As detailed in this specific case, the patient possesses a novel heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G) located in the DNA-binding domain. This mutation impairs the transcriptional control of target genes including *INS* (insulin) and *SLC2A2* (GLUT2). Consequently, there is reduced expression of insulin and glucose transporters, directly causing decreased insulin secretion and glucose intolerance as observed in the patient’s hyperglycemia and elevated postprandial glucose.

The dysregulation extends beyond the beta-cell. As noted in the discussion regarding the patient’s glycosuria, HNF1A transcriptional control also affects renal proteins; specifically, reduced expression of SGLT2 (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2) is associated with this phenotype. This molecular explanation underscores why these patients may present with glycosuria without significant thirst or polyuria initially.

Clinically, this mechanism results in a progressive decline in beta-cell function rather than the abrupt destruction seen in Type 1 diabetes or the fixed genetic defect of glucose sensing seen in Glucokinase-MODY. The patient in this report demonstrated preserved but insufficient secretory capacity, evidenced by a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L on glucagon stimulation test, which declined over time as the transcriptional defect persisted. This distinguishes HNF1A-MODY from autoimmune diabetes, explaining the negative autoantibody status (GADA, IA-2A) observed in this case.

Precise genetic diagnosis, as performed using next-generation sequencing (NGS) per ACMG guidelines in this report, is therefore essential to distinguish this mechanism from Type 2 or autoimmune diabetes. Recognizing the transcriptional defect allows clinicians to anticipate a progressive loss of beta-cell reserve and tailor therapy—such as switching from insulin to GLP-1 receptor agonists in this patient—to preserve residual function while avoiding the hypoglycemia risk associated with sulfonylureas.

### [Management] Why was basal insulin therapy successfully discontinued and replaced with a GLP-1 receptor agonist in this patient, and how does the dose interval of this drug align with the progressive nature of beta-cell decline seen in MODY?

In this patient, the discontinuation of basal insulin degludec and transition to dulaglutide (a once-weekly GLP-1 receptor agonist) was rigorously dictated by the confirmation of **HNF1A-MODY**, a subtype characterized by a **progressive decline in β-cell function** due to a genetic defect in transcriptional regulation. The initial therapy (insulin + metformin + saxagliptin) was empiric for type 2 diabetes, but once the novel *HNF1A* mutation (**p.N270S**) was identified, management was refined to prioritize **β-cell preservation** over simple glycemic control.

Clinically, the switch was justified because HNF1A-MODY often presents with **preserved** endogenous insulin capacity but progressive dysfunction (evidenced here by a stimulated C-peptide of **0.83 nmol/L**). GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as dulaglutide, enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion from β-cells without the excessive hypoglycemia risk associated with insulin or sulfonylureas (which are unstable in this specific genetic context). The case report notes that GLP-1 therapy reduced HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% while avoiding the complications of rapid insulin reliance seen in previously misdiagnosed monogenic diabetes.

Regarding the **dose interval**, the weekly administration of dulaglutide aligns perfectly with the **chronic and progressive** pathology of HNF1A-MODY. Unlike the acute titration required for failing insulin secretion in type 1 diabetes, the slow, genetic decline in β-cell mass and function seen here allows for a stable, sustained pharmacokinetic regimen. Weekly dosing ensures consistent incretin-mediated stimulation of the **moderately preserved β-cells** (identified by the C-peptide results) over time, preventing glucose spikes while accommodating the natural fluctuation in metabolic demand over months or years. This approach allowed for the simplification of the regimen to metformin and saxagliptin later, confirming long-term stability, an outcome difficult to achieve with high-dose basal insulin alone in this population.

### [Management] What is the specific metabolic risk associated with SGLT2 inhibitor therapy in patients with HNF1A-MODY that contrasts with their use in Type 2 Diabetes, and what physiological mechanism underlies this risk?

Based on the findings presented in Liu et al., patients with HNF1A-MODY harbor a specific, heightened risk of **severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** when treated with SGLT2 inhibitors, a risk that contrasts with their generally safe and beneficial profile in Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) management.

While SGLT2 inhibitors are widely used in T2D to promote weight loss and glycemic control via renal excretion of glucose, the case report explicitly warns that "risks associated with using SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY patients, such as increased dehydration risk, potential vascular collapse, genital infections and diabetic ketoacidosis, may outweigh the potential benefits."

The physiological mechanism underlying this unique risk involves the transcriptional control of the sodium-glucose transporter 2 (SGLT2). As detailed in the case discussion, "glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is attributed to reduced expression of SGLT2, which is under the transcriptional control of HNF1A." In HNF1A-MODY, the genetic defect leads to a natural reduction in SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules. Consequently, pharmacologic inhibition of this "residual SGLT-2 activity" can precipitate severe metabolic decompensation. In a patient with already compromised endogenous regulation of glucose transport, the removal of the remaining transporter activity removes a critical safety buffer, leading to the potential for severe euglycemic ketoacidosis and dehydration observed in the reported literature.

### [Clinical Pearls] In this case report, how were the principles of the ACMG guidelines applied to classify a novel genetic variant (p.N270S) that was not present in established databases like ClinVar?

**Clinical Teaching Point: ACMG Guidelines for Novel Variants in HNF1A-MODY**

In this case, the classification of the novel HNF1A variant **p.N270S** demonstrates how the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines are applied when a variant is absent from established databases like ClinVar. While the absence of the variant in truth tables and population databases does not automatically prove benignity, it necessitates the accumulation of additional positive evidence to justify a *Likely Pathogenic (LP)* classification. In this patient, this challenge was overcome by integrating three specific lines of evidence:

1.  **Computational Prediction:** In silico analysis showed a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, indicating a high probability of functional impact.
2.  **Structural Context:** The residue was located in the DNA-binding domain and was adjacent to known pathogenic residues, suggesting a similar mechanism of dysfunction.
3.  **Family Segregation:** The variant segregated with the disease phenotype in the family, confirmed by the presence of diabetes in the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother.

The combination of these factors—despite the absence of the variant in ClinVar—provided sufficient confidence for an LP classification. This accurate genetic resolution allowed for a definitive shift in management: insulin therapy was discontinued and replaced with a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin, successfully lowering HbA1c to 6.0%. This underscores the critical role of rigorous variant classification using ACMG principles in unmasking MODY cases and guiding precisely matched medical therapy.

### [Clinical Pearls] Given that the Exeter MODY probability calculator was used in this study, what specific limitation of this tool affects its accuracy when calculating diagnostic probability for non-White or Asian populations, and how should clinicians account for this?

**Clinical Teaching Point: Populations and Diagnostic Probability Tools**

Based on this report, the primary limitation of the Exeter MODY probability calculator is its derivation from data within a White European population. Consequently, the tool may overestimate the probability of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) in non-White or Asian populations.

In this case, the calculator returned a probability of 62.4% for our patient. Clinicians must interpret these numerical estimates cautiously when evaluating patients of diverse ancestry, as the tool does not necessarily reflect the varying genetic architecture and disease prevalence observed in Asian and other non-White cohorts. The reported risk should not be used to override established clinical evidence, such as the autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, and specific beta-cell function profiles seen in this patient.

Ultimately, the specificity required for managing MODY mandates genetic confirmation. While the calculator aids in identifying candidates for risk stratification, the definitive diagnosis relies on molecular testing (e.g., next-generation sequencing), which remains the gold standard regardless of the calculator's ethnic-limited training data. This approach ensures treatment precision, as genetic phototyping allows for appropriate transition from insulin-baselines to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, as demonstrated by the successful management in this HNF1A-MODY case.

### [Management] What long-term monitoring parameters were prioritized for this patient after the transition to a multidrug regimen, and why is the assessment of these markers critical given the unique prognosis of monogenic diabetes compared to Type 2 Diabetes?

In this patient, the prioritized long-term monitoring parameters are **glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c)**, **urinary microalbumin/creatinine ratio (microalbuminuria)**, **diabetic retinopathy screening**, and **serum C-peptide levels** to track beta-cell function.

The assessment of these markers is critical given the unique prognosis of HNF1A-MODY compared to Type 2 Diabetes (T2D). While T2D is characterized primarily by progressive insulin resistance and eventual beta-cell failure, HNF1A-MODY involves a genetic autosomal dominant defect in beta-cell transcription and function that can present with glucose intolerance in the absence of insulin resistance. Consequently, the prognosis depends not on weight loss or autoimmunity (as in Type 1) but on the trajectory of **beta-cell preservation** achieved through targeted therapy.

As outlined in the **Outcome and follow-up** section of the case report, successful transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin regimen allowed the patient to maintain HbA1c below 6.5% without insulin while preventing microvascular complications. The case explicitly notes the absence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy at the conclusion of the 6-month timeline, establishing **ongoing surveillance for long-term complications** as a standard protocol.

Furthermore, monitoring **C-peptide levels** is essential because, as noted in the **Discussion**, HNF1A-MODY involves a "progressive decline in β-cell function." The case highlights that distinguishing MODY from T2D is a diagnostic dilemma due to overlapping clinical features like obesity. Unlike T2D, where long-term management often focuses on glycemic control alone, HNF1A-MODY monitoring must ensure that beta-cell function is not eroded by adjunct pharmacotherapy (e.g., GLP-1s are preferred over sulfonylureas or metformin alone in later stages to prevent hypoglycemia progression). By prioritizing these markers, clinicians can prevent the misapplication of T2D-specific guidelines (such as avoiding SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY, where dehydration risks outweigh benefits) and ensure the preservation of the distinct, albeit progressive, beta-cell phenotype described in the genetic evaluation of this patient.
