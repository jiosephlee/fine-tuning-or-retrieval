Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient with cardiac surgery undergoing antithrombotic prophylaxis, how does the onset of thrombocytopenia prior to the classic postoperative day 5-10 window relate to the history of preoperative heparin exposure, and what implication does this have for the diagnostic window of HIT?

**Clinical Teaching Point: Recognizing Preoperative Heparin Exposure as a Risk Factor for Early-Onset HIT**

During a postoperative assessment, the onset of cardiac surgery-associated thrombocytopenia is classically assigned a risk window between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10. However, as presented in the St Bernard et al. case report, thrombocytopenia occurring **prior** to this window (specifically evolving within the first few days) must raise suspicion for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) if a patient has a history of prior heparin exposure.

The immunologic reasoning is straightforward but critical for timing accuracy. The transfer of the immune-mediated response relies on the formation of IgG antibodies against the heparin–platelet factor 4 complex. In a standard surgical course where heparin is administered *only* intraoperatively or postoperatively, the lag time for sufficient antibody formation and platelet activation typically peaks after POD 5. However, **repeated or sustained antigen exposure prior to surgery**, such as the use of 2000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH) during preoperative catheterization in the Queen's case, provides an earlier sensitization of the patient's immune system. As the authors note in the Discussion, only about 10% of patients account for early onset thrombocytopenia, specifically linked to this recent preoperative exposure.

**Diagnostic Implication:** You should view the "POD 5–10 window" as a statistical guideline rather than a rigid biological cutoff. In the presence of preoperative heparin history (e.g., PCI catheterization, arterial line flushing, or percutaneous interventions), HIT must be considered if thrombocytopenia develops at POD 1–4. This prompt in the report underscores that the diagnosis should not be discounted based on the postoperative day, if the clinical suspicion is clinically and temporally grounded. In this specific case, the history of preoperative UFH translated to an atypical profile of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), which required a broader search for heparin-independent and dependent antibodies.

**Take-Home Principle:** In cardiac surgery patients with postoperative thrombocytopenia before POD 5, meticulously review all historical heparin exposures. Do not rule out HIT—or its autoimmune variant—based solely on the classic postoperative onset timeline.
