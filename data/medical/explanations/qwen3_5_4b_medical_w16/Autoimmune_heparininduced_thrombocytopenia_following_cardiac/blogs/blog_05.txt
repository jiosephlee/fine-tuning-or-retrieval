Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## The Stroke Climax: Managing Neurological Complications in HIT

Most Westerners learn heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) as a postoperative nightmare of bleeding that turns catastrophic into thrombosis. But what if that first sign of trouble isn't bleeding, but paralysis? In this scenario, a 70-year-old cardiac surgery patient presents with the "classic" triad of thrombocytopenia, high D-dimer, and hypercoagulability, culminating in a parietal stroke. This case serves as a reminder that HIT is a biological immunologic storm to be managed, not just a laboratory artifact.

The patient arrived unanticoagulated with preoperative heart catheterization. This is where the case gets tricky. He received only 2000 U of UFH during the cath, seemingly low, but subsequent heart surgery involved 45,000 U of UFH for cardiopulmonary bypass. Typically, HIT antibody onset after cardiac surgery happens days 5 to 10, aligning with postoperative heparin exposure. Yet, the platelet count dropped precipitously on postoperative day 1 (POD1), reaching 50×10⁹/L by POD5. Why? A literature review for this patient is essential to rule out other causes. This early onset was the key. The culprit was heparin given prior to surgery, linking the trigger to the intrinsic immune response of the heart and vascular system.

Several weeks later, this wasn't just about blood work anymore. The patient's worsening postoperative delirium and the continued downward spiral of platelets (to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L) dictated the need for brain imaging. This is where the stroke manifested. The patient had suffered a parietal infarct. In many HIT cases, stroke is a protective mechanism; in this case, it was the harbinger of death. The challenge lies in balancing the risk of recurrent thoracic thrombosis against the risk of hemorrhage or further ischemia. In this case, the stroke was the "climax" because it validated the need for aggressive but specific anticoagulation.

In the laboratory, the standard of care became the proverbial hurdle. The screening PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive. But here is the twist: the serotonin-release assay (SRA) was "indeterminate." This doesn't mean the doctor doesn't fight HARD for a diagnosis; it means we have to look closer at the data. When the lab discovered the SRA profile, it showed serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin without added heparin. This is the hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), where antibodies activate platelets without heparin. Furthermore, adding a monoclonal antibody failed to fully inhibit the serotonin release. This unique profile, with partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade, is not common. It suggests the antibodies are holding a high affinity that is not easily neutralized by blocking Fc receptors.

Once the diagnosis of aHIT was confirmed, the treatment path became complex. High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was given to quench the antibodies, and directly thrombin inhibition was started with argatroban. However, the patient's recovery was not linear. There was no brisk platelet count rise. Why? Because for aHIT, antibodies persist longer, and high-dose IVIg has variable efficacy. It was decided to switch to fondaparinux, given the patient's neurological status and the clinical picture of persistent hypercoagulability despite argatroban therapy. Fondaparinux allowed for easier management than the PTT-based argatroban, but it required supplemental antithrombin levels before switching. By POD62, the platelets recovered to normal, and asymptomatic neurological function returned.

This case highlights a rare but dangerous subset of HIT. The "stay ground in the case report" instruction ensures we see the timeline and labs from the source. The most critical aspect for the bedside clinician was the recognition that HIT antibodies can sustain platelet activation independently of heparin (hence the aHIT working principles) and that their duration often extends beyond the typical immune window, requiring long-term follow-up AFP testing. The patient's antibodies returned to a more typical profile by POD124, showing that the autoimmune nature is transient but active early.

When facing a composite picture of stroke and thrombocytopenia, we must resist the urge to stop anticoagulation entirely in high-risk HIT patients. For this patient, the transition to Fondaparinux was a calculated move to prevent re-infarction while allowing for a consolidated immune load to resolve over time. It is a lesson in patience and identification of the antibody phenotype.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Timing is Specific:** In HIT, onset before POD5 can occur due to preoperative heparin exposure, communicated in the history (cath, procedural sedation).
*   **Serum SRA vs. ELISA:** An "indeterminate" SRA with serotonin release at 0 U/mL suggests aHIT rather than classic heparin-dependent HIT.
*   **Neurological Dilemma:** Balancing hemorrhagic risks with thrombotic complications requires a dual-approach strategy; do not withhold Heparin in the absence of clear hemodynamic bleed.
*   **Antibody Clearance:** aHIT antibodies can persist for months and may shift phenotype over time; SRA testing for atypical profiles is crucial in the post-recovery phase.
