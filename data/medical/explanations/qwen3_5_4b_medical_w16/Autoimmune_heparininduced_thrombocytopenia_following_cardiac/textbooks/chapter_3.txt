Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 3: ## Pathophysiology of Autoimmune HIT and Clinical Variability

To understand the clinical presentation of this patient, one must first distinguish between the classic mechanisms of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and the immunologic deviations seen in the autoimmune variant, or aHIT. In classic HIT, approximately one in six patients develops antineuraminic antibodies following heparin exposure, but these antibodies are typically directed against the polymer of heparin and platelet factor 4 (PF4). Upon binding, immune complexes of heparin and PF4 crosslink and activate collagen receptors on platelet surface, stimulating the expression of mRNA for P-selectin and Fibrinogen-related protein-alpha (FgRA). Crucially, this process relies on platelet Fc receptor (FcγR) clustering, which amplifies the inflammatory cascade. The serotonin-release assay (SRA) used in the patient’s workup exploits this mechanism by measuring serotonin release from human platelet aggregography. In classic HIT, this serotonin release is partial and consistently dependent on heparin concentration. In contrast, aHIT involves autoantibodies with heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. As documented in this patient, aHIT antibodies can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin, evidenced by the strong serotonin release (96%) observed at 0 U/mL heparin in this case. This heparin-independent activity distinguishes aHIT from classic HIT and typically correlates with more severe platelet activation and thrombotic complications.

A fundamental challenge in diagnosing HIT during the period immediately following cardiac surgery is the immunologic time lag. Typically, aHIT manifests 8 to 10 days post-exposure as the immune system produces IgG antineuraminic antibodies. However, classic HIT cases usually fall on or after postoperative day 5. The unusual early onset of this patient’s thrombocytopenia (beginning on POD1) was attributed to preoperative exposure. For many patients, the immune response required to generate detectable antibodies exceeds the time available during the typical window of postoperative heparin exposure. In this case, the patient received intra-procedural unfractionated heparin (UFH) during a preoperative heart catheterization. This preoperative exposure acted as an immunological inoculum, sensitizing the patient sufficiently to mount a rapid response upon the return of UFH during cardiopulmonary bypass surgery. Consequently, the clinical course did not conform to the standard 5-to-10-day postoperative window, leading to the paradox of platelet count drops occurring well before POD5.

The clinical variability within aHIT is not solely dependent on the time of onset but is also fluidly correlated with the reactivity of the circulating antibodies, which often manifests as profound thrombocytopenia and thrombosis. In the traditional HIT paradigm, thrombocytopenia is driven by platelet consumption, but aHIT is characterized by what resembles a consumptive coagulopathy. The patient presented with significant fibrinogen depletion (dropping to 2.49 g/L) and rising d-dimer levels during argatroban treatment, alongside the appearance of circulating schistocytes. This triad suggests that aHIT antibodies may stimulate the coagulation cascade directly or more potently recruit inflammatory pathways, causing widespread clot formation and fibrin usage in addition to direct platelet destruction. The severity of the thrombocytopenic nadir (13×10⁹/L) and the subsequent parietal stroke highlight that aHIT carries a significantly higher relative risk of thrombotic complications. This increased severity appears directly correlated with the unusually strong antibody profile; while treatment corrected the coagulopathy, antibody-driven activation likely remained potent, necessitating prolonged anticoagulation and immunomodulation.

Finally, the diagnostic interpretation of the SRA must account for Fc receptor blockade, which serves as a critical differentiator between classic and aHIT antibody behavior. The SRA typically employs secondary controls to assess antibody conversion to platelet activation via Fc receptors. When serum containing classic HIT antibodies is mixed withUFH, serotonin release correlates with heparin concentration, and adding an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) should largely inhibit the release (dropping to <20%). However, in this patient’s sample, the addition of the Fc receptor-blocking antibody resulted in only partial inhibition, with 76% serotonin release observed in the presence of IV.3. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade represents a novel serological phenotype in the literature. It suggests that the autoantibodies in this patient may utilize mechanisms beyond simple Fc receptor clustering or possess properties that partially bypass the blockade. Recognizing this distinct inhibition profile is vital in diagnostic reasoning; while it initially labeled the result 'indeterminate,' completing the profile with long-term follow-up showing a shift toward heparin-dependent characteristics confirmed aHIT with evolving antibody reactivity.
