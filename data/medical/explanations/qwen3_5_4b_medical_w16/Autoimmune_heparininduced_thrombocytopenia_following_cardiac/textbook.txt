Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Suspicion

This case began with a patient in his seventies presenting with exertional shortness of breath and a known history of atrial fibrillation maintained on oral apixaban. The clinical context involved urgent cardiac intervention following diagnostic heart catheterisation performed two days prior to the scheduled cardiac surgery. A critical detail in this presentation was the timing and dosing of anticoagulation; despite pre-operative orders to hold the antiplatelet drug, the patient underwent heart catheterisation without holding his apixaban, and the intraprocedural anticoagulant dose was lower than usual. Without holding antiplatelet therapy, the patient received UFH during the procedure, and subsequently received a 2000-unit dose of UFH intraoperatively for cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Intraoperative meals and drugs are often forgotten, but the primary driver of heparin exposure here was the pre-operative and intra-operative UFH doses. The initial platelet count prior to surgery was 210×109/L, which is well within the normal reference range, setting a baseline for subsequent observation.

The timeline of the patient's course is particularly instructive for understanding the pathophysiology of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Following cardiac surgery, the platelet count trended downward, dropping to 155×109/L on postoperative day (POD1). While the first drop was relatively minor, the count fell significantly and sustained a trajectory that reached 50×109/L by POD5. This progressive decline continued, reaching a nadir of 26×109/L by POD6 before further dropping to 13×109/L. Concurrent with this thrombocytopenia, clinical signs of neurological deterioration emerged, including worsening postoperative delirium. CT brain imaging subsequently confirmed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe. The presence of thrombosis alongside severe thrombocytopenia in a post-cardiac surgery setting is a hallmark of HIT. However, the reporter highlights the atypical nature of this onset, as the thrombotic stroke occurred alongside the profound decline in platelets, placing the patient in a high-risk hypercoagulable state.

Differential diagnosis evaluation focused on excluding common post-operative causes of severe thrombocytopenia, such as sepsis or cardiogenic shock. This case did not present with evidence of infection; blood and urine cultures remained negative, and the patient maintained stable haemodynamics without symptoms of critical illness. Similarly, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was deemed unlikely given the normal haptoglobin levels and the initial absence of schistocytes on peripheral blood smear. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was eventually used, but the initial suspicion during evaluation centered on HIT. Specifically, the exclusion of other etiologies relied on the patient's demographic history, the rapidity of the platelet fall exceeding 50% over 5 days, and the strong positive screening results for HIT antibodies. Recognizing the exclusion of TTP and drug reactions—which would typically present with schistocytes or urticaria and fever, respectively—allowed the team to focus on the immune-mediated etiology driven by the heparin exposure.

Instructional focus on the timing of thrombocytopenia is vital for identifying autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Classically, HIT manifests between postoperative days 5 and 10. In this case, the significant onset occurred prior to the standard window (before POD5), fundamentally altering the diagnostic reasoning regarding the source of heparin exposure. Usually, intraoperative UFH is cleared during surgery, but the discovery of elevated antibodies reflected a heparin-independent phenotype triggered by the preoperative heart catheterisation. This early onset is linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure, where the patient was exposed to UFH during the procedure despite being in the same hospital setting as the cardiac surgery. Understanding that the immune response to heparin can initiate immediately upon exposure is a key learning point for clinicians, as patients with preoperative heparin exposure should be monitored closely for the onset of HIT, even when the term “classic” HIT often defaults to post-op days 5-10.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Interpreting Serological Findings

In the evaluation of postoperative thrombocytopenia, particularly within the critical window following cardiac surgery, distinguishing between classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and autoimmune HIT (aHIT) requires a rigorous interpretation of serological data. While the screening immunoassay—the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)—provides a high-probability indicator of antibody presence, it is insufficient for definitive diagnosis due to the potential for cross-reactivity or false positives. In this case, the patient presented with a screening result of 2.2 optical density (OD) units, which is significantly above the normal reference range of less than 0.4 OD units. This strong positivity, combined with the timing of a profound platelet decline and thrombotic complications, strongly suggests an immune-mediated etiology. However, because a high screening sensitivity does not confirm the specific pathophysiology or sub-type, the threshold for definitive diagnosis remains the platelet activation tests, specifically the serotonin-release assay (SRA).

The SRA serves as the gold standard confirmatory test by measuring IgG antibodies binding to platelet Factor 4 (PF4)-heparin complexes, thereby inducing platelet degranulation and serotonin release. In typical classic HIT, the presence of heparin is required to form the PF4-antibody-platelet complex; therefore, serotonin release should increase with added heparin concentration (0.1, 0.3, and 1.0 U/mL) but generally cease or decrease at supratherapeutic levels (100 U/mL). This suppression at high heparin concentrations occurs because excess heparin does not facilitate PF4 binding, preventing complex formation and subsequent platelet activation. In our patient, the SRA initially yielded an "indeterminate" profile. The serum induced strong serotonin release (97–99%) at low but relevant concentrations of heparin (0.1 and 0.3 U/mL) and suppressed release (0%) at high concentrations (100 U/mL), which initially mimicked classic HIT. However, this result did not fit the classic curve entirely, prompting a deeper investigation into serology.

The second defining feature of autoimmune HIT is the ability of HIT antibodies to activate platelets independently of heparin. In the absence of heparin (0 U/mL), there is no PF4-heparin bridge to facilitate antibody interaction; therefore, classic HIT presentations typically have no serotonin release at this concentration. Conversely, aHIT antibodies impinge directly on platelet surface activating receptors (such as the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa complex) without needing the heparin-PF4 interface. A crucial diagnostic clue in this case was the SRA finding of 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin in the presence of patient serum. This deviation from the classic reference range (<20% release) confirms the heparin-independent nature of the antibodies, distinguishing this case from typical HIT despite the presence of heparin-dependent features at low concentrations.

Diagnostic ambiguity often arises when antibodies display complex serological profiles. To resolve this, laboratories utilize platelet coagulation inhibition studies using a monoclonal antibody (IV.3). In classic HIT, these antibodies typically bind to the Fc receptor, and the addition of IV.3 (which blocks Fc receptor binding) inhibits further serotonin release. Here, the addition of the Fc receptor-blocking antibody failed to completely inhibit the serotonin release observed at 0.1 U/mL heparin, resulting in 76% serotonin release rather than the expected inhibition to <20%. This partial resistance is a hallmark of aHIT, particularly in severe cases where the autoimmune mechanism involves high-avidity antibodies that are difficult to neutralize with Fc receptor blockade alone. Recognizing this partial inhibition prevented misclassification as classic HIT and justified the highly aggressive treatment strategy involving therapeutic-dose intravenous immunoglobulin and direct thrombin inhibition, tailored to the severity of the hypercoagulability.

Ultimately, the interpretation of these serological findings was not static but evolved over the course of recovery. The initial indeterminate and aHIT-like profile suggested a unique, severe case requiring a distinct exclusion of other etiologies and careful monitoring. The subsequent testing revealed a transitional phase where antibodies waned, eventually showing a more typical heparin-dependent profile. This longitudinal serological monitoring, coupled with the clinical resolution of the stroke and platelet recovery, validated the initial serological diagnosis of aHIT. For the reader, this emphasizes that a single SRA result may appear atypical, but in the context of high-sensitivity screening, clinical thrombosis, and resistance to Fc receptor blockade, it serves as a diagnostic roadmap for managing a rare and severe form of immune thrombocytopenia.

## Pathophysiology of Autoimmune HIT and Clinical Variability

To understand the clinical presentation of this patient, one must first distinguish between the classic mechanisms of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and the immunologic deviations seen in the autoimmune variant, or aHIT. In classic HIT, approximately one in six patients develops antineuraminic antibodies following heparin exposure, but these antibodies are typically directed against the polymer of heparin and platelet factor 4 (PF4). Upon binding, immune complexes of heparin and PF4 crosslink and activate collagen receptors on platelet surface, stimulating the expression of mRNA for P-selectin and Fibrinogen-related protein-alpha (FgRA). Crucially, this process relies on platelet Fc receptor (FcγR) clustering, which amplifies the inflammatory cascade. The serotonin-release assay (SRA) used in the patient’s workup exploits this mechanism by measuring serotonin release from human platelet aggregography. In classic HIT, this serotonin release is partial and consistently dependent on heparin concentration. In contrast, aHIT involves autoantibodies with heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. As documented in this patient, aHIT antibodies can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin, evidenced by the strong serotonin release (96%) observed at 0 U/mL heparin in this case. This heparin-independent activity distinguishes aHIT from classic HIT and typically correlates with more severe platelet activation and thrombotic complications.

A fundamental challenge in diagnosing HIT during the period immediately following cardiac surgery is the immunologic time lag. Typically, aHIT manifests 8 to 10 days post-exposure as the immune system produces IgG antineuraminic antibodies. However, classic HIT cases usually fall on or after postoperative day 5. The unusual early onset of this patient’s thrombocytopenia (beginning on POD1) was attributed to preoperative exposure. For many patients, the immune response required to generate detectable antibodies exceeds the time available during the typical window of postoperative heparin exposure. In this case, the patient received intra-procedural unfractionated heparin (UFH) during a preoperative heart catheterization. This preoperative exposure acted as an immunological inoculum, sensitizing the patient sufficiently to mount a rapid response upon the return of UFH during cardiopulmonary bypass surgery. Consequently, the clinical course did not conform to the standard 5-to-10-day postoperative window, leading to the paradox of platelet count drops occurring well before POD5.

The clinical variability within aHIT is not solely dependent on the time of onset but is also fluidly correlated with the reactivity of the circulating antibodies, which often manifests as profound thrombocytopenia and thrombosis. In the traditional HIT paradigm, thrombocytopenia is driven by platelet consumption, but aHIT is characterized by what resembles a consumptive coagulopathy. The patient presented with significant fibrinogen depletion (dropping to 2.49 g/L) and rising d-dimer levels during argatroban treatment, alongside the appearance of circulating schistocytes. This triad suggests that aHIT antibodies may stimulate the coagulation cascade directly or more potently recruit inflammatory pathways, causing widespread clot formation and fibrin usage in addition to direct platelet destruction. The severity of the thrombocytopenic nadir (13×10⁹/L) and the subsequent parietal stroke highlight that aHIT carries a significantly higher relative risk of thrombotic complications. This increased severity appears directly correlated with the unusually strong antibody profile; while treatment corrected the coagulopathy, antibody-driven activation likely remained potent, necessitating prolonged anticoagulation and immunomodulation.

Finally, the diagnostic interpretation of the SRA must account for Fc receptor blockade, which serves as a critical differentiator between classic and aHIT antibody behavior. The SRA typically employs secondary controls to assess antibody conversion to platelet activation via Fc receptors. When serum containing classic HIT antibodies is mixed withUFH, serotonin release correlates with heparin concentration, and adding an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) should largely inhibit the release (dropping to <20%). However, in this patient’s sample, the addition of the Fc receptor-blocking antibody resulted in only partial inhibition, with 76% serotonin release observed in the presence of IV.3. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade represents a novel serological phenotype in the literature. It suggests that the autoantibodies in this patient may utilize mechanisms beyond simple Fc receptor clustering or possess properties that partially bypass the blockade. Recognizing this distinct inhibition profile is vital in diagnostic reasoning; while it initially labeled the result 'indeterminate,' completing the profile with long-term follow-up showing a shift toward heparin-dependent characteristics confirmed aHIT with evolving antibody reactivity.

## Therapeutic Management and Anticoagulation Strategies

The management of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) in this case required rapid decision-making to address both life-threatening thrombosis and the risk of anticoagulant-induced bleeding, particularly in a patient presenting with a subsequent parietal stroke. The initial therapeutic strategy prioritized immediate cessation of heparin exposure and the initiation of an appropriate anticoagulant alternative, specifically a direct thrombin inhibitor. In the acute phase of severe HIT, particularly when active thrombosis has already occurred or is strongly suspected, the choice of anticoagulant carries significant weight. Standard function of HIT prophylaxis typically involves low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) or unfractionated heparin. However, in the setting of thrombocytopenia and stroke, the risk of bleeding outweighs the utility of heparin as an anticoagulant. Direct thrombin inhibitors (DTIs) such as argatroban are generally preferred over other alternatives like warfarin, which require concomitant management with bleeding risks and are ineffective for acute HIT, especially during the thrombocytopenia of HIT. In this specific case, argatroban was selected despite the patient’s recent thrombotic stroke because the bleeding risk associated with heparin was deemed the priority concern, given the severe thrombocytopenia and the patient’s need for an alternative to the offending agent. Argatroban offers a mechanism of action that inhibits the clotting factor directly without relying on the coagulation cascade as extensively as other agents, providing a safer profile for patients where even small-volume bleeding complications could be exacerbating neurological deficits.

While selecting the correct anticoagulant, the management protocol must account for the immunological basis of immune-mediated aHIT, which involves the formation of immune complexes. In addition to anticoagulation, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was initiated as an adjunctive therapy to modulate the immune response. In the context of classic HIT, limiting heparin exposure is often sufficient, but in severe autoimmune HIT, the body may produce antibodies that are resistant to standard clearance mechanisms. The case details indicate a regimen of initial IVIG administration of 100 g administered over two consecutive days, followed by a third dose after two days remained without an initial increase in platelet counts. The rationale for high-dose protocols is that they may saturate Fc receptors and modulate the production of autoantibodies more aggressively than standard doses. It is important to note for clinical learning that response to IVIG in severe aHIT may be delayed or suboptimal; in this case, the lack of a brisk platelet correction despite the high dose highlighted the severity of the autoimmune phenotype. Therefore, reliance on IVIG alone without effective anticoagulation is insufficient, necessitating a combined approach of medical management and anticoagulant administration.

Monitoring the efficacy of the therapy requires relying on indirect markers when the platelet count is low and direct tests are not available for immediate titration. In this case, fibrinogen levels and d-dimer trends were utilized as essential surrogates to assess HIT-associated hypercoagulability. The case demonstrated a progressive decrease in fibrinogen levels from 6.59 g/L to a nadir of 2.49 g/L by POD17, while peak d-dimer levels were reached around POD12. Similarly, the appearance of schistocytes (red cell fragments) in the peripheral blood film between POD9 and POD16 indicated active microvascular thrombosis. These markers guided the conclusion that despite platelet recovery, the patient remained hypercoagulable during the treatment phase with argatroban. A common error is assuming clinical stabilization based solely on visible thereon, but in HIT, coagulation parameters often dictate the need for medication intensification or withdrawal. The appearance of schistocytes and rising d-dimers, even as fibrinogen falls, suggests that the coagulation cascade is still being activated despite the current anticoagulant, signaling a need for adjustment in the antithrombotic strategy.

Given that the therapeutic trajectory with argatroban indicated insufficient control over the hypercoagulability state, evidenced by the persistent fibrinogen decline and schistocytosis, a strategic switch in anticoagulation was initiated on POD14. Argatroban was discontinued in favor of fondaparinux, chosen for both efficacy and safety profile. Fondaparinux is a direct factor Xa inhibitor with an oral-subcutaneous formulation that does not require constant anticoagulant monitoring, unlike argatroban which requires a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) target range or intravenous infusion titration. This switch was complicated by the patient’s baseline coagulation profile. Fondaparinux requires functional antithrombin (AT) levels for blockade inhibition; the case noted that supplemental AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to the switch because the patient’s in-vivo AT level was mildly reduced at 0.70 U/mL. Administering AT concentrate prior to fondaparinux started ensures that the patient has the requisite enzyme to transport the agent, which is critical for therapeutic success. This highlights a nuanced management principle: anticoagulant efficacy in HIT patients with underlying consumption or reduced levels must be supported by replacement strategies. By POD62, the platelet count recovered to normal levels, and subsequent neurological assessment showed complete resolution, confirming that the combination of anticoagulant switching, AT supplementation, and immune modulation achieved the desired coagulation and platelet recovery targets.

## Clinical Course, Outcome, and Educational Implications

The clinical trajectory of this patient underscores the severe and protracted nature of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT), a condition often missed or misdiagnosed due to its atypical presentation during the immediate postoperative window. Following the initiation of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin and direct thrombin inhibition with argatroban, the patient’s severe thrombocytopenia continued to warrant intensive monitoring despite anticoagulant coverage. While the patient initially exhibited thrombocytopenia prone to fluctuation, there was an unusually modest platelet response to the initial IVIG bolus, rising from a nadir of 13×10^9/L on postoperative day (POD) 7 to an interim peak of 54×10^9/L. This lack of brisk correction is a hallmark of severe aHIT, distinguishing it from classic heparin-dependent HIT where platelet counts often rebound rapidly with immune modulation and anticoagulation alone. Given the concurrent development of severe HIT-associated hypercoagulability, evidenced by falling fibrinogen levels, rising d-dimer concentrations, and the appearance of schistocytes on blood smears, the treating team recognized that current anticoagulation was insufficient to prevent further microvascular injury. Consequently, the strategy evolved to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on POD 14, supported by supplemental antithrombin concentrate. This switch allowed for a more manageable dosing schedule while addressing the persistent hypercoagulability, ultimately facilitating a steady recovery of the thrombocytopenia to 155×10^9/L by POD 62. Coinciding with this hematological recovery, the acute parietal stroke demonstrated complete neurological resolution, reinforcing the direct link between controlling the underlying immune-mediated thromboembolic risk and restoring clinical function.

This recovery was not merely hematological but required a longitudinal reassessment of the antibody profile to confirm the diagnosis of the autoimmune variant. Consequently, the case provides a clear example of the serophage dynamics inherent in aHIT. Serial serotonin-release assay (SRA) testing allowed surveillance of the immune response beyond the acute survival phase. The initial diagnosis relied on an atypical SRA profile characterized by strong serotonin release in the absence of UFH (0 U/mL) and only partial inhibition by the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Over the subsequent months, while SRA reactivity remained positive, the profiles shifted toward a more characteristic heparin-dependent phenotype. This transition, observed by POD 124 where the assay showed only 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH but maximal release in the presence of UFH, reflects the natural decline of the unique autoimmune antibodies and the dampening of platelet-activating properties. This evolution highlights that the immutable diagnosis of aHIT can sometimes rely on serological persistence, yet the phenotypic expression can drift toward classic HIT profiles as the immune complex load is cleared and standard heparin-dependent antibodies repopulate the repertoire.

Understanding this recovery trajectory in the context of the patient's initial presentation offers several critical lessons for managing early-onset postoperative thrombocytopenia. The most significant takeaway relates to the temporal window of onset; classic cardiac surgery-associated HIT typically manifests between POD 5 and POD 10. However, this patient’s thrombocytopenia began on POD 1, driven by preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin during cardiac catheterization. Clinicians must remain vigilant that heparin exposure prior to a scheduled procedure does not reset the immune initiation timeline, as a lower-than-usual anticoagulant dose (2,000 U) was likely sufficient to initiate an immune response in a sensitized individual. Furthermore, the presence of a new thrombotic event, such as the parietal stroke in this case, combined with a strong positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, should warrant immediate consideration for HIT even before the POD 5 threshold. The case demonstrates that standard prophylactic heparin doses may not clear pre-existing antibodies, meaning "washout" periods are absolute only if intravenous heparin is discontinued entirely, and early screening for HIT antibodies is standard practice whenever thrombocytopenia accompanies cardiac surgery.

Finally, the prevention of diagnostic delay in severe variant HIT relies heavily on recognizing the limitations of standard confirmatory testing when the phenotype is atypical. In this case, the initially indeterminate SRA result was a source of potential diagnostic delay. The profile, defined by serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and partial inhibition of the Fc receptor-blocking antibody, is pathognomonic for aHIT. However, without awareness of this specific variant, treating clinicians may misinterpret the indeterminate platelet activation assay as a negative, leading to insufficient therapeutic intervenation. Clinicians must be prepared to treat severe postoperative thrombocytopenia with suspected hypercoagulability landscape (indicated by schistocytes, elevated D-dimer) as aHIT with high-dose IVIG and empiric direct thrombin inhibitors, rather than waiting for a clean serum profile. Early recognition of the atypical Fc receptor blockade resistance allows for the administration of appropriate high-dose immunoglobulins and the selection of agent-specific alternative anticoagulants, such as argatroban or fondaparinux, thereby preventing the cascading thrombosis events seen in this case.

In summary, the recovery of this patient from aHIT following cardiac surgery highlights the necessity of managing profound thrombocytopenia with aggressive immunological and anticoagulant support. The successful outcome was dependent on adapting management to the presence of severe autoimmune mechanisms rather than adhering to standard HIT protocols. This case reinforces the need for vigilance in early postoperative thrombocytopenia regardless of exposure history, as preoperative heparin can precipitate severe immune-mediated thrombosis within days, and it remains a critical educational example of the spectrum of HIT requiring distinct serological and clinical nuances.
