Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Initial Clinical Course: Sepsis and Disseminated Coagulopathy

The clinical management of *Capnocytophaga* infections is often complicated by the variable latency of symptoms and the occult nature of the causative organism. In this case, a previously healthy woman in her late 40s presented after a dog bite, a known source of transmission for zoonotic zoonotic infections. Although she received tetanus prophylaxis, she failed to collect a prescription for antibiotic therapy on the day of the incident. This delay is critical, as *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a fastidious, facultative anaerobe that can cause fulminant sepsis if not treated promptly. The delay allowed the bacteria to replicate unchecked in the oropharyngeal flora of animals, facilitating its potential invasion of the bloodstream. On admission, the patient was septic and unstable, highlighting the importance of considering zoonotic pathogens in patients with recent dog bites, even in immunocompetent adults who are often mistakenly assumed to be resistant to such infections.

The initial presentation was dominated by rapidly progressive septic shock. Upon arrival, the patient exhibited a high fever (>40 °C), mottled lower extremities, and significant signs of end-organ hypoperfusion. A particularly notable and atypical feature of this case was severe hypoglycemia, with a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L that remained refractory to oral glucose and glucagon. While hypoglycemia is common in *C. canimorsus* sepsis, often attributed to sepsis-induced cortisol resistance or stress hyperglycemia, the profound degree here reinforced the diagnosis of a toxic, overwhelming infection. Blood gas analysis confirmed metabolic acidosis with a lactate level exceeding 5.7 mmol/L, indicating profound anaerobic metabolism consistent with shock. Clinical evaluation included aggressive fluid resuscitation, though the patient maintained adequate diuresis initially, which was later complicated by the rapid progression to anuria.

Laboratory investigations revealed a complex constellation of findings characteristic of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). The workup showed marked systemic inflammation, pancytopenia, elevated liver enzymes, and coagulopathy. The combination of fever, pancytopenia, and coagulopathy supported a diagnosis of sepsis-related DIC, initially prioritized over microangiopathic processes. However, hematological consultation was necessary to differentiate between infectious DIC and hematologic malignancies. Given the pancytopenia and coagulopathy, the differential diagnosis included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Blood flow cytometry and a planned bone marrow biopsy were normal, showing no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypes, which effectively ruled out malignancy. Concurrently, Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (HUS) was considered; however, the clinical and laboratory picture, notably "normal haptoglobin" and "non-compensatory reticulocytes" at presentation despite some inflammatory signs, suggested the dominant pathology was infectious shock rather than primary TMA at this stage.

The management strategy was driven by the severity of the presentation and the need for rapid antibiotic coverage before pathogen confirmation. Broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was initiated upon arrival to cover dog-associated pathogens, including sensitive and resistant Gram-negative anaerobes, including *Capnocytophaga species*. This empirical aggressiveness was appropriate given the patient's septic instability. Further diagnostic workup identified a surgical cause for the mottled extremities (necrotising fasciitis) after clinical and radiological evaluation, but the focus remained on the bacteremia. *C. canimorsus* was confirmed only seven days later by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures, a diagnostic delay prompted by the organism's fastidious nature, which requires specialized molecular methods like real-time nanopore sequencing or standard culture with growth over several days. The patient was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit where the multiorgan failure, including respiratory distress and fluid overload from septic cardiomyopathy, necessitated continuous renal replacement therapy.

This initial phase underscores the importance of distinguishing sepsis with coagulopathy from TMA syndromes like CM-HUS, although both overlap pathophysiologically. In this patient, the initial "TMA correlation" was obscured by the overwhelming septic DIC phase. The sudden onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia occurred later, only after the initial sepsis and coagulopathy began to stabilize. This temporal distinction is pivotal for diagnosing CM-HUS in the context of infection; it suggests the haemolytic process was triggered by the burden of infection or the evolving immune response rather than being the primary sequela of the bite. The initial successful use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and supportive care for sepsis management simplified the clinical decision-making process by stabilizing the patient just as the kidneys failed and the need for dialysis-dependent therapies became apparent, setting the stage for the complex therapeutic decisions regarding complement inhibition required to manage the underlying microangiopathy.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Sepsis to Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA

In this case, the clinical trajectory shifted from an otherwise fulminant response to sepsis following a dog bite to the emergence of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), requiring a distinct diagnostic approach as the inflammatory phase had initially masked the diagnosis. Initially, the patient presented with hypothermia, hypotension, and coagulopathy consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. However, as the septic drive stabilized, new hematological and renal markers emerged, necessitating a rigorous shift in differential diagnosis. The first concrete indicator of TMA was the abrupt drop in haemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over six days without evidence of visible bleeding or significant blood loss. Since the initial clinical improvement with antibiotics and supportive care was followed by isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia, the differential expanded to include microangiopathic hemolytic anaemia (MAHA) secondary to endothelial injury. A peripheral blood smear analysis became pivotal in this phase, revealing 15–20% schistocytes. These jagged red blood cells are the hallmark of mechanical shearing within small vessels and confirm the presence of MAHA, distinguishing it from other causes of hemolysis that might occur in the context of septic shock alone. This was further supported by a rising LDH level and undetectable haptoglobin. An important diagnostic complication in this phase was a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) which initially raised suspicion for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. However, this was likely a false positive triggered by penicillin or prior transfusions, confirmed when repeat testing was negative for the antibody, reinforcing the diagnosis of a thrombotic rather than autoimmune origin.

In the investigation of MAHA, the most critical step is distinguishing TMA from Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP), a differential with significant management implications. TTP, a rare form of glomerulonephropathy defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (activity <10%), must be ruled out using plasma exchange early. In this patient, ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, which effectively excludes TTP. Furthermore, while standard complement tests (C3/C4) were within normal limits, the PLASMIC score calculated based on clinical presentation was 4/7, indicating a low pre-test probability of TTP compared with TMA. The diagnosis of TMA relies on laboratory-based confirmation of schistocytes and platelet depletion, but TTP is defined by a specific enzymatic defect. Once TTP was deemed unlikely, the focus shifted to the most common form of HUS in immunocompetent adults, Shiga toxin-producing E. coli HUS (STEC-HUS). The patient tested negative for Shiga toxin by stool PCR, and the history lacked the prodromal enteric illness typically associated with STEC-HUS. Consequently, the absence of both enteric prodrome and Shiga toxin argues strongly against STEC-HUS, supporting a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) triggered by the unique bacterial isolate rather than common food-borne transmission.

Despite the laboratory evidence for MAHA, renal biopsy was required to distinguish sepsis-induced organ failure from primary renal pathology. The kidney biopsy revealed a complex picture of severe acute tubular necrosis (ATN) alongside ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescent formation. While severe ATN is expected in patients with fulminant sepsis and shock, the presence of glomerular involvement and thrombi is diagnostic of TMA. Although the biopsy showed mixed pathology, the coexistence of tubular necrosis and TMA features provided the histological evidence required to confirm TMA as the active pathological process rather than pure DIC or shock necrosis. This distinction is vital for prognosis, as pure TMA involving glomerular thrombi stabilizes with appropriate immune modulation, whereas ATN may resolve with volume resuscitation. Finally, the diagnostic confidence in CM-HUS was maintained despite the observation of normal complement levels (C3/C4) and normal functional assays for alternative and classical pathways at the time of initial testing. Acquired CM-HUS, unlike genetic forms, often presents with normal baseline complement levels; the formation of a complement-mediated attack on the endothelium can exist without serum protein depletion detectable in standard assays. The reduction in alternative pathway activity noted later was attributed to treatment effects and the consumption cycle following the initiation of plasma exchange and eculizumab, rather than intrinsic complement depletion or a baseline genetic variant. Thus, the combination of excluded differentials (STEC and TTP), the presence of MAHA, and the clinical context of an acquired infection supported the diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS, guiding the decision to initiate eculizumab.

## Pathophysiology: Complement Dysregulation and Infectious Triggers

To understand the complex clinical course of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) in this patient, one must first address the diagnostic ambiguity often encountered in acute thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). A common stumbling block in the recognition of acquired CM-HUS is the stability of serum complement components, specifically C3 and C4. In many textbook descriptions of hereditary atypical HUS, significant consumption of these factors is the hallmark of active disease. However, in acquired forms triggered by infection, C3 and C4 levels may remain within normal reference ranges during the acute exacerbation phase. In this case, preliminary testing revealed C3 and C4 levels were within normal limits during hospitalization. This finding does not contradict the diagnosis of CM-HUS; rather, it necessitates an expanded workup, as standard screening levels may normalize due to renal clearance or hemolysis dynamics, making them insufficient to rule out complement deregulation. The persistence of acute organ failure despite normal levels underscores the clinical necessity of looking beyond standard complement assessment when vascular injury and thrombosis are confirmed.

The precipitating event identified in this case was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia, which represents a specific subset of infection-triggered dysregulation. *C. canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that is frequently found in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Unlike many other pathogens, *C. canimorsus* exhibits variable susceptibility to host immune defenses; while the capsule allows it to evade initial recognition, certain lipopolysaccharides and other surface components are potent activators of the alternative complement pathway. This activation occurs particularly in hosts with compromised regulatory mechanisms. In the immunocompetent host of our patient, the infection induced a compensatory activation of the cascade that, while clearing the bacterium, simultaneously triggered uncontrolled endothelial damage and intravascular coagulation. This paradox highlights a critical pathophysiological concept: the very mechanism of bacterial clearance (complement) may become the driver of the host injury if regulation fails at the host end.

Differentiating between acquired CM-HUS and underlying hereditary complement defects involves a rigorous process of exclusion and genetic confirmation, a process that was completed in this patient. While true hereditary atypical HUS (aHUS) is often linked to pathogenic variants in regulatory genes such as Factor H, Factor I, and B, acquired disease typically lacks such genetic mutations. In the immunocompetent patient, extended complement gene testing returned negative for pathogenic variants. This supports the conclusion of infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a genetic flaw predisposing to spontaneous aHUS. A key diagnostic distinction lies in the longitudinal observation of platelet counts and haemolysis patterns; in this patient, the haemolytic phase and thrombocytopenia developed subsequent to the resolution of sepsis, suggesting the infection acted as an external amplifier within a functional system. The absence of predisposing genetic abnormalities, combined with a clear trigger, defines the acquired phenotype.

This observation aligns with the 'Second Hit' hypothesis, which posits that a genetically susceptible complement system requires an environmental trigger to manifest disease. In this context, the "Second Hit" is represented by the *C. canimorsus* infection. Patients may possess a complement system that is functionally stable until challenged by severe bacterial sepsis. The initial septic phase, dominated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), often masks the specific features of HUS, as the coagulopathy and multiorgan failure overlap significantly with TMA. Once the bacterial clearance occurred and inflammation subsided, the underlying thrombotic cascade registry the underlying endothelial injury, leading to the isolated drop in haemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopaenia. This timeline is critical for therapeutic decision-making, as it distinguishes cases where reduction of the complement evasion level (antibiotics) might suffice versus those requiring complement blockade.

Finally, the hematologic evidence in the case provides further confirmation of the thrombotic mechanism despite the infection. A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG initially raised suspicion of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia; however, this was negated by the repeated negative tests after penicillin prophylaxis was discontinued and transfusion history was accounted for, identifying it as a likely false positive related to foreign protein interactions. Instead, peripheral blood smears revealed schistocytes, indicative of mechanical shearing of red blood cells as they navigate through fibrin thrombi. Furthermore, the absence of a Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* infection and the specific response to complement blockade rather than blood transfusions solidified the diagnosis. The role of antibody-dependent cytotoxicity in this specific instance was weakened by the negative reflexes, pointing instead toward complement-mediated endothelial activation destroying the vessel wall and creating the hemolysis-obsessive production state characterized by microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia.

## Therapeutic Strategy: Plasma Exchange and Eculizumab Implementation

The definitive management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) centers on complement blockade, typically achieved with C5-targeted monoclonal antibodies such as eculizumab. In the acute interobservation setting of this patient, the therapeutic approach involved a two-phase strategy: bridging therapy using plasma exchange (PEX) followed by complement blockade with eculizumab. This sequence was necessitated by the patient's current state of active bacteraemia and multiorgan failure. While plasma exchange is the standard bridging therapy for thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), it is distinct in this case as stabilizing rather than curative intervention. PEX effectively removes circulating complement deposits, inflammatory mediators, and antibody-coated cells that could perpetuate endothelial injury. It serves to stabilize the patient's platelet count and renal function temporarily, preventing hemodynamic collapse, while the mechanism of the antibody itself corrects the underlying complement dysregulation. In the case report, PEX was initiated early in the ICU phase to manage microthrombotic phenomena and support temporary diuresis, facilitating the transfer to the nephrology ward where definitive therapy could be planned once infection status was clarified.

Timing of eculizumab initiation is critically dependent on the resolution of the underlying infectious trigger. Eculizumab inhibits terminal complement fragments (C5a), which are indispensable for the host immune defense against encapsulated bacteria, particularly *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. Initiating therapy during active, uncontrolled bacteraemia poses a significant risk of treatment-emergent fulminant sepsis or meningitis. In this case, the decision to start eculizumab was contingent on the negative blood cultures and clinical immune stability following de-escalation of broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam) to aminopenicillins. The patient entered complement blockade after 8 days of hospitalization and confirmed clearance of the organism. As the case indicates, haemoglobin recovery and stable inflammation markers (even if dysfunctional complement levels persist) were prerequisites for starting the induction therapy, ensuring that the therapeutic benefit of complement blockade did not compromise immunological clearance of the pathogen.

Infection prevention measures were strategically layered to mitigate the specific risks of terminal complement blockade. Protocol dictates that patients receiving eculizumab must be immunized against encapsulated bacteria, such as *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Neisseria meningitidis*, and *Haemophilus influenzae*, whenever possible. Standard protocol typically requires vaccination at least two weeks prior to complement blockade to ensure immune response, though vaccination of serogroups may be less feasible immediately upon descent into haemoglobinemia. In this scenario, the meningococcal vaccine series (Menveo and Bexsero) was administered before the first dose of eculizumab to maximize protection. Concurrently, prophylactic antimicrobial coverage, specifically apocillin (amoxicillin-like) for *Capnocytophaga* species, was continued. This dual strategy of vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis creates a safety net: the vaccine primes the adaptive immune system, while antibiotics provide the fixed barrier against hyporesponsiveness during the first few weeks of eculizumab therapy. This carefully timed intervention minimized the risk of exacerbating sepsis while eradicating the thrombotic crisis.

Clinical markers used to assess the efficacy of this particular therapeutic strategy included trends in platelet count, hemoglobin levels, and serum creatinine. In this case, the initial therapeutic window was defined by the resolution of the "pseudo-leucopenia" and schistocyte production. Haematological remission was characterized by a stabilization of platelets (>70×10⁹/L), correction of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (normalization of LDH and rise in haptoglobin), and reversal of the schistocytosis seen on peripheral smear (15–20%). Concurrently, renal recovery was gauged by diuresis and declining azotemia, evidenced by the spontaneous return of urine output following plasma exchange sessions. The combination of haematological parameters improving and the stabilization of creatinine confirmed the efficacy of the immunomodulatory therapy. The patient remained a candidate for maintenance dosing (1200 mg every 2 weeks) precisely because these markers returned to a treated range, contrasting significantly with the persistence of shock, anuria, or worsening cytopenia seen in untreated CM-HUS.

## Outcome, Follow-up, and Clinical Significance for Treating Rare Triggers

Following the therapeutic intervention described in the case report, the patient's clinical trajectory demonstrated a distinct biphasic recovery, underscoring the importance of recognizing this pattern during sepsis management. The patient initially presented with severe renal failure, progressing rapidly to anuria and complete reliance on continuous renal replacement therapy. Despite initial hematological and renal stabilization following plasma exchange, spontaneous diuresis only returned after the initiation of eculizumab therapy, coinciding with improved haematological parameters and serological markers. Currently, the patient maintains partial renal recovery with a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, indicating that proximal nephron function is preserved despite the maintenance of dialysis. This outcome highlights a critical prognostic principle in TMA management: while early initiation of complement blockade (eculizumab) is strongly associated with haemological remission, sustained renal recovery often depends on the cumulative time-to-onset of therapy and the persistence of complement-mediated endothelial injury. The shift from dialysis-dependent anuria to stable output after 4 months of maintenance therapy (1200 mg every 2 weeks) serves as a model for evaluating efficacy versus necessity in long-term care planning.

Due to the potential severity of the disease phenotype, highly aggressive management was prioritized regarding immunological resetting. Although extended genetic complement testing was eventually performed and returned with no identified pathogenic variants in this patient, the absence of a hereditary predisposition did not immediately clinch the cessation of therapy. In cases of infection-triggered CM-HUS without confirmed genetic risk factors, the decision to continue complement blockade is driven by the clinician's assessment of the infection's impact on disease penetrance—essentially treating the 'infection-triggered' complement dysregulation or secondary response. With serious concerns associated with abrupt withdrawal of treatment in severe cases, clinicians must balance the risk of relapse against the prognosis of nephrectomy or dialysis. In this specific case, the clinical consensus favored continuation due to the severity of the disease, with genetic testing reserved for a phase where the long-term prognosis allows for close monitoring of relapse.

Critical safety considerations were paramount when managing this immunocompetent adult on complement inhibition, specifically regarding the risk of uncontrolled infection. Since *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* relies on its capsule for virulence and evasion of phagocytosis, complement blockade inherently creates a semblance of immune paralysis for encapsulated bacteria. To mitigate this, prophylactic management comprising dual strategies was employed: antimicrobial prophylaxis with amoxicillin (prophylactic Apocillin) and the administration of meningococcal vaccines (Menveo for serogroups A, C, Y, W, and Bexsero for serogroup B). Vaccination was administered only 2 weeks prior to the initiation of complement blockade to ensure adequate immune response or allow for antibody stabilization outside the window of exogenous antibody depletion. This case reinforces the specific guideline that broad-spectrum prophylactic antibiotics should be maintained for at least 2-4 weeks in high-risk individuals initiating eculizumab, as some encapsulated pathogens may persist in the absence of terminal complement function.

Finally, this case illustrates a significant diagnostic challenge where TMA can be obscured during an overwhelming septic presentation, a lesson essential for the differential diagnosis of acquired immunodeficiencies or inflammatory states. In the acute phase, the patient presented with fever, DIC, and multiorgan failure, leading to a preliminary diagnosis of severe sepsis. However, the late development of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and schistocytes suggests an underlying TMA was masked by the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and coagulopathy. This teaching point reinforces that the classic triad of TMA (MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and Acute Kidney Injury) should not be dismissed as merely DIC or septic shock; when schistocytes are identified, TMA remains the primary candidate for exclusion and investigation even during the florid sepsis phase. Early recognition of this overlap allows for timely intervention, which is associated with improved renal outcomes and lower mortality.
