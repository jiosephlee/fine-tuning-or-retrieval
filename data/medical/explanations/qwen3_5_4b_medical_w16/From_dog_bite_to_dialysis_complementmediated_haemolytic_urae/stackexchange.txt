Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Morning Report] In the initial presentation, how did the clinical picture of DIC and sepsis complicate the diagnosis of the subsequent Thrombotic Microangiopathy, and why is the timing of hemolysis significant?

In this case, the initial septic presentation complicated the diagnosis of Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) because the clinical features of sepsis-induced Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) and Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) overlap significantly with those of Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS). At admission, the patient's pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and multiorgan failure were primarily attributed to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* septic shock. As noted in the case report, TMA seemed initially unlikely because the presentation was dominated by severe inflammation and leukopenia, and the coagulopathy suggested a consumptive process rather than the microangiopathic processes of TMA. While the clinical course later evolved to reveal microangiopathic phenomena, the initial diagnosis was obscured by the severity of the infection-related coagulopathy.

Furthermore, the timing of the onset of hemolysis is significant for distinguishing infection-triggered CM-HUS from pre-existing genetic complement dysregulation or primary septic coagulopathy. In this case, isolated hemolysis appeared only after the patient began to improve from the acute sepsis phase (specifically, after 8 days of hospitalization). The report highlights that this lag suggests an infection-triggered mechanism where the initial bacteremia primes complement dysregulation, leading to TMA once the acute septic drive stabilizes. This timing helps reconcile the finding of normal complement levels (C3/C4) during the acute phase, as they may remain within reference ranges in the disease course despite complement dysregulation. Recognizing this delay allows for earlier identification of TMA-specific features (like persistent schistocytosis and thrombocytopenia despite clotting factor recovery), which is critical for initiating life-sustaining therapy like eculizumab and preventing progression to end-stage renal disease.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What specific laboratory finding definitively excludes Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) as a diagnosis of exclusion in a patient presenting with Thrombotic Microangiopathy?

In a patient presenting with the multisystemic triad of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, the specific laboratory finding that definitively excludes Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) is a normal ADAMTS13 activity level.

TTP is pathophysiologically distinct from other forms of Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) due to the acquired severe deficiency of the ADAMTS13 metalloprotease. According to the guideline cited in the Evansen et al. case report, TTP is defined by an ADAMTS13 activity level below 10%. Therefore, in the presence of significant TMA features, any ADAMTS13 activity level above 10% (typically reported as >10% or specifically in the clinical range of 30–100% for this case context) effectively rules out TTP.

This distinction is critical in our case report by Fredriksen et al. The patient presented with features consistent with sepsis-related DIC, which evolved into a TMA phenotype following *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection. Characterization of her condition relied on laboratory confirmation. Although standard complement studies (C3/C4) were normal, the specific diagnostic discriminator was ADAMTS13 activity, which was reported at 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP. This corroborated a diagnosis of Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

Clinically, this lab finding dictates management strategy. TTP is a medical emergency requiring immediate plasma exchange (PEX). However, as seen in the case report, misdiagnosing CM-HUS as TTP and relying solely on PEX may lead to suboptimal outcomes and prolonged transfusion requirements. Conversely, initiating complement blockade (eculizumab) without excluding TTP first carries risks if TTP is active. Thus, the ADAMTS13 assay remains the indispensable laboratory test to triage TMA patients correctly, ensuring the initiation of targeted therapy over broad empirical supportive care.

### [Differential Diagnosis] The patient tested negative for Shiga toxin and lacked bowel symptoms; what specific criteria support the exclusion of STEC-HUS in favor of an infection-triggered CM-HUS diagnosis?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

To establish a diagnosis of infection-triggered complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) over Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC) HUS, the diagnostic process relies on the exclusion of STEC triggers and the identification of an alternative pathophysiological driver. As outlined in this case, three specific criteria separate the two entities in our patient:

1.  **Absence of diarrheal prodrome and negative Shiga toxin:** STEC-HUS typically presents with subacute bloodless diarrhea. This patient presented with a dog bite and rapid-onset sepsis, lacking bowel symptoms. Crucially, stool PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative. As reported, "the absence of prodromal enteric illness and negative microbiological testing argues against STEC-HUS."
2.  **Identification of a complement-activating pathogen:** The diagnosis shifted to CM-HUS once we confirmed *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. The case report highlights that *C. canimorsus* is a zoonotic pathogen capable of triggering TMA, often in periods of complement dysregulation. Unlike STEC, the toxin production was not the driver of a subacute GI infection-disease cascade.
3.  **Temporal dissociation and therapeutic response:** In STEC-HUS, pathology is often driven by the persistent presence of commensal bacteria in the gut. Here, isolated hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia developed *after* the septic phase began to improve ("haemolysis appeared only after sepsis began improving"). Furthermore, the emphasis on infection-triggered CM-HUS is supported by the failure to identify pathogenic complement gene variants, alongside the dramatic clinical improvement following the initiation of eculizumab (as confirmed in our patient: "Following the second and third PEX sessions, the patient’s spontaneous diuresis returned" and "haemolysis... failed to respond to corticosteroid therapy").

Ultimately, the diagnosis is confirmed by the clinical triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the exclusion of TTP via ADAMTS13 activity (43.5%), and the confirmation of the trigger organism (*C. canimorsus*) in the presence of no genetic complement defect.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the pathophysiological mechanism proposing that Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia can directly trigger endothelial injury in the absence of a genetic complement variant?

The pathophysiological mechanism proposed in this case is that *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia acts as a direct convertible trigger for the alternative complement pathway, leading to endothelial injury through unregulated terminal complement activation, independent of inherited predisposition.

While complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) typically requires a genetic predisposition (such as mutations in Factor H or Factor I) to lower the threshold for dysregulation, this patient lacked identified pathogenic variants. In this genetically normal host, the mechanism appears to be driven by an infectious "severity hit." *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* possesses a complex cell wall where the capsule influences serum susceptibility; many strains utilize complement opsonization for persistence, but in this septic context, the host's immune defense against the encapsulated bacteria precipitates excessive complement consumption and deposition on endothelial surfaces. This leads to endothelial cell injury, mediated by pro-inflammatory anaphylatoxins and direct C5b-9 membrane attack complex formation, resulting in thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

This specific pathology was distinguished from thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura by normal ADAMTS13 activity and from standard HUS by the absence of Shiga toxin. Crucially, the patient's clinical diagnosis of CM-HUS was confirmed by a lack of genetic variants and the specific therapeutic response to eculizumab, a terminal complement inhibitor, rather than plasma exchange, which provided only transient benefit. Thus, the teaching point from this case is that while genetic susceptibility is the rule for CM-HUS in adults, severe encapsulated sepsis can act as a potent enough trigger to induce complement-mediated endothelial neglect in immunocompetent hosts, creating a transient state of complement-mediated injury amenable to terminal blockade.

### [Pathophysiology] Why do conventional serum complement levels (C3/C4) fail to rule out complement-mediated HUS during the acute active phase, and how does the functional assay (alternative pathway) clarify this?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

In the acute phase of complement-mediated TMA (CM-TMA), conventional serum complement levels (C3/C4) frequently remain within normal limits, rendering them unreliable for exclusion of the diagnosis. The serum concentrations of C3 and C4 are static markers of protein synthesis and consumption; however, in the setting of infection-triggered TMA like that seen in this patient with *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, the complement activation driven by dysregulated regulation of the alternative pathway does not always result in sufficient consumption to drop levels below the laboratory reference range. As the case report notes, "Complement levels (C3/C4) may be normal during the disease course... normal complement levels (C3/C4) do not exclude CM-HUS."

The functional assay, specifically quantitative alternative pathway activity (AH50), clarifies the mechanism and management strategy by assessing the actual regulatory capacity of the pathway rather than relying on factor concentrations. In this case, while initial C3/C4 and all standard functional assays were normal, follow-up testing six weeks later showed AH50 was markedly reduced to 6% following the initiation of eculizumab therapy. This reduction confirmed target-mediated suppression of the terminal complement, validating that the alternative pathway was the dominant driver of the microangiopathy even when C3/C4 levels appeared benign. This distinction is clinically critical as it redirects diagnostic focus from protein conservation (levels) to pathway regulation (function), guiding the immediate initiation of eculizumab despite normal conventional complement workup.

### [Management] Why was Plasma Exchange (PEX) used as a bridging therapy rather than a definitive treatment, and what is the therapeutic limitation of PEX in this specific context compared to eculizumab?

In this case, plasma exchange (PEX) was employed as bridging therapy rather than definitive treatment because it provides only transient stabilization, whereas eculizumab offers the necessary pathophysiological blockade required for sustained remission. The patient had complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis, a diagnosis confirmed after excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) by a normal ADAMTS13 activity (43.5%). While PEX clears circulating complement activation products and inflammatory mediators, the case report explicitly states it "provides only temporary benefit in CM-HUS, mainly by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators or autoantibodies, without correcting the underlying complement system dysfunction." In contrast, eculizumab provides definitive therapy by targeting the terminal complement component C5, preventing the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) that drives endothelial injury. The patient’s clinical course highlighted this limitation: while "spontaneous diuresis returned... following the second and third PEX sessions," she required "sustained haematological and renal recovery" only after the initiation of eculizumab to suppress complement activation at its source. Thus, PEX was a critical temporary measure to stabilize the patient while the definitive complement blockade of eculizumab took effect and the infection was cleared.

### [Management] What critical precautions regarding vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are required to mitigate the risks of invasive infection associated with eculizumab therapy in a patient with a severe bacterial infection?

**Clinical Teaching Point: Vaccine Timing and Antibiotic Bridge in Eculizumab Therapy**

The risk of invasive infection arising from terminal complement inhibition (e.g., via eculizumab) is significant and requires a structured vaccination and prophylaxis strategy, particularly in the context of susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria such as *Neisseria meningitidis* and *Capnocytophaga* spp.

In the patient described, eculizumab was initiated only after bacteremia was confirmed negative to avoid exacerbating sepsis. However, long-term safety mandates prophylactic measures. According to UK and international case standards, patients receiving eculizumab must be vaccinated for meningococcal disease. Specifically, the case employed valences **Menveo (ACWY)** and **Bexsero (Group B)**. Ideally, these vaccines should be administered **at least two weeks prior** to the start of complement blockade to ensure seroconversion before therapeutic supplementation of terminal complement is discontinued.

If vaccination protocols are not established within this two-week window prior to induction, **antibiotic prophylaxis** is mandatory. In the case report, this was achieved with **oral apoxycillin (Apocillin)**. Prophylaxis should be continued for patients on eculizumab who are not fully protected, serving as a critical safety net against mucosal pathogens like *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. This dual strategy—ensuring vaccination timing allows for immune prevention and using prophylaxis to bridge susceptible periods—mitigates the highest risks of fatal infection associated with complement blockade.

### [Clinical Pearls] Based on the study outcomes, what factor is more predictive of favorable renal recovery than long-term eculizumab maintenance alone: time to initiation of therapy or continuation duration?

### Clinical Teaching Point

Based on the case outcomes and the authors' final conclusions, **time to initiation of eculizumab therapy** is the more significant predictor of favorable renal recovery than the duration of long-term maintenance alone.

In the case presentation, the authors explicitly state in the "Conclusion and broader implications" section that "time-to-initiation of eculizumab therapy are the main determinants of prognosis." They reaffirm this by asserting that "Early recognition of CM-HUS and prompt initiation of complement blockade are the most important predictors of favourable outcome." This principle is reflected in the patient's trajectory: although she achieved haematological remission with treatment, her kidney function remained reduced (serum creatine 175 µmol/L) despite the administration of maintenance eculizumab for four months.

Clinically, this underscores that while long-term maintenance prevents relapse, it cannot repair endothelial injury that occurred during a delay in therapy. As noted in the "Therapeutic strategy" section, plasma exchange provided "only temporary benefit," whereas sustained recovery was achieved only "after complement blockade." In the context of complement-mediated TMA, early blockade halts the generation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) and prevents irreversible tubular and glomerular necrosis. Therefore, early complementary management is the critical intervention to optimize renal sparing, with the duration of therapy serving a secondary, supportive role contingent on the functional status achieved at the time of induction.

### Key Takeaway
In complement activation diseases such as CM-HUS, prioritize the **timing of therapeutic initiation** (eculizumab) to salvage renal function; optimize maintenance dosing only after the underlying endothelial injury has stabilized.
