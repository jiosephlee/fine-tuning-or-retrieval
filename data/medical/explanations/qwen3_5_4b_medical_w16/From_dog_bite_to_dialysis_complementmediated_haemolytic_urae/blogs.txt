Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Zoo Bite, No Anti-Biotics: *Capnocytophaga* and the Rise of CM-HUS

## The Missed Prophylaxis Trap

There is a specific narrative thread that makes this case report stick in the mind of any clinician: the missed tablet. A previously healthy woman in her late 40s was bitten by her dog on the upper lip. The instructions were clear: pick up the prescription. The reality was complicated navigation or simply a gap in the supply chain. She received prophylactic penicillin apocillin on the day of the incident but was unable to collect it. We barely appreciate that time delay from the bite to admission, but that unmonetized delay is often where the difference between a minor wound and fulminant sepsis lies.

This patient epitomizes that "silent period." She developed a high fever, malaise, and later signs of decreased perfusion to her feet. When she arrived, she was septic, not just with fever, but with hypoglycemia that was refractory to both oral glucose and glucagon. Her blood work screamed septic shock—high lactate, DIC markers, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI). At this stage, with a qSOFA score of 1/3, the clinical impression was dominated by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. It was a textbook example of the organism’s ability to cause immunocompromised hosts to suffer septic shock, even though this patient was immunocompetent. But we should pause here for a lesson: in zoonotic bites, *C. canimorsus* isn't just less common than human-to-human *Staph*, it is a premier ticking clock.

## From Sepsis to Thrombotic Microangiopathy

The diagnostic curve of this case is the real shocker. For eight days, her condition worsened, then she hit a ceiling. If we think of sepsis as a wave, the patient hit a high tide of DIC and acidosis, but something began to shift her path. The clinical team noted a sudden isolated drop in hemoglobin without bleeding, followed by persistent thrombocytopenia and rising lactate. The labs revealed schistocytes on the smear. Suddenly, the picture wasn't just sepsis; it was microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA).

This transformation highlights a common pitfall in TMA (Thrombotic Microangiopathy) workups during sepsis. Early on, the markers for TMA—like elevated LDH and low haptoglobin—are completely obscured by the cytokine storm of sepsis. The distinction between DIC secondary to infection and Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS) triggered by the infection is critical yet elusive. The patient had developed schistocytosis, but the DAT (Direct Antiglobulin Test) was initially positive for IgG. Without a repeat test or understanding of the false-positive drivers (often antibiotics or transfusions), one might jump to autoimmune hemolysis. This case shows exactly why flow cytometry and a repeat DAT are necessary. The complement factor H levels were normal on extended testing, but functional alternative pathway activity was low (6%), suggesting active regulatory failure rather than genetic predisposition.

## The Complement Conundrum

Diagnosing CM-HUS as the "second hit" is the turning point for therapy. The ADAMTS13 activity was normal (43.5%), ruling out TTP. Shiga toxin PCR was negative, ruling out STEC-HUS. The patient’s diagnosis was infection-triggered CM-HUS. There is a specific rationale here for waiting to start complement blockade in cases of C. canimorsus nowadays. Eculizumab targets C5, preventing terminal complement activation. However, terminal complement is also crucial for killing encapsulated bacteria. In this patient, eculizumab was initiated only after blood cultures became negative. This is a delicate safety balance, mirroring general guidelines for C5 inhibitors but allowing a specific risk profile for *Capnocytophaga*.

The patient also received meningococcal vaccines before initiating eculizumab (2 weeks prior), ensuring her immune system had higher antibody levels against the organism. We must note that renal recovery in infection-triggered HUS often correlates with early intervention. The patient only achieved hemological remission and partial renal recovery after the combination of plasma exchange and eculizumab. Delayed initiation or incorrect diagnosis can send a patient straight to end-stage kidney disease.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Don't ignore the bite prophylaxis:** Though prophylaxis reduces mortality, missing it doesn't guarantee a benign outcome; *C. canimorsus* is a hyper-virulent organism in some isolates that can rapidly progress to neurologic and metabolic collapse.
*   **Sepsis and TMA are often co-morbid:** Do not assume DIC covers all MS symptoms. In the absence of active blood culture positive sepsis, persistent MAHA and AKI signal a superimposed thrombotic process.
*   **Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM-HUS:** While C3 and C4 levels are often normal in acquired CM-HUS, functional assays like AH50 can reveal the discrepancy, but they should not delay treatment during clinical susceptibility.
*   **EC Reality Check:** Complement inhibitors are highly effective for renal outcomes but carry a high infection risk in active bacteremia. De-escalate early antibiotic use and monitor WBC counts closely.

This case is a powerful reminder of the complexity of diagnosing immune-complex mediated diseases when they follow a life-threatening septic event.



# ADAMTS13 on the Line: Distinguishing TTP from Atypical HUS in Sepsis

A dog bite should generally result in a few days of文件精神 treating a wound, not three weeks of ICU stays for end-stage kidney disease. That’s the lesson you take from most *Capnocytophaga* case reports. But when the presentation is fulminant sepsis, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, the differential tears apart.

The diagnosis of Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) vs. Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS) often becomes a life-or-death decision in the sepsis/MUA overlap. How do you differentiate TTP from HUS when you’re treating a patient in septic shock and their ADAMTS13 levels don't show <10% as expected in TTP?
As seen in this case report from *Tobias Evason Fredriksen et al.*, relying solely on a "sepsis and DIC" label can lead to dangerous delays. The jump to a TMA diagnosis requires a different diagnostic lens.

## The Clinical Blind Spot: Sepsis vs. TMA

The patient in this case presented with a high fever, hypoglycemia, and coagulopathy four days post-dog bite. By all standard metrics, she was treated as classic *Capnocytophaga* sepsis. However, roughly 8 days into the hospital course, her picture shifted.

The patient initially presented with signs of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) — pancytopenia, elevated lactate, and coagulopathy. This is standard for *Capnocytophaga*. Yet, she still developed the classic triad for TMA: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI).

*   **MAHA is not typical for early sepsis-DIC.** Generally, sepsis-DIC is dominated by fibrinogen consumption and bleeding without the structural damage of RBC shear forces.
*   **Normalizing she remained anuric.** This dormant phase of sepsis is critical.

As the case details, the patient was initially stable in her response to antibiotics, but she then developed diffuse skin bleeding and digital ischaemic changes. This microthrombotic phenomenon in the context of DIC should signal to the team to consider the renal decline. Often, clinicians focus on the lactate and pancytopenia, missing the schistocytes on the smear that were emerging from the post-sepsis phase.

## The Diagnostic Pivot: ADAMTS13 and the Complement Trap

The key to distinguishing TTP from CM-HUS in a complex sepsis case comes down to one number: ADAMTS13 activity.

In the provided case, the patient’s ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% early in the course. In TTP, activity is almost always <10%. However, ADAMTS13 can be suppressed in sepsis even if it's not TTP, and initially, this can be confusing.

*   **The PEAL score matters.** The PLASMIC score (discussed in the case) helps triage TTP probability. If ADAMTS13 is normal (>40%), and the history is sepsis-associated, your TB score is a TTP risk factor.
*   **STEC-HUS is the other rule-out.** Simple stool PCR for Shiga toxin provides a crucial negative for STEC-HUS. If the stool is negative, and ADAMTS13 is normal, you lean toward CM-HUS.

The pivotal moment in this case was the realization that the DAT (Coombs test) was a false positive, possibly due to prior transfusions or antibiotics, yet the schistocytes and low haptoglobin were undeniable. The renal biopsy confirmed TMA with fibrin thrombi and crescent formation but *no* evidence of immune complex deposition, supporting an infectious trigger over a primary glomerulopathy.

## Therapeutic Strategy: Bridging to Eculizumab

Once the diagnosis is CM-HUS, the immediate question is management. The case highlights a critical therapeutic dilemma: balance treating the sepsis with the risk of blocking terminal complement activity, which could weaken clearance of encapsulated bacteria.

The patient received three sessions of plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridging therapy. PEX provides transient stabilization in HUS/C5 inhibitors but doesn't correct underlying dysfunction. However, waiting to enter the bridge without preventing infection can be fatal.

*   **Timing is everything.** Do not initiate Eculizumab during active bacteraemia. It raises the risk of uncontrolled infection.
*   **Vaccination is key.** In the case, meningococcal vaccines were given *after* the first dose of Eculizumab induction, reducing the risk of invasive meningococcal disease.
*   **Prophylaxis.** Continue Apocillin (gentamicin/Penicillin) until infection is cleared to prevent breakthrough.

The patient responded well to Eculizumab, achieving haematological remission, though renal function remained reduced (partial recovery). The absence of genetic complement variants points to this as an infection-triggered acute CM-HUS.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Ruling out TTP:** ADAMTS13 levels >10% generally rule out TTP in adults with TMA. Rely on quantitative testing rather than rules of thumb.
*   **Sepsis is a trigger:** Don't ignore TMA development post-sepsis. *Capnocytophaga* is a rare but documented trigger for CM-HUS in immunocompetent hosts.
*   **Clamping down on complement:** Early Eculizumab is the main predictor of renal recovery. However, timing is critical relative to infection.
*   **PCR vs. Culture:** For zoonotic pathogens, molecular methods like real-time nanopore sequencing might be useful for faster diagnosis when cultures lag.



## Twisted Complement Pathways: Why C3 and C4 are Normal

There is a specific type of medical irony that strikes an EM physician just the right way when they treat a patient with a normal dialysis shadow. Imagine a patient treated for sepsis, then dialysis, with a diagnosis of Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS). Your first thought should be, "What pathogen? Shiga toxin?" But then you check the labs. The standard blood work shows everything *normal*. Standard C3 and C4 levels are within reference ranges. You look at the full complement panel. Nothing looks wrong. You hesitate. "Wait, is this real HUS?" If you stop looking here, you miss the boat entirely.

This exact dilemma was faced by our patient, a woman in her late 40s. She presented with a dog bite four days prior, followed by rapid deterioration into septic shock and acute kidney injury (AKI). She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), pancytopenia, and eventually became dialysis-dependent. By the time the picture shifted from "septic shock" to "thrombotic microangiopathy" (TMA), standard serum screening for Complements was bizarrely reassuring. In adult-onset TMA, particularly cases of CM-HUS (Complement-Mediated HUS), C3 and C4 levels are often abnormal, typically showing consumption. But they are not *always* the key to unlocking the diagnosis, and relying on them is a cardinal mistake.

This case isn't a novel presentation of Shiga toxin nephropathy, which is definitely not the answer. This is a case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia acting as the "trigger" for a rare, acquired complement-mediated TMA. But the real learning opportunity here isn't the infection itself; it is the laboratory gap between the clinical reality of a haemolytic crisis and the serological data that was superseded.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Why Standard Labs Fail in Acquired CM-HUS

If you are a clinician working in the ER or ICU who comes across a TMA patient, your refleciton is likely "ADAMTS13, kidney biopsy, sepsis." In the case above, this workflow was actually followed, but a specific lab result caused a delay in the recognition of the underlying pathophysiology. The patient had severe schistocytosis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and undetectable haptoglobin. This screams TMA. Yet, when functional complement assays were unavailable, the standard C3/C4 were normal.

This is because complement testing in acquired TMA can be notoriously misleading. The consumption of C3 specifically within endothelial microvessels doesn't always deplete the serum levels enough to cross the diagnostic threshold. Furthermore, the Alternative Pathway, which is central to complement-mediated HUS, can exhibit consumption dynamics where C3 levels remain stable despite significant downstream activation. The patient's functional assay on the Alternative Pathway (AH50) measured at 6%, which was markedly reduced compared to the serology. This confirms that one dimension of the system is critically compromised even while the serum estimates of total C3/C4 are slightly preserved.

Clinical reasoning is the filter here. If you rely on C3 and C4 alone to rule out TMA, you risk missing a treatable condition. The famous 45% of cases in HUS are acquired rather than inherited, and the clinical picture of MAHA and thrombocytopenia is the strongest predictor of the diagnosis, not the complement levels. The patient in this report showed normal complement levels initially, which delayed the necessary complement blockade, though it was not fatal.

## The Trigger and Timing: Eculizumab in Sepsis

Once the diagnosis of CM-HUS was solidified by the biopsy (showing Fibrin Thrombi in glomeruli) and the exclusion of STEC-negative HUS and TTP, the question became: What now? The patient had cleared the *Capnocytophaga* track, and Eculizumab was initiated. This brings us to a critical therapeutic decision point that is relevant for all TMA cases: is it safe to give a complement inhibitor during active infection?

Technically, we anthropomodulate a machine with a terminal pathway inhibitor and the immune system, and it can go haywire. The patient was vaccinated with meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero) to prevent potential meningococcal or Pneumococcal re-infection post-eclizumab. This is a crucial safeguard because terminal complement blockers strip out the essential chemotactic ability to kill encapsulated bacteria. In this specific case, the initial bacterial load was cleared first with broad-spectrum antibiotics, then was de-escalated once positive for the pathogen.

The logic is simple: You don't need to know the genetic variants to save a life. This patient had no pathogenic variants identified in the genetic testing (pending), yet she responded perfectly to the therapy. The decision to initiate complement blockade when the bacterial counts turned negative minimized the risk of overwhelming sepsis from another pathogen. The lesson here is striking for the TMA board: use early evaluation of the patient's clinical course to decide on treatment initiation. Normal lab values don't mean "No disease" or "No treatment."

## Pathophysiology and the "Second Hit" Theory

Here is the clincher. In acquired CM-HUS, you generally need two things to happen: 1) a trigger and 2) a susceptible host. In this case, the trigger (the dog bite and *C. canimorsus*) was clear. The host susceptibility is the grey area. Even though no genetic variants were confirmed in this patient, the Alternative Pathway activity was low. This suggests a temporal dysregulation rather than a genetic defect. Many patients with acquired CM-HUS do have normal complement genes, but their regulatory proteins are suppressed by the immune response or the infection itself.

This is a high-yield clinical pearl for the complement clinic. You do not need to search for Factor H or B genotypes to start treatment. Infection-triggered TMA should be treated early with the same urgency as Hereditary Angioedema. Delaying treatment until the genetic report comes back is a dangerous game. This patient saw renal function improve only after the eculizumab was started. Waiting for the full complement workup meant missing the window of early stabilization.

So, what do you look at when C3 and C4 are normal? You look at functional assays, you look at the ADAMTS13, you look at the clinical course of sepsis combined with TMA, and you look at the biopsy. In this case, those the same old TMA workups were the only facts that mattered.

**Take-home points**

*   **Normal C3/C4 do not rule out CM-HUS:** Standard complement panels are unreliable in acute TMA表現; functional assays (AH50, C5b-9) are more sensitive for alternative pathway activation.
*   **Infection-triggered TMA:** *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a documented but rare trigger for complement-mediated TMA in immunocompetent patients.
*   **Genetic testing is not mandatory for treatment:** Initiate complement blockade based on clinical TMA features (MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI) and supportive culture status before genetic reports are available.
*   **Diagnostic Exclusion:** Always rule out STEC-HUS and TTP via stool PCR, ADAMTS13 activity, and coagulation profiles before committing to eculizumab.
*   **Clinical Trajectory Matters:** The patient in this case had a delayed diagnosis due to normal serum C3/C4, but achieving haemological remission was possible with early complement blockade once infection was controlled.



## When to Block the Complement: Risks of Eculizumab in Active Infection

Think of eculizumab as a double-edged sword. On one side, it halts the lethal microvascular damage of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). On the other, it removes the very component of the immune system needed to kill encapsulated bacteria. It is a therapeutic paradox we have to navigate carefully.

Consider the case of a 40+ year-old woman bitten by her dog four days prior to arrival. She presented with septic shock caused by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. After initial improvement with antibiotics and supportive care, she developed an unexpected isolated haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI). The biopsy confirmed a Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA). With STEC-HUS and TTP ruled out via stool PCR and ADAMTS13 activity (43.5%), the diagnosis was Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

The critical decision point emerged when the team considered eculizumab therapy. The case highlights a fundamental dilemma in critical care management: does the risk of uncontrolled sepsis outweigh the immediate threat of rapid renal and neurological progression to fulminant sepsis or meningococcemia?

In this patient, the decision wasn't made because the infection was a non-concern, but because the infection was actively killing the host. When discussing the therapeutic strategy, the authors note that while plasma exchange (PEX) was useful as bridging therapy, it only provides transient stabilization. Sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade.

### The Dilemma of Terminal Complement Defects

In solid biology, the terminal complement components (C5b to C9) are essential for membrane attack on opsonized targets, including *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. However, this is also the pathway that drives endothelial damage in CM-HUS. By blocking C5, eculizumab prevents the generation of MAC (Membrane Attack Complex). In the context of active sepsis, there is an increased susceptibility to life-threatening invasive infections, particularly meningococcemia.

The authors specifically addressed this by initiating eculizumab only after blood cultures became negative. They also emphasized that this decision is critical not just for *C. canimorsus* (as seen in the case) but particularly for other encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria meningitidis*.

While Junyon et al (2021) and others have reported infections associated with eculizumab, the risk is often higher when the therapeutic window is narrow. For CM-HUS patients, there is often a pre-existing genetic susceptibility or a *capitaz* (active complement regulatory defect). However, in this report, the patient was immunocompetent with no identified genetic variants, suggesting the infection itself acted as a trigger for the dysregulation rather than a primary genetic disease.

### The Solution in Context

How did they get around it?

The case offers a blueprint for balancing infection risk with TMA management.

1.  **Contact the Microbiology:** Obtain a definitive culture before permanent inhibitor dose. If you are treating for CM-HUS in sepsis, wait until the microbiological evidence of the pathogen is resolved or suppressed as much as possible.
2.  **Bridge Care is Crucial:** The patient received PEX sessions for 8 days prior to eculizumab. This helps manage inflammatory load temporarily but does not fix the underlying genetic or acquired complement dysregulation.
3.  **Vaccination Strategy:** The patient received meningococcal vaccines (Menveo, Bexsero) before maintaining Eculizumab without an immediate infection. Vaccination is safety-driven in Eculizumab patients, and close monitoring of vaccine response is essential.

### Diagnostic Pearls & Caveats

1.  **Normal Complement Does Not Exclude CM-HUS.**
    Standard testing often returns normal C3/C4 in CM-HUS during the disease course. Do not rule out "Atypical HUS" based solely on normal C3/C4 assays in the presence of MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI. The functional alternative pathway assays (AH50) were only run after the initial PEX sessions to differentiate from acute depletion due to the therapy.

2.  **TMA Timing Suggests CM-HUS.**
    The patient developed haemolysis and thrombocytopenia *after* the initial sepsis whooping improvement. This chronological delay supports infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a primary pre-existing genetic defect.

3.  **Differential is Driven by Labs.**
    The PLASMIC score and ADAMTS13 activity are essential. ADAMTS13 >10% rules out TTP. The patient's score of 11-12 (symptomatic) suggests a strongly low likelihood for TTP.

4.  **Prophylaxis and Antibiotics.**
    Once the primary infection is managed, prophylactic antibiotics (like Apocillin/Penicillin) must continue until vaccination and subsequent clearance of the risk factors are established.

## Key Takeaways

*   **Bacteria Blockers:** Eculizumab is essential for CM-HUS, but delaying initiation in active sepsis is a safety mechanism for encapsulated bacteria.
*   **The Terminology:** CHUS is often "Atypical," but the pathology is always C5 inhibition.
*   **Infection Risk:** Infection-triggered CM-HUS should be managed with complement inhibitors only after the triggering pathogen is controlled.
*   **Renal Recovery:** Time to first eculizumab dose is a strong predictor of renal recovery.
*   **Diagnostic Exclusion:** CM-HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion; normal complement levels (C3/C4) do not exclude the diagnosis during the acute phase.



# The Delayed Hemolysis Trap: Identifying Complement-Mediated TMA After Sepsis

## From Sepsis Stability to TMA Unmasking

In our experience, sepsis is all-or-nothing. Progression to shock means trouble. Rapid بهبود recovery (improvement) usually points to differentiation. But consider this case where the clinical picture wasn't linear. We have a patient who stabilized from septic shock, her inflammatory markers were things to get better, but then, something else kicked in. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* wasn't the end of the story.

We present a case where the initial management focused entirely on the infection and the resulting DIC. The sepsis cleared, the organ failure did not, and the patient actually improved—only to develop a new, sinister physiological problem that was unrelated to the active septic drive itself. As the sepsis resolved, the endothelial injury revealed itself.

This "delayed hemolysis" phenomenon is crucial. It teaches us to not rest on our lats when a sepsis patient improves clinically. It forces us to re-read the labs with fresh eyes. In this instance, the patient was initially lethargic and hypotensive from the dog bite, then became completely anuric and anemic. But the real diagnostic pivot point was when, after 8 days of ICU care, she was clinically stable, yet her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 to 68 g/L.

Interestingly, the hemolytic markers didn't correlate with the "worsening" of the sepsis. Sepsis markers were falling. The hemolysis appeared to be the result of uncontrolled complement activation as the immune response phases started to wind down. This dissociation often points toward a post-septic superimposition like CM-HUS rather than a complication of the bacterial invasion itself.

## Why the Diagnosis Was a Puzzle

For the first 8 days of the case, the differential diagnosis was dominated by the "bucket" of broad sepsis syndromes. We had disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), AKI, and thrombocytopenia.

The key to this case wasn't the sepsis itself—it was how the sepsis resolved.

*   **Ruling out Leukemia:** Bone marrow was normal initially.
*   **Ruling out Infections:** Blood cultures fixed *C. canimorsus* but didn't persist as a continuous source of new sepsis once stabilized.
*   **The TTP Question:** ADAMTS13 was high (43.5%), ruling out TTP. In this case, the function of eculizumab and plasma exchange showed why PEX alone wasn't enough.

Two lab tests proved particularly deceptive (in a positive way). The Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) was initially positive, raising suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). However, the patient received multiple transfusions and had antibiotic exposure (Penicillin). This likely caused a false-positive DAT. Once the DAT was repeated 48-72 hours later and negated, we were left with the classic signs of TMA: schistocytes on the smear, undetectable haptoglobin, and rising LDH, happening in the presence of resolving sepsis.

Also, the initial complement workup (C3/C4) was normal. This is a critical pearl to share. In complement-mediated TMA, basic levels (C3/C4) frequently normalize during the acute phase because the system is transiently over-anched or activated systemically.

## The Therapeutic Dilemma: "Is the Infection Active?"

When we started the second phase of therapy, it wasn't just about the damage it had caused—it was that it was still dangerous. We started plasma exchange (PEX) to bridge to eculizumab. PEX worked transiently, bringing diuresis back, but then the hemolysis continued. The definitive treatment that fixed the "hemolysis" was eculizumab.

Eculizumab blocks C5. You might think, "Wait, if we block the complement, can't the bacteria run wild?" You're right.

*   **Risk Mitigation:** We only started Eculizumab after the *C. canimorsus* blood cultures were negative.
*   **Vaccination:** Meningococcal vaccination was required for immune-proofing in the context of eculizumab therapy.
*   **Outcome:** The patient recovered hematologically and partially recovered renal function.

This highlights the fragility of the protocol. Eculizumab is the game changer for CM-HUS, but the timing is everything. Interestingly, she did not have a known genetic complement deficiency (atypical replacement later tests were negative). This was triggered by the infection—the external "second hit" on the complement system.

## Summary

This case reports a scenario where TMA appears *after* the sepsis, not during. The clinical takeaway is that an improving patient is not a resolved patient. Hemolysis is often the next wave of storm.

*   **Hemolysis May Precede the Disease:** In CM-HUS, MAHA can present after sepsis control or recovery, not during the peak septic shock.
*   **False Positives Must Be Checked:** Use repeated DAT testing to avoid false autoimmune diagnoses in infected patients. ADAMTS13 levels rule out TTP in <10% context.
*   **Renal Biopsy is Key:** In septic AKI with schistocytes, rule out STEC-HUS (negative stool PCR) but confirm with TMA biopsy features.
*   **Think "Second Hit":** Infection can trigger CM-HUS even without known genetic predisposition.

If you have a patient recovering from septic shock with persistent AKI and platelets dropping, look closer at the reticulocytes. It's not just inflammation anymore; it's the end of the complement activation storm.
