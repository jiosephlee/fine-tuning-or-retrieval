Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## When to Block the Complement: Risks of Eculizumab in Active Infection

Think of eculizumab as a double-edged sword. On one side, it halts the lethal microvascular damage of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). On the other, it removes the very component of the immune system needed to kill encapsulated bacteria. It is a therapeutic paradox we have to navigate carefully.

Consider the case of a 40+ year-old woman bitten by her dog four days prior to arrival. She presented with septic shock caused by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. After initial improvement with antibiotics and supportive care, she developed an unexpected isolated haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI). The biopsy confirmed a Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA). With STEC-HUS and TTP ruled out via stool PCR and ADAMTS13 activity (43.5%), the diagnosis was Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

The critical decision point emerged when the team considered eculizumab therapy. The case highlights a fundamental dilemma in critical care management: does the risk of uncontrolled sepsis outweigh the immediate threat of rapid renal and neurological progression to fulminant sepsis or meningococcemia?

In this patient, the decision wasn't made because the infection was a non-concern, but because the infection was actively killing the host. When discussing the therapeutic strategy, the authors note that while plasma exchange (PEX) was useful as bridging therapy, it only provides transient stabilization. Sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade.

### The Dilemma of Terminal Complement Defects

In solid biology, the terminal complement components (C5b to C9) are essential for membrane attack on opsonized targets, including *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. However, this is also the pathway that drives endothelial damage in CM-HUS. By blocking C5, eculizumab prevents the generation of MAC (Membrane Attack Complex). In the context of active sepsis, there is an increased susceptibility to life-threatening invasive infections, particularly meningococcemia.

The authors specifically addressed this by initiating eculizumab only after blood cultures became negative. They also emphasized that this decision is critical not just for *C. canimorsus* (as seen in the case) but particularly for other encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria meningitidis*.

While Junyon et al (2021) and others have reported infections associated with eculizumab, the risk is often higher when the therapeutic window is narrow. For CM-HUS patients, there is often a pre-existing genetic susceptibility or a *capitaz* (active complement regulatory defect). However, in this report, the patient was immunocompetent with no identified genetic variants, suggesting the infection itself acted as a trigger for the dysregulation rather than a primary genetic disease.

### The Solution in Context

How did they get around it?

The case offers a blueprint for balancing infection risk with TMA management.

1.  **Contact the Microbiology:** Obtain a definitive culture before permanent inhibitor dose. If you are treating for CM-HUS in sepsis, wait until the microbiological evidence of the pathogen is resolved or suppressed as much as possible.
2.  **Bridge Care is Crucial:** The patient received PEX sessions for 8 days prior to eculizumab. This helps manage inflammatory load temporarily but does not fix the underlying genetic or acquired complement dysregulation.
3.  **Vaccination Strategy:** The patient received meningococcal vaccines (Menveo, Bexsero) before maintaining Eculizumab without an immediate infection. Vaccination is safety-driven in Eculizumab patients, and close monitoring of vaccine response is essential.

### Diagnostic Pearls & Caveats

1.  **Normal Complement Does Not Exclude CM-HUS.**
    Standard testing often returns normal C3/C4 in CM-HUS during the disease course. Do not rule out "Atypical HUS" based solely on normal C3/C4 assays in the presence of MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI. The functional alternative pathway assays (AH50) were only run after the initial PEX sessions to differentiate from acute depletion due to the therapy.

2.  **TMA Timing Suggests CM-HUS.**
    The patient developed haemolysis and thrombocytopenia *after* the initial sepsis whooping improvement. This chronological delay supports infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than a primary pre-existing genetic defect.

3.  **Differential is Driven by Labs.**
    The PLASMIC score and ADAMTS13 activity are essential. ADAMTS13 >10% rules out TTP. The patient's score of 11-12 (symptomatic) suggests a strongly low likelihood for TTP.

4.  **Prophylaxis and Antibiotics.**
    Once the primary infection is managed, prophylactic antibiotics (like Apocillin/Penicillin) must continue until vaccination and subsequent clearance of the risk factors are established.

## Key Takeaways

*   **Bacteria Blockers:** Eculizumab is essential for CM-HUS, but delaying initiation in active sepsis is a safety mechanism for encapsulated bacteria.
*   **The Terminology:** CHUS is often "Atypical," but the pathology is always C5 inhibition.
*   **Infection Risk:** Infection-triggered CM-HUS should be managed with complement inhibitors only after the triggering pathogen is controlled.
*   **Renal Recovery:** Time to first eculizumab dose is a strong predictor of renal recovery.
*   **Diagnostic Exclusion:** CM-HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion; normal complement levels (C3/C4) do not exclude the diagnosis during the acute phase.
