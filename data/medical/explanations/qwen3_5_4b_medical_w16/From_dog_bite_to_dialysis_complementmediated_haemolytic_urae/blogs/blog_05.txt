Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# The Delayed Hemolysis Trap: Identifying Complement-Mediated TMA After Sepsis

## From Sepsis Stability to TMA Unmasking

In our experience, sepsis is all-or-nothing. Progression to shock means trouble. Rapid بهبود recovery (improvement) usually points to differentiation. But consider this case where the clinical picture wasn't linear. We have a patient who stabilized from septic shock, her inflammatory markers were things to get better, but then, something else kicked in. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* wasn't the end of the story.

We present a case where the initial management focused entirely on the infection and the resulting DIC. The sepsis cleared, the organ failure did not, and the patient actually improved—only to develop a new, sinister physiological problem that was unrelated to the active septic drive itself. As the sepsis resolved, the endothelial injury revealed itself.

This "delayed hemolysis" phenomenon is crucial. It teaches us to not rest on our lats when a sepsis patient improves clinically. It forces us to re-read the labs with fresh eyes. In this instance, the patient was initially lethargic and hypotensive from the dog bite, then became completely anuric and anemic. But the real diagnostic pivot point was when, after 8 days of ICU care, she was clinically stable, yet her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 to 68 g/L.

Interestingly, the hemolytic markers didn't correlate with the "worsening" of the sepsis. Sepsis markers were falling. The hemolysis appeared to be the result of uncontrolled complement activation as the immune response phases started to wind down. This dissociation often points toward a post-septic superimposition like CM-HUS rather than a complication of the bacterial invasion itself.

## Why the Diagnosis Was a Puzzle

For the first 8 days of the case, the differential diagnosis was dominated by the "bucket" of broad sepsis syndromes. We had disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), AKI, and thrombocytopenia.

The key to this case wasn't the sepsis itself—it was how the sepsis resolved.

*   **Ruling out Leukemia:** Bone marrow was normal initially.
*   **Ruling out Infections:** Blood cultures fixed *C. canimorsus* but didn't persist as a continuous source of new sepsis once stabilized.
*   **The TTP Question:** ADAMTS13 was high (43.5%), ruling out TTP. In this case, the function of eculizumab and plasma exchange showed why PEX alone wasn't enough.

Two lab tests proved particularly deceptive (in a positive way). The Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) was initially positive, raising suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). However, the patient received multiple transfusions and had antibiotic exposure (Penicillin). This likely caused a false-positive DAT. Once the DAT was repeated 48-72 hours later and negated, we were left with the classic signs of TMA: schistocytes on the smear, undetectable haptoglobin, and rising LDH, happening in the presence of resolving sepsis.

Also, the initial complement workup (C3/C4) was normal. This is a critical pearl to share. In complement-mediated TMA, basic levels (C3/C4) frequently normalize during the acute phase because the system is transiently over-anched or activated systemically.

## The Therapeutic Dilemma: "Is the Infection Active?"

When we started the second phase of therapy, it wasn't just about the damage it had caused—it was that it was still dangerous. We started plasma exchange (PEX) to bridge to eculizumab. PEX worked transiently, bringing diuresis back, but then the hemolysis continued. The definitive treatment that fixed the "hemolysis" was eculizumab.

Eculizumab blocks C5. You might think, "Wait, if we block the complement, can't the bacteria run wild?" You're right.

*   **Risk Mitigation:** We only started Eculizumab after the *C. canimorsus* blood cultures were negative.
*   **Vaccination:** Meningococcal vaccination was required for immune-proofing in the context of eculizumab therapy.
*   **Outcome:** The patient recovered hematologically and partially recovered renal function.

This highlights the fragility of the protocol. Eculizumab is the game changer for CM-HUS, but the timing is everything. Interestingly, she did not have a known genetic complement deficiency (atypical replacement later tests were negative). This was triggered by the infection—the external "second hit" on the complement system.

## Summary

This case reports a scenario where TMA appears *after* the sepsis, not during. The clinical takeaway is that an improving patient is not a resolved patient. Hemolysis is often the next wave of storm.

*   **Hemolysis May Precede the Disease:** In CM-HUS, MAHA can present after sepsis control or recovery, not during the peak septic shock.
*   **False Positives Must Be Checked:** Use repeated DAT testing to avoid false autoimmune diagnoses in infected patients. ADAMTS13 levels rule out TTP in <10% context.
*   **Renal Biopsy is Key:** In septic AKI with schistocytes, rule out STEC-HUS (negative stool PCR) but confirm with TMA biopsy features.
*   **Think "Second Hit":** Infection can trigger CM-HUS even without known genetic predisposition.

If you have a patient recovering from septic shock with persistent AKI and platelets dropping, look closer at the reticulocytes. It's not just inflammation anymore; it's the end of the complement activation storm.
