Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# The Imaging War: CT versus MRCP in Pancreatopleural Fistula

When a child arrives in the intensive care unit with recurrent pleural effusions, the first diagnosis that springs to mind is almost always pulmonary. Think of TB, effusion, empyema, or even malignancy in a young boy. This case report presents a particularly "cunning" presentation of Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF). The patient presented with respiratory distress and right-sided chest pain, only to reveal later that the pathology—a fistula connecting the pancreas to the pleural space—was hiding behind a "pulmonary mask."

## The Diagnostic Pitfall: TB vs. Pancreas

The difficulty of this case wasn't just the rare pathology; it was the traumatic nature of the diagnosis process. The history suggested a child with access to trauma (bicycle handle), but then there was the family history of Tuberculosis ("Koch's contact"). When a pleural fluid aspirate yields a dark red, blood-stained fluid with slightly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) levels, the instinct is to start empiric antitubercular drugs.

In this instance, the child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit, thoracentesis was performed, and antitubercular therapy was initiated. However, the patient returned within days with the same symptoms. Repeated assessments eventually uncovered the mention of blows to the abdomen from the bike handle. While the history eventually cracked, the clinical workup elucidates why thoracic imaging alone is insufficient for this condition.

The diagnostic signature for a PPF is unmissable if you know what to look for: pleural fluid amylase levels. In this case, the fluid was significantly elevated at 6118 IU/L. However, simply knowing the level is not enough; you need to know where the leak is. This leads us to the imaging war.

## The Imaging Dilemma: CT Scans are Great for Sounding the Alarm, MRCP Serves the Plan

Computed Tomography (CT) is the high-horse of emergency imaging. The patient in the case was getting a CT before an intercostal drain (ICD) was considered. The CT was valuable: it showed the hydropneumothorax, the patchy consolidation, and crucially, a "multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas." It drew the eye to the retroperitoneum.

However, the text clearly highlights a specific limitation: CT reliably shows pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation, but it depicts the *actual fistula* in only 33%–47% of cases. If your initial CT focuses on the chest and lungs, you might see fluid tracking along the triangle of the diaphragm, but you might not see the exact connection to the pancreatic duct.

Enter Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP). This test offered the detailed resolution required to confirm the diagnosis. In the case, MRCP was planned on the day of the CT insertion. It revealed the frank fistula, showing the communication between the distal pancreatic duct and the pleural cavity. It visualized the cystic collection tracking through the oesophageal hiatus superiorly into the right pleural cavity.

If MRCP is not available or the patient is unstable, ERCP can be the gold standard, but the imaging hierarchy here favors the soft tissue resolution of MRCP for ductal anatomy visualization. The statistics suggest that for pediatric trauma cases where the primary symptoms mimic pulmonary ones, relying on CT without cross-referencing a duct-specific exam is a significant risk for underscoring.

## Clinical Pearls from the Case

To summarize the teaching points from this challenging case, consider the following.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Pleural Fluid Amylase:** A pleural fluid amylase level ≥1000 IU/L is the most sensitive indicator of PPF; levels can be extremely high (e.g., >6000 IU/L).
*   **Imaging Sensitivity:** CT scans detect a fistula in only 33%–47% of cases; MRCP offers 70%–80% sensitivity for visualizing the ductal anatomy and fistula tract.
*   **The "Pulmonary Mask":** PPF is a diagnostic challenge because the complaints (pleural effusion, dyspnea) mimic pulmonary causes. Always review abdominal trauma history if amylase is elevated in the effusion.
*   **Diagnostic Hierarchy:** While CT is often the first go-to for stability, MRCP is preferred for confirming the fistula timing and planning therapeutic intervention, especially in pediatric trauma with non-specific tubular findings.
