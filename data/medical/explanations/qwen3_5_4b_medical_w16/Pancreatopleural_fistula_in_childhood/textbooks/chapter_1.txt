Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 1: ## Initial Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Ambiguity

The initial recognition of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in a pediatric context is frequently complicated by the "Pulmonary Mask" phenomenon, where the pathology resides abdominally but manifests as thoracic distress. In this case, the patient presented with classic signs of obstructive respiratory compromise, including progressive dyspnoea and right-sided chest pain that worsened over one week. Because the clinical presentation is dominated by respiratory distress and respiratory failure, the initial workup necessitates a thorough thoracic evaluation, often leading to the obscuring of abdominal findings. The patient’s physical examination revealed physical signs indistinguishable from massive thoracic pathology, including reduced chest movements on the right, stony dull percussion over the right subscapular and infra-axillary regions, significantly weaker vocal fremitus, and decreased breath sounds. Crucially, the pleural fluid aspirated was dark red, consistent with bloody effusion or hemorrhagic exudate, which can further conflicate the diagnosis with pyogenic empyema or tuberculosis.

Despite initial management with oxygen and thoracocentesis, which provided temporary relief of symptomatic tachypnoea and improved oxygen saturation, the recurrence of right-sided chest pain and effusion on subsequent admissions highlighted an unresolved source. Initially, this recurrent fluid accumulation was diagnostic of a thoracic infectious etiology. The diagnostic team was strongly guided toward great ultrasound sensitivity for TB, fueled by a positive family history of Koch’s disease and a slightly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid during the second paradoxical admission. This led to the empirical initiation of antitubercular therapy. However, the continued recurrence of dark fluid and the inability to resolve the effusion despite corticosteroids suggested an alternative vascular or secretory pathway, thereby necessitating a deeper search for an abdominal trigger.

The definitive diagnostic pivot occurred only after a meticulous questioning of the patient during the third admission. Key to the resolution was the unrevealed history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior, which evolved into a source of pancreatic injury. When re-evaluated, the abdominal history combined with imaging findings revealed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal level to the tail of the pancreas. This anatomical connection, ruptured following blunt trauma, allowed pancreatic fluid to bypass the retroperitoneal space initially, moving upward through natural diaphragmatic hiatuses (specifically the oesophageal hiatus) to create a pseudo-meal fistula. This case illustrates the potential for PPF to mimic tuberculosis or pneumonia, emphasizing that in pediatric patients with recurrent hematocele or bloody pleural effusions, a thorough search for occult abdominal trauma and pancreatic ductal injury must not be deferred until the thoracic workup is complete.
