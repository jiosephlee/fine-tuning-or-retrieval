Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Strategy and Differential Diagnosis

The clinical reasoning required to diagnose this case was dominated by the diagnostic paradox of a thoracic presentation with an abdominal etiology. The child presented with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions and respiratory distress, clinical features that initially prompted a search for primary thoracic pathology. A critical diagnostic pitfall in this scenario was the potential presence of tuberculosis. The patient's family history involved a grandfather with pulmonary tuberculosis, and the initial pleural fluid analysis showed a slightly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) concentration. Due to the patient's background and the nature of the effusion, the medical team initially presumed tuberculosis. Consequently, the patient was started on empiric antitubercular drugs. However, this diagnosis was ruled out twice upon repeated reassessment. The first detailed back-to-back review noticed the persistence of respiratory symptoms and fluid re-accumulation despite treatment. Upon repeat admission, the search for tuberculosis was further pursued with a GeneXpert test, which returned negative, definitively excluding the organism. The failure of antitubercular therapy combined with the unaffected abdominal workup shifted the diagnostic focus back to the gastro-intestinal tract, specifically after prompting history from the child revealed blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle that could traumatize the pancreas.

While the initial suspicion leaned toward infectious etiologies, a systematic approach to exclusion was required to avoid diagnostic delays. Empiric antibiotic therapy (piperacillin-tazobactam) was initiated based on clinical judgment for possible bacterial infection, yet specific pleural fluid characteristics contradicted an empyema. Examination of the aspirated fluid confirmed a dark red coloration rather than the yellow or purulent nature typical of empyema. Furthermore, the patient remained afebrile and lacked any systemic signs of neutrophilic invasion or sepsis that would typically accompany a bacterial abscess. Turning to malignancy, which is a serious consideration for hemorrhagic or chronic pleural effusions in children, the evaluation included pleural fluid cytology, which revealed no malignant cells. Additionally, the absence of constitutional symptoms such as weight loss, night sweats, or peripheral smear abnormalities supported the argument against leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic disease. This exhaustive exclusion of the major mimics—tuberculosis, empyema, and malignancy—secured a safe therapeutic pathway to explore the abdominal origin of the breathing difficulty, transforming the case from a seemingly simple pulmonary issue to a complex sysom release requiring targeted investigation.

Imaging played a pivotal role in unraveling the anatomy of the problem, with the modality being the key to distinguishing the specific type of pleural leak. During the initial thoracic workup, a contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was ordered before the insertion of an intercostal drain. This scan demonstrated patchy areas of air space consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas. While the CT provided valuable structural information regarding the presence of a collection and the path of least resistance tracking toward the retroperitoneum, it has well-known limitations in visualizing ductal anatomy compared to other modalities. CT reliably shows pancreatic calcifications or cysts but depicts the actual fistulous tract connecting the pancreas to the pleura in only 33% to 47% of cases in the provided literature. To definitively identify the fistula, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was planned and performed. MRCP offered a superior view of the pancreatic ductal system, visualizing the fistula which had a narrow communication with the left pleural cavity alongside a right-sided tract. This imaging confirmed that the fluid was tracking from a disrupted pancreatic duct through the oesophageal hiatus into the pleural space, explaining the air and fluid dynamics seen as hydropneumothorax on the contrast study.

The final confirmation of the diagnosis relied heavily on biochemical markers within the pleural fluid, serving as a pathognomonic clue that distinguished this condition from other causes of exudative effusion, particularly pancreatic leaks from other origins. While various conditions, including Boerhaave‛s syndrome or malignancy, can result in elevated pleural fluid amylase, the magnitude and source of the enzyme were the critical differentiators. In this specific case, pleural fluid analysis revealed significantly elevated amylase concentrations at 6118 U/L and lipase at 7211 U/L. In the context of pediatric PPF, elevated levels in one or both enzymes are highly significant, often supporting the diagnosis when levels exceed 1000 IU/L or when present in combination. Crucially, the distinction between pancreatic-type amylase and salivary-type amylase determines the sensitivity of this marker. Given the high performance values recorded here, after excluding the prior differential diagnoses, the combination of raised pleural amylase, lipase, and clear imaging evidence of tracking from the pancreas was sufficient to establish the diagnosis of Pancreatopleural Fistula. This allows the clinician to treat the condition rather than the symptom, ensuring that the fluid dynamics are managed without further unnecessary thoracic intervention.
