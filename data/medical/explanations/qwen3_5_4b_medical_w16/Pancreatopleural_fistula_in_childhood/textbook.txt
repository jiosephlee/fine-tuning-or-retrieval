Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Initial Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Ambiguity

The initial recognition of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in a pediatric context is frequently complicated by the "Pulmonary Mask" phenomenon, where the pathology resides abdominally but manifests as thoracic distress. In this case, the patient presented with classic signs of obstructive respiratory compromise, including progressive dyspnoea and right-sided chest pain that worsened over one week. Because the clinical presentation is dominated by respiratory distress and respiratory failure, the initial workup necessitates a thorough thoracic evaluation, often leading to the obscuring of abdominal findings. The patient’s physical examination revealed physical signs indistinguishable from massive thoracic pathology, including reduced chest movements on the right, stony dull percussion over the right subscapular and infra-axillary regions, significantly weaker vocal fremitus, and decreased breath sounds. Crucially, the pleural fluid aspirated was dark red, consistent with bloody effusion or hemorrhagic exudate, which can further conflicate the diagnosis with pyogenic empyema or tuberculosis.

Despite initial management with oxygen and thoracocentesis, which provided temporary relief of symptomatic tachypnoea and improved oxygen saturation, the recurrence of right-sided chest pain and effusion on subsequent admissions highlighted an unresolved source. Initially, this recurrent fluid accumulation was diagnostic of a thoracic infectious etiology. The diagnostic team was strongly guided toward great ultrasound sensitivity for TB, fueled by a positive family history of Koch’s disease and a slightly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid during the second paradoxical admission. This led to the empirical initiation of antitubercular therapy. However, the continued recurrence of dark fluid and the inability to resolve the effusion despite corticosteroids suggested an alternative vascular or secretory pathway, thereby necessitating a deeper search for an abdominal trigger.

The definitive diagnostic pivot occurred only after a meticulous questioning of the patient during the third admission. Key to the resolution was the unrevealed history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior, which evolved into a source of pancreatic injury. When re-evaluated, the abdominal history combined with imaging findings revealed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal level to the tail of the pancreas. This anatomical connection, ruptured following blunt trauma, allowed pancreatic fluid to bypass the retroperitoneal space initially, moving upward through natural diaphragmatic hiatuses (specifically the oesophageal hiatus) to create a pseudo-meal fistula. This case illustrates the potential for PPF to mimic tuberculosis or pneumonia, emphasizing that in pediatric patients with recurrent hematocele or bloody pleural effusions, a thorough search for occult abdominal trauma and pancreatic ductal injury must not be deferred until the thoracic workup is complete.

## Pathophysiology and Etiology of Pediatric Pancreatopleural Fistula

The pancreatic pleural fistula (PPF) presents a compelling diagnostic challenge in pediatrics, often masquerading as a primary thoracic pathology due to its presentation with respiratory distress and recurrent pleural effusions. Understanding the predisposing factors requires distinguishing pediatric etiologies from adult presentations. While in adults, alcoholic chronic pancreatitis accounts for the vast majority of anatomical disruptions leading to PPF, pediatric cases often stem from blunt abdominal trauma or genetic susceptibilities to hereditary pancreatitis. In the case presented, the precipitating event was blunt trauma from a bicycle handle, occurring approximately one month prior to the onset of symptoms. However, the persistence of disease progression implies an underlying inflammatory component, as PPF is frequently a complication of acute or chronic pancreatitis. In children, genetic mutations account for a significant portion of hereditary pancreatitis, with mutations in the PRSS1 gene (R122H and N291 variants), STPS1, CFTR, and chymotrypsin C being primary suspects. This contrasts sharply with the adult demographic and suggests that pediatric practitioners must maintain a high index of suspicion for trauma-induced duct disruption or genetic predisposition, even when signs of active gastric inflammation are not immediately obvious or are concealed.

The anatomical route of fluid influx is a critical pathophysiological concept in understanding the specific presentation of recurrence and gravity of the condition. The stomach and the pleural space are anatomically distinct compartments, yet the pancreas, particularly its posterior body and tail, resides retroperitoneally. Fluid leakage typically occurs when enzymatic exudation propagates from a disrupted pancreatic duct or associated pseudocyst into the retroperitoneal space. From this intra-abdominal sanctuary, fluid tracks the path of least resistance along the fascial planes, specifically passing posteriorly to the oesophageal or aortic hiatuses of the diaphragm rather than traversing the sternocentral diaphragm directly. In this case, a contrast-enhanced CT scan and MRCP visualization confirmed a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal level to the tail of the pancreas, which provided the specific anatomical corridor for the fluid to breach the pleural cavity. This retrograde flow through natural diaphragmatic hiatuses sometimes evaded direct imaging in initial thoracic scans, explaining why diagnostic testing can initially focus entirely on pulmonary pathology before abdominal sources are identified.

The nature of the pleural fluid serves as the primary diagnostic marker distinguishing PPF from other differential diagnoses. The hallmark of PPF is an exudative fluid rich in pancreatic enzymes, specifically amylase and lipase, which can exceed thousands of units per liter, as seen in this patient with levels of 6118 and 7211 U/L respectively. This concentration warrants classification as pathognomonic for pancreatic origin and necessitates further investigation to rule out other sources of elevated amylase such as salivary gland damage or malignancy. Discrimination between pancreatic and salivary isoenzyme activity is crucial because salivary amylase can occasionally leak if the amylase rises from bilateral destruction or due to lung conditions. However, the sheer magnitude of the elevation combined with hypoproteinuria and the history of abdominal trauma points directly to the pathway. The initiation of antitubercular therapy, though initially indicated by elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) and contact history, was notoriously delayed in diagnosis by the misattribution of fluid characteristics to pulmonary tuberculosis, highlighting the diagnostic lag when primary enzymatic markers are missed.

Clinically, the consequences of this sustained leakage determine the patient's trajectory and the urgency of intervention. The continuous fistula acts like a "leaky pipe," preventing spontaneous closure and resulting in recurrent, often massive, hemorrhagic or serous effusions that cause hypoxia and tachypnoea, as observed in multiple admissions. This fluid accumulation typically leads to secondary complications such as patchy lung consolidation and hydropneumothorax, indicative of inflammation and air-fluid interface changes within the pleural cavity. In pediatric patients, the underlying venous obstruction or lymphatic compromise can further deepen the pathology, whereas the case here demonstrated that conservative management was sufficient to allow the duct to seal. Therefore, the pathophysiological goal is not merely to clear the fluid but to cease the enzymatic leakage, explaining the necessity of bowel rest and somatostatin analogues like octreotide which reduce exocrine pancreatic secretion to allow the ductal disruption to heal over time.

## Diagnostic Strategy and Differential Diagnosis

The clinical reasoning required to diagnose this case was dominated by the diagnostic paradox of a thoracic presentation with an abdominal etiology. The child presented with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions and respiratory distress, clinical features that initially prompted a search for primary thoracic pathology. A critical diagnostic pitfall in this scenario was the potential presence of tuberculosis. The patient's family history involved a grandfather with pulmonary tuberculosis, and the initial pleural fluid analysis showed a slightly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) concentration. Due to the patient's background and the nature of the effusion, the medical team initially presumed tuberculosis. Consequently, the patient was started on empiric antitubercular drugs. However, this diagnosis was ruled out twice upon repeated reassessment. The first detailed back-to-back review noticed the persistence of respiratory symptoms and fluid re-accumulation despite treatment. Upon repeat admission, the search for tuberculosis was further pursued with a GeneXpert test, which returned negative, definitively excluding the organism. The failure of antitubercular therapy combined with the unaffected abdominal workup shifted the diagnostic focus back to the gastro-intestinal tract, specifically after prompting history from the child revealed blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle that could traumatize the pancreas.

While the initial suspicion leaned toward infectious etiologies, a systematic approach to exclusion was required to avoid diagnostic delays. Empiric antibiotic therapy (piperacillin-tazobactam) was initiated based on clinical judgment for possible bacterial infection, yet specific pleural fluid characteristics contradicted an empyema. Examination of the aspirated fluid confirmed a dark red coloration rather than the yellow or purulent nature typical of empyema. Furthermore, the patient remained afebrile and lacked any systemic signs of neutrophilic invasion or sepsis that would typically accompany a bacterial abscess. Turning to malignancy, which is a serious consideration for hemorrhagic or chronic pleural effusions in children, the evaluation included pleural fluid cytology, which revealed no malignant cells. Additionally, the absence of constitutional symptoms such as weight loss, night sweats, or peripheral smear abnormalities supported the argument against leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic disease. This exhaustive exclusion of the major mimics—tuberculosis, empyema, and malignancy—secured a safe therapeutic pathway to explore the abdominal origin of the breathing difficulty, transforming the case from a seemingly simple pulmonary issue to a complex sysom release requiring targeted investigation.

Imaging played a pivotal role in unraveling the anatomy of the problem, with the modality being the key to distinguishing the specific type of pleural leak. During the initial thoracic workup, a contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was ordered before the insertion of an intercostal drain. This scan demonstrated patchy areas of air space consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas. While the CT provided valuable structural information regarding the presence of a collection and the path of least resistance tracking toward the retroperitoneum, it has well-known limitations in visualizing ductal anatomy compared to other modalities. CT reliably shows pancreatic calcifications or cysts but depicts the actual fistulous tract connecting the pancreas to the pleura in only 33% to 47% of cases in the provided literature. To definitively identify the fistula, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was planned and performed. MRCP offered a superior view of the pancreatic ductal system, visualizing the fistula which had a narrow communication with the left pleural cavity alongside a right-sided tract. This imaging confirmed that the fluid was tracking from a disrupted pancreatic duct through the oesophageal hiatus into the pleural space, explaining the air and fluid dynamics seen as hydropneumothorax on the contrast study.

The final confirmation of the diagnosis relied heavily on biochemical markers within the pleural fluid, serving as a pathognomonic clue that distinguished this condition from other causes of exudative effusion, particularly pancreatic leaks from other origins. While various conditions, including Boerhaave‛s syndrome or malignancy, can result in elevated pleural fluid amylase, the magnitude and source of the enzyme were the critical differentiators. In this specific case, pleural fluid analysis revealed significantly elevated amylase concentrations at 6118 U/L and lipase at 7211 U/L. In the context of pediatric PPF, elevated levels in one or both enzymes are highly significant, often supporting the diagnosis when levels exceed 1000 IU/L or when present in combination. Crucially, the distinction between pancreatic-type amylase and salivary-type amylase determines the sensitivity of this marker. Given the high performance values recorded here, after excluding the prior differential diagnoses, the combination of raised pleural amylase, lipase, and clear imaging evidence of tracking from the pancreas was sufficient to establish the diagnosis of Pancreatopleural Fistula. This allows the clinician to treat the condition rather than the symptom, ensuring that the fluid dynamics are managed without further unnecessary thoracic intervention.

## Conservative Management and Drainage Interventions

The management of Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF) presents a unique clinical challenge due to the rarity of the condition and the lack of standardized treatment guidelines, particularly within the pediatric population. In this case, the therapeutic approach prioritized conservative measures over immediate surgical intervention, relying on the physiological principle that the fistula tract requires a period of ductal rest to close spontaneously. The primary objective of this phase was to minimize the volume and pressure of pancreatic fluid leakage while ensuring the patient remained hemodynamically stable and nutritionally adequate. In the case presented, the patient was immediately placed on a nil per oral (NPO) status upon admission to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit. Strict bowel rest is a foundational component of PPF management because dietary intake, particularly oral food, stimulates pancreatic exocrine function via vagovagal reflexes. This stimulation exacerbates the enzyme output and increases intraductal pressure, effectively widening the fistula tract. Consequently, the patient was placed on a total parenteral nutrition (TPN) protocol to provide essential hydration and calories without engaging the gastrointestinal tract. This nutritional strategy allowed the pancreatic system to divert energy toward ductal closure rather than exocrine secretion, facilitating the reduction of leakage into the pleural cavity.

To further suppress the exocrine drive and reduce ductal pressure, specific pharmacological intervention was initiated. Octreotide, a somatostatin analogue, was started at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (within the recommended range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). Octreotide inhibits the release of various hormones, including insulin and glucagon, and has the unique ability to decrease splanchnic blood flow and pancreatic secretions. By inhibiting these enzymatic outputs, the pharmacological regimen complemented the bowel rest strategy, aiming to create a conducive environment for the fistulized tract to seal without pressure. Percutaneous or surgical drainage was deemed necessary given the recurrence of pleural effusions despite initial attempts at thoracocentesis. The patient received multiple thoracocentesis performed under aseptic precautions, which temporarily relieved respiratory distress, but fluid accumulation recurred rapidly. This necessitated the insertion of an intercostal drain (ICD) during the patient’s third admission. The ICD served a dual purpose: sustained decompression of the pleural space to prevent respiratory compromise and ongoing monitoring of fluid output. Daily monitoring of the drain allowed the clinical team to correlate fluid output with clinical improvement or disease progression, guiding the decision to proceed with ICD removal once the flow ceased.

The diagnostic confirmation of PPF guided the discontinuation of concurrent empiric therapies and the confirmation of the management plan. Following the insertion of the ICD and the observation of dark red pleural fluid, imaging and laboratory markers provided the definitive etiology. Pleural fluid analysis revealed significantly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L), alongside a contrast-enhanced CT and Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) that visualized the pancreatic duct tracking to the pleural space. Although the patient had a family history of tuberculosis and slightly elevated pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA), the absence of fever, negative GeneXpert results, and the high enzymatic profile ruled out active infection. Consequently, the empiric antitubercular drugs were halted immediately. Additionally, Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy (PERT) was started as an adjunctive measure. While the primary rationale in this context was the suppression of enzyme output, the addition of PERT supported patients who may have mild insufficiency or who require enzyme modulation during ductal healing. Over a period of ten days, concordant with the reduction in drain output, the fluid output gradually decreased. The successful plug of the fistula tract is evident when these drainages stop, at which point the intercostal drain and monitoring can be safely terminated. In this instance, the successful resolution led to the removal of the ICD and discharge of the child with no further episodes, highlighting a protocol of conservative management that effectively controlled the pathology without surgical morbidity.

## Clinical Outcome and Pedagogical Lessons

The management of this complex appearance in the hospital course demonstrated the efficacy of conservative medical therapy for pediatric pancreatopleural fistula (PPF). Following the diagnosis and insertion of an intercostal drain to manage fluid fluctuation, the patient was initiated on nil per oral status, total parenteral nutrition, and Somatostatin analogues (Octreotide 30 µg/kg/day) alongside pancreatic enzyme replacement. This regimen aimed to suppress pancreatic exocrine output, thereby reducing the volume of the fistulous leak. Over a period of ten days, fluid drainage from the intercostal drain gradually decreased until complete cessation, allowing for the safe removal of the tube and hospital discharge. This short timeframe to clinical resolution supports the feasibility of a non-operative approach when medical stabilization and source drainage are successful, highlighting the potential for flap closure without major surgical intervention in pediatric trauma settings.

Long-term follow-up confirmed the durability of this resolution. The child has remained symptom-free with regular monthly surveillance, showing no recurrence of pleural effusions or respiratory distress. Functionally, the patient has returned to baseline, participating fully in daily school life and strenuous activities, including running and play, without limitation. This physical rehabilitation is critical, as untreated PPF can lead to chronic respiratory compromise, pleural fibrosis, and permanent disability. The patient's full functional recovery underscores the importance of complete fistula closure to prevent secondary musculoskeletal deformities and scarring associated with chronic intercostal drainage.

The diagnostic journey presented a classic example of the 'Pulmonary Mask,' where respiratory symptoms obscured the primary abdominal pathology. Initially, the recurrent dark red pleural effusion and tachypnea were misdirected toward thoracic causes, including tuberculosis and malignancy, delaying the diagnosis of a pancreatic event. However, by recognizing the association of recurrent bloody effusion with a history of blunt abdominal trauma, the clinicians pivoted to MRCP, which visualized the fistula. This delay illustrates a common clinical pitfall where the 'respiratory mask' of this disease leads to resource-intensive pulmonary workups that fail to identify the source. In pediatric patients where 'Koch's contact' or family history exists, or where Adenosine Deaminase is mildly elevated, clinicians must remain wary of radiologically explaining absences and consider high yield diagnostic escalation to occult abdominal pathology.

The rarity of this condition in the pediatric population further complicates clinical reasoning. While PPF is documented in over 300 adult cases, only 47 cases have been reported in the pediatric age group, significantly increasing the reliance on trauma as a trigger. Blunt abdominal injury, often bears a higher risk, accounting for a significant proportion of pediatric cohorts. This scarcity suggests that clinicians often view adult patterns (e.g., alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis) inappropriately and miss pediatric etiology (e.g., ductal trauma or hereditary mutations). For the resident, the key takeaway is to re-evaluate adolescents or children with recurrent pleural fluid collections, particularly those presenting with a delayed history of trauma. In such cases, the pleural fluid amylase level and the anatomical tracking on imaging signal pancreatic ductal injury, shifting the focus from pulmonary taxation to abdominal pancreatic management. Early recognition prevents unnecessary pulmonary interventions and sepsis, which might otherwise arise from delayed systemic management.
