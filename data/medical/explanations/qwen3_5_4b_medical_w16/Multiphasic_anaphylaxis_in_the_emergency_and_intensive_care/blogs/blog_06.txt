Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Defining the Fracture: Biphasic vs. Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

When you see a patient respond to epinephrine and stay stable, you can declare anaphylaxis resolved. You leave them with a corticosteroid order and send them home to watch for two days. But what happens when the patient discharges, comes back a week later, and shows up again, and *then* comes back again? That’s the nightmare scenario: multiphasic anaphylaxis.

In our case, a woman in her 60s with a history of β-lactam allergy presented to the ED with a severe reaction to a single dose of clarithromycin. She had a classic anaphylactic shock—hypotension, hypoxia, urticaria. Standard of care kicked in: 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, fluids, oxygen. She stabilized. The care team declared safety, admitted her to the internal ward, and started a standard observation period.

Then, the phone rang. She relapsed. They moved her to the ICU. Five days later? She repeated it. A total of four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock in one case. Sometimes the first dose overwhelms the drugs, sometimes it's the underlying cell media. This patient had the latter. The confusion lies in distinguishing this multiphasic course from the more commonly known biphasic anaphylaxis, and understanding why management gets frustratingly complex when you have that many recurrences.

## The Fracture Line: Defining the Recurrences

You might be reading that this patient had four episodes and wondering why I didn’t tell you earlier it was biphasic. In epidemiological terms, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as a recurrence of symptoms some time after the initial reaction *resolves*. Usually within 24 hours, though some outliers show up up to 78 hours later. This patient fits that. But calling it "biphasic" when she relapsed four times stretches the definition.

A multiphasic course presents with multiple episodes of symptom recurrence into full anaphylaxis despite clinical response to standard treatment.

The challenge is that standard guidelines often lean on the definition of biphasic anaphylaxis for monitoring. This patient didn't need a 4-hour watch because she still relapsed after hours; she needed a full admission and monitoring for days, and the data remains evolving here. Differentiating between "true recurrence" (re-exposure to trigger) or "sustained response" (not fully resolved, continuing off the epinephrine line) is notoriously tricky. We need to look at the physiology rather than the timeline. In the case above, the intensity actually lessened with each episode (hypoxia to dyspnea to rash). Is this a burning excitement building phase? No, it looks like a decaying disease process.

## Dosing and Delivery: 0.3mg vs 0.5mg

Let’s talk about the clipboard and the safety bars. This patient weighed 104 kg. She had four episodes total. The first two were managed with standard 0.3 mg auto-injectors. The second two involved individually prepared injections of 0.5 mg.

Think about that. The 0.3 mg is the standard antenatal dose. But for a 100+ kg patient, 0.3 mg is on the low end. The recommended adult dose is 0.01 mg/kg, or a max of 0.5 mg. Many practitioners know the 0.3 mg dose is less than the recommended total max for adults > 50 kg. It might be fine for a start, but in a multiphasic patient who might not have as much each time, you might think it's safer to aim high.

Auto-injectors are a game changer in time-sensitive settings. They minimize treatment delay and reduce hesitation. But in a hospital setting where you might have the 0.5 mg available, you have to be more comfortable. If your team is hesitant to use 0.5 mg, they start to play safe, which explains the failure to get a response initially. The 0.3 mg auto-injector definitely leads to a need for escalation in this patient.

## The Hidden Driver: Tryptase and Mastocytosis

Classic clues often point to this. This patient was a bit older, and the reactions were intense. The Frankfurter trial was an extra clue on top of the standard workup.

This case specifically noted a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria as only two WHO minor criteria were positive. The yellow card was likely the liver—she showed hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography. The neurological symptoms (depression, memory problems) were also mirrored. Tryptase levels were typically elevated during the third episode.

The critical failure here was in the timing of the lab draws. Guidelines recommend 1–2 hours after onset, but a second sample at 27 hours after resolution. If you only look at 1 hour, you miss the baseline. In this multiphasic context, repeated Tryptase engagement might help us understand if this patient had a mast cell disorder driving the recurrence.

## Management Dilemmas: Steroids and Observation

The second dilemma is observation. If this patient had a standard 6-hour observation period, she would have been sent home. Instead, she needed ICU monitoring. Some literature suggests 4–6 hours is not enough. Our patient had a recurrence within 12 hours of admission to the ward before moving to the ICU. The standard recommendation for biphasic anaphylaxis is typically 4–6 hours post-resolution.

Where does this fit? With her multiphasic nature, the risk is high. In her case, corticosteroids were used, but this is controversial. The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology recommends steroids, though a 2019 study notes an increased likelihood of admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. In this case, the team had protocol differences (30 mg prednisone, 8 mg dexamethasone). They used them because the patient had a high recurrence rate.

The management takeaway is clear: observation periods should be longer. The lack of data driving clear recommendations is a massive gap in our field.

## Key Take-Home Messages

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Biphasic vs. Multiphasic:** Focus on how, in clinical practice, multiphasic anaphylaxis often proves to be more dangerous than simple biphasic anaphylaxis. Keep in mind that total recurrence episodes can multiply risk.
- **Epinephrine Dosing:** Adults over 50 kg should receive 0.5 mg of epinephrine rather than 0.3 mg if available. If using auto-injectors, be prepared to escalate based on symptoms.
- **Observation Time:** Standard 4–6 hour observation is insufficient for multiphasic anaphylaxis. Monitor for at least 12–24 hours with higher-risk protocols.
- **Look for Mastocytosis:** If a patient presents with multiphasic anaphylaxis, consider testing for systemic mastocytosis, especially with elevated tryptase levels and organ involvement. Tryptase evaluation should be timed correctly (peak within 4 hours and then 24 hours resolution).
- **Do Not Use Corticosteroids Predictively:** Corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis are not proven to prevent recurrence and carry side effect risks. Use them based on patient-specific risk profiles and hospital protocol.
