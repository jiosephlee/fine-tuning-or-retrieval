Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Tryptase Timing: Why Your Baseline Might Be Wrong in Emergency Settings

Few things in emergency medicine are as frustrating to track down as a paradoxical diagnostic result that falls apart under scrutiny. Take the case of a 60-something woman who presented with multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin. Her clinical course was dramatic—four distinct relapses over a 12-hour window in the ICU. Stabilization took one breath of oxygen and one bolus of saline before the storm triggered again. But let’s be honest, the most valuable lesson we learned from this case wasn’t how to manage the anaphylaxis itself; it was about how we measured the aftermath.

The patient ultimately presented with a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, a condition predisposing patients to this exact kind of multiphasic reactivity. However, the diagnosis was provisional at best because the diagnostic timeline was botched. Systemic mastocytosis patients have chronically elevated tryptase, but the acuity of the reaction requires timing to capture the highest spike. If you rush the baseline? You miss the house. This case serves as a candid reminder that accurate diagnostics in multiphasic anaphylaxis are just as time-sensitive as the Epi pen retrieval.

## The Clinical Course: Four Epi Shots in One Day

To properly understand the leak in the diagnostic pipeline, we need to acknowledge the storm that preceded it. The patient was a known beta-lactam avoidant with significant comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, and depression. The trigger was unassumed: clarithromycin, to which she had no prior history.

The initial presentation to the ED was classic: hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (88% on room air), flushing, and generalized urticaria. The treatment was aggressive and standard. She received the 0.3 mg auto-injector epinephrine, clemastine, and oxygen. While the immediate passage was clear, she was admitted to the acute ward, initially without telemetry. The trajectory was clear as she entered the ICU:

*   One hour post-initial presentation, she was stable.
*   Within 12 hours of admission, she relapsed.
*   Total of four episodes, requiring repeat epinephrine doses.
*   The intensity fluctuated; the subsequent episodes were milder but clinically significant.
*   Adult antihistamines and corticosteroids were used peri-episode.

While the management of the acute shock incident was sound regarding stabilization, the follow-up protocol for the underlying pathology revealed the gap. The text notes that neither continuous epinephrine infusion nor strict expectant management was ultimately chosen, as the risk-benefit analysis was uncertain for multiphasic cases. That said, the patient’s eventual discharge after 3 days was contingent on her not showing symptoms again.

## The Diagnostic Issue: Mis-timed Tryptase Testing

This is where we get to the meat of the FOAM lesson: the timing of the tryptase draw. Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is a rare but clinically important cause of severe, recurrent anaphylaxis. Because the condition manifests beyond normal allergy boundaries—often with hepatomegaly or neuropsychiatric symptoms like the depression this patient suffered—it is crucial to detect it before a fatal relapse hits.

The case analysis reveals a specific gap in the diagnostic protocol:

*   **Guideline Recommendation:** Ideally, you want three samples: one immediately, one 1–2 hours after resolution to catch peak rise (usually 1–2 hours), and a third >24 hours post-resolution to establish a true baseline for comparison.
*   **The Patient's Realization:** The first tryptase sample was taken 1 hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode. The second sample was taken 27 hours after the last recurrence.
*   **The Outcome:** Elevated tryptase levels were confirmed during the third episode, but the *baseline* comparison was compromised because the first acute samples didn't follow the peak-window correctly.

The text highlights that the patient only met two of the two major criteria for formal diagnosis due to limited material in the bone marrow biopsy. However, the combined clinical picture—recurrent anaphylaxis despite interval modernizing treatment, elevated tryptase, and hepatomegaly—clued in the SM diagnosis. It wasn’t the medication that caused the reaction every time; it was the mast cell destabilization.

## Takeaways: Diagnostics Rule

If you are managing a patient with unresolved anaphylaxis or multiphasic presentations, remember the triage of diagnostic data is just as critical as the IV fluid bolus.

*   **Timing is Biology:** Remember the recommendation to take the second tryptase sample 1–2 hours post-symptom onset to capture the peak, not waiting until the patient has calmed down.
*   **Distinguish Suspect vs. Baseline:** Tryptase levels can remain elevated post-reaction, but distinguishing a true elevated baseline (mastocytosis) from a transient post-injury spike requires capturing the resolution phase correctly.
*   **Epi Dosage Matters:** For the 104 kg patient in this report, 0.3 mg auto-injectors were used for the first few doses. While they did the job, it highlights the risk in larger adults where up to 0.5 mg is often cited for adults >50kg.
*   **Sentinel Symptoms:** Unexplained hepatomegaly or history of severe/recursive reactions without clear re-exposure should trigger a mastocytosis workup regardless of the initial negative allergy panels.
*   **Multiphasic is Uncommon but Dangerous:** While biphasic reactions occur in roughly 4–5% of cases, multiphasic presentations require longer observation and vigilant re-evaluation for underlying mast cell disorders, not just a repeat Epi injection.
