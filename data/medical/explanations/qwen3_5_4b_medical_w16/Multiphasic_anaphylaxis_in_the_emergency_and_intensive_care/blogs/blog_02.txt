# When Anaphylaxis Strikes Twice: Uncovering Systemic Mastocytosis in the ED

**When Anaphylaxis Strikes Twice: Uncovering Systemic Mastocytosis in the ED**

There is a classic joke in emergency medicine: when a patient comes back in two hours later saying they are having trouble breathing, you assume the anaphylaxis resolved, then they came back. But when they come back *four times*, and then asymptomatic for weeks, then back again? You aren't just dealing with the allergic reaction; you are dealing with a quest for a diagnosis that wasn't found in the first 10 minutes of resuscitation.

Take-home from this case study is that standard anaphylaxis protocols, while life-saving in the acute moment, often leave clinicians thinking in loops. When a patient stabilizes on epi but continues to relapse, disrupting the model of "epinephrine leads to stability," it is time to look for hidden drivers. In this case, the driver was likely not a simple IgE-mediated allergy to clarithromycin, but widespread mast cell instability suggestive of systemic mastocytosis (SM).

The lesson here isn't just about better epinephrine, it's about recognizing when the "resolution" is actually a trick of the clinical course.

## The Detective Work Behind the Epi Shot

Consider the patient profile: 60s, 104kg, history of diabetes, psoriasis, depression, and prior mild drug allergies. She took clarithromycin, 500mg single dose. Within an hour: hypotension, hypoxia, urticaria, swelling. She got the curve. Epi, Epinephrine. Blood pressure back up. Face less red. Good news.

But the graph didn't go flat. She hit the ICU after the first recurrence. We are talking about four total anaphylactic episodes in the first 12 hours. And a key detail that signaled the complexity: the intensity of symptoms actually lessened with each episode. It moved from severe hypotension and cyanosis to simple dyspnea and rash.

This dampeningcascade doesn't affect septic shock logic, but in anaphylaxis, the expectation of symmetry is dropped. If the first dose is under 0.5mg and you have a large body mass, the symptoms can be driven way too fast. If she had 0.3 mg based on auto-injector limitation, with 104kg. This isn't baking soda, but it is a dosing issue.

Furthermore, the initial treatment was adequate supportive care, but the treatment of choice for biphasic anaphylaxis remained epinephrine. She did not get pre-formed meds until the ward. IT is notable that the only time she got a non-epi was clemastine after the first recurrence.

So why, despite the repeated response, did she relapse? And why did she relapse in a multiphasic pattern rather than a biphasic one?

We need to discuss the underlying mechanism once the excreted is gone. The most likely culprit for a multiphasic reaction that doesn't match the trigger is a mast cell disorder.

## The Diagnostic Gray Zone of Systemic Mastocytosis

Here is the tricky part: the patient didn't meet formal diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis. Only two WHO minor criteria, no major criteria due to biopsy limitations.

This is a massive gray zone for the ED. You can have the clinical picture—chronic urticaria, mast cell activation signs, hypotension triggered by common triggers—but the silver bullet biopsy isn't available immediately. That means clinicians cannot rule it out based on one failed trial.

You can't just do an IgE panel and think you are done if the patient relapses four times. The trial of the drug, in fact, has to be higher suspicion. If you suspect mastocytosis, you need to catch this on the grid.

The key to catching it lies in tryptase. In this case, tryptase levels were increased during the third episode and remained elevated up to follow-up. Guidelines say to get a baseline sample *or* one right after the peak of symptoms, but the ED case missed the timing for the first sample. It was taken one hour after the third episode, which missed the 1-2 hour peak for initial assessment. This is why we need to standardize the sampling in our own metrics. A delayed tryptase check is the first hint that maybe the reaction isn't simple IgE.

## Treatment Nuances and Safety

There are two treatment pearls hidden in this case that apply directly to the daily ED.

First, auto-injectors vs. manual. The patient used 0.3mg. In a patient weighing 104kg. 0.3mg is the adult limit of auto-injectors in practice in this region (e.g., Japan/Netherlands protocol). Guidelines recommend 0.3mg for <50kg and 0.5mg for >50kg. In this patient, the auto-injector was underdosed relative to her weight. This leads to more epinephrine augmentations even in subsequent doses. Breaking the barrier of comfort with manual preparation (preparing 0.5mg with individual volumes) is critical for larger adults.

Second, the corticosteroid debate. This patient received prednisone and dexamethasone. While many guidelines now advise against routine steroids, the "rescue" role is evolving. We still cannot say that steroids are mandatory for every patient, but if the multiphasic pattern is borrowing from steroid protocols to prevent relapse, standard practice is shifting toward cautious use for persistent high-risk patients despite the evidence suggesting lack of benefit in biphasic cases.

## Clinical Pearls and Takeaways

Here are the clinical pearls you can use in the ED or wards when managing complex allergic recurrences.

### Case Summary: Treatment Pearls
*   **Recognize Multiphasic Anaphylaxis:** When a patient stabilizes but relapses multiple times (biphasic = 1 recurrence, multiphasic = 2+ recurrences) without re-exposure to the allergen, ensure you are monitoring for underlying mast cell pathology.
*   **Epi Dosing Issues:** Auto-injectors (0.3mg) should not always be the first-line treatment for adult patients weighing >50kg. Manual administration is preferred if available for better dosing accuracy.
*   **Tryptase Timing:** You need specific sample timing. One sample immediately, one within 1-2 hours (peak), and one 24 hours post-episode to establish baseline. If the ED is done with a single late sample, you miss the clinical clue for mastocytosis.

### Why Screening Matters
*   **Don't Wait for Standard Criteria:** If the pattern persists, think systematically mastocytosis. A formal biopsy may not be needed for treatment of the reaction, but screening (e.g., baseline tryptase) can guide long-term management.
*   **Manage Resource Limitations:** A registry of multiphasic cases (like the one presented) is the only way to guide evidence-based consensus on next steps in multiphasic anaphylaxis management.
*   **Be Vigilant with Obvious Triggers:** In refractory or recurrent anaphylaxis, do not ignore the possibility that the trigger is not totally allergen-driven, but potentially another underlying mast cell disorder.
*   **Review Hospital Protocols:** Steroid use during anaphylaxis is debated. Check if your hospital protocol supports steroids for multiphasic cases despite shifting national guidelines (World Allergy Organisation / RCOG).

### Final Thoughts
The failed treatment of anaphylaxis in the ED is usually due to inadequate support, but if it persists, it's due to a higher likely mast cell pathology. The challenge is ensuring we catch that pathology. In this case, the multiphasic pattern led to the discovery of elevated tryptase and signs consistent with systemic mastocytosis, even though formal criteria were not fully met. This reminds us that the ED is a place for diagnosis as much as resuscitation, and that a good pattern often reveals what a bad diagnosis missed.
