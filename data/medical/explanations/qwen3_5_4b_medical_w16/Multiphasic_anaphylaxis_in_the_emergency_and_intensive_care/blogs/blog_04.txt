Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The Triage Dilemma: ICU vs. Ward for Recurrent Anaphylaxis

Reading through the recent case report on multiphasic anaphylaxis felt less like a standard emergency case and more like a logistical puzzle wrapped in pathophysiology. It’s one thing to manage a patient who goes in, crashes, stabilizes, and leaves. It is quite another for a patient to resolve, then crash again, do it three more times within hours, and keep settling into the ED/ward ICU continuum.

The specific intrigue in this case lies not in the shock itself—while clarithromycin-related anaphylaxis is ominous—but in the treatment trajectory. We see a 104kg patient in her 60s presenting with clarithromycin toxicity. Initial management with Epi 0.3 mg and fluids saved her. She stabilizes. We admit her to the standard ward. We monitor. We re-admit her to the ICU the moment symptoms return a second time.

This raises a critical, often debated question: **What is the clinical threshold that dictates when you move a multiphasic anaphylaxis patient from a standard ward to an ICU setting, and do the resources justify the shift?**

## The Clinical Reality of Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

Biphasic anaphylaxis—the recurrence of symptoms within 24 hours after apparent resolution—is accepted. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, however, presents with *multiple* relapses. It is a rare beast, reported in roughly 0.4% to 6% of cases. While guidelines suggest observation periods of up to 4–6 hours, and 12 hours is a common upper bound, the reality of multiphasic anaphylaxis is that fatigue and physiological instability reset quickly.

In the case presented, the patient exhibited a total of **four anaphylactic episodes** within 12 hours of admission. Each required intramuscular epinephrine. An initial 0.3 mg auto-injector was used twice, followed by two mixed doses of 0.5 mg. 

Why does this matter for triage? Because the intensity of the physiological response changes the risk stratification. If the first episode of relapse triggers a return to hypotension and hypoxia, does the threshold simply change from "observe" to "telemetry"? The decision to move to the ICU in this case was made *after* the first relapse. This highlights the importance of the "mentioning" phase where care shifts from reactive resuscitation to *anticipatory* resource management.

## The Triage Threshold: Ward vs. ICU

The prompt of the report asks us to evaluate the resource allocation decision. Standard wards often have limited nursing for 1:1 observation compared to ICU. However, resource allocation can't risk a cardiovascular collapse.

In this case, the decision seemed binary: Remain on the ward (potentially outside of telemetry) or move to the ICU (higher cost, higher availability of titratable care). The treating team moved to the **ICU** after the first relapse.

**Key decision factors observed:**
*   **Failure of Initial Resolution:** If a patient displays symptoms twice on the ward, the assumption is not a benign course. It is either high-risk mastocytosis or severe IgA resistance.
*   **Resource Efficiency:** Anecdotal evidence in the literature suggests multiphasic courses peak within the first 24 hours. Moving to ICU early allows for continuity of vasoactive support (though not required here since subsequent episodes were still managed with IM epinephrine).
*   **Psychological Burden:** The patient had depression. Agitation was noted. ICU often provides better sedation and care for the multidimensional distress following anaphylaxis.

The takeaway here is subtle: **Don't wait for the patient to be "stable" twice before moving to higher-level monitoring.** If the pattern indicates multiphasic anaphylaxis, the ward should be considered "high risk." ICU admission ensures that if the patient desaturates or hypotensives again, there is a pump or direct line available without transport.

## Epinephrine Dosing and Monitoring in the Recurrent Phase

Another point of contention in this case is the choice of 0.3 mg vs. 0.5 mg epinephrine for adults over 50 kg. Auto-injectors are often 0.3 mg, as 0.5 mg is less standard for oral/throat惹. However, guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg (up to 0.5 mg).

The patient was 104 kg. An initial 0.3 mg dose is mathematically underdosed compared to the 0.5 mg goal. This wasn't fatal (she responded), but it adds to the recurrence burden. The subsequent doses were mixed IV/IM prep.

Furthermore, the lack of telemetry during the first phase is notable. She was in the "acute internal ward" for the first 12 hours total, and no telemetry was provided. Then she moves to the ICU. This suggests the decision was based on the *recurrence* itself becoming the catalyst for telemetry.

In practice, if a patient with anaphylaxis presents with known mastocytosis risk or prior biphasic/shock, the bet on telemetry is high cost but low risk. The case hints at a systemic mastocytosis probability (elevated tryptases, depressed neuropsych symptoms). In mastocytosis, the response to Epi can be blunted, and the recurrence is more frequent than in "classic" allergy.

Therefore, the decision tree looks like this:
*   **Classic Anaphylaxis (Biphasic Risk):** Admit to ward, observe 4–12 hours.
*   **Suspected Multiphasic / Systemic Mastocytosis:** Hospitalize, move to ICU/Telemetry immediately upon any recurrent symptom phase.

## Refining the Protocol

The case also touched on the difficulty of risk stratification. There is no gold-standard predictor for multiphasic anaphylaxis. While severity of initial presentation, delayed epinephrine administration, and body mass index are sometimes touted as predictors, the multiphasic course defies these static criteria.

The management of the patient involved:
*   Multiple IM Epi doses
*   Antihistamines and corticosteroids (prednisone)
*   Discontinuation of the trigger (clarithromycin)
*   Follow-up for tryptase monitoring (which was missed initially)

The protocol seems to suggest that for patients where the *course* is undefined, the safest triage move is to the ICU until proven otherwise, rather than aiming to "discharge early" based on temporary stability.

## Take-home Points

*   **Multiphasic vs. Biphasic:** While biphasic anaphylaxis is defined by a single recurrence, multiphasic anaphylaxis involves **multiple** episodes, requiring stricter resource allocation during stabilization.
*   **The ICU Threshold:** Transfer to an ICU or telemetry setting **after the first recurrence of symptoms** is often justified over prolonged ward observation in multiphasic cases, prioritizing stability over budget.
*   **Auto-Injector Suitability:** 0.3 mg auto-injectors may be beneficial in time-sensitive settings to reduce hesitancy, but **adults over 50 kg often require 0.5 mg dosages**, which should be checked during escalation protocols.
*   **Hidden Pathology:** If anaphylaxis is refractory or multiphasic, consider **Systemic Mastocytosis** and elevated tryptase levels even if formal diagnostic criteria are not met; this can influence future emergency training and monitoring thresholds.
*   **Observation Duration:** It is safer to monitor for signs of recurrence longer than the initial resolution symptoms, especially in elderly patients or those with prior health conditions, as the duration of the first episode did not preclude later recurrences.
