Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## The Steroid Hangover: Why Corticosteroids May Be Harmful in Anaphylaxis

Few things in emergency medicine are as perplexing as a patient who responds beautifully to epinephrine, only to go back to the drawing board a few hours later. In a case recently published by Schneider et al., we present a woman in her 60s, whose course wasn't just biphasic but truly multiphasic. She had an anaphylactic reaction to clarithromycin that resolved, only to relapse into anaphylactic shock four times in the first 12 hours of her hospital stay.

While her rapid response to maximal epinephrine dose rests is indisputable, the subsequent clinical management—specifically the judicious use of corticosteroids—presents a contemporary debate. In this case, clinicians retreated to the "traditional" role of corticosteroids to prevent recurrence after the first relapse. This decision, however, sits uncomfortably alongside emerging evidence and updated guidelines that suggest we might be overdosing on pharmacological "habit" at the expense of patient safety. It makes for a fascinating look at the gap between clinical inertia and evidence-based stewardship.

## The Case of Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

Let's stretch into the clinical details without getting lost in the weeds. This wasn't the standard allergic reaction you see in the "funny" patient. She presented with hypotension, hypoxaemia, urticaria, and agitation immediately following clarithromycin ingestion. Initial management was standard adult anaphylaxis: IM epinephrine (auto-injector + IV bolus), fluids, and antihistamines.

But the real drama unfolded in the ward. Despite a full clinical resolution, she suffered four total episodes of anaphylactic shock. Crucially, the intensity decreased with every episode, moving from deep cyanosis and dyspnea to milder pruritus. Treatment became critical because the manual calculation of empiric epinephrine protocols was challenged, but more importantly, standard monitoring for relapse was breached.

Clinical stewardship here was complicated by the administration of prednisone (30mg daily x 5 days) and dexamethasone (8mg once) due to the "traditional role" of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. While this intervention did not physically cause harm in this specific instance, the choice reinforces a dilemma: are we treating the *potential* for relapse that might actually get worse, or are we treating the *evidence* that suggests steroids might be detrimental or irrelevant?

## The Evidence vs. The Habit

This is where the "Steroid Hangover" comes into play. For decades, the narrative has been "stabilize first, think second, stop later, but cover with steroids for the whole group." However, the case report itself notes the grim reality of this logic. By 2017, a systematic review of 22 studies examined the efficacy of corticosteroids in anaphylaxis, finding evidence against them in 21 studies. One study supported the use of corticosteroids, but the rest suggested no benefit and even the potential for harm.

Recent guidance has shifted. While the 2021 Resuscitation Council UK guidelines acknowledge the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis, they guide clinicians toward observation periods rather than routine pharmacological prophylaxis. Yet, look closely at this patient’s post-discharge care. She was started on levocetirizine, an antihistamine, and given an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). She had no further episodes.

The follow-up data is equally striking regarding diagnostic stewardship. This patient ended up with a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, though she did not meet formal diagnostic criteria. Tryptase levels were elevated, but they weren't sampled according to the recommended protocol (baseline, 1-2 hours, and 24 hours). The failure to follow this protocol means we might have missed a key differentiator for future risk stratification. In this multiphasic scenario, the patient was more "troubled" by her mast cell biology than the protocol allowed.

The lesson here implies that in multiphasic settings, the morphological features and elevated tryptase are more important than the "steroid shield." The case suggests that relying on the shield (steroids) might obscure the real diagnosis (mast cell disorder). If the root cause is a mastocytosis, steroids might suppress symptoms temporarily, but they won't fix the underlying driver, leaving the patient vulnerable to future relapses with high-dose, delayed episodes of an anaphylaxis.

Let's not forget the epinephrine issue. The patient received two 0.3 mg doses via auto-injector and two individually prepared 0.5 mg doses. While the auto-injector is convenient, it risks underdosing in heavier patients (she weighed 104 kg). 2025 retrospective data (cited in the discussion) suggests that underdosing leads to more escalations in care.

## Key Takeaways

*   **Clinical Observation is Mandatory:** Do not discharge based solely on the absence of anaphylaxis symptoms; observe in the ward for 4-6 hours, and be wary if a multiphasic pattern emerges.
*   **Consider the Root Cause:** If patients have recurrent reactions, suspect systemic mastocytosis or other mastocytosis-associated conditions rather than relying solely on the "avoidance" of triggers.
*   **Steroids are Pragmatic, Not Mandate:** While traditional use often involves prednisone on admission, current guidelines generally advise against routine corticosteroids in anaphylaxis management due to limited evidence of harm reduction.
*   **Try to Measure Tryptase:** In cases of suspicion for mast cell disorders, adhere to the minimum two-sample rule for tryptase levels to establish trends and baseline.
*   **Dosing Matters:** Ensure that severe adult anaphylaxis patients get 0.5 mg epinephrine, regardless of whether they use auto-injectors, to prevent possible underdosing.
*   **Don't Fear Algorithm Overdosing:** If you use steroids for a benign reaction, you maintain it as a precaution. But it doesn’t necessarily mean it was the most effective intervention.

## Bottom Line

This case serves as a reminder that while clinical habits carry us through certain scenarios, they should not override the evidence. The multiphasic nature of anaphylaxis often presents a unique challenge. If anaphylaxis recurred in a multiphasic course, evaluate for systemic errors (diagnostic errors like not checking tryptase) before jumping on the defense of prednisone. The "Steroid Hangover" is real, and we should avoid letting it mask the need for deeper diagnostic workups in the most unexpected place: the emergency room.
