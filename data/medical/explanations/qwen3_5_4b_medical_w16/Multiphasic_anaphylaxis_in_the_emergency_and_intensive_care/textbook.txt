## Section 1: Pathophysiology and Classification of Complex Anaphylactic Courses

To understand the clinical presentation of anaphylaxis, one must first understand the fundamental biology of mast cell degranulation. Anaphylaxis is a systemic reaction mediated primarily by the release of pre-formed and newly synthesized mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, and platelet-activating factor from mast cells and basophils. Typically, this process is triggered by antigens stimulating IgE-mediated cross-linking, leading to an immediate surge in inflammatory chemicals that compromise vascular tone and airway integrity. However, the case of the 60-year-old woman presents a more complex scenario known as residual or delayed mast cell activation. In standard anaphylaxis, mediator kinetics imply that symptom resolution signifies a successful neutralization of this inflammatory cascade. In contrast, multiphasic courses suggest that the initial antagonism of mediators—whether by epinephrine, antihistamines, or natural clearance—is transient or insufficient to abolish the endogenous inflammatory drive. This indicates a pathophysiology where mast cell reactivation or a stored reservoir of mediators is still active even after clinical stabilization. In the context of this patient, the analysis pointing toward systemic mastocytosis, despite meeting only minor diagnostic criteria, suggests a potential underlying expansion of mast cells which are less urectile and continue to release mediators than typical non-innesflammatory mast cells, potentially causing a secondary wave of symptoms that mimics a new reaction even without allergen re-exposure.

The classification of these recurrences is critical for guiding clinical observation and management, differentiating between a simple biphasic course and a potentially more dangerous multiphasic event. Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined by major guidelines, such as the UK Resuscitation Council, as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent resolution, provided there is no re-exposure to the potential allergen. The case presented a distinct progression, moving beyond this typical definition to a multiphasic event characterized by multiple relapses fitting anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of hospital admission, totaling four distinct episodes. This distinction is vital because, while the formula for biphasic anaphylaxis (recurrence within 78 hours post-exposure) is well understood, multiphasic courses represent a phenotypic challenge where the patient remains in an aroused state of immune activation. In our patient, the intensity of symptoms varied with each episode, decreasing from deep hypoxaemia and hypotension to mild rashes, suggesting a changing biological response to the same trigger. This variability implies that while the first reaction represented a severe mitochondrial and hemodynamic stress (shock), subsequent waves were similarly managed but required sustained vigilance, highlighting that the biological load was not a single surge but a sustained disturbance.

The mechanism of recurrence is often framed around the kinetics of mediators versus the therapeutic action of epinephrine. A delay in the clearance of mediators or a further set of mast cells degranulating due to a specific threshold of sensitivity is postulated. Unlike re-exposure, which resets the IgE challenge, recurrence is an intrinsic failure of the internal regulatory mechanism. In this specific case, the prescribed 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector was used for the initial episodes, but given the patient's body weight of 104 kg and the fact that the maximum recommended dose is 0.5 mg, the first physiological wave of mediators was clearly under-treated relative to her aspirated mass. This pathophysiological under-dosing contrasts sharply with the pathophysiology of biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis where high body weight often correlates with higher baseline mediator content. The elevated baseline tryptase levels further supported the hypothesis that this patient had an inherently higher mast mediator burden, consistent with possible systemic mastocytosis. Consequently, standard dosing assumptions become less than precise when the pathophysiology involves a "heavy basket" of mast cells. Furthermore, the consensus on corticosteroids is shifting; while the patient received prednisone and dexamethasone due to protocol, recent literature questions their role in reducing recurrence rates. Understanding that coolant steroids do not prevent mast cell swelling or mediator release is essential for recognizing why the recurrences still happened despite the steroid therapy used.

Despite the standard recommendation that the majority of patients do not experience recurrence, the variation in reported incidence of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, ranging from 0.4% to 6.3% with most data pointing to 4-6%, necessitates proactive consideration of these risk factors. For the medical student, the lesson from this case is that "single-dose" assumptions, whether regarding steroids or observation periods, often fail in high-risk cohorts. The case demonstrates a tracked period of observation within the first 12 hours which is clinically aggressive, ensuring that the temporary resolution of symptoms does not mask a delayed recurrence. In the case, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit due to the first relapse, recognizing that the simple hospital ward observation might not capture the dynamic changes of multiphasic shock. Therefore, understanding the pathophysiology of delayed mast cell mediator release allows clinicians to recognize that a patient who resolves from an initial shock is not clinically safe if they present the same signs again within 78 hours, even if the same allergen is not taken. This biological understanding justifies the continued epinephrine access, the monitoring of tryptase levels, and the ICU transition to prevent a fatal outcome as potentially described in similar severe follow-up data.

## Clinical Recognition and Differential Diagnosis in the Acute Settings

## Initial Assessment: Interpretation of Vital Signs and Cutaneous Signs in Anaphylaxis

The most common pitfall in recognizing anaphylaxis in the emergency setting is underestimating the severity of symptoms before skin signs are fully developed or misinterpreting early resolution as stability. In the case presented, the patient arrived with a documented systemic reaction to clarithromycin clinically, characterized by hypotension and hypoxemia immediately preceding visible cutaneous signs. Her vital signs were significantly deranged, showing a non-invasive blood pressure of 74/50 mmHg and a peripheral oxygen saturation of 88% on room air. These findings align with the second-degree hemodynamic instability typical of anaphylactic shock, driven by mast-cell mediated vasodilation and increased vascular permeability leading to relative hypovolemia. It is critical for residents to recognize that while cardiac and lung auscultation were normal initially—relieving immediate concern for cardiac tamponade or massive airway obstruction—the patient was already lightheaded and agitated. The cutaneous examination revealed generalized erythema and urticaria over the entire body, a "full-surface" reaction that correlates with the systemic nature of the mediators (histamine and leukotrienes) released. An important teaching point derived from this case is that vital signs in adults with known allergies may deteriorate to shock (circulatory compromise) even before the classic angioedema or airway obstruction symptoms are detected. The initial drop in blood pressure represents a medical emergency requiring simultaneous physical assessment of circulation and oxygenation, regardless of the presence of a normal heart rate or appropriate respiratory effort, which are often misinterpreted as stability until the intensity of the reaction heightens.

## Differential Diagnosis: Recurrence Patterns versus Sustained Reaction

A key challenge in the acute management of anaphylaxis is distinguishing between a biphasic or multiphasic reaction that resolves and reappears later versus a sustained reaction where the initial diagnosis was incomplete. This patient presented with an initial “good response” to epinephrine and supportive care during admission, implying a successful counter-negative effect of the medication. In contrast to sustained shock, which would fail to respond to additional epinephrine or supportive fluids despite adequate dosing, this patient’s initial epinephrine dose successfully restored hemodynamics. However, this was not the primary concern, as the recurrence of symptoms within the first 12 hours of admission signaled a biphasic pattern. In the context of the case, the differential diagnosis must rigorously account for the absence of re-exposure; the patient was not reintroduced to clarithromycin during the hospital stays, yet symptom intensity eventually resolved spontaneously. This distinguishes multiphasic anaphylaxis from refractory anaphylaxis, which implies persistent symptoms requiring various mechanisms of action rather than return of the clinical picture. The lesson here is that a period of observation must remain active even after clinical resolution. Furthermore, doctors must consider the slow absorption of substances like antibiotics or locally administered medications, but the absence of continuous dosing coupons or contact suggests that minor contaminants or residual systemic cytokines are adequate explanations, provided the second set of triggers (clindamycin rash) suggests a gradual sensitization.

## Timing: The Critical Observation Window

The management window for observation following anaphylaxis acts as the filter for multiphasic anaphylaxis identification. In the presented case, the patient was admitted to the acute internal ward after an initial resolution, with subsequent symptoms developing before she was even transferred to the intensive care unit. Guidelines such as the UK Resuscitation Council UK and Dutch NHG suggest varying observation periods, usually between 4 to 6 hours, but this patient’s recurrence of hypotension and hypoxemia within 12 hours underscores the need for a minimum 24-hour observation window to rule out biphasic reactions. The patient’s discharge occurred only after approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylaxis, and the recurrence occurred in the window where most reactions are expected to manifest (typically within the first 72 hours, though this patient recurred earlier). An observation of less than 24 hours leads to a high false negative rate for identifying high-risk patients who require discharge advice rather than long-term ICU confinement. In the case, the nurse observed visual progression from the first episode of symptoms to the fourth episode in a targeted manner, leading to the identification of a symptomatic patient who would eventually transition to standard care but only after multiple doses of epinephrine were required. ## Section 3: Investigative Logic and Secondary Workup for Systemic Mastocytosis

Following the stabilization of acute anaphylactic episodes, the transition to secondary workup is critical, particularly when atypical presentations like multiphasic anaphylaxis occur. In this case, the persistence of relapses despite adequate epinephrine response prompted an investigation into the patient's immune regulation. The suspicion of Systemic Mastocytosis (SM) arose because multiphasic anaphylaxis—a reaction characterized by recurrence without re-exposure—is distinct from simple allergic pharmacological hypersensitivity. While initial diagnoses often default to allergies to the offending agent (in this case, clarithromycin), the risk of underlying mast cell disorders increases in patients presenting with recurrent shock. The physical examination and clinical course suggest that standard allergic mechanisms may be insufficient to explain the severity and frequency of the relapses, necessitating a search for intrinsic mast cell pathology.

The diagnostic justification for SM was grounded in specific biomarker analysis, specifically the measurement of serum tryptase levels. Tryptase serves as a fibrinolytic release marker and a sensitive indicator of mast cell burden. Pathophysiologically, tryptase levels rise rapidly upon mast cell degranulation, peaking 1–2 hours after symptom onset while baseline levels reflect a stable disease state. The logic of the workup in this case highlights a common diagnostic pitfall: the timing of specimen collection. In this case, a peak level was taken 1 hour after the third episode, and a baseline was obtained 27 hours after resolution. Although the guidelines recommend a sample at the peak (ideally within 4 hours) and a baseline at least 24 hours after resolution to distinguish spikes from chronic elevation, the case utilized data that deviated from these ideal intervals. Despite this procedural variance, the results showed elevated tryptase both during the episode and at baseline, strongly supporting a diagnosis of mast cell hyperactivity rather than a standard drug allergy, where baseline levels remain undetectable usually.

Despite the strong biomarker support, the definitive diagnosis faced limitations regarding WHO criteria. As outlined in the case analysis, a formal diagnosis of Systemic Mastocytosis requires specific histopathologic or molecular findings. The bone marrow biopsy performed in this patient failed to provide adequate material to meet the major criteria for diagnosis, categorizing it as a "MM2" or minor criteria case that did not fully conform to requirements. This highlights the complexity of diagnostic logic when seeking confirmation for rare conditions; elevated biochemistry supports the suspicion, but physical or histologic exclusion is often needed to definitively rule out or rule in the disorder. This leaves the management этапе reliant on clinical judgment, where the combination of abnormal labs and clinical risk stratification guides prognosis over strict binary diagnostic criteria.

Risk stratification in the secondary workup also necessitated strategic decisions regarding further diagnostic testing. In a non-computational setting, allergy skin testing is a standard component for confirming sensitization. However, in this specific clinical scenario, skin testing was deemed contraindicated. The reasoning is based on the balance of risk and safety; given the patient's history of severe, recurrent systemic reactions, the threshold for inducing delayed reactivity of the anaphylactic response was very low. Furthermore, the potential for permanent anaphylactic shock outweighed the theoretical benefit of skin testing at a time when the allergen (clarithromycin) could be easily avoided. Consequently, the workup prioritized observational monitoring and long-term avoidance management rather than attempts to trigger diagnostic recall.

Supporting data points, though non-specific, aligned with the suspected systemic pathology. The case noted hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography and a history of depression and memory issues. Systemic Mastocytosis can lead to organ infiltration, which processively manifests as hepatomegaly, and chronic immune activation has been linked to neuropsychiatric symptoms. While these findings do not alone confirm the diagnosis, they reinforce the systemic nature of the reaction observed. Synthesizing these findings indicates that while the definitive diagnosis was circumstantially pending due to bone marrow limitations, the clinical team utilized the totality of risk factors—multiphasic recurrence, elevated tryptase, organ involvement, and psychiatric history—to guide long-term care and environmental avoidance strategies.

## Section 4: Therapeutic Optimization and Protocol Challenges

Effective management of multiphasic anaphylaxis often hinges on the precise calculation of pharmacological intervention based on the patient's physiology, yet this case highlights the often-practical limitations inherent to emergency protocols. In the initial emergency department presentation, the 104 kg female patient, who was in frank shock with a blood pressure of 74/50 mm Hg, received epinephrine via a standard 0.3 mg auto-injector before transitioning to individualized 0.5 mg intramuscular preparations. This trajectory underscores a critical teaching point regarding adult dosing algorithms. Current guidelines recommend a dose of 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, typically capping at 0.5 mg for adults weighing more than 50 kg. For a patient of this patient size (104 kg), a 0.5 mg dose is clinically required to achieve hemodynamic stability, as the 0.3 mg auto-injector is an underdose. While the initial use of the auto-injector was accepted due to time-sensitive stabilization in the ED, the reliance on manual preparation for the subsequent doses illustrates a common discordance between available automated kits and patient-specific dosing needs. In the multiphasic context described here, where symptom intensity decreased but hypotension recurred repeatedly, the team felt compelled to escalate the dosing strategy to ensure adequate allergic mediator suppression, a decision that aligns with the need for sufficient pre-dose titration in severe shock scenarios.

The decision-making process for adjunctive pharmacotherapy in this multiphasic case involved the traditional use of corticosteroids, which serves as a diagnostic challenge for modern learners aligning practice with current evidence. To the patient's first recurrence of anaphylactic shock in the first 12 hours of admission, the clinicians administered 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for five days, alongside a single 8 mg dose of dexamethasone. This rationale is consistent with established hospital protocols at the time of presentation that view corticosteroids as a preventative measure against biphasic relapses. However, the case report must also contextualize this against recent meta-analyses which suggest corticosteroids offer no benefit in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis and may even increase hospital admission rates due to side effects. While the patient did not develop further relapses, the clinical lesson here is that while corticosteroids are a prevalent standard in the ICU to manage the systemic inflammatory response, their role in secondary prevention is shifting from mandatory standardization to selective or off-protocol use based on the specific recurrence risk. This case specifically illustrates how hospitals may maintain established, albeit controversial, protocols due to a lack of consensus guidelines compared to the evolving research landscape.

Supportive care measures during the recurrent episodes were directed primarily at managing cutaneous symptoms and preventing the need for further mechanical ventilation through hypoxia suppression. The administration of normal saline boluses appears critical, particularly during the second episode, to maintain intravascular volume in a patient with potential distributive shock. Concurrently, the use of antihistamines, specifically clemastine and later levocetirizine, targeted the pruritus and flushing. These agents are likely not the primary drivers of hemodynamic stability but serve to mitigate the significantly uncomfortable cutaneous symptoms that often signal the return of systemic mediators. The case demonstrates a layered approach to stabilization: while epinephrine fixes the airway, breathing, and circulation, adjunctive therapies like antihistamines are deployed for quality of life and symptom deceleration. The administration of transparent 500 mL normal saline solutions during the second episode was pivotal, as early volume depletion and hypovolemia can mimic or exacerbate the progression of anaphylactic shock, complicating accurate assessment of drug efficacy.

Finally, the management logic of the care team faced a crucial decision pathway between escalating therapy with continuous infusion versus an expectant observational approach. As the patient experienced four anaphylactic episodes totaling 12 hours of admission, standard care typically mandates continuous infusion pumps to preemptively treat relapse, yet the case report notes that alternative pre-emptive strategies were considered due to their serious risks and the lack of literature on multiphasic courses. Fortunately, neither approach was ultimately necessary as symptoms spontaneously resolved after the 24-hour mark without renewed relapse. This outcome highlights that while protocols often err towards aggressive intervention due to fear of recurrence, the specific trajectory of multiphasic anaphylaxis can sometimes allow for "expectant" monitoring if the patient’s condition has improved. Myriad protocols exist, and often "trusting" that every episode will not resolve as transiently as the patient's response here led to unnecessary escalation in other clinical settings.

This case reinforces the importance of having a theatre-grade management protocol where escalation can be achieved quickly without hesitation, including having 0.5 mg epinephrine readily available even if 0.3 mg auto-injectors are the standard in the ED setting. Ultimately, therapeutic decisions must balance the risks of overtreatment with the higher mortality risk associated with undertreatment, particularly in complex multiphasic reactions where titration and volume status must be managed centers with 1:1 attention rather than a standard walk-through approach.

## Section 5: Post-Acute Management, Outcomes, and Systemic Recommendations

The transition from the intensive care setting to standard care serves as a critical milestone in the management of anaphylaxis, particularly in multiphasic courses where prolonged observation is required. In this case, the decision to transfer the patient back to the standard internal care ward was contingent upon a period of approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylactic shock. While standard aggressive protocols often recommend strict observation for 6 to 12 hours following diagnosis to detect biphasic recurrence, the multiphasic nature of this presentation necessitated a more conservative approach to ensure safety. The clinical lesson here is that for complex, relapsing anaphylaxis, the threshold for discharge from intensive monitoring should remain higher than for typical first-time episodes. The patient met the safety criteria for transfer once she demonstrated stability after the 24-hour mark, allowing for the continuation of antihistamine therapy on a ward setting. However, discharge planning must never be rushed in multiphasic scenarios; the risk window can extend beyond the first 24 hours, and clinical judgment must weigh the severity of the initial episodes against the stability of the current hemodynamic profile.

Long-term patient education is essential to mitigate the risk of future anaphylactic events, focusing on pharmacological prophylaxis and self-rescue skills. Upon discharge, the patient was started on the antihistamine levocetirizine to manage persistent pruritus and skin symptoms, but education emphasized its role in adjunctive comfort rather than acute reversal. More critically, the patient was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector and taught proficiency in its use. In this specific case, she received a device containing 0.3 mg of epinephrine. While guidelines often simplify adult adult dosing to a maximum of 0.5 mg, the management of these specific regional guidelines and the availability of 0.3 mg devices often dictate the points of reliance for patients. Despite the successful clinical course in this individual instance, systemic barriers remain significant regarding evidence-based management for patients presenting with biphasic or multiphasic reactions. A primary area of contention remains the use of corticosteroids; while standard practice in the acute setting and included in the hospital protocol utilized 30 mg of prednisone and 8 mg of dexamethasone, recent literature and guidelines increasingly advise against routine corticosteroids in anaphylaxis treatment. Conversely, the shortage of specific evidence-based guidelines for multiphasic anaphylaxis is a more critical barrier. Treatment strategies largely mirror standard anaphylaxis management with possible adjustments for dosage, yet risk stratification is difficult due to the exclusion of broad predictors. Facilities like the one in this case rely on expert consensus of local protocols rather than standardized international data, leading to inconsistencies in monitoring duration and adjunctive therapies across institutions.

Future management of patients with atypical or complex anaphylaxis requires addressing this dearth of research through collaborative data collection. The rarity of multiphasic anaphylaxis makes the development of true evidence-based strategies a great challenge, as individual case reports are insufficient to establish conservative, broad guidelines. However, creating a registry of such cases, including data on salient patient presentations, suggested management strategies like epinephrine titration or observation periods, and outcomes, could provide the backbone for data-driven expert-based recommendations. This longitudinal epidemiological data would help validate specific predictions for recurrence and support the development of standardized protocols that balance healthcare resources with the patient safety requirements inherent to this high-risk subset of anaphylactic reactions.
