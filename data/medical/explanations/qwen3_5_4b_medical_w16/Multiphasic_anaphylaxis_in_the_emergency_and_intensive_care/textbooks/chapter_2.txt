Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Recognition and Differential Diagnosis in the Acute Settings

## Initial Assessment: Interpretation of Vital Signs and Cutaneous Signs in Anaphylaxis

The most common pitfall in recognizing anaphylaxis in the emergency setting is underestimating the severity of symptoms before skin signs are fully developed or misinterpreting early resolution as stability. In the case presented, the patient arrived with a documented systemic reaction to clarithromycin clinically, characterized by hypotension and hypoxemia immediately preceding visible cutaneous signs. Her vital signs were significantly deranged, showing a non-invasive blood pressure of 74/50 mmHg and a peripheral oxygen saturation of 88% on room air. These findings align with the second-degree hemodynamic instability typical of anaphylactic shock, driven by mast-cell mediated vasodilation and increased vascular permeability leading to relative hypovolemia. It is critical for residents to recognize that while cardiac and lung auscultation were normal initially—relieving immediate concern for cardiac tamponade or massive airway obstruction—the patient was already lightheaded and agitated. The cutaneous examination revealed generalized erythema and urticaria over the entire body, a "full-surface" reaction that correlates with the systemic nature of the mediators (histamine and leukotrienes) released. An important teaching point derived from this case is that vital signs in adults with known allergies may deteriorate to shock (circulatory compromise) even before the classic angioedema or airway obstruction symptoms are detected. The initial drop in blood pressure represents a medical emergency requiring simultaneous physical assessment of circulation and oxygenation, regardless of the presence of a normal heart rate or appropriate respiratory effort, which are often misinterpreted as stability until the intensity of the reaction heightens.

## Differential Diagnosis: Recurrence Patterns versus Sustained Reaction

A key challenge in the acute management of anaphylaxis is distinguishing between a biphasic or multiphasic reaction that resolves and reappears later versus a sustained reaction where the initial diagnosis was incomplete. This patient presented with an initial “good response” to epinephrine and supportive care during admission, implying a successful counter-negative effect of the medication. In contrast to sustained shock, which would fail to respond to additional epinephrine or supportive fluids despite adequate dosing, this patient’s initial epinephrine dose successfully restored hemodynamics. However, this was not the primary concern, as the recurrence of symptoms within the first 12 hours of admission signaled a biphasic pattern. In the context of the case, the differential diagnosis must rigorously account for the absence of re-exposure; the patient was not reintroduced to clarithromycin during the hospital stays, yet symptom intensity eventually resolved spontaneously. This distinguishes multiphasic anaphylaxis from refractory anaphylaxis, which implies persistent symptoms requiring various mechanisms of action rather than return of the clinical picture. The lesson here is that a period of observation must remain active even after clinical resolution. Furthermore, doctors must consider the slow absorption of substances like antibiotics or locally administered medications, but the absence of continuous dosing coupons or contact suggests that minor contaminants or residual systemic cytokines are adequate explanations, provided the second set of triggers (clindamycin rash) suggests a gradual sensitization.

## Identifying the Trigger: Clarithromycin versus Beta-Lactam Cross-Allergy

Accurate identification of the inciting agent is fundamental to longitudinal management, yet differentiation between current trigger exposure and historical sensitization is a common diagnostic hurdle. This patient presented with a history of a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin (a beta-lactam), which involved localized facial swelling but no systemic signs, and a subsequent switch to clarithromycin (a macrolide) after forty-one days of clindamycin. Despite the patient's known beta-lactam allergy, the acute severity of the response to clarithromycin suggests a direct class-specific reaction rather than a cross-allergy between the macrolide and beta-lactam. However, the prompt for this section requires a recognition that the macrolide class can present with systemic reactions similar to that of beta-lactams in screened patients. The critical diagnostic decision point in this case was the avoidance of skin testing during the acute phase, contingent on the known risk of epinephrine-induced reaction. Because the reaction was clipped (improved) rapidly with intramural fluid, the focus was on immediate stabilization rather than allergen identification under the skin. Clinicians must rely on the temporal relationship with drug administration (introduced a day before the rash) rather than historical sensitization data when deciding on immunization, as the specificity of the beta-lactam cross-reactivity would be ambiguous. The historical presence of local skin reactions to a wasp sting and the beta-lactam allergy served as comorbidities for her vulnerability to severe reactions, but the immediate resolution to clarithromycin focused the case on direct exposure rather than a delayed cross-immune response.

## Timing: The Critical Observation Window

The management window for observation following anaphylaxis acts as the filter for multiphasic anaphylaxis identification. In the presented case, the patient was admitted to the acute internal ward after an initial resolution, with subsequent symptoms developing before she was even transferred to the intensive care unit. Guidelines such as the UK Resuscitation Council UK and Dutch NHG suggest varying observation periods, usually between 4 to 6 hours, but this patient’s recurrence of hypotension and hypoxemia within 12 hours underscores the need for a minimum 24-hour observation window to rule out biphasic reactions. The patient’s discharge occurred only after approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylaxis, and the recurrence occurred in the window where most reactions are expected to manifest (typically within the first 72 hours, though this patient recurred earlier). An observation of less than 24 hours leads to a high false negative rate for identifying high-risk patients who require discharge advice rather than long-term ICU confinement. In the case, the nurse observed visual progression from the first episode of symptoms to the fourth episode in a targeted manner, leading to the identification of a symptomatic patient who would eventually transition to standard care but only after multiple doses of epinephrine were required. The critical teaching point is that observation protocols must consider both the physiological status and the specific pharmacokinetics of the anesthesia and immunological recovery. The brief improvement symptoms are not a guarantee of safety, as the elevated tryptase levels in subsequent episodes confirm the maintenance of mast cell activation beyond the initial acute phase of the patient’s timeline.
