Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 5: ## Section 5: Post-Acute Management, Outcomes, and Systemic Recommendations

The transition from the intensive care setting to standard care serves as a critical milestone in the management of anaphylaxis, particularly in multiphasic courses where prolonged observation is required. In this case, the decision to transfer the patient back to the standard internal care ward was contingent upon a period of approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylactic shock. While standard aggressive protocols often recommend strict observation for 6 to 12 hours following diagnosis to detect biphasic recurrence, the multiphasic nature of this presentation necessitated a more conservative approach to ensure safety. The clinical lesson here is that for complex, relapsing anaphylaxis, the threshold for discharge from intensive monitoring should remain higher than for typical first-time episodes. The patient met the safety criteria for transfer once she demonstrated stability after the 24-hour mark, allowing for the continuation of antihistamine therapy on a ward setting. However, discharge planning must never be rushed in multiphasic scenarios; the risk window can extend beyond the first 24 hours, and clinical judgment must weigh the severity of the initial episodes against the stability of the current hemodynamic profile.

Long-term patient education is essential to mitigate the risk of future anaphylactic events, focusing on pharmacological prophylaxis and self-rescue skills. Upon discharge, the patient was started on the antihistamine levocetirizine to manage persistent pruritus and skin symptoms, but education emphasized its role in adjunctive comfort rather than acute reversal. More critically, the patient was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector and taught proficiency in its use. In this specific case, she received a device containing 0.3 mg of epinephrine. While guidelines often simplify adult adult dosing to a maximum of 0.5 mg, the management of these specific regional guidelines and the availability of 0.3 mg devices often dictate the points of reliance for patients. Clinicians must ensure that this interaction is taught correctly, highlighting that pre-hospital and in-hospital auto-injectors minimize delay but may result in underdosing if the patient is over the weight threshold for the recommended dosage. Analysis of the patient's outcome reveals a distinctive pattern of clinical improvement that informs prognosis in multiphasic anaphylaxis. Throughout the four episodes of anaphylactic shock observed during the patient's hospitalization, the intensity of symptoms progressively lessened, ranging from deep hypoxaemia, hypotension, and severe dyspnoea at the initial ED presentation to milder dyspnoea and rash during final recurrence episodes. This natural history suggests that multiphasic anaphylaxis may not always involve an equal or escalating severity of mediators with each flare; recognizing this favorable trajectory can inform the confidence clinicians have when transitioning patients out of intensive monitoring. However, this pattern does not guarantee resolution, as the heightened baseline tryptase levels and the suspicion of systemic mastocytosis indicate an underlying pathology that predisposes the patient to future risk. The long-term safety of this course is supported by the fact that the patient had no further episodes of anaphylaxis following discharge, though ongoing monitoring is necessary to confirm that this resolution is not transient.

Despite the successful clinical course in this individual instance, systemic barriers remain significant regarding evidence-based management for patients presenting with biphasic or multiphasic reactions. A primary area of contention remains the use of corticosteroids; while standard practice in the acute setting and included in the hospital protocol utilized 30 mg of prednisone and 8 mg of dexamethasone, recent literature and guidelines increasingly advise against routine corticosteroids in anaphylaxis treatment. Conversely, the shortage of specific evidence-based guidelines for multiphasic anaphylaxis is a more critical barrier. Treatment strategies largely mirror standard anaphylaxis management with possible adjustments for dosage, yet risk stratification is difficult due to the exclusion of broad predictors. Facilities like the one in this case rely on expert consensus of local protocols rather than standardized international data, leading to inconsistencies in monitoring duration and adjunctive therapies across institutions.

Future management of patients with atypical or complex anaphylaxis requires addressing this dearth of research through collaborative data collection. The rarity of multiphasic anaphylaxis makes the development of true evidence-based strategies a great challenge, as individual case reports are insufficient to establish conservative, broad guidelines. However, creating a registry of such cases, including data on salient patient presentations, suggested management strategies like epinephrine titration or observation periods, and outcomes, could provide the backbone for data-driven expert-based recommendations. This longitudinal epidemiological data would help validate specific predictions for recurrence and support the development of standardized protocols that balance healthcare resources with the patient safety requirements inherent to this high-risk subset of anaphylactic reactions.
