Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Section 1: Pathophysiology and Classification of Complex Anaphylactic Courses

To understand the clinical presentation of anaphylaxis, one must first understand the fundamental biology of mast cell degranulation. Anaphylaxis is a systemic reaction mediated primarily by the release of pre-formed and newly synthesized mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, and platelet-activating factor from mast cells and basophils. Typically, this process is triggered by antigens stimulating IgE-mediated cross-linking, leading to an immediate surge in inflammatory chemicals that compromise vascular tone and airway integrity. However, the case of the 60-year-old woman presents a more complex scenario known as residual or delayed mast cell activation. In standard anaphylaxis, mediator kinetics imply that symptom resolution signifies a successful neutralization of this inflammatory cascade. In contrast, multiphasic courses suggest that the initial antagonism of mediators—whether by epinephrine, antihistamines, or natural clearance—is transient or insufficient to abolish the endogenous inflammatory drive. This indicates a pathophysiology where mast cell reactivation or a stored reservoir of mediators is still active even after clinical stabilization. In the context of this patient, the analysis pointing toward systemic mastocytosis, despite meeting only minor diagnostic criteria, suggests a potential underlying expansion of mast cells which are less urectile and continue to release mediators than typical non-innesflammatory mast cells, potentially causing a secondary wave of symptoms that mimics a new reaction even without allergen re-exposure.

The classification of these recurrences is critical for guiding clinical observation and management, differentiating between a simple biphasic course and a potentially more dangerous multiphasic event. Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined by major guidelines, such as the UK Resuscitation Council, as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent resolution, provided there is no re-exposure to the potential allergen. The case presented a distinct progression, moving beyond this typical definition to a multiphasic event characterized by multiple relapses fitting anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of hospital admission, totaling four distinct episodes. This distinction is vital because, while the formula for biphasic anaphylaxis (recurrence within 78 hours post-exposure) is well understood, multiphasic courses represent a phenotypic challenge where the patient remains in an aroused state of immune activation. In our patient, the intensity of symptoms varied with each episode, decreasing from deep hypoxaemia and hypotension to mild rashes, suggesting a changing biological response to the same trigger. This variability implies that while the first reaction represented a severe mitochondrial and hemodynamic stress (shock), subsequent waves were similarly managed but required sustained vigilance, highlighting that the biological load was not a single surge but a sustained disturbance.

The mechanism of recurrence is often framed around the kinetics of mediators versus the therapeutic action of epinephrine. A delay in the clearance of mediators or a further set of mast cells degranulating due to a specific threshold of sensitivity is postulated. Unlike re-exposure, which resets the IgE challenge, recurrence is an intrinsic failure of the internal regulatory mechanism. In this specific case, the prescribed 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector was used for the initial episodes, but given the patient's body weight of 104 kg and the fact that the maximum recommended dose is 0.5 mg, the first physiological wave of mediators was clearly under-treated relative to her aspirated mass. This pathophysiological under-dosing contrasts sharply with the pathophysiology of biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis where high body weight often correlates with higher baseline mediator content. The elevated baseline tryptase levels further supported the hypothesis that this patient had an inherently higher mast mediator burden, consistent with possible systemic mastocytosis. Consequently, standard dosing assumptions become less than precise when the pathophysiology involves a "heavy basket" of mast cells. Furthermore, the consensus on corticosteroids is shifting; while the patient received prednisone and dexamethasone due to protocol, recent literature questions their role in reducing recurrence rates. Understanding that coolant steroids do not prevent mast cell swelling or mediator release is essential for recognizing why the recurrences still happened despite the steroid therapy used.

Despite the standard recommendation that the majority of patients do not experience recurrence, the variation in reported incidence of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, ranging from 0.4% to 6.3% with most data pointing to 4-6%, necessitates proactive consideration of these risk factors. For the medical student, the lesson from this case is that "single-dose" assumptions, whether regarding steroids or observation periods, often fail in high-risk cohorts. The case demonstrates a tracked period of observation within the first 12 hours which is clinically aggressive, ensuring that the temporary resolution of symptoms does not mask a delayed recurrence. In the case, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit due to the first relapse, recognizing that the simple hospital ward observation might not capture the dynamic changes of multiphasic shock. Therefore, understanding the pathophysiology of delayed mast cell mediator release allows clinicians to recognize that a patient who resolves from an initial shock is not clinically safe if they present the same signs again within 78 hours, even if the same allergen is not taken. This biological understanding justifies the continued epinephrine access, the monitoring of tryptase levels, and the ICU transition to prevent a fatal outcome as potentially described in similar severe follow-up data.
