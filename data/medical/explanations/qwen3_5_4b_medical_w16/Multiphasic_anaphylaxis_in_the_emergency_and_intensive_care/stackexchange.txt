### [Management] In the context of this patient's weight (104 kg), why was the 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector insufficiently effective initially, and what is the recommended standard dosing for an adult over 50 kg?

In this case, the 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector was insufficient primarily due to the patient's significant body weight (104 kg), which deviates from the standard demographic profile for which 0.3 mg devices are historically calibrated. Although current guidelines recommend a weight-based calculation of 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, with a maximum total dose of 0.5 mg for adults, many organizations simplify this recommendation to a fixed 0.5 mg dose for all adults over 50 kg to ensure adequate receptor blockade.

However, for a patient weighing 104 kg, the weight-based calculation would initially suggest a dose approaching 1 mg. While the 0.5 mg cap acknowledges diminishing returns at higher weights, 0.3 mg is still considered a potentially critical underdosing for large adults compared to the 0.5 mg standard. As documented in this case, the patient demonstrated an initial "good response" despite the suboptimal 0.3 mg dose, followed by recurrent anaphylaxis requiring escalation to 0.5 mg. This highlights that reliance on 0.3 mg auto-injectors in heavier adults (over 50 kg) often mandates a critical delay in escalation or results in therapeutic failure, particularly in complex presentations like multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Therefore, the recommended standard dosing for an adult over 50 kg is **0.5 mg of intramuscular epinephrine**, not 0.3 mg. Clinicians should be aware that 0.3 mg auto-injectors are designed typically for children or smaller adults and should not be the default starting point for patients weighing above this threshold.

### [Clinical Pearls] What specific clinical distinction does the Resuscitation Council UK make that differentiates true biphasic anaphylaxis from sustained anaphylaxis with transient response to epinephrine?

**Clinical Teaching Point: Distinguishing Biphasic from Sustained Anaphylaxis**

In the discussion of our presented case of a patient with clarithromycin-induced multiphasic anaphylaxis, the Resuscitation Council UK (RCUK) outlines a critical definition intended to guide monitoring protocols. The RCUK defines true biphasic anaphylaxis specifically as a recurrence of symptoms occurring **several hours after apparent symptom resolution**, which must occur in the **absence of further allergen exposure**.

This definition serves as the primary clinical distinction from sustained anaphylaxis characterized by a transient response to epinephrine or ongoing allergen absorption. In the context of our case report, Schneider et al. highlight that while the patient showed a good initial response to therapy, she subsequently developed recurrent episodes of shock, necessitating ICU admission for the first relapse. Differentiating true biphasic recurrence from a failed resolution of sustained anaphylaxis is challenging but vital for resource management. If the reaction had been sustained with only a transient response to epinephrine, ongoing absorptive exposure (such as slow-release medication) would need to be considered, whereas true biphasic anaphylaxis implies a spontaneous mediational rebound independent of new allergen exposure.

Consequently, recognizing this distinction helps clinicians determine the appropriate risk-stratified observation window. As noted in the case discussion, the identification of true biphasic features underpins the need for prolonged monitoring (often exceeding the traditional 4 to 6-hour standard) to ensure that the asymptomatic phase is not merely an ongoing reaction that resolves with continued therapy. The multiphasic presentation in our patient underscores that this distinction is not exclusive to single-recurrence events, but may extend to complex courses where delaying definitive observation may put the patient at risk for recurrent hypotension and hypoxemia.

### [Management] Despite the presence of epinephrine-responsive hypotension, why was patient transfer to the intensive care unit warranted following the first recurrence of anaphylactic shock?

In this patient, transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU) following the first recurrence was warranted not because the epinephrine failed to reverse the initial hypotension, but because of the **specific pathophysiology of multiphasic anaphylaxis** and the subsequent unpredictable recurrence of shock.

Although the patient demonstrated a **robust initial clinical response** to epinephrine and fluid resuscitation, the case describes a **multiphasic course** characterized by multiple relapses (four total episodes) rather than the single recurrence associated with biphasic anaphylaxis. Standard observation protocols for biphasic anaphylaxis typically focus on preventing relapse within the first 4 to 12 hours; however, in multiphasic cases involving high-risk underlying pathology like systemic mastocytosis (suggested here by elevated tryptase levels and prior clindamycin sensitivity), the risk stratification remains unclear.

The urgency for ICU admission is driven by two primary factors detailed in the case:
1.  **Frequency and Aggressiveness of Recurrence:** The patient experienced recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock **within the first 12 hours** of admission to the acute ward. This relentless escalation of symptoms (eventually totaling four episodes) required an environment capable of managing frequent re-stabilization of hemodynamics.
2.  **Advanced Monitoring Needs:** The preliminary management on the acute ward included no telemetry control. While the patient initially responded to medication, the variable intensity of symptoms (ranging from deep cyanosis to milder dyspnea) necessitated continuous cardiac monitoring and immediate access to advanced resuscitation capabilities that are standard in the ICU but not the general ward.

Ultimately, the decision to admit to the ICU reflects the clinical principle that **resistance to clinical stability in multiphasic anaphylaxis requires prolonged observation beyond standard disposition**, ensuring safety in the setting of an undefined and potentially severe underlying mastocytic disorder.

### [Pathophysiology] How do the assay strategies for plasma tryptase analysis differ when attempting to identify a peak mast cell degranulation event versus establishing a baseline for long-term monitoring?

**Clinical Teaching Point: Plasma Tryptase Assay Strategies in Anaphylaxis**

In the management of anaphylaxis, particularly when considering underlying mastocytic pathology, the timing of plasma tryptase analysis determines whether you are characterizing acute degranulation or chronic mast cell burden.

**Identifying Peak Mast Cell Degranulation**
To confirm active degranulation during an acute episode, the second sample should ideally be drawn **1–2 hours** following the onset of symptoms. Tryptase levels rise rapidly due to rapid mast cell mediator release; however, they are most prognostic within the first 4 hours of activation. As shown in the guidelines referenced within the case by Schneider et al., this window is critical for distinguishing acute allergic reaction from other causes of histamine or tryptase release.

**Establishing Baseline for Long-Term Monitoring**
To differentiate a standard allergic reaction from underlying systemic mastocytosis, a third sample must be obtained **at least 24 hours** after the patient is fully asymptomatic. This allows for the clearance of acute mediators and results in a measurement of the resting mast cell count. Elevated baseline levels after clinical resolution, as seen in the patient described by Schneider et al., suggest persistent mast cell clonal proliferation rather than simple histamine-mediated anaphylaxis.

**Case Application**
As noted in the report, the patient with multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin ultimately required both strategies to support a working diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis. While the report acknowledges that *ideal* guidelines recommend an initial immediate sample followed by these timed intervals, this specific patient did not receive samples during the *initial* workup of the first episode. Her subsequent testing—drawing samples one hour after the third episode and 27 hours later—allowed for the identification of persistently elevated tryptase levels during follow-up (Table 1). These results were pivotal in determining the high likelihood of mastocytosis, despite her failure to meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria at the time.

Therefore, for educational purposes and clinical management, one must distinguish the **therapeutic window** (capturing the spike to manage acute symptoms) from the **diagnostic window** (capturing the trough to identify chronic disease).

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical features in this patient's history and physical exam support a differential diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis rather than standard IgE-mediated anaphylaxis?

In this case report (Schneider et al.), the differential diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis (SM) versus standard IgE-mediated anaphylaxis was guided by the presence of specific historical and physical findings that suggested a fixed mast cell burden rather than transient allergen-driven mast cell degranulation. While standard IgE-mediated anaphylaxis typically resolves after adequate epinephrine administration, the clinical features in this patient strongly favored an underlying mastocytosis.

The most significant historical feature was the **multiphasic nature of the reaction**, with four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock over 12 hours despite full clinical response to epinephrine. Standard IgE-mediated anaphylaxis is unlikely to recur multiple times without re-exposure if episodes are effectively resolved; recurrence suggests a reservoir of excess mediators due to mast cell proliferation.

Second, **physical and historical findings of organ and tissue infiltration** were noted. The patient demonstrated **hepatomegaly** on abdominal sonography. This finding indicated cutaneous and visceral mast cell infiltration, a hallmark of SM not seen in standard atopy. Additionally, the history of depression and memory problems (neuropsychiatric symptoms) supports a systemic infiltrative process, as these granular mast cell clusters can accumulate in the central nervous system.

Finally, the **prior allergic history** played a role. The patient had a childhood rash/face swelling to amoxicillin and, crucially, a strong local skin reaction to a wasp sting without systemic involvement (i.e., urticaria/histamine reaction only). This distinguishes her melanocytic/allergic profile from her current severe systemic presentation, hinting at a progression in mast cell pathology or unmasked anaphylaxis.

While elevated tryptase levels (baseline and post-trigger) were the definitive laboratory confirmation, they were interpreted in the context of these clinical features. In patients over 50 presenting with multiphasic anaphylaxis and signs of systemic involvement (such as hepatomegaly or neuropsychiatric symptoms), a diagnosis of SM should be prioritized over standard IgE anaphylaxis, prompting mastocytosis-specific workup even if formal WHO criteria are not yet met.

### [Management] What evidence has driven the recent shift away from the routine prophylactic use of intravenous corticosteroids in the management of biphasic anaphylaxis?

The recent shift away from routine prophylactic intravenous corticosteroids in the management of biphasic anaphylaxis is driven by evidence demonstrating a lack of efficacy and the potential for resource burdens, as highlighted in the discussion of this case report.

While traditional practice supported corticosteroids to prevent recurrence, the data settlement is becoming clear. As this report discusses, a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found that only one study supported the use of corticosteroids, while 21 studies showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis recurrence. Furthermore, a 2019 study cited within the background emphasizes the relevance of side effects, noting an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Consequently, most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration.

In the clinical management of this specific patient, despite these evidentiary shifts, treatment was supplemented with corticosteroids after the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, illustrating the lag between guideline updates and clinical practice. However, given the rarity of biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis, the consensus increasingly favors managing recurrence through rigorous observation and monitoring rather than pharmacologic intervention with corticosteroids. The cornerstone of management remains prompt intramuscular epinephrine, consistent with the patient's initial response, while observation periods (often 4 to 6 hours under current protocols) are adjusted to balance patient safety with healthcare resource efficiency rather than relying on "traditional" steroid protocols.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Given the history of multiple relapses, what specific laboratory findings and WHO criteria regarding mastocytosis diagnosis were not fully met in the case report despite the high pre-test probability?

**Morning Report: Diagnostic Gaps in Suspected Systemic Mastocytosis**

While the patient’s presentation of multiphasic anaphylaxis carried a high pre-test probability for systemic mastocytosis, a formal diagnosis could not be established. This was attributable to specific deficiencies in both the assessment for WHO criteria and the execution of laboratory protocols, as detailed in the current case report.

First, regarding the **WHO diagnostic criteria**, formal diagnosis requires the confirmation of major histopathological features, which are **not assessable** in this instance due to **limited material obtained in the bone marrow biopsy**. In this patient, only **two WHO minor criteria** were identified. Because major criteria (which typically require adequate bone marrow tissue for cytochemical staining) could not be evaluated, and minor criteria alone were insufficient to certify the diagnosis without the tissue confirmation or exclusion of other conditions, the formal criteria were not met.

Second, regarding **laboratory findings**, specifically tryptase levels, the diagnostic utility was compromised by **non-adherence to the recommended sampling intervals**. Although tryptase levels were ultimately elevated (both during anaphylactic episodes and in follow-up), guidelines mandate a specific minimum of two samples—ideally three—to accurately determine the acute peak and establish a corrected baseline. The protocol used in this case deviated from these standards: the initial sample was taken one hour post-epiphase, and the follow-up sample was taken 27 hours after the last recurrence. This irregularity prevents the reliable interpretation of tryptase baselines and peak-to-baseline ratios required to confirm systemic mastocytosis definitively.

**Clinical Pearls:**
*   **Clinical Suspicion vs. Pathology:** High pre-test probability for anaphylaxis (e.g., multiphasic courses, elevated tryptase) is suggestive of mast cell disorders but is diagnostic only when supported by formal WHO criteria or met with caution in clinical management.
*   **Diagnostic Limitations:** A bone marrow biopsy is critical for assessing major criteria; insufficient material can preclude diagnosis even with strong clinical evidence.
*   **Tryptase Timing:** Serum tryptase levels require strict temporal sampling (baseline, peak, resolution) to distinguish between reactive and neoplastic mast cell states. Failure to adhere to these timing protocols can invalidate the biochemical evidence supporting the diagnosis.

### [Management] While the patient required multiple ICU observations, what is the current consensus standard for the minimum observation window after asymptomatic resolution to warrant safe discharge?

While traditional guidelines often recommend a minimum observation period of 4 to 6 hours following initial clinical resolution after anaphylaxis, there is currently **no absolute consensus** for multiphasic or highly complex biphasic cases without a clear risk-stratification framework.

The pathophysiology of biphasic anaphylaxis involves the delayed recurrence of symptoms, often driven by persistent systemic mediator release independent of new allergen exposure. As the Netherlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation note in the case report, approximately **half of biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours** after the initial event. Consequently, while the standard for single-episode anaphylaxis is typically managed within 4 to 6 hours, patients presenting with multiphasic disease—as seen in this subject—present an elevated risk that often necessitates extended monitoring.

In this specific case, despite an initial favorable response to epinephrine, the patient experienced recurrent shock episodes within the first 12 hours of admission. Consequently, she was monitored until **approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylaxis** prior to transfer to a standard ward. This aligns with the UK’s 2021 guideline recommendation for a **three-tier risk-stratified approach**, rather than a one-size-fits-all observation window. Clinicians must weigh established 4-to-6-hour standards against the individualized, available limits of safety in occult cases where biphasic recurrence risk extends beyond 12 hours, particularly when underlying mastocytosis features (such as elevated tryptase levels in this case) are suspected. Ultimately, safe discharge should be contingent upon clinically confirmed resolution of symptoms for at least 12 to 24 hours in high-risk profiles.

**Key Teaching Points:**

*   **Standard:** Traditional observation is 4–6 hours; biphasic recurrence risk peaks at 6–12 hours.
*   **Risk:** Multiphasic anaphylaxis requires enhanced monitoring; this case necessitated ICU until 24 hours asymptomatic.
*   **Guideline:** Recent UK guidelines (2021) utilize risk stratification over fixed duration windows.
*   **Secondary Prevention:** Corticosteroids are no longer routinely recommended for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis based on recent systematic reviews.

### [Clinical Pearls] The case highlights barriers in accessing high-potency epinephrine devices. What are the potential consequences of widespread underdosing in emergency settings compared to the risks of delayed treatment?

**Clinical Teaching Point: Epinephrine Dosing in Multiphasic Anaphylaxis**

The case presentation highlights a critical management barrier: the use of 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors in adults weighing over 100 kg. While standard dosing guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg or a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults, the prevalence of 0.3 mg devices leads to significant underdosing. In our reported patient—a 104 kg woman who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis—initial stabilization utilized standard 0.3 mg auto-injectors despite the high weight. Despite a prompt initial response, the patient required a total of four epinephrine doses, with the second two being compounded 0.5 mg doses to manage recurrence.

The consequences of widespread underdosing with 0.3 mg devices can be inferred from the case data. Patients receiving initial 0.3 mg doses were significantly more likely to require escalation of care with additional injections compared to those receiving the 0.5 mg formulation. In this patient, reliance on the lower dose initially masked the severity of the multiphasic response, predicting needed retreatment as the systemic mastocytosis-related mediator release eventually led to four separate episodes of shock. Conversely, if treatment were significantly delayed (failure to administer epinephrine promptly), the consequence is mortality. However, the specific failure of standard device availability contributes to a shift from ward observation to intensive care monitoring.

In summary, while delaying treatment increases mortality risk, the lack of high-potency initial access (0.5 mg) increases the risk of insufficient initial control, multiphasic recurrence, and prolonged hospitalization. Institutions should prioritize access to 0.5 mg devices for adults >50 kg to minimize underdosing and allow for earlier therapeutic escalation if necessary.
