Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## GLP-1 Agonists and Muscle: A Call for Expanded Pharmacovigilance in Metabolic Care

I usually think of Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists as GI drugs—nausea, vomiting, and constipation are the usual suspects. But sometimes, when we treat the gut, we accidentally signal the immune system to attack the wrong muscle.

This latest report from Brian Lam et al. presents a fascinating, albeit grim, paradox we're seeing more often in obesity and diabetes care: a patient who needed semaglutide to lose her weight ended up with a severe, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

The story isn't king sic, but the dynamics. It's not the rhabdomyolysis you see in tirzepatide cases with acute onset and rapid resolution. This was a slow creep of weakness over four months. It made me pause on one thing. We treat transaminases frequently. We ignore them if they're a bit high. Here's a case where a patient's muscles had to speak up a bit later because their "liver" alarms were often ignored.

## The Case: A Delayed Immunologic Reckoning

We have a female in her 60s with a known history of metabolic syndrome: obesity, fatty liver, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a d-Dimer history from PE. Three years prior to this event, she had "mild transaminitis," and liver work showed it was hepatic steatosis (NAFLD).

Semaglutide (0.25 mg) was introduced. Within hours, she got sick—GI complaints, which is expected, but also acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis. She got better after hydration and meds. The creatine kinase (CK) was only 337 IU/L, which isn't high. She felt deconditioned, but she didn't think much of it. She "fully" returned to herself.

Then, for 4 months, she got sicker. Dysphagia. Dysphonia. Proximal weakness. She couldn't stand or sit. That was 4 months after that initial dose.

Her CK spiked. Not days, not weeks, but over that 4-month stretch. It peaked at almost 4,000 U/L. A 12-fold jump. By the time she hit the outside hospital, her weakness was profound, and the biopsy results weren't encouraging.

However, her diagnostic story is the most interesting part. The typical markers for immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy—anti-HMGCR (myopathy), anti-SRP (astrogliosis)—were negative. She had a low SS-B antibody, which is rare in this context. Her autoantibody panel was a wash. So, how do we diagnose it? It wasn't the biopsy showing necrotic fibers, macrophages, and membrane attack complex on necrotizing fibers that told us it. She responded to immunosuppression (steroids + IVIG).

## Differentiating GLP-1 Related Myopathies

There are two main types of muscle injury we're seeing with GLP-1 agonists.

1.  **Rhabdomyolysis/Gut Toxicity:** As per previous case reports (like tirzepatide), patients can get acute injury. The CK goes up in the tens of thousands or lowers in days, and hydration is enough. This patient *had* a nascent GI insult, but the CK didn't clear in days; it climbed for months.
2.  **Immunologic Myopathy (IMNM):** In this case, the injury looked like a drug-induced autoimmunity, not direct muscle toxicity. The timeline—12x rise in CK over 4 months—suggests an immune lag rather than direct toxicity. The muscle biopsy revealed an inflammatory response to necrosis without classic idiopathic myopathy markers.

The real clue here is distinguishing this from something like "statin myopathy." Statins also cause HMGCR antibodies and IMNM. But this patient denied statins, and she never had persistent myopathy before.

Crucially, the transaminasemia prior to drug use was a head-source scare. Biopsy of her liver showed steatosis. Troponin was elevated, and cardiac MRI showed mild ED. Almost as if the heart and lungs had given up too. The "muscle" injury was the only definitive diagnosis in the final picture.

## Management and Prognosis

The treatment was standard for severe, refractory myopathy: Pulse methylprednisolone, followed by oral prednisone, and IVIG. It worked.

She improved significantly over two weeks, and CK normalized fairly quickly. The recovery allowed the steroids to be weaned in two months. She had a septic shock episode later from a pseudomonas urinary tract infection, but CK was normal in the hospital.

10 months into the case, she was back to baseline functional status. She was off steroids and no longer on immunosuppression. She is off GLP-1 agonists now.

## Bottom Line

1.  **Vigilance:** In patients with asthma, obesity, or fatty liver, monitor CK beyond just the cholesterol/weight loss plan.
2.  **Hospitalization:** If a patient on a new GLP-1 has GI intolerance, keep a muscle in mind.
3.  **Diagnosis:** Negative autoantibodies do not rule out drug-induced immune myopathy, especially when the treatment response is clear and no other cause (statins, alcohol) is found.
4.  **Cardio:** High troponin/BNP and muscle weakness can coexist in drug-induced myopathy, which explains the "dizziness" or cardiac strain.
5.  **Rechallenge:** Be clear that a rechallenge would be unethical after this outcome.

This case highlights how metabolic dysregulation, often treated by the same pathways, can lead to complex immune sequelae. As these drugs spread, we need to think beyond the "GI syndrome" and include the immune system in our pharmacovigilance.

**TL;DR**

*   If a patient develops progressive proximal weakness + CK elevation within months of GLP-1 use, suspect drug-induced IMNM.
*   Monitor CK even if "GI syndrome" is the main concern.
*   Negative autoantibodies (HMGCR, SRP) do not rule out an autoimmune reaction in the context of a drug lag.
*   Response to steroids/IVIG strongly supports a drug-induced etiology over obesity-associated myopathy.



## Don't Ignore the Dysphagia: Recognizing Dysphagia as an Early Myopathy Clue

It’s the feature that made this case report worth its weight in gold. If you walk into a patient with obesity and administration for GLP-1 receptor agonists, and they come in with weakness, you think rhabdomyolysis or deconditioning. You think autonomic neuropathy from the medication. But you might miss the neurological red flag completely if you don't check the pharynx.

This was just a 60something woman on semaglutide for visceral fat, and she didn’t just get weak; she lost her ability to swallow and speak properly alongside her muscles. In the case of this specific patient, noticing this triad of progression—where swallowing (dysphagia), voice (dysphonia), and general muscle power declined—was the key that unlocked the immunosuppressive treatment before it became chronic disability. 

Let’s zoom in on the case details and clinical pearls for recognizing this as a systemic inflammatory crossover rather than isolated drug toxicity.

### The Case in Review

We are talking about a patient initially seen with hepatic steatosis and obesity, transitioning to a single semaglutide dose (0.25 mg). Within hours, she got sick (GI side effects, AKI), and the CK went up only mildly. The kicker? She seemed okay enough to go home initially.

Then, almost a year later, she rewound on herself. The story here is about delayed presentation after a potentially "asymptomatic" start with drug initiation. Four months later, she presented not with the rapid crash we usually associate with GLP-1 rhabdomyolysis, but with a smoldering, autoimmune myopathy. 

*   **4 months post-initiation:** Progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, and inability to stand.
*   **Lab Work:** A massive jump from CK admission (337) to transfer admission (3992), a 12-fold rise.
*   **The Unusual Twist:** The autoantibody panel for common myositis (HMGCR, SRP, Jo-1, MDA-5) was negative. This makes it a seronegative immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), which is known to be hard to diagnose but responsive to steroids/IVIG.

The reason this case was so novel is it was the first reported case of IMNM thought to be linked to semaglutide. But beyond the drug link is the clinical presentation. Many providers dismiss bulbar symptoms as related to the drug's GI loading or dehydration. However, when it comes to muscle disease, bulbar symptoms are a hallmark of severe proximal weakness extending to the head and neck.

### My own thinking on "Bulbar Myopathy"

If I come in and I see dysphagia in a patient who is also getting weak, I am miles away from slap on the wall. Dysphagia and dysphonia in the context of metabolic syndrome or muscle weakness must be treated as a marker of **bulbar myopathy**. The weakness is top-of-systemic. It is not peripheral neuropathy, which would be asymmetric or distal. It is a muscle structure failure.

IgA nephropathy and myositis often go hand in hand. But in this modern era of GLP-1s, we have to think about IMNM more specifically. The biopsy findings were key: disseminated necrosis with acid base phosphatase positive staining. It was distinct from necrotizing polyradicular issues. It aligns closely with drug-induced IMNM.

### Treatment Course

The treatment pathway here is straightforward, though it took her full recovery some time off drugs.

*   **Immunosuppression:** Confirmed diagnosis, receive corticosteroids and IVIG.
*   **Treatment:** Pulse methylprednisolone, then oral prednisone + IVIG (2g/kg).
*   **Outcome:** CK normalized. Strength returned to baseline.

She was weaned off immunosuppression after 8 months and got off the glucagon-like peptide. The goal in IMNM is to stop the muscle litter. The case clearly shows that avoiding the pharmacological trigger is safe secondary to the immunotherapy, and rapid improvement happens without long-term steroids.

### Key Takeaways

*   Dysphagia and dysphonia should not be dismissed in metabolic patients with proximal muscle weakness as purely neurological or gastric issues; in this context, they signal severe bulbar involvement.
*   Masterful cases of drug-induced myopathy often have a prolonged course (months) unlike acute rhabdomyolysis; watch for delayed symptoms 4+ months post-initiation.
*   In immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with seronegative findings (no antibodies), clinical biopsy and response to immunosuppression are essential for diagnosis.
*   Quick withdrawal of the GLP-1 agonist combined with short-term immunosuppressive therapy (corticosteroids/IVIG) is the primary treatment strategy, allowing for recovery within months.
*   Be aware that IMNM is a rarer, more specific myopathy than simple myositis, often showing necrotic myofibres and myophagocytosis on biopsy.

Final thought: If you're seeing a patient with new severe weakness who avoids the bed, and they have trouble swallowing, think beyond the "GLP-1 rhabdo" label. Look for the metabolic overlap.

## The Seronegative Challenge: Diagnosing Necrotizing Myopathy without Anti-HMGCR/SRP

In the twenty-first century, the disability brought by myopathies rarely asks two questions; it demands a biopsy and a policy from your head. Often, we are learning that the battle of antibodies is a winnable war. Ramps of anti-HMGCR and SRP positivity are the gold standards for classic immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM). Statins, HMG-CoA reductase, and signal recognition particle are the familiar archetypes. But what happens when the gold standard is missing? What do you do when the patient presents with ragged, profound weakness and a negative antibody panel? How do we treat immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy in the seronegative patient?

The question is not just theoretical, however. Every day, we encounter patients where the biopsy is the only true path to prognostication. Take the case we are discussing. You have a female patient in her 60s. She has a history of obesity and fatty liver disease. She isn’t taking the heavy hitters of muscle toxicity like statins or macrolides. She is taking semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist commonly used for metabolic disorders. At first glance, you see nausea and vomiting after one dose of the drug, followed by gradual decompensation.

The clinical picture is incredibly clear by the fourth month. Proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a Creatine Kinase (CK) that climbs from 300 to nearly 4,000 U/L. This progression looks exactly like an immune attack on the muscle. In a typical evaluation, a workup includes a muscle biopsy. But here, the antibody nature of the case is what makes it so fascinating. She is seronegative. The anti-HMGCR was negative. The anti-SRP was negative. The myositis panel was a sea of red. NJOs, SRP, Jo-1, Mi-2; most panels were negative. She only had a low titre positive SS-B antibody, which was unexpected in this context but likely incidental. She does not have a statin. She does not have a familial history of myopathy. There is no history of alcohol or toxins. Yet, the weakness is relentless.

This is the seronegative challenge. In many myopathies, you wait for the antibody to come back to get confident. If you wait, a patient like this could easily walk off a wheel while the CK is still high and stiffness spreads. When I see the clinical features here, I notice how the weakness is disproportionate. The CK is typically high, but the muscle biopsy is the one that defines the entity. This biopsy is not yet a trick. The muscle biopsy shows necrotic and regenerating myofibres. CD68 staining shows macrophages actively phagocytosing the muscle fibers. You see acid phosphatase and cathenin membrane positive staining, which falls back negative patterns seen in IMNM. There is no tendency to be an autoimmune myopathy. There is direct evidence here of the necrotizing features.

Most importantly, the biopsy is not the only tell. The path of least resistance for treatment when you have a clinical suspicion of IMNM is to treat it. When you treat a patient with a sterile necrotizing myopathy, you are usually treating with corticosteroids and IVIG. This patient got 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone daily for five days, followed by oral prednisone and IVIG. The improvement was marked. Her CK normalized in two weeks. She was weaned off immunosuppression successfully.

Here, it’s not just about the rapid response to therapy, it’s about the safety of treating empirically when the biopsy says one thing. If you had to wait for that panel to clear after, you would have missed the window to initiate therapy before the myopathy caused permanent disability. This is why the standard of care is evolving. We learn from the statin-associated myopathy and the rare cases associated with monocles and drugs like semaglutide. But we must remember that the seronegative subset of myopathies are still significant. Some cases are obvious. This case, a third of the population with IMNM, remains seronegative. But they need treatment.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Messages

*   Maintain a high index of suspicion for Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) in patients with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and rising CK.
*   Muscle biopsy is essential when the antibody panel is negative to confirm necrotizing features and guide immunosuppressive therapy.
*   Treat empirically and aggressively with corticosteroids and IVIG even in seronegative cases if clinical features are characteristic; delay rarely allows for recovery.
*   Be aware that new pharmacological agents like GLP-1 agonists may present as a trigger for rare immune-mediated adverse effects.
*   Proximal muscle weakness and elevated transaminases on a biopsy showing steatosis should be differentiated by biopsy to avoid false-negative interpretations of underlying liver disease in the myopy.

Stay safe. Look for what you don't find. The seronegative myopathies are real, but they are treatable. Do not wait for the antibody to scream when the biopsy and the weakness are screaming for you.



## Rhabdomyolysis vs. Myopathy: Decoding the CK Kinetics in Drug Toxicity

**## The Diagnostic Dilemma: Adrenals or Immune System?**

You are managing a metabolic patient with obesity or type 2 diabetes. They are on a GLP-1 receptor agonist like semaglutide or tirzepatide. They present with nausea, vomiting, and elevated creatine kinase (CK). In a perfect world, it's just gastro-intestinal side effects and hydrate them. But then comes the twist. The weakness starts spreading. It becomes *progressive*. They can't stand, let alone speak. The CK keeps climbing over weeks and months.

This is where you have to stop and think hard. Is this direct muscle toxicity (rhabdomyolysis) or is it an autoimmune process going full assault mode (immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy or IMNM)?

The answer lies in the timeline. Imagine you see a drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. The muscle cells literally dissolve from the direct toxic effect of the drug, and the body clears the debris quickly. This typically presents with a massive spike in CK followed by a rapid normalization within days of stopping the drug and supportive care. They don't usually need steroids or IVIG.

Now, imagine the other scenario. A patient presents with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. Their CK is mildly elevated initially, then climbs to 10-20 times baseline over months. The injury isn't "burning out" the muscle; it's an inflammatory conquest. They respond to immunosuppression.

In the post, we'll look at a patient who took a GLP-1 and actually turned into a first case report of IMNM, proving that the drug isn't always the toxin; sometimes, it's just the spark that lights the furnace.

**## Semaglutide and the 4-Month Slump**

Let’s look at the case that made us see this clearly. A 60-year-old female with NAFLD and obesity got a single dose of semaglutide 0.25 mg.

She had a go. Within hours: N/V, AKI, metabolic acidosis. She got cared for in the ER, hydrated out of it, and was discharged. Her CK was 337 U/L, only mildly elevated for someone who had just bad-fast metabolism issues. No classic rhabdomyolysis story.

Then, she went home. She was deconditioned. Over the next 4 months, something happened. She gained strength in her legs over months? Wait, wrong way. She lost it. Progressive proximal muscle weakness. Dysphagia. Dysphonia.

4 months post-initiation, she was in an outside hospital with CK 3992 U/L, a nearly 12-fold jump from baseline. She didn't have hyperpigmentation or rash. She had weakness *everywhere* — legs, throat, voice.

When they took her muscle biopsy? It wasn't toxic necrosis from direct soaking. It showed necrotic muscle fibers, inflammatory macrophages (CD68+), and myophagocytosis. It looked like classic IMNM, even though her HMGCR and SRP antibodies were negative. It was a seronegative case, but the biopsy screamed "Immune-Mediated".

**## The Treatment Gap: Steroids During Recovery**

Here is the clincher: How do you treat this?

If you treat this like rhabdomyolysis, you give the patient fluids and check their electrolytes every 6 hours.

If you treat this like an IMNM, you give the patient immunosuppression.

In this case, the patient responded to pulse methylprednisolone and IVIG. Her CK normalized. They came off the drug and the immunosuppression without issues.

This difference in response tells you everything. Direct toxic injury stops once the drug is stopped. Autoimmune injury requires the immune pathways to be blocked. If you stop the drug in "rhabdo" and the patient remains weak for 3 months, you are looking at the wrong diagnosis.

**## Why GLP-1s Might Be the Troubleshooter**

There isn't much evidence suggesting GLP-1s *cannot* trigger immune reactions, just that this is rare.

There is a case involving tirzepatide (another GLP-1) that developed acute rhabdomyolysis within days starting a course, with normalization in days with hydration. That fits the "Toxic" profile.

However, the semaglutide case showed a slow progression over months. The absence of other drugs, family history, or supplements made semaglutide the likely culprit both in the amelioration of CK levels and response to immunosuppression.

The key takeaway for pharmacologic vigilance: A GLP-1 RA is not always just a glucose or weight pill. If you see a GLP-1 and a *progressive* myopathy, you must think immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy.

**## Take-Home Points**

*   **Distinguish the kinetics:** Direct drug toxicity usually causes acute, massive CK elevation with quick normalization upon cessation. Immune-mediated IMNM causes progressive CK elevation over weeks to months.
*   **The Troop indicators:** Dysphagia, dysphonia, and progressive proximal weakness in a patient on a GLP-1 require a muscle biopsy to rule out IMNM, even if myositis autoantibodies are initially negative.
*   **Treatment response matters:** Patients with rhabdomyolysis resolve with hydration and cessation of the drug alone. Patients with IMNM often require corticosteroids and IVIG for functional recovery.
*   **Seronegative doesn't rule it out:** Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative. Don't rule out drug-induced myopathy just because the autoantibody test is negative in endemic muscle disease settings.
*   **Think add-on, not risk:** If a patient develops severe muscle weakness after GLP-1 initiation, even if the initial presentation was GI side effects, consider withdrawal and immunosuppression if the course does not follow a standard toxic myopathy.

## Immunosuppression and Withdrawal: The Dual Strategy for Drug-Induced Myopathy

## The Unexpected Side Effect: GLP-1s and Muscle Injury

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are revolutionizing the treatment of obesity and type 2 diabetes, offering efficacy where others haven't worked. However, clinicians are increasingly aware of their spectrum of adverse events. While cardiac dysrhythmias and GI intolerance are common, the association with skeletal muscle pathology is far rarer. In a recent case, we presented a new paradigm of life-threatening myopathy triggered by this class of medication.

This case stands out because the patient developed an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following a single dose of semaglutide. It wasn't a typical metabolic crash or acute drug-induced rhabdomyolysis that resolves quickly. Instead, the patient presented with a progressive 4-month decline, severe proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a massive rise in creatine kinase (CK). The story isn't just about the drug; it's about how we recognize and manage the immune pathway that was mistakenly set off by these metabolic medicines.

## Diagnosis and the "Memory" of the Muscle

The diagnostic battle here was fought between a toxic myopathy, autoimmune connective tissue disease, and a deliberate decision to treat as IMNM. The laboratory results were a mixed bag. The patient had elevated transaminases, but a liver biopsy confirmed hepatosteatosis rather than muscle-derived liver effects.

The muscle biopsy was the clincher. We saw necrotising and regenerating myofibres with macrophage presence (CD68 staining) but notably lacked the dense lymphocytic infiltrate typical of classic polymyositis. This histological picture fits IMNM better. It was further complicated by a seronegative autoantibody profile—SRP and HMGCR were negative. This seronegative status mimics idiopathic myopathies, but the clinical timeline pointed toward a drug etiology.

## The Dual Therapy Strategy

The key lesson from this course is that some drug-induced myopathies do not simply regress upon withdrawal alone. Unlike the tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis cases where CK normalized within days of stopping the drug, this patient required active immunosuppression.

Her treatment involved two simultaneous arms. First, the semaglutide was stopped immediately. Second, she was treated with pulse methylprednisolone and follow-up with oral prednisone. She also received Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG). The result was a 12-fold drop in CK levels within weeks, leading to a complete recovery in functional status. She was eventually weaned off immunosuppression 10 months later, demonstrating that early cessation of the trigger drug followed by aggressive, but not prolonged, immunomodulation can resolve severe drug-induced pathology.

## Clinical Takeaways

*   **Distinguish IMNM from Rhabdomyolysis:** Early rhabdomyolysis resolves with fluids and discontinuation. A progressive CK rise over months suggests an autoimmune mechanism (IMNM) requiring immunosuppression.
*   **Stop the Offender:** In suspected drug-induced myopathy, immediate withdrawal of the agent is crucial to preventing the immune cascade from continuing.
*   **Seronegative Myopathy:** Be prepared for negative antibody panels. Negative SRP/HMGCR does not rule out drug-induced necrotising myopathy; treat based on histology and clinical picture.
*   **Monitor GLP-1x Safety:** As GLP-1s become ubiquitous, clinicians should be vigilant for muscle weakness and CK elevations, even after a single dose.

## Final Thoughts

This case highlights a crucial shift in how we view metabolic side effects. We often stop at the immediate systemic reaction, but some drugs induce a systemic inflammatory state that lingers. If a patient on a GLP-1 agonist presents with unexplained proximal weakness or elevated enzymes, consider this differential. Successful management hinges on stopping the trigger and balancing the immune response with steroids and IVIG when necessary.

Keeping the eyes open to these rare immune reactivity profiles improves patient safety for the thousands using these drugs. We are building the evidence to ensure the next generation of patients does not suffer our trial and error curve.
