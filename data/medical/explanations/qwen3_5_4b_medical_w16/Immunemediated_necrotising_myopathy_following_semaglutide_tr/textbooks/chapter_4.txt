Chapter 4: ## Management Strategies: Pharmacological Intervention for Immune Myositis

The initiation of immunosuppressive therapy in immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is critical for halting disease progression, particularly when the trigger is a centrally active medication like a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist. In cases of acute or rapidly progressive weakness, high-dose intravenous corticosteroids represent the first line of pharmacological intervention. As illustrated in this case, the patient received 1000 mg of methylprednisolone daily for 5 days following the onset of severe myalgia and weakness. This "pulse" regimen is designed to deliver a rapid anti-inflammatory effect to suppress the necrotic muscle fiber damage. Given the temporal relationship between the initial semaglutide exposure and the subsequent muscle failure, the immunomodulatory goal was to penetrate and block the autoimmune cascade while the offending agent was withdrawn. The rationale for pulse steroids over immediate oral administration in this context was the need for faster systemic control due to the severity of proximal muscle involvement and the risk of further deconditioning during the acute phase.

Adjuvant therapy is often required when a response to corticosteroids is delayed or insufficiently robust, a scenario common in cases of persistent severity or refractory features. In this patient, while there was a marked improvement in creatine kinase (CK) levels over two weeks, the clinical muscle weakness persisted, prompting the addition of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). The reason for initiating IVIG at 2 g/kg was to bridge the immunosuppressive gap provided by steroids alone, particularly given the patient’s severe functional decline and the susceptibility of restoring baseline function. IVIG is favored in acute, severe presentations due to its ability to modulate inflammation and neutralize autoantibodies without the need for route-specific access, making it effective in bridging the gap between an acute onset of weakness and the neutralization of underlying inflammation. It is particularly useful when the clinical course requires stabilization faster than steroid tapering alone can achieve.

Oral prednisone serves as the maintenance therapy and a cornerstone for the tapering protocol, transitioned from the intravenous pulse after an acute response is noted. This patient was placed on 1 mg/kg daily of oral prednisone following the completion of the 5-day pulse course. Tapering protocols in IMNM can be protracted, often requiring 6 to 12 months to fully reduce dosages. However, in cases where the trigger agent is removed and the myopathy responds to treatment, as seen here, clinicians can utilize a more rapid taper strategy to minimize steroid toxicity. This patient was successfully weaned off prednisone rapidly over 2 months due to sustained improvement and the normalization of CK levels. The key principle here is to monitor for minimal relapse and maintain functional independence, balancing the morbidity of chronic steroid use against the long-term risks of immunosuppression once the acute inflammatory drive is suppressed.

Monitoring the decline in CK and the recovery of muscle strength provide essential benchmarks for treatment efficacy and the decision-making process regarding tapering. In this case, the initial CK elevation was modest (3992 U/L) but dropped significantly over two weeks following treatment, correlating with the patient's functional recovery. While CK normalization is a strong indicator of response, functional assessment—often tracked via daily daily activities of daily living (ADLs)—remains the primary benchmark for full remission. The definitional marker for recovery extends to the normalization of biomarkers, such as CK, without the need for further immunosuppression. This patient’s return to baseline CK levels within 10 months validated the successful resolution of the myopathy, guiding the decision to discontinue IVIG and reduce steroid reliance significantly.

Facilitation of drug withdrawal success relies heavily on the prompt discontinuation of the suspected offending agent, which, in this case was semaglutide. While a standard pharmacological withdrawal strategy minimizes re-exposure risk, the specific goal here was to prevent the ongoing autoimmune stimulation exacerbated by pharmacological variation. The text notes the avoidance of the offending agent was critical alongside immunosuppression. Additionally, practical considerations such as the cost of the medication and the availability of alternatives informed the long-term management of the patient. The success of the withdrawal process is vital because continued exposure to the immunogenic agent can prevent recovery and lead to recurrent myonecrosis.

Assessment of risk for irreversible damage encompasses both cardiac and skeletal domains, necessitating multimodality imaging and laboratory vetting during the acute phase. In this specific instance, the elevated cardiac biomarkers included a HS-Troponin elevation and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) of 4358 pg/mL, alongside myocardial oedema found on cardiac MRI, suggesting potential myocardial inflammation. Myositis panels were negative for typical ANA, and liver biopsies confirmed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), which required differentiation from the metabolic side effects of the myositis itself. The case underscores the necessity of ruling out myocardial involvement, as cardiac myopathy can be a prominent feature of severe IMNM, and skeletal muscle biopsy is required to visualize necrotic fibers and inflammatory infiltrates with acid phosphatase and macrophage markers. Identifying early cardiac or skeletal damage and managing it with multimodal approaches ensures preservation of long-term organ function.
