Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Management] What was the recommended pharmacological intervention for this patient with severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and what specific clinical indicators influenced the decision to include intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in the treatment regimen?

For this patient with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), the recommended pharmacological intervention consisted of high-dose intravenous corticosteroids—specifically methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 5 days) followed by oral prednisone (1 mg/kg daily)—supplemented by a course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg split over 4 days.

The inclusion of IVIG in the treatment regimen was driven by two specific clinical indicators: **persistent weakness** and the presence of **severe features**. Despite the initiation of pulse steroids for acute stabilization, the patient exhibited profound disabling weakness (unable to stand or sit upright without assistance) and bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia and dysphonia). These severe attributes influenced the decision to escalate immunosuppression with IVIG to mitigate potential respiratory compromise and accelerate functional recovery. The patient demonstrated a marked response to this combination therapy, allowing for a rapid steroid taper and eventual return to baseline functional status while remaining GLP-1 agonist-averse.

### [Pathophysiology] Which autoantibodies are most strongly associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and what additional diagnostic challenge is presented by a patient who tested seronegative for these markers?

**Teaching Point: Anti-HMGCR and Anti-SRP in IMNM**

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is primarily defined by the presence of autoantibodies targeting the hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) or the signal recognition particle (SRP). However, a critical diagnostic challenge arises for approximately one-third of patients who are seronegative for these classic markers.

In this case report, the patient was seronegative for both SRP and HMGCR antibodies. Her diagnosis relied not on serology but on muscle biopsy findings of necrotizing myofibres with inflammatory infiltrates and macrophages, followed by clinical response to immunosuppression. This highlights that in seronegative cases, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion based on clinical features and histopathology rather than antibody panels alone. Furthermore, as noted in the background section, the absence of antibodies in this subset suggests that unidentified drugs or triggers—such as the GLP-1 receptor agonist semaglutide described here—may induce IMNM without the typical serological profile.

### [Pathophysiology] The muscle biopsy in this case revealed specific staining patterns; what immunohistochemical markers (e.g., CD68, acid phosphatase) were noted and how do they correlate with the diagnosis of necrotising myopathy despite the lack of classic autoantibodies?

In this case, the immunohistochemical evaluation provided definitive evidence for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) via **acid phosphatase** and **CD68** staining, which were critical given the patient was seronegative for the classic anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP autoantibodies.

The presence of **CD68 staining** identified a macrophage infiltrate engaged in **myophagocytosis**, while **acid phosphatase** highlighted the necrotic and regenerating myofibers. This pattern is characteristic of IMNM, where necrotic myofibres are cleared primarily by macrophage activity rather than T-cell invasion. This histologic profile supports the diagnosis and distinguishes it from polymyositis, particularly in the context of the "paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates" typically described in IMNM.

This case reinforces that approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative. Therefore, when classic autoantibodies are absent, clinical suspicion, temporal association with an offending agent, and confirmatory muscle biopsy are essential. The immunohistochemical markers validate the necrotising pathology, justifying immunosuppressive treatment despite the absence of classic serology.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How can the timeline of creatine kinase elevation and clinical progression distinguish this case of drug-induced immune-mediated necrotising myopathy from the acute rhabdomyolysis often associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists?

The distinction between immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and drug-induced acute rhabdomyolysis lies in the **kinetics of CK elevation** and the **clinical trajectory** following treatment. While both conditions can follow GLP-1 receptor agonist initiation, they represent different pathophysiologic entities with distinct management demands.

In this case of IMNM, creatine kinase (CK) elevation followed a **gradual, progressive timeline** rather than an immediate crisis. Although the patient presented with a mildly elevated CK of 337 U/L shortly after admission to the first hospitalization (2 days post-dose), the CK burden increased 12-fold to a peak of 3992 U/L over a **4-month period** of progressive weakness. This contrasts sharply with the typical acute rhabdomyolysis pattern described in case reports like the tirzepatide-associated toxicity, where CK spikes into the tens of thousands within hours to days and resolves rapidly upon drug cessation and hydration alone.

Furthermore, the **response to immunosuppression** serves as a critical differentiator. The patient’s CK levels normalized only after the administration of pulsed methylprednisolone and IVIG, along with the cessation of semaglutide. In her case, functional recovery over the long term and normalization of CK levels occurred only after an active immunosuppressive cascade was initiated; this is inconsistent with direct toxic rhabdomyolysis unless hydration and agent withdrawal are sufficient, which is not the standard for severe, persistent cases noted in the literature. Conversely, her clinical course mirrored drug-induced IMNM, characterized by a lag period between exposure and full manifestation of weakness, a high CK peak accessible by tissue necrosis and inflammation (evidenced by biopsy), and a requirement for biologic immunomodulation to achieve remission.

Therefore, for clinicians managing GLP-1 association adverse effects, a **slowly ascending CK elevation over months** requiring immunosuppression for resolution should trigger suspicion of IMNM rather than acute toxic myopathy. Recognizing this timeline difference prevents under-treatment, which can lead to permanent muscle loss or functional impairment in true immunologic cases, as seen with the successful weaning-off of steroids in this patient 10 months later.

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient with a history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and elevated CK, what was the clinical interpretation of simultaneously elevated transaminases that did not normalize upon CK normalization?

The clinical interpretation was that the simultaneous elevation in transaminases reflected the patient’s underlying Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD), rather than muscle necrosis or drug-induced hepatotoxicity. This determination relies on the kinetic dissociation between muscle and liver markers. While muscle breakdown can release both AST and ALT, these levels should normalize concurrently with creatine kinase (CK) if the source is myopathy. In this specific case, the patient’s transaminases (AST 191 U/L and ALT 165 U/L) persisted even after CK levels normalized following immunosuppression and semaglutide discontinuation. Given her biopsy-proven hepatosteatosis three years prior, the authors concluded that the transaminases indicated chronic hepatic pathology, effectively ruling out muscle-derived enzyme elevation.

**Clinical Pearl:**
In the setting of concurrent myopathy and elevated liver enzymes, transaminases that persist despite normalization of CK levels should be attributed to intrinsic hepatic disease (e.g., NAFLD) rather than muscle breakdown.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why were the presence of dysphagia and dysphonia considered critical findings supporting a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy with bulbar involvement?

Dysphagia and dysphonia were critical findings because they signaled a severe extension of the inflammatory myopathy into the cranial musculature, specifically confirming the presence of **bulbar involvement**. In the context of Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM), this extension distinguishes the pathology as a systemic feature requiring aggressive management, rather than a localized toxic reaction.

As detailed in the case report, the patient progressed to a state where she was "no longer able to stand or sit upright" and "noticed difficulty swallowing liquids and solids." These severe clinical features were instrumental in managing the patient as a "severe" IMNM case, warranting the prompt initiation of high-dose IV corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). This intervention was crucial to confirm the diagnosis through clinical response; unlike the direct toxic effect seen in other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (e.g., tirzepatide cases where CK normalizes within days after discontinuation), this patient's marked improvement correlated with immunosuppression and withdrawal from the offending agent. Therefore, the persistence of these bulbar symptoms alongside a 12-fold rise in CK supported the classification of an aggressive autoimmune necrotising myopathy requiring life-saving immunotherapy.

### [Management] Based on the successful course off semaglutide and immunosuppression, what guidance is advised for patients regarding the use of potentially causative medications in future clinical encounters?

Based on this case, the primary guidance for patients is to avoid future re-exposure to GLP-1 receptor agonists, as the cessation of these agents, rather than immunosuppression alone, was essential for sustaining remission. The authors emphasize that while the patient responded well to corticosteroids and IVIG, she was successfully weaned off immunosuppression only after her semaglutide was discontinued for several months. Sustained functional improvement over 10 months was achieved through the long-term avoidance of the suspected trigger.

Because the clinical course aligns with an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) that can mimic autoimmunity in this context, the experience necessitates caution in future clinical encounters for metabolic management. Dr. Lam et al. explicitly noted that a rechallenge would be unethical given the severity of the initial reaction and the probable causality. Therefore, patients who develop proximal muscle weakness, elevated creatine kinase, or dysphagia associated with weight-loss or diabetes medication initiation should be counseled to discontinue or avoid these specific medications to prevent recurrence.

### [Morning Report] What key findings from the cardiac MRI and echocardiogram supported the exclusion of active myocarditis despite the patient's presentation with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and elevated troponin?

In this case, the exclusion of active myocarditis was primarily supported by the absence of Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE) on cardiac MRI, which distinguishes myocardial necrosis from transient edema. While the patient presented with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and an elevated HS-troponin of 62 pg/mL, the echocardiogram demonstrated preserved systolic function with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65–70% and no significant valvular dysfunction.

The definitive pathological finding on the cardiac MRI was the presence of myocardial edema with mild mid-anteroseptal hypokinesis, but crucially, **no definite late gadolinium enhancement**. The absence of LGE indicates a lack of myocardial scarring or fibrosis, which characterizes established or persistent myocardial injury typically associated with active myocarditis. In contrast, stress-induced or self-limiting myocardial strains often present with edema that resolves without fibrotic changes. This distinction was reinforced by the clinical course; follow-up imaging confirmed the **normalization of prior abnormalities** over time. These findings, combined with no significant myocardial scarring or infarction, supported a diagnosis of non-active cardiac injury and allowed clinicians to avoid treating for active myocarditis in favor of managing the immune-mediated necrotising myopathy.
