## Contextual Background: GLP-1 Receptor Agonists, Metabolic Disease, and Immune-Mediated Myopathy

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists represent a pivotal class of pharmacotherapy currently utilized heavily in the management of metabolic disorders, particularly type 2 diabetes and obesity. Their mechanism of action involves the binding to GLP-1 receptors in the pancreatic beta cells and the central nervous system, stimulating insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner while simultaneously suppressing glucagon release and reducing appetite. Clinically, this efficacy leads to significant improvements in glycemic control with a generally low risk of hypoglycemia and a systemic effect of promoting weight loss and reducing cardiovascular risk. However, a growing body of clinical data has highlighted that beyond gastrointestinal intolerance and potential pancreatitis, these agents can impact skeletal muscle metabolism. The primary adverse effects typically observed are gastrointestinal, resulting in nausea, vomiting, and decreased appetite, which can indirectly contribute to weight loss and body composition changes via caloric restriction. More rarely, however, there are reports of acute muscle breakdown and rhabdomyolysis directly associated with this drug class, as exemplified by cases with tirzepatide, where creatine kinase (CK) elevations were dramatic and resolved rapidly upon discontinuation.

Understanding immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is essential to interpret the muscle pathology seen in this context. IMNM is a distinct entity characterized by aggressive proximal muscle weakness and high CK levels, but crucially, by necrotising myofibres with a specific paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates on histology. This distinguishes it from polymyositis, which is a lymphocyte-predominant inflammatory condition. The pathophysiology of IMNM is heavily influenced by specific autoantibodies targeting key cellular substrates. Anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) antibodies account for a significant portion of these cases. HMGCR antibodies target the enzyme involved in cholesterol synthesis, while SRP antibodies directly target the protein-membrane translocation system essential for protein synthesis. In some cases, these autoantibodies can be detected shortly after the initiation of the specific offending agent, suggesting a transient immune dysregulation triggered by the medication.

In the historical context of pharmacovigilance, statin-associated myopathy serves as the most significant benchmark for drug-induced IMNM. Statins are HMGCR inhibitors, and the overproduction of antibodies or chemically induced stress on HMGCR can precipitate an immune response where the body produces autoantibodies against the drug target as an attempt to clear it. While many patients on statins present with myalgia and mild CK elevation, a subset develops severe necrotising myopathy even after the statin is discontinued, a feature often previously misdiagnosed as toxic myopathy. Often, this presentation is followed by improvement with immunosuppression, which established the link between statins and antibody-mediated myopathy. This clinical recognition is critical, as it underscores that the identification of an antibody should not be mandatory if the clinical pattern fits, but the presence of antibodies helps move away from idiopathic classifications toward a defined immunologic etiology.

The primary challenge in this specific case involved identifying the etiology of IMNM without the presence of statins or other common triggers. The patient presented without serum evidence for HMGCR or SRP antibodies and was already seronegative for typical metabolically associated markers, presenting a diagnostic conundrum. This scenario forces clinicians to consider the possibility of a novel non-statins trigger for IMNM. This distinction is crucial for diagnosis, as idiopathic IMNM often requires prolonged or intensive immunosuppressive therapy unlike drug-induced IMNM, which may respond satisfactorily to withdrawal of the offending agent and short-term immunomodulation. Therefore, recognizing the unique presentation of autoimmune myopathy in the absence of a known HMGCR trigger, such as semaglutide, highlights the necessity for broadening the differential diagnosis of IMNM to encompass other metabolic pharmaceuticals as potential culprits, ensuring that pharmacovigilance extends beyond established causal links to statin components.

The trigger for the subsequent diagnosis was the administration of the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, semaglutide. ### Subacute Course: The Emergence of Proximal Weakness and Dysphagia
Following the initial recovery from acute gastro-nephrotoxity, the patient entered a subacute phase distinct in clinical presentation. Approximately four months after receiving the single dose of semaglutide, she began experiencing progressive proximal muscle weakness. The onset of symptoms was subtle, peaking in severity over the second and third months. This weakness was accompanied by bulbar symptoms, specifically dysphagia (difficulty swallowing liquids and solids) and dysphonia. This clinical trajectory—weakness progressing from the chest/shoulders to involve the jaw and pharynx—is highly characteristic of autoimmune myopathies. ### Symptom Distribution: Proximal vs. Distal, Bulbar Symptoms
The distribution of weakness was strictly proximal, sparing the distal extremities. The patient faced challenges primarily climbing stairs, rising from a chair, and arm abduction. Unlike polymyositis which can also affect distal muscles, the myopathic distribution of weakness due to IMNM is typically rapid and severe in proximal muscles. Furthermore, the presence of dysphagia indicates involvement of the mouth floor and intoleraniy of the throat, which can be a precursor to respiratory failure. The absence of rashes, skin changes, or signs of photosensitivity helps distinguish this from dermatomyositis. The distribution highlights the involvement of the limb girdles and bulbar muscles, consistent with the histological finding of widespread myofiber necrosis.

### Laboratory Trajectory: The 12-fold Rise in Creatine Kinase (CK)
Laboratory monitoring provided the crucial evidence distinguishing the etiology. On admission for the weakness, CK was elevated to 3992 U/L, a 12-fold increase from admission value and a significant rise from the baseline elevation during acute intolerance. This rapid and substantial increase in CK activity is suggestive of muscle breakdown, specifically necrosis. While rhabdomyolysis follows a similar pattern, it typically occurs acutely (within hours). A gradual, sustained rise in CK over four months points toward an evolving inflammatory process. The lack of corollary features like severe dehydration at the time of the peak CK, nor significant hematuria, further points away from urine-facing rhabdomyolysis. The persistence of elevated CK despite hydration and improvement post-immunosuppression reinforces the autoimmune nature of the myopathy.

### Distinguishing Early CK Elevation from Rhabdomyolysis Risk
A key teaching point is differentiating the initial admission (acute heart-failure-like picture with mild CK elevation) from the subacute phase (marked muscle weakness with severe CK elevation). In the case of semaglutide-associated rhabdomyolysis cases, typically, the CK elevation resolves rapidly (within days) upon drug discontinuation without the need for immunosuppression. In contrast, this patient had a 12-fold elevation sustained over four months, suggesting an underlying autoimmune process triggered by the drug. Furthermore, liver function tests (AST and ALT) remained elevated relative to CK normalisation, indicating a chronic hepatic component (NAFLD) rather than muscle breakdown. This distinction is critical for management, as it shifts the treatment paradigm from aggressive hydration and supportive care towards immunosuppression.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Integrating Histology, Serology, and Differential Diagnosis

In this case of suspected immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), the primary diagnostic challenge involved distinguishing between a direct toxic reaction to the GLP-1 receptor agonist, a latent autoimmune etiology, and a secondary stress response. The clinical team effectively utilized muscle histology as the definitive diagnostic standard, which revealed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres within the right vastus lateralis. In the context of necrotising myopathy, pathognomonic histological findings are the presence of necrotic myofibres surrounded by macrophages, evidenced here by the presence of CD68 staining indicative of myophagocytosis. A critical teaching point for future learners is that IMNM is characterised by profound necrosis driven by complement-dependent mechanisms rather than T-cell mediated inflammation; hence, the biopsy in this patient showed a notable paucity of CD3-positive T-cells, which typically distinguishes necrotising myopathy from polymyositis. This low inflammatory infiltrate, combined with the membrane attack complex positivity on necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres, confirmed a necrotising pattern over a hyperinflammatory myopathy, although it highlighted the immunological complexity of the patient's presentation.

Serological evaluation often serves as the second pillar of diagnosis, yet it presented a significant discordancy here despite being consistent with the biomarkers present in other forms of IMNM. The patient’s autoantibody panel was negative for anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), the antibodies most strongly associated with classic IMNM and statin toxicity respectively. Furthermore, her myositis panel was negative for Jo-1, MDA-5, and other primary inflammatory myopathy markers. This negative serological profile underscores the acknowledged fact that an estimated third of patients with IMNM are seronegative; therefore, the absence of autoantibodies should not disqualify a diagnosis of necrotising myopathy when clinical and pathologic features are aligned. To differentiate drug-induced toxicity from overt autoimmune myopathy, the team analysed the temporal relationship between the offending agent and the pathological process, alongside ruling out stress-induced causes. In typical cases of drug-induced toxicity or rhabdomyolysis associated with other GLP-1 agonists like tirzepatide, one expects a sharp increase in creatine kinase resolved within days of drug withdrawal without immunosuppression. ## Management Strategies: Pharmacological Intervention for Immune Myositis

The initiation of immunosuppressive therapy in immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is critical for halting disease progression, particularly when the trigger is a centrally active medication like a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist. In cases of acute or rapidly progressive weakness, high-dose intravenous corticosteroids represent the first line of pharmacological intervention. As illustrated in this case, the patient received 1000 mg of methylprednisolone daily for 5 days following the onset of severe myalgia and weakness. This "pulse" regimen is designed to deliver a rapid anti-inflammatory effect to suppress the necrotic muscle fiber damage. Given the temporal relationship between the initial semaglutide exposure and the subsequent muscle failure, the immunomodulatory goal was to penetrate and block the autoimmune cascade while the offending agent was withdrawn. The rationale for pulse steroids over immediate oral administration in this context was the need for faster systemic control due to the severity of proximal muscle involvement and the risk of further deconditioning during the acute phase.

Adjuvant therapy is often required when a response to corticosteroids is delayed or insufficiently robust, a scenario common in cases of persistent severity or refractory features. In this patient, while there was a marked improvement in creatine kinase (CK) levels over two weeks, the clinical muscle weakness persisted, prompting the addition of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). The reason for initiating IVIG at 2 g/kg was to bridge the immunosuppressive gap provided by steroids alone, particularly given the patient’s severe functional decline and the susceptibility of restoring baseline function. IVIG is favored in acute, severe presentations due to its ability to modulate inflammation and neutralize autoantibodies without the need for route-specific access, making it effective in bridging the gap between an acute onset of weakness and the neutralization of underlying inflammation. It is particularly useful when the clinical course requires stabilization faster than steroid tapering alone can achieve.

Oral prednisone serves as the maintenance therapy and a cornerstone for the tapering protocol, transitioned from the intravenous pulse after an acute response is noted. This patient was placed on 1 mg/kg daily of oral prednisone following the completion of the 5-day pulse course. Tapering protocols in IMNM can be protracted, often requiring 6 to 12 months to fully reduce dosages. However, in cases where the trigger agent is removed and the myopathy responds to treatment, as seen here, clinicians can utilize a more rapid taper strategy to minimize steroid toxicity. This patient was successfully weaned off prednisone rapidly over 2 months due to sustained improvement and the normalization of CK levels. The key principle here is to monitor for minimal relapse and maintain functional independence, balancing the morbidity of chronic steroid use against the long-term risks of immunosuppression once the acute inflammatory drive is suppressed.

Monitoring the decline in CK and the recovery of muscle strength provide essential benchmarks for treatment efficacy and the decision-making process regarding tapering. In this case, the initial CK elevation was modest (3992 U/L) but dropped significantly over two weeks following treatment, correlating with the patient's functional recovery. While CK normalization is a strong indicator of response, functional assessment—often tracked via daily daily activities of daily living (ADLs)—remains the primary benchmark for full remission. The definitional marker for recovery extends to the normalization of biomarkers, such as CK, without the need for further immunosuppression. This patient’s return to baseline CK levels within 10 months validated the successful resolution of the myopathy, guiding the decision to discontinue IVIG and reduce steroid reliance significantly.

Facilitation of drug withdrawal success relies heavily on the prompt discontinuation of the suspected offending agent, which, in this case was semaglutide. While a standard pharmacological withdrawal strategy minimizes re-exposure risk, the specific goal here was to prevent the ongoing autoimmune stimulation exacerbated by pharmacological variation. The text notes the avoidance of the offending agent was critical alongside immunosuppression. Additionally, practical considerations such as the cost of the medication and the availability of alternatives informed the long-term management of the patient. The success of the withdrawal process is vital because continued exposure to the immunogenic agent can prevent recovery and lead to recurrent myonecrosis.

Assessment of risk for irreversible damage encompasses both cardiac and skeletal domains, necessitating multimodality imaging and laboratory vetting during the acute phase. Identifying early cardiac or skeletal damage and managing it with multimodal approaches ensures preservation of long-term organ function.

This contrasts with idiopathic IMNM, where patients often require prolonged and intensive immunosuppressive management and may have lingering impairment in muscle strength or CK elevation for the majority of cases, highlighting the short-term risk mitigation and the direct link between remission and drug withdrawal.

The assessment of causality rests on three primary pillars: a reasonable time relationship, the exclusion of alternative causes or other diseases or drugs, and a clinical response associated with drug withdrawal. The six-month time gap (from 12-fold increase to 4 months) and the exclusion of statins (previously likely a confounder in HMGCR-positive myopathy) support the temporal link. Consequently, the probability of causation is established as probable, serving as a basis for future practice rather than an absolute proof of mechanism, though the combination of UMC criteria and the favorable outcome of withdrawal strongly favors a pharmacological etiology over concurrent idiopathic disease.
