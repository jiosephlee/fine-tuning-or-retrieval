Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Pathophysiological Context of Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

### Physiological uterine growth and leiomyoma behavior during pregnancy
Understanding the presentation of abdominal masses in pregnancy requires first establishing the normal physiological trajectory of the gravid uterus. During the second trimester, as seen in this patient, the uterus undergoes profound hypertrophy and hyperplasia of uterine smooth muscle, driven by increased estrogen and progesterone levels. This growth accommodates fetal development and is normally benign. However, uterine smooth muscle is also the substrate for uterine leiomyomas, benign tumors that share follicular characteristics with the uterine wall. Physiologically, leiomyomas are often hormonally responsive; their dual blood vessels and vascular capacity lead to expansion during the first and early second trimesters due to increased angiogenesis, followed by a smaller increase in the late second and third trimesters. In cases where masses are large or atypical, this physiological growth can be confounded by pathological changes. As observed in the presented case, large leiomyomas may undergo degeneration, torsion, or cystic transformation of their pedicles, which disrupts normal vascular supply. While the uterus expands to fit the fetus, the presence of solid, vascular, and often tender masses like those described (the 18 cm superior lesion) alters the abdominal contour significantly. A key pathophysiological principle to recognize during this timeframe is the non-linear growth pattern; while the myoma size increases gradually, sympathetic vasoconstriction or hypoxia can accelerate growth and induce necrosis, presenting as an "acute" condition in a patient with subacute background pathology.

### Presentation of complications: abdominal pain and mechanical dyspnea
Symptomatic masses during pregnancy are frequently misdiagnosed as normal parturient discomforts until the impact becomes severe enough to compromise maternal physiology. In this case report, the patient presented with severe abdominal pain and dyspnoea at 21 weeks gestation, indicative of "steric bulk" complications. Abdominal pain in pregnancy with a palpable mass often signals mechanical stress or ischemia rather than simple pelvic pressure. The upper left quadrant pain was likely due to the tension on the peritoneum as the superior mass (leinomata) stretched the uterine wall and compressed adjacent structures. Mechanical dyspnea, while less common as a primary symptom of pregnancy, is a critical red flag when abdominal masses are present. The uterus (and its associated masses) supports fetal growth but also acts as a compressive burden on the diaphragm; typically, the spine depresses to accommodate the mass. However, when masses extend into the subphrenic space, as seen with the upper 18 cm lesion extending from the left subphrenic region, they impede diaphragmatic excursion. This reduces lung capacity and creates a sensation of dyspnoea disproportionate to the fetal weight alone. Identifying this lack of ventricular clearance or cardiac dysfunction helps differentiate simple uterine enlargement from the restrictive lung disease associated with large, fixed, or pedunculated uterine tumors.

### Clinical examination findings: abdominal distension and bimanual assessment
Physical examination remains a cornerstone of diagnostic reasoning, though it is challenging in obese patients or those with significant underlying pathology. In this patient, the abdomen seemed more enlarged than expected for her gestational age, with a tender swelling and taut-elastic mass occupying the upper left quadrant. This disproportionate distension is a clinical cue to look for pathology beyond the fetus. The bimanual vaginal examination provided critical tactile feedback, revealing a distinct mass in the lesser pelvis that was fixed, hard, rounded, and partially bulging at the posterior fornix. Distinguishing an anterior wall ovarian mass from an intramural or adnexal mass attached to the uterus requires recognizing the "fixed" nature of the bulk compared to the mobility of normal adnexa. In this specific instance, the retroperitoneal and anterior pelvic masses created a false impression of a purely adnexal origin, as the superior mass mimicked decidualized endometrioma features. Recognizing the fixed nature of the lower lesion (9 cm fixed pelvic mass) helps the clinician suspect a larger myomatous origin that may be adherent to the uterine torus or posterior wall, rather than separate benign ovarian cysts. It is essential to note that assessment must be cautious regarding uterine fundal height, which may be obscured by the masses, necessitating careful measurement to prevent misdiagnosis of preterm labor or twins.

### Initial laboratory abnormalities: anemia, infection markers, and differential significance of fever
Laboratory values serve to quantify physiological stress and potential inflammation, though standard cut-offs must be interpreted carefully in pregnancy. Iron deficiency anemia is common in the third trimester due to physiological hemodilution, yet a dropped hemoglobin of 9.9 g/L or 7.5 g/L after surgery suggests significant blood loss or ongoing consumption from necrotic tissue. The case of the presented patient showed elevated ferritin and ferric carboxymaltose levels, requiring transfusion to correct for the anemia. Simultaneously, markers of inflammation, such as a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 1.39 mg/dL and neutrophilia, may reflect physiological pregnancy changes or pathological tissue reaction. A "slight increase" in this case was initially indistinguishable from physiological changes in a woman of 21 weeks, before the malignancy risk was ruled out. The development of low-grade fever during the hospital stay required a differential diagnosis between infection or ischemic necrosis. The presence of fever in the context of a cystic lesion often suggests tissue necrosis, which can be separated from diagnostic infection (e.g., urinary tract infection). Differentiating these is vital, as the elevated CA-125 mentioned was only a slight increase (41.8 U/mL) compared to physiological pregnancy levels, suggesting it was a benign marker rather than a causative oncological signal in this specific instance. Understanding that CA-125 physiologically rises during the first trimester, peaks, and falls in the second, helps prevent unnecessary adverse labeling of routine pregnancy-associated values.
