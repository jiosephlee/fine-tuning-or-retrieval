Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## The CA-125 Paradox: Interpreting Ovarian Tumor Markers in Pregnancy

There is a level of urgency that often accompanies a flip of the white papers that reads "CA-125 elevated". In our trauma bay, an urgent abdomen, a cancer workup starts well below a threshold that you might see on an OB report. But consider this: a non-invasive approach that reads 41.8 U/mL in a 21-week pregnant woman reads the same as a gynecologic oncology file. The reality is that she didn't have ovarian cancer. She had a 18cm pedunculated uterine leiomyoma, twisted and necrotic. The high serum levels were a mix of pregnancy physiology, adenomyosis from the tumor itself, and the inflammatory cycle of the degenerating myoma.

Every clinician knows the normal tri-trimester curve. But looking at that curve, we can sometimes miss the true story of the clamp.

### The Physiology and the Limits of a Single Point

Physiologic elevation is the norm, but single readings are rarely enough. Peak levels in pregnancy occur in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL) again in the third trimester. The second trimester is usually the lowest point (median 14.0 U/mL). This patient presented with 31 weeks plus 6 days. The lab shows 41.8 U/mL. While the median for that time window is 14, the upper limits are often set around 35. If a single upload triggers a pan-retinal dilation and a biopsy, the patient often loses the third trimester.

We need to look beyond the lab to the trend. CA-125 is not just a marker; it's a reactivity. It measures peritoneal irritation as well as tumor burden. Pregnancy, adenomyosis, and even endogenous decidualization can mimic this rise. The goal is to avoid unnecessary surgery on cryostats or unnecessary anxiety. Wait for it. Look for it.

### Imaging: The Safety vs. Accuracy

In the case I shared, the upper limit was the difficult choice. If the MRI had revealed a metastatic pattern, the surgical management might have been more definitive. But the MRI without contrast showed thick walls with solid components suggestive of a myoma. MRI without contrast was chosen over the gadolinium.

This is crucial. Gadolinium is a beta-receptor blocker on the fetal side. You need the check in the head and the teammates. But you don't want the kidney acid burn. Standard of care limits contrast to avoid the risk of renal failure. In our case, the MRI showed unilocular cysts with fluid content inhomogeneity. That's the key. It felt like endometrioma, but the anatomy was wrong. The subclinical rise of the marker shifted from the uterus to the ovary. The mass was uterine. The marker was just the pain to the nerve.

### A Lesson in Surgical Thinking

This case shows us a surgeon's dilemma. You have a patient who is 37 weeks, 2 days with abdominal mass and dyspnoea. The risk of latency is high. The risk of rupture is high. The option is to operate.

Why did the multidisciplinary team choose surgery here? The risk of preterm delivery is increased if you keep a massive scrotal mass. Also, the mass was tied to a pedicle. Rupture, torsion, and infection risks outweigh the cost of surgery.

The decision to leave the caudal mass in situ was not a choice; it was a boundary. Challenging the wall. But to keep the closure, the surgery lasted 2 hours. The patient had mass transfusion, progesterone prophylaxis, and the rest is follow-up.

### What Does This Mean for You?

When you are managing a patient with CA-125 elevation in pregnancy, focus on the *reaction of the marker* rather than the *number* on the screen. The uterus must look at the wife's history and the imaging.

*   **Do not use a single CA-125 level to justify preterm delivery in pregnancy.**
*   **Prioritize non-contrast MRI to avoid fetal toxicity in contrast imaging.**
*   **Clinical symptoms (dyspnea, pain) often drive the decision to cut more than the lab work.**
*   **Understand that CA-125 can rise in adenomyosis and pregnancy, mimicking malignancy patterns.**

The patient had excellent obstetrics. The baby was intact. The mother delivered a baby at 37 weeks. That is the standard. But the mass was 18 cm. That mass was going to kill the oxygenation. It was an intervention based on physiology and the mass effect. It is a classic reminder of how OB medicine and GYN medicine intersect when they crash.



## Safe Imaging in the Obstetric Emergency: US, MRI and Gadolinium Controversies

A 21-week pregnant patient presents with massive abdominal pain and dyspnea. In an emergency setting, your first instinct is likely to secure the abdomen volume, identify malignancy, and radiographically define the pathology. But when you are dealing with a 40-year-old nulliparous woman with a BMI of 33, the stakes shift quickly. The abdominal exam reveals a "taut-elastic" mass occupying the subphrenic space, and dyspnea is severe. The initial workup assumed these were two enor-ous cystic ovarian masses. However, the decision path for confirmatory imaging in this case highlights a critical, often debated hierarchy in obstetric emergencies: where to stop and when to escalate, specifically regarding radiation and contrast agents.

The most conspicuous element of this case wasn't the surgery, but the imaging strategy chosen before the cut. The patient initially underwent ultrasound, which was correct for rapid, bedside assessment. US identified two large, unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularization. This finding was pivotal; peripheral vascularization on color Doppler is a key differentiator between benign fibroids and necrotic torsion or malignancy. It identified a "highly vascularised" upper mass derived from the uterus, rather than just a simple ovarian cyst. In the case series, preoperative US is valued in identifying symptomatic uterine leiomyomas located along the anterior wall, often pedunculated.

However, the case also demonstrates a necessity to avoid unnecessary gadolinium. At 21 weeks, the risk of the growing uterus compressing vessels (like the Iliac or Uterine) is real, but so is the risk of contrast nephrology or theoretical risks to the fetus. The retrospective discussion in the case notes explicitly states: "Since gadolinium-enhanced MRI should not be performed during pregnancy, unless strictly necessary, due to rheumatological, inflammatory or infiltrative skin conditions, risk of stillbirth or neonatal death" (Note: this is based on standard recommendations referenced in the report). The imaging team opted for a non-contrast MRI. This decision was grounded in the "radiologic safety" of the non-contrast scan combined with the high diagnostic yield for the pathology in question. They used the 1.5-T MRI, which avoids the need for contrast to visualize the fluid levels and solid components without the gadolinium curve. This avoids the need for CT scans entirely, which would deliver significant radiation to the uterus, fetal sac, and placenta.

The diagnostic dilemma was complicated because CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL during the second trimester. Usually, CA-125 refers to ovarian cancer risk, but during pregnancy, levels peak in the first and third trimesters (median 23 and 21 U/mL). The second trimester usually drops lower (median 14 U/mL). This elevation fueled suspicion of neoplasm. Even though CA-125 is not diagnostic (as it can reflect endometriosis, adenomyosis, and pregnancy physiology itself), its upwards trend (from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier) combined with the MRI findings of thick fluid walls (1.4 cm) pushed the team toward urgency. The MRI, specifically the T2 fat-sat weighted inhomogeneous fluid content, allowed them to see the mixed solid and cystic components without exposing the fetus to nephrotoxic or potential teratogenic agents.

Surgery came later, but the planning was solidified by these non-invasive modalities. The eventful twist was that the upper mass was actually a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma that had twisted and leaked fluid. This explains the fever and worsening anemia. The lower mass was adherent to the uterine fundus and represented a barrier to vaginal birth in the future. The imaging hierarchy allowed them to determine if the pelvic mass required debulking (upper) or if it could be left alone (lower), balancing the need to salvage the pregnancy against the need to prevent hemorrhage or uterine rupture.

It is vital to remember that the imaging choices in pregnancy are driven by: safety, diagnostic yield, and fetal rhemvironment. In a patient with acute abdominal pain and suspected torsion or malignancy, you need the safety profile of US + non-contrast MRI over CT + Gadolinium-enhanced MRI.

**Key Take-Home Messages**

*   **US First:** Always utilize color Doppler in the first trimester or on presentation to assess vascular flow; peripheral vascularity helps distinguish between benign and malignant tumors.
*   **No Gadolinium:** Standard OB recommendations suggest avoiding gadolinium unless strictly necessary due to potential connective tissue issues and risk of stillbirth/neonatal death. Non-contrast MRI is safe and sufficient for many structural assessments.
*   **Calcium-125 Context:** Do not rely on isolated CA-125 measurements in pregnancy; physiological levels vary by trimester and session trends (serial) hold more value.
*   **Imaging Correlation:** MRI without contrast identified the uterine origin of the masses better than US alone, guiding the surgical decision to excise one and leave another.

In the end, the outcome was excellent, with birth occurring via scheduled C-section at 37 weeks due to the posterior mass size. However, the "patient safety" during the imaging phase was paramount. By choosing US over CT and Non-contrast MRI over Gadolinium, the team prioritized fetal safety without sacrificing the diagnostic value needed for the procedure. Next time you see a "textbook ovarian cancer suspect" in pregnancy, ask if a Gadolinium-enhanced MRI is truly required or if a non-contrast protocol is sufficient.

**Bottom Line**

In pregnancy, imaging choices balance fetal safety with diagnostic certainty. Ultrasound with Doppler is your first line; non-contrast MRI is your middle line; CT and Gadolinium should be last resorts. Always consult the fetal safety profile before ordering the contrast.



# When to Operate on Pregnancy: Indications for Myomectomy and Laparotomy

## The Diagnostic Dilemma of the Second Trimester

There is nothing quite like a pregnant patient in the fourth decade of life with a belly that looks like it should contain a fetus, an elephant, and a massage shop at the same time. Meet our patient: a nulliparous woman in her 40s, BMI 33, presenting at 21 weeks gestation with acute abdominal pain, dyspnea, and a vague fever. On physical exam, her abdomen was palpable as if she were two trimesters plus a half pregnancy over. The bimanual exam revealed a fixed, hard pelvic mass, but the initial ultrasound story was a zoo: two large, unilocular cystic masses, mimicking decidualised endometriomas.

The kicker? The upper mass wasn't an endometrioma. It was a huge, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma, twisted, leaking, and necrotic. In the context of pregnancy, where a uterus is already hyper-insulated, these masses act like time bombs. Some clinicians might instinctively write down "IV hydration, bedrest, progesterone, and wait." In this specific case, that strategy would have failed. The mass could not tolerate further growth, it was intruding on vital structures (causing dyspnea), and necrosis had set in (fever, anemia).

## Why Imaging Matters (But Not as Much as Symptoms)

The CR interventionist does not want a point of view. Instead, we want a decision algorithm. In the setting of atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses during pregnancy, ultrasound is the gold standard but prone to misdiagnosis (as seen in this case). A US showing a large pedunculated mass extends the differential spectrum significantly.

However, advanced imaging is crucial for surgical planning. In this case, a non-contrast MRI without gadolinium confirmed the myomatous origin of the upper lesion and the continuity with the lower cystic mass. It helped determine steric bulk and distance from the uterine cavity. While MRIs without contrast are generally considered safe after the first trimester, gadolinium should be avoided.

Also do NOT rely on CA-125. A second-trimester CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL is low, as the marker typically peaks in the first trimester. By itself, it points toward benign pathology (endometriosis, myoma) rather than malignancy, especially given positive trends from normal limits.

The surgeon in this case made a call to operate at 21 weeks 6 days. Why? The key decision factors were: worsening clinical conditions, symptoms limiting physiological uterine enlargement, and specific risk of complications beyond rupture.

## Surgical Thresholds and Management Choices

When do you cut? The general rule for asymptomatic uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy is expectant management. However, think of the "red flags" that push you from onlooker to theatrics:

*   **Complications:** Acute abdominal pain and fever persisting despite analgesics/PCNK.
*   **Hemodynamics:** Iron deficiency anemia that worsens despite supplementation.
*   **Mass Characteristics:** Twisted or leaking masses (like the necrotic upper mass here).
*   **Anatomical Constraints:** Large masses that limit uterine growth, potentially causing dyspnea or obstructing labor (see lower mass in this case).

In the second trimester, the fetal quality is stable. If the mass is decompressing the diaphragm and nutritionally compromising the mother, the risk of waiting exceeds the risk of surgery. In this case, the surgeon opted for a longitudinal laparotomy. This was overkill for some, but justified by the massive size (18 cm) and intramural location that required precise access to avoid placental involvement.

**Important:** Serial TMZ is necessary during the procedure. The fetal heart rate must be monitored before, during, and after the surgery. In our case, the upper mass was excised lower than a 2-week period. The lower mass was left in situ to minimise obstetric risk.

## Delivery Mode: The Final Calculus

The repair was meticulously closed. Progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively. Antibiotics were guided by urine culture (Klebsiella). The definitive delivery method was the real variable here. The residual pelvic mass was left in situ. But the question becomes: vaginal birth or C-section?

Given the obstruction by the massive cystic/peduncular mass adherent to the posterior wall, the decision was to elect for a caesarean delivery at 37 weeks. Minimising the risk of uterine scar rupture is paramount. Furthermore, the mother underwent excellent follow-up, breastfeeding well, and the residual mass regressed slightly postpartum.

## Key Take-aways

*   **Waitlist vs. Operative:** Asymptomatic myomas in pregnancy can be observed; however, symptoms (pain, anemia, dyspnea), steric bulk, or signs of necrosis/torsion mandate timely surgical intervention.
*   **Imaging:** Non-contrast MRI is preferred for large masses over CT for radiation avoidance; avoid gadolinium unless strictly necessary.
*   **Tumor Markers:** CA-125 is benign in pregnancy; a value of 41.8 U/mL does not confirm malignancy but reflects physiological changes and myoma presence.
*   **Delivery Planning:** If a large myoma is left in situ, assess for intramural obstruction. An obstructed fetus requires a planned C-section to avoid uterine rupture.
*   **Risk vs. Reward:** Surgery in the first trimester carries viability risks. Second trimester surgery balances myoma growth against maternal morbidity.

In the end, the case teaches us that "wait and see" is only for "safe" masses. When the belly hurts, and the mass is compromising the anatomy, the mother comes first. But whenever you are cutting, remember the fetal heart rate is the other variable we are chasing.



## Obstetric Logistics: Managing Uterine Pelvic Masses and Labor Distortion

In the fast-paced world of emergency medicine and obstetrics, having two massive abdominal lumps in a pregnant woman can quickly become a diagnostic spin-off. Imagine a 21-week singleton gestation where the uterus isn't just enlarged, but accompanied by two new, space-occupying lesions. This woman, a nulliparous woman in her 40s with a BMI of 33, arrived with severe abdominal pain in both the left upper quadrant and the pelvis. The clinical picture was deceptive. Initial ultrasound suggested decidualized endometriomas, and the slight elevation in CA-125 led us to wonder about malignancy. However, the case pivots on how you balance the risk of surgical intervention against the mechanical constraints of labor, ultimately deciding that the posterior pelvic mass acts as a physical antagonist to the expulsive phase of birth.

The diagnostic journey here is a prime lesson in avoiding the trap of physiological mimicry. During pregnancy, serum CA-125 can rise physiologically in the first and third trimesters. In our patient, the value was 41.8 U/mL, interpreted as a need for investigation due to the trend, yet it remains non-specific given the high false-positive rate for benign conditions like endometriosis and leiomyomas. When ultrasound showed a hard, irregular mass adherent to the posterior wall, we opted for an MRI without contrast. This was crucial because gadolinium is generally avoided in pregnancy, and high-field (<1.5-T) MRI without contrast reliably mapped the fluid content and solid components. Neoplasia was unlikely given the normal tumor panel and exclusion of lymphadenopathy, cementing the diagnosis: left-overcustomer-like leiomyomas with cystic degeneration.

### The Surgical Dilemma During Gestation

The core decision point occurred during the laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days. We faced a choice: excise everything or leave it. The superior, pedunculated mass posed a threat of acute complications such as torsion and ischemia, prompting its excision. In contrast, the inferior pelvic mass was left in situ to minimize further obstetric trauma during recovery. This decision wasn't arbitrary. Retaining the posterior mass was a deliberate strategy to ensure that if the patient went for a vaginal delivery, the uterus itself wasn't compromised, but it meant accepting a specific logistics risk: booking labor management differently. By 37 weeks, the residual mass (which shrank slightly postpartum to 7.5 cm) would rigidly block the fetal head from engaging or crowding in the lower segment, necessitating a planned cesarean section. If we had waited until rupture, the trauma could be fetal or maternal. This case underscores that in pregnancy, the mode of delivery is often dictated by the obstetrical landmarks created by pathology, not just fetal station.

The medical team also managed the systemic complications vigorously. The patient developed frank anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L) pre-discharge, necessitating IV iron and transfusion to avoid maternal morbidity. Progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraperitoneally to suppress acne and potentially modulate uterine contractility, though compliance with the specific regimen varies. The patient tolerated the procedure well, recovering to discharge in 10 days with normal fetal growth (86th percentile). The key lesson here is that aggressive, conservative management is often not enough when the mass grows by approximately 7% per week during early pregnancy, requiring timely surgical reduction if symptoms persist.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Don't panic at CA-125 during pregnancy:** Physiological elevations occur in early and late pregnancy; rely on structural features (mass morphology, CA-125 trend, and palpation) rather than a single snapshot value.
*   **Contrast-free MRI is your friend in pregnancy:** Avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary; non-contrast MRI provides sufficient detail for grading mass adherence and viability to guide surgical safety.
*   **Labor Distorted by Masses:** Posterior uterine masses may prevent vaginal delivery due to topographical obstruction; plan C-section if the mass size and location preclude fetal engagement.
*   **Timing is critical for Myomectomy:** Document fetal HR before, during, and after surgery; surgical intervention is justified by growth rate (6.90%/week) and mechanical threat rather than cancer risk alone.
*   **Residual Mass Planning:** If you leave a large pelvic myoma in situ, expect a mode of delivery determined by the mass as a barrier, not soft tissue standing up against the fetal head.



# Fevers and Fluids: Distinguishing Mass Ischemia from Urinary Tract Infection

## The Fever Doesn't Always Match the Source

Abdominal pain and fever in pregnancy often trigger an immediate run toward labor support or broad-spectrum antibiotics. It is the "textbook" presentation for chorioamnionitis, placental abruption, or urinary tract infection. However, when you introduce a complex pelvic mass into the picture—especially one that mimics malignancy on imaging—the differential expansion becomes exponentially more dangerous. If you are managing a hypertensive patient or one with complicated pregnancy pathology, the first rule of thumb is that the patient's fever isn't just one thing. In a recent case study involving a nulliparous woman in her 40s presenting at 21 weeks gestation, the fever didn't come from the fluid in the urine, and it didn't come from the placenta. It came from something much deeper. This case revisits the diagnostic trap of distinguishing between infectious sepsis and organic mass pathology when the clinical picture is ambiguous.

## A Looming Threat in the Uterus

The presentation was classic for the pregnant abdomen: severe pain and dyspnea, though the cause wasn't the fetus. A 21-week and 6-day gestational age patient presented with 18 cm and 9 cm abdominal masses, respectively. This combination is alarming. Ultrasound initially suggested decidualized endometriomas or hilar cysts. CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL. However, while CA-125 is indeed a marker that physiologically rises and peaks in pregnancy, its elevation in the presence of large, solid-appearing masses requires vigilance for malignancy. The patient developed a low-grade fever during hospitalization, *Klebsiella pneumoniae* was found in the urine, and the cache remained positive for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 was negative. The combination of fever, pain, and a *Klebsiella* infection infection usually pushes a provider toward systemic antibiotics and IV fluids. But if the fever is secondary to a twisting, leaking myoma, the treatment changes entirely. The distinction lies in the surgery. The "upper" mass was a pedunculated, atypical cystic and taut-elastic leiomyoma that was initially "twisted and leaking."

## Peeling Back the Layers

Why was this so confusing? The fever was multifactorial. The surgical findings revealed the upper mass had undergone ischemia ("twisted and leaking"), which provided the biological stimulus for the fever. However, the urine culture also confirmed a *Klebsiella* infection. The peritoneal fluid was negative for bacterial culture, suggesting the febrile response was primarily driven by the necrotic mass rather than frank peritonitis from a septic urate leak. In pregnancy, when surgical findings contradict the initial imaging, that is the story we must not ignore.

The management of the patient had to weigh the immediate risk of the twisting mass against the prenatal risks of surgery. The decision to proceed with a laparotomy was justified not by the mere presence of fever or UTI, but by the risk of the lesion expanding, rupturing, or compromising fetal oxygenation (*O2 tension*). In contrast, the lower pelvic mass—though large—was left in situ. This was a critical calculation. Removing this mass carrying the pregnancy risk of fetal size and position limitations. If that mass were left untreated, it would shape the delivery (likely forcing a C-section).

## Management Pearl ## When to Cut and When to Wait

The core of this case is the diagnosis of how to distinguish mass ischemia from infection. The key takeaway is this: In a pregnant patient with fever and abdominal mass, if the imaging suggests a contained lesion with "leakage" signs (like thick walls or solid components), treat the burns, not just the fever.

- **Distinguish Ischemia from Infection:** When fever is the primary complaint in the context of a uterine mass, consider the possibility of evolving necrosis or torsion before assuming sepsis. Confirm peritoneal fluid is sterile if surgery is not immediately done to avoid confounding systemic cultures with localized inflammation.
- **Use CA-125 with Caution:** In the second trimester, CA-125 trends are physiologic. A value of 41.8 U/mL is significantly higher than the second-trimester median (14 U/mL), but serial trends are more helpful than a single spike to rule out malignancy.
- **Surgical Threshold:** Surgery during pregnancy is high risk. However, for "sterile" but complicated masses (twisting, leaking, vascular compromise), conservative waiting becomes risky.
- **Follow-up:** If surgery is performed, maintain a strict obstetric surveillance protocol, specifically monitoring fetal cardiac activity.

## The Bottom Line

This case underscores the importance of multidisciplinary decision-making in the care of pregnant women with unrecognized uterine pathology. The differential diagnosis of "mass fever" must include necrosis and ischemia as valid options, not just infection. By prioritizing the tissue innervation and necrotic signs (twisted, leaking) over the initial fever source, the team avoided unnecessary invasive measures on the lower mass while preserving the pregnancy until the final route (C-section) was established. The lesson for EM and OB/GYN alike: Check the peritoneal workup, freeze the diagnosis, and evaluate the mechanical forces.



# The Iron-Deficiency Anemia Loop and Perioperative Planning

There is often a specific sense of unease in the operating room when a pregnant patient with a massive pelvic mass presents with anemia that worsens *after* the diagnosis of pregnancy but potentially *during* the initial stabilization phase. Take this nulliparous woman in her 40s, presenting at 21 weeks gestation with two atypical abdominal masses and dyspnea. The clinical picture at first glance was mechanical compression, but the hematologic nuance revealed a more precarious foundation. She entered the hospital with an Hb of 9.9 g/L—a pre-existing iron deficiency flagged on admission. What makes this case particularly instructive is not just the mass themselves, but the surgical trajectory of the anemia. The Hb dropped precipitously to 7.5 g/L intra- and immediately post-operatively, necessitating aggressive salvage therapy to reach a stable 10.0 g/L.

## The Vascular Cycle of Pregnancy and Myomas

The synergy between vascular pelvic masses and anemia is a classic but often under-recognized component of high-risk obstetrics and gynecologic surgery. Uterine leiomyomas are benign yet distinctively vascular smooth muscle tumors. While pregnancy often drives myomas to grow—size increases by roughly 6.90% every week in early gestation due to hormonal surges—the introduction of surgical manipulation can trigger distinct hemodynamic events. In this patient, the "sprouting" of the masses obscured by the gravid uterus created a mechanical burden. However, the leverage occurred when the surgery revealed a pedunculated mass that was "twisted and leaking."

This specific detachment allowed the underlying, high-flow vascular architecture of the myoma to contribute fresh blood loss on top of a pre-existing depleted supply. The fever that accompanied the admission was likely multifactorial, attributed to both low-grade *Klebsiella* infection and this acute inflammatory response to the hemorrhagic necrosis within the tumor. Here is where the algorithm for planning shifts from routine obstetric care to surgical risk management. You aren't just dealing with a mass; you are dealing with a blood bank deficit in soft tissue.

For the pregnant patient with fibroids, especially those that are intramural or pedunculated, the physical disruption raises the risk of hypoperfusion and coagulopathy if her baseline iron stores are marginal, as indicated by a ferritin level of 25 mcg/L in this read. The decline from 9.9 g/L to 7.5 g/L in the perfusion state suggests that simply covering the pelvic floor with a myomectomy isn't sufficient if the blood volume is already strained by the hemodynamic demands of the amniotic fluid and the fetus. The "synergy" is that the mass, exacerbated by the pregnancy, creates a bleed-prone surface, while the pregnancy physiology maintains a hypercoagulable and fluid-balanced state that makes managing that bleed window of surgical stability complex.

## Managing the Anemia Loop: Transfusion vs. Oral Iron

The decision in the case highlights why "supportive care" doesn't always mean "just take some aspirin or oral iron." In patients with Hb < 7-8 g/L and symptoms like weakness or tachycardia—the patient had worsening anaemia requiring intervention—transfusion is the immediate standard to restore acute perfusion. Intravenous iron like ferric carboxymaltose 1 g was started while passive resuscitation via blood transfusion (two units) was utilized. You can calculate the efficacy from her post-hemoglobin: 10.0 g/L and HCT 30.4%. This combination targets an immediate hemodynamic correction and long-term repletion.

We should look at the algorithm for the patients with known anemia prior to this scenario.
*   **Pre-op Baseline:** Check Hb and Ferritin. If Ferritin is <30 (she had 25), it indicates iron stores are depleted.
*   **Intra-op/Hystero:** If Hb drops <7.5 g/L alone to <7.0 g/L, transfuse. 10 mL/kg blood volume transfusion is often the minimum target for massive arterial lesions.
*   **Repletion:** 341 mg hydroxyprogesterone or similar parenteral iron supplementation should follow once stable. The case notes progesterone prophylaxis was given for the fetus, which is standard when fibroids are removed, but the iron regimen was crucial for the maternal hemoglobin.

The key lesson remains: advanced anemia (Hb < 9 g/L in pregnancy) isn't just physiology—it's a surgical constraint. Massive lesions (18 cm) and iron deficiency combine to limit surgical time and stability. The team opted for a laparotomy rather than laparoscopy which is safer for large masses, and avoided transfusion until the patient was stabilized to avoid preterm labor. This balance of stability and fetal safety is critical.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Pre-operative Assessment:** In patients with vascular pelvic masses and gestational age >10 weeks, check Hb and Ferritin on admission; ferritin <30 mcg/L suggests iron stores are depleted beyond simple "pregnancy anemia."
*   **Perioperative Thresholds:** Hb < 7.5 g/L during or after abdominal surgery requiring stabilization is an indication for blood transfusion to maintain hemodynamic stability, especially in the presence of active bleeding.
*   **Blood Cross-Management:** Administer IV iron (e.g., Ferric Carboxymaltose) immediately post-stabilization to support future physiological demands and cellular regeneration for recovery.
*   **Surgical Strategy:** Plan for laparotomy in large masses (>10-12cm) with prolapse, to maintain stable hemodynamics and safe anesthesia.
*   **Fetal Safety:** Coordinate with obstetrics to avoid preterm labor risk during the procedure, and consider elective C-section if the residual mass poses a barrier to fetal head progression.
