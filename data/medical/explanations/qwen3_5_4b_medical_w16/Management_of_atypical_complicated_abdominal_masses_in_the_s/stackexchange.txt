Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In this patient with an elevated CA-125, how should a pathologically borderline value of 41.8 U/mL during the second trimester be interpreted regarding the likelihood of ovarian malignancy versus benign physiological changes?

In evaluating a patient with an elevated CA-125 during pregnancy, relying on this laboratory value alone to assess the risk of ovarian malignancy is clinically unwise and often misleading. As this case confirms, CA-125 levels fluctuate significantly during gestation, making isolation or static interpretation of a specific numerical value unreliable.

**Clinical Reasoning and Pathophysiology:**
Physiologically, serum CA-125 levels exhibit a unique pattern during pregnancy. Normal values peak in the first trimester (median approx. 23 U/mL) and third trimester (median approx. 21 U/mL), with a relative dip during the second trimester (median approx. 14 U/mL). While the second gestational phase is theoretically the lowest point, levels can still rise significantly due to inflammatory changes, specific pathologies, or paracentesis (though this patient did not have ascites). Furthermore, benign conditions common in both pregnancy and reproductive age—specifically uterine leiomyomas, endometriosis, and pelvic inflammatory disease—frequently elevate CA-125. In the present case, the patient’s level was 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds her 21-week median reference, yet the final pathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with degeneration.

**Diagnosis and Management Principle:**
The presence of large abdominal masses in the second trimester requires a multidisciplinary approach balancing oncological suspicion with obstetric safety. In this scenario, the "slight positivity" of CA-125 (41.8 U/mL) was used in conjunction with clinical findings (pain, tachycardia, anemia) and imaging characteristics (cystic changes, adherence to uterine tissue) to guide decision-making. However, the pathology proved that tumor markers are not diagnostic of the malignancy; in fact, CA-125 can be elevated in benign conditions with a cutoff of less than 19 U/mL.

Therefore, when encountering a borderline CA-125 in pregnancy, especially with atypical imaging features suggestive of degenerative changes rather than solid enhancement, treat it as non-specific. Serial evaluations can provide useful trends for follow-up, but diagnosis must rely on tissue histology when feasible, or clinical judgment based on symptoms (e.g., torsion, necrosis risk) rather than marker concentration. The decision to intervene surgically in this case was driven by the risk of rupture, the*size* of masses, and clinical complications (pain, dyspnea), not solely the CA-125.

### [Management] Outlining the general principles of management, what specific clinical features (e.g., nature of the mass size, symptoms) justify active surgical intervention during the second trimester rather than an expectant approach?

**General Principles for Surgical Management of Symptomatic Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy**

In the management of atypical complicated abdominal masses during pregnancy, the default strategy is expectant management. However, this should be abandoned only when maternal physiological compromise or serious obstetric risk cannot be deferred. Based on this case report, the specific clinical features warranting active surgical intervention include intractable pain, severe symptomatic mass effect, and the imminent threat of mechanical complications.

**Justification for Active Intervention**
In this case, the multidisciplinary team decided on laparotomy at 21 weeks rather than a conservative approach due to three converging risks:
1.  **Symptomatic Compromise:** The patient presented with severe acute abdominal pain (Visual Analogue Scale 5) associated with pulmonary compromise (dyspnea). While analgesics provided slight relief, the marginal benefit did not negate the mechanical threat of massive lesions occupying 18% of the subphrenic space and filling the pouch of Douglas.
2.  **Risk of Acute Obstetric Gynaecology Complications:** The upper mass was identified as a pedunculated leiomyoma with torsion and leakage risks. The Abstract explicitly states surgical management should be considered to avoid complications of rupture, torsion, and infection.
3.  **Limitation of Biological Envelope:** The "steric bulk" of the uterus combined with the massive cystic structures created a restrictive environment for fetal growth and physiological skeletal preparation. Prognostic data from the study notes that unicorns size grows non-linearly during this period, and large masses could outgrow current physiological space limits.

**Clinical Course Rationale**
While the caudal mass was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk regarding scar integrity, the cranial mass was excised via myomectomy because its vascular nature and pedunculated origin precluded safe expectant monitoring. Decision-making emphasized avoiding overtreatment while respecting the "steric bulk" to prevent future hypoxia or necrosis.

**Management Strategy**
When intervention is required in the second trimester:
*   **Timing:** Perform surgery as early as feasible (2nd trimester) to balance fetal safety against maternal risk.
*   **Delivery:** Plan mode of delivery based on anatomical obstruction (C-section may be necessary if residual masses block vaginal delivery), as seen here with the elective caesarean at 37 weeks.
*   **Approach:** Use laparotomy for large, intramural, or complicated masses, provided the risk of uterine rupture is managed (e.g., hemo-prophy, progesterone prophylaxis).
*   **Safety:** Maintain fetal cardiac activity monitoring and avoid non-enhanced MRI unless safety is not established for contrast agents.

**Conclusion**
Surgery is justified when symptoms resolve aside from anxiety or when masses cause symptoms (pain, dyspnea) or threaten maternal/neonatal outcome (torsion/rupture), ensuring that pregnancy preservation does not override maternal safety.

### [Pathophysiology] Considering the hormonal environment of pregnancy, what are the documented weekly rates of growth for uterine leiomyomas between the trimesters, and how might this knowledge guide the timing of surgical planning to affect maternal morbidity?

Based on the data presented in the discussion of this case report, uterine leiomyomas follow a non-linear growth trajectory during pregnancy and puerperium driven by the hormonal environment. Specifically, leiomyomas increase in size by 6.90% annually, with a breakdown showing an average increase of 6.12% between the first and second trimesters (p<0.001) and a slower rate of 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Inversely, after the delivery, the size of myomas significantly decreased, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% by 6 weeks postpartum, typically during the lactation period.

This distinction in growth velocity and regression is critical for surgical planning. For uncomplicated masses, surgical management is usually delayed until after birth to avoid maternal morbidity; a referenced study cited within the report noted that while myomectomy did not significantly increase preoperative or transfusion rates compared to C-section alone, it significantly increased hospitalization duration and surgery duration (p=0.001). However, as illustrated in the current presentation, timing must yield to acute risk when "steric bulk" and systemic symptoms (e.g., dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compression, pain, or signs of necrosis/torsion) exist. The patient in this case underwent resection at 21 weeks because the massive mass threatened hypoxia and organ compromise, and the epitemal mass was restricted from the uterine cavity to minimize future obstetric risks. 

In summary, while the growth rate of 3.10% per week in the second and third trimesters allows for a safer window for elective intervention or observation than the rapid 6.12% phase in early pregnancy, the risk of complications seen in this second-trimester presentation dictates that urgent surgery should not be postponed despite the slowing growth rate. The primary manageplan is to utilize elective third-trimester myomectomy and subsequent caesarean section if the condition permits, whereas acute, symtempotic, or necrotic masses often require immediate laparotomic resection followed by a C-section to balance maternal morbidity and fetal safety.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which specific ultrasonographic or MRI characteristics were pivotal in distinguishing the two masses in this case, particularly the upper pedunculated lesion, from a differential diagnosis of large decidualized endometriomas?

In this case, the differentiation between a pedunculated uterus leiomyoma and decidualized endometriomas was challenging given that both modalities initially mimicked cystic adnexal lesions. While color Doppler ultrasound identified peripheral vascularization, it was insufficient for definitive distinction.

The pivotal imaging characteristics distinguishing the massive upper lesion from an endometrioma came from the **non-contrast MRI**. Specifically, the presence of **focal hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid components** within the otherwise cystic mass was the key discriminator.

Clinically, this finding suggested **cystic degeneration within a myomatous matrix** (leiomyoma) rather than a purely cystic pathologic structure like an endometrioma. Additionally, the **thick walls (1.4 cm)** identified on MRI supported the diagnosis of a symptomatic myoma, whereas endometriomas typically present with thin, hemosiderin-laden walls. The **richtung of the caudal mass adhering to an intrinsic intramural-subserosal myoma** further corroborated a myomatous origin via a urogenital connection despite the initial “steric bulk” obscuring the stalk on imaging.

Ultimately, while the upper mass appeared to lack a visible connective stalk on preoperative imaging (initially favoring an ovarian origin), the combination of **solid components on MRI, high vascularity, and proximity to uterine fundal tissue** facilitated the surgical identification of a large pedunculated uterine leiomyoma. This highlights that in the second trimester, structural tissue characteristics on MRI are crucial to prevent underestimation of the uterine origin of adnexal masses mimicking decidualized endometriosis.

### [Management] Given the persistence of symptoms and the cytological findings in the peritoneal fluid, under what specific circumstances would surgical removal of a post-pregnancy fibroid be prioritized in this same patient over delayed observation?

In the management of peripartum uterine leiomyomas, the general principle—as illustrated in the case by De Nola et al.—favors conservative, expectant management during pregnancy and the immediate postpartum period to allow for expected mass regression and ensure fetal safety. However, surgical removal of a post-pregnancy fibroid should be prioritized over delayed observation under the following specific circumstances, as defined by the decision-making criteria in the case discussion:

**1. Persistent Symptoms without Conservative Benefit**
The case report explicitly states that myomectomy should be considered in cases of "persistent symptoms without benefits from conservative treatment." While this patient's residual pelvic myoma was left in situ and she preferred unscheduled surgery due to "absence of symptoms," any recurrence or persistence of the specific symptoms that necessitated the intra-operative laparotomy during pregnancy (e.g., abdominal pain, anemia, or respiratory compromise) would trigger the priority of surgical intervention. In this context, surgery is prioritized to prevent complications such as hemorrhage or infection that might recur if the mass is left undisturbed.

**2. Oncological Suspicion**
Surgical management should, per the authors, be considered only in the presence of "oncological suspicion." While this patient's peritoneal fluid was "negative for both cultural and cytological tests," validating the decision to observe, the case report emphasizes that "slight positivity of oncological markers (CA-125...)" and diagnostic workup (including cytology and MRI features) are essential to rule out malignancy. Therefore, if the peritoneal fluid or serum markers (e.g., CA-125 trends) indicated malignancy despite the benign intra-pregnancy diagnosis, surgical removal over delay would be prioritized to establish a cure rather than manage a potentially progressive mass.

**3. Anatomical Obstruction to Future Obstetric Function**
The residual myoma in this case was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk, but the text notes it would have "represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour." In the postpartum period, if the mass fails to regress (the text notes size decreases by ~44% postpartum) and remains an anatomical impediment to vaginal delivery or future fertility, removal is prioritized to facilitate vaginal birth options and reduce the risk of laceration during future labor.

**Conclusion**
Therefore, despite the general risk of surgical intervention during pregnancy, post-pregnancy removal is prioritized when the **mass remains symptomatic**, **fails to demonstrate tumor regression typical of the postpartum period**, or **continues to present oncological suspicion**. In the absence of these factors—such as observed regression or symptom resolution—delayed observation remains the preferred strategy.

### [Management] Why was an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks chosen as the mode of delivery rather than attempting a vaginal delivery once the uterus was delivered from the first stage of labor?

An elective caesarean section was chosen over attempted vaginal delivery primarily because the residual caudal uterine leiomyoma posed a mechanical obstruction incompatible with safe vaginal birth. Although the patient underwent second-trimester surgery (laparotomy) to remove the superior mass, a significant caudal mass remained adherent to the posterior uterine wall and persisted through the third trimester. As documented in the case report, "The caudal myoma would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour." Attempting vaginal delivery presented an unacceptably high risk of cesarean birth failure or maternal trauma due to the size and location of this obstetric obstacle. Additionally, proceeding to a planned caesarean at 37 weeks with the existing scar minimized the risk of potential uterine scar rupture compared to the stress of spontaneous labor, aligning with the principle that "The mode of delivery should depend on the size, number and anatomical location of uterine masses."

### [Pathophysiology] Why was a non-contrast MRI preferred over a gadolinium-enhanced study for this patient during the second trimester, considering the safety profile of contrast agents regarding fetal development?

In the management of pregnant patients with complex pelvic or abdominal pathology, the primary objective is diagnostic clarity without compromising fetal safety. As illustrated in this case, the decision to utilize non-contrast MRI over a gadolinium-enhanced study during the second trimester is driven by established safety guidelines regarding contrast agents in pregnancy.

The case report explicitly states that "up-to-date recommendations" advise that "gadolinium-enhanced MRI should not be performed during pregnancy, unless strictly necessary, due to rheumatological, inflammatory or infiltrative skin conditions, risk of stillbirth or neonatal death." While gadolinium crosses the placenta and its association with fetal harm remains a concern, the multidisciplinary team prioritized the non-enhanced modality because non-contrast MRI is reasonably safe after the first trimester and avoids direct exposure to contrast agents. In this specific clinical scenario, the diagnostic yield of MRI—distinguishing between cystic endometriomas, solid leiomyomas with degeneration, and adnexal masses—remains the standard of care when ultrasound is inconclusive.

Therefore, when advanced imaging is required for atypical masses in pregnancy, non-contrast MRI is the preferred standard. It provides the necessary soft-tissue characterization to guide surgical intervention while preserving fetal tissue integrity, reserving contrast administration exclusively for scenarios where non-contrast imaging fails to resolve the diagnosis or where maternal/fetal stability is immediately threatened by diagnostic uncertainty.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why did the surgical team opt to excise the upper mass while leaving the lower pelvic mass in situ, and what key anatomical risks influenced this decision to avoid no untoward cost.

**Clinical Teaching Point: Risk-Stratified Management of Uterine Leiomyomas in Pregnancy**

In this case, the surgical team opted to excise the upper inferiorly pedunculated mass while leaving the lower pelvic mass in situ to balance acute maternal complications against long-term obstetric safety. The rationale is rooted in the differential anatomical risks and the potential for perinatal outcomes:

1.  **Upper Mass Excision (Acute Maternal Risk):** The upper lesion was identified preoperatively as a pedunculated leiomyoma that had become twisted and was "leaking," leading to ischemia, necrosis, fever, and severe pain. Because it possessed a distinct stalk ("cleavage... was not clear" only for the lower mass), it could be gently excised from its uterine implant without extensive disruption of the lower uterine segment. Leaving the necrotic, torsed mass posed an immediate threat of rupture, further infection, or sepsis. Removal provided a definitive, low-morbidity resolution to this acute threat.
2.  **Lower Mass Retention (Obstetric Safety):** The lower mass was described as "fixed" and "adherent to a posterior... myoma of the uterus." The text explicitly states that if removed, this mass would have represented a "huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour." Furthermore, the caudal mass was "difficult to separate" post-surgical exploration. Retaining it in situ avoided the high risk of uterine injury, hemorrhage, or preterm labor associated with dissection in a gravid uterus, particularly given that the decision to proceed to a caesarean delivery later unavoidable minimized the need for vaginal delivery attempts anyway.

This decision underscores a fundamental principle in managing complicated uterine masses during pregnancy: surgical intervention for benign leiomyomas should be limited to cases where the mass poses an immediate threat to maternal viability (e.g., torsion, rupture, severe symptoms) or where conservative management is contraindicated. In this instance, the pelvis was left to minimize surgery duration and maternal morbidity while the mast’s safety was compromised by its potential to obstruct labor, necessitating a 37-week electively scheduled delivery.
