## Clinical Presentation and Pathophysiological Context of Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

### Physiological uterine growth and leiomyoma behavior during pregnancy
Understanding the presentation of abdominal masses in pregnancy requires first establishing the normal physiological trajectory of the gravid uterus. During the second trimester, as seen in this patient, the uterus undergoes profound hypertrophy and hyperplasia of uterine smooth muscle, driven by increased estrogen and progesterone levels. This growth accommodates fetal development and is normally benign. However, uterine smooth muscle is also the substrate for uterine leiomyomas, benign tumors that share follicular characteristics with the uterine wall. Physiologically, leiomyomas are often hormonally responsive; their dual blood vessels and vascular capacity lead to expansion during the first and early second trimesters due to increased angiogenesis, followed by a smaller increase in the late second and third trimesters. In cases where masses are large or atypical, this physiological growth can be confounded by pathological changes. As observed in the presented case, large leiomyomas may undergo degeneration, torsion, or cystic transformation of their pedicles, which disrupts normal vascular supply. While the uterus expands to fit the fetus, the presence of solid, vascular, and often tender masses like those described (the 18 cm superior lesion) alters the abdominal contour significantly. A key pathophysiological principle to recognize during this timeframe is the non-linear growth pattern; while the myoma size increases gradually, sympathetic vasoconstriction or hypoxia can accelerate growth and induce necrosis, presenting as an "acute" condition in a patient with subacute background pathology.

### Presentation of complications: abdominal pain and mechanical dyspnea
Symptomatic masses during pregnancy are frequently misdiagnosed as normal parturient discomforts until the impact becomes severe enough to compromise maternal physiology. In this case report, the patient presented with severe abdominal pain and dyspnoea at 21 weeks gestation, indicative of "steric bulk" complications. Abdominal pain in pregnancy with a palpable mass often signals mechanical stress or ischemia rather than simple pelvic pressure. The upper left quadrant pain was likely due to the tension on the peritoneum as the superior mass (leinomata) stretched the uterine wall and compressed adjacent structures. Mechanical dyspnea, while less common as a primary symptom of pregnancy, is a critical red flag when abdominal masses are present. The uterus (and its associated masses) supports fetal growth but also acts as a compressive burden on the diaphragm; typically, the spine depresses to accommodate the mass. However, when masses extend into the subphrenic space, as seen with the upper 18 cm lesion extending from the left subphrenic region, they impede diaphragmatic excursion. This reduces lung capacity and creates a sensation of dyspnoea disproportionate to the fetal weight alone. Identifying this lack of ventricular clearance or cardiac dysfunction helps differentiate simple uterine enlargement from the restrictive lung disease associated with large, fixed, or pedunculated uterine tumors.

### Clinical examination findings: abdominal distension and bimanual assessment
Physical examination remains a cornerstone of diagnostic reasoning, though it is challenging in obese patients or those with significant underlying pathology. In this patient, the abdomen seemed more enlarged than expected for her gestational age, with a tender swelling and taut-elastic mass occupying the upper left quadrant. This disproportionate distension is a clinical cue to look for pathology beyond the fetus. The bimanual vaginal examination provided critical tactile feedback, revealing a distinct mass in the lesser pelvis that was fixed, hard, rounded, and partially bulging at the posterior fornix. Distinguishing an anterior wall ovarian mass from an intramural or adnexal mass attached to the uterus requires recognizing the "fixed" nature of the bulk compared to the mobility of normal adnexa. In this specific instance, the retroperitoneal and anterior pelvic masses created a false impression of a purely adnexal origin, as the superior mass mimicked decidualized endometrioma features. Recognizing the fixed nature of the lower lesion (9 cm fixed pelvic mass) helps the clinician suspect a larger myomatous origin that may be adherent to the uterine torus or posterior wall, rather than separate benign ovarian cysts. It is essential to note that assessment must be cautious regarding uterine fundal height, which may be obscured by the masses, necessitating careful measurement to prevent misdiagnosis of preterm labor or twins.

### Initial laboratory abnormalities: anemia, infection markers, and differential significance of fever
Laboratory values serve to quantify physiological stress and potential inflammation, though standard cut-offs must be interpreted carefully in pregnancy. Iron deficiency anemia is common in the third trimester due to physiological hemodilution, yet a dropped hemoglobin of 9.9 g/L or 7.5 g/L after surgery suggests significant blood loss or ongoing consumption from necrotic tissue. The case of the presented patient showed elevated ferritin and ferric carboxymaltose levels, requiring transfusion to correct for the anemia. Simultaneously, markers of inflammation, such as a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 1.39 mg/dL and neutrophilia, may reflect physiological pregnancy changes or pathological tissue reaction. A "slight increase" in this case was initially indistinguishable from physiological changes in a woman of 21 weeks, before the malignancy risk was ruled out. The development of low-grade fever during the hospital stay required a differential diagnosis between infection or ischemic necrosis. The presence of fever in the context of a cystic lesion often suggests tissue necrosis, which can be separated from diagnostic infection (e.g., urinary tract infection). Differentiating these is vital, as the elevated CA-125 mentioned was only a slight increase (41.8 U/mL) compared to physiological pregnancy levels, suggesting it was a benign marker rather than a causative oncological signal in this specific instance. Understanding that CA-125 physiologically rises during the first trimester, peaks, and falls in the second, helps prevent unnecessary adverse labeling of routine pregnancy-associated values.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Imaging Interpretation, Markers, and Differential Diagnosis

In the management of atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses during pregnancy, diagnostic accuracy is critical without compromising fetal safety or maternal health. The case presented illustrates how imaging characteristics and laboratory trends guide clinicians in distinguishing between benign structural anomalies like uterine leiomyomas (fibroid) and ovarian cysts, particularly the decidualized endometriomas often mistaken for ovarian pathology. In this patient, the presence of two distinct masses—a giant upper lesion and a pelvic cyst—created a complex diagnostic puzzle at 21 weeks of gestation. Advanced imaging serves as the cornerstone of the workup, as physical examination alone cannot define the anatomical origin of massive lesions distending the abdomen. The primary teaching takeaway is that ultrasound serves as the first-line modality for initial characterization due to its safety profile, while MRI provides superior definitive diagnostic accuracy when ultrasound findings remain equivocal.

Ultrasound imaging in this patient revealed two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. The upper mass, measuring 18×12×7 cm, extended from the subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass (9.5×8×8 cm) appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. In the context of the differential diagnosis, the presence of peripheral vascularization often raises suspicion for active tumor differentiation, such as a vascular leiomyoma, although endometriomas typically display internal vascular flow. The ultrasound evaluation mimicked decidualised endometriomas initially due to the cystic nature and irregular borders of the upper lesion, yet the solid components required further scrutiny. The key differential challenge presented by this imaging modality is distinguishing between a cystic degenerating leiomyoma—which maintains the architecture of uterine tissue—and an adnexal mass with cystic changes. In this specific instance, the lack of a defined connective stalk for the upper mass on ultrasound made it difficult to differentiate between a bulky ovarian cyst and a massive pedunculated myoma, necessitating further investigation to avoid visceral organ misplacement.

To resolve these diagnostic ambiguities, the clinical team proceeded with an MRI examination without contrast medium, a decision driven by the need to balance diagnostic precision with fetal safety. Gadolinium enhancement is generally avoided during pregnancy, especially after the first trimester, unless there is a clear indication of life-threatening pathology or specific hereditary conditions not related to pregnancy. The non-contrast MRI in this case was pivotal because it could precisely determine the distance between the mass and the uterine cavity, identifying the cleavage plane between the masses. The MRI demonstrated thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content with focal hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid components, which are characteristic of decidualised endometriomas or degenerating myomas. While the upper mass remained equivocal regarding a stalk to the uterus on initial imaging, the MRI clarified the lower mass's continuity with the posterior uterine wall, suggesting a polypoid growth rather than an adnexal origin. Requiring specific safety protocols, the resultant imaging confirmed the risk of torsion or necrosis, guiding the surgical team toward an open exploration.

Serum tumor markers, particularly CA-125, present a significant diagnostic pitfall that must be weighed against the clinical picture during pregnancy. In this patient, the CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, showing a slight increasing trend from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. While a normal premenopausal reference is often <35 U/mL, physiological CA-125 levels rise significantly during pregnancy, peaking in the first and third trimesters and dipping slightly in the second trimester. The slight elevation of CA-125 at 21 weeks favored endometrioma in the differential diagnosis initially, as the median value during the second trimester is typically lower (around 14 U/mL). However, reliance on isolated CA-125 measurements is insufficient given the broad range of physiological changes. While CA-125 elevation can be associated with ovarian neoplasms in postmenopausal women, benign conditions and pregnancy itself can elevate the marker. The actual diagnostic reasoning required was to interpret the marker as part of a trend rather than a definitive biopsy, avoiding overtreatment when imaging features (cystic vs. solid) align with benign pathology.

Ultimately, the synthesis of ultrasound, MRI, and tumor markers steered the management toward a conservative approach only in cases where the risk of complications like rupture or torsion outweighs the risk of surgical intervention. In the current case, the "steric bulk" of the masses complicated the fetal head progression, and the clinical deterioration included worsening abdominal pain and dyspnoea. The multidisciplinary team concluded that the upper cyst was a pedunculated, atypical cystic and leiomyoma twist-and-leak risks, evidenced intraoperatively by the masochymtous bleeding and ischemic necrosis. The lesson is that imaging must be interpreted not in isolation but in the context of the clinical presentation. For example, the lack of a defined stalk on early imaging was reversed by the intraoperative finding of a pedicle, highlighting that imaging can sometimes suggest benignity in a complicated setting or the reverse. Careful evaluation of the mass boundaries, vascularity, and attachment (e.g., ovarian vs. uterine) allows for precise surgical planning, whether a myomectomy is required or if conservative expectant management is feasible for the residual mass.

## Surgical Management and Decision-Making in the Second Trimester

The decision to intervene surgically in the second trimester is often as complex as the diagnostic process, requiring a careful balance between maternal safety and fetal viability. In the context of this case, the choice to proceed with laparotomy at 21 weeks of gestation was driven by clear indications necessitating operative management rather than expectant care. The patient presented with severe, acute abdominal pain and restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) caused by the massive abdominal lesions compressing the diaphragm. Crucially, the intraoperative findings confirmed that the upper mass was a pedunculated leiomyoma that had twisted and begun to leak, evidence supported by the patient's preceding fever and leukocytosis. Surgical indication was thus advanced from the realm of "complicated pregnancy" to "emergency/salvage scenario" due to the immediate risk of further ischemia, rupture, and sepsis. While conservative management is the default for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic masses to preserve uterine integrity, the presence of comorbidities such as worsening anemia and mechanical compression necessitates an invasive approach. The presence of CA-125 elevation was noted but secondary to the mechanical and infectious signs; however, it contributed to the differential diagnosis rationale that justified the surgical exploration to rule out malignancy among the ovarian and uterine cystic structures.

In terms of the surgical approach, the multidisciplinary team selected a laparotomy rather than a laparoscopic or robotic intervention. The case report notes that while laparoscopy is often preferred for maternal morbidity in uncomplicated scenarios, specific anatomical constraints dictated the open approach in this instance. The upper lesion measured 18 cm and extended to the subphrenic space, while the lower lesion adhered to the posterior uterine wall. The presence of these large, intramural-subserosal masses with mixed solid and cystic components made minimally invasive resection technically hazardous. Furthermore, the complexity of the dissection required in this high-gestational-age population, combined with the need for precise hemostasis without disrupting the uterine muscle zone critically, favored the longitudinal xiphos-pubic cut. This incision provided optimal exposure for the pedunculated structure and allowed the team to perform a partial myomectomy with a lower rate of adhesion formation compared to an open excursion in an obese patient, aligning with the text's observation that open aspiration or closure for large masses in the 2nd trimester might be more feasible with this specific exposure method.

The intraoperative handling of the pathology required strict adherence to principles of clamping, resection, and closure to preserve fertility while ensuring hemorrhage control. Upon exploration, the surgeon identified a highly vascularised tumour that was twisted, presenting a risk of acute necrosis. The technique involved identifying and clamping the stalk of the loose mass; the text specifically notes the use of a double clamp to secure the tissue before excision. Once the mass was removed from its uterine implant, the surgeon exposed the root and carefully closed it with a single absorbable suture to prevent postoperative bleeding and maintain the integrity of the abdominal wall and myometrium. This technique contrasts with simple devascularisation but ensures that the uterine wall is sealed. Intraoperatively, the team also monitored the fetal condition continuously. Although direct continuous ECG monitoring is less invasive, the report cites the necessity of checking and documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery to maintain oxygenation and hemodynamics. Instrumentation included preoperative transfusion planning and postoperative iron supplementation to manage the baseline anemia of pregnancy and the bleeding associated with the surgery.

Intra-operative management of potential complications extended beyond mechanical hemorrhage control to infection management and fetal stabilization. The patient had a developing infection, confirmed by *Klebsiella pneumoniae* in urine samples with positive inflammatory markers (CRP and WBC). Therefore, antibiotic prophylaxis was initiated with cefazolin intraoperationally and continued postoperatively based on the specific antibiogram. Progesterone prophylaxis was also administered starting intraoperatively and continuing until discharge, a standard practice for second-trimester surgery to support uterine quiescence and reduce the risk of preterm labor. Progesterone was also kept on board as the caproate hydroxyprogesterone was administered until the elective delivery. This pharmacological support is critical when the uterus is manipulated by a laparotomy to ensure that the surgical stress does not trigger cervical shortening or uterine contractions. Fetal viability was maintained through a strict obstetric follow-up, and the absence of fetal distress throughout the procedure facilitated the decision to proceed with the definitive surgical correction.

Finally, the decision-making process regarding the partial resection and leaving the caudal mass in situ represents a classic strategic dilemma in uterine mass management during pregnancy. The text explicitly states that the caudal mass was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. While the upper mass, upon excision, presented as the primary obstruction to the diaphragm and source of sepsis, the lower mass represented a site of potential fetal head obstruction during the expulsive phase of labor. The surgical team prioritized the safety of the remaining pregnancy and future delivery mode. Given the massive size of the posterior myoma, leaving it undisturbed avoided the creation of a new scar on the lower uterine segment that would complicate the eventual elective caesarean delivery or increase the risk of scar rupture. This approach was further supported by the known pathophysiology that uterine leiomyomas follow a non-linear trend, increasing significantly between the first and second trimesters and regressing postpartum. Recognizing this, and the lack of definitive data supporting immediate myomectomy of non-torsional masses in the third trimester versus the eighth week, the team opted for selective intervention. This partial approach ensured that the immediate threat to the fetus's well-being (from maternal distress and birth mechanics) was managed without compromising the future obstetric trajectory.

## Obstetric Delivery Planning and Intra-operative Hematologic Support

Delivery planning in cases involving complicated abdominal or uterine masses is driven primarily by the mechanical obstruction of the vaginal birth canal and the risk of uterine structural integrity compromise. In this case, the decision for an elective caesarean section was dictated by the presence of a residual pelvic mass (9.5 cm) that was fixed to the posterior wall of the uterus. The surgical notes indicate that the caudal myoma presented a huge obstacle for fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour. Consequently, vaginal delivery was contraindicated, rendering the mode of delivery dependent on the anatomical location of the retained mass. The team opted to perform the caesarean section on the same surgical scar once the fetus reached 37 weeks of gestation. This timing represents a critical balance between bringing the pregnancy to term to minimize preterm morbidity and ensuring the myomata did not grow to a size that precluded delivery within the cervix. Elective delivery before the onset of spontaneous labour allows for a sterile field, minimizing the risk of intra-amniotic infection which could be particularly dangerous given the patient's underlying infectious status.

Hematologic management requires active surveillance, informed by both baseline deficiency and intraoperative blood loss. The patient presented with iron deficiency anaemia (Hb 9.9 g/L) which worsened to 7.5 g/L post-surgery, prompting a multidisciplinary approach to transfusion thresholds. In the context of major obstetric surgery, the drop to 7.5 g/L was deemed critical enough to require intravenous iron supplementation and two unit blood transfusions, achieving a postoperative hemoglobin of 10.0 g/L. This approach underscores the importance of monitoring hematologic parameters in pregnant patients undergoing laparotomy, especially when baseline reserves are low and surgical duration is prolonged. Cross-matched blood was administered to avoid hemolysis or incompatibility issues that can complicate neonatal or maternal outcomes. Following recovery, the patient required Iron Deficiency Anaemia management to restore physiological capacity for maternal recovery.

Infection control and antibiotic stewardship are challenged during pregnancy due to physiological increases in host defenses and altered drug pharmacokinetics. During the hospital stay, the patient developed a secondary urinary tract infection, identified by Klebsiella pneumoniae in urine samples confirmed by culture. The management involved antibiogram-guided treatment with intravenous cefazolin adjusted based on sensitivity (highly sensitive) and oral clarithromycin. Post-operative antibiotic coverage was limited strictly to the duration of hospital stay, emphasizing stewardship principles where broad-spectrum coverage is reserved for confirmed or suspected invasive bacterial infections rather than presumed risks. This approach aligns with the challenge of optimizing therapeutic benefits against the risk of antimicrobial resistance during the high-risk period surrounding pregnancy and surgical intervention.

Finally, pharmacologic prophylaxis for preterm labor is a critical component of maintaining pregnancy stability after surgical stress and mass excision. To mitigate the risk of preterm delivery, which is a significant concern after abdominal trauma and hemorrhagic events, the patient received progesterone prophylaxis. This was initiated intraoperatively with progesterone 100 mg intramuscularly and continued at a lower maintenance dose of 100 mg intramuscularly per day until discharge. Post-discharge, the patient received intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg every 3 days) until the elective delivery. This strategy reflects a conservative but proactive approach to fetal lung maturation and visceral placental function, ensuring the uterus remains secure and the fetus is not exposed to stress during the transition period. By the time of the scheduled caesarean section, the patient had achieved homeostasis, evidenced by the excellent maternal and neonatal outcome, consistent with the successful management of infectious and hematologic complications alongside the execution of the delivery plan.

## Postoperative Course and Long-term Clinical Implications

### Maternal Recovery and Clinical Resolution
Following the laparotomy, the first priority for the patient was hemodynamic stabilization and resolution of perioperative complications. Despite the initial nebulized anemia presenting as hemoglobin 7.5 g/L and hematocrit 24.3% post-surgery, definitive management required intravenous iron supplementation (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and two units of allogenic blood to achieve a hemoglobin level of 10.0 g/L. Uncomplicated infection control was achieved through targeted antibiotic therapy based on the urine bacterial load of *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, utilizing cefazolin in conjunction with clarithromycin from the time of hospitalization. This response allowed the patient to be discharged without complications on day 10 postoperatively, at which point the severe localized pain had been resolved, and vital signs stabilized. The use of intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis continued post-discharge to support placental stability, illustrating a protocol where maternal physiological recovery was prioritized alongside the immediate postoperative course. In this instance, the admission of a C-reactive protein of 1.39 mg/dL prior to discharge indicated that the inflammatory response was successfully contained without leading to sepsis, nor did it necessitate prolonged hospitalization.

### Fetal Well-being and Delivery Planning
The management of a gravid uterus with atypical masses required rigorous monitoring of fetal growth and fetal status throughout the recovery period. During the strict obstetric follow-up, fetal growth was calculated consistent with healthy development, achieving the 86th percentile with a strong amniotic fluid index, confirming that the uterine mass at the time of surgery did not compromise oxygenation or nutrient transport. Although the patient underwent an elective Caesarean delivery at 37 weeks gestation, the timing and mode of delivery align with the critical planning mentioned in the case report: the residual pelvic mass, while left in situ, grew to a size that would impede the vertex presentation in the expulsive phase of spontaneous labor. Consequently, performing the Caesarean section minimizes the risk of uterine scar rupture, a critical consideration given the previous lower uterine segment incision for hysterotomy or potential for expansion. The neonatal outcome was described as excellent, reinforcing that surgical intervention of the maternal masses did not yield iatrogenic complications such as intrauterine growth restriction or preterm labor in this specific presentation.

### Pathophysiology of Uterine Fibroid Behavior
The immediate postpartum course included observation of the residual uterine tissue to understand the natural history of leiomyomas. The pathology revealed benign uterine leiomyomas that underwent ischaemic necrosis due to the twisted pedicle of the excised upper mass, explaining the low-grade fever and leukocytosis during the hospital stay. Pathologically and clinically, the remaining lower mass—which persisted at the uterus fundus—followed a predictable trajectory for fibroids in response to hormonal withdrawal and resumption of atrophic hormonal status. Uterine leiomyomas generally follow a non-linear trend of size changes; they increase in size by up to 4.1 cm or 69% every week during the first trimester and 3.1% in the third trimester due to estrogen and progesterone-mediated proliferation. However, regardless of the aggressive size changes during gestation, the third trimester marks a turning point followed by significant decrease after delivery due to the cessation of hormonal stimulation. In this patient, the residual mass measured 7.5 cm by postpartum follow-up, a reduction from the 9 cm instability seen during the pregnancy episode, demonstrating the role of post-delivery hormonal regression in size management.

### Future Management Decisions: Observation versus Reintervention
The decision-making process regarding future gynecological management underscores the balance between symptomatology and elective intervention. While large fibroids may prompt immediate diagnosis and potential myomectomy postpartum, the patient in the present case opted for a deferred approach to measurement and time-sensitive myomectomy. This decision was driven by the evidence showing that the residual mass had slightly reduced to 7.5 cm following the second trimester, and crucially, the patient remained asymptomatic regarding pain, dyspnoea, or dysmenorrhea. Surgical preference is often weighed against the potential morbidity of repeat dissection versus the utility of natural regression. The patient’s high functional profile and decision to reschedule the next surgical step—either elective waiting or partial hysteresis—reflects a cautious strategy where maternal quality of life takes precedence unless symptoms reemerge. Furthermore, the patient’s breastfeeding well-being and desire to fulfill motherhood without planned hysterectomy indicates that the uterine preservation strategy was successfully validated in this case, minimizing the need for further aggressive surgical management.
