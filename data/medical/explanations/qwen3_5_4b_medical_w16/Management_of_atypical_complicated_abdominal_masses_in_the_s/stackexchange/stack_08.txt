Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] Why did the surgical team opt to excise the upper mass while leaving the lower pelvic mass in situ, and what key anatomical risks influenced this decision to avoid no untoward cost.

**Clinical Teaching Point: Risk-Stratified Management of Uterine Leiomyomas in Pregnancy**

In this case, the surgical team opted to excise the upper inferiorly pedunculated mass while leaving the lower pelvic mass in situ to balance acute maternal complications against long-term obstetric safety. The rationale is rooted in the differential anatomical risks and the potential for perinatal outcomes:

1.  **Upper Mass Excision (Acute Maternal Risk):** The upper lesion was identified preoperatively as a pedunculated leiomyoma that had become twisted and was "leaking," leading to ischemia, necrosis, fever, and severe pain. Because it possessed a distinct stalk ("cleavage... was not clear" only for the lower mass), it could be gently excised from its uterine implant without extensive disruption of the lower uterine segment. Leaving the necrotic, torsed mass posed an immediate threat of rupture, further infection, or sepsis. Removal provided a definitive, low-morbidity resolution to this acute threat.
2.  **Lower Mass Retention (Obstetric Safety):** The lower mass was described as "fixed" and "adherent to a posterior... myoma of the uterus." The text explicitly states that if removed, this mass would have represented a "huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour." Furthermore, the caudal mass was "difficult to separate" post-surgical exploration. Retaining it in situ avoided the high risk of uterine injury, hemorrhage, or preterm labor associated with dissection in a gravid uterus, particularly given that the decision to proceed to a caesarean delivery later unavoidable minimized the need for vaginal delivery attempts anyway.

This decision underscores a fundamental principle in managing complicated uterine masses during pregnancy: surgical intervention for benign leiomyomas should be limited to cases where the mass poses an immediate threat to maternal viability (e.g., torsion, rupture, severe symptoms) or where conservative management is contraindicated. In this instance, the pelvis was left to minimize surgery duration and maternal morbidity while the mast’s safety was compromised by its potential to obstruct labor, necessitating a 37-week electively scheduled delivery.
