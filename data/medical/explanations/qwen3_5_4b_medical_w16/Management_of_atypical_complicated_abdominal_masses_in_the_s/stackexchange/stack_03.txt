Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] Considering the hormonal environment of pregnancy, what are the documented weekly rates of growth for uterine leiomyomas between the trimesters, and how might this knowledge guide the timing of surgical planning to affect maternal morbidity?

Based on the data presented in the discussion of this case report, uterine leiomyomas follow a non-linear growth trajectory during pregnancy and puerperium driven by the hormonal environment. Specifically, leiomyomas increase in size by 6.90% annually, with a breakdown showing an average increase of 6.12% between the first and second trimesters (p<0.001) and a slower rate of 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Inversely, after the delivery, the size of myomas significantly decreased, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% by 6 weeks postpartum, typically during the lactation period.

This distinction in growth velocity and regression is critical for surgical planning. For uncomplicated masses, surgical management is usually delayed until after birth to avoid maternal morbidity; a referenced study cited within the report noted that while myomectomy did not significantly increase preoperative or transfusion rates compared to C-section alone, it significantly increased hospitalization duration and surgery duration (p=0.001). However, as illustrated in the current presentation, timing must yield to acute risk when "steric bulk" and systemic symptoms (e.g., dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compression, pain, or signs of necrosis/torsion) exist. The patient in this case underwent resection at 21 weeks because the massive mass threatened hypoxia and organ compromise, and the epitemal mass was restricted from the uterine cavity to minimize future obstetric risks. 

In summary, while the growth rate of 3.10% per week in the second and third trimesters allows for a safer window for elective intervention or observation than the rapid 6.12% phase in early pregnancy, the risk of complications seen in this second-trimester presentation dictates that urgent surgery should not be postponed despite the slowing growth rate. The primary manageplan is to utilize elective third-trimester myomectomy and subsequent caesarean section if the condition permits, whereas acute, symtempotic, or necrotic masses often require immediate laparotomic resection followed by a C-section to balance maternal morbidity and fetal safety.
