Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When to Operate on Pregnancy: Indications for Myomectomy and Laparotomy

## The Diagnostic Dilemma of the Second Trimester

There is nothing quite like a pregnant patient in the fourth decade of life with a belly that looks like it should contain a fetus, an elephant, and a massage shop at the same time. Meet our patient: a nulliparous woman in her 40s, BMI 33, presenting at 21 weeks gestation with acute abdominal pain, dyspnea, and a vague fever. On physical exam, her abdomen was palpable as if she were two trimesters plus a half pregnancy over. The bimanual exam revealed a fixed, hard pelvic mass, but the initial ultrasound story was a zoo: two large, unilocular cystic masses, mimicking decidualised endometriomas.

The kicker? The upper mass wasn't an endometrioma. It was a huge, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma, twisted, leaking, and necrotic. In the context of pregnancy, where a uterus is already hyper-insulated, these masses act like time bombs. Some clinicians might instinctively write down "IV hydration, bedrest, progesterone, and wait." In this specific case, that strategy would have failed. The mass could not tolerate further growth, it was intruding on vital structures (causing dyspnea), and necrosis had set in (fever, anemia).

## Why Imaging Matters (But Not as Much as Symptoms)

The CR interventionist does not want a point of view. Instead, we want a decision algorithm. In the setting of atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses during pregnancy, ultrasound is the gold standard but prone to misdiagnosis (as seen in this case). A US showing a large pedunculated mass extends the differential spectrum significantly.

However, advanced imaging is crucial for surgical planning. In this case, a non-contrast MRI without gadolinium confirmed the myomatous origin of the upper lesion and the continuity with the lower cystic mass. It helped determine steric bulk and distance from the uterine cavity. While MRIs without contrast are generally considered safe after the first trimester, gadolinium should be avoided.

Also do NOT rely on CA-125. A second-trimester CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL is low, as the marker typically peaks in the first trimester. By itself, it points toward benign pathology (endometriosis, myoma) rather than malignancy, especially given positive trends from normal limits.

The surgeon in this case made a call to operate at 21 weeks 6 days. Why? The key decision factors were: worsening clinical conditions, symptoms limiting physiological uterine enlargement, and specific risk of complications beyond rupture.

## Surgical Thresholds and Management Choices

When do you cut? The general rule for asymptomatic uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy is expectant management. However, think of the "red flags" that push you from onlooker to theatrics:

*   **Complications:** Acute abdominal pain and fever persisting despite analgesics/PCNK.
*   **Hemodynamics:** Iron deficiency anemia that worsens despite supplementation.
*   **Mass Characteristics:** Twisted or leaking masses (like the necrotic upper mass here).
*   **Anatomical Constraints:** Large masses that limit uterine growth, potentially causing dyspnea or obstructing labor (see lower mass in this case).

In the second trimester, the fetal quality is stable. If the mass is decompressing the diaphragm and nutritionally compromising the mother, the risk of waiting exceeds the risk of surgery. In this case, the surgeon opted for a longitudinal laparotomy. This was overkill for some, but justified by the massive size (18 cm) and intramural location that required precise access to avoid placental involvement.

**Important:** Serial TMZ is necessary during the procedure. The fetal heart rate must be monitored before, during, and after the surgery. In our case, the upper mass was excised lower than a 2-week period. The lower mass was left in situ to minimise obstetric risk.

## Delivery Mode: The Final Calculus

The repair was meticulously closed. Progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively. Antibiotics were guided by urine culture (Klebsiella). The definitive delivery method was the real variable here. The residual pelvic mass was left in situ. But the question becomes: vaginal birth or C-section?

Given the obstruction by the massive cystic/peduncular mass adherent to the posterior wall, the decision was to elect for a caesarean delivery at 37 weeks. Minimising the risk of uterine scar rupture is paramount. Furthermore, the mother underwent excellent follow-up, breastfeeding well, and the residual mass regressed slightly postpartum.

## Key Take-aways

*   **Waitlist vs. Operative:** Asymptomatic myomas in pregnancy can be observed; however, symptoms (pain, anemia, dyspnea), steric bulk, or signs of necrosis/torsion mandate timely surgical intervention.
*   **Imaging:** Non-contrast MRI is preferred for large masses over CT for radiation avoidance; avoid gadolinium unless strictly necessary.
*   **Tumor Markers:** CA-125 is benign in pregnancy; a value of 41.8 U/mL does not confirm malignancy but reflects physiological changes and myoma presence.
*   **Delivery Planning:** If a large myoma is left in situ, assess for intramural obstruction. An obstructed fetus requires a planned C-section to avoid uterine rupture.
*   **Risk vs. Reward:** Surgery in the first trimester carries viability risks. Second trimester surgery balances myoma growth against maternal morbidity.

In the end, the case teaches us that "wait and see" is only for "safe" masses. When the belly hurts, and the mass is compromising the anatomy, the mother comes first. But whenever you are cutting, remember the fetal heart rate is the other variable we are chasing.
