Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## The CA-125 Paradox: Interpreting Ovarian Tumor Markers in Pregnancy

There is a level of urgency that often accompanies a flip of the white papers that reads "CA-125 elevated". In our trauma bay, an urgent abdomen, a cancer workup starts well below a threshold that you might see on an OB report. But consider this: a non-invasive approach that reads 41.8 U/mL in a 21-week pregnant woman reads the same as a gynecologic oncology file. The reality is that she didn't have ovarian cancer. She had a 18cm pedunculated uterine leiomyoma, twisted and necrotic. The high serum levels were a mix of pregnancy physiology, adenomyosis from the tumor itself, and the inflammatory cycle of the degenerating myoma.

Every clinician knows the normal tri-trimester curve. But looking at that curve, we can sometimes miss the true story of the clamp.

### The Physiology and the Limits of a Single Point

Physiologic elevation is the norm, but single readings are rarely enough. Peak levels in pregnancy occur in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL) again in the third trimester. The second trimester is usually the lowest point (median 14.0 U/mL). This patient presented with 31 weeks plus 6 days. The lab shows 41.8 U/mL. While the median for that time window is 14, the upper limits are often set around 35. If a single upload triggers a pan-retinal dilation and a biopsy, the patient often loses the third trimester.

We need to look beyond the lab to the trend. CA-125 is not just a marker; it's a reactivity. It measures peritoneal irritation as well as tumor burden. Pregnancy, adenomyosis, and even endogenous decidualization can mimic this rise. The goal is to avoid unnecessary surgery on cryostats or unnecessary anxiety. Wait for it. Look for it.

### Imaging: The Safety vs. Accuracy

In the case I shared, the upper limit was the difficult choice. If the MRI had revealed a metastatic pattern, the surgical management might have been more definitive. But the MRI without contrast showed thick walls with solid components suggestive of a myoma. MRI without contrast was chosen over the gadolinium.

This is crucial. Gadolinium is a beta-receptor blocker on the fetal side. You need the check in the head and the teammates. But you don't want the kidney acid burn. Standard of care limits contrast to avoid the risk of renal failure. In our case, the MRI showed unilocular cysts with fluid content inhomogeneity. That's the key. It felt like endometrioma, but the anatomy was wrong. The subclinical rise of the marker shifted from the uterus to the ovary. The mass was uterine. The marker was just the pain to the nerve.

### A Lesson in Surgical Thinking

This case shows us a surgeon's dilemma. You have a patient who is 37 weeks, 2 days with abdominal mass and dyspnoea. The risk of latency is high. The risk of rupture is high. The option is to operate.

Why did the multidisciplinary team choose surgery here? The risk of preterm delivery is increased if you keep a massive scrotal mass. Also, the mass was tied to a pedicle. Rupture, torsion, and infection risks outweigh the cost of surgery.

The decision to leave the caudal mass in situ was not a choice; it was a boundary. Challenging the wall. But to keep the closure, the surgery lasted 2 hours. The patient had mass transfusion, progesterone prophylaxis, and the rest is follow-up.

### What Does This Mean for You?

When you are managing a patient with CA-125 elevation in pregnancy, focus on the *reaction of the marker* rather than the *number* on the screen. The uterus must look at the wife's history and the imaging.

*   **Do not use a single CA-125 level to justify preterm delivery in pregnancy.**
*   **Prioritize non-contrast MRI to avoid fetal toxicity in contrast imaging.**
*   **Clinical symptoms (dyspnea, pain) often drive the decision to cut more than the lab work.**
*   **Understand that CA-125 can rise in adenomyosis and pregnancy, mimicking malignancy patterns.**

The patient had excellent obstetrics. The baby was intact. The mother delivered a baby at 37 weeks. That is the standard. But the mass was 18 cm. That mass was going to kill the oxygenation. It was an intervention based on physiology and the mass effect. It is a classic reminder of how OB medicine and GYN medicine intersect when they crash.
