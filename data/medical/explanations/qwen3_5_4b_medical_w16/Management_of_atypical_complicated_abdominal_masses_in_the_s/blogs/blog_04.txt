Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Obstetric Logistics: Managing Uterine Pelvic Masses and Labor Distortion

In the fast-paced world of emergency medicine and obstetrics, having two massive abdominal lumps in a pregnant woman can quickly become a diagnostic spin-off. Imagine a 21-week singleton gestation where the uterus isn't just enlarged, but accompanied by two new, space-occupying lesions. This woman, a nulliparous woman in her 40s with a BMI of 33, arrived with severe abdominal pain in both the left upper quadrant and the pelvis. The clinical picture was deceptive. Initial ultrasound suggested decidualized endometriomas, and the slight elevation in CA-125 led us to wonder about malignancy. However, the case pivots on how you balance the risk of surgical intervention against the mechanical constraints of labor, ultimately deciding that the posterior pelvic mass acts as a physical antagonist to the expulsive phase of birth.

The diagnostic journey here is a prime lesson in avoiding the trap of physiological mimicry. During pregnancy, serum CA-125 can rise physiologically in the first and third trimesters. In our patient, the value was 41.8 U/mL, interpreted as a need for investigation due to the trend, yet it remains non-specific given the high false-positive rate for benign conditions like endometriosis and leiomyomas. When ultrasound showed a hard, irregular mass adherent to the posterior wall, we opted for an MRI without contrast. This was crucial because gadolinium is generally avoided in pregnancy, and high-field (<1.5-T) MRI without contrast reliably mapped the fluid content and solid components. Neoplasia was unlikely given the normal tumor panel and exclusion of lymphadenopathy, cementing the diagnosis: left-overcustomer-like leiomyomas with cystic degeneration.

### The Surgical Dilemma During Gestation

The core decision point occurred during the laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days. We faced a choice: excise everything or leave it. The superior, pedunculated mass posed a threat of acute complications such as torsion and ischemia, prompting its excision. In contrast, the inferior pelvic mass was left in situ to minimize further obstetric trauma during recovery. This decision wasn't arbitrary. Retaining the posterior mass was a deliberate strategy to ensure that if the patient went for a vaginal delivery, the uterus itself wasn't compromised, but it meant accepting a specific logistics risk: booking labor management differently. By 37 weeks, the residual mass (which shrank slightly postpartum to 7.5 cm) would rigidly block the fetal head from engaging or crowding in the lower segment, necessitating a planned cesarean section. If we had waited until rupture, the trauma could be fetal or maternal. This case underscores that in pregnancy, the mode of delivery is often dictated by the obstetrical landmarks created by pathology, not just fetal station.

The medical team also managed the systemic complications vigorously. The patient developed frank anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L) pre-discharge, necessitating IV iron and transfusion to avoid maternal morbidity. Progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraperitoneally to suppress acne and potentially modulate uterine contractility, though compliance with the specific regimen varies. The patient tolerated the procedure well, recovering to discharge in 10 days with normal fetal growth (86th percentile). The key lesson here is that aggressive, conservative management is often not enough when the mass grows by approximately 7% per week during early pregnancy, requiring timely surgical reduction if symptoms persist.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Don't panic at CA-125 during pregnancy:** Physiological elevations occur in early and late pregnancy; rely on structural features (mass morphology, CA-125 trend, and palpation) rather than a single snapshot value.
*   **Contrast-free MRI is your friend in pregnancy:** Avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary; non-contrast MRI provides sufficient detail for grading mass adherence and viability to guide surgical safety.
*   **Labor Distorted by Masses:** Posterior uterine masses may prevent vaginal delivery due to topographical obstruction; plan C-section if the mass size and location preclude fetal engagement.
*   **Timing is critical for Myomectomy:** Document fetal HR before, during, and after surgery; surgical intervention is justified by growth rate (6.90%/week) and mechanical threat rather than cancer risk alone.
*   **Residual Mass Planning:** If you leave a large pelvic myoma in situ, expect a mode of delivery determined by the mass as a barrier, not soft tissue standing up against the fetal head.
