Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Read the Map: The Role of MSCT in Complex Valve Replacements

There is a line of logic in interventional cardiology where we often operate on the principle of "fix what's broken." When a valve fails, we replace it. But what happens when the double fits? Or rather, when you have to fit inside the double that happens to be inside a double.

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (TAVI) is standard care for most, but when a patient endures ruptures in a double, triple, or quadruple valve configuration, you move into uncharted territory. We present an extraordinary case report of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, a "third valve" scenario in a patient who underwent TAVI in 2014, then again in 2022, followed by this one in 2024.

The primary challenge here isn't just the overlapping stents; it's the prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient required six weeks of intravenous antibiotics prior to this third procedure, and the team confirmed no active infection via blood cultures and TTE. Yet, the anatomy created a risk of coronary ostial obstruction so severe that many might have dismissed the patient for repeat surgery due to prohibitive risk surgery.

This case demonstrates the specific utility of preprocedural multislice computed tomography (MSCT) planning in navigating triple-valve configurations. It serves as a potent reminder that when anatomy becomes complex, alone we are in the unknown, but with the map, we are in high risk—manageable.

## Anatomy in the Maze: The Problem of Serial Valve Interventions

The patient presented in 2014 with a Lotus valve. By 2022, the Lotus had failed, requiring an Evolut prosthesis via a TAV-in-TAV approach. This procedure was already precarious—high pressure ballooning often used to achieve valve expansion. But by 2024, the Evolut had also begun to structurally deteriorate, eventually worsening to severe restenosis amid a history of PVE.

The darkness in prognostic modeling comes from how the prior devices incorporate into the aortic wall. With two valves already present, a third valve implantation risks the "cage effect." What is worse, frame layering can encroach on thoracic valve-to-coronary distances, leading to coronary ostial obstruction. This case isn't just about replacing a valve; it's about leaving enough space for future patent coronary ostia.

### The "High Implantation" Technique

In this case, the procedural team utilized a specific strategy that is gaining traction: high implantation. The team aimed to implant the third valve—at the level of the Evolut leaflets—so that the leaflets of the Evolut were pushed against the frame of the new Acurate neo2.

This technique helps "lock the engine" so that the inner valve is not left in a porous opening that risks failing or narrowing the orifice area further by inward leaflet folding. While this sounds simple, in a triple-stented anatomical base, the difference between achieving an AVA of 1.0 and needing a third hospitalization can be the height of the native valve annulus versus the landing zone distance.

## MSCT: Your Everyday Compass

Where does the team typically get the data to make these decisions? They turn to MSCT. It wasn't just about seeing what you were seeing; it was seeing what you'd *think* you were seeing without the scan.

The preprocedural MSCT provided a 3D model of the valve interaction, allowing the team to visualize the minimum lumen diameter of 13.7mm in the Evolut prosthesis. This was critical in confirming feasibility before the skin was cut. The model allowed the team to verify the coronals artery ostial height and estimate the risk of coronary obstruction.

This case highlights the evidentiary value of innovative 3DMSCT model planning in validating the feasibility of the depth of the total implantation. Evaluating the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and coronary ostia allowed the team to choose a size (S) and implantation depth that would maintain coronary flow without the need for the corollary of coronary protection devices or high-cost angioplasty procedures that follow.

## Why Transcatheter in PVE?

One crucial decision in this case was whether to operate surgically or transcatheter. ESC guidelines often recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE with severe heart failure.

In 2014, the patient had a "decided" TAVI because surgery was too risky. Now, surgery was even #1000 times riskier. Open surgery on a patient who had three previous stents in 3000 cm in the same area would mean total root replacement. It is nearly impossible to remove three transcatheter valves without major aortic damage. So, instead of the typical surgical valve replacement, the decision was to laser the valve to the less invasive nature of a catheter with the higher risk of the surgery.

### Critical procedural markers that make this special
*   **Coronary Access:** Comprehensive MSCT analysis of coronary ostial height and distance to valve frames.
*   **Antimicrobial Clearance:** No long-term suppressive therapy and no active infection found—essential for structural valve reintervention.
*   **Device Selection:** Use of a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve for its low frame height and supra-annular leaflets.
*   **Valve-to-Coronary Distance:** Calculated via preprocedural CT and validated intraoperatively by rapid ventricular pacing and aortography.

## Summary

This case serves as a complex, high-stakes example of the "Triple-Implant" strategy. It highlights that with modern technology and careful planning, the third valve can last longer than just a single valve replacement.

The outcome at 1 year showed a mean gradient of 26 mmHg and an AVA of 1.1 cm². The patient remained physically active and asymptomatic. This is evidence that complex anatomical challenges, while difficult, are technically feasible.

## Key Takeaways

*   **MSCT is your compass in valve failure:** Preoperative imaging allows for precise assessment of valve-to-coronary distance and neo-skirt risk.
*   **High implantation is a survival strategy:** By aligning the new valve with the Evolut leaflets, you can preserve the structural integrity of the previous frame.
*   **Infection control is procedural:** Only after active infection is confirmed resolved should catheter valve reintervention be considered.
*   **The calendar matters:** For 2014, 2022, and 2024, the timeline in combined treatment requires clear communication to reduce the risk of future interventions.

The evidence here is limited because the field is young. But this case shows us that if we plan with the map in front of us, we can navigate the storm of structural valve disease without crashing the ship.
