Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Intervention After Treating PVE: Timing and Risk Assessment for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

You know those cases that make you pause and rethink your own heart team algorithms? This one is that rare case that reads like a thriller but turns out to be a textbook for high-risk structural valve management. We are looking at a man in his late 70s with a ticking clock and a vessel filled with unintended complications. He’s got a Cs-associated paravalvular vegetation history, two previous catheter-based interventions, and eventually needs a third valve—all within a decade. The core clinical challenge here isn’t just managing the mechanical failure; it’s managing the infection timeline against the mechanical re-homing of a biologic frame three times.

The most surprising element of this case is the OK-for-Ground decision-making process regarding the infected history. The 2025 ESC guidelines on infective endocarditis generally scream "surgery or at least mechanical valve" for prosthetic valve endocarditis, especially with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But here, the surgeon decided on TAVI. Why? Because the patient had a 2025 EuroSCORE II of over 27 percent. In the context of a high-risk, prosthetic valve electronics surgery candidate, the risk of total ascending root replacement is prohibitive. Once the *Enterococcus faecalis* was cleared and cultures were negative for at least six weeks, the structural valve failure became a surgical re-engineering problem, not a typical infectious seeding problem.

You have to respect the imaging planning that went into this. This wasn't a blind valve drop in a triple stented anatomy. Preprocedural multislice CT was used to map the minimum lumen diameter of the existing Evolut frame against the potential new Acurate neo2. They specifically looked for the coronary ostial height and calculated the frame-to-coronary distance. The technical pearl here is the implantation depth: they aimed high up the Evolut leaflets to prevent further reduction of the effective orifice area. In the grand scheme of TAVI, valve-in-valve is standard. Valve-in-valve-in-valve after endocarditis is a procedural anomaly, and the plan was flexible enough to minimize the neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia.

**## Timeline and Intervention Timing**

When did they intervene after the *Enterococcus*? This is critical. They didn't operate while the patient was febrile or sicker. The patient received targeted antibiotic therapy for six weeks (IV ampicillin), followed by oral treatment recommendations from infectious disease specialists. Clinical assessment, TE, and CT scans confirmed resolution of infection without vegetations or paravalvular complications before the third valve was deployed. This distinction—treating the heart failure vs. treating the infection—is vital. In this case, infection control was prioritized first, then the prosthetic mechanical failure was addressed as if it were a degenerative case, provided the surgical risk was confirmed too high otherwise.

The procedure itself involved multiple access points: Left radial and left femoral arterial access. They used balloon pre-dilation and rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) to ensure no obstruction happened to a coronary artery during placement. It was done under institutional approval for off-label use of the Acurate neo2. The valve design supports commissural alignment, which is key when you have to balance the leaflets of three distinct devices. Even with the triple mesh burden, the goal was to maintain accessibility for future angiography if needed.

**## Long-Term Outcome and Surveillance**

The 1-year follow-up is the clincher. The patient is active, asymptomatic, and has a mean gradient of 26 mmHg. There was initial hemodynamic worsening (gradient 67 mmHg) at one month, but post-dilation and frame expansion brought it down. The effective orifice area tracked from 0.9 to 1.1 cm², which is significantly better than the 0.5 cm² seen in the third pre-flight. At one year, there was no residual regurgitation and no infective signs. This suggests that keeping the infection non-chronic and proceeding with catheter-based planning is a viable alternative to open surgical replacement in high-risk settings.

The main takeaway here isn't that we should definitely do the third valve for an infected patient. It's that when surgical risk is too high and infection is resolved, the structural collapse of bioprosthetic valves can still be redeemed by a mechanical strategy that accounts for the mechanical reality of the aorta's stented history. It challenges the assumption that PVE precludes re-intervention.

### Take-Home Messages

*   **Timing:** Proceed with late surgical TAV intervention after confirmed resolution of *Enterococcus* infection, not while the vegetation is active.
*   **Risk Stratification:** Evaluate surgical risk (e.g., EuroSCORE II) in combination with prior valve history to justify transcatheter reintervention.
*   **Imaging:** Pre-procedural MSCT is non-negotiable for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategies to assess coronary clearance and stenosis risk.
*   **Implantation Strategy:** High implantation and commissural alignment are essential to preserve coronary flow in multiple layered stents.
*   **Shared Decision-Making:** Multidisciplinary review by cardiology, surgery, and infectious diseases is required for these complex multi-valve scenarios.
