# Device Geometry in High-Impact Redo TAVI: Balloon vs. Self-Expanding

## The Stained Glass of the Aorta

This case struck me as a bit of a beauty conscious of the surgical community because it is so rare. Presenting with a third Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (TAVI) after a history of transfacendial endocarditis is extremely uncommon. In a typical patient, two overlapping valves usually have one tiny chance of survival if they are managed successfully. This man has 3 primary valves stacked in his aorta—Lotus in 2014, Evolut in 2022, and now he tries to implant an Acurate Neo2. Plus, in the meantime, he had serious infective endocarditis (Enterococcus faecalis). Normally, you would say, "Do surgery." But the surgical risk was too high.

So, what matters here is the decision-making process when you try to get a self-expanding valve in a triple stented anatomy. We know very well that in TAVI redo, sometimes you can't get the first valve to work anymore. In this case, the valve had to do the triple magic instead of just saving one life. It comes down to the geometry and the timing of implant.

## Timeline of a Unique Journey

The patient is a man his late 70s. He has a very stressful history, with cardiovascular disease, COPD, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions. He first had a Lotus valve in 2014. That Latent device did the job for a bit, but as he aged, he started having trouble with the 2021 evaluation. By 2022, his gradient was 83 mm Hg.

He got an Evolut in 2022. It worked well. But 1 month later, he was sent to the hospital with fever and heart failure symptoms. That is when the infection happened. The microphone grew on the valve ring and grew on the system, causing a valve restenosis. He was treated with antibiotics for endocarditis. It was resolved.

But 1 year later, the valve failed again. He had a gradient of 49 mmHg. He needed to redo. Since he has a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and some of the enlarged vessels, they knew they had to choose between re-surgery and redo TAVI. So they chose TAVI again, but this time, you have to plan like a surgeon. The team chose a high-implant to keep the valves from closing in a way.

## Why Select Self-Expanding in Triple TAVI?

The blog post describes "Device Geometry," and it is a feeling of double-taking a valve. In TAVI-in-TAV-in-TAV, the self-expanding valve offers a specific advantage in how it sits inside the frame. The initial choice was Evolut, which is a balloon-expandable valve, so even though they used a Lotus valve initially. The difference between the two is just the balloon geometry, and the Acurate Neo2 (Supra-annular) is a device-geometry approach.

The team used Acurate Neo2 size S. The reason was this: the Acurate Neo2 has a low frame height and it allows for the leaflets to be above the stent. This helps with the commissural alignment. The device was aligned with the Evolut valve leaflets. If you just put a self-expanding valve inside a balloon-expandable valve, you have to check how much the annulus is. The Acurate Neo2 has the low frame height, which allows the leaflets to fold in a way that helps with the rate of expansion. It allows the valve to be high implanted to prevent a neo-skirt from forming below the coronary ostia.

Using the ACurate Neo2 in a triple-TAV anatomy allowed them to reduce the risk of PPM (patient-prosthesis mismatch) because the plaque was near the apex of the aorta. They used 3D modeling on the CT scan to ensure the third valve would not block the coronary arteries. They ran a balloon predilation, and they used a Safari guidewire to check the LV. Post-dilation was done because they had to ensure that the frame was expanded correctly. They used the CT plan and the fluoroscopy markers to tell them when to deploy the third valve.

## Clinical Pearls

While the case report does not show the final outcome has to be perfect, it provides a comprehensive plan for TAVI-in-TAV-in-TAV. The main challenge is that the patient had active infection but it was treated before the procedure. The procedure was selected as a self-expanding valve instead of a balloon-expandable valve. This is because the frame height is lower. Also, the valve is able to work better with the frame of the first TAVI.

Here are the main clinical points from this case report.

- **Order 3 valves:** When you have 2 valves in your ring, the third valve is a high-risk exception.
- **Endocarditis Clearance:** Make sure the infection is not active. Check the blood cultures and the ECHO to ensure there are no vegetations.
- **Frame Geometry:** Use self-expanding valves that have low frame heights to reduce the PPM risk in TAV-in-TAV.
- **CT imaging:** The pre-procedure CT is the most important step. You need the 3D modeling to check the coronary arteries and the valves.
- **Post-dilation:** Balloon predilation and post-dilation is important to ensure the valve is not too high or too low in the aorta.

## Long-Term Outcome and Limitations

The follow-up was great. One year later, the valve is working, there is no regurgitation, and the EF is 65. The mean gradient dropped to 26 mm Hg. The patient is active. The only risk is that the TAVI-in-TAV-in-TAV is not fully solved. The valve is a self-expanding device. If this device fails again, the only options are surgical explantation, which is risky.

Another consideration is the future of the patient. They mentioned the possibility of the valve failing. They also said they are using a self-expanding valve for the 3rd time. This is an off-label use of the Acurate Neo2. They are using the 3D modeling to see if there is enough room.

The case report emphasizes the need for multidisciplinary team discussion. The team must include interventionalists, surgeons, imaging, and anesthesia. They also need to think long-term. This is a lifetime management of structural valve disease. The key take away is that the valve selection must be based on the geometry and the risk profile of the patient.

## Conclusion

This case is a classic lesson in complexity. A triple-stented aortic ring after endocarditis is a rarity. The choice of valve was driven by the geometry of the frame and the coronary flow. The patient was stable, so the procedure was worth the trouble. This is a very valuable example for the cardiology community.
