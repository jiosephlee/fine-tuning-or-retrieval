## Structural Valve Deterioration and Serial TAVI Pathophysiology

Bioprosthetic aortic valves, including those used in first-line TAVI, are inherently subject to mechanical wear and tissue remodeling over time. While modern materials such as bovine pericardium offer improved tenant durability, structural valve deterioration (SVD) remains the primary long-term complication across most populations. Pathophysiologically, this occurs through progressive leaflet fibrosis and medial calcification, often accelerated by thromboembolic disease or hemodynamic stress. In clinical case series like that of Merkulov et al., this degeneration becomes the primary driver for reintervention. In the described patient, the 2014 Lotus prosthesis showed clinical and echocardiographic evidence of severe stenosis by 2022, a trajectory consistent with bioprosthetic matricule degradation. Understanding this biological decay is crucial, as it dictates the window of opportunity for early redo-TAVI before the valve mechanics catastrophically fail, leading to atrial fibrillation or acute heart failure.

The pathophysiology of serial interventions, specifically transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter (TAV-in-TAV) configurations, introduces significant changes in local hemodynamics and vascular mechanics compared to solitary valve replacement. When a second valve is placed inside a failed first valve (TAV-in-TAV), the second device effectively treats the stenotic prosthesis of the first device as its own annulus. This creates a layering effect where the stent frame of the previously implanted valve acts as a scaffold for new valvular tissue and calcification. In serial procedures like TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, this effect is magnified. As seen in the patient's case, the Evolut prosthesis was implanted into the Lotus frame in 2022, later necessitating a third valve placement. Over time, the overlapping stent frames accumulate calcification; the outer surface of the frame can act as a nidus for calcium deposition while the inner leaflets characously fuse or calcify. This process increases leaflet stiffness and reduces frame flexibility, fundamentally altering the annulus mechanics and making subsequent leaflet motion dependent on the marginal support rather than native tissue elasticity.

Haemodynamic challenges are likely exacerbated in triple-stented anatomy due to progressive reductions in effective orifice area (EOA) and the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). In the 2020s, the "frame-limited" theory suggests that as older prosthesis frames calcify, their inner diameters shrink, constraining flow through the valve ostia of newer valves placed in only. In our case, preprocedural multislice CT revealed a survival lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm in the Evolut, confirming severe hemodynamic compromise despite the Evolut appearing structurally intact. This calcific narrowing, coupled with the added frame of the Acurate neo2, creates a "triple-skirt" effect that can impede forward flow, resulting in elevated mean gradients (seen as 49 mm Hg pre-procedure in 2024). Surgeons and interventional cardiologists must differentiate between valve malfunction due to structural leaflet closure and obstruction caused by the interaction of multiple nitinol frames.

Furthermore, the proximity of multiple stent valves to the coronary ostia presents a unique risk stratification for coronary interference. As valves become superimposed, the cumulative mesh burden can reduce the distance from the coronary ostia to the aortic ring, potentially leading to obstruction of the ostia or incomplete sealing of the coronary flow. In triple-valve configurations, this risk is heightened and requires precise preoperative planning. The case report highlights this dilemma through the surgeon's decision to perform a high implantation of the Acurate neo2. Theoretical pathophysiology dictates that a high implantation level fixes the leaflets against the superior frame to keep the leaflets open, preventing the "inward folding" that creates a neo-skirt which obstructs coronary ostial flow. While this strategy mitigated the well-documented risk of coronary obstruction in this specific anatomy, it underscores the complexity of managing complex vascular access in serial TAV and the potential for compromised future coronary interventions if not planned for.

Finally, risk stratification regarding procedural complexity in triple-stented anatomy distinguishes these cases from routine redo-TAVI. The decision to attempt a third TAVI procedure carries a significant burden of potential complications, including vascular rupture, pericardial effusion, and hemodynamic instability. In the patient described, open heart surgery was deemed prohibitive (EuroSCORE II of 27.36%) due to the likelihood of requiring deep structural revision. This highlights that the physiological pathophysiology of serial valves—where dense calcification and mechanical rigidity compromise the standard anatomy for mitral valve replacement or surgical excision—necessitates a strategy-driven approach. The procedural success relies on precise frame alignment, commissural orientation, and balloon-assisted expansion to achieve optimal hemodynamics without sacrificing access to the coronary circulation. Recognizing these mechanisms allows clinicians to anticipate the challenges of future valve explantation and planning for lifetime management strategies in structural valve disease.

## Clinical Evaluation, Diagnostic Criteria, and Indication Assessment

Clinical evaluation in a patient with history of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) and prior endocarditis begins with the recognition of specific symptoms that indicate hemodynamic compromise rather than chronic degeneration. In this patient, the clinical presentation included dyspnea, a spastic sensation in the left chest, dizziness, and fatigue, symptoms that had progressively worsened following the initial restoration of valve function after the second procedure. The critical clinical step in this stage is distinguishing between recurrent active infective endocarditis and severe structural valve deterioration. While active infection often presents with fever, the presence of severe valve dysfunction (indicative by hemodynamic changes) is confirmed by the timing of worsening symptoms and echocardiographic findings. Crucially, diagnostic clearance of infection is a prerequisite for any reintervention in this setting, as active vegetations or valvular seeding would preclude safe tissue placement of a transvalvular device. In the absence of fever and with negative prior blood cultures and imaging, the clinical team determined that the primary issue was mechanical valve failure rather than recurrent infection, thereby shifting the focus to structural assessment.

Definitive diagnosis relies on the application of standardized diagnostic criteria using transthoracic echocardiography and multislice computed tomography (MSCT). By the time of the third intervention, transvalvular flow analysis defined the severity of stenosis. The Trasterantranseal echocardiogram (TTE) demonstrated a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg and an aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm². In echocardiographic grading, a valve area less than 1.0 cm² and a mean gradient exceeding 30 mm Hg define severe aortic stenosis. In the context of prosthetic valves, significant gradients usually prompt evaluation for prosthesis-patient mismatch. The identification of severe restenosis, grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation, and a markedly reduced AVA was the "green light" for intervention. However, interim infection management was vital; for the June 2024 procedure, the patient had completed a full course of ampicillin-based antimicrobial therapy, with no long-term suppressive therapy ongoing. This ensured the histologic risk of seeding was minimized, aligning with guidelines that recommend surgery or intervention once infection is cleared in high-risk patients.

In assessing the safety of intervention, the decision to bypass open surgical valve replacement as the primary modality relies heavily on systemic risk stratification and anatomical feasibility. The patient presented with multiple independent risk factors, including debilitating comorbidities such as third-degree AV block requiring a pacemaker, type 2 diabetes, COPD, and previous gastrointestinal bleeding. Most notably, the decision-making framework integrated specific risk calculators, such as the EuroSCORE II, which in this instance yielded a significant value of 27.36%. A EuroSCORE II greater than 10-20% typically categorizes a patient as high surgical risk, often making surgical valve replacement prohibitive. Additionally, the anatomical constraints were decisive; the presence of two overlapping bioprosthetic valves (Lotus and Evolut) meant that surgical access would likely require deep dissection of the deep stent framework and the potential for total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. The combination of prohibitive surgical risk and the lack of immediate need for valve access during an open procedure steered the team toward a strategy-driven transcatheter solution, specifically avoiding the high risks of cardiopulmonary bypass.

The integration of pre-procedural imaging played a decisive role in the indication assessment. While standard TTE provided the functional candidates for intervention, more complex strategies required MSCT to assess the safety of a triple-valve configuration. The scan revealed that the minimum lumen diameter of the existing Evolut prosthesis was 13.7 mm while confirming that the coronary ostia were clear of obstructions or mesh entrapment. The "indication" for a third valve was thus twofold: the severity of dysfunction which threatened the failing ventricular remodeling (low EF of 65%) and the high surgical mortality that would necessarily result from a redo-valve operation. The synthesis of diverse clinical data—symptom burden, hemodynamic thresholds, infectivity status, and surgical risk score—allowed the clinical team to confidently indicate that a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach was the most viable, life-saving treatment option given the patient's unique journey.

## Advanced Imaging, Strategic Planning, and Device Selection

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) is rarely a mechanical procedure but a complex clinical decision rooted in advanced imaging and strategic planning. In a patient with multiple prior valve procedures and a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), the risk of procedural failure significantly increases due to anatomical distortion and firewall overlap. Pre-procedural planning must occur in a multidisciplinary Heart Team setting, involving interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, and imaging specialists to evaluate the trade-off between surgical explantation/transplant and a high-risk catheter approach. In this specific case, the multidisciplinary consensus was driven by the patient's prohibitive surgical risk profile. With a comparative risk estimation (EuroSCORE II) of 27.36%, combined with significant comorbidities including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, and prior pacemaker implantation, open surgical valve replacement would have necessitated total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. Such an endeavor carries extremely high morbidity in a patient with severe calcification and previous valve stents extensively integrated into the aortic wall. Consequently, the strategy favored a strategy-driven catheter intervention, contingent upon rigorous imaging to ensure coronary protection and hemodynamic success.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) and detailed evaluation of the coronary ostia serve as the cornerstone of procedural feasibility assessment in triple-valve anatomies. Pre-procedural CT analysis is critical to map the interaction between the overlapping frames of the Lotus, Evolut, and the planned Acurate neo2 prostheses. The primary concern is preventing iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction, or the formation of a "neo-skirt," which occurs when the upper skirt of the new valve encroaches upon the ostia. In this case, MSCT revealed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the overlying Evolut prosthesis, indicating potential compromise. The imaging team specifically analyzed the coronary ostial height, the sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and the distance between the intended neo-valve and the original coronary flow. Special attention was paid to measuring the landing zone and validating that template expansion did not restrict cardiac output, ensuring that the third implant could be deployed safely without requiring additional coronary protection devices.

Device selection for serial TAV-in-TAV procedures requires careful consideration of the mechanical design of the prosthesis, specifically the frame height and leaflet position relative to previous valves. The Acurate neo2 was selected over other options in this case due to its supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height. These characteristics were essential to execute a deliberate high implantation strategy. By implanting the self-expanding biological valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets—rather than in the native annulus—the pre-existing leaflets were effectively pushed back against the stent frame, preventing further inward leaflet folding that was evident in the prior Evolut degeneration. This high placement also minimized the risk of creating a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia, which is a significant complication in sequential interventions. The low-profile nature of the device provided flexibility in deployment, allowing for precise commissural alignment with the previous valves to maintain the effective orifice area and reduce the risk of severe prosthetic-patient mismatch (PPM).

Finally, targeted imaging landmarks were utilized to ensure that frame-to-frame interaction did not compromise coronary flow or future reaccess. Magnetic resonance angiography or detailed MSCT post-processing allowed for virtual simulation, assessing whether patient anatomy would withstand the cumulative stent mesh burden of triple-layering. While no intentional frame fracture was performed, the plan included a high-pressure balloon post-dilation to ensure the radial expansion of the Evolut frame, enhancing the effective orifice area. This imaging-guided planning was not merely about the current intervention but also included long-term considerations: in a high-risk patient with such a triple-stented anatomy, future physiological outcomes or valve replacements would be exceptionally limited, necessitating a strategy that optimized hemodynamic output for longevity. The successful integration of these imaging data points enabled the team to visualize the safety margin for coronary perfusion, confirming that the chosen TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach was feasible without the extreme risk of coronary obstruction.

## Procedural Execution and Technical Maneuvers

The procedural execution for this patient was defined by the need to navigate a complex, triple-stented anatomy within the setting of a prior prosthetic valve endocarditis diagnosis. Given the prohibitive surgical risk and the deep integration of previous valve frames, the intervention was determined to be catheter-based despite the patient's high EuroSCORE II risk. A dual-access strategy was employed, utilizing both the left femoral and the left radial arteries. Dual access in complex sequential TAVI procedures offers critical redundancy; the femoral approach permitted the main deployment catheter and larger sheaths required for the procedure, while radial access served as a potential backup for immediate complications or controlled access to the aortic root if necessary. The procedural team utilized a Safari guidewire positioned in the left ventricle via the femoral access to navigate the sequential stent frames. This was a crucial technical maneuver, as the tandem valves required careful manipulation to traverse the overlapping cages without causing dissection or perforation, ensuring that the先端 of the guidewire managed the flow dynamics safely before the valve deployment balloon was engaged.

Preoperative preparation involved balloon pre-dilation, a technical maneuver used in complex frames to expand the calcified landing zone and clear the path for valve implantation. In this specific case, the balloon was sized 22/40 mm and deployed under rapid ventricular pacing (RVP) at 180 beats per minute. The rationale for RVP during balloon inflation is not standard in all TAVI cases but is occasionally employed to maximize coronary flow clearance. The pacing rate significantly reduced left ventricular end-diastolic volume during the procedure's critical windows of hemodynamic stress, thereby creating a window where the prosthetic valve leaflets were less likely to completely obstruct the coronary ostia during immediate expansion. Simultaneous aortography was conducted during this phase to visualize coronary ostial height relative to the expanded balloon. This allowed the team to confirm that coronary ostia were not compressed by the stent frames without causing aortic rupture or hemorrhage. This functional check served as a binary pass/fail safety checkpoint before the deployment of the definitive prosthesis.

The deployment of the Acurate neo2 (size S) followed strict alignment protocols designed to mitigate the geometric constraints of a triple-valve configuration. Pre-procedural MSCT data had dictated the strategy of commissural alignment relative to the existing Evolut valve leaflets. By aligning the new valve's commissures with the leaflets of the implanted Evolut frame, the team aimed to prevent a "prosthesis-patient mismatch" (PPM) and preserve the effective orifice area. The implantation depth was chosen via high-positioning, guided by CT coordination plans to protect the coronary ostia and facilitate future access. This strategy ensured that the new valve remained seated well above the landing zone of the stem cages to avoid interference from the previous Evolut frame. Post-dilation was performed using a 20/40 mm balloon under RVP to optimize the valve expansion and ensure the frames expanded sufficiently to allow leaflet motion. The final positioning was verified via aortography, confirming correct deployment and ensuring that the neo-scar had not caused obstruction to coronary vessel flow. This alignment attempt was essential for achieving acceptable haemodynamic performance in the constricted aortic annulus without mechanical injury to the coronary arteries.

Verification of procedural success focused on immediate assessment of both hemodynamics and coronary perfusion, which served as the primary endpoints for procedural safety. Immediate post-procedure transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) confirmed successful deployment with full leaflet mobility and no evidence of aortic regurgitation grade 0. However, on the timing of the same procedure (7 June 2024), the TTE showed a mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg and an AVA of 0.9 cm², indicative of severe PPM that required further optimization or was a result of the specific landing zone interaction. To address this, immediate coronary angiography was performed via the radial access pigtail catheter to ensure that the high-velocity Doppler stream did not compromise coronary perfusion. While the immediate gradient was high, the aortography confirmed that the valve was stable and not causing coronary obstruction. Final aortography concluded that the positioning remained uncompromised, with the lack of perivalvular leak and transvalvular regurgitation. The verification phase concluded that while the valve was technically functional, the combined valve frame burden demanded monitoring, which subsequently led to the observed improvement in hemodynamic parameters (gradient reduction to 26 mm Hg at one month) attributed to the partial expansion of the prior Evolut frame and optimized commissural alignment.

## Long-Term Outcomes and Implications for Lifetime Valve Management

The long-term feasibility of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure in this patient is best quantified through longitudinal follow-up data, standardized against the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) framework. At the one-year mark (June 2025), the patient demonstrated sustained technical and clinical success, aligning with the surrogate markers of safety and performance defined in VARC-3 criteria. Initial procedural metrics, such as the absence of new thromboembolic events and non-potential cardiovascular complications, indicate a low adverse event profile compared to patients requiring open surgical salvage. Hemodynamically, the trajectory was favorable, transitioning from a significantly elevated post-procedural gradient of 67 mm Hg immediately after implantation to a stable mean gradient of 26 mm Hg at one year. These values fall below the symptomatic threshold typically defining significant stenosis, confirming that the added structural load of the third bioprosthetic frame did not precipitate functional underperfusion. Similarly, the effective orifice area (AVA) plateaued at 1.1 cm², a size that ensures an adequate effective orifice area elevation index (EOA) without risking patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), which was a primary concern during the sequential interventions.

The durability of these outcomes is critically linked to hemodynamic stability observed over the 12-month period, reflecting the physiological resilience of the underlying left ventricle despite the cumulative mechanical burden of three valves. Electrocardiographical indicators and echocardiographic ejection fractions demonstrate this adaptation; while the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) recovered to 65% at the one-year follow-up, this represents a sustained improvement over the initial 42% recorded prior to the second valve. This persistent LVEF preservation suggests that the structural valve dysfunction resolved through mechanical re-expansion rather than requiring systemic inotropic support or hemodynamic re-construction. The clinical stability noted at 1-year follow-up, characterized by preserved exercise tolerance and absence of decompensation, provides evidence that the progression of structural deterioration was successfully arrested. This outcome is significant for understanding that while TAVI is established for initial degeneration, its application in multi-prosthesis scenarios can yield comparable long-term trajectory to secondary interventions, provided the targeting anatomy is precise.

A profound clinical lesson derived from this case is the careful balance between controlling infection risk and managing prosthetic valve failure in the setting of prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Historically, the presence of PVE has often warranted surgical aortic root replacement due to concerns regarding antibiotic resistance, vegetations, and the risk of graft seeding. However, this case illustrates that once active infection is resolved via antimicrobial therapy, structural valve deterioration can be managed transcatheterly, particularly in patients with prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II >25%). This setting challenges the dogma that TAVI must be abandoned in the face of prior PVE; instead, it highlights that in the absence of active vegetations or perivalvular abscesses confirmed by transesophageal echocardiography and CT, the priority shifts to risk stratification rather than exclusion. The decision to proceed with a third TAVI was driven by high surgical mortality risk and the specific anatomy of the triple-stented frame, rather than the infectious history itself, providing a pre-existing alternative pathway for complex structural failures where surgical risk remains prohibitive.

Effective lifetime management for patients undergoing serial transcatheter valve implantations, particularly consecutive TAV-in-TAV interventions, requires a shift from single-event planning to iterative cardiovascular surveillance protocols. Given the cumulative risk of coronary ostial obstruction or frame impingement associated with successive valve frames, patients should be monitored for structural valve deterioration using multislice CT to evaluate valve-to-coronary distance and minimal lumen diameter in the aortic annulus. Every reassessment should be coupled with an evaluation of the need for coronary reaccess, as the "surgical backup" plan—such as total aortic root replacement—is significantly more hazardous in the context of deep stent fixation and modified aortic physiology. Furthermore, the implications of this case extend to the necessity of early multidisciplinary heart team discussions, such as the one reviewed for this intervention, which is essential for establishing a sustainable "lifetime valve management" strategy. As imaging and procedural planning capabilities advance, the management pathway for complex structural valve disease will likely become more dependent on pre-operative 3D modeling to anticipate inter-frame interactions, ultimately guiding the development of standardized long-term follow-up guidelines for patients with multiple prosthetic devices.
