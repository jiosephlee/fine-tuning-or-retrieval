Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Intervention After Treating PVE: Timing and Risk Assessment for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

You know those cases that make you pause and rethink your own heart team algorithms? This one is that rare case that reads like a thriller but turns out to be a textbook for high-risk structural valve management. We are looking at a man in his late 70s with a ticking clock and a vessel filled with unintended complications. He’s got a Cs-associated paravalvular vegetation history, two previous catheter-based interventions, and eventually needs a third valve—all within a decade. The core clinical challenge here isn’t just managing the mechanical failure; it’s managing the infection timeline against the mechanical re-homing of a biologic frame three times.

The most surprising element of this case is the OK-for-Ground decision-making process regarding the infected history. The 2025 ESC guidelines on infective endocarditis generally scream "surgery or at least mechanical valve" for prosthetic valve endocarditis, especially with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But here, the surgeon decided on TAVI. Why? Because the patient had a 2025 EuroSCORE II of over 27 percent. In the context of a high-risk, prosthetic valve electronics surgery candidate, the risk of total ascending root replacement is prohibitive. Once the *Enterococcus faecalis* was cleared and cultures were negative for at least six weeks, the structural valve failure became a surgical re-engineering problem, not a typical infectious seeding problem.

You have to respect the imaging planning that went into this. This wasn't a blind valve drop in a triple stented anatomy. Preprocedural multislice CT was used to map the minimum lumen diameter of the existing Evolut frame against the potential new Acurate neo2. They specifically looked for the coronary ostial height and calculated the frame-to-coronary distance. The technical pearl here is the implantation depth: they aimed high up the Evolut leaflets to prevent further reduction of the effective orifice area. In the grand scheme of TAVI, valve-in-valve is standard. Valve-in-valve-in-valve after endocarditis is a procedural anomaly, and the plan was flexible enough to minimize the neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia.

**## Timeline and Intervention Timing**

When did they intervene after the *Enterococcus*? This is critical. They didn't operate while the patient was febrile or sicker. The patient received targeted antibiotic therapy for six weeks (IV ampicillin), followed by oral treatment recommendations from infectious disease specialists. Clinical assessment, TE, and CT scans confirmed resolution of infection without vegetations or paravalvular complications before the third valve was deployed. This distinction—treating the heart failure vs. treating the infection—is vital. In this case, infection control was prioritized first, then the prosthetic mechanical failure was addressed as if it were a degenerative case, provided the surgical risk was confirmed too high otherwise.

The procedure itself involved multiple access points: Left radial and left femoral arterial access. They used balloon pre-dilation and rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) to ensure no obstruction happened to a coronary artery during placement. It was done under institutional approval for off-label use of the Acurate neo2. The valve design supports commissural alignment, which is key when you have to balance the leaflets of three distinct devices. Even with the triple mesh burden, the goal was to maintain accessibility for future angiography if needed.

**## Long-Term Outcome and Surveillance**

The 1-year follow-up is the clincher. The patient is active, asymptomatic, and has a mean gradient of 26 mmHg. There was initial hemodynamic worsening (gradient 67 mmHg) at one month, but post-dilation and frame expansion brought it down. The effective orifice area tracked from 0.9 to 1.1 cm², which is significantly better than the 0.5 cm² seen in the third pre-flight. At one year, there was no residual regurgitation and no infective signs. This suggests that keeping the infection non-chronic and proceeding with catheter-based planning is a viable alternative to open surgical replacement in high-risk settings.

The main takeaway here isn't that we should definitely do the third valve for an infected patient. It's that when surgical risk is too high and infection is resolved, the structural collapse of bioprosthetic valves can still be redeemed by a mechanical strategy that accounts for the mechanical reality of the aorta's stented history. It challenges the assumption that PVE precludes re-intervention.

### Take-Home Messages

*   **Timing:** Proceed with late surgical TAV intervention after confirmed resolution of *Enterococcus* infection, not while the vegetation is active.
*   **Risk Stratification:** Evaluate surgical risk (e.g., EuroSCORE II) in combination with prior valve history to justify transcatheter reintervention.
*   **Imaging:** Pre-procedural MSCT is non-negotiable for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategies to assess coronary clearance and stenosis risk.
*   **Implantation Strategy:** High implantation and commissural alignment are essential to preserve coronary flow in multiple layered stents.
*   **Shared Decision-Making:** Multidisciplinary review by cardiology, surgery, and infectious diseases is required for these complex multi-valve scenarios.



# The Functional Anatomy of Triple Stenting: Coronary Access Preservation

## The Complexity of Triple-Valve Physiology

Let's talk about the "Holy Grail" of structural heart failure management: keeping the heart moving when the machinery breaks down. In a young patient with an aortic valve that went south, you would expect surgical replacement. In an elderly patient with advanced aortic stenosis, you take up a TAVI. But what happens when you replant the growth spigot, and then need to replant the very spigot again? You end up in a scenario that borders on science fiction: a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

This isn't just theoretical; in the April/June 2025 literature, we are seeing a man in his late 70s undergo this specific trifecta. He went in with a Lotus valve in 2014. By 2022, he needed a second entry point with an Evolut valve due to structural valve deterioration. By 2024, structural failure again after a bout of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) demanded a third prosthesis using an Acurate neo2 valve. The rarity isn't the triplicate algorithm itself, but the management of the anatomy once you get there. Three bioprosthetic valves stacked in the aortic root.

The real question isn't just "Can we put a valve there?"; the question is, "Will this design kill the guy's coronary arteries, or the future heart surgeons trying to dial back if things go sideways?" In this case, it is a perfect storm of comorbidities—multiple prior PCI, Copley Simpson syndrome (high surgical risk history), and active endocarditis—that forced the hand toward a catheter-based solution when a scalpel had meant a Caucasian (wait, no, human) heart transplant in reality.

This case carries significant weight for interventional cardiologists. The height of the previous valve frames determines where the next valve frames can land. If you let the third valve sit high, the effective orifice area might collapse. If you let it sit too deep, you risk pseudo-sclerosis and neoringing. Plus, you can't ignore the coronary ostia. Three overlapping frames could mean one blockage epicentered in the left main or LAD.

## Strategy: High Implantation

The strategy here wasn't random magic; it was computed tomography (CT) magic. Before the knife was drawn (or the sheath was deployed), they ran a multislice CT (MSCT) specifically for a "functional anatomy" check. This recorded the coronary artery relations. Look at Figure 1 from the paper you'll read, and you can see the stent landscape. It was a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7mm in the Evolut prosthesis.

The decision to implant the Acurate neo2 was not just about hemodynamic flow but about the future. They opted for a high implantation—placing the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets. Normally, you implant down. Here, you went up. Why? Because this action prevents the formation of a "neo-skirt" below the coronary ostia. A neo-skirt is a ring of dehiscenced prosthesis that obstructs the coronary outflow. By landing higher, they fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame while reducing the risk of the frame expanding too far and compressing the coronaries.

This detail matters immensely for the "lifelong contract" of someone requiring TAVI. When you treat a vessel, you treat the system. If you lock up the coronary ostia now, you can't deal with it later. The benefit is a stable EF of 65% and an AVA of 1.1 cm² at one year, without the residual gradients of 83 mmHg seen in the previous interventional attempt.

## Technical Execution and Coronary Safety

Let's look at the "hands-on" details. They used a Safari guidewire in the left ventricle via the femoral access. This isn't standard; this is a peri-procedural safety check. By pacing the ventricle, they held the heart stiff and deployed.
*   **Balloon Pre-dilation:** 22/40mm in a controlled manner.
*   **Proximity Check:** They verified coronary flow remained unobstructed under fluoroscopy.

This is where the decision-making process matters the most. In many serial TAVI cases, you might skip the CT planning if you wanted to get it done fast. Here, the risk was too high. The patient had a previous TAV-in-TAV with a history of PVE. Even after antibiotics for *Enterococcus faecalis*, the structural integrity was compromised. The mechanical obstruction followed the biological failure. The team didn't just treat the numbers; they treated the anatomy.

They aligned the commissures with the Evolut. This ensures that the leaflets don't bunch up as if they were in a closet. The post-dilation used a 20/40mm balloon to push the leaflets open without compromising the coronary ostia. The result of this high-level imaging and manual alignment is a clean aorta without the dreaded gradient.

## Future Outlook for Triple TAV

The outcome is traditionally "stable," but the risk profile for the *next* 10 to 20 years is unknown. If this wears out, is the surgical option an explant? No. The surgical field is clogged with multiple bioprosthetic frames. This patient has a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and prohibitive risks for open surgery. This patient is essentially "catheter dependant" if the third valve fails again.

The ""go to surgical" option is now extremely limited. You might invent balloon techniques like BASILICA, where you balloon the valve to force leaflet laceration and prevent iatrogenic coronary obstruction. But that requires the whole heart team to be in the room. In this triple-stent scenario, if you have to "fry the valve," you also risk coronary artery obstruction.

This case reminds us that the age range of the TAV patient is shifting from 80 to 70 to maybe 65. When you treat a 70-year-old who has multiple stents and a binary valve history, you are in the business of "lifetime maintenance."

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Patient Profile:** In high-risk TAV patients with prior valvular endocarditis, serial catheter reintervention must balance structural repair against surgical salvage.
*   **Imaging is Key:** Preprocedural MSCT is mandatory for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV to assess coronary ostia height and landing zone stability.
*   **High Implantation:** In this complex anatomy, a high implantation strategy was utilized to align leaflets with the Evolut frame, preventing valve-economy collapse and minimizing the neo-skirt effect.
*   **Coronary Surveillance:** Post-procedural coronary flow must be confirmed via rapid ventricular pacing and aortography; sequential valve frames can create a mesh burden that complicates future access.
*   **Long-Term Planning:** A triple-stent configuration limits surgical options, necessitating a shared decision-making process regarding the one-year durability that remains vital for the life of the valve.



## Read the Map: The Role of MSCT in Complex Valve Replacements

There is a line of logic in interventional cardiology where we often operate on the principle of "fix what's broken." When a valve fails, we replace it. But what happens when the double fits? Or rather, when you have to fit inside the double that happens to be inside a double.

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (TAVI) is standard care for most, but when a patient endures ruptures in a double, triple, or quadruple valve configuration, you move into uncharted territory. We present an extraordinary case report of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, a "third valve" scenario in a patient who underwent TAVI in 2014, then again in 2022, followed by this one in 2024.

The primary challenge here isn't just the overlapping stents; it's the prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient required six weeks of intravenous antibiotics prior to this third procedure, and the team confirmed no active infection via blood cultures and TTE. Yet, the anatomy created a risk of coronary ostial obstruction so severe that many might have dismissed the patient for repeat surgery due to prohibitive risk surgery.

This case demonstrates the specific utility of preprocedural multislice computed tomography (MSCT) planning in navigating triple-valve configurations. It serves as a potent reminder that when anatomy becomes complex, alone we are in the unknown, but with the map, we are in high risk—manageable.

## Anatomy in the Maze: The Problem of Serial Valve Interventions

The patient presented in 2014 with a Lotus valve. By 2022, the Lotus had failed, requiring an Evolut prosthesis via a TAV-in-TAV approach. This procedure was already precarious—high pressure ballooning often used to achieve valve expansion. But by 2024, the Evolut had also begun to structurally deteriorate, eventually worsening to severe restenosis amid a history of PVE.

The darkness in prognostic modeling comes from how the prior devices incorporate into the aortic wall. With two valves already present, a third valve implantation risks the "cage effect." What is worse, frame layering can encroach on thoracic valve-to-coronary distances, leading to coronary ostial obstruction. This case isn't just about replacing a valve; it's about leaving enough space for future patent coronary ostia.

### The "High Implantation" Technique

In this case, the procedural team utilized a specific strategy that is gaining traction: high implantation. The team aimed to implant the third valve—at the level of the Evolut leaflets—so that the leaflets of the Evolut were pushed against the frame of the new Acurate neo2.

This technique helps "lock the engine" so that the inner valve is not left in a porous opening that risks failing or narrowing the orifice area further by inward leaflet folding. While this sounds simple, in a triple-stented anatomical base, the difference between achieving an AVA of 1.0 and needing a third hospitalization can be the height of the native valve annulus versus the landing zone distance.

## MSCT: Your Everyday Compass

Where does the team typically get the data to make these decisions? They turn to MSCT. It wasn't just about seeing what you were seeing; it was seeing what you'd *think* you were seeing without the scan.

The preprocedural MSCT provided a 3D model of the valve interaction, allowing the team to visualize the minimum lumen diameter of 13.7mm in the Evolut prosthesis. This was critical in confirming feasibility before the skin was cut. The model allowed the team to verify the coronals artery ostial height and estimate the risk of coronary obstruction.

This case highlights the evidentiary value of innovative 3DMSCT model planning in validating the feasibility of the depth of the total implantation. Evaluating the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and coronary ostia allowed the team to choose a size (S) and implantation depth that would maintain coronary flow without the need for the corollary of coronary protection devices or high-cost angioplasty procedures that follow.

## Why Transcatheter in PVE?

One crucial decision in this case was whether to operate surgically or transcatheter. ESC guidelines often recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE with severe heart failure.

In 2014, the patient had a "decided" TAVI because surgery was too risky. Now, surgery was even #1000 times riskier. Open surgery on a patient who had three previous stents in 3000 cm in the same area would mean total root replacement. It is nearly impossible to remove three transcatheter valves without major aortic damage. So, instead of the typical surgical valve replacement, the decision was to laser the valve to the less invasive nature of a catheter with the higher risk of the surgery.

### Critical procedural markers that make this special
*   **Coronary Access:** Comprehensive MSCT analysis of coronary ostial height and distance to valve frames.
*   **Antimicrobial Clearance:** No long-term suppressive therapy and no active infection found—essential for structural valve reintervention.
*   **Device Selection:** Use of a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve for its low frame height and supra-annular leaflets.
*   **Valve-to-Coronary Distance:** Calculated via preprocedural CT and validated intraoperatively by rapid ventricular pacing and aortography.

## Summary

This case serves as a complex, high-stakes example of the "Triple-Implant" strategy. It highlights that with modern technology and careful planning, the third valve can last longer than just a single valve replacement.

The outcome at 1 year showed a mean gradient of 26 mmHg and an AVA of 1.1 cm². The patient remained physically active and asymptomatic. This is evidence that complex anatomical challenges, while difficult, are technically feasible.

## Key Takeaways

*   **MSCT is your compass in valve failure:** Preoperative imaging allows for precise assessment of valve-to-coronary distance and neo-skirt risk.
*   **High implantation is a survival strategy:** By aligning the new valve with the Evolut leaflets, you can preserve the structural integrity of the previous frame.
*   **Infection control is procedural:** Only after active infection is confirmed resolved should catheter valve reintervention be considered.
*   **The calendar matters:** For 2014, 2022, and 2024, the timeline in combined treatment requires clear communication to reduce the risk of future interventions.

The evidence here is limited because the field is young. But this case shows us that if we plan with the map in front of us, we can navigate the storm of structural valve disease without crashing the ship.



# Device Geometry in High-Impact Redo TAVI: Balloon vs. Self-Expanding

## The Stained Glass of the Aorta

This case struck me as a bit of a beauty conscious of the surgical community because it is so rare. Presenting with a third Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (TAVI) after a history of transfacendial endocarditis is extremely uncommon. In a typical patient, two overlapping valves usually have one tiny chance of survival if they are managed successfully. This man has 3 primary valves stacked in his aorta—Lotus in 2014, Evolut in 2022, and now he tries to implant an Acurate Neo2. Plus, in the meantime, he had serious infective endocarditis (Enterococcus faecalis). Normally, you would say, "Do surgery." But the surgical risk was too high.

So, what matters here is the decision-making process when you try to get a self-expanding valve in a triple stented anatomy. We know very well that in TAVI redo, sometimes you can't get the first valve to work anymore. In this case, the valve had to do the triple magic instead of just saving one life. It comes down to the geometry and the timing of implant.

## Timeline of a Unique Journey

The patient is a man his late 70s. He has a very stressful history, with cardiovascular disease, COPD, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions. He first had a Lotus valve in 2014. That Latent device did the job for a bit, but as he aged, he started having trouble with the 2021 evaluation. By 2022, his gradient was 83 mm Hg.

He got an Evolut in 2022. It worked well. But 1 month later, he was sent to the hospital with fever and heart failure symptoms. That is when the infection happened. The microphone grew on the valve ring and grew on the system, causing a valve restenosis. He was treated with antibiotics for endocarditis. It was resolved.

But 1 year later, the valve failed again. He had a gradient of 49 mmHg. He needed to redo. Since he has a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and some of the enlarged vessels, they knew they had to choose between re-surgery and redo TAVI. So they chose TAVI again, but this time, you have to plan like a surgeon. The team chose a high-implant to keep the valves from closing in a way.

## Why Select Self-Expanding in Triple TAVI?

The blog post describes "Device Geometry," and it is a feeling of double-taking a valve. In TAVI-in-TAV-in-TAV, the self-expanding valve offers a specific advantage in how it sits inside the frame. The initial choice was Evolut, which is a balloon-expandable valve, so even though they used a Lotus valve initially. The difference between the two is just the balloon geometry, and the Acurate Neo2 (Supra-annular) is a device-geometry approach.

The team used Acurate Neo2 size S. The reason was this: the Acurate Neo2 has a low frame height and it allows for the leaflets to be above the stent. This helps with the commissural alignment. The device was aligned with the Evolut valve leaflets. If you just put a self-expanding valve inside a balloon-expandable valve, you have to check how much the annulus is. The Acurate Neo2 has the low frame height, which allows the leaflets to fold in a way that helps with the rate of expansion. It allows the valve to be high implanted to prevent a neo-skirt from forming below the coronary ostia.

Using the ACurate Neo2 in a triple-TAV anatomy allowed them to reduce the risk of PPM (patient-prosthesis mismatch) because the plaque was near the apex of the aorta. They used 3D modeling on the CT scan to ensure the third valve would not block the coronary arteries. They ran a balloon predilation, and they used a Safari guidewire to check the LV. Post-dilation was done because they had to ensure that the frame was expanded correctly. They used the CT plan and the fluoroscopy markers to tell them when to deploy the third valve.

## Clinical Pearls

While the case report does not show the final outcome has to be perfect, it provides a comprehensive plan for TAVI-in-TAV-in-TAV. The main challenge is that the patient had active infection but it was treated before the procedure. The procedure was selected as a self-expanding valve instead of a balloon-expandable valve. This is because the frame height is lower. Also, the valve is able to work better with the frame of the first TAVI.

Here are the main clinical points from this case report.

- **Order 3 valves:** When you have 2 valves in your ring, the third valve is a high-risk exception.
- **Endocarditis Clearance:** Make sure the infection is not active. Check the blood cultures and the ECHO to ensure there are no vegetations.
- **Frame Geometry:** Use self-expanding valves that have low frame heights to reduce the PPM risk in TAV-in-TAV.
- **CT imaging:** The pre-procedure CT is the most important step. You need the 3D modeling to check the coronary arteries and the valves.
- **Post-dilation:** Balloon predilation and post-dilation is important to ensure the valve is not too high or too low in the aorta.

## Long-Term Outcome and Limitations

The follow-up was great. One year later, the valve is working, there is no regurgitation, and the EF is 65. The mean gradient dropped to 26 mm Hg. The patient is active. The only risk is that the TAVI-in-TAV-in-TAV is not fully solved. The valve is a self-expanding device. If this device fails again, the only options are surgical explantation, which is risky.

Another consideration is the future of the patient. They mentioned the possibility of the valve failing. They also said they are using a self-expanding valve for the 3rd time. This is an off-label use of the Acurate Neo2. They are using the 3D modeling to see if there is enough room.

The case report emphasizes the need for multidisciplinary team discussion. The team must include interventionalists, surgeons, imaging, and anesthesia. They also need to think long-term. This is a lifetime management of structural valve disease. The key take away is that the valve selection must be based on the geometry and the risk profile of the patient.

## Conclusion

This case is a classic lesson in complexity. A triple-stented aortic ring after endocarditis is a rarity. The choice of valve was driven by the geometry of the frame and the coronary flow. The patient was stable, so the procedure was worth the trouble. This is a very valuable example for the cardiology community.



## Reading the Hemodynamics: Interpreting Gradients Over the First Year

In interventional cardiology, we’re taught to treat gradient spikes as immediate failures. Standing in contrast to that dogma, consider this case where the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure actually performed *better* as the weeks passed. A rather puzzling hemodynamic trajectory that keeps the foundation of serial valve interventions sharp.

The intrigue here isn't just about the triple-transcatheter valve itself, but specifically how the hemodynamics evolved. Read the gradients. They don't settle immediately. They swing. And they likely reflect the complex interplay of structural adaptation and balloon pre-dilation in a high-risk patient with a rough history.

### The Hemodynamic Trajectory

Let's look closely at the hemodynamic data from this patient with a history of extensive prior TAVI and treated endocarditis. The procedural strategy was driven by the need to avoid the high surgical risk of a redo open valve replacement, but the numbers told a dynamic story:

*   **Pre-procedure (Jan 2024):** Severe stenosis with a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg and a reduced AVA of 0.5 cm².
*   **Immediate Post-procedure (June 2024):** Surprisingly, the gradient jumped to 67 mm Hg. The AVA was 0.9 cm², initially read as a Prosthetic Valve Mismatch (PPM).
*   **1 Month Follow-up:** The gradient normalized significantly to 27 mm Hg, with the AVA expanding to 1.1 cm².
*   **1-Year Follow-up:** The patient remained asymptomatic with a mean gradient of just 26 mm Hg and preserved EF.

By the first month, valve leaflets had developed functional remodeling. The "post-dilation effect" and leaflet expansion didn't happen in a vacuum; rather, time allowed prior valves to mechanically adapt to the surrounding stent.

### Why the Initial "Rise" in Gradient?

This initial spike in mean gradient and AVA can be explained by the complexity of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV placement. In this scenario, the new self-expanding valve (Acurate neo2) had to engage in a high-pressure balloon post-dilation to ensure fixation within a double-layered stent structure.

Although commissural alignment was optimized, the immediate constraint on the aortic annulus was high due to the "frame layering" from 2014’s Lotus and 2022’s Evolut. This compression logic initially narrowed the effective orifice area. Over time, the third valve was not just an implant; it was a trigger for regenerative expansion of the prior Evolut frame. This mechanism allowed to increase the effective orifice area without resorting to frame fracture, provided the compression of the stent was sufficient.

Key technical procedural power was achieved by high implantation of the Acurate neo2. This kept the new leaflets from folding down inside the Evolut frame but instead allowed the Evolut frame to expand over time. It's a balance we must manage.

### Technical Pearls for Complex TAVI Surveillance

This case presents a crucial lesson for long-term surveillance of serial valve interventions. The immediate post-procedure gradient does not capture long-term function. It is why we must look at the temporal pattern, not just the pre-op cross-section.

*   **Dynamic Remodeling:** Expect to see gradients stabilize over the first 1–3 months in complex redo cases. An immediate gradient of 67 mmHg does not indicate procedural failure if it drops to 27 mmHg by month 1.
*   **Pre-procedural Imaging is Key:** Detailed MSCT analysis was used to evaluate coronary ostial height, valve-to-coronary distance, and interaction between the frames. Without this, the immediate 67 mmHg gradient might have been mistaken for catastrophic coronary obstruction.
*   **Infection Status Matters:** Management strategy shifted from surgical valve replacement (high risk) to catheter-based intervention (low risk TAVI) once antimicrobial therapy for *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis was completed and infection status resolved.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Serial TAVI Hemodynamics:** In complex triple-intervention cases, hemodynamic parameters (gradients and AVA) often show dynamic improvement over weeks rather than immediately post-device placement.
*   **The "Reactor" Spike:** If you see a high gradient immediately post-TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, do not panic. Understand it as compression of overlapping frames that may expand over time.
*   **Survival > Security:** The long-term durability of the Acurate neo2 but also the Evolut frame showed excellent stability at 1-year, but patients must be monitored for future frame mechanics, such as potential leaflet flaps or frames that limit future coronary access.

The bottom line? TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is a high-bar, high-risk proposition. It can work, but it requires patience in interpretation of the numbers.

You might think, "Just do TAV and done." But with three stents stacked up in the ascending aorta frame, the anatomy is a maze. The surveillance needs to be aggressive but not just TTE every three months. You need to visualize the interaction of the frames without opening the cage.

## Key Points to Remember

*   **Long-term surveillance is critical in triple-valve anatomy** to detect calcification or stent corrosion that could jeopardize coronary access.
*   **Pre-procedural MSCT is non-negotiable** prior to TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV to evaluate valve-to-coronary distances and planned high-implantation strategies.

*   **1-year follow-up was asymptomatic** in this case, suggesting the durability of the super strategy can be preserved if managed carefully.

## Managing the Future of Valve Management

This case highlights that the valve landscape is different. Every new valve adds a new dimension. The team's decision to use a self-expanding Acurate neo2 in this specific context was to minimize the drastic drop in leaflet function. They aligned the 3D frame so it didn't fold over the 2D Evolut leaflets.

Surveillance now must expand to include *three-dimensional* functional assessment. A TTE showing low gradients might be misleading if there is a neo-skirt that affects coronary flow.
