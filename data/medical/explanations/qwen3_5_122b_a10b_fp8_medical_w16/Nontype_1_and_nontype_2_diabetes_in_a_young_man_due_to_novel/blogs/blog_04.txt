Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Autonomic Neuropathy and Renal Thresholds: Hidden Clues in HNF1A-MODY

We often treat "young-onset diabetes" as a binary choice: Type 1 (autoimmune, insulin-dependent) or Type 2 (metabolic, resistant). But lurking in the shadows of this diagnostic dichotomy is a monogenic mimic that changes everything once identified. Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), specifically *HNF1A*-MODY, accounts for up to 65% of all MODY cases. Yet, it remains chronically underdiagnosed because clinicians rarely look past the blood sugar numbers.

A recent case report from Liu et al. drops a fascinating bombshell that challenges how we screen these patients: **severe autonomic dysfunction** presenting alongside classic glycosuria.

This isn't just another story about a genetic variant; it's a reminder that *HNF1A* is a transcription factor with systemic reach, affecting not just pancreatic beta cells, but also the kidney and the nervous system. Let's dive into why spotting these extra-pancreatic clues could save your patient from unnecessary insulin pens and help them avoid the pitfalls of misdiagnosis.

### The Case That Broke the Mold

Meet a young man who walked into his annual checkup with hyperglycemia and glycosuria. His labs were alarming: an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, and post-prandial spikes hitting nearly 20 mmol/L. He was symptomatic—polydipsic, polyuric, losing weight—but notably **not** in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

On first glance? Classic Type 1 or advanced Type 2. He had a family history stretching back three generations (grandmother, mother, brother), all diagnosed before age 30. Autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) were negative. He wasn't obese (BMI 24.6 kg/m²), though he did have dyslipidemia suggestive of metabolic syndrome—a red flag that usually pushes us toward T2D thinking.

But then came the curveball during workup: **Sudomotor testing**. Using electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan), they found severely reduced function in his hands. This indicates significant small-fiber autonomic neuropathy—a finding highly unusual for someone so young without decades of uncontrolled hyperglycemia or established DKA complications.

Simultaneously, they noted profound glycosuria out of proportion to his glucose levels initially thought to be standard osmotic diuresis. Genetic sequencing confirmed the culprit: a novel heterozygous mutation in *HNF1A* (p.N270S).

### Why Glycosuria Happens Without SGLT2 Inhibitors

If you see glycosuria in a normoglycemic or mildly hyperglycemic patient, you think SGLT2 inhibitors or Fanconi syndrome. But here's the physiology lesson: *HNF1A* is a master regulator expressed in the proximal renal tubule where it drives the expression of **SGLT2**.

When *HNF1A* is mutated, SGLT2 expression plummets. The renal threshold for glucose reabsorption drops precipitously (often to <8–9 mmol/L). This means even moderate elevations in blood sugar result in massive urinary glucose losses long before you'd expect it in T1D or T2D. This "low threshold" phenomenon is a hallmark clinical pearl for *HNF1A*-MODY that distinguishes it from other forms of diabetes where glycosuria tracks linearly with hyperglycemia severity until very high thresholds are reached.

### The Autonomic Connection You Missed

The presence of severe sudomotor dysfunction in this young patient is perhaps the most striking teaching point of this case report. While autonomic neuropathy is common in long-standing T1D/T2D due to chronic glucotoxicity and oxidative stress, seeing it at diagnosis—or shortly thereafter—in a young person should scream "monogenic."

*HNF1A* plays a critical role in neuronal development and function beyond the pancreas and kidney. While not every *HNF1A*-MODY patient presents with overt neuropathy immediately upon diagnosis, its presence serves as a potent phenotypic marker that something is wrong with the developmental programming of these tissues, rather than just acquired metabolic damage. Ignoring this sign led to months of insulin therapy before genetic confirmation allowed for de-escalation to oral agents/GLP-1s in this case.

### From Insulin Dependent to Oral Maintenance: The Power of Precision Medicine

Before genetics returned positive results, this patient was started on basal insulin degludec plus metformin and saxagliptin—the standard "shotgun" approach for ambiguous young-onset diabetes. Once *HNF1A*-MODY was confirmed via Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS), the treatment paradigm shifted entirely within one month. Insulin was stopped immediately due to preserved but insufficient beta-cell function (C-peptide >0.6 nmol/L after stimulation) and replaced with Dulaglutide (a GLP-1 RA). Six months later? HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0%. Eventually stabilized on Metformin + Saxagliptin alone.

Why does this matter? Because treating *HNF1A*-MODY with sulfonylureas or GLP-1 RAs targets the underlying pathophysiology (impaired insulin secretion sensitivity) far better than exogenous insulin ever could for these patients—and avoids hypoglycemia risks associated with sulfonylureas if dosed correctly or if beta-cell reserve wanes further over time (though GLP-1s remain excellent alternatives). Conversely, putting these patients on SGLT2 inhibitors can be dangerous; since their baseline renal SGLT2 activity is already genetically suppressed by their mutation, adding an inhibitor can precipitate euglycemic DKA and severe volume depletion due to excessive osmotic diuresis at low glucose levels [Liu et al., Discussion].

### Take-Home Points for the Busy Clinician

Don't wait for a genetic panel to order when you see this constellation of findings in anyone under 35:
*   **Think Beyond T1/T2:** If you see young-onset diabetes (<35y) with negative autoantibodies AND no significant obesity/metabolic syndrome features, calculate your pre-test probability for MODY immediately using tools like the Exeter Calculator—even if imperfect for non-European populations, it flags high-risk candidates worth testing anyway.**
*   **Glycosuria = Low Renal Threshold:** Unexplained glycosuria relative to blood glucose levels suggests defective SGLT2 expression driven by *HNF1A*, not necessarily poor control requiring immediate intensification.**
*   **Autonomic Signs Are Early Warnings:** Severe sudomotor dysfunction or other small fiber neuropathies appearing early in disease course are rare in typical TDM but documented phenotypes in *HNF1A*-MODY; don't dismiss them as "stress" or incidental.**
*   **Avoid SGLT2 Inhibitors Initially:** Be extremely cautious prescribing SGLT2 inhibitors until genotyping rules out *HNF1A*-MODY; combining drug-induced blockade with genetic suppression creates a perfect storm for euglycemic DKA.**
*   **Genetics Change Management:** A positive result doesn't just satisfy curiosity—it allows safe discontinuation of insulin initiation/continuation and guides selection toward sulfonylureas or incretin-based therapies tailored to residual beta-cell function.**
