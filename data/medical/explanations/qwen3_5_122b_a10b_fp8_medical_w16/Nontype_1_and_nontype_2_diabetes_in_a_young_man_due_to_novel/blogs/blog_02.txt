Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The Glycosuria Trap: Why Renal Thresholds Matter in HNF1A-MODY Diagnosis

We often teach residents that glycosuria is simply a marker of "spillover"—when blood sugar climbs above the renal threshold (usually around 180 mg/dL or 10 mmol/L), the kidneys can't hold it back anymore. But what if your patient's kidneys are spilling glucose at a much lower threshold? What if that specific finding isn't just a sign of uncontrolled diabetes, but the very fingerprint of a specific monogenic disorder?

That's exactly the scenario we saw in a recent case report from Liu et al., describing a young man with a novel *HNF1A* mutation who fell through the cracks of standard Type 1 and Type 2 diagnostics. His story isn't just about finding a rare gene; it's about understanding why **disproportionate glycosuria** should ring alarm bells in your mind long before you order genetic sequencing. And perhaps more importantly, it highlights a terrifying contraindication regarding one of our favorite new drug classes: SGLT2 inhibitors.

## The Patient Who Didn't Fit the Mold

Let's meet our patient: a young adult presenting with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia—polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss—but with a twist. He wasn't obese (BMI 24.6 kg/m²), didn't have acanthosis nigricans, and critically, his autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2) were negative. Yet, he had significant hyperglycemia with an HbA1c of 10.2% and glycosuria.

Initially, he was treated empirically for Type 2 Diabetes with insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin. While his sugars improved slightly, he still had elevated HbA1c and concerning signs of autonomic neuropathy (severely reduced sudomotor function). The clinician team correctly pivoted when they noticed his strong family history: his mother was diagnosed in her late 20s, his grandmother in her 30s, and his brother at age 20. This three-generation pattern screams autosomal dominant inheritance.

Genetic testing revealed the culprit: a novel heterozygous variant in *HNF1A* (p.N270S). This confirmed **HNF1A-MODY** (formerly known as MODY3). But here is where the physiology gets fascinating—and clinically dangerous if misunderstood.

## The Pathophysiology: Why They Leak Sugar Early

To understand this case, you have to look at what *HNF1A* actually does. It's not just a pancreatic beta-cell transcription factor; it regulates gene expression in the liver and kidneys too. Specifically, *HNF1A* drives the expression of **SGLT2** (sodium-glucose cotransporter-2) in the proximal renal tubule.

In healthy individuals, SGLT2 reabsorbs almost all filtered glucose up until plasma concentrations hit ~180 mg/dL. In *HNF1A*-MODY patients, the mutation leads to reduced SGLT2 expression. Consequently, their renal threshold for glucose drops dramatically—often well below 150 mg/dL (sometimes even lower).

This means our patient could have "normal" or only mildly elevated blood sugars by Type 2 standards and still present with frank glycosuria on dipstick testing. If you see a young person with glycosuria but blood glucose that doesn't seem high enough to justify it based on standard thresholds, think **MODY**. Don't dismiss it as "early T2D." That glycosuria is happening because their kidney plumbing is genetically altered to leak sugar earlier than yours or mine would.

## The Therapeutic Pivot: From Insulin to GLP-1 RAs

Once the diagnosis was secured via Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS), everything changed for this patient. He wasn't insulin-deficient in the absolute sense seen in Type 1 DM; rather, he had progressive beta-cell dysfunction typical of MODY3. His stimulated C-peptide was low-normal (0.83 nmol/L), indicating he still had some capacity to secrete insulin if stimulated correctly.

He was successfully weaned off basal insulin degludec entirely—a major win for quality of life—and switched to **dulaglutide** (a GLP-1 receptor agonist) combined with metformin. Within six months, his HbA1c plummeted from 10.2% to 6.0%. Later, he was stepped down further to metformin plus saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor), maintaining excellent control without injections or hypoglycemia fears common with sulfonylureas in older protocols (though sulfonylureas remain first-line for many HNF1A-MODY cases).

This case underscores that precision medicine isn't just academic buzzwording; it changes regimens from lifelong insulin dependence to oral agents or injectables that preserve beta-cell function without hypoglycemia risk profiles associated with traditional therapies in this specific genotype group.

## ⚠️ The Danger Zone: Avoiding SGLT2 Inhibitors Here

Now for the most critical takeaway for emergency physicians and hospitalists reading this: **Do not prescribe SGLT2 inhibitors to patients with suspected or confirmed HNF1A-MODY.**

Why? Because these drugs work by inhibiting SGLT2 transporters to force glucose excretion into urine. Patients with *HNF1A*-MODY already have genetically suppressed SGLT2 function and an abnormally low renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to their mutation-induced downregulation of these transporters.

If you give them an SGLT2 inhibitor:
*   You are essentially hitting an already broken brake system harder than intended by nature itself.
*   This creates an extreme risk of profound osmotic diuresis leading to **severe dehydration** and hemodynamic instability (vascular collapse).
*   Most dangerously, because they are losing massive amounts of glucose regardless of serum levels, they are at high risk for **euglycemic Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)**. Their bodies switch rapidly to ketone production due to perceived starvation signals from urinary glucose loss, even while blood sugar remains deceptively normal or only mildly elevated upon admission labs check-out thinking they are stable because they aren't "hyperglycemic" enough by T2D standards? No! They are primed for catastrophic volume depletion and acidosis at blood sugars that wouldn't trigger DKA mechanisms in other diabetics until much higher levels were reached?
