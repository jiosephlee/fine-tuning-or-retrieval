# Beyond Type 1 and 2: Decoding the 'Young Diabetic' with a Three-Generation History

We love a good diagnostic puzzle in emergency medicine, but few scenarios are as frustratingly common—and potentially transformative—as the "young diabetic." You see them constantly: a patient under 25 presenting with significant hyperglycemia. Your brain immediately jumps to the binary: Is it autoimmune Type 1? Or is it early-onset Type 2 driven by the rising tide of pediatric obesity?

But what if it's neither? What if you're looking at a monogenic form of diabetes that requires a completely different treatment algorithm, one that doesn't involve lifelong insulin injections or the heavy metabolic burden of standard T2D regimens?

A recent case report out of Taiwan forces us to confront exactly this scenario. A young man presented with an HbA1c of 10.2% and classic symptoms of polydipsia and weight loss. Initially, he was treated as having Type 2 diabetes with basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. But once the genetic plot thickened, his story shifted from "standard care" to precision medicine success, dropping his HbA1c to a stunning 6.0% within months—all without insulin. The culprit? A novel mutation in *HNF1A*, causing Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) type 3.

This isn't just about finding a rare gene; it's about recognizing when your standard differential is failing you and knowing how to pivot before you lock a patient into a suboptimal treatment path for life.

### The Clinical Clues That Don't Fit the Mold

Let's break down why this patient raised red flags for something other than vanilla T1D or T2D. He was young, yes, but he lacked acanthosis nigricans—a hallmark of insulin resistance in T2D—and didn't fit the typical obese phenotype often associated with early-onset metabolic syndrome, though he did have dyslipidemia which complicates things slightly.

The real smoking gun was the family history. This wasn't just "grandma had diabetes." This was a clear autosomal dominant pattern stretching back three generations: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and now him and his younger brother in their early 20s. When you see diabetes striking multiple generations at such young ages without the usual risk factors for Type 2 (like severe obesity), your radar should instantly ping toward monogenic causes.

Furthermore, the lab work told a nuanced story that defied simple categorization. His autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2) were negative, ruling out classic autoimmune destruction seen in T1D. Yet, his beta-cell function wasn't dead either; his glucagon stimulation test showed preserved but insufficient C-peptide secretion (0.83 nmol/L). In true T1D at this stage of symptomatic presentation with an HbA1c over 10%, we usually expect much lower secretory reserve unless they are caught very early in the honeymoon phase—which his severe symptoms argued against. Conversely, T2D patients usually have high C-peptide levels reflecting compensatory hyperinsulinemia until late-stage burnout occurs decades later. This "Goldilocks" zone of beta-cell dysfunction is classic for MODY.

Another subtle but vital clue mentioned in the report was glycosuria appearing alongside hyperglycemia without DKA. While glycosuria happens in uncontrolled T1/T2 due to exceeding the renal threshold, *HNF1A*-MODY specifically lowers that renal threshold due to defective SGLT2 expression in the kidney tubules. It's a unique physiological quirk that can sometimes hint at the diagnosis even before genetics come back positive.

### The Trap of Probability Calculators and BMI

It is tempting to reach for tools like the Exeter University MODY probability calculator to guide our decision-making on who gets genetic testing. In this case, plugging in the numbers gave a calculated probability of roughly 62%. That sounds convincing enough to warrant testing! But here is where we must exercise caution: these calculators were largely derived from White European cohorts. They may overestimate MODY likelihood in Asian populations or underestimate it if specific phenotypic nuances aren't captured perfectly by the algorithm's inputs relying heavily on BMI and strict family history reporting rules which may be incomplete due to undiagnosed relatives or new mutations.

Moreover, relying solely on BMI as a discriminator is becoming increasingly dangerous as global obesity rates climb up across all demographics. This patient had features suggestive of metabolic syndrome (high triglycerides), yet still carried *HNF1A*-MODY pathology. If we had dismissed him because he didn't look like "textbook thin MODY," we would have missed the diagnosis entirely and kept him on unnecessary insulin therapy longer than needed. Precision medicine demands we look past body habitus when the family history screams otherwise.

### From Insulin Dependence to GLP-1 Agonists: Why Diagnosis Matters So Much

Why does getting this right matter so much? Because *HNF1A*-MODY responds beautifully to low-dose sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, often rendering insulin unnecessary except during acute illness or pregnancy complications requiring rapid stabilization initially (which happened here before genetic results returned).

In this case report, once genetic testing confirmed the novel *p.N270S* variant in *HNF1A*, clinicians were able to de-escalate therapy rapidly. # The Glycosuria Trap: Why Renal Thresholds Matter in HNF1A-MODY Diagnosis

We often teach residents that glycosuria is simply a marker of "spillover"—when blood sugar climbs above the renal threshold (usually around 180 mg/dL or 10 mmol/L), the kidneys can't hold it back anymore. But what if your patient's kidneys are spilling glucose at a much lower threshold? What if that specific finding isn't just a sign of uncontrolled diabetes, but the very fingerprint of a specific monogenic disorder?

That's exactly the scenario we saw in a recent case report from Liu et al., describing a young man with a novel *HNF1A* mutation who fell through the cracks of standard Type 1 and Type 2 diagnostics. His story isn't just about finding a rare gene; it's about understanding why **disproportionate glycosuria** should ring alarm bells in your mind long before you order genetic sequencing. And perhaps more importantly, it highlights a terrifying contraindication regarding one of our favorite new drug classes: SGLT2 inhibitors.

## The Patient Who Didn't Fit the Mold

Let's meet our patient: a young adult presenting with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia—polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss—but with a twist. He wasn't obese (BMI 24.6 kg/m²), didn't have acanthosis nigricans, and critically, his autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2) were negative. Yet, he had significant hyperglycemia with an HbA1c of 10.2% and glycosuria.

Initially, he was treated empirically for Type 2 Diabetes with insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin. While his sugars improved slightly, he still had elevated HbA1c and concerning signs of autonomic neuropathy (severely reduced sudomotor function). The clinician team correctly pivoted when they noticed his strong family history: his mother was diagnosed in her late 20s, his grandmother in her 30s, and his brother at age 20. This three-generation pattern screams autosomal dominant inheritance.

Genetic testing revealed the culprit: a novel heterozygous variant in *HNF1A* (p.N270S). This confirmed **HNF1A-MODY** (formerly known as MODY3). But here is where the physiology gets fascinating—and clinically dangerous if misunderstood.

## The Pathophysiology: Why They Leak Sugar Early

To understand this case, you have to look at what *HNF1A* actually does. It's not just a pancreatic beta-cell transcription factor; it regulates gene expression in the liver and kidneys too. Specifically, *HNF1A* drives the expression of **SGLT2** (sodium-glucose cotransporter-2) in the proximal renal tubule.

In healthy individuals, SGLT2 reabsorbs almost all filtered glucose up until plasma concentrations hit ~180 mg/dL. In *HNF1A*-MODY patients, the mutation leads to reduced SGLT2 expression. Consequently, their renal threshold for glucose drops dramatically—often well below 150 mg/dL (sometimes even lower).

This means our patient could have "normal" or only mildly elevated blood sugars by Type 2 standards and still present with frank glycosuria on dipstick testing. If you see a young person with glycosuria but blood glucose that doesn't seem high enough to justify it based on standard thresholds, think **MODY**. Don't dismiss it as "early T2D." That glycosuria is happening because their kidney plumbing is genetically altered to leak sugar earlier than yours or mine would.

## The Therapeutic Pivot: From Insulin to GLP-1 RAs

Once the diagnosis was secured via Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS), everything changed for this patient. He wasn't insulin-deficient in the absolute sense seen in Type 1 DM; rather, he had progressive beta-cell dysfunction typical of MODY3. His stimulated C-peptide was low-normal (0.83 nmol/L), indicating he still had some capacity to secrete insulin if stimulated correctly.

He was successfully weaned off basal insulin degludec entirely—a major win for quality of life—and switched to **dulaglutide** (a GLP-1 receptor agonist) combined with metformin. Within six months, his HbA1c plummeted from 10.2% to 6.0%. Later, he was stepped down further to metformin plus saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor), maintaining excellent control without injections or hypoglycemia fears common with sulfonylureas in older protocols (though sulfonylureas remain first-line for many HNF1A-MODY cases).

This case underscores that precision medicine isn't just academic buzzwording; it changes regimens from lifelong insulin dependence to oral agents or injectables that preserve beta-cell function without hypoglycemia risk profiles associated with traditional therapies in this specific genotype group.

## ⚠️ The Danger Zone: Avoiding SGLT2 Inhibitors Here

Now for the most critical takeaway for emergency physicians and hospitalists reading this: **Do not prescribe SGLT2 inhibitors to patients with suspected or confirmed HNF1A-MODY.**

Why? Because these drugs work by inhibiting SGLT2 transporters to force glucose excretion into urine. Patients with *HNF1A*-MODY already have genetically suppressed SGLT2 function and an abnormally low renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to their mutation-induced downregulation of these transporters.

If you give them an SGLT2 inhibitor:
*   You are essentially hitting an already broken brake system harder than intended by nature itself.
*   This creates an extreme risk of profound osmotic diuresis leading to **severe dehydration** and hemodynamic instability (vascular collapse).
*   Most dangerously, because they are losing massive amounts of glucose regardless of serum levels, they are at high risk for **euglycemic Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)**. Their bodies switch rapidly to ketone production due to perceived starvation signals from urinary glucose loss, even while blood sugar remains deceptively normal or only mildly elevated upon admission labs check-out thinking they are stable because they aren't "hyperglycemic" enough by T2D standards? No! They are primed for catastrophic volume depletion and acidosis at blood sugars that wouldn't trigger DKA mechanisms in other diabetics until much higher levels were reached? # From Insulin to GLP-1: Precision Medicine Strategies for HNF1A-MODY Management

We love a good "zebra" hunt in emergency medicine, but sometimes the real win isn't finding a rare disease—it's realizing your patient *doesn't* have the common one you've been treating them for. Consider this scenario: a young man walks into your clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2%, significant weight loss, and glycosuria. Your brain immediately jumps to Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) due to the catabolic state or perhaps aggressive Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) given the rising tide of metabolic syndrome in younger demographics. You start him on basal insulin, metformin, and maybe a DPP-4 inhibitor. He improves slightly, but he's still tethered to needles, and something feels off about the trajectory.

This exact clinical conundrum played out in a recent case report from Liu et al., where a young man with a strong family history of early-onset diabetes was initially mismanaged as T2D before genetic testing revealed the true culprit: **HNF1A-MODY** (Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young). What makes this case particularly juicy for us clinicians isn't just the identification of a novel *HNF1A* mutation; it's the dramatic therapeutic pivot that followed. Once genetics confirmed the diagnosis, we saw insulin successfully discontinued and replaced with a GLP-1 receptor agonist, leading to an HbA1c drop from 10.2% down to 6.0% in just three months. This case forces us to rethink our algorithmic approach to monogenic diabetes and challenges the dogma that sulfonylureas are always the undisputed king of HNF1A-MODY treatment.

### The Diagnostic Dilemma: Why We Miss MODY

MODY accounts for roughly 1% to 6% of young-onset diabetes cases, yet it remains notoriously underdiagnosed. The classic triad usually triggers suspicion: onset before age 25, an autosomal dominant family history across at least three generations, and negative pancreatic autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A, ZnT8). In our patient's case, the pedigree was undeniable—grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, brother at age 20. Yet, he presented with symptoms mimicking T1D (polydipsia, weight loss) alongside markers often seen in T2D (elevated triglycerides).

The clincher here was the negative autoantibody panel combined with preserved—but insufficient—endogenous insulin secretion on glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L). While many providers stop at "negative antibodies = not T1D," they often miss the middle ground where MODY lives. Furthermore, traditional tools like the Exeter MODY probability calculator can be tricky; while this patient scored a high probability (62%), these calculators were largely derived from White European cohorts and may underestimate risk or misclassify presentations in diverse populations or those with co-existing metabolic features like obesity or dyslipidemia. Without genetic confirmation via Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS), this patient would likely have remained on lifelong insulin therapy unnecessarily.

### The Novel Mutation and Pathophysiology

Genetic testing revealed a heterozygous variant in *HNF1A*: p.N270S (c.809A>G). This wasn't just another textbook mutation; it was novel—previously unreported in ClinVar or major databases. Using ACMG guidelines, researchers classified it as Likely Pathogenic based on its location in the DNA-binding domain (critical for transcription factor function), segregation with disease in the family, and high predictive scores from AlphaMissense (*Alpha* models are getting scary good at this stuff).

Understanding *why* this matters is key to understanding why standard T2D drugs might fail or why specific therapies work so well here. *HNF1A* is a transcription factor regulating genes involved in hepatic glucose production and renal glucose handling. Mutations lead to defective GLUT2 expression in pancreatic beta cells and reduced SGLT2 expression in renal tubules. The latter explains the prominent glycosuria seen even at relatively modest blood glucose levels—a hallmark clue that often gets overlooked because we assume glycosuria implies massive hyperglycemia when actually it reflects a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to SGLT2 downregulation caused by the mutation itself.

### Beyond Sulfonylureas: The Rise of GLP-1 Agonists

Here is where things get clinically exciting—and where our case report pushes back against tradition. For decades, low-dose sulfonylureas have been taught as the gold-standard first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY because they bypass some of the defective signaling pathways by directly closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels to stimulate insulin release. And yes, they work beautifully for many patients with mild phenotypes or near-normal C-peptides early on.

However, our patient illustrates a nuance that is increasingly relevant as we manage older adolescents or adults with declining beta-cell reserve: **hypoglycemia risk**. Sulfonylureas carry an inherent risk of hypoglycemia that can be quite profound if dosing isn't meticulous once beta-cell function wanes further down the line. In contrast, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a "glucose-dependent" mechanism—they stimulate insulin secretion only when glucose levels are elevated and suppress glucagon release without triggering dangerous lows when euglycemic is reached naturally by diet/exercise changes alone? ## Autonomic Dysfunction in Monogenic Diabetes: Unmasking Silent Neuropathy Early

We often get caught up in the binary debate of Type 1 versus Type 2 when we see a young patient with hyperglycemia. But what happens when the story doesn't fit either box? What if your "Type 2" patient is lean, has a three-generation pedigree of early-onset diabetes, and lacks the classic markers of insulin resistance? Or worse, what if your "Type 1" patient isn't ketotic and has preserved (though declining) beta-cell function?

This is the classic trap of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). While much of the literature focuses on the genetic nuances or the switch from insulin to sulfonylureas, there's a subtle, often overlooked clinical feature that can actually help us spot these cases *before* genetic confirmation lands on our desks. We're talking about **autonomic dysfunction**.

In a fascinating recent case report by Liu et al., a young man presented with significant hyperglycemia and glycosuria. His HbA1c was a staggering 10.2%, yet he wasn't in ketoacidosis. He had no acanthosis nigricans, and his family history screamed monogenic inheritance (grandmother, mother, and brother all diagnosed in their 20s or 30s). But here is the kicker: while his retinal exam and urine albumin were pristine—no classic microvascular complications—he had **severely reduced sudomotor function** in his hands.

Using electrochemical skin conductance testing (Sudoscan), they found profound autonomic neuropathy in a patient who had technically just been diagnosed with diabetes months prior. No retinopathy. No nephropathy. Just silent, early autonomic failure. This case forces us to reconsider how we screen young diabetics and why genetic testing isn't just about picking pills; it's about predicting complications before they become irreversible.

### The MODY Mimicry Problem

Let's be honest: diagnosing MODY is hard because it mimics everything else. HNF1A-MODY accounts for roughly one-third to two-thirds of all MODY cases. These patients are usually non-obese (BMI ~24-25 kg/m²), have a strong family history across generations, and present with post-prandial spikes that can look terrifyingly like T1D or poorly controlled T2D.

In this specific case, the patient initially looked like someone needing aggressive insulin therapy due to an HbA1c >10%. They were started on basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor—a standard "shotgun" approach for ambiguous young-onset diabetes. It worked enough to drop the HbA1c to 9.1%, but once genetic testing returned positive for a novel *HNF1A* mutation (p.N270S), everything changed. The team wisely stopped the insulin and transitioned him to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide), eventually simplifying to metformin and saxagliptin alone with excellent control (HbA1c 6%).

But while the medication switch got all the headlines regarding precision medicine, the autonomic findings are what make this case unique for bedside teaching. Autonomic neuropathy is typically considered a complication of long-standing diabetes—something you expect after decades of poor control or advanced T2D/T1D pathology. Finding it this early in an untreated/moderately treated monogenic case suggests that *HNF1A* dysfunction might hit small fibers harder or earlier than we thought.

### Why Sudomotor Testing Matters Now

The use of Sudoscan (electrochemical skin conductance) provided an objective measure of small fiber integrity that traditional exams missed entirely. The patient had no symptoms suggestive of gastroparesis or orthostatic hypotension at presentation, yet his hands showed severely reduced conductance.

Why does this matter? Because autonomic dysfunction impacts more than just sweating; it influences gastric emptying, heart rate variability, and even glucose absorption dynamics from meals (since gut motility changes affect post-prandial peaks). If we ignore this early sign, we miss a crucial piece of the prognostic puzzle. Furthermore, recognizing autonomic involvement reinforces the need for strict glycemic control immediately upon diagnosis, even if traditional markers like albuminuria haven't appeared yet.

It also serves as a red flag against certain drug classes. For instance, SGLT2 inhibitors are generally contraindicated or used with extreme caution in *HNF1A*-MODY because these patients already have reduced renal glucose reabsorption thresholds (leading to glycosuria) and altered fluid handling mechanisms driven by defective SGLT2 expression under *HNF1A* transcriptional control. Adding an SGLT2i could precipitate euglycemic DKA or severe dehydration—a risk amplified if autonomic regulation of volume status is already compromised.

### The Take-Home Message

This case reminds us that "young-onset diabetes" requires more than just checking antibodies and ordering an A1C sheet next year if it looks weird today. We need to think about genetics earlier than ever before, especially when there is multi-generational involvement without typical autoimmune markers or severe obesity. And perhaps most importantly, we shouldn't wait for retinopathy to tell us there's nerve damage happening underneath the hood.

Here are your clinical pearls from this case:

*   **Suspect MONOGENIC DIABETES EARLY:** In any patient <35 years old with non-ketotic hyperglycemia and a family history spanning at least two generations (especially maternal lineage), consider MODY regardless of BMI or metabolic syndrome features which may be confounding factors now due to rising obesity rates globally.
*   **AUTONOMIC SCREENING IS CRITICAL:** Do not assume neuropathy takes decades to develop in monogenic forms like *HNF1A*-MODY. Incorporate non-invasive sudomotor testing (like Sudoscan) into your initial workup for young diabetics with atypical presentations; silent neuropathy may be present before microvascular complications appear elsewhere.
*   **PRECISION DRUG SELECTION SAVES LIVES:** Confirming *HNF1A*-MODY allows you to de-escalate from insulin rapidly if appropriate (often switching first-line therapy toward sulfonylureas or GLP-1 RAs depending on severity) AND avoid dangerous agents like SGLT2 inhibitors which pose high risks of euglycemic DKA and dehydration in this specific genotype due to inherent SGLT2 downregulation defects at baseline levels too!
*   **NEGATIVE AUTOANTIBODIES ARE NOT ENOUGH:** Just because GAD65/IA-2/ZnT8 are negative doesn't guarantee T2D; combine negative serology with preserved C-peptide (>0.6 nmol/L stimulated) plus family history as triggers for immediate NGS panels rather than waiting years for "classic" progression patterns that never happen anyway!



# Novel Variants & ACMG Guidelines: Interpreting New Genetic Findings in Clinical Practice

We often fall into the trap of thinking that if a genetic variant isn't in ClinVar, it's benign—or worse, irrelevant. But in the era of Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS), "not reported" simply means "new," not necessarily harmless. Today, we're diving into a fascinating case from *Liu et al.* that challenges our reliance on database lookups and forces us to get back to first principles: applying the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines to classify novel variants when the answer key doesn't exist yet.

The story centers on a young man presenting with classic hyperglycemia and glycosuria but lacking the autoimmune markers of Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) or the phenotypic hallmarks of Type 2 Diabetes (T2D). He wasn't obese, didn't have acanthosis nigricans, yet his HbA1c was soaring at 10.2%. His family history screamed "monogenic": his mother, grandmother, and brother all had early-onset diabetes. Despite negative autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) and a preserved-but-insufficient C-peptide response on glucagon stimulation, he was initially treated as if he had T2D with insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. It took genetic testing to flip the script entirely.

Here is where things get interesting for the clinician interpreting genetics. The lab identified a heterozygous substitution in *HNF1A*: **p.N270S** (c.809A>G). When you run this through ClinVar or VarSome? Nothing comes up. It's a ghost in the machine—a variant never before documented in literature or public databases. If you stopped there, you might dismiss it as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) and stick your patient on insulin forever. But this team didn't stop there; they applied rigorous ACMG criteria to prove this was **Likely Pathogenic**, unlocking a precision medicine switch that allowed them to wean him off insulin completely onto a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin, dropping his HbA1c to 6.0% in three months.

So, how do you justify calling a VUS "Likely Pathogenic" when there is zero historical precedent? The power lies in converging lines of evidence beyond simple database matching. First, consider **segregation**. This mutation tracks perfectly with disease status across three generations in the family; every affected relative carries it, implying a direct causal link. Second, look at the **bioinformatics prediction**. The variant sits right next to known pathogenic residues in the highly conserved DNA-binding domain of the HNF1A protein—the exact spot where transcription factors need to grab onto DNA to work. Tools like AlphaMissense gave this specific change a staggering pathogenicity score of 0.995 (where 1 is definitely pathogenic), signaling that evolutionary conservation predicts disruption here. Finally, we have the **phenotype match**: HNF1A-MODY is characterized by low renal thresholds for glucose (explaining his glycosuria despite moderate hyperglycemia) and progressive beta-cell failure without autoimmunity. The clinical picture fit the gene perfectly; the gene just happened to be wearing new clothes no one had seen before.

This case underscores a critical shift in modern endocrinology: genetic diagnosis is not just about finding what we know; it's about rigorously evaluating what we don't know yet using standardized frameworks. For clinicians managing young-onset diabetes who test negative for antibodies but have strong family histories, waiting for a "known" mutation to appear in a database is dangerous advice. By integrating segregation data, computational scores like AlphaMissense, and structural domain knowledge, we can confidently upgrade VUS designations and tailor therapy accordingly—saving patients from unnecessary insulin injections and preventing future complications through early intervention with agents like sulfonylureas or GLP-1 RAs tailored specifically for their monogenic defect.

### Key Take-Homes

*   **"Novel" Does Not Mean Benign:** Absence from ClinVar or VarSome does not rule out pathogenicity; always apply ACMG guidelines using segregation analysis, bioinformatic predictions (e.g., AlphaMissense), and protein domain context before dismissing new variants as VUS.
*   **Clinical Phenotype Guides Interpretation:** In cases of early-onset diabetes with negative autoantibodies and strong familial clustering (*especially* multi-generational), suspect MODY even if BMI is borderline; use clinical features like low renal glucose threshold (glycosuria) as clues toward *HNF1A* pathology.
*   **Therapeutic Implications Are Drastic:** Confirming *HNF1A*-MODY allows for de-escalation from insulin to oral agents (sulfonylureas) or newer classes like GLP-1 RAs/DPP-4 inhibitors, which can achieve excellent glycemic control while preserving beta-cell function and avoiding hypoglycemia risks associated with aggressive insulin regimens.
*   **Avoid SGLT2 Inhibitors Caution:** While effective for T2D/SGLT-mediated issues generally, exercise extreme caution with SGLT2 inhibitors in *HNF1A*-MODY patients due to pre-existing defects in SGLT2 expression which may precipitate severe dehydration or euglycemic DKA.
*   **Use Probability Calculators Wisely:** Tools like the Exeter MODY probability calculator are helpful starting points (>60% probability warrants testing), but remember they are calibrated largely on White European cohorts; maintain high clinical suspicion regardless of ethnicity if the pedigree fits monogenic patterns.
