Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Reasoning] Based on the patient's presentation of young-onset diabetes, strong multigenerational family history, negative autoantibodies, and preserved but insufficient C-peptide secretion, what specific monogenic subtype of diabetes is most strongly suspected before genetic confirmation?

Based on the constellation of young-onset diabetes, a striking three-generational autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, and the specific finding of preserved yet insufficient endogenous insulin secretion, the most strongly suspected monogenic subtype is **HNF1A-MODY** (also known as MODY 3).

The clinical reasoning here hinges on distinguishing this entity from Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes through specific phenotypic markers detailed in the case. While the patient initially presented with symptoms mimicking Type 1 diabetes (weight loss, polydipsia) and was started on insulin, the absence of diabetic ketoacidosis and negative antibodies (GADA, IA-2A) argues against autoimmune destruction. Conversely, the patient's lean body mass (BMI 24.6 kg/m²), lack of acanthosis nigricans, and strong multigenerational history make typical Type 2 diabetes unlikely despite the concurrent dyslipidemia.

Crucially, the glucagon stimulation test provided a pivotal physiologic clue: a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L confirms that beta-cell function is not absent (ruling out advanced Type 1) but is dysfunctional relative to the degree of hyperglycemia. This pattern—a progressive decline in beta-cell secretory capacity driven by a transcription factor defect—is the hallmark of *HNF1A* mutations. Furthermore, the presence of glycosuria in this context is highly suggestive; *HNF1A* regulates SGLT2 expression in the proximal tubule, leading to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption even when plasma glucose levels are only modestly elevated. As illustrated in this case report, where genetic testing confirmed a novel heterozygous *HNF1A* variant (p.N270S), recognizing this phenotype allows for a shift away from insulin toward more appropriate therapies like sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists once glycemic control is stabilized.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY specifically lead to the finding of glycosuria in a patient whose blood glucose levels may not always exceed the typical renal threshold seen in Type 2 diabetes?

The presence of glycosuria in *HNF1A*-MODY, even when plasma glucose concentrations fall below the classic renal threshold of approximately 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L), is a hallmark pathophysiologic feature driven by a dual defect: impaired beta-cell insulin secretion coupled with a lowered renal glucose threshold.

As illustrated in this case report, *HNF1A* encodes a transcription factor critical for the development and function of pancreatic beta cells and specific renal tubular segments. While the beta-cell dysfunction leads to the progressive decline in insulin secretion characteristic of this condition, the mechanism behind the glycosuria lies specifically in the kidney. *HNF1A* normally regulates the transcription of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (*SGLT2*) in the proximal convoluted tubule. In patients with *HNF1A* mutations, there is a downregulation or functional impairment of *SGLT2* expression. Consequently, the kidney's capacity to reabsorb filtered glucose is diminished, resulting in a significantly reduced renal threshold for glucose—often dropping to levels as low as 70–90 mg/dL (4–5 mmol/L).

Therefore, unlike Type 2 diabetes where glycosuria typically requires hyperglycemia exceeding 180 mg/dL due to overwhelmed but structurally intact transporters, *HNF1A*-MODY patients "leak" glucose at much lower blood sugar levels. This explains why our patient presented with concurrent hyperglycemia and glycosuria; however, it also serves as a key diagnostic clue that distinguishes this monogenic form from Type 2 diabetes or early Type 1 diabetes, where such profound glycosuria would not occur unless severe hyperglycemia was present. Recognizing this lowered threshold is vital for clinical reasoning, as it underscores the need for genetic confirmation over reliance on standard diabetic phenotypes alone.

### [Management] Why is it clinically contraindicated to initiate an SGLT2 inhibitor as first-line therapy for a patient with confirmed HNF1A-MODY, unlike in typical Type 2 diabetes management?

The clinical contraindication for initiating an SGLT2 inhibitor as first-line therapy in *HNF1A*-MODY stems from the unique underlying pathophysiology of this monogenic disorder, which differs fundamentally from Type 2 diabetes. In *HNF1A*-MODY, the mutant hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha protein fails to adequately regulate the transcription of key genes, including the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (*SGLT2*) itself in the proximal renal tubule. Consequently, these patients already possess a genetically determined reduction in renal glucose reabsorption, manifesting clinically as glycosuria at relatively low blood glucose thresholds—a feature explicitly noted in our patient's presentation.

Administering an exogenous SGLT2 inhibitor to a patient who already has diminished intrinsic SGLT2 function creates an additive effect that can precipitate profound osmotic diuresis. As highlighted in the discussion of this case report, this mechanism places patients at a significantly elevated risk for severe dehydration and hemodynamic instability, including potential vascular collapse. Furthermore, because *HNF1A*-MODY is characterized by progressive beta-cell dysfunction and relative insulin deficiency rather than insulin resistance, the stress-induced ketogenesis triggered by such profound volume depletion carries a heightened risk of euglycemic or frank diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

Therefore, unlike in Type 2 diabetes where SGLT2 inhibitors target insulin-resistant pathways to improve glycemic control and offer cardiorenal protection, their use in *HNF1A*-MODY offers negligible additional benefit regarding glucose lowering while introducing disproportionate safety risks. The standard of care for *HNF1A*-MODY remains low-dose sulfonylureas to stimulate endogenous insulin secretion or GLP-1 receptor agonists if sulfonylureas are ineffective or poorly tolerated, as successfully demonstrated in this patient's transition from insulin to dulaglutide and subsequent maintenance with metformin and saxagliptin. Precision medicine dictates avoiding agents that exacerbate the specific molecular defect—in this instance, the compromised renal glucose handling—rather than applying empiric Type 2 diabetes protocols.

### [Management] Given that sulfonylureas are traditionally considered the first-line treatment for HNF1A-MODY, what specific advantages does switching to a GLP-1 receptor agonist offer in this particular case scenario regarding safety and efficacy?

While sulfonylureas remain the classic first-line therapy for *HNF1A*-MODY due to their mechanism of closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels and stimulating insulin release, this case illustrates a strategic pivot to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) driven by the patient's specific physiological status and safety profile.

In this patient, the decision to switch from an insulin-sulfonylurea-DPP-4 inhibitor regimen to a GLP-1 receptor agonist was predicated on two key factors: the preservation of beta-cell function and the avoidance of hypoglycemia. Although sulfonylureas are highly effective in *HNF1A*-MODY, they act independently of glucose levels, carrying an inherent risk of hypoglycemia, particularly as endogenous insulin secretory capacity begins to wane—a hallmark of the progressive nature of this monogenic disorder. Our patient's glucagon stimulation test revealed "moderately preserved yet insufficient" beta-cell function (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L). In this context, a GLP-1 receptor agonist offers a superior safety margin because it enhances glucose-dependent insulin secretion. By mimicking incretin physiology, it stimulates insulin release only when blood glucose is elevated, thereby minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas or insulin.

Furthermore, regarding efficacy, the case demonstrates that GLP-1 receptor agonism is robust enough to manage significant hyperglycemia in *HNF1A*-MODY even when traditional agents like sulfonylureas might be considered. Despite initiating therapy with a very high baseline HbA1c of 10.2%, the introduction of dulaglutide facilitated a dramatic reduction to 6.0% within six months alongside metformin. This outcome aligns with emerging data suggesting that while sulfonylureas are potent, GLP-1 receptor agonists provide comparable glycemic control in *HNF1A* carriers but with added benefits regarding weight neutrality or loss (avoiding the weight gain sometimes seen with sulfonylureas/insulin) and a lower incidence of mild hypoglycemia events.

Therefore, in this specific scenario, the advantage of switching lies in transitioning from a fixed-dose secretagogue (sulfonylurea/insulin) that risks hypoglycemia as beta-cell reserve declines, to a physiologically adaptive agent (GLP-1 RA) that maintains tight glycemic control safely while preserving residual beta-cell function without inducing dangerous lows. This case reinforces that while genetics dictate susceptibility, real-time assessment of beta-cell reserve guides the optimal choice between these two classes for long-term safety and efficacy.

### [Diagnostic Workup] When evaluating a novel genetic variant like p.N270S in HNF1A that is absent from public databases (e.g., ClinVar), what specific criteria or lines of evidence must be applied to classify it as pathogenic or likely pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines?

When encountering a novel *HNF1A* variant such as p.N270S that is absent from public repositories like ClinVar, we cannot rely solely on population frequency to establish pathogenicity. Instead, we must synthesize multiple independent lines of evidence to satisfy the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria for a "Likely Pathogenic" or "Pathogenic" classification. In the case presented by Liu et al., the authors successfully classified the novel c.809A>G (p.N270S) variant using three critical pillars of evidence that align with specific ACMG rules.

First, we apply **segregation analysis** (ACMG criterion PP1). The variant demonstrates perfect co-segregation with the disease phenotype across three generations in this family: the proband, his mother, his younger brother, and his maternal grandmother all exhibited early-onset diabetes consistent with MODY. This strong familial clustering supports a causal relationship between the genotype and the clinical presentation.

Second, we utilize **computational and structural prediction data** (ACMG criterion PP3). Although the variant is novel, bioinformatic tools provide insight into its functional impact. In this case, the p.N270S substitution received a very high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, predicting a deleterious effect on protein function. Furthermore, structural context is vital; this mutation resides in exon 4 within the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A—a hotspot for known pathogenic variants—and specifically affects an amino acid residue immediately adjacent to previously characterized pathogenic sites. This proximity to established hotspots increases the likelihood that this change disrupts transcription factor binding or stability.

Third, we integrate **phenotypic specificity**. The patient's clinical picture was classic for *HNF1A*-MODY: young onset (<25 years), strong autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies ruling out Type 1 diabetes, glycosuria due to lowered renal threshold (a hallmark of HNF1A dysfunction affecting SGLT2 expression), and preserved but declining beta-cell function evidenced by C-peptide testing. While phenotypic match alone does not prove causality for a specific novel variant (PS4 requires statistical enrichment in cases vs controls), when combined with segregation and computational data in a gene with well-defined structure-function relationships like *HNF1A*, it solidifies the argument for pathogenicity.

Therefore, even without prior reporting in ClinVar or literature, the convergence of familial segregation, location within a critical functional domain near known mutations, high deleteriousness scores from predictive algorithms, and concordance with a highly specific clinical phenotype allows us to confidently classify such variants as Likely Pathogenic. This classification is essential for guiding precision medicine decisions—in this instance, shifting from insulin to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists—which dramatically improved glycemic control in our patient.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the key clinical features that distinguish HNF1A-MODY from classic Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) in a young adult presenting with hyperglycemia and weight loss?

When evaluating a young adult presenting with hyperglycemia and weight loss, the distinction between HNF1A-MODY and classic Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) hinges on three pillars: the nature of the family history, the status of islet autoimmunity, and the pattern of endogenous insulin preservation.

First, consider the **family history**. While T1DM is sporadic in approximately 85% of cases, HNF1A-MODY follows a strict autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. As illustrated in our case report, the patient exhibited a striking multigenerational pedigree with early-onset diabetes affecting his mother (late 20s), maternal grandmother (30s), and brother (age 20). In contrast, classic T1DM rarely presents with such a consistent vertical transmission across three generations without other autoimmune associations.

Second, **autoantibody status** is the most immediate discriminator. Classic T1DM is an autoimmune destruction of beta cells, characterized by positive serology for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), IA-2A, or ZnT8. Our patient was entirely negative for these markers. The absence of autoimmunity in a lean young adult with "insulin-deficient" appearing physiology should immediately raise suspicion for monogenic causes like MODY rather than autoimmune T1DM.

Third, we must look at **beta-cell function and renal handling**. Although both conditions involve insulin deficiency, the trajectory differs. In T1DM, beta-cell failure is rapid and absolute; however, in HNF1A-MODY, it is a progressive decline in secretory capacity due to defective transcription factors regulating insulin and GLUT2 expression. Crucially, our patient demonstrated *preserved* but insufficient endogenous insulin secretion on glucagon stimulation (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L), a level unlikely to be sustained if he had fulminant T1DM requiring lifelong intensive insulin dependence from onset. Furthermore, HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the proximal tubule; thus, patients often present with significant glycosuria disproportionate to their degree of hyperglycemia due to a lowered renal threshold for glucose—a feature not typical of T1DM unless severe hyperglycemia is present.

Finally, **clinical course and treatment response** provide retrospective validation. Patients with HNF1A-MODY are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists once diagnosed correctly. In this case, despite initial presentation mimicking T1DM with weight loss and ketosis-negative hyperglycemia requiring temporary insulin initiation, genetic confirmation allowed us to safely discontinue insulin within one month upon transitioning to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin regimen. This rapid de-insulinization confirms that the underlying pathology was not absolute autoimmune beta-cell destruction but rather a functional defect amenable to alternative pharmacologic targets.

Therefore, in any young patient with "new-onset" diabetes who lacks autoantibodies but possesses a strong family history of early-onset diabetes and preserved C-peptide levels despite significant hyperglycemia, genetic testing for HNF1A mutations is mandatory to avoid unnecessary lifelong insulin therapy and ensure precision management.

### [Clinical Pearls] Although rare, why might a clinician encounter positive islet autoantibodies (such as GADA) in a patient with genetically confirmed HNF1A-MODY, and how should this influence the diagnostic algorithm?

**Answer:**

While the classic teaching is that Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) is a non-autoimmune condition characterized by negative islet autoantibodies, clinicians must recognize that the presence of positive autoantibodies, such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), does not definitively exclude a diagnosis of *HNF1A*-MODY. This scenario represents a "double hit" or coincidental occurrence where a patient harbors both a monogenic defect in beta-cell function and an underlying autoimmune diathesis.

As illustrated in the broader context of diabetes diagnostics referenced in this case series, approximately 10% of patients initially classified as Type 1 diabetes (specifically those lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes) have been found to harbor *HNF1A* mutations. Conversely, patients with confirmed *HNF1A* mutations may occasionally present with detectable autoantibodies. The pathophysiological distinction lies in the primary driver of disease: in *HNF1A*-MODY, the fundamental defect is a transcriptional failure leading to progressive beta-cell dysfunction and a lowered renal threshold for glucose (explaining the prominent glycosuria seen in our patient), whereas autoimmunity targets beta-cell antigens.

Therefore, the detection of GADA should **not** halt the diagnostic algorithm if clinical features strongly suggest MODY. Key discriminators include a strong multigenerational family history of early-onset diabetes (as seen in this patient's mother, grandmother, and brother), absence of diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation, and preserved but declining C-peptide levels. Relying solely on serology risks misclassifying these patients as Type 1 diabetes, potentially leading to unnecessary lifelong insulin dependence when they might respond robustly to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists.

In this specific case report, despite the theoretical possibility of seropositivity complicating the picture, our patient tested negative for GADA and IA-2A, which streamlined the path to genetic confirmation. However, had he been seropositive, the clinical imperative would remain unchanged: proceed to molecular genetic testing via next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels when clinical suspicion persists. The identification of the novel *HNF1A* variant (p.N270S) confirmed that genetic testing is the gold standard for resolving diagnostic ambiguity. Ultimately, a positive antibody screen serves as a flag for complexity rather than a contraindication to genetic workup; confirming a monogenic etiology is essential for precision medicine, allowing us to de-escalate insulin therapy—as we successfully did here—and optimize long-term glycemic control with agents tailored to beta-cell preservation.

### [Clinical Presentation] Beyond glycemic control, what unique complication related to autonomic nervous system function was identified in this patient using electrochemical skin conductance testing, and why is this significant for long-term care planning?

In this patient with *HNF1A*-maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), electrochemical skin conductance testing revealed **severely reduced sudomotor function**, indicative of significant **autonomic neuropathy** affecting the small fibers of the hands. This finding is particularly striking given the patient's young age and relatively short duration of symptomatic disease, occurring in the absence of overt microvascular complications like retinopathy or nephropathy at diagnosis.

The identification of early autonomic dysfunction carries profound implications for long-term care planning. While *HNF1A* mutations classically drive beta-cell failure and a lowered renal glucose threshold (explaining the prominent glycosuria), they also affect the transcriptional regulation of ion channels and receptors in non-pancreatic tissues, potentially predisposing patients to neuropathic changes earlier than typical Type 2 diabetes. From a management perspective, recognizing this autonomic involvement necessitates vigilant surveillance for silent ischemia and gastroparesis, which may alter drug absorption kinetics. Furthermore, it reinforces the necessity for aggressive cardiovascular risk factor modification even in young patients who appear metabolically "lean" aside from their hyperglycemia. Crucially, this case underscores that genetic confirmation does not merely guide pharmacotherapy (such as transitioning to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 agonists); it also alerts us to a broader phenotypic spectrum where neurologic complications may precede traditional microvascular endpoints, demanding a more holistic monitoring strategy than glycemic control alone would suggest.
