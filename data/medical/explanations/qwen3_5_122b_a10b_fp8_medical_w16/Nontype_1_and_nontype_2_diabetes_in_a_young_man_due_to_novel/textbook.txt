Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The identification of monogenic diabetes often begins with recognizing a discrepancy between a patient's clinical phenotype and their expected physiological response to standard diabetic classifications. In this case, the index patient, a young adult male, presented not through acute emergency services but incidentally during an annual health examination. He exhibited marked hyperglycemia and glycosuria yet lacked the classic precipitating factors for either typical type 1 diabetes (autoimmune destruction) or type 2 diabetes (metabolic syndrome-driven insulin resistance). His Body Mass Index (BMI) was recorded at 24.6 kg/m², placing him within the normal range, and notably, he displayed no cutaneous stigmata of insulin resistance such as acanthosis nigricans. This physical profile immediately challenges the default assumption of type 2 diabetes in a young patient, as adiposity-related insulin resistance is rarely absent in that demographic unless the disease duration is extremely short or the underlying pathophysiology is distinct. Furthermore, while the patient reported symptomatic triad manifestations of polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss—symptoms common to all forms of severe hyperglycemia—the absence of acanthosis nigricans serves as a subtle but critical clinical clue pointing away from severe peripheral insulin resistance toward a primary secretory defect.

The most compelling feature driving the differential diagnosis in this scenario was the patient's robust family history, which followed a clear autosomal dominant transmission pattern. The pedigree revealed early-onset diabetes affecting three consecutive generations: the maternal grandmother diagnosed in her thirties, the mother in her late twenties, and a younger sibling at age twenty. This vertical transmission pattern is highly characteristic of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), specifically contrasting sharply with type 1 diabetes, which typically lacks such predictable multigenerational clustering without coincidental familial aggregation. While type 2 diabetes does run in families, the specific ages of onset here—well before age 30—are unusually early for sporadic type 2 disease driven solely by lifestyle factors. The convergence of early onset across three generations strongly suggested a single-gene disorder where one mutant allele is sufficient to cause disease, necessitating a shift in diagnostic thinking from environmental/autoimmune etiologies to genetic causes.

Laboratory investigations further complicated the picture by presenting features that overlapped with both major types of diabetes while simultaneously excluding key hallmarks of each. Upon initial workup, the patient's glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was critically elevated at 10.2%, accompanied by a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L and a post-load glucose of 19.8 mmol/L following an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Despite these severe elevations indicative of profound beta-cell failure or extreme insulin resistance, there was a striking absence of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). In typical type 1 diabetes presentations with comparable HbA1c levels and weight loss, DKA would be anticipated due to absolute insulin deficiency preventing lipolysis suppression; its absence here implies that enough endogenous insulin remained circulating to suppress ketogenesis but insufficient to maintain euglycemia or prevent glycosuria. This "non-ketotic" state in the setting of severe hyperglycemia creates a diagnostic gray zone where clinicians must consider partial beta-cell defects rather than total destruction or complete resistance.

Finally, the presence of glycosuria without concurrent ketosis provided another layer of specificity regarding renal physiology versus systemic metabolism. Glycosuria occurred because blood glucose levels exceeded the renal threshold for reabsorption; however, in HNF1A-mutated patients specifically discussed later in this text series, this phenomenon can also be exacerbated by a genetically lowered renal threshold due to defective SGLT2 expression in proximal tubules—a mechanism unique to this subtype compared to other diabetics where glycosuria strictly correlates with supranormal plasma glucose concentrations regardless of genetics. The combination of incidental detection in a lean young adult with no signs of metabolic syndrome, coupled with a powerful family history suggestive of Mendelian inheritance and laboratory findings showing severe hyperglycemia devoid of acidosis, encapsulates the classic diagnostic dilemma faced when encountering MODY. Without immediate genetic insight available at admission, these conflicting signals initially led clinicians down a pathway treating for presumed type 2 diabetes before genetic confirmation redirected management toward precision medicine tailored to HNF1A dysfunction.

## Pathophysiological Mechanisms and Differential Diagnosis Strategy

Understanding the unique pathophysiology of *HNF1A*-maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is essential for distinguishing it from the more prevalent forms of diabetes mellitus. Unlike Type 1 diabetes, characterized by autoimmune-mediated destruction of pancreatic $\beta$-cells, or Type 2 diabetes, driven primarily by peripheral insulin resistance and relative $\beta$-cell failure, *HNF1A*-MODY arises from a monogenic defect in a specific transcription factor. The *HNF1A* gene encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha, a protein critical for regulating the expression of numerous genes involved in glucose homeostasis across multiple tissues, including the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Within pancreatic $\beta$-cells, HNF1$\alpha$ drives the expression of key proteins required for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, such as GLUT2 (glucose transporter 2) and pyruvate kinase. Consequently, mutations in this gene lead to a progressive decline in $\beta$-cell function and a blunted insulin secretory response to rising blood glucose levels, rather than an absolute lack of insulin production seen in advanced Type 1 diabetes or the profound resistance typical of Type 2.

A distinctive clinical feature resulting from this molecular mechanism is glycosuria occurring at relatively modest levels of hyperglycemia. While glycosuria in classic diabetes usually reflects blood glucose exceeding the renal threshold (approximately 10 mmol/L), patients with *HNF1A*-MODY often exhibit glycosuria at much lower thresholds. This phenomenon stems from the fact that HNF1$\alpha$ also regulates the expression of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the proximal renal tubules. Loss-of-function mutations downregulate SGLT2 activity, impairing glucose reabsorption and lowering the renal threshold for glucose excretion. In the presented case, the patient's prominent glycosuria alongside hyperglycemia served as an early clue pointing toward a tubular handling defect characteristic of MODY rather than simple overflow due to extreme hyperglycemia alone.

Differentiating this condition from Type 1 diabetes relies heavily on serological profiling to rule out autoimmunity. In Type 1 diabetes, circulating autoantibodies such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 antibodies (ZnT8) indicate immune-mediated $\beta$-cell attack. Conversely, *HNF1A*-MODY is strictly non-autoimmune; therefore, these markers must be negative to support a diagnosis of MODY. In this case report, the complete absence of GADA and IA-2A was pivotal in excluding Type 1 diabetes despite the patient's young age and weight loss. Furthermore, while rare instances of co-existing autoimmunity exist, their absence combined with a strong multigenerational family history strongly favors a monogenic etiology over an autoimmune one.

The distinction from Type 2 diabetes requires careful interpretation of metabolic markers and functional testing. Patients with Type 2 diabetes typically display clear signs of insulin resistance, such as acanthosis nigricans, central obesity (high BMI), dyslipidemia indicative of metabolic syndrome, and elevated fasting C-peptide levels reflecting compensatory hyperinsulinemia. Although this patient exhibited some components suggestive of metabolic syndrome (elevated triglycerides), he lacked acanthosis nigricans and had a normal BMI (24.6 kg/m²), making classic insulin-resistant Type 2 less likely but not impossible given rising global obesity trends where phenotypic overlap increases. Crucially, functional assessment via the glucagon stimulation test provided deeper insight into $\beta$-cell reserve. The test measures stimulated C-peptide levels to gauge remaining endogenous insulin secretion capacity; a stimulated level greater than 0.6 nmol/L generally indicates preserved function inconsistent with advanced Type 1 but variable depending on disease stage in MODY or Type 2. Here, the patient's peak C-peptide was 0.83 nmol/L following glucagon administration. This result signifies "moderately preserved" yet insufficient secretion—a hallmark intermediate state often seen in MODY where cells retain viability but fail to respond adequately to physiological stimuli due to defective signaling pathways mediated by HNF1$\alpha$.

Ultimately, establishing a differential diagnosis follows an algorithmic approach: recognizing early-onset diabetes (<25 years) with three generations of vertical transmission prompts suspicion for MODY; negative autoantibodies exclude Type 1; and functional testing helps distinguish from early-onset Type 2 where insulin resistance dominates but $\beta$-cell compensation is initially robust or eventually fails differently than in MODY progression. Once these common etiologies are reasonably excluded through clinical history and basic biochemistry—as demonstrated by this patient's negative antibodies and specific pattern on C-peptide testing—the standard becomes molecular genetic confirmation via next-generation sequencing panels targeting known MODY-associated genes like *HNF1A*. This stepwise differentiation is not merely academic; it fundamentally alters management trajectories by shifting therapy away from lifelong immunosuppression or aggressive insulin regimens toward targeted agents like sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists that specifically address the underlying defect in insulin secretion dynamics.

## Confirmatory Genetic Testing and Variant Interpretation

Following the exclusion of autoimmune type 1 diabetes through negative serology and the recognition of a strong multigenerational family history suggestive of monogenic inheritance, the diagnostic pathway shifted toward molecular genetics. While clinical scoring systems, such as the Exeter MODY probability calculator, provided an initial estimate of likelihood, they cannot definitively distinguish between specific genetic etiologies or rule out phenocopies like early-onset type 2 diabetes. Consequently, next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels emerged as the gold standard for confirmation in this case. Unlike traditional Sanger sequencing, which analyzes one gene at a time and is inefficient for disorders with heterogeneous genetic causes, NGS allows for the simultaneous interrogation of dozens of genes associated with monogenic diabetes. This high-throughput approach enabled the efficient detection of rare variants across the entire coding sequence of *HNF1A* and other relevant loci, ensuring that no causative mutation was missed due to pre-conceived biases regarding gene selection.

The application of this technology led to the identification of a specific heterozygous substitution in exon 4 of the *HNF1A* gene: c.809A>G, resulting in the amino acid change p.N270S. Crucially, this variant represented a novel finding; it was absent from major public repositories such as ClinVar and VarSome, meaning there were no historical precedents or published case reports to immediately validate its pathogenicity. In clinical genetics, encountering a "private" or novel variant presents a significant interpretive challenge. Without prior documentation linking the variant to disease, clinicians must rely on rigorous bioinformatic prediction tools and functional inference rather than simple database lookup. The diagnostic team utilized computational models predicting protein structure changes and evolutionary conservation scores; notably, the AlphaMissense score for this variant was exceptionally high (0.995), strongly suggesting a deleterious effect on protein function. Furthermore, structural analysis placed this mutation within the critical DNA-binding domain of the HNF1α transcription factor, a region densely populated by known pathogenic variants that disrupt insulin regulation and renal glucose handling mechanisms.

To establish clinical validity for this novel variant in accordance with American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, investigators synthesized multiple lines of evidence beyond mere sequence alteration. The first pillar of proof was segregation analysis within the pedigree. The variant was shown to co-segregate perfectly with the diabetic phenotype across three generations: it was present in the proband's mother (diagnosed in her late 20s), grandmother (diagnosed in her 30s), and younger brother (diagnosed at age 20). This pattern satisfied criteria for autosomal dominant transmission consistent with *HNF1A*-MODY biology. Secondarily, while functional assays were not explicitly detailed in every case report setting, the location of p.N270S adjacent to previously characterized pathogenic residues provided strong mechanistic plausibility that this specific amino acid substitution would impair transcriptional activity required for beta-cell maturation and SGLT2 expression in renal tubules. By integrating these factors—the novel nature confirmed by NGS absence from controls, high predictive algorithmic scores, critical domain localization, and perfect familial cosegregation—the variant was classified as likely pathogenic (or pathogenic depending on strictness applied). This molecular confirmation transformed the diagnosis from presumptive based on clinical clues to definitive based on genomic architecture.

The successful characterization of this novel mutation underscores why genetic testing is indispensable even when variants are not listed in global databases. Relying solely on ClinVar or similar registries would have resulted in classifying this patient's condition as a "Variant of Uncertain Significance," leaving him without a clear diagnosis or targeted therapy guidance. Instead, applying ACMG frameworks allowed clinicians to confidently attribute his hyperglycemia to *HNF1A*-MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus. This distinction proved therapeutically vital; knowing the underlying mechanism permitted the discontinuation of unnecessary basal insulin—a common default treatment for presumed type 1 or advanced type 2—and facilitated a strategic switch to agents targeting residual beta-cell sensitivity and incretin pathways appropriate for monogenic defects. Ultimately, this case illustrates that modern precision medicine relies not just on detecting mutations already cataloged by others but also on expertly interpreting novel findings through standardized criteria to guide life-long management plans for patients with complex presentations like early-onset dysglycemia accompanied by unexpected autonomic neuropathy markers such as reduced sudomotor function.
