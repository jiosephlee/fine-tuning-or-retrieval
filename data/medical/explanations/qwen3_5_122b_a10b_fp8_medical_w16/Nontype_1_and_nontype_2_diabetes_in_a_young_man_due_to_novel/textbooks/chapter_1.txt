Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The identification of monogenic diabetes often begins with recognizing a discrepancy between a patient's clinical phenotype and their expected physiological response to standard diabetic classifications. In this case, the index patient, a young adult male, presented not through acute emergency services but incidentally during an annual health examination. He exhibited marked hyperglycemia and glycosuria yet lacked the classic precipitating factors for either typical type 1 diabetes (autoimmune destruction) or type 2 diabetes (metabolic syndrome-driven insulin resistance). His Body Mass Index (BMI) was recorded at 24.6 kg/m², placing him within the normal range, and notably, he displayed no cutaneous stigmata of insulin resistance such as acanthosis nigricans. This physical profile immediately challenges the default assumption of type 2 diabetes in a young patient, as adiposity-related insulin resistance is rarely absent in that demographic unless the disease duration is extremely short or the underlying pathophysiology is distinct. Furthermore, while the patient reported symptomatic triad manifestations of polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss—symptoms common to all forms of severe hyperglycemia—the absence of acanthosis nigricans serves as a subtle but critical clinical clue pointing away from severe peripheral insulin resistance toward a primary secretory defect.

The most compelling feature driving the differential diagnosis in this scenario was the patient's robust family history, which followed a clear autosomal dominant transmission pattern. The pedigree revealed early-onset diabetes affecting three consecutive generations: the maternal grandmother diagnosed in her thirties, the mother in her late twenties, and a younger sibling at age twenty. This vertical transmission pattern is highly characteristic of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), specifically contrasting sharply with type 1 diabetes, which typically lacks such predictable multigenerational clustering without coincidental familial aggregation. While type 2 diabetes does run in families, the specific ages of onset here—well before age 30—are unusually early for sporadic type 2 disease driven solely by lifestyle factors. The convergence of early onset across three generations strongly suggested a single-gene disorder where one mutant allele is sufficient to cause disease, necessitating a shift in diagnostic thinking from environmental/autoimmune etiologies to genetic causes.

Laboratory investigations further complicated the picture by presenting features that overlapped with both major types of diabetes while simultaneously excluding key hallmarks of each. Upon initial workup, the patient's glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was critically elevated at 10.2%, accompanied by a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L and a post-load glucose of 19.8 mmol/L following an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Despite these severe elevations indicative of profound beta-cell failure or extreme insulin resistance, there was a striking absence of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). In typical type 1 diabetes presentations with comparable HbA1c levels and weight loss, DKA would be anticipated due to absolute insulin deficiency preventing lipolysis suppression; its absence here implies that enough endogenous insulin remained circulating to suppress ketogenesis but insufficient to maintain euglycemia or prevent glycosuria. This "non-ketotic" state in the setting of severe hyperglycemia creates a diagnostic gray zone where clinicians must consider partial beta-cell defects rather than total destruction or complete resistance.

Finally, the presence of glycosuria without concurrent ketosis provided another layer of specificity regarding renal physiology versus systemic metabolism. Glycosuria occurred because blood glucose levels exceeded the renal threshold for reabsorption; however, in HNF1A-mutated patients specifically discussed later in this text series, this phenomenon can also be exacerbated by a genetically lowered renal threshold due to defective SGLT2 expression in proximal tubules—a mechanism unique to this subtype compared to other diabetics where glycosuria strictly correlates with supranormal plasma glucose concentrations regardless of genetics. The combination of incidental detection in a lean young adult with no signs of metabolic syndrome, coupled with a powerful family history suggestive of Mendelian inheritance and laboratory findings showing severe hyperglycemia devoid of acidosis, encapsulates the classic diagnostic dilemma faced when encountering MODY. Without immediate genetic insight available at admission, these conflicting signals initially led clinicians down a pathway treating for presumed type 2 diabetes before genetic confirmation redirected management toward precision medicine tailored to HNF1A dysfunction.
