### [Pathophysiology] Based on the histological findings described in this case, what specific cellular markers and patterns distinguish Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) from classic Polymyositis?

The critical histological distinction between Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) and classic Polymyositis lies in the dominant cell type mediating myofiber destruction and the density of the inflammatory infiltrate. As illustrated in this case, IMNM is characterized by prominent **myophagocytosis** and necrosis with a **paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates**.

In classic Polymyositis, the pathology is driven by endomysial infiltration of **CD8+ T-cells**, which directly invade non-necrotic muscle fibers expressing MHC Class I antigens. Conversely, in IMNM, the primary effector cells are **macrophages**. In our patient's biopsy of the vastus lateralis, we observed abundant macrophages identified by **CD68** staining actively engulfing necrotic debris (myophagocytosis), alongside scattered **rare CD3+ T-cells**. Furthermore, IMNM frequently demonstrates deposition of the **membrane attack complex (MAC/C5b-9)** on both necrotic and viable non-necrotic myofibers, indicating complement-mediated injury—a feature less central to the T-cell mediated cytotoxicity seen in Polymyositis.

While Polymyositis typically presents with a dense lymphocytic infiltrate surrounding and invading fibers, this case highlights the "necrotizing" phenotype where inflammation is sparse relative to the extent of fiber necrosis. This histological pattern, combined with the clinical absence of other connective tissue disease features and the presence of severe weakness with markedly elevated CK, supports the diagnosis of IMNM even in this seronegative patient (negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP). Recognizing this macrophage-dominant, T-cell-poor pattern is essential to differentiate IMNM from Polymyositis, as it dictates a therapeutic approach focused on B-cell modulation and complement inhibition (e.g., IVIG) rather than solely targeting T-cell pathways.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the clinical trajectory of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically differ from the presentation of IMNM triggered by a medication like semaglutide, particularly regarding Creatine Kinase (CK) trends after drug cessation?

The critical distinction between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and medication-triggered immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) lies in the temporal evolution of creatine kinase (CK) levels following the cessation of the offending agent.

In classic **drug-induced rhabdomyolysis**, the injury is a direct toxic effect on the myocyte membrane. As illustrated by the referenced case comparing semaglutide-associated IMNM to a concurrent tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis case, the latter presents with an acute, massive spike in CK that begins to normalize within days of stopping the drug and initiating supportive care (hydration), without requiring immunosuppression. The trajectory is self-limiting once the toxin is removed.

Conversely, **IMNM** represents an autoimmune process that persists even after the trigger is withdrawn. In our case report, the patient received only a single dose of semaglutide yet exhibited a progressive clinical decline over four months, with CK levels rising 12-fold from 337 U/L to nearly 4,000 U/L *after* drug exposure ceased. Unlike simple toxicity, where removal of the agent halts damage, IMNM involves ongoing autoantibody-mediated or cell-mediated destruction (often anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP positive, though this patient was seronegative). Consequently, CK levels in IMNM do not spontaneously resolve upon drug discontinuation; they require specific immunomodulation—such as corticosteroids and IVIG—to suppress the inflammatory cascade. In this patient, normalization of CK and functional recovery occurred only after immunosuppressive therapy was initiated, confirming that the pathology had transitioned from a potential trigger event to a sustained autoimmune state.

Therefore, when evaluating myopathy in a patient recently exposed to a new medication: if CK levels fail to trend downward within days of cessation or continue to rise over weeks despite withdrawal of the drug, one must suspect an evolving immune-mediated etiology like IMNM rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Given that this patient tested negative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies, how should a clinician interpret the diagnosis of IMNM in the context of seronegative presentations?

When evaluating a patient with clinical and histological features of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) who tests negative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies, the clinician must recognize that **seronegativity does not exclude the diagnosis**. As highlighted in this case report, approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative, reflecting either the presence of currently unidentified autoantibodies or alternative pathogenic mechanisms triggered by specific exposures.

In this specific case, the diagnosis relied heavily on the integration of clinical trajectory and histopathology rather than serology alone. The patient presented with classic IMNM features: progressive proximal muscle weakness, markedly elevated creatine kinase (peaking near 4,000 U/L), and dysphagia/dysphonia. Crucially, the muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis by demonstrating necrotic and regenerating fibers with prominent myophagocytosis (macrophage infiltration) and a relative paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates—the histological hallmark distinguishing IMNM from polymyositis. Furthermore, immunohistochemistry revealed membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, supporting an antibody-mediated complement activation process even in the absence of detectable circulating autoantibodies.

The interpretation here is further nuanced by the temporal relationship with semaglutide. Unlike typical drug-induced toxic rhabdomyolysis, which resolves rapidly upon drug cessation without immunosuppression, this patient's condition progressed over four months after a single dose of semaglutide and required corticosteroids and IVIG for resolution. This clinical course mimics the behavior of statin-associated IMNM prior to the discovery of anti-HMGCR antibodies: an immune-driven process triggered by a drug that persists independently of ongoing exposure once initiated. Therefore, in seronegative presentations, the diagnosis should be anchored on the triad of compatible clinical severity, characteristic "necrotizing" biopsy findings with minimal inflammation, and exclusion of other etiologies. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for drug-induced IMNM in seronegative patients with this phenotype, as early recognition allows for appropriate immunomodulatory therapy and prevents unnecessary long-term toxicity from misdiagnosis as simple toxic myopathy or polymyositis.

### [Management] What is the recommended initial therapeutic approach for a patient presenting with severe, progressive proximal weakness and CK >3000 U/L consistent with IMNM, and why might IVIG be added early in the course?

For a patient presenting with severe, progressive proximal weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (>3000 U/L) consistent with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), the recommended initial therapeutic approach is aggressive immunosuppression, typically beginning with high-dose systemic corticosteroids. As illustrated in the case of the patient developing IMNM following semaglutide exposure, this involves intravenous pulse methylprednisolone (e.g., 1 g/day for 5 days) followed by high-dose oral prednisone (1 mg/kg/day).

However, in cases characterized by severe disability—such as profound inability to stand or sit—and critical bulbar involvement like dysphagia and dysphonia, as seen in this patient, early adjunctive therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is frequently warranted. The rationale for adding IVIG early lies in the need for rapid disease control when corticosteroid monotherapy may be insufficient to halt the aggressive myonecrosis or when the clinical presentation threatens vital functions like airway protection and nutrition. In the reported case, despite five days of pulse steroids, the patient's weakness remained severe; consequently, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg over four days. This combination strategy facilitated a marked reduction in CK levels within two weeks and enabled a relatively rapid taper of corticosteroids once functional stability was achieved. Unlike idiopathic IMNM, which often necessitates long-term maintenance therapy with multiple agents, drug-associated IMNM (potentially triggered here by semaglutide) may respond favorably to a shorter course of combined immunosuppression once the offending agent is withdrawn and acute inflammation is suppressed.

### [Morning Report] In evaluating a patient with new-onset myopathy and elevated transaminases (AST/ALT), what clinical clues help differentiate primary muscle pathology from concurrent hepatic steatosis or other liver etiologies?

In evaluating a patient presenting with new-onset myopathy and elevated transaminases, the critical distinction lies in determining whether the enzyme elevation reflects primary hepatic injury or is merely a "red herring" resulting from massive skeletal muscle breakdown. As illustrated in this case of semaglutide-associated immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), both AST and ALT are abundant in skeletal muscle; therefore, their elevation does not automatically imply liver pathology.

The primary clinical clue differentiating these entities is the magnitude of the creatine kinase (CK) elevation relative to the transaminases. In pure hepatic disease, AST and ALT elevations are typically modest, rarely exceeding ten times the upper limit of normal unless there is fulminant failure. Conversely, in significant myopathies like IMNM, CK levels often soar into the thousands—this patient's CK peaked at 3992 U/L, while her AST and ALT were only mildly elevated (191 U/L and 165 U/L, respectively). When transaminase elevation appears disproportionate to clinical signs of liver dysfunction but correlates tightly with a disproportionately higher CK level, muscle pathology must be considered the primary driver.

Furthermore, historical context and specific biomarkers provide essential differentiation. In this case, the patient had documented biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) three years prior to symptom onset, explaining her baseline transaminitis. Crucially, her liver enzymes remained stable even after her CK normalized following treatment for IMNM, confirming that her chronic AST/ALT elevation was hepatic in origin (steatosis) and unrelated to the acute myopathic process. Additionally, while aldolase is another useful marker less common in the liver than muscle, its utility here is secondary to the dramatic CK spike. Finally, unlike drug-induced rhabdomyolysis where enzymes normalize rapidly upon cessation of the offending agent—as seen with tirzepatide-associated cases—the progressive nature of this patient's weakness and rising CK over four months post-exposure signaled an evolving autoimmune process requiring immunosuppression rather than simple withdrawal alone. Thus, when faced with elevated transaminases and weakness, always check a CK; if it is markedly elevated out of proportion to liver synthetic function or known hepatic history, suspect myopathy first.

### [Clinical Pearls] Considering the patient's cardiac MRI findings of myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement and subsequent normalization, what is the significance of monitoring troponin and BNP in patients with severe IMNM?

In patients with severe immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), particularly when extramuscular involvement is suspected, the monitoring of troponin and BNP serves as a critical surrogate for detecting subclinical cardiac injury, even in the absence of overt heart failure symptoms. As illustrated in this case, the patient presented with elevated high-sensitivity troponin and markedly elevated pro-BNP alongside a cardiac MRI demonstrating myocardial edema but notably lacking late gadolinium enhancement (LGE).

The presence of myocardial edema without LGE indicates active, reversible inflammation—consistent with early myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy—rather than established fibrosis or irreversible infarction. In the context of systemic IMNM, skeletal muscle enzymes like creatine kinase (CK) reflect myocyte necrosis, but they do not capture concurrent cardiac involvement. Troponin elevation here signals ongoing cardiomyocyte injury, while the disproportionate rise in BNP reflects increased wall stress from myocardial edema and transient systolic dysfunction (evidenced by the mild hypokinesis noted on MRI).

Crucially, serial monitoring of these biomarkers provides a dynamic assessment of therapeutic response. In this case, the normalization of both troponin and BNP paralleled the resolution of skeletal muscle weakness and the return to normal CK levels following immunosuppression and drug withdrawal. Furthermore, the subsequent normalization of cardiac MRI findings confirms that the initial biomarker elevations represented a reversible inflammatory process rather than permanent structural damage. Therefore, tracking troponin and BNP allows clinicians to identify "silent" cardiac involvement in IMNM, gauge the severity of systemic inflammation affecting the heart, and objectively verify complete remission alongside musculoskeletal recovery.

### [Morning Report] Why is it critical to obtain a detailed medication history specifically regarding GLP-1 receptor agonists when evaluating unexplained necrotizing myopathy, even if the exposure was limited to a single dose?

This case illustrates a critical, albeit rare, phenomenon where a single exposure to a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist can trigger an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), distinguishing it from simple drug toxicity. When evaluating unexplained necrotizing myopathy, obtaining a specific medication history for GLP-1 agonists—even if exposure was limited to a single dose—is essential because the pathophysiology appears to involve an aberrant immune activation rather than direct cytotoxicity.

In the classic model of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, such as that reported with tirzepatide, muscle injury is typically acute and self-limiting; creatine kinase (CK) levels spike immediately and normalize rapidly upon drug cessation and supportive care without the need for immunosuppression. However, as demonstrated in this case report, the reaction to semaglutide follows a distinct trajectory: a "latent" period followed by progressive worsening over months. Our patient received only one 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide three months prior to her severe presentation, yet her CK levels rose 12-fold over four months, accompanied by profound proximal weakness and dysphagia. This delayed progression argues against a direct toxic effect and instead supports an autoimmune mechanism where the initial antigenic stimulus primes the immune system, leading to sustained myofiber destruction mediated by macrophages and complement deposition, as evidenced by the biopsy showing myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex positivity.

Furthermore, unlike statin-associated IMNM which is frequently linked to anti-HMGCR antibodies, this patient was seronegative for known myositis-specific autoantibodies (including anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR). This highlights that GLP-1 agonists may represent a novel trigger for seronegative IMNM or induce unique autoantibodies not yet identified. Clinically, recognizing this association prevents misdiagnosis as idiopathic polymyositis or dermatomyositis and dictates appropriate management: while immediate withdrawal of the offending agent is necessary—as seen here where avoidance contributed to long-term remission—these patients often require targeted immunosuppression (corticosteroids and IVIG) to halt the ongoing autoimmune cascade, unlike toxic myopathies which resolve with cessation alone. Therefore, failing to inquire about recent GLP-1 exposure could lead to unnecessary prolonged immunotherapy or missed opportunities for early intervention in these emerging cases.

### [Management] If a patient with confirmed IMNM fails to respond to corticosteroids alone, what second-line agents are typically considered before resorting to long-term maintenance therapy, based on general principles for refractory cases?

In the management of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) that proves refractory to corticosteroid monotherapy, the standard of care involves the early introduction of steroid-sparing immunomodulatory agents. Based on general rheumatologic principles for refractory IMNM—and illustrated by the successful escalation in our case—the most appropriate second-line interventions to consider are intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or rituximab.

The pathophysiology of IMNM, characterized by complement-mediated necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration, often renders it less responsive to steroids alone compared to classic inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis. Consequently, guidelines recommend a "hit hard and fast" approach for severe or steroid-resistant cases to prevent irreversible muscle atrophy and functional decline. In this specific case report, the patient presented with severe proximal weakness, dysphagia, and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels that failed to resolve with high-dose pulse methylprednisolone and subsequent oral prednisone. Recognizing this lack of adequate response, the treating team appropriately escalated therapy by adding IVIG (2 g/kg over 4 days). This combination resulted in a marked reduction in CK levels and significant functional improvement within two weeks.

While our patient's unique etiology—likely triggered by semaglutide—allowed for eventual remission with a relatively short course of immunosuppression upon drug cessation, this scenario underscores a critical teaching point: in typical idiopathic or persistent drug-induced IMNM where the offending agent cannot be withdrawn or the disease remains active despite steroids, reliance on corticosteroids alone is insufficient. Clinicians must be prepared to initiate potent adjunctive therapies such as IVIG or conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) promptly. Delaying these second-line agents risks prolonged disability and exposure to high-dose steroid toxicity without achieving disease control. As demonstrated here, timely escalation to IVIG facilitated rapid recovery and allowed for a successful taper off immunosuppression once the trigger was removed and inflammation subsided.
