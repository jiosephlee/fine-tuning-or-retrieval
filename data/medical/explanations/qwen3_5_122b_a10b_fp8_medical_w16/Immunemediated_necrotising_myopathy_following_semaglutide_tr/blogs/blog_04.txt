Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Cardiac Involvement in Necrotizing Myopathy: A Silent Threat?

We often think of Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) as a story written entirely in skeletal muscle. You see the proximal weakness, the sky-high CK, and the necrosis on biopsy, and your brain immediately goes to "treat the legs." But here's a case that demands we expand our field of view. What happens when the heart gets caught in the crossfire of a necrotizing process triggered by something as ubiquitous as a weight-loss injection?

This week, we're dissecting a fascinating case report from *Lam et al.* detailing a woman in her 60s who developed severe IMNM shortly after a single dose of semaglutide. While the narrative arc focuses heavily on her dramatic muscle decline—progressing from inability to stand to profound dysphagia—the real plot twist lies in her heart. Despite having no classic signs of heart failure or ischemia initially, she walked into the hospital with elevated high-sensitivity troponin and a staggering proBNP of 4358 pg/mL. Her cardiac MRI didn't show scars or infarcts; instead, it revealed myocardial edema and mild hypokinesis. Was this just stress cardiomyopathy from being bedbound and septic later on? Or was this silent myocarditis masquerading as part of her systemic autoimmune storm?

Let's dive into why distinguishing drug-induced IMNM from simple toxicity matters, how we missed (or almost missed) the cardiac component, and what this means for your next patient presenting with unexplained weakness after starting those trendy GLP-1 agonists.

### The Semaglutide Connection: Toxicity vs. Autoimmunity

First, let's address the elephant in the room: semaglutide. We know these GLP-1 receptor agonists are game-changers for metabolic disease, but they aren't free agents. We've heard whispers of rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide—a direct toxic hit causing massive CK spikes that resolve quickly upon stopping the drug and hydrating. That's not what happened here.

Our patient took **one** dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg). Yes, she got sick immediately with nausea and vomiting requiring ICU-level respiratory support and hydration for acute kidney injury. But unlike typical drug-induced rhabdo where enzymes crash down once you stop the insult, her CK went on a four-month rollercoaster upward, peaking at nearly 4000 U/L long after she stopped taking the pill. She couldn't walk or swallow properly months later.

The timeline is crucial here. If this were pure toxicity, we'd expect normalization within days or weeks of cessation. Instead, we saw a delayed, progressive autoimmune phenotype confirmed by muscle biopsy showing necrotic fibers with myophagocytosis and sparse lymphocytes—the textbook definition of IMNM. Crucially, she was seronegative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies (which happen in roughly one-third of IMNM cases). This reinforces that while statins are famous for triggering anti-HMGCR myopathy via molecular mimicry or haptenization, other drugs might trigger "seronegative" variants through different mechanisms that we haven't fully mapped out yet.

### When Your Heart Screams Before It Fails

Here is where things get scary—and educational for us emergency physicians and internists managing complex presentations. Our patient wasn't complaining of chest pain or palpitations until much later when she developed atrial fibrillation during her second admission for sepsis. Yet, early labs told a different story: Troponin was elevated (62 pg/mL), and proBNP was astronomical (>4000).

When you see elevated troponin in a patient with massive muscle breakdown (CK ~4000), your first instinct should always be to rule out skeletal muscle artifact or concurrent MI. However, standard troponin assays don't usually cross-react significantly enough to cause such high numbers unless there is actual cardiac injury happening simultaneously—or unless you are dealing with specific isoforms that confuse older assays (though modern hs-cTnI/T is quite specific).

The cardiac MRI provided the smoking gun: myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). LGE typically signifies fibrosis or scar tissue seen in chronic myocarditis or infarction. Its absence suggested an acute inflammatory process rather than established damage. Combined with the clinical context of active systemic inflammation (high CRP/ESR) and widespread skeletal muscle necrosis, this pointed toward **concurrent inflammatory myocarditis**.

Why does this matter? Because cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies is notoriously variable but carries high mortality if ignored. Some patients remain asymptomatic with only biomarker bumps; others develop fulminant arrhythmias or pump failure overnight. In this case, treating the systemic inflammation worked wonders—not just for her legs but seemingly for her heart too. As her CK normalized and steroids tapered off over 10 months, her echo normalized completely, and she never suffered recurrent AFib or heart failure events off immunosuppression.

### Diagnostic Pitfalls: Don't Blame Everything on Muscle Breakdown

One of the biggest traps in evaluating IMNM is attributing every lab abnormality to skeletal muscle destruction or pre-existing comorbidities like NAFLD (Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease). This patient had known fatty liver dating back three years with mildly elevated transaminases before ever touching semaglutide. When her AST/ALT spiked alongside her CK during admission, it could easily have been dismissed as "just muscle leakage" superimposed on fatty liver disease—which it partially was—but recognizing that some enzyme elevations might reflect true multi-system organ involvement prevents premature closure on diagnosis.

Similarly, relying solely on autoantibody panels can lead to false reassurance being seronegative doesn't mean "not autoimmune." Up to 33% of IMNM cases lack detectable antibodies against HMGCR or SRP today simply because our testing hasn't evolved fast enough to catch newer targets linked to novel drug exposures like GLP-1s yet! Biopsy remains king when clinical suspicion runs high despite negative serologies especially when facing progressive weakness disproportionate to initial exposure duration!

Finally consider timing carefully regarding rechallenge ethics obviously impossible here since reaction severity precluded repeating dose nonetheless observing clear improvement upon withdrawal supports causality according WHO criteria even absent definitive proof mechanism involved!

### Take-Home Pearls

*   **Think Beyond Rhabdo:** If a patient develops worsening proximal weakness and rising CK weeks-to-months *after* stopping a potential offender drug (like semaglutide), suspect immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis which resolves quickly upon cessation*.
    
*   **Cardiac Screening is Mandatory:** Elevated troponin/BNP + inflammatory myopathy = assume concomitant myocarditis until proven otherwise Obtain ECG/Echo/Cardiac MRI early Even if asymptomatic because silent involvement can progress rapidly! 
    
*   **Seronegativity Doesn't Rule Out Autoimmunity:** Nearly one-third of IMNM patients test negative for known antibodies Do not delay empiric treatment based purely on negative panel results
