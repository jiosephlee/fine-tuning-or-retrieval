Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# The GLP-1 Paradox: Distinguishing Muscle Wasting from True Myopathy

Let's talk about the elephant in the emergency department: the sheer volume of patients walking through our doors on Semaglutide, Tirzepatide, and their cousins. As these GLP-1 receptor agonists skyrocket in popularity for obesity and Type 2 diabetes, we are seeing a predictable pattern of "muscle wasting" or sarcopenia. But here is the twist that keeps us up at night: how do we tell when that muscle loss is just a side effect of caloric deficit versus a full-blown, life-threatening autoimmune catastrophe?

We recently reviewed a fascinating case report from *Lam et al.* that forces us to confront exactly this dilemma. It involves a woman in her 60s who took **one single dose** of Semaglutide. Four months later, she couldn't stand up without help, struggled to swallow her own saliva, and had a Creatine Kinase (CK) level nearing 4,000 U/L. This isn't simple atrophy; this is Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM). And while previous reports linked GLPs to rhabdomyolysis, they looked nothing like this.

This case is a masterclass in why we need to look past the obvious "drug toxicity" label when the timeline doesn't fit. Let's break down why distinguishing between toxic rhabdo and autoimmune IMNM matters, especially as these meds become part of our daily lexicon.

### The Timeline Tells the Tale

If you've ever managed drug-induced rhabdomyolysis—whether from statins or toxins—you know the rhythm. You see an acute insult, a massive spike in CK (often >10,000), and once you stop the drug and hydrate, those numbers crash back to baseline within days. That was exactly what happened in a recent case report involving Tirzepatide: acute onset, massive CK spike, rapid resolution upon cessation without needing steroids.

Our patient with Semaglutide? Totally different story. She got sick immediately after her first dose with nausea and vomiting (classic GI intolerance), requiring hospitalization for dehydration and acute kidney injury. But here is where it gets weird: she didn't get better after discharge. Over the next four months, while completely off the drug, she slowly deteriorated into profound proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia). Her CK crept up 12-fold over months rather than spiking acutely.

When I see progressive weakness worsening *after* stopping a suspect agent over a period of months, my brain switches from "toxic myopathy" to "autoimmune trigger." The exposure acted as an ignition switch for an immune cascade that kept burning long after the fuel source was removed. This mirrors the classic history of Statin-associated Autoimmune Myopathy (SAAM), where patients continue to worsen even after quitting statins until they hit them hard with immunosuppression.

### The Diagnostic Trap: Seronegativity and Liver Confusion

One of the most challenging aspects of this case was the serology. When you think IMNM, your mind jumps straight to Anti-HMGCR or Anti-SRP antibodies. They are the hallmarks of necrotizing myopathies triggered by statins or spontaneous events respectively. Yet, our patient tested negative for everything on a comprehensive myositis panel: no HMGCR, no SRP, no Jo-1, no MDA-5. She was seronegative.

Does this rule out IMNM? Absolutely not. Roughly one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative at presentation. If you rely solely on antibody panels here, you miss the diagnosis entirely. The clinician must trust the clinical phenotype: severe proximal weakness + massively elevated CK + biopsy showing necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration = IMNM until proven otherwise.

There was also a red herring lurking in her labs: elevated transaminases (AST/ALT). With her history of NAFLD and obesity, it's easy to assume liver pathology explains every bump in LFTs. However, remember that AST is found abundantly in skeletal muscle too! In this case context—with CK near 4k—the AST elevation was almost certainly muscular in origin superimposed on chronic fatty liver disease. Don't let pre-existing comorbidities blind you to new patterns; always correlate enzyme trends with clinical strength and specific markers like aldolase if available (though not mentioned here).

### Biopsy is King When Antibodies Lie Silent

Because she was seronegative and had such an unusual presentation relative to standard toxic reactions, tissue diagnosis became critical. The muscle biopsy confirmed what we feared but hoped wasn't true: scattered necrotic fibers with prominent phagocytosis (macrophages cleaning up debris) but very few T-cells invading the space around them ("paucity of lymphocytic infiltrate"). Immunostaining highlighted membrane attack complexes on both necrotic and viable fibers—a signpost pointing directly toward complement-mediated destruction typical of IMNM.

Without that biopsy result confirming necrosis without significant inflammation from other causes (like vasculitis or inclusion body myositis), treating her empirically might have felt risky given her age and comorbidities (she had recent sepsis risk factors). Once confirmed though, the path forward became clear: aggressive immunomodulation plus permanent avoidance of GLP-1s forevermore.

### Treatment Response Validates the Mechanism

The therapeutic outcome offers strong retrospective evidence for our hypothesis regarding causality vs coincidence. She received pulse-dose methylprednisolone followed by oral tapering AND IVIG (2 g/kg over 4 days). Unlike pure toxic rhabdo which resolves with fluids alone, she required this heavy-handed immunosuppression to turn things around—and it worked beautifully within two weeks as evidenced by plummeting CK levels and regained mobility. Most importantly? She stayed off drugs indefinitely after being weaned off steroids at home eight months ago with zero relapse. If this were purely genetic or idiopathic IMNM unrelated to Semaglutide, would such a short course have yielded such durable remission? Probably not; idiopathic cases usually demand years of maintenance therapy like methotrexate or rituximab upfront. The fact that she recovered quickly supports the theory that removing the antigenic trigger allowed her immune system to reset faster than usual once suppressed initially—a hallmark of drug-triggered autoimmunity where removal stops ongoing stimulation effectively enough when paired with induction therapy [citation needed].

### Take-Home Points for Clinicians

So how do we apply this lesson tomorrow morning when your next obese diabetic presents with fatigue? Keep these pearls front-and-center:

*   **Trust Your Gut Over Timelines:** Drug-induced *toxicity* typically follows an acute spike-and-recovery curve upon cessation (*e.g.,* tirzepatide rhabdo). If weakness progresses *for months* after stopping a GLP-1 agonist despite hydration/stopping meds immediately consider **Autoimmune Necrotizing Myopathy**.
*   **Serology Can Be Deceptive:** Do not rule out IMNM based on negative anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP results alone; roughly **33% of cases are seronegative**. Diagnosis relies heavily on clinical suspicion supported by biopsy findings (necrosis + macrophage dominance + sparse lymphocytes).
*   **Don't Blame Everything on Fatty Liver:** Elevated AST/ALT in patients with known NAFLD should still prompt consideration of muscle sources if accompanied by disproportionately high CK levels
