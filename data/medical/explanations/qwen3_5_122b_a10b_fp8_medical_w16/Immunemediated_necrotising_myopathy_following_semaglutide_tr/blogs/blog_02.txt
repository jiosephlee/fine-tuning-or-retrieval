Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Semaglutide, Seronegative Myopathy, and the Power of Biopsy: A New Trigger for IMNM?

We usually think of "drug-induced" muscle injury as a straightforward story: a toxin hits hard, enzymes spike immediately, the drug stops, and the patient recovers. Think of classic statin toxicity or acute rhabdomyolysis from dehydration. But what happens when the clock keeps ticking *after* you've stopped the offending agent? What happens when your standard autoimmune panel comes back completely blank, leaving you staring at a critically ill patient with profound weakness?

This week, we're diving into a fascinating and sobering case report that flips the script on how we view GLP-1 receptor agonists and their potential link to **Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM)**. Published recently by Lam et al., this case describes the first known instance of IMNM triggered by semaglutide—a medication now ubiquitous in emergency departments and primary care clinics worldwide. More importantly, it highlights a critical diagnostic pitfall: relying solely on serology when the real answer lies under the microscope.

## The Timeline That Didn't Fit Toxicity

Let's walk through the timeline, because it tells us everything we need to know about why this wasn't simple rhabdo. A woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD receives her very first dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg). Within hours, she crashes with severe GI symptoms, vomiting, acute kidney injury from hypovolemia, and metabolic acidosis requiring ICU-level care. Her Creatine Kinase (CK) on admission is mildly elevated at 337 U/L—high enough to worry about muscle strain from vomiting or hypoperfusion, but certainly not the sky-high numbers (>10k or >50k) we see in fulminant rhabdomyolysis.

Here is where things get tricky. She gets fluids, stabilizes, goes home deconditioned but seemingly okay regarding her kidneys. Then comes the twist: over the next **four months**, she doesn't just plateau; she deteriorates. Progressive proximal weakness sets in until she can't stand or sit upright. Dysphagia and dysphonia develop—the scary signs of bulbar involvement that should trigger an immediate "myositis alert." By month four, her CK has skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L (a 12-fold increase from baseline).

If this were direct drug toxicity (like tirzepatide-associated rhabdo described in previous literature), we would expect the CK to plummet once the drug cleared its half-life days ago. Instead, we saw a delayed onset and relentless progression despite complete cessation of semaglutide three months prior to her worst presentation. This trajectory screams **autoimmunity** rather than direct toxicity.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: When Panels Lie Flat

When this patient hit our hypothetical ED or Rheum clinic with massive weakness and high CK, the reflex order is usually a myositis panel. You check for HMGCR (the classic statin-linked antibody), SRP (associated with severe necrosis), Jo-1 (antisynthetase syndrome), and others. In this case? **Everything came back negative.** Even her ANA was negative; she had only a lone low-titer SS-B positivity which didn't fit a classic Sjögren's picture.

Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative. If you stop working up based on negative bloodwork here, you miss the diagnosis entirely. This is where clinical rigor demands we look past the lab values and trust the pathology labs.

The muscle biopsy was the hero of this story. Despite lacking circulating antibodies against HMGCR or SRP, the histology was textbook for IMNM:
*   **Predominantly necrotic fibers:** The architecture was falling apart without significant lymphocytic invasion initially.
*   **Myophagocytosis:** Macrophages eating dead muscle fibers were abundant (highlighted by CD68 staining).
*   **Membrane Attack Complex (MAC):** Positive staining on both necrotic *and* viable fibers indicated complement-mediated damage driving ongoing destruction even away from obvious cell death sites.
*   **Paucity of Lymphocytes:** Unlike polymyositis where T-cells invade endomysially here they were sparse compared to macrophages and complement deposition.

This confirms that seronegativity does not rule out an autoimmune process driven by mechanisms other than detectable circulating autoantibodies—or perhaps antibodies targeting epitopes not covered by current commercial panels yet exist waiting to be discovered in GLP-1 users specifically.

## Treatment Response vs Idiopathic Disease

One might wonder if treating seronegative IMNM differs from antibody-positive disease. Interestingly, this patient's response suggested something unique about drug-triggered cases versus idiopathic ones. After starting high-dose steroids (pulse methylprednisolone followed by prednisone) plus IVIG for 2 g/kg over 4 days, she saw dramatic improvement within two weeks with normalization of CK levels shortly thereafter. Crucially, unlike many idiopathic IMNM patients who require years of aggressive immunosuppression to prevent relapse, she was successfully tapered off all meds within months and has remained in remission for eight months since stopping therapy—all while avoiding semaglutide permanently.

This suggests that removing the inciting antigen source allows for faster resolution once immunity is dampened acutely—a crucial distinction for prognosis counseling if similar cases emerge elsewhere!
