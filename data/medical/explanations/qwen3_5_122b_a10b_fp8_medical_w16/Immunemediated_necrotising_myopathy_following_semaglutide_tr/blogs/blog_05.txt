# From Trigger to Remission: When Drug-Induced IMNM Responds Differently Than Its Idiopathic Cousin

We usually think of Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) as a stubborn beast. If you've ever managed a patient with anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP myopathy, you know the drill: massive CK elevations, profound proximal weakness, and a relentless need for heavy-duty immunosuppression that drags on for months, sometimes years. These patients rarely just "get better" once they stop their statin; they require a slow, arduous climb through methotrexate, azathioprine, rituximab, or chronic IVIG to keep the fire from reigniting.

But what happens when the autoimmune storm isn't sparked by an internal glitch or a classic toxin like a statin, but by something entirely new entering the mix? And what if removing that spark allows us to turn down the volume on immunosuppression much sooner than usual?

That's exactly what happened in a fascinating recent case report from *Lam et al.*, detailing a woman in her 60s who developed fulminant IMNM shortly after her **first and only dose** of semaglutide. What makes this case particularly juicy for our emergency and critical care colleagues isn't just the novelty of the trigger—it's the trajectory. Unlike the grueling marathon required for idiopathic IMNM, this patient achieved full remission with a relatively short course of corticosteroids and IVIG after simply stopping the offending agent.

Let's dive into how this played out and what it tells us about distinguishing drug-induced autoimmunity from its spontaneous counterparts.

## The Clinical Picture: A Perfect Storm After One Dose

The patient arrived with a four-month history of progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. On paper, she looked like textbook severe myopathy. Her Creatine Kinase (CK) skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L (from a baseline that was never explicitly recorded but implied to be normal). She couldn't stand without help and was struggling to swallow anything liquid or solid.

Here is where the timeline gets critical. Three years prior, she had been diagnosed with NAFLD and started on lifestyle modifications. Two-and-a-half years ago, she finally got prescribed semaglutide for weight management. **Within hours** of her very first injection (0.25 mg), she crashed hard—nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure from dehydration, and even transient atrial fibrillation requiring ICU-level respiratory support. She survived that acute hit with fluids and supportive care but was discharged feeling weak ("deconditioned," as noted in the chart).

Over the next four months, instead of recovering strength as one would expect from simple deconditioning or resolving gastroenteritis sequelae, she deteriorated relentlessly into frank neuromuscular collapse. By the time she reached our attention via transfer to a tertiary center for rheumatology evaluation, she was essentially bedbound with bulbar involvement.

The workup confirmed necrotizing myopathy on biopsy: scattered necrotic fibers with prominent myophagocytosis (macrophages eating dead muscle) but notably sparse lymphocytic infiltration—a hallmark of IMNM. Crucially, every standard myositis antibody came back negative: no anti-HMGCR (the usual suspect in statin-associated cases), no anti-SRP, no antisynthetase antibodies. She was seronegative IMNM.

## The Twist: Why Did This Patient Get Better So Fast?

This is where we pivot to the core teaching point of this case report: **Therapeutic trajectories differ based on etiology.**

In idiopathic IMNM (or even classic statin-induced HMGCR+ myopathy), stopping the trigger doesn't always stop the process because your body has already launched an autonomous autoimmune cascade against self-antigens (like HSP70 or SRP complexes). You have to suppress that runaway train aggressively for a long time until tolerance is somehow restored—or perhaps indefinitely if relapse occurs upon tapering.

However, in this semaglutide-associated case, the authors argue—and I agree—that this represents **drug-triggered autoimmunity**. The hypothesis here is compelling: Semaglutide acted as an exogenous antigen or adjuvant that ignited an immune response in a susceptible host (perhaps someone with underlying genetic susceptibility or subclinical autoimmunity masked by her obesity/liver issues). Once that specific trigger was removed permanently (she took zero subsequent doses), there was no continuous source driving the production of pathogenic antibodies against self-tissues.

Consequently, when she received pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a single round of high-dose IVIG (2 g/kg over 4 days), her system calmed down remarkably fast. Her CK plummeted within two weeks. She didn't need Rituximab maintenance; she didn't need Methotrexate bridging; she didn't even need repeated rounds of IVIG due to cost constraints alone! She tapered off steroids over two months and has remained completely asymptomatic with normal labs for eight months off all immunosuppression.
