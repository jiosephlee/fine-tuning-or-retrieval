# Beyond the Stomach: When GLP-1 Agonists Trigger Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy

We've all seen the headlines and heard the chatter about GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide and tirzepatide. They are revolutionizing the management of obesity and type 2 diabetes, but they aren't without their side effects. Most of us are familiar with the GI misery—nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea—that often leads to dose reductions or discontinuation. We even know that these agents can cause sarcopenia or, in rare instances, direct toxic rhabdomyolysis that spikes CK levels acutely and resolves rapidly once the drug is stopped.

But what happens when a patient presents not with acute collapse, but with a slow, progressive march toward paralysis four months *after* a single dose? What if that "muscle issue" isn't just toxicity, but a full-blown autoimmune storm?

This week, we're diving into a fascinating case report by Lam et al., detailing the first probable link between semaglutide and **Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM)**. It's a reminder that in the era of weight-loss wonder drugs, we need to keep our index of suspicion high for things that don't fit the standard textbook timeline.

## The Case: A Delayed Reaction

Let's meet our patient: a woman in her 60s with obesity, NAFLD, hypertension, and hypothyroidism. Three years prior to her current crisis, she had workups for elevated liver enzymes which turned out to be steatohepatitis. Two and a half years ago, she finally started semaglutide.

Here is where it gets interesting. After her **first ever dose** (0.25 mg), she didn't just get nauseous; she got sick enough to require ICU-level care for dehydration, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis within 48 hours. She recovered from this acute hit with fluids and supportive care. Her CK at that moment was mildly elevated at 337 U/L—unremarkable for rhabdo.

Fast forward four months. She wasn't getting better; she was getting worse. She developed profound proximal muscle weakness, couldn't stand or sit upright without help, and struggled with dysphagia and dysphonia. When she finally made it to the ER (via transfer), her CK was skyrocketing at **3992 U/L**.

This timeline should immediately set off alarm bells for anyone thinking "drug-induced rhabdomyolysis." Classic toxic rhabdo usually peaks early and falls fast once the toxin is removed. Here, the patient had been off semaglutide for four months when her CK peaked. This lag time suggests an immune-mediated process rather than direct cellular toxicity.

## The Workup: Seronegative but Histologically Definitive

The diagnostic journey here mirrors what many of us see in complex neuromuscular cases: lots of noise in the labs until you hit gold-standard tissue diagnosis.

Her myositis panel came back essentially blank. No anti-HMGCR (the classic statin-associated antibody), no anti-SRP, no antisynthetase syndrome markers (Jo-1, etc.), and even negative ANA except for an isolated low-titer SS-B (which is likely an epiphenomenon given the negative SSA). About one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative, so we can't rule out IMNM based on bloodwork alone—a crucial pearl for emergency physicians who might stop searching if antibodies are missing.

The clincher was the muscle biopsy of the vastus lateralis. Pathology showed dispersed necrotic fibers with prominent macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) but notably few lymphocytes—the hallmark "paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates" that defines necrotizing myopathy as distinct from polymyositis or dermatomyositis. Immunohistochemistry confirmed membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, confirming an active immune attack on muscle membranes even without circulating autoantibodies detectable on standard panels.

Interestingly, she also had some cardiac involvement—mild myocardial edema on MRI and transient troponin elevation—but no structural damage or late gadolinium enhancement suggestive of permanent infarction or fibrosis. This highlights that IMNM isn't always purely skeletal; systemic involvement can occur even when serology is silent.

## Treatment Strategy: Don't Just Stop the Drug

If this were simple toxic rhabdomyolysis from tirzepatide (as reported recently), stopping the drug would have been curative within days. Our patient needed more aggressive intervention because her immune system had already crossed its own threshold into autoimmunity.

She received pulse-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone tapering over two months. Crucially, due to persistent severe weakness despite steroids alone, she required **IVIG** (2 g/kg split over 4 days). This dual approach is often necessary in severe IMNM cases to halt antibody production and modulate inflammation rapidly.

The outcome was gratifying but instructive: she normalized her CK within two weeks of starting treatment and regained her baseline functional status within 10 months—all while off immunosuppression for eight months prior to her last follow-up visit compared to idiopathic IMNM which often requires long-term maintenance therapy like methotrexate or azathioprine). This rapid remission supports the hypothesis that removing the trigger (semaglutide) allowed her immune system to reset faster than it would have in spontaneous idiopathic cases—but only *because* we intervened aggressively with immunosuppression during the flare.
