Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The patient, a woman in her 60s with a background of obesity, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), hypertension, and hypothyroidism, presented with a complex clinical trajectory that began with an acute adverse event and evolved into a severe neuromuscular disorder. Her medical history was significant for chronic transaminitis diagnosed three years prior, which was biopsied and confirmed as steatohepatitis consistent with NAFLD. Despite lifestyle modifications, her metabolic profile remained suboptimal, prompting the initiation of semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, 2.5 years after her initial liver diagnosis. Crucially, the patient had no history of statin use, significant alcohol consumption, or exposure to other known myotoxins such as red yeast rice or mushrooms, which are important historical exclusions when evaluating acquired myopathies. The absence of these risk factors initially narrowed the differential diagnosis away from common drug-induced toxicities like statin-associated autoimmune necrotizing myopathy (SAANM).

The clinical course commenced immediately following a single subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg). Within hours, the patient developed profound nausea and vomiting that persisted for two days, necessitating hospitalization for complications including acute renal failure secondary to severe volume depletion and metabolic acidosis requiring intensive care support for respiratory compromise. During this initial admission for acute toxicity, her creatine kinase (CK) level was modestly elevated at 337 U/L. This early finding represents a critical diagnostic pivot point; while the patient exhibited signs of systemic stress and renal impairment that could theoretically precipitate minor muscle enzyme leakage, the magnitude of CK elevation was inconsistent with frank rhabdomyolysis or massive muscle necrosis at that specific moment. The immediate gastrointestinal reaction and subsequent dehydration were classic side effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists but masked the incipient autoimmune process that would soon manifest.

Over the subsequent four months following discharge from her initial hospitalization—a period where she remained completely off semaglutide—the patient's condition deteriorated insidiously rather than resolving. Instead of recovering from simple deconditioning following her acute illness, she developed progressive proximal muscle weakness that severely impacted her mobility to the point where she could neither stand nor sit upright without assistance. Concurrently, she developed bulbar symptoms characterized by dysphagia affecting both solids and liquids and dysphonia indicating laryngeal muscle involvement. This temporal evolution distinguishes the case from typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis associated with GLP-1 agonists or other agents like tirzepatide, where CK elevations typically peak acutely upon exposure and normalize rapidly within days of drug cessation once hydration is restored. In contrast, this patient's CK levels surged twelve-fold over four months to reach 3992 U/L while she was entirely free of the offending agent, suggesting a self-perpetuating immune-mediated mechanism rather than a direct toxic effect limited to the duration of drug presence in circulation.

The diagnostic challenge lay in differentiating this subacute progression from other etiologies of progressive weakness in an elderly patient with comorbidities. Common causes such as infectious myositis were excluded given the lack of fever or systemic inflammatory signs other than elevated muscle enzymes. Metabolic myopathies were considered unlikely given the normal thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels and the specific pattern of proximal weakness combined with bulbar involvement rather than generalized fatigue alone. Furthermore, the persistence and worsening of symptoms despite adequate nutritional rehabilitation post-discharge argued against simple disuse atrophy or residual effects from her initial acute kidney injury. The clinical picture ultimately pointed toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), a distinct pathological entity characterized by aggressive weakness and necrotic muscle fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. The timeline—where an acute toxic trigger appeared to ignite a delayed autoimmune cascade resulting in progressive necrosis weeks to months later—highlighted the complexity of diagnosing drug-associated IMNM compared to straightforward toxic myopathies that resolve promptly upon withdrawal of the causative agent.

## Pathophysiology and Differential Diagnosis Framework

To comprehend the clinical trajectory of this case, one must first distinguish Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) from other forms of muscle injury, particularly toxic myopathies and classic inflammatory myopathies like polymyositis. IMNM is a distinct clinicopathologic entity characterized by severe, progressive proximal muscle weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels. The hallmark of its pathophysiology lies in the histological finding of extensive myofibre necrosis accompanied by a "paucity" of lymphocytic infiltrates. Unlike polymyositis, where T-cells invade non-necrotic fibers, or dermatomyositis, which presents with perifascicular atrophy and prominent perimysial inflammation, IMNM biopsies reveal widespread necrosis dominated by macrophages clearing debris (myophagocytosis) rather than a dense T-cell mediated attack. This specific histological pattern reflects an autoimmune process targeting the sarcolemma itself, often driven by antibodies against proteins involved in protein synthesis or degradation pathways, leading to rapid fiber destruction that outpaces regeneration unless treated aggressively with immunosuppression.

A critical diagnostic challenge in this case involves differentiating IMNM from drug-induced toxic rhabdomyolysis, especially given the temporal relationship with semaglutide administration. Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically follows a kinetic profile of acute onset immediately following exposure to the offending agent (such as statins or direct toxins), resulting in massive CK elevations that resolve rapidly—often within days to weeks—upon cessation of the drug and supportive care like hydration. In contrast, the patient's course exhibited a delayed progression; although she experienced immediate gastrointestinal toxicity after a single dose of semaglutide requiring hospitalization for acute kidney injury, her muscle weakness and CK levels worsened progressively over four months *after* the drug had been discontinued. If this were simple toxic rhabdomyolysis induced by semaglutide, one would expect CK normalization shortly after discharge once the toxin was cleared. Instead, the 12-fold increase in CK over months and the persistence of weakness indicate an ongoing autoimmune cascade triggered by the drug but sustained independently of its presence, necessitating immunomodulatory therapy rather than mere observation or hydration.

The role of autoantibodies serves as both a diagnostic anchor and a source of complexity in IMNM classification. Approximately two-thirds of IMNM cases are associated with detectable autoantibodies: anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (anti-HMGCR) or anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP). Anti-HMGCR is frequently linked to statin-exposed patients who develop persistent myopathy even after stopping the medication, while anti-SRP is associated with more severe disease and higher mortality rates. However, roughly one-third of patients with confirmed IMNM are seronegative for these markers, as was the case here where an extensive myositis panel including HMGCR and SRP was negative. This seronegative status does not exclude the diagnosis but highlights that other unidentified antigens or triggers may exist. In this specific scenario, the absence of anti-HMGCR makes a classic statin-associated etiology impossible (given no statin use), while the negative SRP points toward either a novel antigenic target or a unique drug-induced mechanism specific to GLP-1 receptor agonists that mimics the seronegative phenotype of idiopathic IMNM.

Interpreting laboratory data required careful differentiation between hepatic and muscular sources for elevated transaminases (AST and ALT). Muscle tissue contains significant amounts of AST and ALT; therefore, profound muscle breakdown can elevate these enzymes independently of liver pathology. In this patient's history, mild transaminitis predated semaglutide use by three years and was biopsy-proven to be due to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Crucially, while her CK levels normalized significantly after treatment for IMNM, her AST and ALT remained mildly elevated relative to baseline liver parameters but stable compared to their previous chronic state. This dissociation allowed clinicians to attribute the massive acute spikes in AST/ALT during her initial presentation primarily to muscle breakdown superimposed on chronic hepatic steatosis, rather than acute hepatitis or primary liver failure complicating her rhabdomyolysis. Recognizing this distinction prevented unnecessary workups for viral or autoimmune hepatitis during her acute neuromuscular crisis.

Finally, evaluating potential cardiac involvement is essential when managing severe myopathies presenting with dyspnea and elevated biomarkers like troponin and BNP. Inflammatory myopathies can rarely involve cardiac muscle directly (myocarditis) or cause conduction abnormalities; alternatively, systemic stress can induce transient myocardial dysfunction such as stress-induced cardiomyopathy (Takotsubo). In this case, initial labs showed elevated high-sensitivity troponin and pro-BNP alongside new-onset atrial fibrillation during an episode of severe metabolic acidosis and hypovolemia. However, advanced imaging clarified the etiology: echocardiography preserved ejection fraction despite some regional wall motion abnormalities on cardiac MRI suggestive of edema without definitive late gadolinium enhancement indicative of fibrosis or infarction. As her systemic condition improved with immunosuppression and fluid resuscitation, cardiac biomarkers normalized without evidence of permanent structural damage or scarring on follow-up imaging. This supported a diagnosis of reversible stress-related myocardial strain rather than primary autoimmune myocarditis driving her respiratory compromise, reinforcing that her primary pathology remained confined to skeletal muscle systems consistent with IMNM.

## Diagnostic Workup and Multimodal Evidence Synthesis

Establishing the diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) in this case required a rigorous synthesis of longitudinal laboratory data, advanced imaging, and definitive histopathological confirmation, particularly given the absence of classic myositis-associated autoantibodies. The laboratory trajectory provided the first critical clue distinguishing this condition from simple drug toxicity or rhabdomyolysis. While the patient initially presented with a modest creatine kinase (CK) elevation of 337 U/L shortly after her first dose of semaglutide—likely reflecting acute dehydration or minor muscle stress—the subsequent four-month course revealed a dramatic 12-fold increase in CK, peaking at 3992 U/L. This progressive escalation occurred despite the cessation of the offending agent, a timeline inconsistent with direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, which typically resolves rapidly upon withdrawal. Concurrently, inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) were mildly elevated, supporting an active inflammatory process rather than pure metabolic derangement. Crucially, the patient's transaminases (AST and ALT) were elevated; however, the historical context of biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and the persistence of enzyme elevations even after CK normalization indicated that these values reflected chronic hepatic steatosis rather than muscle breakdown.

Serological profiling played a pivotal role in narrowing the differential diagnosis by systematically excluding common autoimmune etiologies. The patient tested negative for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and a comprehensive myositis panel, including anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies, which are hallmarks of statin-associated necrotizing myopathy and primary IMNM, respectively. Additionally, antibodies associated with interstitial lung disease overlap syndromes (such as Jo-1, PL-7, PL-12) and other connective tissue diseases were absent. Although a low-titer positive anti-SS-B antibody was noted in isolation without anti-SS-A or ANA positivity, this finding lacked specific clinical correlation to explain the severe myopathy and did not alter the working diagnosis toward Sjögren's syndrome or other overlap conditions. This "seronegative" status is clinically significant, as approximately one-third of IMNM cases lack identifiable autoantibodies, necessitating reliance on histological evidence for confirmation.

Advanced imaging further localized the pathology and assessed extra-muscular involvement. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the bilateral femurs demonstrated symmetric muscular edema predominantly affecting proximal muscle groups, specifically the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles. This pattern of symmetric proximal involvement is characteristic of inflammatory myopathies and helped guide the site selection for biopsy away from areas that might show only age-related or disuse atrophy changes. Furthermore, cardiac evaluation via echocardiography and cardiac MRI revealed mild regional hypokinesis and myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement or significant scarring. While these findings raised concern for concurrent myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy—a known complication in severe systemic inflammation—they ultimately normalized on follow-up imaging alongside clinical recovery, suggesting they were secondary to the systemic inflammatory state rather than primary cardiac autoimmune pathology.

The diagnostic gold standard was achieved through muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis, which provided unequivocal evidence of necrotizing myopathy with minimal lymphocytic inflammation. Histological examination revealed dispersed necrotic fibers accompanied by signs of regeneration, confirming ongoing muscle injury and repair cycles. Unlike polymyositis or dermatomyositis, which are characterized by dense endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates attacking viable fibers, this biopsy exhibited a paucity of T-cells (rare CD3-positive cells). Instead, immunohistochemical staining highlighted extensive macrophage infiltration marked by CD68 positivity surrounding necrotic fibers with clear evidence of myophagocytosis—the engulfment of dead muscle fibers by macrophages. This macrophage-dominant pattern is pathognomonic for IMNM. Additionally, staining for the membrane attack complex (MAC) was positive on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, indicating complement-mediated cytotoxicity as a key mechanism of fiber destruction even in seronegative patients. Together with negative liver biopsy findings that ruled out worsening hepatitis as a cause for systemic decline, these multimodal findings solidified the diagnosis of semaglutide-associated IMNM in a seronegative patient lacking classic autoimmune markers but exhibiting distinct histopathologic features responsive to immunomodulation.
