# Beyond the Stomach: When GLP-1 Agonists Trigger Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy

We've all seen the headlines and heard the chatter about GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide and tirzepatide. They are revolutionizing the management of obesity and type 2 diabetes, but they aren't without their side effects. Most of us are familiar with the GI misery—nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea—that often leads to dose reductions or discontinuation. We even know that these agents can cause sarcopenia or, in rare instances, direct toxic rhabdomyolysis that spikes CK levels acutely and resolves rapidly once the drug is stopped.

But what happens when a patient presents not with acute collapse, but with a slow, progressive march toward paralysis four months *after* a single dose? What if that "muscle issue" isn't just toxicity, but a full-blown autoimmune storm?

This week, we're diving into a fascinating case report by Lam et al., detailing the first probable link between semaglutide and **Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM)**. It's a reminder that in the era of weight-loss wonder drugs, we need to keep our index of suspicion high for things that don't fit the standard textbook timeline.

## The Case: A Delayed Reaction

Let's meet our patient: a woman in her 60s with obesity, NAFLD, hypertension, and hypothyroidism. Three years prior to her current crisis, she had workups for elevated liver enzymes which turned out to be steatohepatitis. Two and a half years ago, she finally started semaglutide.

Here is where it gets interesting. After her **first ever dose** (0.25 mg), she didn't just get nauseous; she got sick enough to require ICU-level care for dehydration, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis within 48 hours. She recovered from this acute hit with fluids and supportive care. Her CK at that moment was mildly elevated at 337 U/L—unremarkable for rhabdo.

Fast forward four months. She wasn't getting better; she was getting worse. She developed profound proximal muscle weakness, couldn't stand or sit upright without help, and struggled with dysphagia and dysphonia. When she finally made it to the ER (via transfer), her CK was skyrocketing at **3992 U/L**.

This timeline should immediately set off alarm bells for anyone thinking "drug-induced rhabdomyolysis." Classic toxic rhabdo usually peaks early and falls fast once the toxin is removed. Here, the patient had been off semaglutide for four months when her CK peaked. This lag time suggests an immune-mediated process rather than direct cellular toxicity.

## The Workup: Seronegative but Histologically Definitive

The diagnostic journey here mirrors what many of us see in complex neuromuscular cases: lots of noise in the labs until you hit gold-standard tissue diagnosis.

Her myositis panel came back essentially blank. No anti-HMGCR (the classic statin-associated antibody), no anti-SRP, no antisynthetase syndrome markers (Jo-1, etc.), and even negative ANA except for an isolated low-titer SS-B (which is likely an epiphenomenon given the negative SSA). About one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative, so we can't rule out IMNM based on bloodwork alone—a crucial pearl for emergency physicians who might stop searching if antibodies are missing.

The clincher was the muscle biopsy of the vastus lateralis. Pathology showed dispersed necrotic fibers with prominent macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) but notably few lymphocytes—the hallmark "paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates" that defines necrotizing myopathy as distinct from polymyositis or dermatomyositis. Immunohistochemistry confirmed membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, confirming an active immune attack on muscle membranes even without circulating autoantibodies detectable on standard panels.

Interestingly, she also had some cardiac involvement—mild myocardial edema on MRI and transient troponin elevation—but no structural damage or late gadolinium enhancement suggestive of permanent infarction or fibrosis. This highlights that IMNM isn't always purely skeletal; systemic involvement can occur even when serology is silent.

## Treatment Strategy: Don't Just Stop the Drug

If this were simple toxic rhabdomyolysis from tirzepatide (as reported recently), stopping the drug would have been curative within days. Our patient needed more aggressive intervention because her immune system had already crossed its own threshold into autoimmunity.

She received pulse-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone tapering over two months. Crucially, due to persistent severe weakness despite steroids alone, she required **IVIG** (2 g/kg split over 4 days). This dual approach is often necessary in severe IMNM cases to halt antibody production and modulate inflammation rapidly.

The outcome was gratifying but instructive: she normalized her CK within two weeks of starting treatment and regained her baseline functional status within 10 months—all while off immunosuppression for eight months prior to her last follow-up visit compared to idiopathic IMNM which often requires long-term maintenance therapy like methotrexate or azathioprine). This rapid remission supports the hypothesis that removing the trigger (semaglutide) allowed her immune system to reset faster than it would have in spontaneous idiopathic cases—but only *because* we intervened aggressively with immunosuppression during the flare.

# Seronegative IMNM: Navigating Diagnosis When Autoantibodies Are Missing

We've all been there: a patient presents with crushing proximal weakness, sky-high creatine kinase (CK) levels, and a terrifyingly clear picture of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) on muscle biopsy. You reach for your mental checklist, order the myositis panel, and wait anxiously for the results. Then comes the call: "Everything's negative." No anti-HMGCR. No anti-SRP. Just... silence.

This diagnostic dilemma isn't just theoretical; it's happening right now in our emergency departments and clinics, particularly as new classes of metabolic drugs flood the market. A fascinating new case report highlights exactly this scenario—a woman in her 60s who developed severe IMNM shortly after a *single* dose of semaglutide, yet tested completely negative for the hallmark autoantibodies usually associated with necrotizing myopathy.

This case forces us to confront a critical reality: **a negative antibody panel does not rule out IMNM.** In fact, up to one-third of patients with this devastating condition are seronegative. Let's dive into what happened here, why the biopsy matters more than the bloodwork in these moments, and how we manage patients when the classic biomarkers go missing.

### The Semaglutide Trigger: More Than Just GI Distress?

The patient was a woman in her 60s with obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Two and a half years after her NAFLD diagnosis, she was started on semaglutide for weight management. The timeline is striking—and concerning for anyone prescribing GLP-1 receptor agonists today.

Within hours of that *first* 0.25 mg dose, she developed severe nausea and vomiting so profound she required hospitalization for acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis. She recovered from the acute dehydration but was left deconditioned. Over the next four months, however, she didn't just fail to regain strength; she deteriorated rapidly. She became unable to sit or stand without assistance and developed progressive dysphagia and dysphonia—red flags for bulbar involvement that should immediately ring alarm bells for neuromuscular pathology.

When she finally presented to neurology four months post-exposure, her CK had skyrocketed from a modest 337 U/L at her initial admission to nearly **4,000 U/L**. She had lost significant muscle mass and function despite having stopped the drug months prior. If this were simple drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (like we've seen occasionally with other agents), we would expect CK levels to normalize quickly once the toxin is removed and hydration is optimized. Instead, her disease was progressing aggressively without further exposure to the trigger.

### The Diagnostic Conundrum: Seronegative but Histologically Positive

Here is where the case gets tricky for us diagnosticians. The workup was exhaustive. They ran every myositis panel available: anti-Jo-1, PL-7/12, MDA-5, TIF1-gamma, NXP-2—and crucially, both **anti-HMGCR** and **anti-SRP**, which are the heavy hitters for necrotizing myopathy. All came back negative. Even an antinuclear antibody (ANA) screen was negative (save for a lone, isolated SS-B positivity that seemed clinically irrelevant given the absence of SS-A or other lupus features).

So, do you stop looking? Absolutely not. This is where the **muscle biopsy** becomes your best friend and your definitive diagnostic tool when serology fails to cooperate.

The biopsy of her vastus lateralis told a story that blood tests couldn't capture yet:
*   Dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers were abundant.
*   Immunohistochemistry showed prominent macrophage infiltration (CD68+) with myophagocytosis—the body trying to clean up dead muscle fibers.
*   Crucially, there was a **paucity of lymphocytes** (rare CD3+ cells).
*   Membrane attack complex deposition was noted on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers.

These findings are pathognomonic for **Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy**. The distinction between "toxic" injury (which clears up fast) and "immune-mediated" injury (which persists due to autoimmunity triggered by the drug) lies in this histology: ongoing necrosis driven by macrophages and complement activation rather than primary T-cell invasion or direct toxicity alone. Even though she lacked the specific antibodies we associate with statin-induced IMNM (anti-HMGCR), her tissue pathology confirmed an autoimmune process that wouldn't resolve on its own.

### Management Without Biomarkers

So how do you treat a patient who looks like they have IMNM but lacks the "proof" of antibodies? You treat based on phenotype and histology just as aggressively as you would if they were seropositive.

This patient received high-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg/day. Because her weakness remained severe despite steroids alone, IVIG (2 g/kg) was added early in her course—a move that proved pivotal given her refractory nature initially compared to standard steroid-responsive myositides like dermatomyositis or polymyositis which sometimes respond faster to monotherapy alone in milder forms—but here she needed punchy dual therapy early on due to severity of bulbar involvement and rapid decline? # The Statin Parallel: What Semaglutide Teaches Us About Drug-Induced Autoimmunity

We've all seen the classic "drug-induced" myopathy story: a patient starts a statin, gets some aches, their CK spikes into the thousands, they stop the pill, and everything goes back to normal within days. That's toxic myopathy. We know the drill. But what happens when the drug stops working as the *cause* and instead acts as the *trigger* for a self-perpetuating autoimmune firestorm?

That is exactly what makes this newly reported case from Lam et al. so fascinating—and slightly terrifying for anyone prescribing GLP-1 receptor agonists. We are looking at a woman in her 60s who received **a single dose** of semaglutide. Four months later, she wasn't just recovering; she was collapsing with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. Her Creatine Kinase (CK) didn't just spike; it climbed twelve-fold over four months to nearly 4,000 U/L. This isn't acute rhabdomyolysis. This is **Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM)**, and it appears to be the first documented case linking this specific pathology to semaglutide.

### Beyond the Acute Toxic Effect
To understand why this case hits differently than your average adverse event report, you have to look at the timeline and the histology. When we think of drug-induced muscle injury with GLP-1s (or tirzepatide), we usually think of volume depletion leading to acute rhabdo or direct toxicity causing rapid CK normalization upon cessation. In a previous tirzepatide case cited in the literature, CK levels normalized in days after stopping the drug without immunosuppression.

This patient did something different. She stopped semaglutide immediately after her first dose due to severe GI symptoms and renal issues from dehydration. Yet, her muscle destruction accelerated for four months *after* the drug was cleared from her system. By the time she presented for definitive workup, she couldn't stand or sit upright without assistance. The muscle biopsy confirmed it: scattered necrotic fibers with macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) and a distinct lack of significant lymphocytic infiltrate—classic features of IMNM. Crucially, unlike many other autoimmune conditions, she tested negative for every major myositis-specific antibody on her panel (including anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP). She was seronegative IMNM.

### The Statin Ghost
Here is where the historical parallel becomes clinically critical. If you read older literature on statins before we understood autoimmunity, you'll see patients labeled with "toxic myopathy" who never got better after stopping statins because they actually had **Statin-Associated Autoimmune Myopathy (SAAM)**. They had developed anti-HMGCR antibodies triggered by the drug exposure. Even though they weren't on statins anymore, their immune system kept attacking their muscle tissue because it now recognized HMGCR as foreign antigenically altered or exposed by the drug interaction.

This semaglutide case feels like we are seeing SAAM all over again, but with a newer class of metabolic drugs and potentially novel antigens we haven't identified yet (since she was seronegative). The key lesson here is that **the absence of ongoing drug exposure does not guarantee resolution.** If a patient on a GLP-1 agonist presents with unexplained proximal weakness and rising CK weeks or months after starting (or even shortly after stopping) therapy, you must consider that you might be dealing with an autoimmune process that requires immunosuppression, not just supportive care or fluid resuscitation.

### Diagnosis and Management Pearls
Diagnosing this entity is tricky because it mimics idiopathic IMNM so closely. The patient had no rash (ruling out dermatomyositis), no interstitial lung disease on HRCT, and no specific autoantibodies. The diagnosis relied heavily on the temporal relationship ("reasonable time relationship" per WHO criteria) and exclusion of other causes like thyroid dysfunction or metabolic myopathies.

Management mirrored severe idiopathic IMNM protocols rather than simple toxic reactions:
*   **High-dose steroids:** Pulse IV methylprednisolone followed by tapering oral prednisone.
*   **IVIG:** Administered early due to severity (bulbar involvement/dysphagia) and refractory nature despite initial steroid pulse therapy in some contexts (though here it was part of an aggressive upfront regimen).
*   **Avoidance:** Absolute avoidance of GLP-1 RAs going forward is mandatory; rechallenge would be unethical given the severity of her reaction.

The outcome was surprisingly favorable compared to many chronic autoimmune cases: she recovered full function within 10 months and was successfully weaned off all immunosuppression within 2 months of treatment initiation once her CK normalized—a course suggesting that while drug-triggered IMNM can be severe, early recognition leads to excellent reversibility if caught before permanent disability sets in.

### Take-Home Points for the Clinician
*   **Think Beyond Toxicity:** Don't assume muscle weakness in a diabetic/obese patient starting weight-loss meds is just deconditioning or acute rhabdo from dehydration if symptoms persist or worsen after stopping the agent.
*   **The Timeline Matters:** A delayed onset or progressive worsening weeks/months after initiating (or ceasing) a GLP-1 RA should raise suspicion for immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), mirroring the pathophysiology seen in statin-associated autoimmune myopathy (SAAM).
*   **Seronegativity Doesn't Rule It Out:** Up to one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative for standard panels (anti-HMGCR/SRP). Do not dismiss IMNM solely based on negative antibody testing if clinical suspicion is high; rely on biopsy findings showing necrosis with macrophage dominance and minimal T-cell infiltrate.
*   **Treatment Paradigm Shift:** Unlike toxic rhabdomyolysis which resolves with fluids and rest, suspected drug-triggered IMNM requires prompt immunosuppression (corticosteroids ± IVIG) alongside strict avoidance of the offending agent to prevent permanent functional loss.



# Red Flags in Metabolic Therapy: When "Deconditioning" is Actually Necrotizing Myopathy

We've all seen the patient walking through the ED doors: someone in their 60s with obesity, recently started on a GLP-1 receptor agonist like semaglutide for weight management, complaining of fatigue, nausea, and general "weakness." The narrative is often straightforward. They lost weight; they're less active; maybe they got a bit dehydrated from the GI side effects. It's easy to chalk it up to deconditioning or a lingering viral bug.

But what if that "generalized weakness" isn't just being out of shape? What if the patient is developing **immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)**?

This week, we're diving into a sobering case report by Lam et al. that challenges our assumptions about GLP-1 safety profiles. It describes the first reported case of IMNM temporally linked to semaglutide use. The kicker? The patient didn't develop massive rhabdomyolysis immediately after the first dose. Instead, she developed a slow-burning, progressive neuromuscular catastrophe characterized by **progressive dysphagia and dysphonia**—symptoms that are terrifyingly specific when you know what to look for.

## The Case: A 4-Month Slow Burn

Let's walk through the timeline, because the chronology here is critical for understanding why this gets missed initially.

A woman in her 60s with a history of obesity and NAFLD started semaglutide (0.25 mg). Within hours of that *single* dose, she crashed hard: intractable nausea/vomiting, acute renal failure from dehydration, and metabolic acidosis requiring ICU-level care and non-invasive ventilation. Crucially, her Creatine Kinase (CK) at that moment was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L. She was discharged after five days once her kidneys recovered and her GI symptoms settled.

Here is where things get tricky. Over the next **four months**, she didn't bounce back to baseline. Instead, she declined precipitously. She couldn't stand or sit upright without assistance. Then came the red flags that should have rung every alarm bell in your head: **difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids (dysphagia)** and **voice changes (dysphonia)**.

By month four, her CK had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L (peak 3992). A muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis: necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration but a *paucity* of lymphocytes—the histologic hallmark of IMNM. Interestingly, she was seronegative for the usual suspects (anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP were negative), placing her in that mysterious ~30% of IMNM cases without known autoantibodies.

She required high-dose steroids and IVIG to turn the tide but eventually made a full recovery once immunosuppression was tapered and semaglutide was permanently avoided.

## Why This Matters for Emergency Medicine

Why am I writing this for EM docs? Because the initial presentation of GLP-1 toxicity usually involves vomiting and dehydration—classic stuff we manage easily. But this case highlights a delayed phenotypic evolution where **bulbar symptoms** masquerade as something benign or unrelated.

When an obese patient on weight-loss meds presents with unexplained proximal weakness *plus* new-onset dysphagia or dysphonia, you cannot dismiss it as simple deconditioning or "just getting older." In the context of IMNM, bulbar involvement signals aggressive disease that can lead to aspiration pneumonia and respiratory failure if missed early.

The distinction between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (which typically resolves quickly upon stopping the drug) and drug-triggered IMNM is vital. In classic rhabdo triggered by toxins like statins or extreme exertion, CK spikes fast but falls fast once the insult stops. In this case—and likely in others—we saw a **12-fold rise in CK over four months** *after* exposure ceased (or rather, after a single exposure). This lag time suggests an autoimmune mechanism has been ignited, not just direct cellular toxicity requiring washout time alone.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Seronegativity

One of the most frustrating aspects of modern autoimmune medicine is seronegativity. We love our panels: Anti-Jo-1, Anti-MDA5, Anti-HMGCR... but roughly one-third of IMNM patients don't have these antibodies yet identified by current assays.

In this case report, every standard myositis panel came back negative except for a lone low-titer SS-B antibody (which lacked clinical correlation for Sjogren's). If you rely solely on antibody testing to rule out myopathy in these patients, you will miss them. You must trust the clinical picture:
*   Proximal muscle weakness disproportionate to age/activity level
*   Bulbar symptoms (swallowing/voice)
*   Disproportionately high CK relative to acute trauma
*   Muscle MRI showing edema
*   Biopsy showing necrosis with minimal inflammation

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

If you see an obese patient on GLP-1 agonists presenting with weakness or swallowing difficulties tomorrow morning, run through this mental checklist:

*   **Don't ignore Bulbar Signs:** New dysphagia or dysphonia in a patient on weight-loss medication is NOT normal aging or mild deconditioning until proven otherwise; it is a sign of potential life-threatening myopathy until ruled out by neurology/rheumatology workup.
*   **Time Course Matters:** If CK continues to climb weeks or months after stopping the offending agent (or after an initial acute reaction), suspect an immune-mediated process rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.
*   **Seronegative Doesn't Mean Negative:** Up to 30% of IMNM cases lack identifiable autoantibodies (like HMGCR or SRP). Do not let a negative myositis panel dissuade you from pursuing biopsy if clinical suspicion remains high based on MRI and enzyme trends.
*   **Biopsy is King:** When labs are equivocal but clinical suspicion is high for necrotizing myopathy (proximal weakness + high CK), push for early muscle biopsy looking for necrosis with macrophage predominance rather than lymphocytic infiltration (as seen in polymyositis).

# Therapeutic Triage: Why This Patient Needed IVIG When Others Might Not

We usually think of "Ozempic" and its cousins as the miracle workers for weight loss and glycemic control. But every now and then, a case report lands on your desk that forces you to pause and reconsider the entire risk-benefit calculus of these GLP-1 receptor agonists. Today, we're dissecting a fascinating case from *BMJ Case Reports* that bridges the gap between metabolic medicine and neuromuscular emergencies: **Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM)** triggered by a single dose of semaglutide.

What makes this case particularly juicy—and terrifying—isn't just the rarity of the association, but the specific therapeutic trajectory required to save this patient's function. This wasn't a simple case of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis where you flush with fluids and pray; it was a full-blown autoimmune storm requiring aggressive dual-modality immunosuppression. If you manage anything from emergency departments to rheumatology clinics, understanding why this patient needed IVIG when others might not is crucial.

## The Timeline That Doesn't Add Up (At First Glance)

Let's walk through the timeline, because it holds the key to our differential diagnosis. A woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD receives her very first dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg). Within hours, she crashes with nausea, vomiting, acute kidney injury, and metabolic acidosis. She gets hydrated, stabilizes, and goes home with a Creatine Kinase (CK) level of only 337 U/L—mildly elevated, certainly not rhabdo-levels.

Fast forward four months. She isn't getting better; she's getting worse. She presents with profound proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK that has skyrocketed to **3992 U/L**.

Here is the pivot point: In classic toxic rhabdomyolysis (like we saw in a recent tirzepatide case report), CK peaks acutely and drops precipitously within days of stopping the drug once hydration is optimized. It does *not* climb 12-fold over four months after a single exposure while off the medication. This delayed progression signaled that we weren't dealing with direct toxicity anymore; we were dealing with an immune system that had lost its brakes. The muscle biopsy confirmed it: necrotic fibers, myophagocytosis by macrophages (CD68+), and complement deposition (MAC+), but notably **paucity** of lymphocytes. This is the histologic signature of IMNM.

## Seronegative Dilemma: To Treat or Not to Treat Aggressively?

One of the biggest traps in myopathy management is waiting for an antibody result before acting. This patient was seronegative across the board—negative for HMGCR (the usual suspect in statin-associated necrotizing myopathy), negative for SRP, negative for Jo-1, Mi-2—you name it. Only a low-titer SS-B was positive, which was likely an incidental finding given her negative ANA/SS-A profile.

In idiopathic IMNM, especially when seronegative or anti-HMGCR positive, we know these patients often require long-term, heavy-duty immunosuppression (steroids + methotrexate/mycophenolate/rituximab) because their disease doesn't just stop when you remove a trigger; they have an intrinsic autoimmune loop running hot.

However, this case offered us a critical teaching moment on **drug-induced vs. idiopathic** trajectories. Because there was such a tight temporal link to semaglutide (despite the delay in symptom onset) and no other autoimmune stigmata (no rash, no ILD on CT chest), the team made the call to go "big guns" early but planned for a rapid exit strategy. They didn't wait around for second-line agents like azathioprine or methotrexate to kick in over weeks or months. They went straight for high-dose pulse steroids **plus** IVIG immediately upon transfer to tertiary care due to her severe bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia) and massive CK load.

## The Decision Matrix: Why Dual Therapy?

So why add IVIG? Why not just hammer her with methylprednisolone?

This patient had signs suggesting impending respiratory compromise via bulbar weakness and significant functional disability (unable to stand or sit without assistance). In severe necrotizing myopathies with rapid progression or life-threatening complications (swallowing difficulties leading to aspiration risk), monotherapy can sometimes feel too slow to reverse the cytokine cascade driving myofiber destruction.

The literature on IMNM supports that refractory cases or those with severe initial presentation benefit from upfront combination therapy:
*   **Steroids:** For broad anti-inflammatory suppression.
*   **IVIG:** Provides neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies (even if undetectable on standard panels) and modulates Fc receptors on macrophages—the very cells eating her muscle fibers seen on biopsy.

The result? A dramatic drop in CK within two weeks and restoration of function that allowed for rapid tapering later on. Contrast this with idiopathic HMGCR-positive myopathy patients who often relapse quickly upon steroid tapering; this patient stayed off immunosuppression entirely eight months later with normal function and enzymes back at baseline simply by avoiding semaglutide forever more after one dose!

## Take-Home Pearls for Your Next Shift

This case serves as both a warning bell for prescribers and a roadmap for managers facing similar presentations:

*   **Time Course Matters:** Drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically resolves quickly after cessation; if CK continues to climb weeks or months after stopping a GLP-1 RA (or any new med), think **IMNM**, not just toxicity.
*   **"Seronegative" Does Not Mean Benign:** Do not let negative autoantibody panels dissuade you from treating severe necrotizing myopathy aggressively if clinical suspicion is high based on biopsy findings (macrophage-rich necrosis).
*   **Escalate Early in Severe Cases:** For patients presenting with bulbar symptoms or profound weakness in suspected drug-triggered IMNM, consider upfront dual therapy (High-dose Steroids + IVIG) rather than waiting for steroid response alone to prevent permanent disability or airway issues.
*   **Prognosis Differs by Etiology:** Unlike idiopathic IMNM which often requires chronic maintenance therapy, true drug-induced IMNM may resolve completely with removal of the trigger plus short-course immunosuppression ("hit hard then get out"). Recognizing this distinction prevents overtreatment but requires confidence in early intervention during the acute phase.
*   **Don't Ignore Transaminases:** Elevated AST/ALT in this context are almost always muscle-derived until proven otherwise; rely on LDH/CK ratios and clinical context rather than assuming primary liver pathology unless biopsy proves otherwise (as seen here where liver biopsy confirmed only steatosis).
