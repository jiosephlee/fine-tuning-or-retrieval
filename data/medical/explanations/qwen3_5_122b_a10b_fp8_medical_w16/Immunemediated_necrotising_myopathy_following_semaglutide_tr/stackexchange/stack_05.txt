Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] In evaluating a patient with new-onset myopathy and elevated transaminases (AST/ALT), what clinical clues help differentiate primary muscle pathology from concurrent hepatic steatosis or other liver etiologies?

In evaluating a patient presenting with new-onset myopathy and elevated transaminases, the critical distinction lies in determining whether the enzyme elevation reflects primary hepatic injury or is merely a "red herring" resulting from massive skeletal muscle breakdown. As illustrated in this case of semaglutide-associated immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), both AST and ALT are abundant in skeletal muscle; therefore, their elevation does not automatically imply liver pathology.

The primary clinical clue differentiating these entities is the magnitude of the creatine kinase (CK) elevation relative to the transaminases. In pure hepatic disease, AST and ALT elevations are typically modest, rarely exceeding ten times the upper limit of normal unless there is fulminant failure. Conversely, in significant myopathies like IMNM, CK levels often soar into the thousands—this patient's CK peaked at 3992 U/L, while her AST and ALT were only mildly elevated (191 U/L and 165 U/L, respectively). When transaminase elevation appears disproportionate to clinical signs of liver dysfunction but correlates tightly with a disproportionately higher CK level, muscle pathology must be considered the primary driver.

Furthermore, historical context and specific biomarkers provide essential differentiation. In this case, the patient had documented biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) three years prior to symptom onset, explaining her baseline transaminitis. Crucially, her liver enzymes remained stable even after her CK normalized following treatment for IMNM, confirming that her chronic AST/ALT elevation was hepatic in origin (steatosis) and unrelated to the acute myopathic process. Additionally, while aldolase is another useful marker less common in the liver than muscle, its utility here is secondary to the dramatic CK spike. Finally, unlike drug-induced rhabdomyolysis where enzymes normalize rapidly upon cessation of the offending agent—as seen with tirzepatide-associated cases—the progressive nature of this patient's weakness and rising CK over four months post-exposure signaled an evolving autoimmune process requiring immunosuppression rather than simple withdrawal alone. Thus, when faced with elevated transaminases and weakness, always check a CK; if it is markedly elevated out of proportion to liver synthetic function or known hepatic history, suspect myopathy first.
