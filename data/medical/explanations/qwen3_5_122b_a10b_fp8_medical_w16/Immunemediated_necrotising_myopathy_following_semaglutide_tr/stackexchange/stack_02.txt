Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the clinical trajectory of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically differ from the presentation of IMNM triggered by a medication like semaglutide, particularly regarding Creatine Kinase (CK) trends after drug cessation?

The critical distinction between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and medication-triggered immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) lies in the temporal evolution of creatine kinase (CK) levels following the cessation of the offending agent.

In classic **drug-induced rhabdomyolysis**, the injury is a direct toxic effect on the myocyte membrane. As illustrated by the referenced case comparing semaglutide-associated IMNM to a concurrent tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis case, the latter presents with an acute, massive spike in CK that begins to normalize within days of stopping the drug and initiating supportive care (hydration), without requiring immunosuppression. The trajectory is self-limiting once the toxin is removed.

Conversely, **IMNM** represents an autoimmune process that persists even after the trigger is withdrawn. In our case report, the patient received only a single dose of semaglutide yet exhibited a progressive clinical decline over four months, with CK levels rising 12-fold from 337 U/L to nearly 4,000 U/L *after* drug exposure ceased. Unlike simple toxicity, where removal of the agent halts damage, IMNM involves ongoing autoantibody-mediated or cell-mediated destruction (often anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP positive, though this patient was seronegative). Consequently, CK levels in IMNM do not spontaneously resolve upon drug discontinuation; they require specific immunomodulation—such as corticosteroids and IVIG—to suppress the inflammatory cascade. In this patient, normalization of CK and functional recovery occurred only after immunosuppressive therapy was initiated, confirming that the pathology had transitioned from a potential trigger event to a sustained autoimmune state.

Therefore, when evaluating myopathy in a patient recently exposed to a new medication: if CK levels fail to trend downward within days of cessation or continue to rise over weeks despite withdrawal of the drug, one must suspect an evolving immune-mediated etiology like IMNM rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.
