Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# The Great Mimicker: When 'Tuberculosis' is Actually a Pancreatic Leak

Imagine a pediatric patient presenting with recurrent, massive, bloody pleural effusions. You've done the thoracentesis twice, the fluid is dark red, and the child keeps coming back for breathlessness and chest pain. Now, add a family history of tuberculosis (Koch's contact) and a mildly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid. What's your working diagnosis? For many of us, the rabbit hole leads straight down the path of empiric anti-tubercular therapy (ATT).

This was exactly the trap set in a recently published case report involving a young boy who spent weeks bouncing between admissions, getting drained repeatedly, and even starting ATT—all while missing the true culprit hiding in his abdomen. The final diagnosis wasn't an infectious nightmare or a malignancy; it was a **pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)** triggered by a forgotten bicycle accident from a month prior.

This case is a stark reminder that when you hear "hemorrhagic pleural effusion" in a child with respiratory distress, you must keep your eyes on two very different anatomical compartments: the chest *and* the belly.

## The Pulmonary Mask

The term "pulmonary mask" isn't just poetic license; it's a clinical reality for PPFs. In this specific case, the pathology originated from blunt abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handlebar impact. While pancreatic injuries in children are often associated with high-energy mechanisms or underlying genetic predispositions (like *PRSS1* or *SPINK1* mutations), this boy had no initial abdominal complaints. Instead, pancreatic enzymes tracked retroperitoneally, ascended through the esophageal hiatus, breached the diaphragm, and flooded his right pleural space.

The result was classic respiratory distress: tachypnea, hypoxia (saturation dipping to 88%), and stony dullness on percussion. Because 68% of PPF cases present primarily with respiratory symptoms rather than abdominal pain, clinicians naturally focus their energy on ruling out pneumonia, empyema, TB, or malignancy. In this boy's case, the "red herrings" were potent:
*   **Dark Red Fluid:** Often suggests hemothorax or malignancy, but here it represented enzymatically active pancreatic fluid mixed with blood from tissue irritation.
*   **Elevated ADA:** Usually the hallmark of TB pleurisy, yet here it was falsely reassuring enough to start ATT before confirming PCR/Genexpert results were negative.
*   **Recurrent Effusions:** After every thoracentesis provided temporary relief ("the tank emptied"), he would return days later because the faucet—the leaking pancreatic duct—was still wide open.

It took three separate admissions and persistent digging into the history to uncover that one-month-old bicycle trauma. Had that detail surfaced earlier, we might have spared him unnecessary toxic medications and invasive procedures focused solely on his lungs.

## The Amylase Clue That Changes Everything

So how do we spot this mimic before it becomes a multi-week odyssey? The answer lies in chemistry as much as imaging. While elevated amylase can be seen in esophageal rupture (Boerhaave's) and certain malignancies, **pleural fluid amylase** is your most powerful discriminator against TB and simple parapneumonic effusions.

In this case report, once MRCP finally visualized the fistula tract tracking from the pancreatic tail to the chest wall, they measured what really mattered:
*   **Pleural Fluid Amylase:** 6118 U/L
*   **Pleural Fluid Lipase:** 7211 U/L

These numbers aren't just "elevated"; they are astronomical compared to serum levels or typical inflammatory markers. A pleural fluid amylase level >1000 IU/L is widely considered pathognomonic for PPF when combined with compatible imaging. Crucially, distinguishing between salivary-type amylase (seen in Boerhaave's) and pancreatic-type amylase can further refine your differential if iso-electric focusing is available. But frankly, if you see an amylase level in the thousands in a bloody effusion with no obvious esophageal perforation symptoms (like violent vomiting), stop thinking about lungs and start thinking about pancreas immediately.

## From Drainage to Definitive Management

Once diagnosed via MRCP—which offered superior visualization of the fistulous tract compared to CT—the management strategy shifted entirely from infectious control to metabolic suppression of the pancreas. The team initiated conservative medical management first-line:
1.  **Bowel Rest (NPO):** To minimize pancreatic stimulation via food intake.
2.  **Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN):** To maintain caloric needs without gut activation.
3.  **Octreotide Infusion:** At 30 µg/kg/day to suppress exocrine secretion effectively acting as a chemical clamp on the leaky pipe.
4.  **Enzyme Replacement Therapy:** To aid digestion during recovery despite NPO status initially holding off enteral feeding logic somewhat counter-intuitively until stability was reached (though protocols vary).

Over just 10 days of this regimen plus continued chest tube drainage for decompression rather than cure alone, output ceased completely. No surgery was required initially; no endoscopic stenting was needed immediately because conservative measures succeeded where antibiotics failed miserably against nature itself! Success rates for conservative management hover around low teens historically but appear higher when caught early without ductal disruption requiring mechanical repair—though adult data suggests ~50% closure rates eventually regardless! Key takeaway here though remains clear though... don't rush straight to laparotomy unless absolutely necessary!

## Amylase in the Chest: Decoding the Pathognomonic Threshold

We've all been there: a pediatric patient rolls into the ED or PICU with recurrent, massive pleural effusions. They're tachypneic, hypoxic, and in pain. You tap them, they improve, and then—bam—they come back three days later with the exact same problem. The initial reflex? It's almost always pulmonary. Is it pneumonia? Is it a complicated parapneumonic effusion? Or, given the global prevalence of TB in many regions, could it be tuberculosis?

This was exactly the trajectory for a teenage boy presenting with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions and respiratory distress. After two failed aspirations and a brief course of empiric anti-tubercular therapy (which turned out to be a red herring), the team dug deeper into the history. The breakthrough wasn't a new scan or a fancy biomarker; it was a forgotten detail: **bicycle handlebar trauma** one month prior.

When they finally connected that abdominal trauma to his thoracic symptoms and ran the numbers on his pleural fluid, they found something staggering: **pleural fluid amylase of 6118 U/L**. That number didn't just raise an eyebrow; it screamed "Pancreatopleural Fistula" (PPF).

In this post, we're breaking down how to stop chasing your tail when faced with recurrent hemorrhagic effusions in kids and why checking that amylase level is the single most critical step you can take before reaching for another chest tube.

### The Great Masquerade: When Abdomen Looks Like Lung

Pancreatopleural fistula is a rare beast, especially in pediatrics where fewer than 50 cases have ever been documented. It represents a "leaky pipe" scenario where pancreatic secretions escape a disrupted duct or pseudocyst (often from blunt trauma or chronic pancreatitis), track up through the diaphragm via the esophageal or aortic hiatus, and dump directly into the pleural space.

The clinical trap here is profound because **the pathology is abdominal, but the presentation is purely thoracic.** In adults, alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis is the usual culprit. In children, think trauma (like our case) or genetic predispositions (PRSS1, SPINK1 mutations). Because these kids rarely complain of epigastric pain—the classic sign of pancreatitis—we often waste precious time working up lungs while ignoring the belly. Our patient was treated for TB and suspected malignancy simply because he had bloody fluid and respiratory distress. Only when we stopped assuming it was lung disease did we find the answer in his pancreas.

### The Magic Number: Why >1000 IU/L Changes Everything

Let's talk about the lab value that saves lives here: **Pleural Fluid Amylase**.

While serum amylase might be elevated in systemic inflammation or even kidney failure, pleural fluid amylase tells a different story. In our case report, the level hit nearly **6200 U/L**. This isn't just "elevated"; it's pathognomonic for PPF once you rule out other specific causes.

Here is how to interpret those numbers clinically:
*   **>1000 IU/L:** This threshold is highly specific for PPF. While simple parapneumonic effusions or empyemas can have mildly elevated amylase (usually <3x serum levels), they rarely breach this ceiling unless there is significant tissue necrosis involving adjacent structures (which would look very different on imaging).
*   **Massive Elevation:** Levels like 6118 U/L essentially rule out infectious etiologies like empyema or uncomplicated viral/bacterial pneumonia as primary drivers of *that* specific fluid volume and composition.
*   **Isoenzyme Differentiation:** This is where things get nuanced. High amylase can also occur in esophageal perforation (Boerhaave's syndrome) or certain malignancies (lung/pancreatic adenocarcinoma). However, Boerhaave's typically presents with salivary-type amylase isoforms rather than pancreatic isoforms. Furthermore, Boerhaave's usually presents acutely with severe mediastinitis after violent retching/vomiting—a history distinct from our child's gradual onset post-trauma. Malignancy would show malignant cells on cytology and likely systemic signs like weight loss and night sweats over months, not weeks.

If you see an exudative effusion with amylase >1000 IU/L in a child with any hint of abdominal history—even remote—you must suspect PPF immediately. Do not wait for an MRI if you need to stabilize drainage; send that sample now.

# From Handlebars to Hiatus: Understanding Retroperitoneal Tracking in Trauma

We've all seen the classic "handlebar injury" in pediatrics: a kid rides their bike, slams the handlebars into their epigastrium, and lands with a bruised tummy. Usually, we worry about splenic lacerations, hepatic tears, or a blowout of the duodenum. But there is a sneaky, rare complication of blunt abdominal trauma that often masquerades as primary lung disease for weeks before we catch it: the **pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)**.

In a recent case report by Vaish et al., we see a perfect storm of this phenomenon. A previously healthy boy presents with *recurrent* right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain, and respiratory distress. He gets tapped three times. Every time, they pull out nearly a liter of dark-red fluid. The team rightly worries about tuberculosis (given family contacts) or even malignancy. They start empiric anti-TB meds. And yet, the fluid keeps coming back.

The smoking gun? A forgotten history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle just one month prior. Once that piece of history surfaced, the puzzle snapped together: this wasn't a lung problem; it was a plumbing issue originating in the pancreas and draining straight into the chest.

### The Anatomy of the Leak: Why Posterior Matters

Here is where the physiology gets fascinating and why this diagnosis is so frequently delayed. When blunt force hits the pancreas against the vertebral column, it can shear or rupture the pancreatic duct or create a pseudocyst. The critical factor determining where that enzymatic soup goes depends entirely on *where* the rupture happens relative to Gerota's fascia and the retroperitoneum.

If the tear is anterior, pancreatic juice leaks freely into the peritoneal cavity, giving you frank ascites and peritonitis—classic acute abdomen stuff. Easy to spot on exam or FAST scan.

But if—as in this case—the injury involves the **posterior aspect** of the pancreas (often near the tail), the fluid doesn't have anywhere to go but backward into the retroperitoneal space. Think of this as nature's path of least resistance. The fluid tracks superiorly along retroperitoneal planes, bypassing the diaphragm's muscular bulk by slipping through natural openings: primarily the **aortic** or **esophageal hiatus**.

Once past these hiatuses, the fluid enters the posterior mediastinum and breaches parietal pleura to accumulate in either hemithorax. While both sides are possible, right-sided effusions are disproportionately common in PPF cases (like our patient). Why? Likely because of anatomical asymmetry favoring flow toward the right pleural space via esophageal pathways and perhaps differential compliance of the diaphragmatic crura on that side.

This creates what we call a **"pulmonary mask."** The child presents with tachypnea, stony dullness to percussion, and hypoxia—all screaming "pleural effusion." Unless you ask specifically about abdominal trauma weeks ago or think outside thoracic box, you're going down rabbit holes searching for TB pneumonia or leukemia while ignoring an injured gland sitting quietly behind them stomach wall.

### Diagnostic Clues: Amylase is King (and Queen)

So how do we cut through the noise when we see recurrent bloody/serous effusions? You need two things: high suspicion and specific labs.

First, look at your pleural fluid chemistry immediately upon tap (before you assume it's hemorrhagic due to trauma). If your amylase is sky-high—think thousands—it's almost certainly pancreatic in origin. In our case report, pleural fluid amylase was **6118 U/L** and lipase was **7211 U/L**. That is not just "elevated"; that is diagnostic territory when paired with clinical context. Remember that while salivary-type amylase can be seen in some malignancies or esophageal ruptures (Boerhaave's), *pancreatic-type* amylase dominance strongly points to PPF once other causes are excluded via cytology and imaging history.

Secondly, don't rely solely on CT for visualization of the tract itself. While CT is great for seeing associated pseudocysts or calcifications (our patient had a multiloculated collection tracking from paraesophageal space to pancreatic tail), it misses actual fistulous tracts nearly half the time (**33–47%** sensitivity). This is where **MRCP** shines as our best non-invasive tool here (**70–80% sensitivity**), clearly delineating that tubular cystic collection extending from distal body/tail up through esophageal hiatus into right pleura as seen in this case. ERCP remains gold standard for therapeutic planning but carries procedural risks; MRCP gives us that roadmap safely first thing morning round-up time-wise wise choice!

### Management Pearls: Rest Before Cutting

## Pediatric vs. Adult PPF: Why Genetics Trump Alcohol in Children

We've all been there: a kid walks into the ED with significant respiratory distress, a massive pleural effusion, and a chest X-ray that screams "pulmonary problem." You tap the chest, drain a liter of dark, bloody fluid, and the child feels better—only to return three days later with the exact same presentation. This cycle repeats, leading down a rabbit hole of TB workups, malignancy screens, and repeated thoracenteses while the true culprit sits silently in the abdomen.

This is the classic trap of **Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF)**, a rare but devastating complication where pancreatic enzymes find their way from the retroperitoneum straight into the pleural space. While adults with PPF often have a decades-long history of alcohol abuse and chronic pancreatitis, the pediatric landscape tells a very different story. In children, this isn't about ethanol toxicity; it's about blunt trauma and genetic predisposition. And if you don't suspect it early, your patient will keep coming back to the ICU.

Let's look at a recent case that perfectly illustrates this diagnostic odyssey. A young boy presented with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain, and hypoxia. Initially, he looked like he had tuberculosis (given family contacts) or perhaps a malignancy causing hemorrhagic effusions. He was started on antitubercular therapy empirically—a common pitfall when faced with elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) in endemic areas. But here was the twist: every time he was drained of dark red fluid, he improved transiently before crashing again five days later.

The breakthrough didn't come from another chest scan; it came from digging deeper into the social history during his third admission. The child revealed a crucial detail buried under weeks of respiratory symptoms: **blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior.** Once that link was made, imaging shifted focus to the pancreas. A CT showed a complex collection tracking from the pancreas tail up through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. An MRCP confirmed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, and finally, pleural fluid analysis dropped the hammer: amylase levels were sky-high at 6118 U/L and lipase at 7211 U/L. The diagnosis? Trauma-induced PPF masquerading as refractory pleural disease.

What makes this case so instructive for emergency physicians and pediatricians is how distinct pediatric PPF is from its adult counterpart. In adult literature reviews (like those from Japan involving over 100 cases), nearly **99%** of PPF cases are driven by chronic alcoholic pancreatitis. These patients typically have years of ductal damage before they ever develop an effusion. They walk in with abdominal pain *and* respiratory distress because their bodies are screaming about both organs simultaneously.

In children? Alcohol is essentially non-existent as an etiology. Instead, we see two main drivers: **blunt abdominal trauma** (accounting for roughly 32% of pediatric cases) and **genetic mutations**. Hereditary pancreatitis plays a massive role here, with mutations in genes like *PRSS1*, *SPINK1*, *CFTR*, and chymotrypsin C responsible for up to 30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. The mechanism is fascinatingly mechanical yet elusive: pancreatic fluid leaks from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst (often posterior), tracks up through natural diaphragmatic openings (usually the esophageal or aortic hiatus), and floods the chest cavity via gravity and negative intrathoracic pressure. Because these kids often lack significant abdominal pain—the "pancreatic" component is masked by thoracic symptoms—they become "respiratory patients" rather than "abdominal patients."

The danger lies in this **"Pulmonary Mask."** As noted in this case report, clinicians often exhaust all thoracic possibilities before considering an abdominal source. By that time, your patient has undergone multiple invasive procedures and potentially received unnecessary medications (like anti-TB drugs). The key to unlocking this diagnosis is recognizing that **pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L** is practically pathognomonic for PPF once you rule out esophageal perforation (Boerhaave's) and malignancy (which usually show salivary-type amylase rather than pancreatic-type).

So, how do we manage these little guys? Unlike adults who might require surgical resection immediately due to advanced fibrotic changes, children are surprisingly responsive to conservative measures first line—if caught early enough. The protocol involves strict bowel rest (**NPO**), Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN), octreotide infusion to suppress pancreatic exocrine secretion, and adequate drainage via intercostal catheter or thoracocentesis. In our case report's patient, this conservative approach worked beautifully within 10 days; drainage ceased, the fistula closed, and he was discharged without needing surgery or endoscopic stenting initially. However, remember that conservative management has a relatively low success rate (~12%) in pediatrics compared to endoscopic stenting (~48-100%). If medical management fails after 2-3 weeks or if there is ductal disruption requiring decompression, ERCP with stenting becomes your best friend before jumping to surgery like distal pancreatectomy or Puestow procedures.

Ultimately, this case reinforces that when you see recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusions in a child—even one who denies current abdominal pain—you must hunt for an occult pancreatic source until proven otherwise. Don't let the "pulmonary mask" fool you into thinking this is just another TB case or idiopathic effusion. Ask about bicycle handles one month ago! Ask about family history of early-onset diabetes or pancreatitis! And always check that amylase level on the tap fluid if it looks suspiciously bloody or serous-bloody without obvious coagulopathy clues elsewhere. Early recognition prevents unnecessary lung biopsies, stops the cycle of readmissions, gets these kids back to school faster, and reminds us that sometimes the answer to a chest problem lies deep in the belly button zone—or rather behind it in retroperitoneum!

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Think Abdomen When Lungs Fail:** Recurrent unilateral hemorrhagic pleural effusions in children should trigger immediate suspicion for Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF), especially if there is any history of blunt abdominal trauma—even if remote (e.g., bicycle handle injury weeks prior).
*   **The Amylase Threshold:** A pleural fluid amylase level **≥1000 IU/L** is highly specific for PPF once Boerhaave syndrome and malignancy are excluded; distinguish between pancreatic isoamylase (PPF) vs salivary isoamylase (other causes).
*   **Pediatric Etiology Differs Drastically:** Unlike adults where chronic alcoholism drives >99% of cases, pediatric PPF is primarily caused by **blunt trauma** or **genetic mutations** (*PRSS1*, *SPINK1*). Do not assume alcohol involvement exists in children!
*   **Don't Miss Genetic Clues:** In cases of recurrent or unexplained pancreatitis/effusion without clear trauma history consider genetic testing for hereditary pancreatitis syndromes early; these children lack long-standing alcohol-induced damage but still develop fistulas due to ductal fragility/mutations.
*   **First-Line Management:** Start conservatively with NPO status TPN octreotide infusion + adequate drainage; reserve ERCP/stenting or surgery only if medical management fails after ~2–3 weeks given lower initial failure rates compared to adults who often need procedural intervention sooner due to underlying chronic disease burden



## Conservative First? Navigating Medical Management vs. Endoscopic Intervention in Pediatric Pancreatopleural Fistula

We've all been there: a kid comes in gasping for air, chest full of "mystery" fluid, and your brain immediately jumps to the lungs. You tap the effusion, it refills. You tap it again, and *still* it refills. You start chasing TB, malignancy, or empyema, throwing antibiotics and anti-TB drugs at a problem that isn't even in the chest. That was exactly the trajectory for this 11-year-old boy who cycled three times through our emergency department before we found the real culprit: a **pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)** hiding behind a "pulmonary mask."

This case is a classic reminder that in pediatric medicine, when you see recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusions—especially with a history of blunt abdominal trauma you might have forgotten—the pancreas is often the silent partner in crime. But beyond the diagnostic thrill of catching a missed trauma history, this case offers a critical teaching moment on the controversial treatment algorithm for PPFs: Do we push straight to invasive procedures, or do we give conservative medical management a fighting chance?

### The Diagnostic Rabbit Hole
Our patient arrived with dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. Initial aspirations yielded liters of dark red fluid. Because he had a family history of tuberculosis and his Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated, we fell into the trap many clinicians face: assuming infectious etiology in endemic areas. He was started on antitubercular therapy (ATT) while his lungs continued to fill up.

The turning point wasn't magic; it was persistence. On the third admission, digging deeper into the history revealed blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle just one month prior—a detail easily buried under layers of respiratory distress and bullying-induced secrecy by the child himself. Once we connected that dot, the imaging spoke volumes. A contrast-enhanced CT showed a complex collection tracking from the pancreas tail up through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural space. The MRCP confirmed bilateral fistulas (right > left), and frankly, nothing screams PPF louder than pleural fluid amylase levels hitting **6118 U/L** (and lipase at 7211 U/L). At that point, ATT was stopped immediately, and we pivoted to pancreatic management.

### The Great Debate: Conservative vs. Aggressive
Here is where things get tricky for those of us trying to build an evidence-based protocol for kids with PPFs. Unlike adults where chronic alcoholism drives most cases (leading to fibrotic ducts that rarely heal spontaneously), pediatric PPFs are often traumatic or genetically driven (PRSS1/SPINK1 mutations). The anatomy is different, so why would we treat them exactly like adults?

In this specific case report, we chose a **conservative-first approach**. We put him Nil Per Os (NPO), started Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN), initiated an Octreotide infusion at 30 µg/kg/day, and managed drainage via an intercostal drain (ICD). Surprisingly—and perhaps optimistically—this worked beautifully within **10 days**. Drainage ceased, the ICD came out, and he went home symptom-free.

However, before you rush to adopt this as your universal standard of care for every child with PPF, let's look at the hard numbers provided in our discussion section:
*   **Conservative Success Rate:** In pediatric populations specifically relying on bowel rest + somatostatin analogues alone, success rates hover around a dismal **12%**.
*   **Endoscopic/Surgical Success:** Conversely, endoscopic stenting via ERCP boasts near **100%** short-term success in pediatrics (>90% long-term), while surgical options run around 93%.

So how do we reconcile our patient's rapid recovery with data suggesting conservative management fails 88% of the time? It's likely that this patient had a relatively clean traumatic disruption without significant ductal stenosis or chronic inflammation preventing closure. For some kids with acute traumatic leaks and intact ductal architecture downstream from the leak, giving it two weeks of "pancreatic vacation" (bowel rest + octreotide) is reasonable before committing to sedation and instrumentation.

### When to Escalate: The Algorithm
The takeaway isn't "always try meds first," but rather "try meds first *if* there are no contraindications," but have your ERCP team on speed dial because they probably won't wait long enough for failure if you're lucky enough to catch them early enough! Here is how I'm thinking about stepping up care based on current literature and this case experience:

*   **Step 1: The Trial.** Start with NPO, TPN/Optimized enteral feeding distal to ampulla if tolerated later on, Octreotide (somatostatin analogue), and adequate drainage via ICD or pigtail catheter. Give this 2–3 weeks maximum if output decreases steadily.
*   **Step 2: The Pivot.** If drainage persists beyond 2–3 weeks without significant reduction (<50ml/day trend), or if output increases despite maximal medical therapy, stop waiting for spontaneous closure. Move quickly to **ERCP**.
    *   *Why ERCP?* It allows visualization of the ductal anatomy and placement of trans-papillary stents across the leak site. This drops intraductal pressure below systemic venous pressure in many cases ("stent bridge"), allowing the fistula tract to seal from within while maintaining nutritional flow via pancreatic juice diversion away from the leak.
*   **Step 3: The Heavy Hitters.** If ERCP fails due to severe stricture distal to the leak or complete transection where stenting isn't feasible (>5mm gap), then surgical intervention becomes necessary (distal pancreatectomy or Roux-en-Y pancreatojejunostomy). Mortality here remains low (<5%), but morbidity is higher than endoscopy.

### Key Take-Home Points
*   **Think Abdomen Before Lungs:** Recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusions in children require checking pleural fluid amylase/lipase levels immediately; levels >1000 IU/L are highly suggestive of PPF until proven otherwise.
*   **Don't Ignore Trauma:** Even remote blunt abdominal trauma (like a bike handlebar injury) can present weeks later as respiratory distress due to slow-tracking pseudocysts/fistulas; always ask about abdominal hits during recurrent admissions for "unexplained" effusions.
*   **Medical Management Has Limits:** While bowel rest + Octreotide works miraculously for select acute traumatic cases (like ours), be aware that pediatric success rates are historically low (~12%). Do not delay escalation if output doesn't taper rapidly within 48–72 hours of initiation or persists beyond 2 weeks.
