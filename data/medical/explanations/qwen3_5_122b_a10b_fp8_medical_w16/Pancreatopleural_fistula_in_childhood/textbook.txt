Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Clinical Presentation and the 'Pulmonary Mask' Phenomenon

The clinical narrative of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in the pediatric population is frequently characterized by a deceptive presentation where the primary site of pathology—the pancreas—remains silent while the thorax bears the brunt of the symptoms. In this case, a late-childhood male presented with a classic triad of progressive respiratory distress, right-sided chest pain, and recurrent pleural effusions. The initial physical examination revealed significant signs consistent with a large-volume pleural collection rather than intrinsic lung parenchymal disease. Specifically, the patient exhibited stony dullness on percussion across the right subscapular and axillary regions, coupled with significantly diminished vocal fremitus and absent breath sounds in these areas. Despite these profound localizing signs pointing toward fluid accumulation, systemic markers of primary pulmonary infection such as high-grade fever or purulent sputum were notably absent. The patient's oxygen saturation dropped to 88%–89% on room air, necessitating immediate transfer to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit for supplemental oxygen, yet the underlying etiology remained obscured by what can be termed a "pulmonary mask."

This phenomenon describes the tendency of PPF to mimic primary thoracic pathologies, leading clinicians down a diagnostic pathway focused exclusively on pulmonary or infectious etiologies. In this specific case, the patient's history included a known contact with pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch's disease), and subsequent pleural fluid analysis showed a mildly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level. These factors created a strong pre-test probability for tuberculous pleurisy, resulting in the initiation of empirical anti-tubercular therapy during two separate admissions. However, this management strategy failed because it addressed only the manifestation (the effusion) rather than the root cause (pancreatic leakage). The recurrence of massive, dark-red hemorrhagic effusions despite appropriate thoracic interventions served as a critical red flag. In clinical practice, recurrent pleural effusions that refill rapidly after aspiration or drainage should prompt an immediate re-evaluation of the differential diagnosis beyond common infectious or inflammatory causes. The persistence of symptoms despite aggressive thoracic management suggests an ongoing extravasation mechanism that requires identification of the source tract.

A pivotal factor contributing to this diagnostic delay was an incomplete history regarding antecedent events. During the first two admissions, crucial information regarding blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior was withheld by the patient, likely due to fear or social circumstances such as school bullying. This omission prevented early consideration of traumatic pancreatitis as a potential precursor to PPF. It underscores a vital lesson in pediatric medicine: children may conceal histories of trauma due to psychosocial factors, requiring clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion even when explicit history is lacking. When combined with imaging findings such as patchy consolidations and hydropneumothorax on CT scans—which were initially interpreted as complications of presumed pneumonia or TB—the true trajectory of pancreatic fluid tracking from the retroperitoneum through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural space was missed until advanced imaging clarified the anatomy.

Ultimately, recognizing PPF requires shifting focus from the thoracic cavity back to the abdomen when faced with unexplained recurrent effusions that lack typical infectious markers like neutrophilic exudates or fever. The presence of dark-red fluid upon aspiration further complicated matters by raising concerns for malignancy or hemothorax; however, cytology ruled out malignant cells, and coagulation profiles were unremarkable for spontaneous bleeding disorders. The definitive clue emerged only after persistent questioning revealed the hidden trauma history and correlation with imaging showing a continuous tract between the pancreatic tail and the pleural space. This case illustrates that while respiratory distress is often assumed to be pulmonary in origin, especially in children with known risk factors for lung disease, clinicians must consider extra-thoracic sources when standard therapies fail repeatedly. Early recognition prevents unnecessary invasive procedures and prolonged exposure to ineffective medications like anti-tubercular drugs or antibiotics that do not address pancreatic enzyme leakage driving inflammation and fluid production in the chest cavity.

## Pathophysiology and Diagnostic Reasoning

The development of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a complex anatomical journey where pancreatic secretions bypass the abdominal cavity to accumulate in the thorax. In this pediatric case, the initiating event was blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle, which caused a disruption of the pancreatic duct, specifically in the tail region. When the posterior aspect of the pancreas is injured, leaked enzymatic fluid does not freely enter the peritoneal cavity to cause ascites; instead, it tracks into the retroperitoneal space. From this location, the fluid follows the path of least resistance, ascending through natural diaphragmatic openings—most commonly the esophageal or aortic hiatus—to breach the parietal pleura and collect within the pleural cavity. This mechanism explains why a patient with significant intra-abdominal pathology may present exclusively with respiratory distress, chest pain, and massive pleural effusion, effectively masking the true abdominal origin of the disease. The "pulmonary mask" phenomenon observed here often leads clinicians to pursue thoracic etiologies first, delaying recognition of the underlying pancreatic injury until recurrent effusions fail to respond to standard pulmonary therapies.

Differentiating PPF from other causes of hemorrhagic or amylase-rich pleural effusion requires a rigorous, systematic exclusion process grounded in both clinical history and specific laboratory markers. Upon initial presentation with bloody pleural fluid and a history of tuberculosis contact in this patient, tuberculosis remained a primary differential despite the absence of classic systemic symptoms like fever or weight loss; however, negative GeneXpert testing and only marginally elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels eventually ruled out tuberculous pleurisy. Similarly, malignancy was excluded through normal peripheral smears and negative cytology for malignant cells in three separate aspirations. Infectious empyema was deemed unlikely due to the lack of neutrophilic exudate and fever. Crucially, Boerhaave's syndrome (spontaneous esophageal rupture) must be considered whenever amylase-rich fluid is found in the chest; however, this diagnosis was dismissed because the patient maintained normal bowel habits and lacked a history of violent retching or vomiting preceding symptoms. Hepatic hydrothorax and renal causes were similarly excluded via normal liver function tests, imaging, and renal profiles. This stepwise elimination highlights that while many conditions can produce high-amylase effusions, only PPF typically combines massive elevation with a clear trajectory from retroperitoneal tracking without concurrent gastrointestinal perforation signs.

The biochemical hallmark that definitively shifts suspicion toward PPF is a pleural fluid amylase concentration exceeding 1000 IU/L; in this case report, levels reached 6118 IU/L for amylase and 7211 IU/L for lipase. While salivary-type amylase elevations can occur in esophageal ruptures or certain malignancies (like ovarian or lung cancer), pancreatic-type hyperamylasia is virtually pathognomonic for PPF when correlated with compatible imaging. The magnitude of elevation reflects continuous leakage from an active ductal defect rather than passive diffusion or local inflammation. Consequently, once such extreme values are identified alongside a history of abdominal trauma or pancreatitis, further investigation must pivot immediately toward visualizing pancreatic anatomy rather than continuing an exhaustive pulmonary workup.

Imaging plays distinct but complementary roles in confirming this diagnosis and mapping the fistulous tract. Computed tomography (CT) serves as an excellent initial screening tool to identify retroperitoneal collections and their spatial relationship to surrounding structures; in this patient, CT successfully demonstrated an elongated lobulated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region down to the tail of the pancreas, providing strong circumstantial evidence for PPF even though CT alone identifies actual fistula tracts in only about half of cases. However, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) proved superior for definitive diagnosis by non-invasively delineating ductal anatomy without ionizing radiation—a critical consideration in pediatrics. MRCP confirmed bilateral communication between distended pancreatic ducts and both pleural cavities via the esophageal hiatus, visualizing tubular cystic collections that extended superiorly through the diaphragm. This modality provided the necessary roadmap showing exactly where the duct was narrowed proximal to the leak and how far superiorly it tracked into the chest, guiding subsequent management decisions regarding conservative therapy versus potential endoscopic intervention.

## Management Strategies: From Conservative to Interventional Approaches

The management of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in pediatric patients requires a nuanced approach that balances immediate respiratory stabilization with definitive strategies to halt pancreatic fluid leakage. In the presented case, the initial priority was addressing the life-threatening respiratory compromise caused by massive, recurrent pleural effusions. Upon presentation with tachypnea, hypoxia, and stony dullness on percussion, the patient required immediate respiratory support via supplemental oxygen and therapeutic thoracentesis to relieve lung compression. While initial aspirations provided temporary relief, the rapid reaccumulation of dark red fluid necessitated the placement of a right-sided intercostal drainage (ICD) catheter. This step served a dual purpose: it acted as a continuous decompression mechanism to maintain adequate ventilation and functioned as a critical monitoring tool. By quantifying the volume and character of the drainage, clinicians could objectively assess the efficacy of subsequent medical therapies and determine when the fistulous tract had successfully sealed.

Once hemodynamic and respiratory stability were achieved through drainage, the therapeutic focus shifted to conservative medical management aimed at reducing pancreatic exocrine secretion and allowing spontaneous closure of the fistula. The cornerstone of this strategy involves complete bowel rest, implemented as a nil per oral (NPO) status to eliminate gastrointestinal stimulation that triggers pancreatic enzyme release. To sustain nutritional needs during this period without stimulating digestion, total parenteral nutrition (TPN) was initiated. Concurrently, Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy (PERT) was administered; while primarily used in malabsorption syndromes, in this context, it helps mitigate feedback inhibition loops that might otherwise exacerbate pancreatic activity or manage concurrent digestive insufficiency. The pharmacological backbone of this regimen was Octreotide, a synthetic somatostatin analogue. Octreotide works by binding to somatostatin receptors on acinar cells, directly inhibiting the release of pancreatic enzymes and bicarbonate while simultaneously reducing splanchnic blood flow. This potent suppression of exocrine function lowers intraductal pressure across the disrupted ductal segment, creating favorable conditions for the fistulous tract to heal spontaneously without further leakage into the pleural space.

The decision to pursue conservative management first aligns with current clinical understanding that many pediatric PPF cases resolve without invasive intervention if managed aggressively for an adequate duration. In this specific case report, the combination of NPO status, TPN, Octreotide infusion at 30 µg/kg/day, and effective ICD drainage resulted in a gradual decline in fluid output over ten days until drainage ceased entirely. However, clinicians must remain vigilant regarding criteria for escalating care should conservative measures fail. Medical management is typically trialed for 2 to 3 weeks; if high-output drainage persists beyond this window or if there is no significant reduction in volume after one week despite optimal dosing of somatostatin analogues, escalation is warranted. The next tier of intervention involves endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), which serves both diagnostic and therapeutic roles by allowing visualization of ductal anatomy and placement of trans-papillary stents or balloon dilation to bypass strictures and reduce intraductal pressure. ERCP boasts high success rates in pediatric populations for closing fistulas associated with ductal anomalies or stenosis that are amenable to stenting.

Surgical intervention represents the final line of defense reserved for cases refractory to both medical management and endoscopic therapy, or when anatomical factors preclude less invasive approaches such as severe ductal obstruction or distally located fistulas where stent placement is technically unfeasible. Surgical options include distal pancreatectomy for localized disease involving the tail or body of the pancreas—relevant here given the trauma location—or internal drainage procedures like Roux-en-Y pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure) for diffuse ductal disruption. These operations carry higher morbidity compared to conservative or endoscopic routes but offer definitive resolution with low recurrence rates when indicated correctly throughout this progression from stabilization to potential surgery hinges on serial monitoring of ICD output as a surrogate marker for fistula patency; cessation of drainage signifies successful closure and permits removal of the chest tube and transition back to enteral feeding under careful observation before discharge.
