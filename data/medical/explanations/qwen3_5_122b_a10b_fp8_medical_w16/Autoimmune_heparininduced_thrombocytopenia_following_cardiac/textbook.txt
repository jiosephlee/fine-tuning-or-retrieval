Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The clinical narrative begins with an elderly male patient presenting with a complex cardiovascular history, including a two-year history of exertional dyspnea, longstanding atrial fibrillation managed with apixaban, and an incidental systolic murmur. His preoperative workup involved a diagnostic heart catheterization performed one week prior to definitive surgery. Crucially, because the patient had not withheld his apixaban prior to the procedure, the interventional team utilized a transradial approach with a significantly reduced dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH), administering only 2000 units to mitigate bleeding risks. Despite this modified anticoagulation strategy, angiography revealed significant coronary artery disease and valvular pathology, necessitating urgent cardiac intervention involving aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta grafting, and coronary artery bypass grafting. During the subsequent cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) procedure, standard high-dose UFH (45,000 units) was administered to ensure adequate systemic anticoagulation. The surgery itself was uncomplicated hemodynamically, and the patient was transitioned off bypass without major blood loss or transfusion requirements.

Postoperatively, the patient entered the standard recovery protocol for cardiac surgery patients. On postoperative day 1 (POD1), his platelet count was recorded at 155×10^9/L, representing a mild decline from his baseline of 210×10^9/L; this magnitude of drop is frequently observed immediately after CPB due to hemodilution and platelet consumption by the extracorporeal circuit. Standard thromboprophylaxis was initiated on POD1 with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), specifically dalteparin. However, contrary to the expected trajectory where platelet counts stabilize or begin to recover by days three to five in uncomplicated cases, this patient exhibited a steady and concerning decline in platelet numbers from POD1 through POD5, dropping to 50×10^9/L. By POD6, the count plummeted further to approximately 24×10^9/L. Concurrently, while initially attributed to postoperative delirium—a common finding in elderly cardiac patients—the patient's mental status deteriorated. Brain imaging revealed an acute ischemic stroke in the left parietal lobe despite the absence of focal neurological deficits on physical examination at that moment. This combination of rapidly progressive thrombocytopenia and new-onset arterial thrombosis immediately raised suspicion for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT), although the timing presented a diagnostic challenge against conventional teaching.

The primary diagnostic dilemma in this case arose from the temporal profile of the thrombocytopenia. Classic HIT typically manifests between days 5 and 10 following heparin exposure in patients without recent prior sensitization; this "classic window" allows sufficient time for the immune system to generate IgG antibodies against platelet factor 4 (PF4)-heparin complexes. In contrast, this patient's platelet count began falling almost immediately after surgery (starting POD1) and continued downward well before day 5. Under standard algorithms, such rapid onset often leads clinicians away from HIT toward alternative diagnoses like dilutional effects or consumptive coagulopathies unrelated to immunity. However, careful review of the patient's history revealed that he had received UFH just one week earlier during his preoperative catheterization. This prior exposure served as a sensitizing event; upon re-exposure during cardiac surgery—even though separated by seven days—the patient possessed circulating memory B-cells capable of mounting an accelerated secondary immune response. Consequently, preformed or rapidly generated antibodies led to immediate platelet activation and destruction upon re-administration of heparin during CPB and subsequent LMWH prophylaxis, effectively compressing the typical latency period into an "early onset" presentation that mimics other causes of perioperative thrombocytopenia but carries distinct pathophysiological implications for management urgency.

To confirm HIT as the working diagnosis and rule out confounding etiologies common in post-cardiac surgery patients, a rigorous differential diagnosis was applied based on laboratory data and clinical context. Sepsis-driven disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was deemed unlikely given the patient's stable hemodynamics throughout the course and negative blood/urine cultures; furthermore, fibrinogen levels were markedly elevated rather than depleted early on—a pattern consistent with an acute-phase reactant response rather than consumptive coagulopathy associated with infection-driven DIC similarly ruled out by normal renal function tests which argued against microangiopathic processes driven by organ failure. ## Pathophysiology of Autoimmune HIT and Serological Complexity

Understanding the transition from clinical suspicion to definitive diagnosis in this case requires a nuanced grasp of the immunopathology distinguishing classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) from its autoimmune variant (aHIT). In classic HIT, the pathogenesis begins with the formation of immune complexes composed of heparin and platelet factor 4 (PF4). Under normal conditions, PF4 is released from platelet alpha-granules; however, when exposed to unfractionated heparin (UFH), particularly at high concentrations used during cardiopulmonary bypass, PF4 undergoes a conformational change that exposes neoepitopes. Patients develop IgG antibodies against these PF4-heparin complexes. These large immune complexes bind simultaneously to multiple platelets via their Fab regions targeting PF4 and their Fc regions engaging the platelet Fc gamma receptor IIa (FcγRIIa). This simultaneous binding causes receptor cross-linking, triggering potent intracellular signaling cascades that lead to massive platelet activation, aggregation, and consumption. Crucially, this process is strictly heparin-dependent; the presence of free heparin is required to bridge the PF4 molecules into the specific lattice structure recognized by the antibody.

In contrast, autoimmune HIT represents a distinct subset where the pathogenic IgG antibodies possess "heparin-independent" platelet-activating properties. While these antibodies still recognize PF4, they can induce platelet activation even in the absence of exogenous heparin. This occurs because the autoantibodies in aHIT are capable of cross-linking FcγRIIa receptors directly or via endogenous sources of polyanions that mimic heparin's role in stabilizing the complex. Consequently, patients with aHIT often present with more severe clinical phenotypes than those with classic HIT, characterized by profound thrombocytopenia and a higher propensity for extensive thrombosis despite anticoagulation withdrawal or switching. The mechanism allows for persistent platelet activation even if circulating heparin levels drop or are removed entirely, explaining why this patient's condition worsened rapidly post-operatively and why standard management strategies sometimes fail to halt the hypercoagulable state immediately.

The laboratory evaluation in this case highlighted these pathophysiological differences through specific serological patterns. The initial screening utilized a PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), which detected antibodies binding to immobilized PF4-polyanion complexes. This test yielded a strongly positive result with an optical density (OD) of 2.2 units, significantly exceeding the threshold of 0.4 OD units. While a high OD value indicates a high concentration of anti-PF4/PAX antibodies and correlates with a higher probability of true HIT upon functional testing, it does not distinguish between classic and autoimmune variants on its own. Therefore, while the ELISA raised immediate alarm for HIT given the patient's dropping platelet count and new stroke, it necessitated confirmatory functional assays to define the nature of the antibody activity.

The confirmatory Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) provided critical insight into the unique serological profile of this patient but initially presented an "indeterminate" result due to deviations from classic patterns. In a standard positive SRA for classic HIT, adding low-to-moderate concentrations of UFH induces significant serotonin release (>20%), whereas adding supraphysiological doses (e.g., 100 U/mL) inhibits release ("high-dose inhibition") because excess heparin prevents the formation of large enough immune lattices required for cross-linking. Furthermore, classic HIT shows negligible release in the absence of added heparin (0 U/mL). In this patient's case, however, serum incubated without any added UFH induced 96% serotonin release—a hallmark of aHIT demonstrating that his antibodies could activate platelets independently of exogenous heparin. Additionally, at low UFH concentrations (0.1 and 0.3 U/mL), release was robust (97–99%). While high-dose inhibition was eventually observed at 100 U/mL UFH (0% release), confirming some dependence on complex stoichiometry at extreme concentrations, the dominant feature was spontaneous activation without added antigen exposure.

Perhaps the most distinctive finding in this case was the response to Fc receptor blockade using monoclonal antibody IV.3 in conjunction with low-concentration UFH stimulation. Typically, when IV.3 blocks FcγRIIa receptors on donor platelets during an SRA run with classic HIT antibodies, serotonin release is almost completely inhibited (<20%), proving that activation is mediated solely through Fc receptor engagement by immune complexes formed with exogenous heparin. In this patient's sample containing both serum and 0.1 U/mL UFH, IV.3 failed to fully block activation; instead, serotonin release remained markedly elevated at 76%. This partial resistance suggests that while some component of activation may involve Fc-receptor cross-linking facilitated by trace amounts of endogenous factors or weak interactions with low-dose exogenous UFH, another mechanism contributes significantly to platelet degranulation that is refractory to simple steric hindrance by IV.3 alone. This novel serological phenotype underscores that aHIT antibodies can engage alternative pathways or form aggregates so stable or numerous that blocking one receptor site does not abrogate activation ## Therapeutic Strategy Management in Severe Hypercoagulability

The management of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) presenting with active thrombosis and profound thrombocytopenia requires an immediate and aggressive shift away from standard prophylactic measures toward therapeutic-grade anticoagulation tailored to the patient's hemodynamic and neurological status. Upon suspicion of HIT, the first critical step is the absolute cessation of all heparin products, including unfractionated heparin used for line flushing, and the discontinuation of any cross-reactive agents. In this specific case, despite the patient being on prophylactic-dose fondaparinux, this agent was halted immediately upon the recognition of active arterial thrombosis (stroke) and severe platelet depletion. While fondaparinux is often considered a safe alternative for HIT due to its minimal cross-reactivity with HIT antibodies, its use in a setting of active hemorrhagic risk or unstable thrombosis necessitates caution; furthermore, switching to a more controllable agent was prioritized given the complexity of the patient's presentation.

The selection of argatroban as the primary therapeutic anticoagulant was driven by the unique constraints imposed by the patient's concurrent acute ischemic stroke and extreme thrombocytopenia (platelet nadir dropping to 13×10⁹/L). Unlike direct oral anticoagulants or subcutaneous agents like fondaparinux, argatroban offers a short half-life and rapid reversibility via simple infusion cessation, which is crucial if intracranial hemorrhage develops or worsens. Additionally, argatroban does not rely on renal clearance, making it suitable for patients where organ function might fluctuate post-operatively. The choice reflected a risk-benefit analysis where the ability to tightly titrate anticoagulation via activated partial thromboplastin time (targeting 55–85 seconds) outweighed the convenience of once-daily subcutaneous dosing. This approach aimed to suppress the intense platelet-activating activity characteristic of aHIT while minimizing the risk of catastrophic bleeding in a brain recently subjected to infarction.

Given that aHIT involves massive autoantibody-driven platelet activation independent of heparin concentration, anticoagulation alone may be insufficient to halt the cytokine storm and microvascular thrombosis. Consequently, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was introduced as an adjunctive therapy to block FcγRIIa receptors on platelets, thereby inhibiting antibody binding and downstream activation. The dosing strategy employed involved administering 100 g on two consecutive days, followed by a third dose due to an inadequate initial platelet response. This aggressive regimen addresses the underlying pathophysiology of aHIT directly, attempting to "break" the cycle of immune complex formation even when standard antiplatelet mechanisms are overwhelmed by autoimmunity. The delayed platelet recovery observed in this case underscores that while IVIG is mechanistically sound for aHIT, its efficacy can vary significantly based on antibody titer and affinity severity.

Monitoring therapeutic efficacy in this context extends beyond simple platelet counts, which may remain depressed due to consumption despite successful intervention. Clinicians must interpret serial markers of systemic hypercoagulability and endothelial injury closely. In this case study, falling fibrinogen levels served as an indirect marker of consumption coagulopathy; conversely, rising fibrinogen suggested effective suppression of coagulation activation. Similarly, D-dimer kinetics provided insight into fibrinolysis rates associated with ongoing clot turnover; peak levels around postoperative day 12 indicated persistent thrombus burden before eventual resolution. Furthermore, the transient appearance of schistocytes on peripheral blood smears signaled microangiopathic hemolysis secondary to shear stress from widespread microthrombi. The persistence or disappearance of these markers helped guide decisions regarding whether current anticoagulation intensity was sufficient or required escalation or modification.

Once clinical stability was achieved and signs of consumptive coagulopathy subsided—evidenced by stabilizing fibrinogen trends and resolving schistocytosis—a transition back to fondaparinux was executed on postoperative day 14. This switch balanced long-term manageability against acute safety concerns; fondaparinux allows for convenient once-daily subcutaneous administration compared to continuous intravenous infusion monitoring required for argatroban. However, because fondaparinux acts indirectly via antithrombin III supplementation is mandatory if antithrombin levels are depleted by consumption during acute HIT phases; thus, 1000 U of antithrombin concentrate was administered prior to initiation to ensure adequate drug activity. This stepwise progression from potent intravenous inhibition with close monitoring to manageable outpatient-compatible therapy illustrates a pragmatic adaptation to evolving clinical data, ensuring sustained protection against recurrent thrombosis while facilitating discharge planning and eventual return to oral anticoagulation with apixaban once antibody titers waned sufficiently.
