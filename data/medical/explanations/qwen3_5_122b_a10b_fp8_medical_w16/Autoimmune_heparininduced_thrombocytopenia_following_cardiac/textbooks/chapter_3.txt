Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 3: ## Therapeutic Strategy Management in Severe Hypercoagulability

The management of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) presenting with active thrombosis and profound thrombocytopenia requires an immediate and aggressive shift away from standard prophylactic measures toward therapeutic-grade anticoagulation tailored to the patient's hemodynamic and neurological status. Upon suspicion of HIT, the first critical step is the absolute cessation of all heparin products, including unfractionated heparin used for line flushing, and the discontinuation of any cross-reactive agents. In this specific case, despite the patient being on prophylactic-dose fondaparinux, this agent was halted immediately upon the recognition of active arterial thrombosis (stroke) and severe platelet depletion. While fondaparinux is often considered a safe alternative for HIT due to its minimal cross-reactivity with HIT antibodies, its use in a setting of active hemorrhagic risk or unstable thrombosis necessitates caution; furthermore, switching to a more controllable agent was prioritized given the complexity of the patient's presentation.

The selection of argatroban as the primary therapeutic anticoagulant was driven by the unique constraints imposed by the patient's concurrent acute ischemic stroke and extreme thrombocytopenia (platelet nadir dropping to 13×10⁹/L). Unlike direct oral anticoagulants or subcutaneous agents like fondaparinux, argatroban offers a short half-life and rapid reversibility via simple infusion cessation, which is crucial if intracranial hemorrhage develops or worsens. Additionally, argatroban does not rely on renal clearance, making it suitable for patients where organ function might fluctuate post-operatively. The choice reflected a risk-benefit analysis where the ability to tightly titrate anticoagulation via activated partial thromboplastin time (targeting 55–85 seconds) outweighed the convenience of once-daily subcutaneous dosing. This approach aimed to suppress the intense platelet-activating activity characteristic of aHIT while minimizing the risk of catastrophic bleeding in a brain recently subjected to infarction.

Given that aHIT involves massive autoantibody-driven platelet activation independent of heparin concentration, anticoagulation alone may be insufficient to halt the cytokine storm and microvascular thrombosis. Consequently, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was introduced as an adjunctive therapy to block FcγRIIa receptors on platelets, thereby inhibiting antibody binding and downstream activation. The dosing strategy employed involved administering 100 g on two consecutive days, followed by a third dose due to an inadequate initial platelet response. This aggressive regimen addresses the underlying pathophysiology of aHIT directly, attempting to "break" the cycle of immune complex formation even when standard antiplatelet mechanisms are overwhelmed by autoimmunity. The delayed platelet recovery observed in this case underscores that while IVIG is mechanistically sound for aHIT, its efficacy can vary significantly based on antibody titer and affinity severity.

Monitoring therapeutic efficacy in this context extends beyond simple platelet counts, which may remain depressed due to consumption despite successful intervention. Clinicians must interpret serial markers of systemic hypercoagulability and endothelial injury closely. In this case study, falling fibrinogen levels served as an indirect marker of consumption coagulopathy; conversely, rising fibrinogen suggested effective suppression of coagulation activation. Similarly, D-dimer kinetics provided insight into fibrinolysis rates associated with ongoing clot turnover; peak levels around postoperative day 12 indicated persistent thrombus burden before eventual resolution. Furthermore, the transient appearance of schistocytes on peripheral blood smears signaled microangiopathic hemolysis secondary to shear stress from widespread microthrombi. The persistence or disappearance of these markers helped guide decisions regarding whether current anticoagulation intensity was sufficient or required escalation or modification.

Once clinical stability was achieved and signs of consumptive coagulopathy subsided—evidenced by stabilizing fibrinogen trends and resolving schistocytosis—a transition back to fondaparinux was executed on postoperative day 14. This switch balanced long-term manageability against acute safety concerns; fondaparinux allows for convenient once-daily subcutaneous administration compared to continuous intravenous infusion monitoring required for argatroban. However, because fondaparinux acts indirectly via antithrombin III supplementation is mandatory if antithrombin levels are depleted by consumption during acute HIT phases; thus, 1000 U of antithrombin concentrate was administered prior to initiation to ensure adequate drug activity. This stepwise progression from potent intravenous inhibition with close monitoring to manageable outpatient-compatible therapy illustrates a pragmatic adaptation to evolving clinical data, ensuring sustained protection against recurrent thrombosis while facilitating discharge planning and eventual return to oral anticoagulation with apixaban once antibody titers waned sufficiently.
