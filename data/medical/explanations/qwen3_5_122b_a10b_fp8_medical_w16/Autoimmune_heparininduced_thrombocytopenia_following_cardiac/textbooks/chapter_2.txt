Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of Autoimmune HIT and Serological Complexity

Understanding the transition from clinical suspicion to definitive diagnosis in this case requires a nuanced grasp of the immunopathology distinguishing classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) from its autoimmune variant (aHIT). In classic HIT, the pathogenesis begins with the formation of immune complexes composed of heparin and platelet factor 4 (PF4). Under normal conditions, PF4 is released from platelet alpha-granules; however, when exposed to unfractionated heparin (UFH), particularly at high concentrations used during cardiopulmonary bypass, PF4 undergoes a conformational change that exposes neoepitopes. Patients develop IgG antibodies against these PF4-heparin complexes. These large immune complexes bind simultaneously to multiple platelets via their Fab regions targeting PF4 and their Fc regions engaging the platelet Fc gamma receptor IIa (FcγRIIa). This simultaneous binding causes receptor cross-linking, triggering potent intracellular signaling cascades that lead to massive platelet activation, aggregation, and consumption. Crucially, this process is strictly heparin-dependent; the presence of free heparin is required to bridge the PF4 molecules into the specific lattice structure recognized by the antibody.

In contrast, autoimmune HIT represents a distinct subset where the pathogenic IgG antibodies possess "heparin-independent" platelet-activating properties. While these antibodies still recognize PF4, they can induce platelet activation even in the absence of exogenous heparin. This occurs because the autoantibodies in aHIT are capable of cross-linking FcγRIIa receptors directly or via endogenous sources of polyanions that mimic heparin's role in stabilizing the complex. Consequently, patients with aHIT often present with more severe clinical phenotypes than those with classic HIT, characterized by profound thrombocytopenia and a higher propensity for extensive thrombosis despite anticoagulation withdrawal or switching. The mechanism allows for persistent platelet activation even if circulating heparin levels drop or are removed entirely, explaining why this patient's condition worsened rapidly post-operatively and why standard management strategies sometimes fail to halt the hypercoagulable state immediately.

The laboratory evaluation in this case highlighted these pathophysiological differences through specific serological patterns. The initial screening utilized a PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), which detected antibodies binding to immobilized PF4-polyanion complexes. This test yielded a strongly positive result with an optical density (OD) of 2.2 units, significantly exceeding the threshold of 0.4 OD units. While a high OD value indicates a high concentration of anti-PF4/PAX antibodies and correlates with a higher probability of true HIT upon functional testing, it does not distinguish between classic and autoimmune variants on its own. Therefore, while the ELISA raised immediate alarm for HIT given the patient's dropping platelet count and new stroke, it necessitated confirmatory functional assays to define the nature of the antibody activity.

The confirmatory Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) provided critical insight into the unique serological profile of this patient but initially presented an "indeterminate" result due to deviations from classic patterns. In a standard positive SRA for classic HIT, adding low-to-moderate concentrations of UFH induces significant serotonin release (>20%), whereas adding supraphysiological doses (e.g., 100 U/mL) inhibits release ("high-dose inhibition") because excess heparin prevents the formation of large enough immune lattices required for cross-linking. Furthermore, classic HIT shows negligible release in the absence of added heparin (0 U/mL). In this patient's case, however, serum incubated without any added UFH induced 96% serotonin release—a hallmark of aHIT demonstrating that his antibodies could activate platelets independently of exogenous heparin. Additionally, at low UFH concentrations (0.1 and 0.3 U/mL), release was robust (97–99%). While high-dose inhibition was eventually observed at 100 U/mL UFH (0% release), confirming some dependence on complex stoichiometry at extreme concentrations, the dominant feature was spontaneous activation without added antigen exposure.

Perhaps the most distinctive finding in this case was the response to Fc receptor blockade using monoclonal antibody IV.3 in conjunction with low-concentration UFH stimulation. Typically, when IV.3 blocks FcγRIIa receptors on donor platelets during an SRA run with classic HIT antibodies, serotonin release is almost completely inhibited (<20%), proving that activation is mediated solely through Fc receptor engagement by immune complexes formed with exogenous heparin. In this patient's sample containing both serum and 0.1 U/mL UFH, IV.3 failed to fully block activation; instead, serotonin release remained markedly elevated at 76%. This partial resistance suggests that while some component of activation may involve Fc-receptor cross-linking facilitated by trace amounts of endogenous factors or weak interactions with low-dose exogenous UFH, another mechanism contributes significantly to platelet degranulation that is refractory to simple steric hindrance by IV.3 alone. This novel serological phenotype underscores that aHIT antibodies can engage alternative pathways or form aggregates so stable or numerous that blocking one receptor site does not abrogate activation
