Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The clinical narrative begins with an elderly male patient presenting with a complex cardiovascular history, including a two-year history of exertional dyspnea, longstanding atrial fibrillation managed with apixaban, and an incidental systolic murmur. His preoperative workup involved a diagnostic heart catheterization performed one week prior to definitive surgery. Crucially, because the patient had not withheld his apixaban prior to the procedure, the interventional team utilized a transradial approach with a significantly reduced dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH), administering only 2000 units to mitigate bleeding risks. Despite this modified anticoagulation strategy, angiography revealed significant coronary artery disease and valvular pathology, necessitating urgent cardiac intervention involving aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta grafting, and coronary artery bypass grafting. During the subsequent cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) procedure, standard high-dose UFH (45,000 units) was administered to ensure adequate systemic anticoagulation. The surgery itself was uncomplicated hemodynamically, and the patient was transitioned off bypass without major blood loss or transfusion requirements.

Postoperatively, the patient entered the standard recovery protocol for cardiac surgery patients. On postoperative day 1 (POD1), his platelet count was recorded at 155×10^9/L, representing a mild decline from his baseline of 210×10^9/L; this magnitude of drop is frequently observed immediately after CPB due to hemodilution and platelet consumption by the extracorporeal circuit. Standard thromboprophylaxis was initiated on POD1 with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), specifically dalteparin. However, contrary to the expected trajectory where platelet counts stabilize or begin to recover by days three to five in uncomplicated cases, this patient exhibited a steady and concerning decline in platelet numbers from POD1 through POD5, dropping to 50×10^9/L. By POD6, the count plummeted further to approximately 24×10^9/L. Concurrently, while initially attributed to postoperative delirium—a common finding in elderly cardiac patients—the patient's mental status deteriorated. Brain imaging revealed an acute ischemic stroke in the left parietal lobe despite the absence of focal neurological deficits on physical examination at that moment. This combination of rapidly progressive thrombocytopenia and new-onset arterial thrombosis immediately raised suspicion for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT), although the timing presented a diagnostic challenge against conventional teaching.

The primary diagnostic dilemma in this case arose from the temporal profile of the thrombocytopenia. Classic HIT typically manifests between days 5 and 10 following heparin exposure in patients without recent prior sensitization; this "classic window" allows sufficient time for the immune system to generate IgG antibodies against platelet factor 4 (PF4)-heparin complexes. In contrast, this patient's platelet count began falling almost immediately after surgery (starting POD1) and continued downward well before day 5. Under standard algorithms, such rapid onset often leads clinicians away from HIT toward alternative diagnoses like dilutional effects or consumptive coagulopathies unrelated to immunity. However, careful review of the patient's history revealed that he had received UFH just one week earlier during his preoperative catheterization. This prior exposure served as a sensitizing event; upon re-exposure during cardiac surgery—even though separated by seven days—the patient possessed circulating memory B-cells capable of mounting an accelerated secondary immune response. Consequently, preformed or rapidly generated antibodies led to immediate platelet activation and destruction upon re-administration of heparin during CPB and subsequent LMWH prophylaxis, effectively compressing the typical latency period into an "early onset" presentation that mimics other causes of perioperative thrombocytopenia but carries distinct pathophysiological implications for management urgency.

To confirm HIT as the working diagnosis and rule out confounding etiologies common in post-cardiac surgery patients, a rigorous differential diagnosis was applied based on laboratory data and clinical context. Sepsis-driven disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was deemed unlikely given the patient's stable hemodynamics throughout the course and negative blood/urine cultures; furthermore, fibrinogen levels were markedly elevated rather than depleted early on—a pattern consistent with an acute-phase reactant response rather than consumptive coagulopathy associated with infection-driven DIC similarly ruled out by normal renal function tests which argued against microangiopathic processes driven by organ failure.
