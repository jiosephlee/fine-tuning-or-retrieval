Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Beyond Day Five: Unmasking 'Rapid-Onset' HIT in Cardiac Patients with Recent Pre-op Exposure

We love rules in emergency medicine and critical care. They keep us safe, they guide our algorithms, and they prevent cognitive overload. One of the oldest "rules" regarding Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) is the timing. If your patient hasn't received heparin recently, or if their platelet drop happens *immediately* upon waking up from surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), you cross off HIT from the list. You chalk it up to hemodilution, surgical consumption, or maybe just the general chaos of being off-pump. After all, the textbook says HIT peaks between days 5 and 10.

But what happens when the rulebook meets a patient who got primed weeks ago? What if that drop isn't dilution—it's a ticking clock waiting for a second hit?

This week, we're diving into a fascinating case report published by St Bernard et al. detailing a brutal encounter with **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** in a post-cardiac surgery patient. It's a story of how ignoring an "early" platelet drop led to a devastating stroke, and how understanding the nuances of recent heparin exposure can save lives before the fifth postoperative day even arrives.

## The Setup: When "Routine" Gets Complicated

Let's meet our protagonist: an elderly gentleman with a history of atrial fibrillation (on apixaban) presenting with dyspnea and valvular disease. His journey started with a standard diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to his definitive repair. Here lies the first clue: despite being on apixaban, his interventionalist didn't hold the meds properly and opted for a transradial approach with a low-dose bolus of unfractionated heparin (UFH): just 2,000 units. Small potatoes, right? Probably nothing worth worrying about compared to what comes next.

One week later, he undergoes complex cardiac surgery—aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta replacement with grafting, CABG, and LAA excitation—all under massive doses of UFH for CPB (45,000 units!). The surgery goes smoothly. No major bleeds. Hemodynamics rock solid. But then comes the lab draw sequence that should make every intensivist pause.

On Post-Operative Day 1 (POD1), his platelets sit at 155 × 10^9/L—a mild dip expected after any big operation thanks to fluid shifts and consumption on the circuitry. By POD2 through POD4, things get worse as planned prophylaxis switches over to Low Molecular Weight Heparin (dalteparin). Then, something strange happens on POD5: the platelets crash hard down to 50 × 10^9/L.

Now, here is where most clinicians might stop thinking about HIT because it happened so fast after bypass surgery starts counting clocks from zero again—or perhaps attribute it simply to the new dalteparin start date if they aren't looking closely enough at pre-op events. But look closer at the timeline: this wasn't day five since admission; it was effectively day six since *any* significant heparin exposure if you count that tiny cath lab bolus from seven days prior combined with today's stormy washout of heparins during surgery.

By PO D6, despite stopping dalteparin and switching him straight away onto fondaparinux hoping against hope that whatever chemical culprit caused this was gone...his plates plummet further—to single digits eventually—and he develops confusion turning frankly alarming delirium without obvious sepsis markers or metabolic derangement driving it home alone either!
