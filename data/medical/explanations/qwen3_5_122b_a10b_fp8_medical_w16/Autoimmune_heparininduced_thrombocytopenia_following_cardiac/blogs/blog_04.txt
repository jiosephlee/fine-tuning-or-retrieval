Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Stroke Despite Low Counts: Why Bleeding Risk Scores Mislead in Autoimmune HIT Cases

We often teach our trainees that "low platelets equal bleed risk." It's a comforting heuristic: if your platelet count drops below 50, hold the warfarin. If it dips under 20, stop everything and give platelets. But what happens when that rule of thumb almost gets a patient killed? What if holding back anticoagulation because of profound thrombocytopenia leads directly to a devastating arterial stroke?

Today, we're diving into a chilling yet instructive case report published recently by St Bernard et al., detailing an elderly man who suffered a massive parietal stroke despite his platelet count plummeting to **13 × 10⁹/L**. This isn't just another story of Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT); this is a textbook example of **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**, a terrifying variant where the pathophysiology defies standard expectations.

This case shatters the illusion that thrombocytopenia always correlates inversely with bleeding risk. Instead, it forces us to confront the reality that in specific subsets of HIT, the platelet *activation* machinery goes haywire independently of how few plates remain in circulation. Let's break down why conventional safety algorithms failed here and why aggressive anticoagulation remains the lifeline—even when those numbers look scary enough to make even seasoned intensivists sweat.

### The Setup: When "Too Early" Means Trouble
The patient was an elderly gentleman undergoing complex cardiac surgery involving an aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta graft repair. Standard stuff for cardio-thoracic teams, right? Unfractionated heparin (UFH) used intraoperatively for cardiopulmonary bypass, then transitioned to prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin (dalteparin) post-op.

By Post-operative Day (POD) 1, things looked fine with a slight dip in platelets—a common occurrence after sternotomy due to hemodilution and consumption. But then came the curveball: from POD 1 to POD 5, his platelet count didn't stabilize; it crashed from 155 × 10⁹/L down to **50 × 10⁹/L**, eventually hitting rock bottom at **13 × 10⁹/L** by POD 7. 

Conventional teaching suggests HIT rarely presents before POD 4-5 unless there was recent prior heparin exposure. Here, however, the culprit was traced back to a heart catheterization one week earlier where he received UFH. That brief exposure primed his immune system so effectively that upon reintroduction during open-heart surgery, his body launched an immediate counter-offensive against its own vasculature. While he wasn't actively bleeding (haptoglobin was elevated suggesting inflammation but no frank hemolysis), he woke up confused with new neurologic deficits. Imaging revealed an acute parietal cortical infarction. Classic HIT logic might suggest stopping all anticoagulation immediately due to the stroke and critical thrombocytopenia—but doing so would have guaranteed death by recurrent thrombosis.

### Decoding the Lab Beastie: More Than Just Antibodies
If you've ever stared at Serotonin Release Assays (SRAs), you know they can be tricky. Classic HIT requires heparin-dependent antibodies: add heparin = serotonin released; remove heparin = silence. Not so fast here.

In this patient, the lab data told a wilder story indicative of **aHIT**:
*   **Spontaneous Activation:** His serum caused robust serotonin release (**96%**) even with **zero** added heparin.
*   **"False Positive" Profile:** Usually, adding excess heparin inhibits binding ("high-dose inhibition"). They saw this at 100 U/mL, confirming specificity issues weren't causing false positives elsewhere.
*   **Fc-Receptor Resistance:** The real kicker was the response to IV.III monoclonal antibody (which blocks FcγRIIA receptors). Typically, blocking these receptors stops platelet activation in HIT samples entirely (<20%). In this guy? It barely dampened things—he still had **76%** serotonin release despite blockade. This suggested antibodies capable of activating platelets through mechanisms potentially beyond simple Fc-receptor cross-linking or perhaps utilizing multiple epitopes simultaneously with high avidity.

This is distinct pathology compared to classic HIT or Drug-Immune Complex mediated thrombocytopenias like DILENZA or quinidine-induced cases where complement plays a bigger role but doesn't necessarily drive such violent spontaneous aggregation without exogenous antigen stimulation.
