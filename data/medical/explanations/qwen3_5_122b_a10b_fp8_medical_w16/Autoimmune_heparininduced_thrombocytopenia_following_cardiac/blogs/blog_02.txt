Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# When Serotonin Release Doesn't Fit: Decoding Atypical Autoimmune HIT Antibody Profiles

We often teach residents that the "textbook" Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) follows a predictable script: exposure, latency, precipitous platelet drop, and a confirmatory lab test where the serotonin release assay (SRA) screams positivity *with* heparin and goes quiet *without*. But medicine loves to break rules, especially when dealing with the darker corners of autoimmunity. Today's case isn't just another story of deep venous thrombosis post-bypass; it's a masterclass in how **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** can masquerade as something else entirely because your gold-standard labs throw up their hands and call the result "indeterminate."

Imagine an elderly gentleman recovering from complex cardiac surgery—aortic valve replacement, aortic root replacement, and CABG. He starts off stable. By Post-operative Day (POD) 5, his platelets begin their slow, steady slide down to 50 ×10^9/L. No fever, no obvious source of sepsis. Then comes the twist: he develops a new focal deficit masked as delirium, and a head CT reveals an acute parietal stroke. You suspect HIT immediately. You send the panel. The PF4/Polyanion ELISA lights up strong (2.2 OD). It looks like a slam dunk for classic HIT... until you get the SRA back, and it doesn't make sense according to the algorithm you memorized for boards.

This is exactly where things got messy—and fascinating—in this case report by St Bernard et al., published recently in *Blood Advances*. The patient didn't fit the mold because his disease process didn't either. We aren't looking at simple heparin-dependent antibodies here; we are staring down the barrel of true **autoimmune** pathology driven by heparin-independent mechanisms, complicated by a bizarre lab signature that almost led us astray.

Let's dive into why this SRA looked so weird. Classically, if you suspect HIT but want to rule out nonspecific activation or look for severity markers, you add an anti-FcγIIa receptor blocker (the IV.3 monoclonal antibody) during the assay. If the platelets stop releasing serotonin in its presence, great—that confirms Fc-receptor mediated activation typical of classic HIT IFN-gamma induced pathophysiology? No wait, let me correct myself there—it confirms classic IgG binding causing cross-linking which is blocked by IV3. Wait, I am getting ahead of my own narrative flow! Let's reset that mechanism clearly: Classic HIT involves antibodies bridging PF4/heparin complexes to Fc receptors on platelets. If you block those receptors with IV.3, platelet activation should cease entirely (<20% release). That is the definition of a clean diagnostic signal.

In our patient's first run-of-the-SRA however? Disaster—or rather confusion ensued! Even though they added potent amounts of unfractionated heparin at low doses (triggering massive serotonin release >90%), adding that same inhibitory antibody failed to shut down the party properly. Instead of dropping below 20%, serotonin release lingered stubborny around 76%. Furthermore—and this is critical—the patient's serum lit up like Christmas even with **zero** heparin present (96% release!). How does one interpret an SRA that says "Yes," then whispers "Maybe"? Or worse yet? The answer lies in understanding that while classic HIT requires heparin-PF4 complexes to trigger Fc-receptors via antigenicity alone might activate directly via alternative pathways independent external triggers altogether? Actually simply stated: His antibodies weren't waiting for heparin; they were aggressive enough to bind PF4 spontaneously OR perhaps interact differently leading directly activating them regardless
