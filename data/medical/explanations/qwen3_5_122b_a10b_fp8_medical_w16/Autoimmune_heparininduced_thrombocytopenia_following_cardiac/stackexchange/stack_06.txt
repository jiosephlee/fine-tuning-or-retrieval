Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In the differential diagnosis of post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia, which specific laboratory finding helps distinguish Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia from Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) or Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) in the early stages before schistocytes appear?

In the complex landscape of post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia, distinguishing Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) from Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) or Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) requires careful interpretation of coagulation parameters and platelet function assays, particularly before morphologic evidence like schistocytes appears. As illustrated in this case of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), the key differentiator lies in the **preservation of coagulation factors** despite severe thrombocytopenia and thrombosis.

Unlike DIC, where widespread microvascular thrombosis consumes clotting factors leading to prolonged PT/INR and PTT alongside low fibrinogen, classic HIT is a pro-thrombotic state driven by platelet activation rather than consumption of the coagulation cascade. In this patient's early presentation (Postoperative Day 5), despite a precipitous drop in platelets to 50×10⁹/L and subsequent stroke, his prothrombin time, INR, and PTT remained entirely normal. Crucially, his fibrinogen was markedly *elevated* at 6.59 g/L. While one might expect low fibrinogen in a consumptive process like DIC or TMA, this elevation reflects the acute-phase inflammatory response to surgery and confirms that the coagulation cascade itself is intact—a hallmark that argues strongly against DIC. Similarly, TMA was initially excluded by the absence of hemolysis markers (normal haptoglobin) and schistocytes on the peripheral smear at that stage.

Therefore, when faced with post-operative thrombocytopenia and new thrombosis, a normal PT/PTT combined with preserved or elevated fibrinogen should immediately raise suspicion for HIT over DIC or TMA. Furthermore, as demonstrated by the atypical Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) in this case—showing significant serotonin release even in the absence of heparin (heparin-independent activation)—the diagnosis can be confirmed by identifying platelet-activating antibodies that may not strictly require heparin for binding, a distinct feature of autoimmune HIT that can mimic TMA clinically but retains the specific serological signature of anti-PF4/heparin complexes. The definitive separation relies on recognizing that HIT causes thrombocytopenia through immune-mediated platelet destruction without depleting plasma coagulation proteins.
