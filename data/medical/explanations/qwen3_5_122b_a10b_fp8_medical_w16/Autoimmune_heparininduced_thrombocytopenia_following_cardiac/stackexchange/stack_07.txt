Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the expected evolution of Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) profiles over time in a patient recovering from Autoimmune HIT, transitioning from the acute phase to convalescence?

In a patient recovering from autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT), the evolution of Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) profiles follows a distinct trajectory from an atypical, heparin-independent pattern toward a conventional, heparin-dependent signature as the disease resolves.

During the **acute phase** of active aHIT, the SRA characteristically demonstrates robust platelet activation even in the **complete absence of heparin**. As illustrated in the case report, this patient's acute-phase serum induced 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin, a hallmark distinguishing aHIT from classic HIT where activation is strictly heparin-dependent. Furthermore, acute aHIT antibodies often display partial resistance to inhibition by FcγRIIa-blocking monoclonal antibodies (such as IV.3); in this case, inhibition was incomplete (76% release persisted), creating an "indeterminate" profile that nonetheless signaled severe autoimmunity.

As the patient transitions into **convalescence**, typically coinciding with platelet count recovery and resolution of hypercoagulability, the antibody profile evolves. Serial testing reveals a gradual shift: the spontaneous (heparin-independent) serotonin release diminishes significantly. In this specific case, by postoperative day 124—after full hematologic recovery—the final SRA demonstrated only 15% release in the absence of heparin but maintained 100% release at therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.3 U/mL). 

This progression indicates that while high-affinity, pathogenic autoantibodies capable of activating platelets independently of antigen drive the acute crisis, they eventually wane or undergo affinity maturation/selection favoring classical heparin-dependent binding patterns. Therefore, clinicians should recognize that an initially "atypical" or indeterminate SRA does not rule out HIT; rather, serial monitoring may reveal a conversion to a classic heparin-dependent profile as the autoimmune process subsides.
