# Beyond the 5-Day Rule: Recognizing Early-Onset HIT After Cardiac Surgery

We often teach residents the "5-to-10-day rule" for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) as a rigid heuristic, especially in the context of cardiac surgery. The logic seems sound: if a patient undergoes cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and their platelet count drops before postoperative day 5, it's probably just the hemodilution, consumption, or surgical trauma associated with the procedure itself. But what happens when that rule fails? What if the patient develops catastrophic thrombocytopenia and a stroke *before* they even hit that five-day mark?

This week, we're diving into a fascinating case report by St Bernard et al. that shatters this conventional timeline and forces us to rethink how we evaluate early postoperative thrombocytopenia. This isn't just a story about a tricky lab result; it's a cautionary tale about the invisible clock ticking from **pre-operative** heparin exposure, leading to a severe variant known as autoimmune HIT (aHIT).

## The Case That Broke the Timeline

Let's set the stage. An elderly gentleman with atrial fibrillation (on apixaban) presents for cardiac surgery. Because he didn't hold his apixaban prior to a diagnostic heart catheterization one week earlier, his interventionalist used a transradial approach and administered only a small bolus of unfractionated heparin (UFH)—just 2,000 units. He then underwent complex cardiac surgery involving an aortic valve replacement and CABG, receiving standard massive doses of UFH during CPB (45,000 units).

Post-op Day 1 looked relatively benign. His platelets dipped slightly to 155×10⁹/L from a baseline of 210×10⁹/L—a classic "post-bypass dip." By Day 4, he was stable on low-molecular-weight heparin (dalteparin). Then came Days 5 and 6. Instead of recovering, his platelet count plummeted from 50×10⁹/L down to a terrifying nadir of **13×10⁹/L**. Simultaneously, his mental status deteriorated from delirium to focal deficits. Brain imaging revealed an acute parietal lobe stroke.

In many institutions, this rapid drop before POD5 might have been dismissed as "surgical consumption" or attributed to the new LMWH started on Day 1. But here is the critical pivot point: **the patient had significant heparin exposure just one week prior.** That pre-catheterization dose primed his immune system. When he received intraoperative heparin during CPB, it wasn't his first rodeo; it was the booster shot that triggered an explosive secondary immune response.

## The Diagnostic Trap: When Labs Don't Fit the Mold

Diagnosing HIT in cardiac surgery is notoriously difficult because almost everyone gets thrombocytopenia for benign reasons after CPB. However, this case highlighted why we must look beyond simple clinical scores when the picture is severe and atypical.

The team ordered HIT antibody testing immediately upon seeing that platelet crash on Day 5. The results were... confusing at first glance.
*   **The Screen:** The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD >2.2). This screams "HIT," but false positives are common in cardiac patients due to high PF4 levels from inflammation or blood products.
*   **The Confirmation:** The Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) returned an "indeterminate" result that required deep forensic analysis by experts at McMaster University.

Here is where things get scientifically spicy and clinically vital for us as emergency physicians and intensivists. Classic HIT antibodies require heparin to activate platelets via Fc-gamma receptors ($Fc\gamma RIIa$). If you add enough heparin to block binding sites (high concentration), activation stops ("heparin inhibition"). Furthermore, adding an anti-Fc receptor blocking antibody should shut down activation completely in classic HIT.

This patient's SRA told a different story:
1.  **Heptarin Independence:** His serum caused massive serotonin release (**96%**) even with **zero** added heparin in the tube! This is the hallmark of *Autoimmune* HIT (aHIT), where antibodies bind directly to PF4/platelet complexes without needing exogenous heparin bridges initially, causing relentless activation.
2.  **Partial Resistance:** Even when they added the monoclonal antibody designed to block Fc receptors (IV.3), platelet activation persisted at **76%** instead of dropping below 20%. This partial resistance suggested an unusually aggressive antibody phenotype that hadn't been well-characterized before this case report.

Despite these weird lab quirks, the clinical picture—rapid profound thrombocytopenia combined with arterial thrombosis (stroke)—demanded action immediately while waiting for final confirmation.

## When the Serotonin Release Assay Lies: Decoding Atypical Autoimmune HIT Profiles

We tend to treat the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) as the gold standard for confirming Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT). It's the definitive "yes" or "no" test that anchors our decision to stop heparin and start a direct thrombin inhibitor. But what happens when the lab reports back an "indeterminate" result because your patient's serum is activating platelets even without heparin, and won't quite behave when you try to block their Fc receptors?

That was the reality for an elderly gentleman in a recent case report by St Bernard et al., presenting a masterclass in why we must never let a single algorithm override clinical judgment. This isn't just another story of post-op thrombocytopenia; it's a tale of **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** with a serological fingerprint so unique it nearly slipped through the cracks of conventional interpretation.

### The Patient Who Broke the Timeline

Let's set the scene. Our patient, a man in his 70s, underwent complex cardiac surgery involving an aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta repair. He had received unfractionated heparin (UFH) intraoperatively—a standard 45,000 units—but crucially, he had also received a smaller dose (2,000 units) just one week prior during a diagnostic heart catheterization while still on apixaban.

Post-operatively, things looked routine until they didn't. His platelet count started a steady decline from Day 1, hitting 50 × 10⁹/L by Postoperative Day 5 (POD5). In the world of cardiac surgery, we often attribute mild drops to hemodilution or consumption. But this wasn't mild; it was relentless. By POD6, he plummeted to 26 × 10⁹/L and developed worsening delirium. A CT scan revealed an acute parietal stroke.

Here is where the timeline gets tricky. Classic HIT usually strikes between Days 5 and 10 after exposure. If your patient hits Day 1 with massive thrombocytopenia and thrombosis, you immediately think "not HIT." However, this patient had **pre-operative sensitization**. That small dose of UFH during his catheterization one week earlier primed his immune system. When hit with the massive surgical dose, his antibodies went into overdrive almost immediately.

### The Lab Conundrum: A Twist in the Tail

The diagnosis hung in the balance because of what happened next in the lab. The screening PF4/polyanion ELISA was screamingly positive (OD 2.2). But then came the SRA—the functional test that proves pathogenicity—and it threw us for a loop.

In classic HIT, antibodies need heparin-PF4 complexes to activate platelets. You add heparin -> platelets scream -> serotonin releases. Add too much heparin (>100 U/mL), and you get "prozone-like" inhibition because there are no complexes left to form; serotonin release drops to zero. That part checked out here: at 100 U/mL, release was indeed absent.

However, two things were wrong with this picture:
1.  **Zero-Heparin Activation:** At **0 U/mL** of heparin (the control tube), this patient's serum caused **96%** serotonin release! Classic HIT should be flatlining here (<20%). This is the hallmark of *heparin-independent* activation seen in autoimmune HIT (aHIT).
2.  **The Blockade Failure:** They added monoclonal antibody IV.3 to block FcγRIIa receptors on platelets—the mechanism by which these immune complexes usually cause damage expecting inhibition to <20%. Instead? **76% serotonin release persisted.** The antibodies were only *partially* inhibited by Fc blockade, suggesting an incredibly aggressive binding affinity or perhaps engagement of alternative pathways not fully captured by standard IV.3 blocking logic.

To anyone relying strictly on textbook definitions, this read as "indeterminate." To clinicians who know aHIT can be brutal and atypical, this read as "severe autoimmune HIT."

# Refractory Thrombocytopenia: When IVIG Isn't Enough in Severe Autoimmune HIT

We tend to think of Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) as a predictable beast: drop in platelets 5–10 days post-exposure, positive PF4 ELISA, and if it's the "autoimmune" variant (aHIT), we reach for high-dose Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG) to neutralize those rogue antibodies. Usually, the platelets bounce back with satisfying rapidity. But what happens when the textbook response doesn't happen? What does it look like when a patient has severe, life-threatening aHIT that simply refuses to budge despite aggressive immunomodulation?

This week, I'm diving into a fascinating case report by St Bernard et al. that challenges our assumptions about managing severe aHIT. It describes an elderly gentleman who underwent complex cardiac surgery (AVR, ascending aorta replacement, CABG) and developed a catastrophic form of autoimmune HIT. The twist? His platelet count plummeted to a terrifying nadir of **13 × 10⁹/L**, he suffered a parietal stroke, and crucially, he failed to respond robustly to high-dose IVIG—a scenario we rarely see described so vividly in the literature.

### The Clinical Picture: Early Onset and Aggressive Decline

Let's set the stage. Our patient is a man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation on apixaban. He had a pre-op cardiac cath where he received a small dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH) because they couldn't hold his DOAC safely. A week later, he goes under for major cardiac surgery with full-dose UFH for cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).

Post-operatively, things start looking grim faster than expected. While early post-CABG thrombocytopenia is common due to hemodilution and circuit consumption, this wasn't just "post-pump dip." From Day 1 to Day 5, his platelets didn't stabilize; they crashed from 155 down to 50 × 10⁹/L. By Day 6, they hit **26 × 10⁹/L**. Simultaneously, his mental status worsened from delirium to frank confusion. A CT brain revealed an acute ischemic stroke in the left parietal lobe.

Here is where the clinical reasoning gets critical. In the post-cardiac surgery setting, differentiating HIT from other causes of thrombocytopenia can be tricky. You have sepsis, DIC from surgical stress, or drug reactions to consider. However, the combination of **early-onset** thrombocytopenia (<Day 5), profound severity (nadir <20), and new thrombosis should immediately ring alarm bells for HIT—even if you think the timing is off for *classic* HIT. In this case, the "rapid-onset" nature was actually explained by the pre-operative UFH exposure during his catheterization one week prior. The immune system was already primed; the intra-op heparin was just the spark that lit the firecracker.

### The Lab Conundrum: That Indeterminate SRA

The PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive (OD 2.2), but here is where it gets messy—and educational for anyone interpreting lab data in real-time. The Serotonin Release Assay (SRA), our gold standard for confirming functional antibodies, came back "indeterminate." Why?

In classic HIT, adding high concentrations of heparin inhibits platelet activation (the "heparin neutralization" step). In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), antibodies activate platelets *without* needing heparin present at all. This patient's serum showed massive serotonin release at **0 U/mL** heparin—confirming heparin-independent activation typical of aHIT. However, when they added an Fc-receptor blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) meant to stop this activation via FcγRIIa receptors, it only *partially* inhibited the reaction (76% release remained). Usually, IVIG-like blockade should crush that activity below 20%. This partial resistance suggested an exceptionally potent antibody repertoire that wasn't playing by standard rules yet another reason why relying solely on algorithmic interpretation can lead you astray without clinical correlation.

### When IVIG Fails: Recognizing Treatment Failure

So far so good—we suspect severe aHIT due to pre-op sensitization and an atypical lab profile. We treat aggressively: we stop all heparins/low molecular weight heparins immediately and start therapeutic Argatroban while loading up with **high-dose IVIG** (1g/kg x 2 days).

In many cases of aHIT reported in literature at this stage, you'd expect a dramatic rebound in platelet counts within 48 hours as the IVIG saturates Fc receptors and clears circulating immune complexes. Not here. Despite receiving massive doses of IVIG over three consecutive days totaling hundreds of grams total load over two days... nothing happened effectively enough to stop the bleeding diathesis or reverse the thrombocytopenia quickly enough to feel safe against further strokes or hemorrhage complications from such extreme cytopenias associated with such extreme levels of circulating immune complexes still causing destruction despite being blocked temporarily by exogenous IgG molecules competing for binding sites on macrophages which may have become overwhelmed by sheer volume/pathogenicity! 

Wait—I mean seriously though-the patient's count lingered around **13-54 × 10⁹/L**. The lack of brisk recovery was our first clue that something deeper was going wrong beyond simple antibody blockade failure alone; perhaps overwhelming cytokine storm-like effects driving ongoing consumption despite theoretical neutralization attempts failing entirely due either excessive antigen burden exceeding available competitive inhibitors OR potentially distinct epitope targeting mechanisms rendering them ineffective altogether regardless dosage adjustments made thereafter! 

Furthermore—and this is huge—we saw biochemical signs of uncontrolled hypercoagulability persisting despite Argatroban therapy:
*   Falling fibrinogen levels
*   Rising D-Dimers peaking around POD12
*   Appearance of schistocytes on peripheral smear starting POD9

These weren't just numbers; they were screaming signals that the microvasculature was still clogged with micro-thrombi consuming factors faster than we could replace them or prevent their formation via anticoagulation alone! Standard practice might suggest sticking with Argatroban longer since it has short half-life safety margin compared alternatives—but sometimes persistence indicates insufficient efficacy requiring escalation strategy changes sooner rather than waiting until disaster strikes again next time round anyway!

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Early-Onset HIT After Cardiac Surgery:** If thrombocytopenia starts dropping before POD5 after cardiac surgery involving CPB+UFH + recent pre-op cath/heparin exposure → Think Rapid-Onset HIT/aHIT immediately rather than dismissing as surgical consumption alone!
    
*   **Atypical SRA Profiles Exist:** Be aware that some severe forms of auto-HIT show partial resistance to Fc-receptor blockade agents during testing; don't rule out diagnosis based purely on ## The Hidden Stroke: Navigating Neurological Complications in Post-Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia

We often think of Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) as a lab diagnosis first—a falling platelet count that triggers a reflexive order for a PF4 ELISA. But in the chaos of the ICU, particularly after cardiac surgery, the most dangerous manifestations of HIT can be terrifyingly silent or masquerade as something mundane.

Consider this scenario: An elderly man recovers from complex aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta repair. By postoperative day (POD) 5, his platelet count begins a steady decline, hitting 50×10⁹/L. He's not bleeding; he's just "confused." His family says he's acting out of character since yesterday. You might chalk this up to the usual suspects: postoperative delirium, lingering effects of anesthesia, or perhaps a urinary tract infection.

But here's the twist that turned this case report into a critical learning moment: **that confusion wasn't delirium.** It was an acute parietal lobe stroke.

This case, recently detailed by St Bernard et al., underscores a lethal diagnostic trap. While thrombocytopenia grabbed our attention, the neurological insult was hiding in plain sight, exacerbated by the fact that the patient had *preoperative* heparin exposure during a catheterization one week prior. This led to an unusually early onset of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), culminating in profound thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10⁹/L), severe consumptive coagulopathy, and a catastrophic ischemic event that presented solely as worsening mental status.

### The Diagnostic Tension: Delirium vs. Embolus

In the post-cardiac surgery population, distinguishing between metabolic delirium and structural brain injury is notoriously difficult. Patients are often on multiple medications, recovering from cardiopulmonary bypass (which itself causes neurocognitive dysfunction), and may have baseline cognitive deficits due to age or vascular disease.

In this specific case, the patient's platelets dropped precipitously from 210×10⁹/L pre-op to 50×10⁹/L by POD5. The trigger? Unconventional timing. Classic HIT typically manifests between POD5 and POD10 for patients with *postoperative* exposure only. However, this patient received unfractionated heparin (UFH) during a transradial heart catheterization just one week earlier. That "primed" his immune system with anti-PF4/heparin antibodies before he even went under for surgery. When hit again with massive intraoperative heparin loads during cardiopulmonary bypass, his antibody titers surged immediately upon re-exposure, accelerating the timeline significantly.

When his platelets plummeted further to 26×10⁹/L on POD6 despite stopping low-molecular-weight heparin (dalteparin) and starting fondaparinux prophylaxis, his mental status deteriorated rapidly from mild delirium to profound confusion. The team finally ordered a CT head only because of this progression—not because of focal signs like hemiparesis or aphasia—and found a large acute infarct in the left parietal lobe extending to the cortex.

### The Lab Conundrum: A Serological Wildcard

What makes this case truly fascinating for the hematologist and intensivist alike is the laboratory profile of his autoimmunity. This wasn't your garden-variety HIT; it was **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**.

Standard HIT antibodies require heparin to activate platelets via Fc receptors on PF4-heparin complexes. In contrast, aHIT antibodies can activate platelets *without* heparin present at all—a mechanism driven by their ability to cross-link Fc receptors directly or via alternative pathways involving PF4 oligomers independent of exogenous heparin binding sites in certain contexts (though still dependent on endogenous PF4).

The diagnostic curveball here came from the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA):
*   **Classic HIT:** Strong activation at low-to-moderate heparin doses; no activation at high doses ("prozone" effect).
*   **Patient's Profile:** Massive serotonin release (96%) at **zero** heparin concentration (the hallmark of aHIT).
*   **The Curveball:** Even when they added an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3), which normally shuts down platelet activation in classic HIT, this patient's serum still released 76% serotonin at low heparin concentrations. This partial resistance to blockade suggested an exceptionally aggressive antibody phenotype never before described in literature with such severity regarding Fc-receptor interaction dynamics.

### Management Challenges: Fighting Fire with Fire?

Treating this patient was like trying to put out a fire while standing on gasoline drop by drop. He had profound thrombocytopenia (<20k) and active thrombosis simultaneously—a setup where giving anticoagulation feels counterintuitive but withholding it is fatal.

Initial management involved switching to therapeutic argatroban and administering high-dose Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG). Why IVIG? Because in severe aHIT cases where antibodies drive massive platelet destruction via Fc receptors regardless of heparin presence, blocking those receptors with IVIG can sometimes halt consumption faster than anticoagulation alone can prevent new clots.

However, even with IVIG and argatroban, his course was grim for nearly two weeks:
*   Platelets dipped further to 13×10⁹/L before climbing slowly back up over six weeks.
*   D-dimers skyrocketed while fibrinogen crashed, signaling ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite therapeutic anticoagulation markers being targeted correctly via PTT adjustments for argatroban appears insufficient to stop the storm initially? ## Anticoagulation Dilemmas: Argatroban vs. Fondaparinux in Severe Post-Stroke Autoimmune HIT

We often teach residents that Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a "stop heparin, start non-heparin" algorithm. But what happens when the algorithm hits a wall? What if your patient isn't just dropping platelets, but actively forming clots in their brain while sitting on the lowest platelet count you've ever seen? And what if the standard "go-to" agents seem to fail or carry unacceptable risks in this specific, high-stakes scenario?

Today, we're diving into a fascinating and terrifying case report by St Bernard et al., detailing a patient who developed **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** following cardiac surgery. This wasn't your garden-variety HIT. We're talking about a profound thrombocytopenia dropping to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L, an acute ischemic stroke, and a bizarre serological profile that challenged even expert hematologists. More importantly, this case forces us to rethink how we manage anticoagulation when the bleeding risk feels imminent but the clotting risk is catastrophic.

### The Perfect Storm: Early Onset and Atypical Serology

The patient was an elderly gentleman undergoing complex cardiac surgery (AVR, ascending aortic replacement, CABG). While intraoperative heparin is the norm for cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), his platelet count didn't crash immediately post-op. Instead, it began a steady decline starting on Postoperative Day 1 (POD1), hitting 50×10⁹/L by POD5 and plummeting to 24×10⁹/L by POD6.

Here's where it gets tricky for the clinician: Classic HIT rarely presents this early in cardiac surgery patients unless they had significant *pre-operative* heparin exposure. In this case, the culprit was traced back to a diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior where he received 2000 U of unfractionated heparin (UFH). That "priming" event set the stage for an explosive immune response upon re-exposure during CPB.

But the real twist wasn't just the timing; it was the lab work. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2). However, the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA)—the gold standard—came back "indeterminate." Why? Because the patient's antibodies were activating platelets **without** heparin (96% release at 0 U/mL), confirming **Autoimmune HIT**. Even more unique was that adding an Fc-receptor blocking antibody (IV.3) only partially inhibited this activation. This partial resistance suggested an unusually aggressive antibody phenotype that doesn't play by the usual rules of Fc-gamma receptor clustering.

### The Therapeutic Tightrope: Stroke + 13k Platelets

By POD6, the patient suffered an acute parietal lobe stroke as his platelets hit rock bottom at 13×10⁹/L. You can imagine the conference room debate: Do we treat with full anticoagulation knowing we might cause an intracranial hemorrhage in someone with <15k platelets? Or do we withhold anticoagulation and let another massive clot form?

The team chose to walk the tightrope with **argatroban**, a direct thrombin inhibitor administered via continuous IV infusion. It was chosen over fondaparinux initially because of its short half-life (~45 mins), offering theoretical safety if emergency neurosurgery became necessary due to hemorrhagic conversion of the stroke. They also loaded up on **high-dose IVIG** (100g x 3 days) to try and blunt that relentless autoimmune platelet activation—a cornerstone strategy for aHIT that targets Fc receptors directly.

However, here lies the crux of our learning opportunity: Despite IVIG and argatroban, the patient continued to show signs of rampant consumption coagulopathy. His fibrinogen dropped progressively, D-dimers skyrocketed, and schistocytes appeared on his smear from POD9 onward. The argatroban simply wasn't taming the hypercoagulable storm driven by those rogue autoantibodies fast enough—or perhaps not effectively enough—to stop microvascular thrombosis.

### The Pivot: Why Switch to Fondaparinux?

On POD14, facing persistent markers of consumptive coagulopathy despite optimal argatrobon dosing, the team made a bold pivot: they switched from IV argatroban to **therapeutic-dose fondaparinux** (10 mg SC daily).

This decision flies in the face of traditional teaching for two reasons:
1.  **Renal Clearance:** Fondaparinux is renally cleared and has a longer half-life (~17 hours). In a post-cardiac surgery patient with potential renal fluctuations or active bleeding risk, stopping it quickly is impossible compared to argatroban or bivalirudin.
2.  **"Off-Label" Status & Monitoring:** While fondaparinux is widely used off-label for HIT in Europe and Canada, US guidelines historically favored DTIs or DOACs more recently (though evidence is evolving). Furthermore, unlike warfarin or DTIs monitored by PT/INR or PTT respectively, fondaparinux lacks routine bedside monitoring tools like anti-Xa assays in many institutions during acute care settings.

So why make this move? The answer lies in mechanism and consistency of action against autoimmune drivers of HIT. Argatroban inhibits free thrombin beautifully but may struggle when large amounts of thrombin are generated locally within immune complexes or when platelet activation itself drives further coagulation cascades independent of circulating thrombin levels alone. Fondaparinux acts upstream as an indirect Factor Xa inhibitor via antithrombin III suppression of Xa activity—a potent blockage point for propagating clots once antithrombin levels were corrected with supplementation in this case.

The result? A dramatic turnaround. After switching to fondaparinux (with supplemental antithrombin), his consumptive coagulopathy halted entirely. Platelets slowly crept back up over weeks, eventually normalizing by POD62, and his neurological deficits resolved completely without new bleeds or strokes. Follow-up SRA testing months later confirmed that these wild autoantibodies eventually waned into typical heparin-dependent patterns before disappearing altogether.

### Take-Home Pearls from This Case

*   **Don't rule out HIT just because it's early:** If a cardiac surgery patient drops plates before POD5-Hit Window check carefully for recent pre-op heparin exposure (like cardiac catheterization); pre-existing sensitization can trigger rapid-onset autoimmune HIT immediately post-bypass.*
*   **Recognize Autoimmune HIT phenotypes:** When SRA shows strong platelet activation *without* added heparin (>20% release at 0 U/mL), think aHIT immediately.*
*   **IVIG is critical but not always sufficient:** High-dose IVIG should be part of your arsenal for severe aHIT with life-threatening thrombosis or extreme thrombocytopenia (*nadir <20*), but don't rely on it alone if hypercoagulability persists.*
*   **Re-evaluate anticoagulant failure:** If a patient remains consumptive on argatroban/bivalirudin despite adequate PTT targets in severe HIT/aHIT contexts consider switching mechanisms (e.g., to fondaparinux) even with bleeding risks; sometimes stopping downstream propagation isn't enough—you need upstream blockade.*
*   **Monitor closely during transitions:** When moving from DTI to fondaparinux ensure antithrombin levels are adequate (<70% requires replacement) since fondaparinux activity depends heavily on endogenous antithrombin availability.*
*   **Longitudinal testing matters:** Serology evolves! Initial indeterminate or atypical SRA profiles can normalize over time; serial testing helps confirm resolution and guides safe return to oral anticoagulants.*
