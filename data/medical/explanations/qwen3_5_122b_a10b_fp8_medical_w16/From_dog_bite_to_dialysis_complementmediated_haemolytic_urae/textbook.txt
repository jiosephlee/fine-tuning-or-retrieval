Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma: Sepsis vs. Thrombotic Microangiopathy

The clinical narrative begins with a critical exposure history that sets the stage for a rare but devastating infectious complication. The patient, a previously healthy woman in her late 40s, sustained a dog bite to the upper lip four days prior to admission. Although she received tetanus prophylaxis, a crucial lapse in care occurred when prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin was never dispensed or initiated due to logistical barriers. This delay allowed *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod residing in canine oropharyngeal flora, to proliferate unchecked. In immunocompetent hosts, such bites typically result in localized cellulitis; however, in this case, the lack of timely antibiotic coverage facilitated rapid bacterial translocation and systemic invasion. The patient's subsequent presentation of high fever exceeding 40°C, profound malaise, and numbness with discoloration of the feet represents the transition from local inoculation to fulminant bacteremia, a known precursor to severe sepsis and multiorgan failure associated with this organism.

Upon arrival at the emergency department, the patient exhibited a classic triad indicative of severe septic shock complicated by microvascular catastrophe: refractory hypoglycemia, mottled extremities with cool skin perfusion, and rapid hemodynamic deterioration. The hypoglycemia was particularly notable for its resistance to both oral glucose and glucagon administration, reaching a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L; this phenomenon is often driven by cytokine-mediated suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis and increased peripheral glucose consumption during overwhelming sepsis. Physical examination revealed mottling and cyanosis of the lower extremities without signs of necrotizing fasciitis upon surgical evaluation. These cutaneous findings signaled widespread microthrombosis and ischemia rather than simple vasoconstriction, hinting at an underlying coagulopathy that mimics thrombotic processes even before specific hematologic markers confirm them.

Laboratory investigation on admission unveiled a constellation of findings consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia rather than isolated organ dysfunction. The patient presented with pancytopenia—a reduction across all three cell lines—and significant coagulopathy characterized by prolonged clotting times and low fibrinogen levels typical of consumptive coagulopathy. Metabolic profiling showed severe metabolic acidosis with an elevated lactate of 5.7 mmol/L, reflecting tissue hypoperfusion and mitochondrial dysfunction inherent to septic shock. Crucially, the initial interpretation favored DIC over Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) because classic markers of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) were either absent or confounded by the acute inflammatory state. Specifically, haptoglobin levels were reported as normal, and reticulocyte counts were non-compensatory; these findings led clinicians to initially rule out Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS). In the context of active DIC and severe inflammation, haptoglobin can be consumed by binding free hemoglobin released from lysed cells but may also appear "normal" due to its role as an acute-phase reactant produced by the liver during sepsis, thereby masking true hemolysis until later stages when synthetic function fails or hemolysis becomes overtly dominant.

The diagnostic landscape at admission was fraught with complexity as physicians navigated differentials between acute leukemia, Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), and sepsis-induced TMA. The presence of pancytopenia raised immediate concern for bone marrow infiltration or failure common in leukemia or HLH; however, flow cytometry and subsequent bone marrow planning yielded normal results regarding blast counts and phenotypic abnormalities, effectively excluding malignancy and making HLH less likely given the absence of specific criteria like extreme ferritin elevation or splenomegaly at that juncture. Consequently, the working diagnosis settled on severe sepsis with secondary DIC explaining the multiorgan involvement including acute kidney injury (AKI) and liver enzyme elevation. It is vital for trainees to recognize that early in the course of infection-triggered TMA, clinical features often overlap significantly with sepsis-induced DIC; both present with thrombocytopenia and organ failure. The distinction lies in recognizing that while DIC involves widespread clotting factor consumption leading to bleeding risks alongside thrombosis, TMA is primarily driven by endothelial injury causing platelet-rich microthrombi that occlude small vessels without necessarily consuming clotting factors to the same extent initially seen here. As illustrated in this case, the absence of schistocytes on initial smears or clear evidence of hemolysis does not definitively exclude evolving TMA when superimposed on fulminant sepsis; rather, it necessitates vigilant serial monitoring as the clinical picture evolves once the primary infectious insult begins to resolve under appropriate antibiotic therapy.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Unmasking Complement-Mediated HUS After Sepsis Resolution

The diagnostic trajectory of this case illustrates a critical clinical lesson: the resolution of overt sepsis does not necessarily signal the end of organ-threatening complications. Initially, the patient's presentation was dominated by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia manifesting as septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and multiorgan failure. During this acute phase, pancytopenia and coagulopathy masked the specific signature of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). However, a distinct temporal shift occurred approximately eight days into the hospitalization. As inflammatory markers normalized, platelet counts began to recover, and coagulation parameters corrected—signs of resolving DIC—the patient experienced a sudden, isolated deterioration characterized by a precipitous drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. This dissociation between the improving sepsis profile and the emergence of new hematologic crisis signaled a superimposed pathological process rather than simple sepsis sequelae. The clinical picture evolved into classic microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), evidenced by undetectable haptoglobin, rising lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and the appearance of schistocytes on peripheral smear, necessitating a rigorous re-evaluation of the differential diagnosis for TMA.

The first major diagnostic pivot involved distinguishing Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) from other forms of TMA. Given the presence of MAHA and thrombocytopenia, TTP was a life-threatening possibility that required immediate exclusion. The diagnostic standard relies heavily on ADAMTS13 activity levels; severe deficiency (<10%) confirms TTP. In this case, although the PLASMIC score initially suggested a low pre-test probability for TTP (score 4/7), definitive laboratory confirmation was required. The subsequent measurement of ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5% effectively ruled out TTP, as values above 10% make severe deficiency virtually impossible. Consequently, plasma exchange (PEX) served primarily as a temporizing bridge to stabilize the patient while awaiting confirmatory data for other etiologies, rather than as a curative measure for an underlying protease deficiency.

Simultaneously, the team had to address the confounding finding of a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT), or Coombs test, which initially raised suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). A positive DAT led to an empirical trial of high-dose corticosteroids; however, the lack of response in hemoglobin levels and platelet counts rendered AIHA unlikely upon further reflection. In retrospect, this was recognized as a false-positive result driven by drug interference or prior transfusion exposure rather than true autoimmunity. Penicillin-class antibiotics are well-known to bind to red blood cell membranes in vivo or in vitro, potentially causing non-immune adsorption that yields a positive DAT without true complement-mediated lysis. The persistence of schistocytes and thrombocytopenia despite steroid therapy reinforced that the mechanism was mechanical fragmentation within microvasculature rather than antibody-mediated destruction. Furthermore, Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded through negative stool PCR analysis and the absence of a prodromal diarrheal illness, narrowing the differential significantly toward complement-mediated mechanisms or secondary infection-triggered TMA.

Definitive characterization of the pathology relied on renal biopsy findings once coagulopathy had resolved sufficiently to allow safe tissue sampling under anticoagulation precautions absent from standard practice in active DIC but feasible here due to stabilized clotting times. The histopathology provided crucial differentiation between ischemic injury caused by sepsis alone versus frank thrombotic microangiopathy. While severe acute tubular necrosis was present—a common sequela of prolonged hypotension—the biopsy revealed glomerular fibrin thrombi and scattered crescent formation without immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence or primary glomerulopathy on electron microscopy. These findings confirmed endothelial injury with intraluminal thrombus formation characteristic of TMA. Combined with normal C3/C4 levels—which do not exclude CM-HUS—and functional assays showing alternative pathway dysregulation only after treatment initiation, the diagnosis solidified as complement-mediated HUS triggered by *C. canimorsus*. This case underscores that in patients recovering from severe bacterial sepsis who develop unexplained MAHA and renal failure despite resolving DIC and negative ADAMTS13/TTP workups, clinicians must maintain high suspicion for secondary CM-HUS requiring targeted complement inhibition rather than supportive care alone.

## Pathophysiology: Infectious Triggers and Dysregulation of the Alternative Complement Pathway

The fundamental mechanism underlying complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) is the loss of control over the alternative complement pathway, leading to uncontrolled amplification and consumption of complement components directly on host cell surfaces. Under physiological conditions, this pathway serves as a rapid, innate immune defense against pathogens; however, in CM-HUS, regulatory proteins such as Factor H, Factor I, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46) fail to adequately inhibit the C3 convertase. This results in a "runaway" reaction where C3b deposits indiscriminately on endothelial cells rather than being cleared or regulated. The subsequent formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) causes direct endothelial injury, triggering platelet adhesion, microthrombus formation, and the mechanical shearing of red blood cells that characterizes microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). While approximately 50% to 70% of CM-HUS cases are driven by germline mutations in genes encoding these regulatory proteins or their receptors, disease penetrance is incomplete. Consequently, a clinical manifestation typically requires an external precipitating event—a "second hit"—such as infection, pregnancy, or medication exposure, to tip the balance from compensated homeostasis to overt thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

In this specific case report, the patient's clinical trajectory illustrates the concept of infection-triggered CM-HUS in an individual without a demonstrable inherited predisposition. Extensive genetic screening failed to identify pathogenic variants in complement genes, distinguishing this presentation from classic familial atypical HUS. Instead, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* served as the potent environmental trigger capable of activating the alternative pathway in a host who might otherwise have remained asymptomatic. The interaction between *C. canimorsus* and the host immune system involves a complex paradox regarding serum sensitivity. As an encapsulated Gram-negative rod found in canine flora, *C. canimorsus* possesses surface structures that can confer varying degrees of resistance to complement-mediated lysis; some strains express capsules that limit opsonization and phagocytosis. However, during bacteremia, the sheer burden of bacterial antigens and the host's attempt to clear these organisms can overwhelm local regulatory mechanisms. The bacterial components likely acted as potent activators of C3 convertase on vascular endothelium. In a susceptible host—even one without a known mutation—the intense inflammatory milieu and direct bacterial activation could disrupt the delicate equilibrium between activation and regulation, leading to localized endothelial damage specifically within the renal microvasculature where filtration pressures are highest.

A common diagnostic pitfall in understanding this pathophysiology is the expectation that standard serum complement levels (C3 and C4) must be low during an acute episode. In many systemic inflammatory states involving massive complement consumption, such as sepsis-associated DIC or lupus nephritis, C3 and C4 levels are indeed depleted because they are consumed faster than they can be synthesized by the liver. However, in CM-HUS, especially when triggered by infection rather than primary genetic defects causing chronic low-level activation, baseline C3 and C4 concentrations often remain within normal reference ranges at presentation. This occurs because the dysregulation is primarily localized to the site of endothelial injury (the glomerulus) rather than representing a global systemic depletion of circulating factors. Furthermore, acute-phase reactants produced during severe sepsis can upregulate hepatic synthesis of complement proteins simultaneously with their consumption at tissue sites, masking any net decrease in serum concentration. Therefore, normal C3/C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS; instead, they reflect that the pathological process is driven by qualitative dysregulation or localized consumption rather than quantitative global exhaustion alone.

The downstream consequence of this unchecked alternative pathway activation is a cascade culminating in thrombotic occlusion of glomerular capillaries. Once C5b-9 forms on endothelial surfaces lacking sufficient protection from regulatory proteins like CD46 or soluble Factor H complexes, it induces sublethal injury that exposes pro-thrombotic subendothelial matrix proteins such as von Willebrand factor multimers. Simultaneously, activated endothelium releases pro-inflammatory cytokines and downregulates anticoagulant molecules like thrombomodulin and heparan sulfate proteoglycans. This environment promotes platelet aggregation and fibrin deposition within small vessels. In the kidneys seen in this patient's biopsy—characterized by fibrin thrombi within glomeruli—ischemic damage ensues as these microthrombi obstruct blood flow to podocytes and tubules. The resulting ischemia leads to acute tubular necrosis and glomerular collapse (ischaemic glomeruli), explaining the profound acute kidney injury and eventual anuria observed before therapeutic intervention restored perfusion through complement blockade with eculizumab. Understanding this sequence underscores why simply treating the infection or managing coagulopathy with plasma exchange alone was insufficient; definitive resolution required halting the terminal complement step responsible for sustaining endothelial destruction until healing could occur.
