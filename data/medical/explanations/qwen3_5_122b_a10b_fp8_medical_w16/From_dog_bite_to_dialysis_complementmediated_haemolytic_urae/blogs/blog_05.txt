Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Genetic Luck vs. Environmental Trigger: Acquired CM-HUS Without Mutations

We tend to think of Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS) as a game of Russian roulette played with your DNA. The classic teaching is that a "first hit" exists—a germline mutation in *CFH*, *CFI*, or *MCP*—waiting for a "second hit," usually an infection, to tip the scales into catastrophic endothelial damage. It's a seductive narrative because it explains why some people get sick while their asymptomatic relatives don't. But what happens when the gun fires, the chamber spins, and we find absolutely no genetic smoking gun?

That is exactly the puzzle presented in a recent case report by Fredriksen et al., detailing the journey of a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who went from a simple dog bite on her lip to dialysis-dependence via **_Capnocytophaga canimorsus_-mediated CM-HUS**.

What makes this case particularly fascinating isn't just the rarity of the pathogen or the dramatic clinical course; it's the therapeutic implication of finding **no pathogenic complement variants** in a patient with severe, life-threatening TMA. This forces us to confront a shifting paradigm: Can severe environmental insults alone be enough to unleash complement dysregulation, and if so, does that change how long we keep patients on eculizumab?

## From Lip Bite to Dialysis: A Sepsis Masquerade

The story begins four days after a dog bite. No fancy immunosuppression, no chronic illnesses—just a healthy woman who didn't fill her penicillin prescription. She arrived at the ED in fulminant septic shock: fevers over 40°C, profound hypoglycemia refractory to dextrose, and mottled extremities. The initial workup screamed Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC): pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and acute kidney injury (AKI). _Capnocytophaga canimorsus_ was suspected immediately given the exposure history and confirmed seven days later by blood culture.

She survived the sepsis storm with aggressive antibiotics and supportive care, eventually stabilizing hemodynamically. But then came the twist. As her inflammatory markers improved and platelets began to recover from their sepsis-induced nadir, she suddenly crashed again—but this time with **isolated hemolysis**. Her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L in six days without overt bleeding. Platelets stayed stubbornly low (30–40 × 10⁹/L), haptoglobin vanished, and LDH skyrocketed.

Here lies the diagnostic trap: The Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) was initially positive for IgG, leading clinicians down the rabbit hole of Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA). High-dose steroids were started, but they failed miserably. It wasn't until a peripheral smear revealed 15–20% schistocytes that the diagnosis pivoted back toward Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA).

ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (ruling out TTP), Stx-PCR was negative (ruling out STEC-HUS), and renal biopsy confirmed fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli consistent with TMA. With standard C3/C4 levels normal—a common red herring—the diagnosis settled on CM-HUS triggered by _C. canimorsus_.

## The Genetic Paradox: When "Atypical" Isn't Genetic

The patient underwent extensive genetic sequencing for complement regulators (*CFH*, *CFI*, *CFB*, *MCP*, etc.). The result? **No pathogenic variants found.**

This is where things get intellectually spicy for us as clinicians. Historically, we've labeled these cases "atypical HUS" implying an inherent genetic flaw waiting for an activator. However, this case suggests that massive antigenic load and intense complement activation from a virulent organism like _C. canimorsus_ might be sufficient to overwhelm even a genetically "normal" regulatory system temporarily.

_Capnocytophaga_ is notorious for this; its capsule allows it to resist phagocytosis but renders it highly susceptible to complement-mediated lysis. In susceptible hosts (whether due to subtle polymorphisms we haven't detected yet or purely acquired dysregulation), this interaction spirals out of control, causing widespread endothelial injury and microthrombi.

The fact that she responded robustly to eculizumab confirms that terminal complement inhibition was necessary regardless of whether there was an underlying germline mutation or not. But does this distinction matter for long-term management? That is the million-dollar question hanging over her outpatient follow-up today as she remains on maintenance eculizumab with only partial renal recovery (creatinine 175 µmol/L).

## Clinical Pearls: Navigating TMA in Severe Sepsis

If you remember nothing else from this case, let these principles guide your next shift when faced with post-septic TMA:

*   **Sepsis doesn't always equal DIC:** If coagulopathy resolves but MAHA persists or worsens after sepsis improvement, suspect superimposed TMA immediately. Don't assume it's all residual DIC just because CRP is coming down slowly.
*   **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS:** Up to half of patients with confirmed CM-HUS have normal complement levels during active disease because consumption balances production or because the assay measures total protein rather than functionally activated complexes.
*   **DAT positivity can be misleading:** Drug-induced AIHA or transfusion artifacts can yield false-positive Coombs tests in critically ill patients; rely heavily on smear morphology (schistocytes) and clinical context over serology alone when differentiating AIHA from MAHA/TTP/HUS.
*   **Eculizumab timing matters:** While Plasma Exchange (PEX) provided transient stabilization here by clearing cytokines and autoantibodies, true hematologic remission only occurred after eculizumab blockade was initiated early in the course of established TMA.
*   **Vaccinate before blocking:** Always administer meningococcal vaccines (and consider prophylactic antibiotics) before starting anti-C5 therapy, especially when dealing with organisms like _Neisseria_ or _Capnocytophaga_, which are cleared via terminal complement pathways. Waiting until cultures are negative reduces infection risk but shouldn't delay therapy indefinitely once TMA is confirmed; balance is key here based on local protocols and stability.

## To Treat Forever or Not To Treat Forever?

The lingering question for this patient—and for anyone managing acquired CM-HUS without mutations—is duration of therapy. Current guidelines lean toward indefinite treatment if genetic defects are present due to high relapse rates upon withdrawal. However, in cases where no mutation is found and there is a clear precipitating event like severe bacterial sepsis that has resolved? The data is murky at best observational at worst speculative regarding stopping rules for those without mutations vs those with them may favor shorter courses if renal function recovers sufficiently—but only under strict surveillance protocols designed specifically around individualized risk assessment strategies tailored each patient scenario carefully balancing recurrence risks against drug toxicity costs long-term burdens placed families healthcare systems alike! 

Until prospective trials clarify these thresholds, we must weigh the risk of relapse against lifelong dependency on expensive biologics with infectious risks attached—especially since our patient has already proven resilient enough to survive both sepsis AND successful transition off dialysis despite lacking those pesky genetic errors everyone expected us to find! Perhaps future research will redefine "atypical HUS" less about genetics & more about phenotype response patterns observed clinically moving forward... stay tuned!
