Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Beyond TTP and Stool Toxins: Navigating the Diagnostic Labyrinth of Adult Acute Kidney Injury

We love a classic board question. You see anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ failure? Your brain should instantly scream **Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia **(MAHA) and race through the "Big Three" differentials: TTP, STEC-HUS, and Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). It's usually a clean algorithmic flowchart in your head: check ADAMTS13, order a stool PCR, maybe send off complements. If those come back normal, you move on to something else.

But medicine rarely respects algorithms until they've humbled us once or twice.

Today we're dissecting a fascinating case from *BMJ Case Reports* where a dog bite didn't just give a wound—it triggered a cascade leading to dialysis-dependence via **_Capnocytophaga canimorsus_-induced CM-HUS**. What makes this case a pearl worth hanging around is how easily the true pathology was masked by overwhelming sepsis and Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), and how the subsequent resolution required navigating tricky lab interpretations regarding ADAMTS13 and complement levels. Let's break down the journey from "just sepsis" to life-saving eculizumab.

## The Setup: Dog Bite Gone Wrong

Our patient is a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who suffered a minor dog bite to her upper lip four days prior to admission. No antibiotics were taken initially (she couldn't get the script filled!). Instead of local cellulitis, she crashed hard into septic shock with hyperthermia, profound hypoglycemia (refractory to dextrose/glucagon), lactic acidosis, and mottled extremities.

Initial workup painted the picture of catastrophic multiorgan failure: pancytopenia, coagulopathy fitting for DIC, and acute kidney injury (AKI). Blood cultures eventually grew _Capnocytophaga canimorsus_, confirming our worst fears about zoonotic bites in adults. She was admitted to the ICU for aggressive resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics (Pip/Tazo), and eventually Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT) as she went anuric with developing cardiac dysfunction.

At this stage, everything looked like typical Gram-negative sepsis complicated by DIC. But then came plot twist #1. As her infectious parameters improved and inflammatory markers dropped on Day 8-ish, she suddenly plummeted again—not with fever or tachycardia, but with **isolated massive hemolysis** (hemoglobin dropping from 111 to 68 g/L) and persistent thrombocytopenia despite clearing the bacteremia.

## The Great Mimicker: Sepsis vs. Superimposed TMA

Here lies the first major teaching point: **Sepsis/DIC can mask TMA**, or vice versa. Initially, her hematologic team rightly hesitated to diagnose HUS because HUS classically spares white cells (leukopenia here pointed toward HLH or leukemia/marrow suppression), and haptoglobin wasn't crashing immediately due to acute-phase reactant dynamics in sepsis. Bone marrow biopsies were benign; malignancy was ruled out.

However, once the septic storm calmed slightly on the nephrology ward, the veil lifted. We saw pure Mechanical Destruction of RBCs:
*   Schistocytes comprising 15–20% of red cells on smear.
*   Undetectable haptoglobin.
*   Sky-high LDH.
*   Persistent thrombocytopenia that wouldn't recover despite treating the infection.

She was now clearly manifesting Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA). The challenge shifted from "Is it infection?" to "**What kind of TMA?**" Because getting this wrong dictates whether you plunk them on plasma exchange indefinitely or start complement inhibition—a drug that carries significant risk in an already fragile host with recent bacteremia (_Neisseria_/_Capnocyphoga_ risk).

## Decoding the Labs: Why Normal Doesn't Mean Negative

This is where things get nuanced for our readership preparing for boards or managing complex wards rounds alike. We need to address two specific pitfalls highlighted by this case report regarding ADAMTS13 and Complement Levels.

First up: **ADAMTS13 Activity**. In classic adult teaching—if it's >10%, it ain't TTP right? Right? Well... mostly yes, but look at her value: **43.5%**. That is definitely above the <10% cutoff for TTP definitively excluding it via ELISA criteria used today (*PLASMIC* score would be low too). Yet, it sits in a grey zone compared to near-normal controls (~60-90%). Some older texts might worry about mild deficiencies triggering subtle issues; modern consensus says anything >10% effectively rules out acquired severe deficiency driving TTP-like physiology unless there's clear inhibitor interference elsewhere—but crucially here—the biopsy proved TMA regardless!
