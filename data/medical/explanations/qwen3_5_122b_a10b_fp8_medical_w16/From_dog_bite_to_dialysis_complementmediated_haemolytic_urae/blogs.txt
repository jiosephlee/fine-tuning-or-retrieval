Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Beyond the Bite: Unmasking Complement-Mediated HUS After *Capnocytophaga* Sepsis

We often think of dog bites as minor injuries that might need a Tetanus shot and a course of Augmentin. But occasionally, a seemingly innocuous scratch or bite unleashes a cascade of pathology that defies our standard algorithms. Consider this case: a previously healthy woman in her late 40s, four days post-dog bite, crashing into septic shock with profound hypoglycemia, pancytopenia, and coagulopathy. The initial narrative screams *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia leading to Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC). And for the first week, that's exactly what we treat. She receives broad-spectrum antibiotics, transfusions, and dialysis for acute kidney injury (AKI). She stabilizes. The fever breaks. The white count rebounds. It looks like victory.

Then, just as the sepsis resolves, the plot thickens. Instead of returning to baseline, her hemoglobin plummets again. Her platelets refuse to recover. Schistocytes flood the peripheral smear. What looked like resolving DIC has morphed into something far more insidious: Complement-mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by the very infection we just fought off. This case is a masterclass in why "sepsis" isn't always the whole story and how TMA can masquerade behind the veil of DIC until it's almost too late to intervene effectively.

### The Diagnostic Trap: DIC vs. TMA

The initial presentation was classic for fulminant *C. canimorsus*. We're talking about an encapsulated Gram-negative rod found in canine saliva that loves to cause catastrophic sepsis in humans, especially those with splenic dysfunction (though our patient was immunocompetent). She presented with refractory hypoglycemia—a known but under-recognized feature of severe capnocytophagia—and signs of multiorgan failure including liver enzyme elevation and coagulopathy.

In the ER and early ICU days, the differential was crowded: acute leukemia? Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)? Or simply overwhelming sepsis-induced DIC? The presence of leukopenia made HUS seem unlikely initially, as classic HUS usually presents with normal or elevated white counts while consuming platelets and RBCs. Furthermore, haptoglobin was normal and reticulocytes weren't compensating yet because the hemolysis hadn't fully kicked in. We treated aggressively for sepsis with Piperacillin/Tazobactam, ruled out necrotizing fasciitis surgically, and waited for cultures to grow—which took seven days because *C. canimorsus* is notoriously fastidious.

By Day 8, she was transferred to nephrology on dialysis but appeared clinically stable regarding her infection markers. Then came the pivot point: an isolated drop in hemoglobin from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without active bleeding or hemodynamic collapse. Platelets lingered in the 30–40 × 10⁹/L range while inflammatory markers improved significantly elsewhere. This dissociation is your red flag. If it were pure sepsis recovery or resolving DIC, you'd expect platelet counts to rise as coagulation factors normalize and inflammation subsides instead of remaining stubbornly low while hemolysis accelerates.

### Unmasking the Microangiopathy

When you see falling Hb + persistent thrombocytopenia + rising LDH + undetectable haptoglobin in a post-septic patient who isn't bleeding out everywhere else, you must suspect Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA). But which type?

First rule out TTP immediately using ADAMTS13 activity; anything above 10% rules it out (ours was 43%). Next, check for Shiga toxin; ours was negative via PCR ruling out STEC-HUS. That leaves us with Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), formerly known as atypical HUS (aHUS). Here lies the trap: **normal C3 and C4 levels do NOT rule out CM-HUS.** Up to half of patients have normal complement levels during their acute episode because consumption happens locally at the endothelium or is masked by synthesis rates during inflammation recovery.

Our patient's renal biopsy provided the smoking gun: fibrin thrombi in glomeruli and ischemic changes consistent with TMA, but crucially **no** immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence and no evidence of primary glomerulopathy on electron microscopy. This confirmed that we were dealing with a complement-driven process rather than an immune-complex mediated vasculitis or drug reaction (despite a fleeting positive Direct Antiglobulin Test which turned out to be a false positive likely due to penicillin exposure).

### Why Did It Happen Now? The Pathophysiological Paradox

It feels counterintuitive that CM-HUS appears *after* the sepsis seems controlled rather than concurrently with it. The mechanism here involves a perfect storm of bacterial virulence and host genetics/environmental triggers. *C. canimorsus* possesses a polysaccharide capsule that offers some resistance to complement-mediated killing, yet its interaction with host factors can trigger massive alternative pathway activation on vascular endothelium rather than just clearing bacteria from circulation.

Think of it this way: The infection acts as the "second hit." Even without identifying a germline mutation in complement regulatory genes (like Factor H or I) in this specific patient—which happened here—the sheer magnitude of complement activation driven by the bacteria overwhelmed local regulation mechanisms on the kidney endothelium once systemic inflammation began to wane and clearance mechanisms shifted focus back homeostatic balance attempts went awry triggering unchecked lysis platelet consumption microthrombi formation downstream organ damage ensued specifically kidneys suffered worst leading dialysis dependency eventually partial recovery only after targeted intervention blocked terminal pathway component C5 preventing further attack vessels walls themselves!

### Therapeutic Dilemma: To Block or Not To Block?

Treating CM-HUS during active bacteremia is fraught with danger because eculizumab blocks C5, disabling one of our primary defenses against encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and yes—*Capnocytophaga*. Starting eculizumab too early could turn a contained infection into fatal sepsis recurrence; waiting too long risks irreversible renal loss or death from ongoing thrombotic storms inside tiny vessels throughout body organs beyond just kidneys alone!

The strategy employed here strikes a delicate balance:
1.  **Clearance First:** Ensure blood cultures are negative before initiating terminal complement blockade if possible (though clinical judgment sometimes necessitates earlier action if deterioration is rapid).
2.  **Bridge Therapy:** Use Plasma Exchange (PEX) temporarily while awaiting confirmatory labs or preparing vaccines/antibiotic coverage against meningococcal disease (*N.* meningitidis serogroups A,C,Y,W,B). Note that PEX helped stabilize transiently but didn't cure; true recovery only happened after eculizumab administration proved effective at stopping ongoing microvascular thrombosis events directly targeting root cause dysfunction itself!

## The Normal C3/C4 Trap: Why Standard Labs Miss Atypical HUS

We love a good dog bite story, but usually, we're worried about rabies or *Pasteurella*. Recently, a case report from Norway reminded us that the canine mouth harbors a far more insidious villain in specific contexts: *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. But the real teaching point here isn't just about the organism; it's about the diagnostic blind spot that nearly derailed our understanding of this patient's trajectory.

Imagine a previously healthy woman in her late 40s. Four days after a dog bite to her lip, she crashes with septic shock, profound pancytopenia, and DIC. It looks like textbook fulminant bacteremia. She gets antibiotics, survives the storm of sepsis, and moves to the nephrology ward. Then comes the plot twist: she starts crashing again, not from infection recurrence, but from isolated hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia.

She has all the hallmarks of Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA): schistocytes on smear, sky-high LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury. Yet, when you order the "rule-out" panel for Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS), you get normal C3 and C4 levels. The reflex is to think, "Well, if complements aren't consumed, it can't be complement-mediated." That logic is dangerously wrong.

### The Pathophysiological Paradox

Here is where we need to pause and rethink our mental model of complement consumption. In classic immune-complex mediated diseases like Lupus Nephritis or Cryoglobulinemia, you see low C3 *and* low C4 because both the classical and alternative pathways are activated simultaneously. It's a fire consuming fuel on multiple fronts.

However, CM-HUS (formerly known as atypical HUS) is driven primarily by dysregulation of the **alternative pathway**. This pathway acts as an amplification loop that can run away autonomously once triggered. Crucially, this runaway process does not necessarily deplete total serum levels of C3 or C4 enough to fall outside reference ranges during the acute phase. The dysregulation happens at the level of control proteins (like Factor H or Factor I), leading to local endothelial destruction and thrombus formation in the kidneys without causing massive systemic consumption detectable by standard serology.

In this specific case report, the patient had perfectly normal C3/C4 levels initially. Even functional assays for classical and lectin pathways were normal. It wasn't until specialized testing revealed a markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 at 6%) that the diagnosis clicked into place—but even then, those results came after she had already received plasma exchange and eculizumab. If you relied solely on standard C3/C4 levels to make your decision-making algorithm stop at "not CM-HUS," you would have missed a treatable cause of renal failure entirely.

### Navigating the Differential in Sepsis-Associated TMA

Differentiating TMA subtypes in a septic patient is notoriously difficult because sepsis itself can induce secondary TMA through endothelial injury and cytokine storms ("sepsis-associated TMA"). So how do we know when to suspect true CM-HUS?

First, look at the timeline relative to infection control. In this patient's case, hemolysis emerged *after* clinical signs of sepsis improved and bacterial clearance was underway. If it were purely sepsis-driven endothelial damage resolving with antibiotics alone, the hemolysis should have abated as inflammation subsided instead of exploding into frank MAHA weeks later.

Second, rule out TTP aggressively but efficiently. This patient had an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%. Remember that TTP is defined by severe deficiency (<10%). Anything above 10% effectively rules out TTP as the primary driver. Similarly, Shiga toxin PCR was negative, ruling out STEC-HUS (typical HUS). Once you've knocked out TTP and STEC-HUS in an adult with MAHA and AKI who doesn't fit an obvious secondary cause (like malignant hypertension or drug toxicity), CM-HUS rises to the top of the list—even with normal complement levels.

The renal biopsy was definitive here: fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries with no immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence confirmed TMA without an immune-complex etiology (ruling out lupus or cryoglobulins). This histologic picture combined with persistent hemolysis despite resolving infection pointed squarely toward complement dysregulation triggered by *C. canimorsus*.

### The Therapeutic Dilemma: To Block or Not to Block?

Treating CM-HUS requires eculizumab (a terminal complement inhibitor). But doing so in a patient recently cleared of *C. canimorsus* creates a genuine clinical tension. Eculizumab blocks C5a generation and MAC formation—the very mechanisms needed to kill encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. Starting it too early could unleash uncontrolled reinfection; waiting too long could result in irreversible end-stage renal disease (ESRD).

In this case report, the team struck a careful balance: they ensured blood cultures were negative before administering eculizumab and initiated robust prophylaxis with meningococcal vaccines (*Menveo* and *Bexsero*) plus penicillin prophylaxis until full vaccine coverage was established (which takes weeks). They also used plasma exchange as a bridge—not because it cures CM-HUS long-term (it doesn't fix the underlying regulatory defect), but because it removes circulating inflammatory mediators while buying time for eculizumab to work and vaccines to take effect. The return of spontaneous diuresis happened only after eculizumab was introduced, confirming that blocking terminal complement was necessary for organ recovery in this scenario despite normal baseline complement levels.

### Take-Home Points for Your Next Shift

*   **Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out CM-HUS:** Up to 50% of patients with genetically confirmed CM-HUS present with normal total complement levels during acute episodes because only the alternative pathway is locally dysregulated without massive systemic consumption.
*   **ADAMTS13 >10% excludes TTP:** Do not wait for genetic results if ADAMTS13 activity confirms >10%; move quickly down the differential toward HUS/TMA causes once TTP is excluded clinically or biochemically in favor of other etiologies like infection-triggered events or STEC-HUS if indicated by history/labs are available immediately; otherwise proceed empirically based on clinical suspicion while awaiting confirmatory testing if resources allow for immediate action plans are made concurrently!
*   **Sepsis can be a trigger:** Don't dismiss new-onset MAHA/AKI simply because there was recent sepsis; infection can act as the "second hit" triggering latent complement dysregulation even in patients without known genetic mutations (*de novo* presentation).
*   **Biopsy matters:** Renal biopsy showing thrombotic microangiopathy without immune deposits helps distinguish primary CM-HUS from secondary forms caused by immune complexes or drugs when serology is equivocal!
*   **Vaccinate before blocking:** If starting eculizumab post-infection clearance is deemed necessary due to progressive organ damage despite supportive care measures being insufficiently effective alone—ensure appropriate vaccinations AND antimicrobial prophylaxis protocols are strictly adhered to minimize risk associated with terminal complement inhibition therapy!



# Eculizumab vs. Active Bacteremia: Navigating the Therapeutic Paradox

We've all seen the classic teaching: "Don't give eculizumab until you've cleared the infection." It's a mantra drilled into us because blocking terminal complement (C5) essentially blinds your innate immune system to encapsulated bacteria. But what happens when the patient is dying from a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) triggered by that very same encapsulated bug? Do you watch them progress to end-stage renal disease waiting for cultures to clear, or do you gamble on a targeted therapy that could unleash a catastrophic secondary infection?

This isn't just theoretical. A recent case report by Fredriksen et al. brings this exact nightmare to life, describing a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who went from a seemingly minor dog bite to dialysis dependence in less than two weeks. Her journey through *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis and subsequent complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) offers a masterclass in navigating one of the trickiest therapeutic paradoxes in modern critical care nephrology.

### The Setup: Dog Bite to Sepsis Shock
The patient arrived with a four-day history of worsening fever, malaise, and numbness in her feet after being bitten on the lip by her own dog. She hadn't taken her prescribed antibiotics—a common scenario that sets the stage for disaster with fastidious organisms like *C. canimorsus*. On admission, she was in full-blown septic shock with profound metabolic acidosis, refractory hypoglycemia, and pancytopenia.

Initially, this looked like textbook Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) secondary to overwhelming sepsis. The clinical team correctly identified *C. canimorsus* as the likely culprit given the exposure history and started broad-spectrum coverage with piperacillin/tazobactam. For several days, she stabilized slightly; inflammatory markers trended down, and coagulopathy resolved. She was transferred off the ICU floor, looking like she might pull through with supportive care alone.

Then came the plot twist. As her sepsis began to recede, she developed **isolated**, severe hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia. Her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without bleeding. Schistocytes flooded her peripheral smear (15–20%), haptoglobin vanished, and LDH skyrocketed. The initial suspicion of autoimmune hemolytic anemia was quickly dashed when high-dose steroids failed and the Direct Antiglobulin Test (Coombs) proved falsely positive—likely due to prior transfusions or penicillin exposure—and then turned negative on repeat testing.

With ADAMTS13 activity at 43% (ruling out TTP) and Shiga toxin PCR negative (ruling out STEC-HUS), the diagnosis crystallized: Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS), triggered by *C. canimorsus*. The kidney biopsy confirmed it with fibrin thrombi in glomeruli and ischemic changes consistent with TMA.

### The Dilemma: To Block or Not to Block?
Here lies the crux of our educational challenge. The gold standard treatment for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against C5 that halts uncontrolled complement activation on endothelial surfaces. Early initiation is strongly correlated with renal recovery; delays often mean permanent dialysis dependence or death from multiorgan failure caused by microvascular thrombosis.

However, *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated Gram-negative rod found in canine saliva, sharing a biological vulnerability profile with *Neisseria meningitidis*. Terminal complement components are absolutely critical for lysing these organisms via membrane attack complexes (MAC). By administering eculizumab while viable bacteria are present—or even lingering in low numbers—you theoretically remove the patient's primary defense mechanism against fulminant reinfection or uncontrolled bacteremia.

In this case, the treating team faced a ticking clock:
1.  **Risk of Waiting:** Continued microvascular thrombosis leading to irreversible kidney loss or fatal organ ischemia if they waited too long for bacterial clearance before giving eculizumab.
2.  **Risk of Acting:** Inducing overwhelming sepsis if they gave eculizumab before confirming total sterilization of blood cultures or establishing robust prophylaxis against encapsulated organisms (*N.* meningitidis and *C.* canimorsus).

## ADAMTS13 >10% and Schistocytes Present: The Algorithm for Adult TMA

We've all seen the classic "triad" presentation in the ED: a patient walks in with anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI). The immediate reflex for many clinicians is to shout "TTP!" and order plasma exchange (PEX) before lunch. But here's the hard truth: **not all thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs) are created equal.**

If you treat every TMA like Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP), you might miss the diagnosis that actually needs a different weapon entirely. We recently reviewed a fascinating case from *BMJ Case Reports* that perfectly illustrates this diagnostic tightrope walk: a previously healthy woman who went from a simple dog bite to life-threatening sepsis, and then, paradoxically, developed Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

Her journey highlights exactly why understanding the algorithm for adult TMA isn't just academic—it's the difference between saving kidneys and losing them.

### The Patient: A Dog Bite Gone Wrong

Let's set the scene. A woman in her late 40s presents four days after being bitten by her dog on the upper lip. She hadn't taken her prescribed antibiotics. She arrives in fulminant septic shock with hyperpyrexia, hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, and signs of Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC). Blood cultures eventually grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod notorious for causing fulminant sepsis in asplenic or immunocompromised hosts—but this patient was immunocompetent.

She gets treated aggressively with broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam), supportive care, and ICU-level monitoring. Surprisingly, she starts to turn the corner. The sepsis resolves, inflammatory markers drop, and her coagulopathy normalizes. She is stable enough to move from the ICU to a nephrology ward for dialysis-dependent AKI.

But then comes the plot twist. Just as she seems to be recovering, she develops **isolated hemolysis**. Her hemoglobin plummets from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without bleeding. Platelets stay stubbornly low (30–40 × 10⁹/L). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) spikes, haptoglobin becomes undetectable, and a peripheral smear reveals 15–20% schistocytes.

She has Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA). The question now shifts from "How do we fix the sepsis?" to "What kind of TMA is this?"

### Navigating the Diagnostic Maze: TTP vs. STEC-HUS vs. CM-HUS

When faced with MAHA + Thrombocytopenia + AKI in an adult, your brain should immediately fire up a three-pronged differential: TTP, Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC-HUS), and Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS). Here is how our patient walked us through that decision tree.

**Step 1: Rule Out TTP First**
TTP is the medical emergency that kills fastest if untreated. You need an ADAMTS13 level immediately. If it's <10%, it's TTP until proven otherwise. In this case, ADAMTS13 came back at **43.5%**. That effectively rules out TTP. Even though her PLASMIC score was intermediate-low (4/7), waiting for labs while giving PEX as a bridge is standard practice because missing TTP is catastrophic. But once ADAMTS13 returns >10%, you must pivot your thinking away from TTP-specific therapies toward other causes of TMA.

**Step 2: Check for STEC-HUS**
Next up is *E. coli*. Did she have diarrhea? Was there a prodrome? No history of enteric illness here, but guidelines demand you check anyway via stool PCR for Shiga toxin-like toxins. It was negative in this case. This leaves us with non-STEC HUS—specifically CM-HUS or secondary TMAs driven by infection/drugs/autoimmunity.

**Step 3: The Trap of Normal Complement Levels**
Here lies one of the biggest pitfalls in diagnosing CM-HUS (formerly called atypical HUS). Clinicians often look at C3 and C4 levels expecting them to be low because "complement consumption" sounds like they should be depleted by definition of the disease name (**C**omplement-mediated H**U**S). They aren't always! In fact, C3/C4 levels are often within normal limits during an active flare because production outpaces consumption or because specific regulatory proteins are mutated without altering total serum levels of C3/C4 protein itself. Relying solely on standard complement panels can lead to missed diagnoses of CM-HUS if you don't consider functional assays or genetic testing later on.

### The Therapeutic Pivot: Why PEX Isn't Enough

So we have a patient with MAHA, low platelets, AKI, schistocytes present, ADAMTS13 >10%, Shiga toxin negative, and normal C3/C4 levels but clear histologic evidence of TMA on renal biopsy (fibrin thrombi in glomeruli). The working diagnosis is **Complement-Mediated HUS triggered by *C. canimorsus*** infection.

This brings us to the critical therapeutic distinction that defines modern management of these cases: **Plasma Exchange vs. Eculizumab.**

In our case report's timeline, PEX was initiated immediately upon suspicion while waiting for confirmatory data—a reasonable "bridge." And guess what happened? After three sessions of PEX using Octaplasma plasma exchange units? Her spontaneous diuresis returned temporarily! It looked like magic! But wait... did we cure her? No! PEX removes circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators; it does not stop the upstream dysregulation driving the alternative pathway activation permanently in CM-HUS once those factors return or re-equilibrate without blocking terminal complement activation itself (*e.g.*, eculizumab/ravulizumab binding C5). # Genetic Luck vs. Environmental Trigger: Acquired CM-HUS Without Mutations

We tend to think of Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS) as a game of Russian roulette played with your DNA. The classic teaching is that a "first hit" exists—a germline mutation in *CFH*, *CFI*, or *MCP*—waiting for a "second hit," usually an infection, to tip the scales into catastrophic endothelial damage. It's a seductive narrative because it explains why some people get sick while their asymptomatic relatives don't. But what happens when the gun fires, the chamber spins, and we find absolutely no genetic smoking gun?

That is exactly the puzzle presented in a recent case report by Fredriksen et al., detailing the journey of a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who went from a simple dog bite on her lip to dialysis-dependence via **_Capnocytophaga canimorsus_-mediated CM-HUS**.

What makes this case particularly fascinating isn't just the rarity of the pathogen or the dramatic clinical course; it's the therapeutic implication of finding **no pathogenic complement variants** in a patient with severe, life-threatening TMA. This forces us to confront a shifting paradigm: Can severe environmental insults alone be enough to unleash complement dysregulation, and if so, does that change how long we keep patients on eculizumab?

## From Lip Bite to Dialysis: A Sepsis Masquerade

The story begins four days after a dog bite. No fancy immunosuppression, no chronic illnesses—just a healthy woman who didn't fill her penicillin prescription. She arrived at the ED in fulminant septic shock: fevers over 40°C, profound hypoglycemia refractory to dextrose, and mottled extremities. The initial workup screamed Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC): pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and acute kidney injury (AKI). _Capnocytophaga canimorsus_ was suspected immediately given the exposure history and confirmed seven days later by blood culture.

She survived the sepsis storm with aggressive antibiotics and supportive care, eventually stabilizing hemodynamically. But then came the twist. As her inflammatory markers improved and platelets began to recover from their sepsis-induced nadir, she suddenly crashed again—but this time with **isolated hemolysis**. Her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L in six days without overt bleeding. Platelets stayed stubbornly low (30–40 × 10⁹/L), haptoglobin vanished, and LDH skyrocketed.

Here lies the diagnostic trap: The Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) was initially positive for IgG, leading clinicians down the rabbit hole of Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA). High-dose steroids were started, but they failed miserably. It wasn't until a peripheral smear revealed 15–20% schistocytes that the diagnosis pivoted back toward Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA).

ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (ruling out TTP), Stx-PCR was negative (ruling out STEC-HUS), and renal biopsy confirmed fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli consistent with TMA. With standard C3/C4 levels normal—a common red herring—the diagnosis settled on CM-HUS triggered by _C. canimorsus_.

## The Genetic Paradox: When "Atypical" Isn't Genetic

The patient underwent extensive genetic sequencing for complement regulators (*CFH*, *CFI*, *CFB*, *MCP*, etc.). The result? **No pathogenic variants found.**

This is where things get intellectually spicy for us as clinicians. Historically, we've labeled these cases "atypical HUS" implying an inherent genetic flaw waiting for an activator. However, this case suggests that massive antigenic load and intense complement activation from a virulent organism like _C. canimorsus_ might be sufficient to overwhelm even a genetically "normal" regulatory system temporarily.

_Capnocytophaga_ is notorious for this; its capsule allows it to resist phagocytosis but renders it highly susceptible to complement-mediated lysis. In susceptible hosts (whether due to subtle polymorphisms we haven't detected yet or purely acquired dysregulation), this interaction spirals out of control, causing widespread endothelial injury and microthrombi.

The fact that she responded robustly to eculizumab confirms that terminal complement inhibition was necessary regardless of whether there was an underlying germline mutation or not. But does this distinction matter for long-term management? That is the million-dollar question hanging over her outpatient follow-up today as she remains on maintenance eculizumab with only partial renal recovery (creatinine 175 µmol/L).

## Clinical Pearls: Navigating TMA in Severe Sepsis

If you remember nothing else from this case, let these principles guide your next shift when faced with post-septic TMA:

*   **Sepsis doesn't always equal DIC:** If coagulopathy resolves but MAHA persists or worsens after sepsis improvement, suspect superimposed TMA immediately. Don't assume it's all residual DIC just because CRP is coming down slowly.
*   **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS:** Up to half of patients with confirmed CM-HUS have normal complement levels during active disease because consumption balances production or because the assay measures total protein rather than functionally activated complexes.
*   **DAT positivity can be misleading:** Drug-induced AIHA or transfusion artifacts can yield false-positive Coombs tests in critically ill patients; rely heavily on smear morphology (schistocytes) and clinical context over serology alone when differentiating AIHA from MAHA/TTP/HUS.
*   **Eculizumab timing matters:** While Plasma Exchange (PEX) provided transient stabilization here by clearing cytokines and autoantibodies, true hematologic remission only occurred after eculizumab blockade was initiated early in the course of established TMA.
*   **Vaccinate before blocking:** Always administer meningococcal vaccines (and consider prophylactic antibiotics) before starting anti-C5 therapy, especially when dealing with organisms like _Neisseria_ or _Capnocytophaga_, which are cleared via terminal complement pathways. Waiting until cultures are negative reduces infection risk but shouldn't delay therapy indefinitely once TMA is confirmed; balance is key here based on local protocols and stability.

## To Treat Forever or Not To Treat Forever?

The lingering question for this patient—and for anyone managing acquired CM-HUS without mutations—is duration of therapy. Current guidelines lean toward indefinite treatment if genetic defects are present due to high relapse rates upon withdrawal. However, in cases where no mutation is found and there is a clear precipitating event like severe bacterial sepsis that has resolved? The data is murky at best observational at worst speculative regarding stopping rules for those without mutations vs those with them may favor shorter courses if renal function recovers sufficiently—but only under strict surveillance protocols designed specifically around individualized risk assessment strategies tailored each patient scenario carefully balancing recurrence risks against drug toxicity costs long-term burdens placed families healthcare systems alike! 

Until prospective trials clarify these thresholds, we must weigh the risk of relapse against lifelong dependency on expensive biologics with infectious risks attached—especially since our patient has already proven resilient enough to survive both sepsis AND successful transition off dialysis despite lacking those pesky genetic errors everyone expected us to find! Perhaps future research will redefine "atypical HUS" less about genetics & more about phenotype response patterns observed clinically moving forward... stay tuned!
