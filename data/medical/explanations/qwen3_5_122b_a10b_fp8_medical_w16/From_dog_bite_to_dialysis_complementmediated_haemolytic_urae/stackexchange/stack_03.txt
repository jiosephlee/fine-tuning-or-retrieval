Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Diagnostic Reasoning] Why is a Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) potentially unreliable in distinguishing autoimmune hemolytic anemia from drug-induced or infection-associated hemolysis in patients who have recently received multiple blood transfusions or beta-lactam antibiotics?

The unreliability of the Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) in this setting stems from the phenomenon of **passive adsorption** and **drug-induced hapten formation**, which can generate false-positive results that mimic true autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA).

In the case presented, the patient's initial DAT was positive for IgG, leading to a presumptive diagnosis of AIHA and a trial of high-dose corticosteroids. However, this result was a diagnostic pitfall caused by two specific factors inherent to her clinical course: recent exposure to beta-lactam antibiotics (phenoxymethylpenicillin and piperacillin/tazobactam) and the administration of multiple blood product transfusions. Beta-lactams can bind tightly to red blood cell membranes, acting as haptens that recruit IgG antibodies, thereby yielding a positive DAT even in the absence of an intrinsic autoimmune process. Similarly, recent transfusions introduce donor erythrocytes coated with alloantibodies or residual recipient antibodies that can be detected by the Coombs reagent.

Crucially, this false-positive DAT obscured the true underlying pathology: **complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS)** triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis. As evidenced by the subsequent clinical course, the patient failed to respond to steroids—a hallmark clue against AIHA—and instead exhibited classic features of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), including persistent thrombocytopenia and a peripheral smear revealing 15–20% schistocytes. The definitive correction came upon repeating the DAT after clearing the confounding variables, which yielded a negative result.

Therefore, in patients with recent transfusion history or beta-lactam exposure presenting with hemolysis, a positive DAT must be interpreted with extreme caution. It does not confirm AIHA until other causes of TMA are rigorously excluded via smear review (looking for schistocytes rather than spherocytes) and assessment of clinical response to immunosuppression. Relying solely on the DAT in this context risks delaying life-saving therapies like eculizumab or plasma exchange for conditions like CM-HUS or TTP.
