Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] Given that Eculizumab blocks terminal complement component C5, what specific life-threatening infection risk is introduced, and what mandatory prophylactic measures must be implemented before or concurrently with initiating this therapy?

Eculizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to the complement component C5, prevents its cleavage into C5a and C5b. By blocking the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), it halts the uncontrolled complement-mediated endothelial damage characteristic of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS). However, this mechanism carries a profound and specific risk: **encapsulated bacterial infections**, most notably invasive disease caused by *Neisseria meningitidis* (meningococcus).

The terminal complement pathway is critical for the opsonization and lysis of encapsulated organisms. Without a functional C5-C9 cascade, the host loses a primary defense mechanism against these pathogens. Consequently, patients receiving eculizumab face a significantly elevated risk of fulminant meningococcemia, which can progress to septic shock and death with alarming speed. While *Neisseria* is the classic concern, the case you reviewed illustrates that this vulnerability extends to other encapsulated bacteria; indeed, our patient's original insult was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, an organism whose clearance relies heavily on complement-mediated killing. Initiating eculizumab in the setting of active bacteremia would theoretically exacerbate such infections, underscoring why we waited until blood cultures cleared in this case.

To mitigate this life-threatening risk, strict adherence to prophylactic guidelines is mandatory before or concurrently with the first dose of eculizumab. The standard of care involves **vaccination against all four major serogroups of *N. meningitidis*** (A, C, W, Y) and **serogroup B**. As detailed in our case report, this patient received Menveo (covering A, C, Y, W) and Bexsero (covering serogroup B). Ideally, these vaccinations should be completed at least two weeks prior to initiating therapy to allow for an adequate immune response. Because vaccine efficacy may be suboptimal during active treatment or if therapy cannot be delayed for two weeks—as was necessary here due to the severity of her thrombotic microangiopathy—we must implement **concurrent antimicrobial prophylaxis**. In this patient's course, we administered prophylactic penicillin (phenoxymethylpenicillin/Apocillin) because the vaccination window could not be met prior to drug initiation. Patients must also receive education regarding early signs of meningococcal infection and seek immediate medical attention for any febrile illness. This dual strategy of vaccination plus antibiotic coverage remains non-negotiable when prescribing terminal complement inhibitors.
