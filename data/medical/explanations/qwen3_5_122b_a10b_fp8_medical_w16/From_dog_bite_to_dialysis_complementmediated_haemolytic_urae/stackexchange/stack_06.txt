Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the limitations of measuring serum C3 and C4 levels when diagnosing Complement-Mediated HUS, and why might these values appear normal despite active disease?

The measurement of serum C3 and C4 levels has significant limitations in the diagnosis of Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS), primarily because **normal levels do not exclude the diagnosis**. As illustrated in our case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-induced CM-HUS, the patient's standard complement studies (C3 and C4) were within normal limits despite active, life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

This phenomenon occurs due to the specific pathophysiology of CM-HUS. Unlike classic immune-complex mediated diseases where complement consumption leads to low C3/C4, CM-HUS is driven by **dysregulation** of the alternative complement pathway rather than simple consumption. The underlying defect often involves mutations or autoantibodies against regulatory proteins (such as Factor H, Factor I, or Membrane Cofactor Protein), leading to uncontrolled amplification of the C3 convertase on endothelial surfaces. Because this dysregulation is localized to the site of vascular injury and does not necessarily result in global systemic depletion of circulating complement proteins, serum levels can remain within reference ranges. Furthermore, in acute phases triggered by infection—as seen here with *C. canimorsus*—the synthesis of complement proteins by the liver may keep pace with consumption, masking any potential deficit.

Therefore, relying solely on C3 and C4 levels carries a high risk of missing the diagnosis. In this patient, the definitive clue was not a depressed complement level but rather the clinical context: persistent hemolysis and thrombocytopenia emerging after sepsis resolution, coupled with a kidney biopsy confirming TMA and an ADAMTS13 activity >10% (ruling out TTP). Diagnostic confirmation ultimately relied on **functional assays**, specifically demonstrating markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50) and/or identifying pathogenic genetic variants, rather than static protein quantification. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for CM-HUS even when "routine" complement screens are normal, particularly when TMA persists after the resolution of an infectious trigger.
