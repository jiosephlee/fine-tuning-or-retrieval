Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient presenting with fever, pancytopenia, and coagulopathy following a dog bite, what specific organism must be suspected, and why might standard blood culture results be initially misleading or delayed?

In a patient presenting with fever, pancytopenia, and coagulopathy following a dog bite, **_Capnocytophaga canimorsus_** must be the primary suspect. As illustrated in the case of the 40-year-old woman who progressed from a simple lip bite to fulminant sepsis and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, _C. canimorsus_ is a fastidious, encapsulated gram-negative rod residing in the canine oropharynx that can precipitate overwhelming bacteremia, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) even in immunocompetent hosts.

Standard blood culture results are often initially misleading or significantly delayed because _C. canimorsus_ is notoriously slow-growing and fastidious. It requires specific conditions to thrive, leading to prolonged incubation times before colonies become visible; in our case report, confirmation took seven days despite immediate suspicion upon presentation. During this diagnostic window, clinicians must rely heavily on the clinical triad of recent animal exposure, septic shock with DIC, and rapid multiorgan failure to initiate empiric coverage with beta-lactams (such as piperacillin-tazobactam or ceftriaxone), as delays in appropriate therapy are associated with mortality rates approaching 30%. Furthermore, one must maintain a high index of suspicion for secondary complications like complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), which may manifest as isolated hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia only after the initial septic phase begins to resolve, complicating the differential diagnosis between infection-driven DIC and primary TMA.
