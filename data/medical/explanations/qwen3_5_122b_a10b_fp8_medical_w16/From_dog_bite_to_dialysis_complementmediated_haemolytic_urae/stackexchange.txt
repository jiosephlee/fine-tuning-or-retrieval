Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient presenting with fever, pancytopenia, and coagulopathy following a dog bite, what specific organism must be suspected, and why might standard blood culture results be initially misleading or delayed?

In a patient presenting with fever, pancytopenia, and coagulopathy following a dog bite, **_Capnocytophaga canimorsus_** must be the primary suspect. As illustrated in the case of the 40-year-old woman who progressed from a simple lip bite to fulminant sepsis and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, _C. canimorsus_ is a fastidious, encapsulated gram-negative rod residing in the canine oropharynx that can precipitate overwhelming bacteremia, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) even in immunocompetent hosts.

Standard blood culture results are often initially misleading or significantly delayed because _C. canimorsus_ is notoriously slow-growing and fastidious. It requires specific conditions to thrive, leading to prolonged incubation times before colonies become visible; in our case report, confirmation took seven days despite immediate suspicion upon presentation. During this diagnostic window, clinicians must rely heavily on the clinical triad of recent animal exposure, septic shock with DIC, and rapid multiorgan failure to initiate empiric coverage with beta-lactams (such as piperacillin-tazobactam or ceftriaxone), as delays in appropriate therapy are associated with mortality rates approaching 30%. Furthermore, one must maintain a high index of suspicion for secondary complications like complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), which may manifest as isolated hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia only after the initial septic phase begins to resolve, complicating the differential diagnosis between infection-driven DIC and primary TMA.

### [Morning Report] How does the timing of symptom onset distinguish Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) from Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) in the context of severe sepsis, and what specific laboratory finding would confirm TMA once DIC has resolved?

In this case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis complicated by thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), the temporal evolution of symptoms serves as the critical discriminator between Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) and TMA. Initially, the patient's presentation was dominated by classic signs of sepsis-induced DIC: profound coagulopathy with prolonged PT/PTT, hypofibrinogenemia, and pancytopenia occurring concurrently with hemodynamic collapse. Crucially, in pure DIC, hematologic parameters generally track with the resolution of the septic insult; as the infection is controlled and inflammation subsides, coagulation profiles normalize.

However, this patient exhibited a distinct "second hit" phenotype. Approximately eight days into her hospitalization, after her inflammatory markers normalized and coagulation parameters fully recovered (indicating resolved DIC), she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin accompanied by persistent thrombocytopenia. This dissociation—where macrovascular clotting resolves while microvascular thrombosis worsens—is the hallmark distinguishing feature here. The emergence of overt hemolysis (evidenced by undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH) and the appearance of schistocytes (15–20%) on the peripheral smear *after* the normalization of coagulation studies confirms a superimposed TMA rather than persistent DIC.

To definitively confirm TMA once DIC has resolved, one must demonstrate **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)** characterized specifically by **schistocytes** on a peripheral blood smear in conjunction with thrombocytopenia and organ injury (in this case, dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury). In this specific scenario, where TTP was excluded by ADAMTS13 activity >10% and STEC-HUS was ruled out by negative stool PCR, the diagnosis was cemented by renal biopsy findings showing fibrin thrombi in glomeruli without immune complex deposition. Furthermore, functional complement testing revealing a markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 of 6%) provided the mechanistic confirmation of Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS), distinguishing it from other etiologies.

### [Diagnostic Reasoning] Why is a Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) potentially unreliable in distinguishing autoimmune hemolytic anemia from drug-induced or infection-associated hemolysis in patients who have recently received multiple blood transfusions or beta-lactam antibiotics?

The unreliability of the Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) in this setting stems from the phenomenon of **passive adsorption** and **drug-induced hapten formation**, which can generate false-positive results that mimic true autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA).

In the case presented, the patient's initial DAT was positive for IgG, leading to a presumptive diagnosis of AIHA and a trial of high-dose corticosteroids. However, this result was a diagnostic pitfall caused by two specific factors inherent to her clinical course: recent exposure to beta-lactam antibiotics (phenoxymethylpenicillin and piperacillin/tazobactam) and the administration of multiple blood product transfusions. Beta-lactams can bind tightly to red blood cell membranes, acting as haptens that recruit IgG antibodies, thereby yielding a positive DAT even in the absence of an intrinsic autoimmune process. Similarly, recent transfusions introduce donor erythrocytes coated with alloantibodies or residual recipient antibodies that can be detected by the Coombs reagent.

Crucially, this false-positive DAT obscured the true underlying pathology: **complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS)** triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis. As evidenced by the subsequent clinical course, the patient failed to respond to steroids—a hallmark clue against AIHA—and instead exhibited classic features of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), including persistent thrombocytopenia and a peripheral smear revealing 15–20% schistocytes. The definitive correction came upon repeating the DAT after clearing the confounding variables, which yielded a negative result.

Therefore, in patients with recent transfusion history or beta-lactam exposure presenting with hemolysis, a positive DAT must be interpreted with extreme caution. It does not confirm AIHA until other causes of TMA are rigorously excluded via smear review (looking for schistocytes rather than spherocytes) and assessment of clinical response to immunosuppression. Relying solely on the DAT in this context risks delaying life-saving therapies like eculizumab or plasma exchange for conditions like CM-HUS or TTP.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiological 'paradox' regarding *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* and the complement system: how does the bacterium's interaction with complement contribute to both host defense and the subsequent development of Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS)?

The "paradox" you describe lies in the dual role of the alternative complement pathway: it is simultaneously the primary mechanism of bacterial clearance and the potential driver of catastrophic host tissue injury in this specific clinical scenario.

**Host Defense Mechanism:**
*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is an encapsulated Gram-negative rod. As detailed in the case report, the host's innate immune defense relies heavily on the alternative complement pathway to opsonize and lyse these organisms. The polysaccharide capsule often confers resistance to phagocytosis, making complement-mediated lysis essential for survival. In this patient, initial complement activation was necessary to control the bacteremia following the dog bite; indeed, the case notes that many *C. canimorsus* isolates are serum-sensitive, meaning their destruction depends on intact complement function. Without this activation, the infection would likely have progressed unchecked to fatal sepsis earlier in the course.

**Pathogenesis of HUS (The Paradox):**
However, in susceptible individuals—or potentially due to overwhelming antigen load—the same robust complement activation becomes dysregulated. The case illustrates a classic "two-hit" model where an environmental trigger (*C. canimorsus*) precipitates a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in a host who may lack identifiable germline mutations (this patient had no pathogenic variants despite extensive testing).

The pathophysiological sequence proceeds as follows:
1.  **Uncontrolled Activation:** The bacterial surface components trigger excessive consumption of alternative pathway regulators (Factor H, Factor I). Even though baseline C3/C4 levels were normal initially—a common finding in CM-HUS—the local regulation fails at the endothelial interface.
2.  **Endothelial Injury:** Unchecked formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) occurs directly on vascular endothelium, particularly in the renal microvasculature. This causes direct endothelial cell death and detachment.
3.  **Microthrombosis:** Exposed subendothelial matrix promotes platelet adhesion and aggregation, leading to widespread microvascular thrombosis. This manifests clinically as Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA), evidenced by schistocytes and elevated LDH, and thrombocytopenia due to platelet consumption within these clots.
4.  **Renal Failure:** The occlusion of glomerular capillaries leads to ischemic injury and acute kidney injury, as confirmed by this patient's biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and crescent formation without immune complex deposition.

**Clinical Resolution:**
The resolution of this paradox required interrupting the cycle at its terminus while managing the infectious risk. Standard supportive care and plasma exchange provided only transient stabilization because they could not stop ongoing terminal complement activation at the cellular level. As highlighted in this case, definitive management required **eculizumab**, a monoclonal antibody blocking C5 cleavage into C5a and C5b-9. By inhibiting terminal complement assembly, we halted further endothelial damage while allowing time for bacterial clearance via antibiotics and non-complement mediated mechanisms (e.g., neutrophils once opsonized by other means or cleared after capsular shedding).

Thus, *C. canimorsus* acts as a potent trigger that converts a protective immune response into a self-destructive cascade of hemolysis and renal failure when regulatory thresholds are exceeded, necessitating targeted complement blockade for survival despite the inherent risk of recurrent encapsulated bacterial infection.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating an adult with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, what is the critical role of ADAMTS13 activity levels in differentiating Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) from Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS), and what threshold defines TTP?

In the evaluation of an adult presenting with the classic triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI), determining the etiology of the thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is paramount because the treatments differ radically. The measurement of ADAMTS13 activity is the definitive discriminator between Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) and Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

The critical threshold defining TTP is an ADAMTS13 activity level **below 10%**. This severe deficiency reflects an acquired or congenital lack of the von Willebrand factor-cleaving protease, leading to the accumulation of ultra-large vWF multimers and subsequent platelet-rich microthrombi. In contrast, CM-HUS is characterized by dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, where ADAMTS13 activity is typically preserved or only mildly reduced.

Our case illustrates this distinction clearly. The patient initially presented with *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), which complicated the clinical picture. As her infection improved, she developed a distinct phase of isolated MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia with dialysis-dependent AKI. While plasma exchange was initiated empirically as a bridge, our ADAMTS13 assay returned at **43.5%**. This result definitively excluded TTP, as values above 10% render that diagnosis highly unlikely regardless of clinical suspicion. Consequently, we shifted focus to CM-HUS, confirmed by the exclusion of Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*, normal C3/C4 levels (which do not rule out CM-HUS), and renal biopsy findings consistent with TMA. The preservation of ADAMTS13 activity guided us away from prolonged reliance on plasma exchange—which offers only transient benefit in CM-HUS—and toward the targeted therapy of eculizumab, resulting in hematologic remission and partial renal recovery. Thus, obtaining ADAMTS13 levels is not merely academic; it is a decisive step that prevents misdiagnosis and directs life-saving therapy toward complement inhibition rather than continued plasmapheresis alone.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the limitations of measuring serum C3 and C4 levels when diagnosing Complement-Mediated HUS, and why might these values appear normal despite active disease?

The measurement of serum C3 and C4 levels has significant limitations in the diagnosis of Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS), primarily because **normal levels do not exclude the diagnosis**. As illustrated in our case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-induced CM-HUS, the patient's standard complement studies (C3 and C4) were within normal limits despite active, life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

This phenomenon occurs due to the specific pathophysiology of CM-HUS. Unlike classic immune-complex mediated diseases where complement consumption leads to low C3/C4, CM-HUS is driven by **dysregulation** of the alternative complement pathway rather than simple consumption. The underlying defect often involves mutations or autoantibodies against regulatory proteins (such as Factor H, Factor I, or Membrane Cofactor Protein), leading to uncontrolled amplification of the C3 convertase on endothelial surfaces. Because this dysregulation is localized to the site of vascular injury and does not necessarily result in global systemic depletion of circulating complement proteins, serum levels can remain within reference ranges. Furthermore, in acute phases triggered by infection—as seen here with *C. canimorsus*—the synthesis of complement proteins by the liver may keep pace with consumption, masking any potential deficit.

Therefore, relying solely on C3 and C4 levels carries a high risk of missing the diagnosis. In this patient, the definitive clue was not a depressed complement level but rather the clinical context: persistent hemolysis and thrombocytopenia emerging after sepsis resolution, coupled with a kidney biopsy confirming TMA and an ADAMTS13 activity >10% (ruling out TTP). Diagnostic confirmation ultimately relied on **functional assays**, specifically demonstrating markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50) and/or identifying pathogenic genetic variants, rather than static protein quantification. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for CM-HUS even when "routine" complement screens are normal, particularly when TMA persists after the resolution of an infectious trigger.

### [Management] Compare the mechanisms of action of Plasma Exchange (PLEX) versus Eculizumab in the treatment of CM-HUS. Why was PLEX utilized as a bridge therapy rather than definitive monotherapy in this case?

The fundamental distinction between Plasma Exchange (PLEX) and Eculizumab in Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS) lies in their ability to address the underlying pathophysiology. PLEX acts as a temporizing measure by mechanically removing activated complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, and potential autoantibodies from circulation while replenishing missing regulatory factors like Factor H. However, it does not correct the inherent dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway; once the infusion stops, the uncontrolled cascade resumes. In contrast, Eculizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds with high affinity to terminal complement component C5, preventing its cleavage into C5a and C5b. By blocking the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), Eculizumab halts the downstream endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy at its source, providing definitive disease control.

In this specific case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered CM-HUS, PLEX was appropriately utilized as a "bridge" therapy rather than definitive monotherapy for two critical reasons derived from our clinical course. First, while PLEX provided transient stabilization—evidenced by the return of spontaneous diuresis after the final sessions—it failed to sustain hematological remission or prevent persistent thrombocytopenia and hemolysis on its own. Second, and perhaps more importantly in this scenario involving active bacterial sepsis, there was a significant theoretical risk that PLEX alone might remove protective complement factors necessary for clearing the encapsulated *C. canimorsus*, potentially exacerbating the infection without stopping the TMA. Therefore, we initiated PLEX to stabilize the patient's microvascular thrombosis while awaiting confirmatory ADAMTS13 results and arranging for Eculizumab. Once blood cultures cleared and meningococcal vaccination was administered to mitigate infectious risk, we transitioned to Eculizumab to achieve sustained blockade of the terminal complement pathway. As seen in this patient's trajectory, durable hematological remission and partial renal recovery were only realized after the introduction of Eculizumab, confirming that while PLEX buys time in acute crises, it cannot replace targeted complement inhibition as the cornerstone of long-term management for CM-HUS.

### [Management] Given that Eculizumab blocks terminal complement component C5, what specific life-threatening infection risk is introduced, and what mandatory prophylactic measures must be implemented before or concurrently with initiating this therapy?

Eculizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to the complement component C5, prevents its cleavage into C5a and C5b. By blocking the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), it halts the uncontrolled complement-mediated endothelial damage characteristic of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS). However, this mechanism carries a profound and specific risk: **encapsulated bacterial infections**, most notably invasive disease caused by *Neisseria meningitidis* (meningococcus).

The terminal complement pathway is critical for the opsonization and lysis of encapsulated organisms. Without a functional C5-C9 cascade, the host loses a primary defense mechanism against these pathogens. Consequently, patients receiving eculizumab face a significantly elevated risk of fulminant meningococcemia, which can progress to septic shock and death with alarming speed. While *Neisseria* is the classic concern, the case you reviewed illustrates that this vulnerability extends to other encapsulated bacteria; indeed, our patient's original insult was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, an organism whose clearance relies heavily on complement-mediated killing. Initiating eculizumab in the setting of active bacteremia would theoretically exacerbate such infections, underscoring why we waited until blood cultures cleared in this case.

To mitigate this life-threatening risk, strict adherence to prophylactic guidelines is mandatory before or concurrently with the first dose of eculizumab. The standard of care involves **vaccination against all four major serogroups of *N. meningitidis*** (A, C, W, Y) and **serogroup B**. As detailed in our case report, this patient received Menveo (covering A, C, Y, W) and Bexsero (covering serogroup B). Ideally, these vaccinations should be completed at least two weeks prior to initiating therapy to allow for an adequate immune response. Because vaccine efficacy may be suboptimal during active treatment or if therapy cannot be delayed for two weeks—as was necessary here due to the severity of her thrombotic microangiopathy—we must implement **concurrent antimicrobial prophylaxis**. In this patient's course, we administered prophylactic penicillin (phenoxymethylpenicillin/Apocillin) because the vaccination window could not be met prior to drug initiation. Patients must also receive education regarding early signs of meningococcal infection and seek immediate medical attention for any febrile illness. This dual strategy of vaccination plus antibiotic coverage remains non-negotiable when prescribing terminal complement inhibitors.
