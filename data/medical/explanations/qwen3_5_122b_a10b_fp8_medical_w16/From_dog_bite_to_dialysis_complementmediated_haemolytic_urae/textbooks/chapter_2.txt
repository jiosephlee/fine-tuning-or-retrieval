Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Reasoning: Unmasking Complement-Mediated HUS After Sepsis Resolution

The diagnostic trajectory of this case illustrates a critical clinical lesson: the resolution of overt sepsis does not necessarily signal the end of organ-threatening complications. Initially, the patient's presentation was dominated by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia manifesting as septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and multiorgan failure. During this acute phase, pancytopenia and coagulopathy masked the specific signature of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). However, a distinct temporal shift occurred approximately eight days into the hospitalization. As inflammatory markers normalized, platelet counts began to recover, and coagulation parameters corrected—signs of resolving DIC—the patient experienced a sudden, isolated deterioration characterized by a precipitous drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. This dissociation between the improving sepsis profile and the emergence of new hematologic crisis signaled a superimposed pathological process rather than simple sepsis sequelae. The clinical picture evolved into classic microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), evidenced by undetectable haptoglobin, rising lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and the appearance of schistocytes on peripheral smear, necessitating a rigorous re-evaluation of the differential diagnosis for TMA.

The first major diagnostic pivot involved distinguishing Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) from other forms of TMA. Given the presence of MAHA and thrombocytopenia, TTP was a life-threatening possibility that required immediate exclusion. The diagnostic standard relies heavily on ADAMTS13 activity levels; severe deficiency (<10%) confirms TTP. In this case, although the PLASMIC score initially suggested a low pre-test probability for TTP (score 4/7), definitive laboratory confirmation was required. The subsequent measurement of ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5% effectively ruled out TTP, as values above 10% make severe deficiency virtually impossible. Consequently, plasma exchange (PEX) served primarily as a temporizing bridge to stabilize the patient while awaiting confirmatory data for other etiologies, rather than as a curative measure for an underlying protease deficiency.

Simultaneously, the team had to address the confounding finding of a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT), or Coombs test, which initially raised suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). A positive DAT led to an empirical trial of high-dose corticosteroids; however, the lack of response in hemoglobin levels and platelet counts rendered AIHA unlikely upon further reflection. In retrospect, this was recognized as a false-positive result driven by drug interference or prior transfusion exposure rather than true autoimmunity. Penicillin-class antibiotics are well-known to bind to red blood cell membranes in vivo or in vitro, potentially causing non-immune adsorption that yields a positive DAT without true complement-mediated lysis. The persistence of schistocytes and thrombocytopenia despite steroid therapy reinforced that the mechanism was mechanical fragmentation within microvasculature rather than antibody-mediated destruction. Furthermore, Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded through negative stool PCR analysis and the absence of a prodromal diarrheal illness, narrowing the differential significantly toward complement-mediated mechanisms or secondary infection-triggered TMA.

Definitive characterization of the pathology relied on renal biopsy findings once coagulopathy had resolved sufficiently to allow safe tissue sampling under anticoagulation precautions absent from standard practice in active DIC but feasible here due to stabilized clotting times. The histopathology provided crucial differentiation between ischemic injury caused by sepsis alone versus frank thrombotic microangiopathy. While severe acute tubular necrosis was present—a common sequela of prolonged hypotension—the biopsy revealed glomerular fibrin thrombi and scattered crescent formation without immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence or primary glomerulopathy on electron microscopy. These findings confirmed endothelial injury with intraluminal thrombus formation characteristic of TMA. Combined with normal C3/C4 levels—which do not exclude CM-HUS—and functional assays showing alternative pathway dysregulation only after treatment initiation, the diagnosis solidified as complement-mediated HUS triggered by *C. canimorsus*. This case underscores that in patients recovering from severe bacterial sepsis who develop unexplained MAHA and renal failure despite resolving DIC and negative ADAMTS13/TTP workups, clinicians must maintain high suspicion for secondary CM-HUS requiring targeted complement inhibition rather than supportive care alone.
