Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The clinical narrative begins with a man in his forties presenting to the emergency department with a three-week history of progressively worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness localized to the hip flexors, accompanied by mild myalgia. Despite having no significant past medical history and being a lifelong non-smaker, the prominence of the cervical edema immediately directed attention toward life-threatening surgical emergencies or acute allergic reactions. The initial clinical suspicion favored Ludwig's angina, a rapidly spreading cellulitis of the floor of the mouth and neck spaces, driven by the visible diffuse swelling and inflammation. However, this hypothesis was quickly challenged by vital signs; the patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable without tachypnea, making an aggressive bacterial infection less likely despite the alarming appearance of the neck. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck confirmed extensive edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space but failed to identify a drainable abscess or discrete fluid collection typical of deep neck space infections. Consequently, as infectious etiologies were effectively ruled out by both clinical stability and cross-sectional imaging, the diagnostic focus shifted toward vascular or allergic mechanisms.

With structural and infectious causes excluded, the working diagnosis migrated toward idiopathic angioedema, prompting referral to clinical immunology. This line of reasoning appeared logical given the rapidity and severity of the soft tissue swelling, which mimicked classic presentations mediated by either histamine release or bradykinin accumulation. Standard protocols for evaluating angioedema would typically involve assessing for triggers such as ACE-inhibitor use or hereditary deficiencies in C1 esterase inhibitor. In this case, however, several "red flags" rendered these pathways improbable. The patient explicitly denied exposure to known precipitants like angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or NSAIDs, lacked a personal or family history of recurrent swelling episodes, and exhibited laboratory values that did not fit the profile of hereditary angioedema; specifically, complement levels (C3 and C4) were preserved. Furthermore, the persistence of swelling over three weeks without fluctuation characteristic of histaminergic events suggested a chronic inflammatory process rather than an acute hypersensitivity reaction. The failure to respond to standard supportive care for presumed allergy further underscored that this was not a simple mast-cell mediator event.

The pivot from a misdiagnosis of angioedema relied heavily on recognizing subtle cutaneous and musculoskeletal findings that were easily overlooked amidst dramatic cervical edema. While classic dermatomyositis stigmata such as Gottron's papules or a frank heliotrope rash were absent upon initial inspection—a common pitfall in atypical presentations—the physical examination revealed critical nuances indicative of connective tissue disease. Notably, nailfold capillaroscopy identified periungual microhemorrhages and avascular areas alongside periungual erythema, findings highly specific for inflammatory myopathies but distinct from traumatic injury or poor perfusion seen in sepsis. Additionally, generalized erythema extending across the face overlying the nasolabial folds provided another clue that differentiated this condition from pure mucosal edema often seen in true angioedema. These dermatologic subtleties served as anchors for reconsidering an autoimmune etiology where inflammation targets collagen-rich structures rather than just vascular permeability channels alone.

Perhaps most diagnostically decisive was the identification of functional impairment that could not be explained by mechanical obstruction alone. Although dysphagia was initially attributed to external compression from neck swelling upon nasendoscopy—which surprisingly showed no gross anatomical distortion—it became evident upon closer scrutiny that swallowing difficulties stemmed from intrinsic muscle dysfunction rather than extrinsic mass effect. Concurrently, careful manual muscle testing uncovered reduced power (4/5) in the hip flexors alongside mild myalgia, establishing a pattern of symmetric proximal weakness inconsistent with neuropathic processes like Guillain-Barré syndrome or focal compressive lesions typical of superior vena cava syndrome. When coupled with markedly elevated creatine kinase levels exceeding 16,000 U/L on subsequent testing—and corroborated by elevations in AST, ALT, and LDH reflecting widespread muscle breakdown—the picture coalesced into severe myositis masquerading as pseudohypoaldosteronism-like edema rather than true angioedema. This realization transformed the management strategy from observation or antihistamines to urgent immunomodulation targeting underlying muscle inflammation driving both weakness and non-cardiogenic soft tissue swelling via direct myopathic infiltration rather than plasma extravasation through leaky vessels alone.

## Pathophysiology of Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis and Non-Histaminergic Edema

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) represent a heterogeneous group of rare autoimmune disorders characterized primarily by chronic muscle inflammation and weakness, often accompanied by distinctive cutaneous manifestations. While the epidemiology shows a bimodal distribution with peaks in childhood (ages 5–14) and adulthood (ages 45–64), the clinical phenotype varies significantly based on the underlying autoantibody profile. Dermatomyositis, distinct from polymyositis and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, is defined by a humoral immune response targeting the microvasculature of skeletal muscle. In this specific case, the patient's presentation was driven by anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti-SAE1) antibodies. To understand why this patient presented with massive neck swelling mimicking angioedema rather than isolated weakness, one must appreciate the molecular role of SAE1 and the resulting tissue-level pathology.

The immunopathogenesis of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis centers on the disruption of "sumoylation," a post-translational modification process analogous to ubiquitination but utilizing Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier (SUMO) proteins instead. The SAE1 enzyme functions as a heterodimer with SAE2 to activate SUMO proteins before they are conjugated to target substrates, regulating diverse cellular processes including transcription, DNA repair, and nuclear transport. Autoantibodies directed against SAE1 serve as highly specific biomarkers for a subset of dermatomyositis patients. Unlike other myositis antibodies that may predict specific malignancies or interstitial lung disease patterns with absolute certainty, anti-SAE1 positivity defines a distinct clinical entity often characterized by prominent cutaneous involvement and significant mucosal dysfunction. The presence of these antibodies triggers a cascade where complement activation leads to endothelial injury within the muscle fascicles, initiating an inflammatory infiltrate that distinguishes this condition from other forms of myopathy.

A critical concept in understanding this case is the distinction between true angioedema and the "pseudo-angioedema" observed here. Classic angioedema—whether histaminergic (allergic) or bradykinin-mediated (hereditary or ACE-inhibitor induced)—results from increased vascular permeability allowing plasma leakage into the subcutaneous or submucosal tissues without direct parenchymal destruction. In contrast, the neck swelling in anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis arises from active myositis itself. Histologically, dermatomyositis exhibits a characteristic perifascicular atrophy where inflammation targets capillaries at the periphery of muscle fascicles. When this inflammatory process involves muscles located superficially in the neck (such as the strap muscles), extensive edema develops within the inflamed muscle bellies and surrounding connective tissue planes. This creates a palpable mass effect that clinically mimics soft tissue infection or allergic swelling but lacks fever or hives and does not respond to antihistamines or corticosteroids alone without addressing the underlying muscular inflammation. Therefore, what appeared to be idiopathic angioedema was actually profound inflammatory infiltration of cervical musculature extending into adjacent spaces like the retropharyngeal area.

While classically associated with hallmark rashes such as Gottron's papules over knuckles and heliotrope eruptions on eyelids, anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis presents with considerable phenotypic variability that can lead to diagnostic delays. In many cases, cutaneous signs precede muscle weakness; however, in others, systemic symptoms dominate early presentation. This patient lacked classic Gottron's papules upon initial inspection but exhibited subtler signs such as periungual telangiectasias and microhemorrhages visible only under capillaroscopy, alongside generalized facial erythema sparing the nasolabial folds—a pattern sometimes termed "Holster sign" when occurring on lateral thighs. These subtle findings underscore that relying solely on pathognomonic rashes can be misleading; clinicians must maintain suspicion for IIM even when classic lesions are absent or obscured by overwhelming edema. The absence of typical rash does not rule out SAE1-driven disease if other supportive features like elevated creatine kinase and specific autoantibodies are present.

Finally, recognizing anti-SAE1 status carries immediate implications for anticipating systemic complications beyond musculoskeletal decline. Literature indicates that patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies have a high propensity for bulbar involvement and interstitial lung disease (ILD). Approximately 46% of these patients experience dysphagia due to striated muscle inflammation affecting the pharynx and esophagus, which directly correlates with this patient's severe swallowing difficulties leading to aspiration pneumonitis and pulmonary nodules seen on imaging. Furthermore, ILD occurs in roughly 43% of cases, necessitating vigilant monitoring of respiratory function even in asymptomatic phases regarding breathing mechanics initially masked by neck swelling. Additionally, because all adult-onset dermatomyositis confers an increased risk of malignancy—with anti-SAE1 carrying an approximate 18% risk—the identification of this antibody mandates rigorous cancer screening protocols involving cross-sectional imaging and endoscopic evaluation once acute stabilization is achieved. Thus, understanding the specific pathophysiology drives both acute management strategies aimed at suppressing muscular inflammation rapidly to prevent airway compromise and long-term surveillance tailored to high-risk organ systems associated with this serotype.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Multimodal Workup Strategy

Once the initial suspicion of infectious deep neck space pathology was ruled out by computed tomography (CT) and the clinical picture failed to align with classic hereditary or acquired angioedema, the diagnostic focus shifted toward identifying an underlying inflammatory etiology capable of mimicking soft tissue edema. The first critical step in this algorithm was the systematic exclusion of primary angioedema mechanisms. Clinicians ordered complement studies, specifically measuring C3, C4, and C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantity. In both hereditary and acquired angioedema mediated by bradykinin pathways, one would expect low C4 levels and dysfunctional or deficient C1 esterase inhibitor. However, this patient's normal complement profile effectively excluded these conditions, reinforcing the realization that the profound neck swelling was not driven by histamine release or bradykinin excess, but rather represented "pseudoangioedema" secondary to another process. With common causes eliminated, attention turned to the musculoskeletal system prompted by subtle physical exam findings—specifically periungual erythema and proximal muscle weakness—which were easily overlooked in the context of dramatic cervical swelling.

The pivot point in confirming dermatomyositis arrived with the analysis of muscle injury biomarkers. While routine inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein were elevated nonspecifically, the measurement of creatine kinase (CK) provided immediate diagnostic clarity. The patient exhibited a massively elevated CK level exceeding 16,000 U/L, a magnitude far beyond what is seen in minor trauma or viral myositis and highly specific for severe rhabdomyolysis or fulminant myositis. Crucially, clinicians must recognize that markedly elevated liver enzymes (AST > ALT) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) often accompany such extreme CK elevations in myositis; AST is abundant in skeletal muscle just as it is in hepatocytes, meaning isolated interpretation without considering muscle sources can lead to misdiagnosis of hepatic pathology. In this case, the constellation of hypertransaminasemia coupled with sky-high CK served as a biochemical fingerprint pointing directly to widespread muscle destruction rather than primary organ failure or drug-induced toxicity.

To synthesize these laboratory findings into a comprehensive anatomical assessment, advanced multimodal imaging was deployed to visualize the extent of disease activity before proceeding to invasive procedures. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs revealed characteristic patterns of active myositis, showing edema and inflammation preferentially affecting specific muscle groups such as the obturator internus and externus. Concurrently, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) scanning offered a whole-body survey that confirmed diffuse metabolic uptake across multiple muscle beds—including those in the head and neck region responsible for the visible swelling—while simultaneously serving as a vital tool for paraneoplastic screening by excluding metabolically active malignancies elsewhere in the body. These imaging modalities not only corroborated the serological suspicions but also mapped the burden of disease to explain functional deficits like dysphagia caused by pharyngeal muscle infiltration.

Definitive confirmation required histopathological correlation through a muscle biopsy, which remains the gold standard for distinguishing between overlapping syndromes such as immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy versus classic dermatomyositis. The biopsy specimen demonstrated hallmark features including perifascicular atrophy—a pattern where fibers surrounding fascicles show disproportionate wasting—and necrotic myofibers undergoing regeneration. Most importantly, immunohistochemical staining revealed deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) within perifascicular capillaries; this microvascular injury pattern is pathognomonic for dermatomyositis and distinguishes it from other inflammatory myopathies that lack this specific vascular signature. Finally, serologic profiling identified high titers of anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. While earlier workups had ruled out hereditary angioedema biochemically, this specific autoantibody provided molecular confirmation of an autoimmune etiology known to associate with cutaneous prominence and variable degrees of muscular involvement. By integrating negative complement screens with positive enzymatic surrogates of muscle necrosis, characteristic imaging patterns, distinct histology, and specific autoimmunity profiles, clinicians successfully navigated from a misleading presentation of neck swelling to a precise diagnosis requiring urgent immunomodulatory intervention.
