Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The CK Clue: Why Every Patient with Unexplained Edema Needs a Creatine Kinase Level

Imagine a patient walking into your Emergency Department with a progressively swollen neck. He's febrile? No. Tachycardic? Maybe, but not shockingly so. He looks like he might have Ludwig's angina or a deep neck space infection. You call ENT, they order a CT, and sure enough, there's diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. But here's the kicker: the antibiotics aren't working, the cultures are sterile, and the swelling isn't resolving. You pivot to "idiopathic angioedema," run a C1 esterase inhibitor panel, check complements—all normal. The patient is stuck in a diagnostic limbo between infectious disease and allergy/immunology, while his condition quietly deteriorates.

This was exactly the scenario for a man in his 40s who presented with what looked like classic angioedema but turned out to be something far more sinister: **anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis**. And the single test that cracked the case wide open wasn't a fancy genetic panel or an advanced MRI—it was a humble creatine kinase (CK) level that came back at **16,184 U/L**.

This case is a stark reminder that in the ED, when you see "angioedema" that doesn't fit the mold—especially if it involves the neck without urticaria or hypotension—you must keep inflammatory myopathies on your radar. The cost of missing this diagnosis isn't just wasted time; it's delayed initiation of life-saving immunosuppression for a disease that can rapidly compromise the airway through dysphagia and muscle infiltration rather than just mucosal swelling.

### When Angioedema Isn't Angioedema

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy classically defined by proximal muscle weakness and pathognomonic skin rashes like Gottron's papules or heliotrope eruptions. However, this case highlights one of its most deceptive presentations: **pseudoangioedema**. While true histamine or bradykinin-mediated angioedema is common in ER practice, DM can cause subcutaneous tissue edema due to severe underlying muscle inflammation (myositis). This swelling can be profound enough to mimic Ludwig's angina or deep neck abscesses, leading to unnecessary surgical consultations and invasive procedures.

In our patient, the "swelling" was actually inflamed strap muscles and fascial planes. Crucially, he lacked some of the classic cutaneous stigmata initially; he didn't have Gottron's papules or a full-blown heliotrope rash on arrival. Instead, he had subtle periungual erythema and nailfold microhemorrhages visible only on close inspection (and capillaroscopy later). If you miss these subtle clues because you're focused on ruling out infection, you miss the window for early intervention.

The real game-changer here was recognizing that **dysphagia** in this context wasn't just mechanical obstruction from swelling; it was muscular failure. The barium swallow confirmed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration risk—a direct consequence of myositis affecting the pharyngeal constrictors and cricopharyngeus muscles. Without addressing the underlying autoimmune process, no amount of intubation or tracheostomy planning would solve the root problem once acute inflammation subsides with steroids and IVIG.

### The Power of One Number: CK > 16k

Why did this patient get misdiagnosed initially? Because muscle weakness was described as "mild" in his hip flexors relative to his dramatic neck appearance. In many inflammatory myopathies, skin manifestations can precede overt weakness, or weakness can be subtle until significant muscle mass is lost or inflammation peaks locally (like in the neck).

However, **serum CK does not lie.** A level of 16,184 U/L is incompatible with simple viral myalgia or localized trauma; it screams massive muscle necrosis/inflammation. In this specific case of anti-SAE1 positive DM:
*   The CK elevation was disproportionate to the subjective complaint of weakness at presentation.
*   Transaminases (AST/ALT) were also elevated (AST 396 U/L), often confusing clinicians into thinking liver pathology first—but remember AST is abundant in skeletal muscle too!
*   LDH was elevated (692 U/L), another marker of tissue turnover consistent with myositis.

If you order a CK whenever you see unexplained edema—particularly if it involves the face or neck—and rule out obvious cardiac ischemia or trauma first—you could catch these cases before they spiral into respiratory failure from aspiration pneumonia or complete airway collapse from laryngeal edema caused by myositis rather than fluid shift alone.

### Anti-SAE1: A Specific Beast Worth Knowing About

The serology returned positive for **anti-SAE1 antibodies** (signal intensity 173). This specific subtype of dermatomyositis has distinct characteristics worth committing to memory:
*   It presents frequently with cutaneous features but can lack classic rashes initially (as seen here).
*   It carries a high rate of dysphagia (~46%) and interstitial lung disease (~43%).
*   It is associated with subcutaneous edema/pseudoangioedema more frequently than other subtypes like Jo-1 or Mi-2.
*   Unlike some other DM subtypes where malignancy risk is sky-high immediately upon diagnosis, SAE1 still requires aggressive cancer screening (this patient had high-risk features: male sex + dysphagia + DM), but outcomes can be favorable with appropriate therapy if caught early.

Our patient required an aggressive multimodal approach: pulse dose methylprednisolone followed by IVIG loading doses (2 g/kg), Rituximab, and Mycophenolate Mofetil. Note that Methotrexate was avoided due to pre-existing lung concerns (patchy consolidation/aspiration pneumonitis). It took weeks for him to recover swallowing function enough to remove his NG tube and months for his CK to normalize and swelling to resolve completely. Had we waited longer for "classic" rash development or ignored the CK spike because "he walked fine," he likely would have suffered catastrophic aspiration events during those critical first days in hospital.

### Take-Home Points for Frontline Clinicians

This case serves as a powerful audit trail for our own practice patterns regarding unexplained edema and "atypical" presentations of autoimmune disease in the ED:

*   **Think Beyond Histamine:** When evaluating "angioedema," always consider non-histaminergic/non-bradykinin causes if there are no hives, no hypotension history, and no ACE-inhibitor exposure—specifically inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis causing pseudoangioedema from myositis.
*   **Order That CK Early:** A creatine kinase level should be part of your standard workup for unexplained facial/neck swelling accompanied by any degree of dysphagia or vague proximal weakness, even if weakness seems minor compared to the edema volume. Levels >1000 should trigger immediate rheumatology/immunology consultation regardless of other labs being normal (like C3/C4).
*   **Look Closely at Skin Nails:** Don't dismiss subtle nailfold changes (telangiectasia, hemorrhages) as benign; they are often the earliest visible clue pointing toward connective tissue disease when classic rashes are absent or evolving slowly ("Holster sign" appeared later in this case).
*   **Watch Out for Aspiration Risk:** Dysphagia in undiagnosed dermatomyositis is dangerous due to silent aspiration leading to chemical pneumonitis; prioritize airway protection strategies beyond just ruling out foreign bodies while waiting for serologies back if suspicion remains high after initial negative infectious workup.
