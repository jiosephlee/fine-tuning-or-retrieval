Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Beyond Gottron's Papules: Decoding Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis and Its Unique Phenotype

We often teach our trainees to hunt for the "classic" signs of dermatomyositis: the violaceous heliotrope rash, the scaly Gottron's papules, or the tell-tale shawl sign. But what happens when the textbook presentation goes AWOL? What does a patient look like when they walk into your ED with massive, progressive neck swelling that mimics angioedema, yet their creatine kinase is skyrocketing and their classic rashes are conspicuously absent?

This week, we're dissecting a fascinating case report that serves as a stark reminder: **not all neck swelling is angioedema.**

A previously healthy man in his 40s presents to the ED with a three-week history of worsening neck swelling and some vague hip weakness. The initial workup screams "Ludwig's Angina." He gets a CT neck showing diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal inflammation. ENT surgeons scrub in (metaphorically), rule out infection, and find no abscess. No fever. Normal WBC (mostly). The team pivots to "idiopathic angioedema," suspecting either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated pathways. They check complements—normal. They ask about ACE inhibitors—he's not on them. Nothing fits.

Enter the "atypical" clue: subtle nailfold changes (microhemorrhages) and a rash on his lateral thigh. Crucially, he lacks the pathognomonic heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules that would have immediately flagged this as dermatomyositis (DM) for many clinicians. However, because the neck swelling didn't fit the angioedema mold perfectly, someone ordered a Creatine Kinase (CK).

**The result? 16,184 U/L.**

That single number changed everything. This wasn't angioedema; it was myositis masquerading as soft tissue edema. Further serology revealed he was strongly positive for **anti-SAE1 antibodies**, confirmed by a muscle biopsy showing perifascicular inflammation and C5b-9 deposition—the hallmark of DM.

### Why This Case Matters: The Anti-SAE1 Phenotype

This isn't just another story of missed DM; it highlights a specific serological subset that behaves differently than the "classic" cases we see in textbooks. Anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis is distinct, aggressive in certain ways, and often deceptive in others.

First, let's talk about the **skin**. While classically associated with cutaneous features like heliotrope eruptions and Gottron's papules, this patient lacked them entirely at presentation. In fact, up to 30% of anti-SAE1 patients may lack these classic signs initially or have them evolve later. Instead, this patient presented with **"pseudoangioedema"**—subcutaneous edema caused by profound muscle inflammation rather than vascular leakage from histamine or bradykinin. This phenomenon is rare; studies suggest only ~6-8% of DM patients present with significant edema, making this an incredibly tricky mimic for ER physicians and ENT specialists alike.

Secondly, consider the **dysphagia and lung risk**. The anti-SAE1 phenotype carries a heavy burden of visceral involvement. Literature reviews indicate that nearly half (46%) of SAE1-positive patients suffer from dysphagia due to pharyngeal muscle weakness (which explains our patient's severe aspiration risk seen on barium swallow), and almost as many develop Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD). In our case, despite no frank fibrosis on CT initially, he had patchy consolidations from aspiration and an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on PFTs—a warning sign for future ILD progression if untreated aggressively.

Finally, there is the **malignancy risk**. With SAE1 positivity combined with dysphagia and male sex, this patient fell into a high-risk category for paraneoplastic syndromes. He underwent extensive screening including PET/CT and tumor markers (all negative so far), but remains under strict surveillance for GI malignancies later on—a standard protocol given that malignancy occurs in roughly 18% of SAE1 cases compared to other subsets where rates vary wildly based on age and gender.

### Management Nuances: Treading Carefully Around the Lungs

The treatment journey here offers valuable lessons too. Given the severity of his muscle involvement (CK >16k) and airway compromise from dysphagia/aspiration pneumonia risk, he received pulse-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by IVIG loading doses and Rituximab induction alongside Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF).

Why MMF over Methotrexate? Here lies a critical clinical pearl regarding SAE1 specifically: **Avoid Methotrexate if there is any hint of lung involvement.** Since SAE1 patients have such a high propensity for developing ILD (43%), using MTX—which can itself induce pulmonary toxicity—is generally avoided unless absolutely necessary and lungs are pristine. Our patient had abnormal PFTs and aspiration changes; MMF was chosen as a safer alternative for long-term maintenance without exacerbating potential lung injury. It took him 2 months off feeds before swallowing returned to normal—a testament to how slow recovery can be even after CK normalization once significant structural inflammation sets in.

### Take-Home Points for the Front Line

*   **Think Muscle When Edema Doesn't Fit:** If you see "angioedema" that doesn't respond to antihistamines/steroids or has normal complement levels—especially if accompanied by proximal weakness or subtle skin/nail changes—check a CK immediately before ruling out inflammatory myopathy out completely.
*   **Don't Wait for Classic Rashes:** Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis can present *without* heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules initially relying instead on pseudoangioedema from myositis or subtle nailfold capillary changes to give away the diagnosis.
*   **Know Your Serotypes:** Anti-SAE1 positivity predicts high risks for dysphagia (~46%) and Interstitial Lung Disease (~43%). Screen aggressively for both early in admission to prevent aspiration events and monitor PFTs closely thereafter.
*   **Medication Selection Matters:** Be cautious with Methotrexate in SAE1-positive patients due to overlapping pulmonary risks; Mycophenolate Mofetil is often preferred when ILD risk exists or develops concurrently with myositis treatment initiation via IVIG/Rituximab bridges if needed rapidly enough!
*   **Malignancy Surveillance Is Non-Negotiable:** High-risk profiles (male + dysphagia + specific antibodies) warrant comprehensive cancer screening protocols beyond basic labs—including CT thorax/abdomen/pelvis plus eventual endoscopic evaluation—even if initial imaging appears clean!
