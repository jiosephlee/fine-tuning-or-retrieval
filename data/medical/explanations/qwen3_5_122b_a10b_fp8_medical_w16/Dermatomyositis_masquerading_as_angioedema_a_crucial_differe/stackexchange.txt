Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient presenting with unexplained neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness, what specific physical examination findings would most strongly raise suspicion for dermatomyositis over classic histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

When evaluating a patient with unexplained neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness, the distinction between classic angioedema and dermatomyositis hinges on identifying cutaneous stigmata of myositis and recognizing the inflammatory nature of the edema. Unlike histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, which presents with rapid-onset, often painful or pruritic swelling without intrinsic muscle pathology, dermatomyositis manifests with "pseudoangioedema" driven by underlying myositis and fascial inflammation.

As illustrated in the case of the 40-year-old man initially misdiagnosed with idiopathic angioedema, the most discriminating physical examination findings are subtle cutaneous changes located away from the site of maximal swelling. Specifically, you must scrutinize the nailfolds for **periungual erythema**, **telangiectasias**, or **microhemorrhages** (often visible via capillaroscopy), as these were key clues in our patient who lacked the classic heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules at presentation. Additionally, inspect sun-exposed areas for a **"shawl sign"** (erythema across the upper back and shoulders) or a **"Holster sign"** (rash over the lateral thighs), both highly specific for dermatomyositis.

Crucially, differentiate the quality of the neck swelling: in dermatomyositis, the edema is often non-tender but firm due to muscle infiltration and fasciitis, whereas angioedema typically involves subcutaneous tissues without muscular tenderness or functional deficit. The presence of concomitant **proximal muscle weakness** (e.g., difficulty rising from a chair or lifting arms) alongside dysphagia should immediately trigger a search for myopathic signs rather than allergic mediators. In this case, despite normal C1 esterase inhibitor levels and complement studies ruling out hereditary angioedema, it was the detection of periungual changes and hip flexor weakness that prompted a creatine kinase check, revealing massive elevation (>16,000 U/L) and confirming the diagnosis of anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis. Therefore, a meticulous inspection of the hands for nailfold vascular changes is essential to avoid missing this treatable mimic of angioedema.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the pathophysiology of 'pseudoangioedema' in dermatomyositis differ mechanistically from hereditary angioedema (C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency) and ACE-inhibitor induced angioedema?

The "pseudoangioedema" observed in this patient with anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis represents a distinct pathophysiological entity driven by **direct inflammatory infiltration and edema of the skeletal musculature**, specifically the strap muscles and deep neck structures, rather than the vascular permeability mechanisms seen in true angioedema.

In contrast to **hereditary angioedema (HAE)**, which results from C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency leading to unchecked activation of the kallikrein-kinin system and excessive **bradykinin** production, and **ACE-inhibitor induced angioedema**, where impaired degradation of bradykinin causes similar vasoactive mediator accumulation, dermatomyositis-associated swelling is neither histamine nor bradykinin-mediated. As evidenced by this case, the patient's complement levels (C3, C4) and C1 esterase function were entirely normal, effectively ruling out the classic pathways of HAE or acquired C1-INH deficiency. Furthermore, the lack of response to antihistamines or epinephrine (implied by the failure of initial workups for idiopathic angioedema) supports a non-histaminergic etiology.

Mechanistically, the swelling in dermatomyositis stems from **immune-mediated myositis**. The hallmark pathology involves perifascicular atrophy and inflammation mediated by complement deposition (specifically C5b-9 membrane attack complexes on capillaries), as confirmed by this patient's muscle biopsy showing necrotic fibers and perimysial inflammation. The "angioedema" appearance is actually severe myogenic edema resulting from extensive muscle fiber necrosis, regeneration attempts, and associated fascial inflammation. In this specific case, MRI and PET scans localized this process to the bilateral obturator internus/externus muscles and hip adductors, with profound metabolic activity in the head and neck regions corresponding to the visible cervical swelling. Therefore, while HAE and ACE-inhibitor reactions involve rapid fluid extravasation into submucosal tissues due to vasoactive mediators acting on endothelial tight junctions, pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis is a manifestation of active muscle destruction and interstitial inflammation that mimics soft tissue swelling clinically but requires immunosuppression rather than bradykinin antagonists or antihistamines for resolution.

### [Diagnostic Reasoning] Given the patient's presentation of subacute neck swelling, dysphagia, and markedly elevated creatine kinase, which specific autoantibody was identified in this case, and what are two distinct clinical associations (e.g., malignancy risk, organ involvement) often seen with this antibody subtype?

In this case of a patient presenting with subacute neck swelling, dysphagia, and markedly elevated creatine kinase, the specific autoantibody identified was **anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti-SAE1)**. This antibody targets the SUMO-activating enzyme, distinguishing this variant of dermatomyositis from other myositis-specific antibodies like anti-Jo-1 or anti-MDA5.

Based on the clinical data and literature cited in this report, two distinct clinical associations frequently seen with anti-SAE1 positivity are:

1.  **High Prevalence of Dysphagia:** Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries a significant burden of bulbar dysfunction. Literature reviews indicate that approximately **46%** of patients with this antibody subtype develop dysphagia, which in this case manifested as severe pharyngeal dysphagia leading to aspiration and the need for prolonged nasogastric feeding.
2.  **Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD):** There is a strong association between anti-SAE1 antibodies and pulmonary involvement, occurring in roughly **43%** of cases. While ILD is classically linked to antisynthetase syndromes (e.g., anti-Jo-1), it remains a major morbidity driver in SAE1-positive patients, necessitating vigilant respiratory monitoring even in the absence of classic cutaneous markers like Gottron's papules at initial presentation.

Additionally, while malignancy risk varies across myositis subsets, anti-SAE1 is associated with an approximate **18% malignancy rate**, warranting comprehensive age-appropriate cancer screening as outlined by IMACS guidelines, particularly given the patient's male sex and presence of dysphagia.

### [Management] When evaluating a new-onset inflammatory myopathy like dermatomyositis, why is it critical to assess for interstitial lung disease prior to initiating certain immunosuppressive therapies such as methotrexate, and how did this influence the treatment selection in this specific case?

In the evaluation of new-onset inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis, assessing for interstitial lung disease (ILD) before initiating specific immunosuppressants is a critical safety step because certain agents, particularly methotrexate, carry a well-documented risk of inducing or exacerbating pulmonary fibrosis. While methotrexate is often a first-line steroid-sparing agent for myositis, its use is relatively contraindicated in patients with pre-existing ILD or those with antibody profiles strongly associated with rapid-onset lung injury. Failure to screen could precipitate life-threatening acute respiratory failure in an already vulnerable patient.

In this specific case, the decision to avoid methotrexate was directly driven by the patient's pulmonary status and antibody profile. Although the patient was a lifelong non-smoker and lacked classic radiographic fibrosis on initial CT, his pulmonary function tests revealed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, and he exhibited significant dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis—a known complication that complicates the pulmonary picture. Furthermore, while anti-SAE1 antibodies are classically associated with cutaneous prominence and dysphagia, they are also linked to ILD in approximately 43% of cases. Given the presence of restrictive physiology and the inherent risk of methotrexate-induced pneumonitis superimposed on active muscle inflammation affecting the respiratory musculature, the treating team opted against methotrexate. Instead, they selected a regimen comprising high-dose corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), rituximab, and mycophenolate mofetil. This approach allowed for aggressive control of the severe myositis and pseudo-angioedema while minimizing iatrogenic pulmonary toxicity, ultimately resulting in full recovery of muscle strength and resolution of swallowing dysfunction without worsening respiratory compromise.

### [Clinical Pearls] Based on the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines mentioned in the case, what factors contributed to classifying this patient as 'high risk' for underlying malignancy, and what constitutes an appropriate screening strategy for such patients?

Based on the IMACS guidelines referenced in this case, the classification of "high risk" for underlying malignancy in this patient was driven by a confluence of specific demographic, clinical, and serologic factors. While dermatomyositis itself carries an inherent increased risk of paraneoplastic syndromes compared to other inflammatory myopathies, this patient possessed two distinct **high-risk** criteria: the diagnosis of dermatomyositis (specifically with significant cutaneous and muscular involvement) and the presence of **dysphagia**. Furthermore, he exhibited two **intermediate-risk** factors: male sex and positivity for **anti-SAE1 antibodies**. Although anti-SAE1 is often associated with cutaneous-dominant disease and a lower malignancy rate than anti-TIF1-γ or anti-NXP2, the IMACS framework aggregates these variables; the combination of adult-onset dermatomyositis with dysphagia elevates the pre-test probability sufficiently to mandate aggressive screening.

Consequently, the appropriate screening strategy for such high-risk patients must be comprehensive and multimodal, as illustrated by our workup. Initial evaluation should include a thorough history and physical examination focused on constitutional symptoms and lymphadenopathy. Imaging is paramount; in this case, we utilized a CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis alongside an FDG-PET scan to identify occult metabolic activity or structural masses not visible on standard cross-sectional imaging. Laboratory assessment included age-appropriate tumor markers (such as CEA, CA-19-9, CA-125, and PSA), acknowledging their limited sensitivity but utility as adjuncts. Crucially, because solid organ imaging can miss mucosal lesions, endoscopic evaluation is mandatory. As outlined in our follow-up plan for this patient, upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy (gastroscopy and colonoscopy) are essential components of the initial screen to rule out colorectal or gastric malignancies that might be asymptomatic at presentation. Given that malignancy can precede or coincide with myositis diagnosis, IMACS recommends repeating this extensive screening annually for at least four years in high-risk individuals who remain negative on initial evaluation.

### [Pathophysiology] If a muscle biopsy is performed on a patient with suspected dermatomyositis, what is the hallmark histological pattern of inflammation and what specific immunohistochemical finding regarding the complement system confirms the diagnosis?

Based on the case of anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis presented here, the hallmark histological pattern observed on muscle biopsy is **perifascicular atrophy**, characterized by foci of necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerating myofibers specifically located at the periphery of the muscle fascicles. Unlike polymyositis, where inflammation is predominantly endomysial and involves individual fibers throughout the fascicle, dermatomyositis exhibits a distinct topographic distribution of pathology centered around the fascicular border.

The specific immunohistochemical finding that confirms this diagnosis is the deposition of the **membrane attack complex (complement component C5b-9)** within the **capillaries of the perifascicular endomysium**. As illustrated in Figure 4 of our case report, this microvascular injury is driven by complement activation, leading to ischemia and subsequent atrophy of the adjacent outermost muscle fibers. While our patient's clinical presentation was dominated by profound neck swelling mimicking angioedema—a manifestation arising from muscle inflammation rather than histaminergic or bradykinin pathways—the underlying pathophysiology remains rooted in this complement-mediated microangiopathy. Identifying C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular capillaries alongside perifascicular atrophy provides definitive pathological confirmation of dermatomyositis, distinguishing it from other inflammatory myopathies even when classic cutaneous signs like Gottron's papules are absent or subtle.

### [Management] This patient developed hemolytic anemia while on trimethoxazole-sulfamethoxazole for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis despite having normal G6PD levels. What alternative prophylactic agent was utilized in this case, and why might this complication occur even without G6PD deficiency?

In this case, the alternative prophylactic agent utilized was **nebulized pentamidine** (300 mg every 4 weeks).

Regarding the mechanism of hemolysis in the absence of Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency: While G6PD deficiency is the classic predisposing factor for oxidative drug-induced hemolysis with sulfonamides like trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), it is not an absolute requirement. TMP-SMX generates reactive oxygen species that can overwhelm the antioxidant defenses of red blood cells. Even in patients with "normal" quantitative G6PD activity, functional deficits may exist due to variant enzymes with reduced stability under stress, or the oxidative burden may simply exceed the capacity of other protective pathways (such as glutathione synthesis) independent of G6PD status. Furthermore, patients with underlying autoimmune conditions like dermatomyositis may have heightened susceptibility to drug-induced hematologic toxicity due to concurrent immune dysregulation or subclinical membrane fragility. When TMP-SMX precipitated hemolytic anemia in this patient despite normal screening levels, switching to nebulized pentamidine provided effective *Pneumocystis jirovecii* coverage without systemic absorption and minimal risk of cross-reactivity or further oxidative stress.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Beyond the classic triad of Gottron's papules, heliotrope rash, and proximal weakness, what are some 'atypical' cutaneous or musculoskeletal presentations of dermatomyositis that can lead to misdiagnosis as infectious processes (like Ludwig's angina) or vascular emergencies?

In this case, the critical teaching point is recognizing that dermatomyositis can present with **pseudoangioedema**—specifically severe, non-pruritic neck and facial swelling—mimicking life-threatening conditions like Ludwig's angina or idiopathic angioedema. While the classic triad includes Gottron's papules, heliotrope rash, and proximal weakness, "atypical" presentations often lack these hallmark cutaneous signs initially, leading to diagnostic delay.

The pathophysiology here differs fundamentally from histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. As seen in our patient, the swelling results from **muscle inflammation and edema** within the cervical strap muscles and surrounding soft tissues, driven by complement-mediated microangiopathy (evidenced by C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular capillaries) rather than vascular permeability alone. Consequently, patients may present with a "boggy," erythematous neck that is non-tender but associated with profound dysphagia and myalgia, yet they remain afebrile with normal white cell counts relative to the degree of swelling—a key discriminator against deep neck space infections like Ludwig's angina.

Crucially, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for myositis in any patient with unexplained subcutaneous edema, particularly when accompanied by subtle clues such as **periungual telangiectasias**, nailfold hemorrhages, or the **Holster sign** (erythema over the lateral thighs), even in the absence of classic rashes. In our case report, the absence of Gottron's papules and heliotrope rash initially obscured the diagnosis; however, the presence of nailfold changes and significant proximal weakness (hip flexors) prompted a creatine kinase (CK) check. The resulting CK elevation (>16,000 U/L) confirmed massive muscle breakdown. Furthermore, this specific phenotype was strongly associated with **anti-SAE1 antibodies**, a serotype known to present with cutaneous dominance and variable muscle involvement but rarely associated with interstitial lung disease compared to other subsets like anti-MDA5.

Therefore, when evaluating "angioedema" that fails to respond to antihistamines or epinephrine—or when neck swelling is disproportionate to allergic triggers—one must consider inflammatory myopathies. A simple CK level and careful inspection of nailfolds can differentiate between a vascular emergency requiring airway intervention and an autoimmune process requiring urgent immunosuppression to prevent respiratory failure from bulbar muscle weakness.
