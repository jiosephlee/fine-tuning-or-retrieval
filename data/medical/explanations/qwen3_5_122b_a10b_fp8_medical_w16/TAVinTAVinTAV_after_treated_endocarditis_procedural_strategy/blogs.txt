Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## The Triple Threat: Navigating Feasibility and Planning in 'TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV' Procedures

We often talk about "lifetime management" in structural heart disease, a phrase that sounds benign until you realize it implies your patient might outlive every single iteration of transcatheter technology available today. For decades, we assumed TAVI was a one-and-done solution or perhaps a bridge to surgery. But as our cohorts age and survive longer, we are entering an era where the "third generation" isn't just a new device model—it's literally a third valve inside the same annulus.

Is there any limit to how many layers we can stack? Or does physics eventually win?

Consider the case of a gentleman in his late 70s with a cardiac history so dense it reads like a textbook chapter on cardiovascular progression. He started with a Lotus valve in 2014, survived three separate PCI runs for coronary disease along the way, and then faced the dreaded scenario of structural valve deterioration (SVD). In 2022, he underwent a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis. Two years later, disaster struck—not just mechanical failure, but *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). After six weeks of aggressive antibiotics and resolution of the infection, he returned in 2024 with severe restenosis and regurgitation. His LV EF had bounced back nicely to 66%, but his valve area was shrinking fast (0.5 cm²).

With two previous valves already cemented into his anatomy and an EuroSCORE II of nearly 28%, surgical explantation was essentially impossible without replacing the entire aortic root. The Heart Team faced a stark question: Do they send him home with palliative care, or attempt a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**?

They chose to intervene. What followed wasn't just a lucky shot; it was a masterclass in CT-guided precision engineering within biological constraints.

### The Anatomy of Stacking Three Valves

The primary fear in multi-tiered TAVI isn't just getting the new valve seated; it's creating a "neo-skirt"—that massive cylinder of metal struts from all three devices that narrows the effective orifice area (EOA) to microscopic proportions or blocks coronary arteries entirely. In this case, the interplay between three distinct platforms—the retractable-frame Lotus (2014), the self-expanding Evolut (2022), and the final self-expanding Acurate neo2 (2024)—created a labyrinthine landscape.

Standard sizing algorithms fall apart here. You aren't just measuring an annulus; you are measuring the innermost lumen of two existing stents embedded in calcified tissue. Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) became the non-negotiable hero of this story. It revealed that despite the triple-layering, there was still enough room for maneuverability if—and only if—the strategy was perfect.

The critical pivot point was deciding *where* to land this third beast. If deployed too low, the combined struts would crush coronaries or create fatal patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). If too high, you lose stability. The team opted for **high implantation**, aligning the Acurate neo2 specifically with the leaflet plane of the middle Evolut valve. Why? To trap those failing Evolut leaflets against its own frame rather than letting them flop inward into the flow stream. This prevented further narrowing and kept coronary ostia clear by minimizing neo-skirt formation below them.

### Procedural Nuances: It's Not Just About Expansion

When they finally went ahead with local anesthesia and bilateral access (radial + femoral), every step felt like defusing a bomb wire by wire. They utilized rapid pacing to minimize movement during balloon predilation through three concentric rings—a feat requiring immense tactile skill given the resistance offered by two prior frames.

Crucially, they did not rely solely on fluoroscopy for alignment; they leveraged pre-procedural CT modeling to guide **commissural alignment**. Aligning the new valve's posts with those underneath helps maintain patency for future coronary access—a luxury that vanishes quickly when you start adding steel layers around fragile vessels like scaffolding gone wrong. Even though full 3D virtual expansion software wasn't explicitly cited as being used intra-procedurally in this report, the pre-op workup mimicked its output by mapping exact distances from coronary ostia to predicted strut positions.

Interestingly, immediate post-deployment hemodynamics looked grim initially: mean gradient spiked to 67 mmHg! But remember, acute results don't always predict chronic reality in these complex stacks. Within one month, thanks to continued remodeling and possibly some delayed radial expansion from high-pressure ballooning pushing outward against rigid old frames, that gradient dropped to 27 mmHg with stable leaflet motion at one year follow-up. The patient walked away asymptomatic—a testament to patience and precise execution over immediate perfectionism during deployment itself? # To Cut or Not to Cut? Managing Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis After Multiple Prior TAVIs

Let's be honest: the textbook algorithm for Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE) usually screams "Surgery." Whether it's heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or large vegetations, the standard of care leans heavily toward explantation and aortic root replacement. But medicine rarely lives perfectly inside the textbook box, especially when we're talking about the evolving landscape of structural heart disease in our most fragile patients.

We just got word on a case that throws a wrench—and perhaps a new roadmap—into this rigid thinking. Imagine a man in his late 70s with an absolutely stacked CV resume: three-vessel CAD with four prior PCIs, COPD, Type 2 DM, GI bleeds, and a pacemaker. His valve journey started back in 2014 with a Lotus TAVI. Then came the inevitable sequelae: he needed a **TAV-in-TAV** with an Evolut in 2022. Three months later, disaster struck—he developed *Enterococcus faecalis* PVE.

Standard protocol says send him to surgery now. But look closer at the numbers: his EuroSCORE II was sitting at a staggering **27.36%**. An emergent triple-decked root replacement wasn't just risky; it was potentially fatal. So, what did the Heart Team do? They didn't cut them open immediately. They hit pause, crushed the bug with six weeks of IV ampillin-based therapy, confirmed sterility with TOE and CT scans, and then went ahead and performed something almost unheard of: **a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.**

Yes, they implanted an Acurate neo2 inside two other valves in a patient who just survived endocarditis. And at one year? He's walking around asymptomatic with preserved EF and no recurrence. Let's break down why this worked and what it means for your next complex consult.

### When Guidelines Meet Reality

It is crucial to acknowledge that the 2025 ESC guidelines still recommend surgery for PVE complications like heart failure or uncontrolled sepsis. Nobody is suggesting we abandon these rules lightly. However, this case forces us to confront the reality of "prohibitive" surgical risk vs. "absolute" contraindication to catheter intervention.

In this gentleman's scenario, surgical exploration wouldn't just involve removing one valve; it would require dissecting through layers of metallic scaffolding integrated into his ascending aorta over nearly a decade of stenting and inflammation. The technical hazard of removing three overlapping valve frames surgically is immense. Conversely, once active infection was eradicated (verified by negative cultures and imaging), treating the resulting *structural dysfunction* became analogous to any other failed bioprosthesis—just one that happened to sit atop a previous infected unit that had already healed up chemically.

The paradigm shift here isn't ignoring endocarditis; it's recognizing that **"treated" PVE creates a window where anatomical feasibility trumps historical stigma.** If you can prove the fire is out, you can start fighting the mechanics of the burn scars—but you better have excellent maps before you step onto that battlefield.

### The Art of Triple-Stenting Planning

What made this TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV possible wasn't luck; it was aggressive pre-procedural mapping via Multislice Computed Tomography (MSCT). You cannot fly blind into three concentric rings of metal without knowing exactly where your coronary arteries are hiding relative to those frames.

The team faced massive constraints:
*   **Neo-Skirt Formation:** Stacking valves often causes leaflets to get trapped behind new frames ("neo-skin"), narrowing the effective orifice area (EOA) instantly upon implantation due to compression against existing struts.
*   **Coronary Obstruction:** With every added layer of stent mesh moving closer to the sinuses of Valsalva, the risk of burying coronary ostia skyrockets.
*   **Access Preservation:** Future coronary engagement could become impossible if commissures aren't aligned correctly across all three devices.

Their solution? A highly strategic high-implant technique using an Acurate neo2 (size S). Why this valve? Its supra-annular leaflet positioning allows for higher placement relative to lower-profile frames like the Evolut below it. By aligning the new device specifically with the leaflets of the middle valve (the Evolut), they managed to pin those old leaflets flat against the stent struts rather than letting them fold inward into flow space—a classic cause of immediate hemodynamic collapse in nested TAVR procedures.

They verified everything beforehand: coronary heights were safe, sinus dimensions accommodated the extra bulk, and there was zero vegetation on TOE or MSCT when they pulled trigger finger on June 2024. Crucially, they achieved commissural alignment to keep future coronary access alive—an absolute priority given his heavy CAD history—even though doing so in triple-layered anatomy is like threading needles while riding a rollercoaster in reverse gear!

# High Stakes Landing Zones: Mitigating Coronary Obstruction in Sequential Re-interventions

Let's face it: the days of considering a single Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (TAVI) as a "lifetime" solution are fading fast. As our patients live longer and valve technologies improve, we are entering an era of sequential re-interventions that pushes the boundaries of anatomy, physics, and imagination. Today, we're diving into a case report that isn't just rare; it's almost unprecedented: a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** procedure performed successfully one year ago in a gentleman whose journey began with a Lotus valve back in 2014. But the real drama here wasn't the sheer number of valves—it was navigating the treacherous landscape of coronary obstruction risks created by three overlapping metal frames, all compounded by a recent bout of *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis.

This case serves as a masterclass in how modern heart teams can tackle the "impossible." When this patient presented in 2024 with severe symptomatic stenosis (mean gradient 49 mmHg) and moderate-to-severe regurgitation through his second-generation Evolut prosthesis, the surgical option was essentially off the table. His EuroSCORE II was nearly 28%, he had extensive comorbidities including COPD and prior GI bleeds, and surgically extracting three integrated stent structures embedded in the aortic wall would likely require a total root replacement. The path forward? Another transcatheter intervention. But before picking up a catheter, the team had to solve a geometric puzzle that could easily lead to catastrophic coronary occlusion: how to squeeze another self-expanding valve inside two existing ones without sealing off the coronary arteries?

The crux of this challenge lies in the concept of the **"neo-skirt."** Every time you add a valve frame, you increase the thickness of the stent layers at your landing zone. In previous iterations or if deployed too low, these overlapping struts create an artificial barrier that hangs down over the coronary ostia, blocking future access or causing acute obstruction. In this specific case, computed tomography angiography (MSCT) became non-negotiable. It didn't just show us where to put the valve; it mapped out the precise interactions between the frames of the original Lotus (the outermost layer), the intervening Evolut (middle layer), and the proposed Acurate neo2 (inner layer). The data revealed a minimum lumen diameter of merely 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame—a tight squeeze indeed—but critically highlighted sufficient space between the predicted strut locations and the coronary ostia *if* placed correctly.

So, what was their secret weapon? A deliberate **high-implantation strategy**. Instead of aiming for standard annular depth, the operators utilized pre-procedural modeling to align their new Acurate neo2 valve specifically with leaflets of the middle-layer Evolut prosthesis. By anchoring higher up, they ensured that any potential inward-folding leaflets from previous generations were pinned against the stent framework rather than drifting downward to form an obstructive curtain near the sinuses. They weren't trying to replace tissue; they were engineering a scaffold that kept flow paths clear for both blood and future catheters.

The execution relied heavily on precision timing and hemodynamics monitoring under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm. After aggressive balloon predilation to maximize radial force within that tiny 13.7 mm corridor, they deployed the S-size Acurate neo2. Crucially, they prioritized commissural alignment—not just for optimal flow today, but to ensure that if this patient needs PCI tomorrow or five years from now, there is actually wire access available through that lattice work of stainless steel and nitinol balloons immediately afterward. Post-deployment ballooning helped expand not only the new valve but also slightly dilated layers beneath it, improving leaflet mobility despite no formal fracture being performed initially (though high pressure likely did some heavy lifting).

One-month follow-up told an encouraging story: gradients plummeted from 67 mmHg right after implantation down to 27 mmHg as LV remodeling occurred and edema subsided. By one year? The patient is asymptomatic walking miles away from his baseline distress levels with stable EF at 65% and no recurrence of his earlier infection (*E. faecalis*). It proves that even after treated endocarditis—which often scares clinicians away from placing foreign material—the door remains open for catheter-based salvation provided strict criteria regarding infection clearance are met and anatomical planning is flawless.

However, let's temper our enthusiasm with reality checks for those reading this who manage similar complex cases next week:
*   **Imaging is king:** You cannot wing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures without multislice CT detailing frame overlap angles, sinus dimensions, and exact valve-to-coronary distances; standard echo simply won't cut it here.
*   **High placement matters:** Intentionally implanting supracoronarily can mitigate neo-skirt formation by pinning old leaflets upward instead of letting them hang down over inflow/outflow tracts.
*   **Future-proofing**: Commissural alignment isn't optional anymore when dealing with triple-stents; losing coronary access permanently limits life-saving options later if things go wrong again down line anyway so plan accordingly upfront!

## Three Valves Deep: When Immediate Gradients Lie After TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

We often teach residents to trust the immediate post-deployment numbers. If the valve pops in, there's no leak, and the pressure trace looks flat? Success. But what happens when your patient leaves the lab with a "failed" gradient, only to wake up six months later feeling brand new?

Today we're dissecting a truly unique case from Merkulov et al. involving a gentleman in his late 70s who just became one of the few documented survivors of a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** procedure—and did so *after* surviving prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

But here's the plot twist that caught our attention: Immediately after deploying that third valve, this patient looked like a disaster on paper. His mean gradient skyrocketed to **67 mmHg**, and his indexed effective orifice area (EOAi) plunged to **0.4 cm²/m²**. By every textbook definition available right now, he had severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). Yet, fast-forward 12 months, and he is walking miles without dyspnea with a normal gradient of 26 mmHg.

How does a "severely mismatched" valve suddenly start working perfectly without another intervention? The answer lies in the biomechanics of stacking metal on top of metal and the phenomenon of delayed hemodynamic normalization.

### The Stacking Challenge: From Lotus to Evolut to Acurate

To understand why this happened, we have to respect the geometry of what went down here. This isn't just a simple repeat procedure; it is a logistical nightmare solved with exquisite precision.

Back in 2014, the patient got a **Lotus valve** (a mechanically expanding device known for its distinct frame structure). Seven years later, facing restenosis, they deployed a **Medtronic Evolut** inside it. Then came PVE (*Enterococcus faecalis*), which destroyed the leaflets, leading to severe regurgitation and stenosis again. By 2024, with an EuroSCORE II of nearly 27% and prohibitive surgical risk due to two fused stent layers buried deep in his ascending aorta, surgery was effectively off the table.

The Heart Team opted for a third TAVI using an **Edwards Sapien/Acurate neo2**? Wait, let me check the specifics... actually, they chose an **Acurate neo2**. This self-expanding platform was selected specifically because its supra-annular leaflet positioning could sit higher relative to the existing frames, potentially sparing coronary ostia that were getting squeezed by previous generations. They utilized aggressive CT planning to align commissures and avoid blocking coronaries—a move that worked flawlessly regarding coronary patency but initially masked a different problem: flow resistance through three concentric rings of stent struts.

### The Acute Trap: Why That 67 mmHg Mean Gradient Wasn't What It Seemed

When you deploy a self-expanding valve inside two other rigid structures, you aren't just placing tissue in an annulus; you are fighting physics. The immediate post-op echo showing severe PPM wasn't necessarily due to incorrect sizing or permanent narrowing. Instead, it reflects **acute frame friction and incomplete radial expansion.**

Think about it: You have an outer ring (the original Lotus), an intermediate ring (the Evolut), and now an inner ring (the Acurate neo2). Even if you deploy the new valve fully at room temperature or controlled body temp in these constrained environments, the outward radial force generated by the nitinol frame has to overcome not just calcification or scar tissue from endocarditis recovery, but also the sheer physical resistance of two embedded stents compressing inwardly.

Furthermore, there is likely some degree of **"leaflet crimping."** In single TAVIs, we worry about leaflets being pinned against calcium. Here, the old Evolut leaflets were pushed back against their own struts by the incoming Acurate frame. Initially, these trapped leaflets create turbulence and resistance—hence that terrifyingly high gradient of 67 mmHg immediately post-bailout. The heart muscle was screaming because it couldn't push blood through three stacked lattices yet.

### The Remodeling Phase: How Aggressive Ballooning Sets Things Free

So how did he get better? The secret sauce appears to be **high-pressure balloon post-dilation** combined with biological remodeling over time.

During the procedure described in this report, the operators didn't stop at deployment; they aggressively post-dilated with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing. This maneuver served two critical functions:
1.  It expanded the Acurate frame against its maximum capacity within tight limits.
2.  Crucially, it likely induced micro-fractures or plastic deformation in the inner struts of the underlying Evolut and potentially compressed/splayed out parts of even older Lotus architecture enough to widen the actual flow channel slightly beyond what static imaging suggested days prior.

However, mechanical expansion is only half the story. Over weeks and months as LV pressures normalized and inflammation subsided post-endocarditis therapy (ampicillin for 6 weeks earlier), those internal leaflets settled into their optimal orientation ("creep"), reducing drag forces across all three layers simultaneously. The transition from severe PPM (Acute) to mild-moderate stenosis (Chronic) represents true dynamic adaptation where stiffness gave way to compliance through sustained hemodynamic stress relief provided by careful medical management before full stabilization occurred at ~1 month marks seen on echo data points mentioned above!

## Lifetime Management: Is Your Third TAVi Aligned for Tomorrow's Crisis?

We often treat TAVR as a "one-and-done" solution, especially in our octogenarians. But we are entering an era where patients aren't just surviving longer; they are surviving *multiple* valve failures. When the first valve fails, we drop a second inside it. What happens when that one fails too? Welcome to the wild frontier of **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**, a scenario so rare it usually warrants a footnote in a textbook rather than a headline in the literature. Yet, this isn't science fiction anymore—it's happening right now, and the stakes for future coronary access have never been higher.

Consider the case of a septuagenarian male with a cardiovascular CV that reads like a checklist of every major structural heart challenge imaginable. His journey began back in 2014 with a 27 mm Lotus valve placement. By 2022, facing severe restenosis with an EF plummeting to 42%, he underwent his first redo: a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis. Just three months later, disaster struck—not another calcification issue, but *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). After six weeks of aggressive antibiotics and documented cure, the valve degenerated again by early 2024. He now faced severe stenosis (mean gradient 49 mm Hg), moderate-to-severe regurgitation, and two layers of metal already occupying his annulus. Surgery was effectively off the table due to prohibitive EuroSCORE II (>27%), comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeds), and the sheer nightmare of dissecting three fused stent frames from the aortic root. The answer? A third TAVI: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

But here is where this case moves beyond mere procedural bravado into critical educational territory: **Commissural Alignment**.

The procedural goal wasn't just to get another leaflet moving; it was to ensure that if this patient ever needed emergency PCI ten years down the line—or sooner—we wouldn't be hunting for needle-sized holes through a labyrinth of overlapping struts. Using advanced MSCT modeling, the Heart Team chose an Acurate neo2 prosthesis specifically for its ability to facilitate high implantation and commissural alignment relative to the underlying Evolut frame. They didn't rely on luck or fluoroscopy alone; they used pre-procedural CT to map the exact interaction between the new frame's posts and the old frame's cells.

The technical execution was meticulous. Under rapid pacing, they deployed the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets. Why? To fixate those failing leaflets against the outer stent ring rather than letting them fold inward into a "neo-skirt" that would obliterate coronary flow. Post-deployment angiography confirmed something remarkable: despite having three concentric metallic cages sandwiched together, coronary perfusion was patent without any protective wire tricks like BASILICA or BEAT-LVOT procedures being necessary at this stage. At one-year follow-up, the patient is asymptomatic with preserved LV function (EF 65%) and stable gradients (26 mm Hg). He survived not just one failure cycle, but three distinct hemodynamic crises without needing surgical rescue.

This case forces us to confront a shifting paradigm in structural heart medicine. We can no longer think about TAVR as an isolated event for today's physiology; we must plan for tomorrow's catastrophes. Every time we nestle a valve inside another valve, we increase the density of metal struts covering the coronary ostia by roughly double or triple depending on geometry. If you don't align your commissures correctly during index implantations—even your second or third—the path to future coronary cannulation becomes statistically impossible without resorting to risky techniques like bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVVF) or even emergent hybrid operating room conversions which carry massive morbidity in this demographic.

Furthermore, this case validates that PVE is not necessarily an absolute contraindication for repeat transcatheter intervention *if* strict criteria are met: completed antibiotic courses, negative workup for active vegetation on TOE/CT, and prohibitive surgical risk factors favoring transcatheter salvage over root replacement surgery which would essentially involve replacing half the ascending aorta in an elderly COPD patient. The key takeaway here is that while infection complicates tissue integrity and accelerates degeneration (likely causing leaflet fibrosis and restriction in this case), it doesn't preclude mechanical correction once sterilized—if you have enough room inside your annulus to put another shoebox inside two other shoeboxes!
