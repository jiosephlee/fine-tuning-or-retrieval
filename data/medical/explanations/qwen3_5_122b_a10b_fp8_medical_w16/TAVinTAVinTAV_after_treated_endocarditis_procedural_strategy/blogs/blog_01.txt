Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Beyond the Fourth Time? Feasibility of 'Triple-Stacked' TAVR After Endocarditis

We've all heard the mantra: "Once a TAVR fails, it's usually the last one before surgery." Then comes the era of *Valve-in-Valve*, and we accept that a second life is possible if the annulus holds up. But push past two layers, add a history of **prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)**, and suddenly you're staring down the barrel of something most guidelines would politely suggest is impossible. Yet, here we are with a fascinating case report from Merkulov et al., detailing a **"TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV"** procedure successfully performed in a septuagenarian male after treated endocarditis.

This isn't just about stacking metal rings; it's about rewriting the rulebook on lifetime management for structural heart disease. The patient, now in his late 70s, presents with a cardiovascular résumé that reads like a thriller novel: three separate TAVRs over a decade, a bout of *Enterococcus* endocarditis requiring six weeks of IV antibiotics, and eventual catastrophic hemodynamic collapse from severe restenosis. Traditional thinking screams "Surgical Explant," but given his EuroSCORE II of nearly 28%, COPD, diabetic nephropathy-like physiology, and three-vessel CAD status, open surgery was effectively contraindicated. The team didn't back down; they leaned heavily on modern CT physics and strategic valve selection to pull off what was thought to be non-feasible.

Let's break down the chronology because the sequence dictates the solution. Back in 2014, our protagonist got his first ride: a **Lotus** valve (the old Boston Scientific adjustable-flow marvel). It held until 2021 when things went south fast—gradients spiked to 83 mmHg, and LV function tanked to 42%. Enter stage right: **TAV-in-TAV #1**. They deployed an Evolut R/Medtronic self-expanding valve inside the Lotus in 2022. Hemodynamics improved initially (EF bounced to 54%), but fate intervened shortly thereafter with *E. faecalis* bacteremia and frank PVE. After aggressive antimicrobial stewardship confirmed cure via negative cultures and TOE, the valve began failing again due to mixed mechanisms: likely leaflet fibrosis from infection combined with standard structural decay.

By mid-2024, gradients were climbing again (49 mmHg mean), AVA collapsed to 0.5 cm², and regurgitation returned. The question wasn't whether to intervene—it was how? You cannot simply throw any valve into this mosh pit of stent struts without risking immediate coronary occlusion or creating an impenetrable neo-laminar barrier leading to severe Prosthesis-Patient Mismatch (PPM).

The secret sauce here was **meticulous CT planning** coupled with specific device architecture choices. The team utilized multislice CT (MSCT) not just for sizing, but for virtual reconstruction of the "three-layer sandwich" created by the Lotus + Evolut frames. Crucially, they identified that high implantation was necessary to avoid crushing coronary flow beneath a massive "neo-skirt." They chose the **Acurate neo2**, specifically because its top-hinge design allows for precise placement above the inflow ring of underlying devices while maintaining excellent coaptation heights relative to coronaries.

During the procedure, they employed rapid pacing and careful commissural alignment—not just for aesthetics today, but for tomorrow's potential coronary intervention. The goal was to fixate the failed inner leaflets against the outer stent struts rather than letting them billow backward into the lumen. Initial post-op echo showed some grim numbers (Mean Gradient 67 mmHg!), suggesting acute severe PPM despite perfect deployment. But remember this lesson: **acute post-deployment gradients are notoriously misleading in stacked valves.** One month later? That gradient plummeted to 27 mmHg as adjacent tissue remodeling occurred and frames expanded slightly under pressure loads (aided perhaps by high-pressure post-dilation fracturing minor calcific interfaces). At one year? He's walking fine with normal EF and no recurrence of infection or heart failure signals.
