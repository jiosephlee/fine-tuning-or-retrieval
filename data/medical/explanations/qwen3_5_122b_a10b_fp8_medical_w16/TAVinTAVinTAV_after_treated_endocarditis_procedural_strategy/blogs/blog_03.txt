# Three Valves Deep: Solving the "Impossible" Gradient Paradox in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

We often talk about the "lifetime management" of structural heart disease, but few cases illustrate just how far we've pushed the envelope quite like the recent case report from Russia detailing a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** procedure. Let's get straight to why this matters: usually, when we see a new valve sitting inside two others with massive overlap, our brains immediately scream *coronary obstruction* or *severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM)* leading to inevitable failure. But here, the team didn't just survive; they thrived. They navigated a path through a "triple-layered" stent sandwich in a patient with a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis, turning a near-fatal hemodynamic disaster into a 1-year victory lap.

What makes this case truly fascinating isn't just the audacity of placing three valves sequentially—it's the **hemodynamic paradox** observed right after deployment and how it taught us something profound about frame physics. When the third valve (an Acurate neo2) finally dropped into place atop the previous Lotus and Evolut devices, things looked grim on paper. Immediate post-procedure echo showed a mean gradient of 67 mmHg and an indexed area of 0.4 cm²/m²—classic textbook definitions of severe PPM. If you saw these numbers five minutes ago, your gut reaction would be panic: "Did we pick the wrong size? Is there thrombosis?" Yet, four weeks later, that gradient plummeted to 27 mmHg with normalization of flow. How does a severely malfunctioning valve fix itself without another operation? It turns out, understanding the mechanics of *frame-on-frame friction* is now as important as knowing your annulus sizing algorithms.

Let's break down the player roster before diving into the drama. This gentleman in his late 70s wasn't having an easy ride initially. Back in 2014, he got a Boston Scientific Lotus valve because surgery was too risky given his CAD and comorbidities. Fast forward eight years, and that original Lotus had calcified into concrete with an insane mean gradient of 83 mmHg and dropping EF. So, they went for round two in 2022 with a Medtronic Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure. That fixed him temporarily until life threw curveballs: he developed *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) mere months later. After aggressive antibiotics cleared the infection, the story took its wildest turn: by early 2024, despite being infection-free, that second-generation construct was failing again with severe stenosis and regurgitation. Surgery was effectively off-limits due to EuroSCORE II >27% and fused anatomy; if you tried open repair then-root replacement would likely have been necessary anyway. The Heart Team decided to go for broke with a third attempt: TAV-in-TAW-in-TAV using an Acurate neo2 in mid-2024.

The real masterclass here lies in the procedural strategy derived from heavy-duty MDCT planning. You cannot fly blind when nesting three metal cages together; you'd lose every bit of oxygen supply downstream instantly if you weren't precise. The team meticulously mapped out coronaries relative to the expected "neo-skirt"—that dangerous pileup of tissue and struts that forms above multiple layers—which could easily occlude those vessels upon inflation. They opted for **high implantation**, deliberately aligning the new Acurate leaflets with the existing Evolut leaflets rather than burying them deep inside old structures. Why? To prevent those old flimsy leaves from flopping back inward ("leaflet buckling") and narrowing your channel even further once compressed by gravity and pressure waves from breathing cycles over days/months. Plus keeping everything higher gave better odds at accessing coronaries later on if needed—even though crossing all three rings already feels like threading needles underwater!
