Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Endocarditis vs. Structural Deterioration: When Is It Safe to Retract Cathetically After Three Valves?

Let's face it: we usually think about "lifetime management" of the aortic valve as something reserved for our youngest TAVI candidates—the ones getting their first valve at age 75 who might need another one before they pass away. But rarely does anyone talk about the "fourth dimension": What happens when you run out of space, run out of standard pathways, and then throw in a nasty bout of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)?

We just got word of a case that sounds almost impossible until you read the fine print: **A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** procedure in a gentleman now in his late 70s. Even crazier? His second valve failed because of *Enterococcus* endocarditis, yet three years later, he walked back into the lab for a third transcatheter intervention—and did so with flying colors.

This isn't just a story about stacking metal rings inside other metal rings; it's a masterclass in defying the textbook algorithms when reality gets messy. Today, we're diving into how the authors navigated the treacherous waters of treating structural deterioration in a post-PVE heart, challenging the dogma that says "infection equals surgery."

### The Patient Who Wouldn't Quit
Our protagonist is a man in his late 70s with a cardiovascular CV longer than most internists' coffee orders. He started down this road back in 2014 with arterial hypertension, three-vessel CAD (multiple PCI stents over the decades), COPD, Type 2 DM, and an AV block requiring a pacemaker all crammed into his history. That same year, he received his first life-saving device: a 27 mm **Lotus** valve.

Fast forward seven years to 2021. Symptoms return. Echo shows severe stenosis with a mean gradient climbing to 83 mmHg and an ejection fraction dropping to 42%. Enter Stage 2: A **TAV-in-TAV**. They deployed a 23 mm Medtrony Evolut inside the old Lotus. Not perfect—they needed open vascular repair for a femoral hematoma—but hemodynamically, she was singing again with EF bouncing back to 54%.

Then came plot twist #1: **Endocarditis.** Just three months later, fever returns. Cultures scream *Enterococcus faecalis*. The new Evolut is compromised. Guidelines (and common sense) usually say get them to surgery ASAP if there's PVE causing heart failure or uncontrolled sepsis. But look at this guy again: EuroSCORE II nearly **28%** (yes, really), COPD, GI bleeds, and two layers of calcified titanium already fused to his annulus. Surgery wasn't just risky; it was likely fatal without replacing half his ascending aorta too. So they opted for aggressive antibiotics instead—a full six-week course plus maintenance—clearing the infection completely before moving on.

By mid-2024, despite being infection-free for two years, her mechanical fate sealed itself again. Severe restenosis returned (*Eco*-friendly bacteria leave scars!). Mean gradient spiked to 49 mmHg with severe AR and an absurdly small Area of only **0.5 cm²**. She was symptomatic again: dyspnea on walking just meters out your door length. Time for Round Three? Or death sentence?

They chose Option C: **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**, aiming straight through both previous generations using advanced CT modeling techniques never seen together before!

#### Why Did This Work Against All Odds?
Standard teaching dictates that any hint of prior PVE pushes us toward surgical exploration unless absolutely contraindicated surgically—which ironically applies here twice-over due to extreme frailty combined with anatomical nightmare fuel (`stacked` frames = hard dissection plane + high stroke/deadness risk). Yet somehow... somehow... these folks pulled off planting an entire new generation (**Edwards Acurate Neo-S**) directly onto existing infrastructure without killing him—or worse—heating up latent bugs again!
