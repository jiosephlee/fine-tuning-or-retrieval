Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# High Stakes Planning: Navigating Coronaries in Triple-Layered Anatomy

We often think of "Valve-in-Valve" (ViV) as the new normal in structural heart disease. But imagine a scenario where you aren't just placing one valve inside another; you're navigating through *three* concentric layers of metallic scaffolding, all sitting atop a history of infected prosthetics. That is exactly the reality we face in a recently published case report by Merkulov et al., detailing a rare **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** procedure.

This isn't just a story about pushing buttons on a delivery system; it's a masterclass in why modern TAVI relies less on brute force and almost entirely on precision engineering derived from Multi-Detector Computed Tomography (MDCT). When your patient has survived invasive endocarditis and two previous transcatheter interventions, the margin for error vanishes. One misstep could seal off the coronary arteries behind three generations of metal struts—an absolute catastrophe that leaves the operator with nowhere to go surgically or percutaneously.

Let's dive into how this septuagenarian navigated life with four distinct cardiac events over ten years, culminating in a third TAVI that hinged entirely on pre-procedural imaging strategy.

## The Patient Journey: From Degeneration to Endocarditis Back to Degeneration

Our protagonist, a man in his late 70s, entered our narrative back in 2014 with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis. Due to his prohibitive surgical risk profile—marked by COPD, diabetes, multivessel CAD, and GI bleeds—he received a **Lotus valve**. By 2022, structural valve deterioration set in hard. Mean gradients skyrocketed to 83 mmHg, and LV function plummeted. It was time for round two: a **TAV-in-TAV** using an Evolut prosthesis.

Things looked promising initially until disaster struck three months later. Fever returned, cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*, and the patient developed Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE). After six weeks of aggressive IV antibiotics and confirmation of infection clearance via TEE and CT, he faced yet another dilemma: recurrence of severe stenosis and regurgitation in early 2024.

With an EuroSCORE II of nearly 28% and deep tissue integration from two prior devices plus scarring from endocarditis, open-heart surgery was deemed effectively impossible—it would have likely required total root reconstruction. The Heart Team chose the path of least resistance but highest technical demand: **Third-generation ViV**. They needed to fit an Acurate neo2 inside an Evolut, which sat inside a Lotus. All while ensuring the right and left main coronaries didn't get crushed by the "neo-skirt."

## Why MDCT Wasn't Just Optional—It Was Mandatory

If there is any lesson here for us working in emergency medicine or general cardiology, it is that when dealing with complex structural revisions, standard echo is necessary but insufficient. You cannot see what you cannot measure on 2D planes. The real hero of this case was the MDCT planning session before a single wire crossed the groin.

In typical TAVR planning, we look at annulus sizing and calcium distribution. Here, the stakes shifted dramatically toward **coronary geometry amidst layered hardware**. The team had to answer questions most of us rarely consider: How does the strut density change as you stack valves? Where exactly will the leaflets fold when compressed between these three rings? Will they form a barrier ("neo-skirt") that blocks flow?

Specifically, they focused on three critical metrics identified by MDCT:
1.  **Sinus of Valsalva Dimensions:** Were there enough millimeters of space above the sinotubular junction to accommodate extra frame material without impinging on flow?
2.  **Neoskirt Formation Potential:** Would the old Evolut leaflets get pushed down into an inverted position upon compression by the new Acurate valve? If so, did they create a tent-like structure hanging over the coronary ostia?
3.  **Valve-to-Coronary Distance:** Crucially measuring the gap between the expected final position of the outermost valve frame and the actual origin of the coronary ostia *through* three layers of metal meshwork transparency issues (which CT resolves better than fluoroscopy).

Without this roadmap, blind implantation would have been Russian Roulette with lethal odds for myocardial infarction or stroke if embolic debris dislodged from decades-old calcification or vegetation remnants wasn't accounted for (though none were found here). Their data showed that despite having zero room for maneuverability traditionally speaking—the sinus width was tight—they could achieve safety through precise vertical placement logic dictated solely by volumetric rendering software modeling interactions between frames before touching them physically together intraoperatively!
