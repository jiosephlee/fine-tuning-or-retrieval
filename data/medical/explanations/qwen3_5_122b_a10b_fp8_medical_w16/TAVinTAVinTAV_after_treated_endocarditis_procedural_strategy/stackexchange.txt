Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] In a patient with a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) presenting with new-onset severe valve dysfunction, what are the critical preprocedural requirements before considering a repeat transcatheter intervention instead of surgical explantation?

In a patient with a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) presenting with severe valve dysfunction, the critical preprocedural requirement before considering a repeat transcatheter intervention over surgical explantation is **definitive confirmation of infection resolution** coupled with **comprehensive multidisciplinary anatomical assessment**.

As illustrated in our case report, the decision to pursue a "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" procedure was predicated on rigorous proof that the *Enterococcus faecalis* infection was fully eradicated. The Heart Team relied on a convergence of data: completion of a full six-week antimicrobial course, negative repeat blood cultures, and crucially, the absence of vegetations or paravalvular abscesses on both transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT). Without this absolute certainty that the infection is quiescent, attempting another percutaneous intervention would carry an unacceptably high risk of seeding the new prosthesis or failing to treat an occult perivalvular extension.

Furthermore, given the prohibitive surgical risk inherent in this patient's profile—including prior open vascular repairs, severe comorbidities, and the technical difficulty of explanting deeply integrated stent frames—the second pillar of preprocedural planning is **high-resolution CT angiography for anatomical feasibility**. In this specific scenario, MSCT was indispensable for evaluating the "triple-stented" anatomy. It allowed us to measure the minimum lumen diameter of the existing Evolut frame (13.7 mm), calculate valve-to-coronary distances to rule out obstruction by a potential neo-skirt, and plan for high implantation depths to preserve coronary flow.

Therefore, the threshold for proceeding with transcatheter salvage in post-PVE patients is not merely hemodynamic severity but the dual verification of sterile status and a CT-validated roadmap that ensures coronary patency and adequate landing zones despite complex frame layering. Only when these conditions are met does the transcatheter approach offer a viable alternative to high-risk surgical root replacement.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the 'neo-skirt' phenomenon contribute to coronary obstruction risk in TAV-in-TAV procedures, and what specific implantation strategy was utilized in this case to mitigate this risk while maintaining hemodynamic efficacy?

**Answer:**

The "neo-skirt" phenomenon represents a critical mechanical hazard in TAV-in-TAV (and increasingly TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) procedures. It occurs when the leaflets of the innermost, failing valve are pushed outward by the new valve's frame but then fold back inward or are compressed against the outer valve's stent struts. This creates a dense, multilayered fabric barrier—the "neo-skirt"—that extends toward the sinuses of Valsalva. If this structure forms below the coronary ostia, it physically obstructs coronary blood flow and renders future coronary cannulation impossible due to the inability to traverse the thickened, rigid mesh.

In this specific case, mitigating this risk required a departure from standard deployment depths. The team employed a **high-implantation strategy**, guided by meticulous preprocedural multislice CT (MSCT) modeling. Rather than seating the third-generation Acurate neo2 valve deeply within the existing Evolut frame—which would have trapped the previous leaflets and formed a neo-skirt occluding the coronaries—the device was intentionally deployed higher. The goal was to align the new valve's inflow with the *leaflets* of the intermediate Evolut prosthesis. By doing so, the old leaflets were pinned firmly against the outer Lotus frame, preventing them from prolapsing inward to create an obstructive skirt below the coronary ostia.

This strategic placement preserved a patent channel for coronary flow and maintained potential access for future interventions, despite the extreme complexity of three concentric stent layers. Crucially, this was balanced against hemodynamic needs: aligning with the Evolut leaflets maximized the effective orifice area by preventing inward leaflet collapse, thereby reducing patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). While immediate post-procedure gradients were elevated due to severe PPM in this triple-stented anatomy, subsequent remodeling and balloon optimization led to sustained hemodynamic improvement at one year without compromising coronary patency. This case underscores that in complex redo scenarios, anatomical precision regarding implantation depth is paramount to balancing acute hemodynamics with long-term coronary accessibility.

### [Clinical Pearls] Despite immediate post-procedure echocardiography showing a mean gradient of 67 mmHg and severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), the patient's symptoms resolved and gradients normalized by one month. What physiological mechanisms explain this delayed hemodynamic improvement in a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenario?

**Clinical Pearl:** In complex TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures, an acute post-procedural echocardiogram demonstrating severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) and elevated gradients does not necessarily predict permanent hemodynamic failure. Delayed normalization of gradients over weeks to months is often attributable to **continued radial force-mediated expansion of the nested stent frames** and **remodeling of the leaflet geometry**.

In this specific case, the immediate post-procedure mean gradient of 67 mmHg reflected a "locked" state where the newly implanted Acurate neo2 valve had not yet fully expanded the underlying Evolut and Lotus frames. The acute finding of severe PPM (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²) was likely exacerbated by the restricted mobility of the prior valve leaflets being pinned against the new frame. However, several mechanisms facilitated recovery by one month:

1.  **Radial Expansion and Frame Remodeling:** The self-expanding nature of the Acurate neo2, combined with aggressive post-dilation (using a 20/40 mm balloon), exerts continuous outward radial force. Over days to weeks, this force gradually expands the rigid metal struts of the two underlying prostheses (the Evolut and Lotus), effectively increasing the true internal diameter of the triple-layered conduit beyond what was achieved acutely.
2.  **Leaflet Optimization:** The procedural strategy utilized high implantation to fixate the prior Evolut leaflets against its own stent frame. As the outer frame expanded, it likely prevented inward folding of these older leaflets ("neo-skirt" formation), thereby unmasking a larger effective orifice area that was mechanically constrained immediately post-deployment.
3.  **Hemodynamic Adaptation:** The patient's left ventricle, which had shown recovery in ejection fraction (from 42% to 66%), underwent favorable reverse remodeling with reduced chamber volumes. This decreased stroke volume demand relative to valve area may have contributed to lower measured gradients as flow dynamics stabilized.

Therefore, clinicians should interpret acute high gradients in multi-valve TAVR scenarios with caution, recognizing that full hemodynamic optimization may lag behind procedural success due to delayed mechanical expansion of nested devices. Serial imaging is essential before labeling such cases as failures or considering re-intervention for PPM.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When selecting a third-generation transcatheter heart valve for a 'TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV' procedure involving two prior devices (Lotus and Evolut), why was the Acurate neo2 chosen over other available platforms, specifically regarding its impact on coronary access and effective orifice area?

In this complex "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" scenario involving a Lotus valve, followed by an Evolut, and now a third intervention, the selection of the **Acurate neo2** was driven principally by its unique supra-annular leaflet architecture and lower frame profile, which directly addressed the twin threats of coronary obstruction and prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM).

When stacking multiple devices, the primary hemodynamic penalty arises from the accumulation of frame struts and the potential for prior valve leaflets to fold inward into the new orifice, creating a "neo-skirt" that restricts flow. The Acurate neo2 features a **supra-annular design**, meaning its leaflets sit above the sealing skirt. In this case, we leveraged this geometry to deploy the device at a higher implantation depth relative to the existing Evolut frame. By aligning the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut leaflets rather than burying them deep within the annulus, we effectively pinned these redundant leaflets against the outer stent struts. This maneuver prevented inward folding and maximized the residual effective orifice area (EOA), mitigating severe PPM despite three layers of metal scaffolding.

Furthermore, regarding coronary access—the most feared complication in multi-valve interventions—the Acurate neo2's **low-profile upper crown** and lack of an external skirt extending significantly above the valve plane offered superior clearance compared to other self-expanding or balloon-expandable alternatives. The pre-procedural CT modeling confirmed that this specific platform minimized interference with the coronary ostia. Additionally, its design facilitates better opportunities for **commissural alignment**. Aligning the new device's commissures with those of the underlying Evolut reduces strut overlap at key angles, theoretically preserving a clearer pathway for future coronary cannulation if needed. Thus, in a patient with prohibitive surgical risk and triple-layered anatomy, the Acurate neo2 represented the optimal balance between maximizing flow through a narrowed channel and maintaining accessibility to vital coronary arteries.

### [Morning Report] Why is computed tomography (CT) angiography considered superior to transthoracic echocardiography for procedural planning in complex multi-layered valve interventions, particularly concerning the assessment of coronary ostial height and frame interaction?

Computed tomography (CT) angiography is superior to transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) for procedural planning in complex multi-layered valve interventions because it provides the necessary three-dimensional spatial resolution to visualize the intricate geometry of overlapping stent frames and their relationship to the aortic root structures. While TTE offers valuable hemodynamic data, as seen in our case where it identified severe restenosis and regurgitation, it lacks the ability to precisely delineate the "neo-skirt" formed by multiple valve layers or measure critical distances such as coronary ostial height relative to the innermost stent struts.

In this specific case of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient's anatomy involved three distinct prosthetic generations: a Lotus valve, an Evolut prosthesis, and finally an Acurate neo2. The decision to proceed relied entirely on multislice CT (MSCT) findings, which revealed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame and allowed us to map the exact interaction between these frames. Crucially, CT enabled us to assess the risk of coronary obstruction by measuring the valve-to-coronary distance and analyzing how the anticipated new frame would sit relative to the existing ones. This visualization was pivotal in selecting a high-implantation strategy for the Acurate neo2; we deliberately aligned it with the Evolut leaflets to prevent inward folding that could further occlude flow or create a barrier to future coronary access. Without CT's capacity to model these complex frame interactions and verify adequate sinus dimensions, attempting this third intervention would have carried an unacceptable risk of acute coronary occlusion or catastrophic prosthesis-patient mismatch. Thus, CT serves as the indispensable roadmap for navigating the "lifetime management" challenges inherent in serial transcatheter interventions.

### [Management] According to current ESC guidelines, surgical replacement is generally preferred for prosthetic valve endocarditis. Under what specific anatomical and clinical conditions did the Heart Team justify proceeding with a third TAVI in this patient despite these general recommendations?

In this complex case, the Heart Team justified proceeding with a third TAVI (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) over the standard recommendation for surgical replacement by identifying a convergence of **prohibitive surgical risk**, **strict exclusion of active infection**, and **anatomical feasibility** confirmed by advanced imaging.

First, regarding the clinical condition of infection, the decision hinged on the fact that the patient's prosthetic valve endocarditis (caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*) was fully treated. The patient had completed a six-week course of targeted antibiotics, and crucially, comprehensive workup—including transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT)—confirmed the complete resolution of infection with no evidence of active vegetations, abscesses, or paravalvular extension. Current ESC guidelines permit transcatheter intervention in PVE only when surgery is contraindicated and infection is controlled; this patient met both criteria.

Second, the surgical risk was deemed prohibitive. The patient possessed an EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and significant comorbidities including COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding, and a history of vascular complications requiring open repair after his second TAVI. Anatomically, the presence of two deeply integrated prior valve frames (Lotus and Evolut) meant that surgical explantation would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, carrying an unacceptably high mortality risk compared to a percutaneous approach.

Finally, anatomical feasibility was rigorously established through CT-guided planning. The team identified that high implantation of a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve could avoid coronary obstruction by navigating the "neo-skirt" above the coronary ostia while maximizing the effective orifice area. By aligning commissures and utilizing post-dilation to expand the intermediate frame without fracturing it, they achieved a viable hemodynamic solution where surgery offered little chance of success. Thus, the deviation from standard surgical preference was driven by a life-saving balance between extreme operative danger and a meticulously planned percutaneous alternative in a sterile field.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the primary long-term concerns regarding future coronary interventions in patients who have undergone multiple sequential TAVI procedures, and how does commissural alignment play a role in mitigating these risks?

The primary long-term concern for patients undergoing multiple sequential TAVI procedures, such as the "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" scenario described in this case, is the **progressive impairment of coronary artery access** and the risk of **severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM)** due to the accumulation of stent struts.

As each new valve is deployed within the previous one, the layers of metal frames create a dense "mesh barrier." This stratification significantly increases the difficulty of cannulating the coronary ostia for future percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) or diagnostic angiography. In our patient, who already had a history of complex multivessel disease and prior PCI, preserving the ability to reach the coronaries was paramount. Additionally, stacking multiple valves inherently reduces the effective orifice area (EOA); if not meticulously managed, this leads to severe PPM, characterized by persistently elevated transvalvular gradients despite technically successful implantation.

**Commissural alignment** serves as a critical mitigation strategy against these risks. When deploying a new valve inside an existing one, aligning the commissures (the points where leaflets attach) of the new prosthesis with those of the underlying frame creates larger gaps between stent struts at these specific locations. These "commissural windows" act as natural channels that facilitate catheter engagement and wire crossing for future coronary access.

In this specific case, careful preprocedural CT planning allowed us to align the Acurate neo2's commissures with those of the prior Evolut valve. Furthermore, we employed a **high-implantation strategy**, positioning the third valve at the level of the second valve's leaflets. This prevented inward leaflet folding and minimized the formation of a "neo-skirt" that could obscure coronary ostia. While immediate post-procedure hemodynamics initially suggested severe PPM (mean gradient 67 mmHg), aggressive post-dilation and favorable remodeling led to significant gradient reduction over time. However, this case reinforces that in triple-stented anatomy, procedural success relies not just on sealing leaks but on strategic alignment to ensure that life-saving coronary interventions remain feasible should they be needed decades later.
