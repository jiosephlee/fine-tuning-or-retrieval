Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Clinical Presentation and Complex Patient History

The patient, a man in his late seventies, presented to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center exhibiting classic symptoms of severe hemodynamic compromise secondary to aortic valve dysfunction. His primary complaints included exertional dyspnea, a spastic sensation in the left chest wall during physical activity (specifically walking distances of 200–300 meters), dizziness, and profound fatigue. These symptoms reflect the physiological consequences of fixed left ventricular outflow obstruction, where the failing heart cannot adequately increase cardiac output to meet metabolic demands during stress. While these presenting features are typical of severe aortic stenosis, they occur here in the context of a uniquely complex medical trajectory involving multiple prior transcatheter interventions and a recent bout of life-threatening infection.

Underlying this acute presentation is a heavy burden of comorbidities that significantly elevates the patient's perioperative risk profile and complicates therapeutic decision-making. Beyond arterial hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus, the patient suffers from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022, which limits anticoagulation strategies often required for structural heart procedures. Perhaps most critically, he has extensive coronary artery disease characterized by three-vessel involvement, having undergone four separate percutaneous coronary interventions (PCIs) between 2009 and 2014 targeting the right coronary artery, left anterior descending (LAD) artery, and circumflex artery. Additionally, his conduction system disease manifested as a third-degree atrioventricular block in 2014, necessitating permanent pacemaker implantation shortly before his first valve intervention. This constellation of conditions places him at prohibitive risk for conventional surgical valve replacement or complex redo sternotomy.

The evolution of this patient's structural heart disease began in 2014 with progressive degeneration leading to severe aortic stenosis. Due to his high surgical risk profile at that time, he underwent transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) with a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). For several years, this intervention stabilized his condition; however, by December 2021, clinical deterioration recurred with worsening exertional dyspnea. Echocardiographic evaluation revealed severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis with a dramatic rise in mean transvalvular gradient to 83 mm Hg and a concurrent decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) from 54% to 42%. Recognizing the failure of the initial prosthesis and the continued unsuitability for surgery due to his comorbidities and prior sternotomy avoidance goals, the clinical team opted for a "valve-in-valve" strategy. In May 2022, he underwent his second TAVI procedure (TAV-in-TAV), receiving a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis (Medtronic) inside the failed Lotus valve. While this successfully restored hemodynamics initially—improving EF back to 54%—the procedure was marred by immediate vascular complications, specifically a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm that required open surgical repair.

The clinical course took another precarious turn just three months after the second intervention when the patient was hospitalized with persistent fever and worsening heart failure symptoms in September 2022. Diagnostic workup identified severe restenosis of the newly implanted Evolut prosthesis alongside bacteremia positive for *Enterococcus faecalis*, confirming a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). This represents one of the most formidable challenges in structural heart medicine: managing valve dysfunction superimposed on active infection within an already compromised anatomical space containing two overlapping metal frames. The patient was managed with aggressive antimicrobial therapy consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment followed by oral suppression as directed by infectious disease specialists. Crucially, all subsequent management relied on rigorous confirmation that the infection had been eradicated; repeated blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) eventually confirmed resolution with no evidence of active vegetation or paravalvular abscess. Once the infection was cleared but as symptoms recurred due to mechanical degeneration exacerbated by the inflammatory insult, the stage was set for what would become an unprecedented third transcatheter intervention: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Multimodal Imaging Strategy

The diagnostic journey in this complex case hinged on distinguishing between active prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) and structural valve deterioration (SVD) in a patient with a "triple-stented" aortic root. Following the initial presentation of dyspnea, chest pain, and fatigue, the clinical team faced a critical differential diagnosis: was the patient's hemodynamic collapse driven by recurrent infection or by mechanical failure of the existing prostheses? The differentiation was paramount because active endocarditis typically mandates prolonged antibiotic therapy and often surgical intervention, whereas structural failure in a prohibitive surgical candidate is an indication for repeat transcatheter intervention. The team utilized a multimodal approach beginning with blood cultures, which identified *Enterococcus faecalis*, confirming an infectious etiology. However, the timing of the intervention depended entirely on establishing that the infection was resolved. Clinical markers of inflammation were monitored alongside serial transthoracic echocardiograms (TTE), but definitive exclusion of active vegetations or paravalvular abscesses required transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE). Performed prior to the third intervention, TEE confirmed the absence of vegetations and perivalvular complications, validating that the patient had successfully completed six weeks of targeted antimicrobial therapy and was free from active sepsis. This cleared the pathway for considering a mechanical solution to the valve dysfunction rather than continued medical management for infection.

Once infection was ruled out as an active process, the focus shifted to quantifying the severity of prosthetic valve dysfunction through rigorous hemodynamic assessment. The TTE findings revealed severe aortic stenosis characterized by a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg and an aortic valve area (AVA) calculated via the continuity equation to be merely 0.5 cm². Concurrently, there was moderate-to-severe intraprosthetic regurgitation. These values indicated profound hemodynamic compromise that explained the patient's symptomatic decline despite previous improvements in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). The presence of two overlapping bioprosthetic valves visible on imaging suggested that leaflet thickening, calcification, or thrombosis within the nested frames was restricting forward flow. The persistence of these severe gradients despite optimized medical therapy confirmed that conservative management was insufficient and that mechanical correction was necessary to prevent further cardiac decompensation.

With the decision to intervene established, precise anatomical mapping became the cornerstone of procedural planning through multislice computed tomography (MSCT). In a standard TAVI procedure, sizing relies on annular dimensions; however, in this TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenario, the relevant metric was the internal lumen diameter available within the most recent prosthesis—the 2022 Evolut valve. MSCT measurements revealed a critically narrowed minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within this frame. This finding was decisive: it confirmed that any new device would need to traverse this restrictive space without causing catastrophic obstruction or embolization. Furthermore, MSCT provided a three-dimensional roadmap essential for navigating the complex geometry created by three generations of stent struts. The imaging allowed operators to visualize exactly how the frames interacted, identifying areas where metal overlap might impede future coronary access or create excessive resistance to balloon expansion during deployment.

Perhaps the most life-threatening risk in sequential TAVI procedures is coronary artery obstruction caused by "neo-skirt" formation or displacement of native leaflets against coronary ostia by successive devices. In this case, where three layers of stent material already existed in close proximity to the coronary arteries, this risk was exponentially higher. Detailed CT analysis focused specifically on sinus of Valsalva dimensions, coronary ostial heights, and valve-to-coronary distances relative to each other and to the anticipated position of the new Acurate neo2 prosthesis. The team evaluated whether high implantation could be achieved safely to ensure that neither the new valve frame nor displaced tissue from previous valves would occlude coronary flow upon inflation. This virtual simulation determined that high implantation aligned with specific landmarks on the Evolut frame would preserve coronary patency while maximizing effective orifice area without requiring protective measures like BASILICA or surgical bypass grafting at this stage.

Finally, these anatomical findings were weighed against surgical risk stratification to justify an off-label third transcatheter approach over surgical explantation. The patient's EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, reflecting extreme operative mortality risk driven by age, severe COPD, diabetes mellitus, prior gastrointestinal bleeding history, and complex coronary artery disease requiring multiple prior interventions beyond just PCI for CAD (specifically noting his extensive history). Beyond statistical scores, anatomical reality dictated that open-heart surgery would likely require total aortic root replacement due to deep fixation and integration of two prior transcatheter valves into the aortic wall structures; such an operation carries prohibitive morbidity in this demographic compared to percutaneous alternatives. Consequently, the convergence of resolved infection status on TEE/Blood culture data, severe hemodynamic failure on echo demonstrating gradients >40 mmHg with tiny AVA (<0.6 cm²), feasible yet narrow landing zones identified on MSCT (>13 mm lumen), safe coronary clearance predicted by CT modeling, and prohibitive surgical risk formed an integrated rationale supporting a CT-guided TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV ## Procedural Execution: The TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Technique

The execution of a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with two prior devices and a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis represents one of the most complex challenges in structural heart medicine. In this case, the procedural strategy was dictated by the need to navigate a "triple-stented" anatomy while mitigating the risks of coronary obstruction, severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), and residual regurgitation. The intervention was performed under local anesthesia utilizing a combined access strategy of the left radial and left femoral arteries. This hybrid approach facilitated simultaneous diagnostic angiography and therapeutic device delivery while minimizing vascular trauma in a patient with a history of femoral pseudoaneurysm requiring surgical repair. The entire procedure relied heavily on preprocedural multislice computed tomography (MSCT) modeling to visualize the spatial relationship between the existing Lotus and Evolut frames, ensuring that the new device could be deployed safely without compromising coronary perfusion.

Valve selection was pivotal to overcoming the anatomical constraints imposed by two overlapping stent structures. The team chose the Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthesis over other available platforms due to its specific design advantages for narrow, multi-layered landing zones. Unlike some balloon-expandable or lower-profile self-expanding valves, the Acurate neo2 features a supra-annular leaflet configuration, meaning the functional leaflets sit above the outer frame skirt. This design allows for a larger effective orifice area (EOA) relative to the external footprint of the device, which is critical in preventing severe PPM when deploying into an already restricted lumen measuring only 13.7 mm. Furthermore, the device's upper stabilizing arches allow for precise positioning without excessive radial force that could damage the fragile tissue surrounding the previous prostheses or cause embolization. The selection was further justified by the valve's capacity for commissural alignment, a feature essential for preserving future coronary access through layers of metal struts that would otherwise obscure catheter navigation.

The core technical maneuver distinguishing this procedure from standard TAV-in-TAV interventions was the deliberate strategy of high implantation. Preprocedural CT analysis revealed that placing the third valve too deeply would result in the inward folding of the leaflets from both underlying prostheses into the new valve's inflow tract, drastically reducing hemodynamic performance. To counteract this, the Acurate neo2 was deployed at a higher vertical position, aligning specifically with the leaflet plane of the middle Evolut prosthesis rather than sitting flush against its inflow edge. This "high placement" technique served two distinct physiological goals: first, it prevented the mechanical restriction caused by folded leaflets collapsing into the new valve's opening; second, it minimized the formation of a "neo-skirt"—the composite barrier created by overlapping stent frames below the coronary ostia—which poses a significant risk for acute coronary obstruction and hinders future catheter engagement. By anchoring above these critical structures, clinicians ensured that coronary flow remained unimpeded despite three concentric layers of stenting.

Optimization maneuvers were executed with precision to ensure maximal expansion and stability within this rigid environment. Prior to deployment, balloon predilation was performed using a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute to temporarily halt cardiac output and stabilize catheter position during inflation. Crucially, simultaneous aortography confirmed that coronary flow remained patent throughout this aggressive dilation phase. Following device release under fluoroscopic guidance with attention to rotational orientation for commissural alignment, post-dilation was conducted using a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing again to optimize radial expansion against both native calcification and stiff metallic struts from prior valves. This step was vital not only for sealing paravalvular leaks but also for mechanically fracturing or expanding any restrictive elements of the inner Evolut frame that might impede leaflet motion in subsequent cycles. Final confirmation via angiography verified correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion pressures, and an absence of both transvalvular and paravalvular regurgitation before concluding the procedure under local anesthesia without sedation-induced hemodynamic instability.
