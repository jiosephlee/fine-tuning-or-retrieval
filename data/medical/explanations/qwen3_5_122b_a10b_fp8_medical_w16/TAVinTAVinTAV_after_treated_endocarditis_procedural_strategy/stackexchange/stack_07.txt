Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the primary long-term concerns regarding future coronary interventions in patients who have undergone multiple sequential TAVI procedures, and how does commissural alignment play a role in mitigating these risks?

The primary long-term concern for patients undergoing multiple sequential TAVI procedures, such as the "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" scenario described in this case, is the **progressive impairment of coronary artery access** and the risk of **severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM)** due to the accumulation of stent struts.

As each new valve is deployed within the previous one, the layers of metal frames create a dense "mesh barrier." This stratification significantly increases the difficulty of cannulating the coronary ostia for future percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) or diagnostic angiography. In our patient, who already had a history of complex multivessel disease and prior PCI, preserving the ability to reach the coronaries was paramount. Additionally, stacking multiple valves inherently reduces the effective orifice area (EOA); if not meticulously managed, this leads to severe PPM, characterized by persistently elevated transvalvular gradients despite technically successful implantation.

**Commissural alignment** serves as a critical mitigation strategy against these risks. When deploying a new valve inside an existing one, aligning the commissures (the points where leaflets attach) of the new prosthesis with those of the underlying frame creates larger gaps between stent struts at these specific locations. These "commissural windows" act as natural channels that facilitate catheter engagement and wire crossing for future coronary access.

In this specific case, careful preprocedural CT planning allowed us to align the Acurate neo2's commissures with those of the prior Evolut valve. Furthermore, we employed a **high-implantation strategy**, positioning the third valve at the level of the second valve's leaflets. This prevented inward leaflet folding and minimized the formation of a "neo-skirt" that could obscure coronary ostia. While immediate post-procedure hemodynamics initially suggested severe PPM (mean gradient 67 mmHg), aggressive post-dilation and favorable remodeling led to significant gradient reduction over time. However, this case reinforces that in triple-stented anatomy, procedural success relies not just on sealing leaks but on strategic alignment to ensure that life-saving coronary interventions remain feasible should they be needed decades later.
