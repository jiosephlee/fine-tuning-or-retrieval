Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] How do the physiological fluctuations of serum CA-125 during pregnancy complicate the differentiation between benign uterine pathology (such as leiomyomas or endometriosis) and malignant ovarian neoplasms?

**Clinical Pearl:** The utility of serum CA-125 in differentiating benign uterine pathology from malignant ovarian neoplasms during pregnancy is severely compromised by physiological fluctuations and the inherent cross-reactivity of the marker with benign conditions. As illustrated in this case of a patient in her fourth decade presenting with massive abdominal masses in the second trimester, relying on a single CA-125 measurement above the standard cutoff (≥35 U/mL) can lead to diagnostic confusion and potentially unnecessary anxiety regarding malignancy.

Physiologically, CA-125 levels exhibit a biphasic pattern during gestation, typically peaking in the first trimester (median ~23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (~21 U/mL), with a nadir occurring in the second trimester (median ~14 U/mL). While our patient's level of 41.8 U/mL exceeded both the standard reference range and the expected median for her gestational age, this elevation did not confirm ovarian carcinoma. Instead, it reflected the complex interplay of benign etiologies known to elevate CA-125, including endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammation, and notably, uterine leiomyomas undergoing degeneration or torsion.

In this specific case, imaging suggested two distinct entities: an upper mass mimicking a decidualized endometrioma and a lower mass appearing continuous with a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma. The "slight" elevation of CA-125 initially favored an endometriotic origin for the upper lesion; however, surgical exploration revealed that *both* masses were actually giant, pedunculated leiomyomas—one of which was twisted and ischemic. This underscores a critical pathophysiological principle: serous mesothelial irritation caused by hemorrhage, necrosis, or inflammation from degenerating fibroids can stimulate peritoneal production of CA-125 independent of malignancy. Consequently, an isolated elevated value cannot reliably distinguish between a benign degenerating myoma and an epithelial ovarian tumor.

Therefore, clinical reasoning must prioritize morphological characterization via high-resolution ultrasound and non-contrast MRI over biomarker interpretation alone. As demonstrated here, despite the elevated CA-125 suggesting potential malignancy or endometriosis, the definitive diagnosis relied on imaging features—specifically the continuity of the caudal mass with uterine tissue—and ultimately histopathology confirming benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis. In pregnant patients with suspected adnexal or uterine masses, serial monitoring of trends may offer some insight into progression versus stability, but decisions regarding surgical intervention should be driven by symptomatology (pain, obstruction), risk of acute complications (torsion/rupture), and anatomical constraints rather than biomarker thresholds alone.

### [Clinical Pearls] Given the safety profile of imaging modalities in the second trimester, why is non-contrast MRI preferred over contrast-enhanced MRI or CT when characterizing complex abdominal masses in pregnancy, and what specific anatomical details does it provide that ultrasound may miss?

In the second trimester of pregnancy, non-contrast MRI is the preferred advanced imaging modality for characterizing complex abdominal masses because it avoids the teratogenic and fetotoxic risks associated with ionizing radiation (CT) and gadolinium-based contrast agents. While gadolinium crosses the placenta freely and has been linked to an increased risk of stillbirth, neonatal death, and rheumatologic or dermatologic conditions in offspring, non-contrast MRI utilizing T1- and T2-weighted sequences provides high-resolution anatomical data without compromising fetal safety. As demonstrated in the case of this 40-year-old woman with acute abdominal pain and dyspnea, non-contrast MRI was pivotal when ultrasound proved inconclusive regarding the origin and extent of two massive lesions.

Specifically, while ultrasound is excellent for initial screening, it can be limited by acoustic shadowing from bowel gas, obesity (BMI 33 in this case), and the sheer "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus and adjacent masses. In our patient's case, ultrasound suggested decidualized endometriomas but failed to definitively clarify the relationship between the caudal mass and the uterine wall. Non-contrast MRI provided superior soft-tissue contrast resolution, revealing that the caudal mass was continuous with a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma exhibiting cystic degeneration—a detail obscured on ultrasound by overlapping structures. Furthermore, MRI accurately characterized the internal architecture of the larger upper mass as having inhomogeneous fluid content with solid components and thickened walls, allowing us to distinguish between potential etiologies like torsion versus simple cysts despite the inability to use Doppler flow enhancement as effectively as in non-pregnant states. Crucially, MRI confirmed there was no lymphadenopathy or distant spread, supporting a benign diagnosis despite a mildly elevated CA-125 level. By delineating that the upper mass was a pedunculated leiomyoma prone to torsion (which explained her fever and pain) rather than an ovarian malignancy, MRI guided our decision for targeted laparotomy—excising only the symptomatic upper mass while leaving the pelvic mass intact to minimize obstetric risk—ultimately ensuring an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

### [Management] In a pregnant patient presenting with acute abdominal pain, fever, and a large pedunculated mass, what are the critical indications that warrant surgical intervention rather than continued expectant management with analgesics and antibiotics?

In the management of a pregnant patient with acute abdominal pain, fever, and a large mass, the decision to proceed with surgical intervention rather than continued expectant management hinges on the presence of **acute surgical complications** or **refractory symptoms threatening maternal or fetal stability**. As illustrated in the case of the 40-year-old woman with a giant pedunculated leiomyoma, conservative therapy with analgesics and antibiotics is appropriate only when symptoms are manageable and the mass is stable. However, surgery becomes mandatory when there is evidence of **torsion**, **ischemic necrosis**, **intractable pain**, or **mechanical compromise** that fails to resolve with medical optimization.

In this specific case, the critical indication for laparotomy was the development of a "twisted and leaking" pedunculated mass causing systemic inflammation (low-grade fever) and localized ischemia (necrotic changes confirmed on pathology), alongside severe mechanical restriction manifesting as dyspnea and uncontrolled pain despite paracetamol. While fever alone might suggest infection treatable with antibiotics, the constellation of acute pain unresponsive to conservative measures combined with imaging suggesting complex vascular pathology necessitated immediate exploration to prevent catastrophic outcomes such as spontaneous rupture, sepsis from necrotic tissue, or preterm labor secondary to uterine irritation. Furthermore, the potential for rapid tumor growth during pregnancy exacerbates these risks; therefore, once a diagnosis of a complicated, symptomatic pedunculated fibroid is established—particularly where torsion is suspected—the risk-benefit analysis shifts decisively toward surgical excision to preserve both maternal health and the ongoing pregnancy. Expectant management is contraindicated when the mass itself acts as an active source of physiological decompensation rather than a static anatomical finding.

### [Management] Beyond ruling out malignancy, what specific intraoperative measures regarding fetal monitoring and pharmacologic prophylaxis are recommended to minimize the risk of preterm labor during a myomectomy performed in the second trimester?

In the context of a second-trimester myomectomy, where the primary goal is to remove symptomatic pathology while preserving the pregnancy, two critical intraoperative measures emerge from this case to mitigate the risk of preterm labor: continuous fetal surveillance and aggressive pharmacologic suppression of uterine contractility.

Regarding **fetal monitoring**, the standard of care mandates serial documentation of fetal heart rate before, during, and immediately following the surgical intervention. As highlighted in our case and supported by large multicentric data (Zhao et al.), verifying fetal cardiac activity prior to incision establishes a baseline, while intermittent monitoring—specifically via transvaginal ultrasound in this instance—ensures ongoing fetal viability throughout the dissection. Given that uterine manipulation carries an inherent risk of inducing contractions or compromising placental perfusion, real-time assessment allows for immediate recognition of fetal distress or bradycardia, prompting potential modification of surgical technique or rapid closure if necessary.

Pharmacologically, **prophylactic progesterone** is essential to stabilize the myometrium. In this patient, we initiated intramuscular progesterone (100 mg) intraoperatively immediately upon exposure, continuing daily dosing until discharge. This was subsequently transitioned to depot caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg every 3 days) to maintain therapeutic levels through the remainder of the gestation. The rationale is twofold: first, surgical trauma and anesthesia can precipitate prostaglandin release and uterine irritability; second, progesterone acts directly on the myometrium to inhibit gap junction formation and reduce contractile frequency. While broad-spectrum antibiotics (cefazolin) were administered here specifically due to concurrent *Klebsiella* bacteriuria identified preoperatively, they serve primarily as infection control rather than direct antenatal prophylaxis against labor; thus, the cornerstone of preventing iatrogenic preterm birth in myomectomy remains high-dose progestin therapy combined with meticulous hemostasis and minimal uterine handling.

### [Clinical Reasoning] Why might a clinician choose to leave a large, fixed pelvic mass (such as a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma) in situ during a pregnancy complicated by another symptomatic mass, despite the potential for future obstruction?

In managing a pregnant patient with multiple large pelvic masses, the decision to leave a fixed, asymptomatic posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma *in situ* during a second-trimester intervention is driven by the imperative to minimize obstetric risk while addressing immediate life-threatening pathology. As illustrated in this case, the primary indication for surgery was the upper, pedunculated mass causing acute torsion, ischemic necrosis, and severe compressive symptoms (pain and dyspnea). Removing this specific lesion resolved the acute crisis.

Conversely, attempting to resect the caudal, fixed pelvic mass concurrently would have introduced unnecessary danger. This mass was firmly adherent to the posterior uterine wall ("torus"), making dissection technically hazardous with a high probability of precipitating catastrophic hemorrhage or iatrogenic uterine injury. Such trauma significantly elevates the risks of preterm labor, fetal loss, and uterine rupture—outcomes far outweighing the theoretical benefit of removing an asymptomatic fibroid during pregnancy. The case report highlights that uterine leiomyomas generally regress postpartum; indeed, our patient's residual mass decreased in size after delivery.

Furthermore, leaving the mass *in situ* directly influenced the plan for delivery rather than necessitating immediate removal. Because the 9 cm fixed mass occupied the pouch of Douglas and obstructed the birth canal, it rendered vaginal delivery impossible. Consequently, the surgical strategy shifted from "curative myomectomy" to "obstetric preservation," planning for an elective cesarean section at term to bypass the obstruction safely. This approach aligns with current principles: surgical intervention in pregnancy should be reserved for complications like torsion or suspected malignancy, prioritizing fetal safety and deferring non-essential myomectomies until after delivery when hormonal regression may reduce tumor burden and surgical difficulty.

### [Pathophysiology] Considering the natural history of uterine leiomyomas during gestation versus the puerperium, how does tumor size typically change across trimesters, and how does this influence the timing of definitive surgical management for asymptomatic residual masses post-delivery?

Based on the case report and established literature regarding uterine leiomyomas in pregnancy, the natural history of these tumors follows a distinct, non-linear trajectory driven by hormonal exposure and hemodynamic shifts. During gestation, particularly in the first trimester, leiomyomas exhibit rapid expansion due to high circulating estrogen and progesterone levels; studies cited in the discussion indicate an average weekly growth rate of approximately 6.12% between the first and second trimesters. While growth continues into the third trimester, the rate slows significantly to roughly 3.10%. Conversely, the puerperium marks a period of substantial involution. As seen in our patient's residual pelvic mass, which regressed from 9 cm to 7.5 cm post-partum, myomas typically decrease in size by an average of 44% within six weeks after delivery due to the abrupt withdrawal of steroid hormones and resolution of uterine edema.

This predictable pattern directly informs the timing of definitive surgical management for asymptomatic residual masses. Because a significant proportion of fibroids will spontaneously regress following parturition, immediate postpartum intervention is generally contraindicated for asymptomatic patients. Aggressive surgery during the puerperium exposes the patient to unnecessary risks—such as hemorrhage from a highly vascularized involuting uterus and potential injury to adjacent structures—without guaranteed benefit if the mass shrinks naturally. Therefore, as illustrated by this patient's successful delay in further resection despite having a "huge obstacle" mass initially noted during pregnancy, the standard of care is expectant management with serial monitoring for at least several months post-delivery. Definitive surgical intervention should be reserved only for masses that remain symptomatic or fail to regress sufficiently after this period of physiological involution, thereby avoiding overtreatment and optimizing maternal safety.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When differentiating between decidualized endometriomas and cystic-degenerating leiomyomas on imaging in pregnancy, what specific sonographic or MRI features (e.g., vascularity, relationship to uterine wall) help distinguish these entities?

In distinguishing between decidualized endometriomas and cystic-degenerating leiomyomas during pregnancy, the most discriminating feature is the **anatomical continuity with the uterine wall** and the pattern of **vascularity**, rather than internal signal characteristics alone. As illustrated in our case of a patient presenting with two large abdominal masses in the second trimester, initial ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI can be misleading; both lesions appeared as unilocular cystic masses with inhomogeneous fluid content, leading to a preliminary diagnosis of decidualized endometriomas.

However, the critical diagnostic pivot lies in evaluating the relationship to the myometrium. While decidualized endometriomas typically arise from the ovary and may show peripheral vascularity without a connection to the uterus, cystic-degenerating leiomyomas maintain a distinct structural tether to the uterine corpus. In this specific case, although MRI failed to clearly delineate a "cleavage plane" for either mass due to steric bulk, it did demonstrate that the caudal mass was in **continuity with a known posterior intramural-subserosal myoma**. Conversely, the lack of an identifiable feeding vessel or stalk for the upper mass initially favored an ovarian origin based on imaging alone. Yet, intraoperative findings corrected this impression: both masses were ultimately confirmed as pedunculated uterine leiomyomas undergoing degeneration and torsion.

Therefore, when imaging suggests ambiguity between these entities, one must scrutinize for **myometrial continuity** even if obscured by pregnancy-induced distortion. A mass that appears adherent or continuous with a pre-existing fibroid strongly favors a degenerating leiomyoma over an endometrioma. Furthermore, while both entities may exhibit increased vascularity due to hormonal stimulation or inflammation (torsion/necrosis), the presence of a **pedicle arising directly from the uterine serosa**—as seen surgically here—is pathognomonic for a pedunculated myoma. Relying solely on tumor markers like CA-125 is insufficient, as they can be mildly elevated in both benign endometriosis and degenerating fibroids during pregnancy; thus, morphological assessment of attachment points remains the gold standard for differentiation prior to surgical intervention.

### [Management] What factors dictate the choice between vaginal delivery and cesarean section in a patient with known uterine fibroids discovered incidentally or managed surgically during pregnancy?

The choice between vaginal delivery and cesarean section in a patient with uterine fibroids during pregnancy is dictated primarily by the **size, number, and anatomical location** of the masses relative to the fetal pelvis and the integrity of prior uterine incisions. While the mere presence of leiomyomas does not mandate a cesarean delivery, specific spatial constraints can create an absolute indication for surgical birth.

As illustrated in the case of Rosalba De Nola et al., a patient presenting with large, complex abdominal masses in the second trimester required careful multidisciplinary planning. Although one large pedunculated mass causing acute symptoms (torsion and ischemia) was resected via laparotomy to prevent maternal compromise, a second, fixed caudal mass—a cystic degenerating intramural-subserosal leiomyoma—was intentionally left *in situ* to minimize obstetric risk. The decision regarding mode of delivery hinged on the residual anatomy: this caudal mass was located at the posterior aspect of the uterine isthmus. As noted in the case report, such a mass represents a "huge obstacle" that would physically block fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labor. Consequently, despite the desire to avoid unnecessary surgery, an elective cesarean section became mandatory at 37 weeks to prevent obstructed labor and potential uterine rupture at the site of previous myomectomy scars.

Therefore, when evaluating these patients, clinicians must assess whether fibroids occupy the lower uterine segment or distort the pelvic inlet. If a myoma is positioned such that it obstructs descent or compromises the safety of a trial of labor—particularly in conjunction with a prior hysterotomy—cesarean delivery is indicated. Conversely, if fibroids are small or located away from the birth canal (e.g., high fundal), vaginal delivery may be safely attempted. The guiding principle remains balancing maternal safety against fetal outcomes by preventing mechanical obstruction while minimizing surgical trauma where possible.
