Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Physiological vs. Pathological: Interpreting Rising CA-125 Levels in Pregnant Patients with Adnexal Masses

We've all seen the labs come back where everything looks fine until you hit that one tumor marker sitting just above the reference range. In a non-pregnant patient, an elevated CA-125 sends us down a rabbit hole of CT scans, PET studies, and referrals to gynecologic oncology. But throw a pregnancy into the mix, and suddenly your "normal" cutoff becomes a moving target. What happens when a pregnant patient presents with massive abdominal masses, severe dyspnea, and a CA-125 that's creeping upward? Do you operate immediately fearing ovarian carcinoma, or do you hold tight knowing that pregnancy itself plays havoc with serology?

This question isn't theoretical; it's exactly the dilemma faced by a 40-year-old nulliparous woman in her second trimester described in a recent case report by De Nola et al. She arrived at the ER with acute left upper quadrant pain, pelvic discomfort, and significant dyspnea. On exam, she looked like she was carrying twins (or triplets) despite being only 21 weeks along. Imaging confirmed two colossal masses: an 18 cm lesion filling the left subphrenic space and a 9 cm mass anchoring the pouch of Douglas. To make matters more confusing, her CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL—elevated—and trending upward from 36 U/mL two weeks prior.

Here lies the crux of the issue: Is this ovarian cancer masquerading as pregnancy complications, or is it something else entirely? If you rely solely on that static lab value against standard adult references (<35 U/mL), you might lean heavily toward malignancy. However, physiology tells a different story. We know that CA-125 naturally spikes in the first trimester due to mesothelial stimulation from chorionic villi and again in the third trimester as parturition approaches. By the second trimester, levels typically dip to their lowest point (often around 14 U/mL). So, seeing anything above 35 U/mL in this window *is* abnormal relative to baseline pregnancy norms. Yet, as this case brilliantly illustrates, "abnormal" doesn't automatically equal "cancer."

The multidisciplinary team here didn't panic; they synthesized data. They noted that while CA-125 was high for gestational age, other markers (CEA, CA 19-9, CA 15-3) were pristine. More importantly, they recognized that benign conditions like leiomyomas and endometriosis can elevate CA-125 during pregnancy due to local inflammation or decidualization. The real clincher wasn't just the lab work; it was the clinical picture of mechanical obstruction causing respiratory compromise and pain that analgesics couldn't fix alone. When conservative management failed and the risk of torsion or rupture loomed large due to the sheer size of these tumors ("steric bulk"), surgery became unavoidable—not because they feared metastasis necessarily proved beyond doubt by labs alone, but because the anatomy threatened both mother and fetus regardless of histology.

What made this case so instructive was what happened inside the OR rather than outside it on paper charts. Upon laparotomy via xiphopubic incision (chosen over laparoscopy likely due to mass size and gestational age constraints), they found no ovarian malignancy whatsoever. Instead, they discovered a giant pedunculated uterine leiomyoma! It had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis—a perfect storm explaining her fever and rising CRP—and another cystic-degenerating fibroid below it stuck fast to the uterine wall. The "ovarian masses" visualized on ultrasound were actually uterine in origin all along—a classic pitfall when dealing with decidualized tissues that mimic endometriomas or cysts on imaging.
