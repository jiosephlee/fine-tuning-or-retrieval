Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When 'Endometriomas' Aren't: Deciphering Atypical Myomas in Pregnancy via Non-Contrast MRI

We've all seen the classic teaching: "If it looks like an endometrioma and bleeds like an endometrioma, treat it like an endometrioma." But what happens when that rule breaks down in the most high-stakes environment imaginable—a pregnant patient in her fourth decade?

Consider a 40-year-old G1P0A1 woman, BMI 33, presenting at 21 weeks gestation with severe left upper quadrant pain, pelvic discomfort, and dyspnea. Ultrasound paints a terrifying picture: two massive lesions. One is an 18 cm beast filling the left subphrenic space; the other is a 9.5 cm mass deep in the Pouch of Douglas. Both appear cystic with irregular internal echoes. The radiology report screams *decidualized endometriomas*. The tumor marker panel throws us another curveball: CA-125 is elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35). In a non-pregnant woman, this might trigger a panic for malignancy or advanced endometriosis requiring aggressive intervention.

But here's where the plot twists: **They weren't endometriomas.** They were giant, atypical uterine leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration and torsion. And getting to that diagnosis didn't require risky contrast agents or blind exploration—it required understanding the limitations of biomarkers in pregnancy and leveraging non-contrast MRI as our ultimate tie-breaker.

### The Diagnostic Trap: Why CA-125 Lies During Pregnancy

One of the biggest pitfalls in managing abdominal masses during pregnancy is over-reliance on serum tumor markers. As we know from basic pathophysiology, CA-125 isn't specific to ovarian cancer; it's a marker of mesothelial irritation. It spikes naturally during menstruation, inflammation, and—crucially—during pregnancy itself due to increased permeability of the serosa and direct contact between amniotic fluid and maternal tissues.

While CA-125 typically dips in the second trimester (median ~14 U/mL), this patient sat stubbornly above 40 U/mL with an upward trend. In isolation, this feels ominous. However, literature confirms that benign conditions like fibroids, PID, and even simple uterine distension can elevate these levels. Relying solely on this number pushed the differential heavily toward malignant ovarian pathology or complex endometriosis, potentially leading to unnecessary radical surgery or delay due to fear of teratogenicity from further workup.

This case reminds us that **context is king**. An isolated elevated CA-125 in a symptomatic pregnant patient should never dictate surgical strategy without corroborating structural evidence of malignancy (like ascites, nodularity on CT/MRI with contrast contraindications notwithstanding).

### The Imaging Pivot: Non-Contrast MRI as the Game Changer

When ultrasound hits its ceiling—which it inevitably does when dealing with "steric bulk" from a gravid uterus obscuring anatomy—we need a higher-resolution map. Enter MRI without gadolinium.

Gadolinium-based contrast agents cross the placenta freely and carry theoretical risks for renal toxicity and stillbirth; thus, they are generally avoided unless absolutely life-saving information cannot be obtained otherwise. Fortunately, modern non-contrast sequences are powerful enough to solve this puzzle. In this case, T2-weighted fat-saturated images provided clarity that ultrasound missed:
*   **Vascular Architecture:** While US showed peripheral flow, MRI highlighted thickened walls (1.4 cm) and focal solid components within the cystic spaces that looked suspicious for degeneration rather than simple hemorrhage.
*   **Anatomical Continuity:** Crucially, MRI failed to identify a distinct "stalk" separating the masses from the uterus for either lesion initially suspected as adnexal. Instead, it revealed continuity between the caudal mass and a known posterior intramural/subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration (~6 cm).
*   **Exclusion of Malignancy:** No lymphadenopathy was visualized, pushing away from metastatic disease despite the worrying lab values.

The MRI essentially told us: *"These aren't floating ovaries; these are part of your uterus acting weird."* This distinction shifted our plan from potential oophorectomy/hysterectomy considerations to targeted myomectomy strategies aimed purely at symptom relief while preserving fetal viability.

### Surgical Strategy: To Cut or Not to Cut?

The decision to operate at 21 weeks was driven by physiology failing under mechanical load: severe dyspnea from diaphragmatic splinting and escalating pain suggesting impending torsion or necrosis (supported by her low-grade fever). Conservative management wasn't working; she was deteriorating into anemia (Hb dropped to 7.5 g/L post-op) and restricting lung function needed for fetal growth monitoring later on.

The surgical approach highlights a nuanced principle in obstetric surgery: **Aggressive conservation.**
Upon entering via midline laparotomy (chosen over laparoscopy due to mass size >8cm and gestational age constraints), we found exactly what we feared but hoped wouldn't happen yet: the upper mass was indeed a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis—the source of her fever and pain! It was excised cleanly with double clamping of the stalk to minimize blood loss—a critical maneuver since myomas become incredibly vascular during pregnancy due to hormonal stimulation.

However, we made a deliberate choice *not* to remove the second pelvic mass sitting right against the cervix/posterior wall despite its size (~9 cm). ### Take-Home Pearls for Your Next Shift

This case underscores several critical lessons for emergency physicians and OB/GYNs navigating complex abdominal presentations in pregnancy:

*   **Beware Biomarker Bias:** Do not anchor on elevated CA-125 alone during pregnancy; levels fluctuate physiologically across all trimesters and can be raised by benign causes like fibroids or inflammation without implying malignancy. Serial trends matter more than absolute numbers in isolation.
