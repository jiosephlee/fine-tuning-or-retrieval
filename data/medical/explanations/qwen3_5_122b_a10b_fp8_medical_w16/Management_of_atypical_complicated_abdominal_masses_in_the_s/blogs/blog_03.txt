Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Beyond Expectant Management: Navigating Acute Complications of Large Uterine Fibroids During Gestation

We love "watchful waiting" in OB/GYN. It's the default setting for almost everything, especially when a fetus is involved. But sometimes, the biology of a massive tumor overrides the mantra of conservation. Today, we're dissecting a fascinating case from *De Nola et al.* where a pregnant woman didn't just have "fibroids"; she had a life-threatening mechanical crisis that forced a hand into the second trimester.

This isn't your standard red-degenerating fibroid story. We're talking about a 40-year-old nulliparous woman at 21 weeks gestation presenting with severe LUQ and pelvic pain, coupled with dyspnea. Imaging revealed two monsters: an 18 cm mass filling the left subphrenic space and a 9 cm mass deep in the Pouch of Douglas. The clinical picture was dire enough to mimic malignancy or catastrophic obstetric events, yet the final pathology turned out to be something we see frequently but rarely manage so aggressively mid-gestation: **twisted, ischemic pedunculated leiomyomas.**

### The Diagnostic Trap: When Benign Looks Malignant

The real teaching moment here starts with how easily these benign entities masquerade as high-stakes emergencies. On ultrasound, these masses looked like giant decidualized endometriomas or complex ovarian neoplasms. The clinician's brain immediately jumps to "cancer" when you see an 18 cm cystic-solid mass pushing against the diaphragm, especially when CA-125 is elevated (in this case, 41.8 U/mL).

Here is the pearl: **CA-125 is notoriously unreliable in pregnancy.** While it peaks in the first and third trimesters normally, it can creep up in the second trimester due to anything from endometriosis to simple inflammation or even large fibroids themselves. Relying solely on this marker would have led us down a rabbit hole of unnecessary anxiety or inappropriate workups.

The team wisely pivoted to non-contrast MRI once ultrasound hit its limits due to acoustic shadowing from the gravid uterus and massive tumors. The MRI confirmed the anatomy but couldn't definitively rule out malignancy versus benign degeneration because of the "steric bulk." The lack of contrast was intentional—gadolinium crosses the placenta and carries theoretical risks—but it left some ambiguity regarding vascularity and exact tissue characterization that only surgery could resolve.

### The Red Flags That Mandated Surgery

So why operate? Why not give Tylenol, bed rest, and hope for term? Because this patient crossed several absolute thresholds where expectant management becomes malpractice:

1.  **Mechanical Cardiopulmonary Compromise:** The upper mass wasn't just sitting there; it was compressing the diaphragm, causing true restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) despite normal O2 sat initially. As pregnancy progresses and the uterus grows upward, that space disappears completely. Waiting meant risking maternal respiratory failure.
2.  **Acute Ischemia/Torsion:** During laparotomy, they found exactly what caused the fever and escalating pain: a twisted pedicle leading to ischemic necrosis and leakage of fluid/blood products into the peritoneum. This is akin to ovarian torsion but with a much larger target organ attached to the uterus. Leaving this alone risks sepsis and disseminated necrotizing fasciitis-like spread within the peritoneal cavity.
3.  **Diagnostic Uncertainty:** With masses this large and heterogeneous, you cannot safely assume benignity without histology if they are symptomatic. The risk of missing an occult sarcoma or aggressive germ cell tumor outweighed surgical risk in this context.

### Surgical Nuances: What They Did Right (And Left Alone)

The surgical strategy deserves applause for its restraint amidst chaos. They performed a longitudinal laparotomy (xiphoid-pubis) rather than attempting a risky laparoscopy on an 18 kg-equivalent distended abdomen with distorted anatomy—a pragmatic choice given gestational age and mass size.

They removed *only* what was killing her right now: the twisted upper pedunculated myoma. They meticulously closed the uterine defect with absorbable sutures after clamping off feeding vessels to minimize hemorrhage risk to both mom and baby. Crucially, they left the lower pelvic mass *in situ*. Why? Because digging around in a tight pelvis with an 18-week uterus carrying twin-sized masses increases preterm labor risk exponentially without immediate benefit if that mass isn't acutely compromised.

Post-op care was equally rigorous:
*   **Progesterone Prophylaxis:** Started intra-operatively (IM micronized progesterone) to suppress uterine contractions triggered by manipulation and stress hormones released during surgery/necrosis resolution continued until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery via long-acting caproate hydroxyprogesterone injection every few days until delivery... wait, let me rephrase that clearly for clarity.) -> Continued IM progesterone supplementation daily post-op, transitioning to weekly injections (hydroxyprogesterone caproate) until term to maintain uterine quiescence.*
*   **Antibiotic Stewardship:** Targeted therapy based on urine cultures (*Klebsiella*), acknowledging that urinary stasis from compression likely contributed to their UTI/fever picture alongside tumor necrosis.*
*   **Fetal Monitoring:** Continuous documentation of FHR before, during, and after surgery—a non-negotiable standard.*

The outcome? Excellent maternal recovery (despite needing blood products for anemia exacerbated by chronic bleeding/degeneration), successful continuation of pregnancy to 37 weeks, and elective C-section delivering a healthy neonate without uterine rupture or placental abruption issues linked directly to prior myomectomy scars.*

### Take-Home Pearls for Your Next Shift

When managing pregnant patients with large abdominal/pelvic masses beyond expectant management protocols:* 

**🔴 Recognize Mechanical Compression Early**  
Don't dismiss dyspnea or tachypnea as "just being pregnant." If imaging shows massive retroperitoneal/subphrenic displacement compromising lung mechanics or bowel obstruction signs emerge mid-second trimester → consider urgent decompression/surgery.*  

**🟡 Interpret Tumor Markers With Extreme Caution**  
Elevated CA‑125 >35 U/mL occurs commonly in benign conditions including fibroids/endometriosis/pregnancy itself; never use isolated elevations alone justify radical oncologic interventions without corroborative imaging/histology confirmation.*  

**🟢 Know When To Cut vs Wait**  
Indications for mid-trimester myomectomy include:<br>• Pedunculated torsion/ischemia<br>• Severe refractory pain unresponsive to analgesics<br>• Threatened rupture/sepsis<br>• Irreversible maternal cardiorespiratory compromise<br>*Avoid removing asymptomatic incidental findings unless they obstruct future vaginal delivery pathways!*  

**🔵 Plan Delivery Strategically From Day One**
