Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Art of Partial Myomectomy: Why Leaving Masses Behind Can Save a Pregnancy

We love a good "what would I do?" scenario in the ED, but few situations force us to weigh maternal physiology against fetal survival quite like managing a giant, symptomatic abdominal mass in the second trimester. Imagine your patient: a 40-year-old primigravida at 21 weeks, presenting with severe left upper quadrant and pelvic pain, dyspnea, and a fever. Imaging reveals two colossal masses—one towering into the subphrenic space compressing her lungs, another anchoring deep in the pouch of Douglas. The initial workup screams malignancy or catastrophic hemorrhage. You call OB/GYN, Surgery, and Anesthesia for a huddle. Do you scrub in for a full debulking? Or do you play the long game?

This isn't hypothetical. It's exactly what happened in a recent case report from Italy involving a woman with BMI 33 carrying twins (wait, no, singleton) where the surgical team made a counter-intuitive move that saved the pregnancy: they removed half the problem and left the rest alone.

### The Diagnostic Tightrope

Let's set the stage. Our patient arrived with VAS pain scores of 5/10 at two distinct sites and mild dyspnea. On exam, she had an 18 cm mass filling her LUQ and a fixed 9 cm mass in her pelvis. Ultrasound painted a confusing picture: two large cystic structures with peripheral vascularity. One looked like it could be an ovarian endometrioma; the other appeared adherent to a posterior uterine fibroid undergoing cystic degeneration.

Here is where things get tricky for anyone relying solely on tumor markers. Her CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35). In a non-pregnant woman over 50, that flags ovarian cancer immediately. But remember our physiology lesson: CA-125 rises naturally in pregnancy due to mesothelial irritation from the expanding uterus and decidua formation. While it peaked in the first trimester and dipped in the second (usually around 14 U/mL), this patient's level was stubbornly high. Add to that her anemia (Hb 9.9 g/L), leukocytosis, and low-grade fever driven by a concurrent *Klebsiella* UTI, and you have a perfect storm masking the true pathology.

They wisely skipped contrast-enhanced MRI due to fetal safety concerns regarding gadolinium but utilized non-contrast MRI to map out anatomy without ionizing radiation or nephrogenic systemic fibris risk factors relevant here too much less so than iodinated contrast). The scan confirmed thick-walled cysts with solid components but couldn't definitively rule out malignancy or clarify if the caudal mass was truly separate from the uterus.

### The Surgical Pivot Point

By week 21+6, conservative management failed. Pain persisted despite IV paracetamol, dyspnea worsened due to mechanical compression of the diaphragm by an 18-cm mass, and there was genuine fear of torsion or rupture as the uterus grew larger every day waiting for term delivery wasn't an option anymore—the risks outweighed them now! 

So off went she to OR... What did they find upon entering via midline vertical incision? Surprise! That terrifying "ovarian" mass up top? It turned out to be **a massively twisted pedunculated leiomyoma** arising directly from the uterine fundus! It was ischemic-necrotic near its stalk—a ticking time bomb explaining her fever and pain perfectly down below lay another beast attached firmly behind-the-wall-myoma seemingly inseparable from vital structures without risking catastrophe! 

Now comes *the* decision moment most textbooks gloss over: **Do you try to remove everything?**

Removing both masses would likely require extensive dissection near major vessels potentially damaging uteroplacental circulation causing massive hemorrhage necessitating hysterectomy—or worse triggering preterm labor leading NICU admission premature infant death possibly even maternal mortality depending how far gone things got before stabilization occurred anyway!! Instead their plan evolved quickly based on real-time findings: **"Take out what hurts right now; leave what might wait."** They excised only the pedunculated upper myoma which posed immediate threat via torsion/necrosis/sepsis risk while deliberately leaving intact that deeply embedded pelvic lesion hoping hormones post-partum shrink it naturally later once baby born safely delivered either vaginally cesarean-section whichever safest route determined closer term approached nearer thirty-seven weeks gestation mark hit finally allowing elective repeat Cs-section instead rushing emergently because obstructed labor forced hand prematurely!!!
