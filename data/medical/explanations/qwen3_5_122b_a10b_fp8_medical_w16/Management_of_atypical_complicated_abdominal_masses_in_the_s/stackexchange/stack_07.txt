Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When differentiating between decidualized endometriomas and cystic-degenerating leiomyomas on imaging in pregnancy, what specific sonographic or MRI features (e.g., vascularity, relationship to uterine wall) help distinguish these entities?

In distinguishing between decidualized endometriomas and cystic-degenerating leiomyomas during pregnancy, the most discriminating feature is the **anatomical continuity with the uterine wall** and the pattern of **vascularity**, rather than internal signal characteristics alone. As illustrated in our case of a patient presenting with two large abdominal masses in the second trimester, initial ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI can be misleading; both lesions appeared as unilocular cystic masses with inhomogeneous fluid content, leading to a preliminary diagnosis of decidualized endometriomas.

However, the critical diagnostic pivot lies in evaluating the relationship to the myometrium. While decidualized endometriomas typically arise from the ovary and may show peripheral vascularity without a connection to the uterus, cystic-degenerating leiomyomas maintain a distinct structural tether to the uterine corpus. In this specific case, although MRI failed to clearly delineate a "cleavage plane" for either mass due to steric bulk, it did demonstrate that the caudal mass was in **continuity with a known posterior intramural-subserosal myoma**. Conversely, the lack of an identifiable feeding vessel or stalk for the upper mass initially favored an ovarian origin based on imaging alone. Yet, intraoperative findings corrected this impression: both masses were ultimately confirmed as pedunculated uterine leiomyomas undergoing degeneration and torsion.

Therefore, when imaging suggests ambiguity between these entities, one must scrutinize for **myometrial continuity** even if obscured by pregnancy-induced distortion. A mass that appears adherent or continuous with a pre-existing fibroid strongly favors a degenerating leiomyoma over an endometrioma. Furthermore, while both entities may exhibit increased vascularity due to hormonal stimulation or inflammation (torsion/necrosis), the presence of a **pedicle arising directly from the uterine serosa**—as seen surgically here—is pathognomonic for a pedunculated myoma. Relying solely on tumor markers like CA-125 is insufficient, as they can be mildly elevated in both benign endometriosis and degenerating fibroids during pregnancy; thus, morphological assessment of attachment points remains the gold standard for differentiation prior to surgical intervention.
