Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] What factors dictate the choice between vaginal delivery and cesarean section in a patient with known uterine fibroids discovered incidentally or managed surgically during pregnancy?

The choice between vaginal delivery and cesarean section in a patient with uterine fibroids during pregnancy is dictated primarily by the **size, number, and anatomical location** of the masses relative to the fetal pelvis and the integrity of prior uterine incisions. While the mere presence of leiomyomas does not mandate a cesarean delivery, specific spatial constraints can create an absolute indication for surgical birth.

As illustrated in the case of Rosalba De Nola et al., a patient presenting with large, complex abdominal masses in the second trimester required careful multidisciplinary planning. Although one large pedunculated mass causing acute symptoms (torsion and ischemia) was resected via laparotomy to prevent maternal compromise, a second, fixed caudal mass—a cystic degenerating intramural-subserosal leiomyoma—was intentionally left *in situ* to minimize obstetric risk. The decision regarding mode of delivery hinged on the residual anatomy: this caudal mass was located at the posterior aspect of the uterine isthmus. As noted in the case report, such a mass represents a "huge obstacle" that would physically block fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labor. Consequently, despite the desire to avoid unnecessary surgery, an elective cesarean section became mandatory at 37 weeks to prevent obstructed labor and potential uterine rupture at the site of previous myomectomy scars.

Therefore, when evaluating these patients, clinicians must assess whether fibroids occupy the lower uterine segment or distort the pelvic inlet. If a myoma is positioned such that it obstructs descent or compromises the safety of a trial of labor—particularly in conjunction with a prior hysterotomy—cesarean delivery is indicated. Conversely, if fibroids are small or located away from the birth canal (e.g., high fundal), vaginal delivery may be safely attempted. The guiding principle remains balancing maternal safety against fetal outcomes by preventing mechanical obstruction while minimizing surgical trauma where possible.
