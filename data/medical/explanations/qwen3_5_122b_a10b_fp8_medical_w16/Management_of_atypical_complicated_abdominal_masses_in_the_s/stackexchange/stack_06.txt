Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] Considering the natural history of uterine leiomyomas during gestation versus the puerperium, how does tumor size typically change across trimesters, and how does this influence the timing of definitive surgical management for asymptomatic residual masses post-delivery?

Based on the case report and established literature regarding uterine leiomyomas in pregnancy, the natural history of these tumors follows a distinct, non-linear trajectory driven by hormonal exposure and hemodynamic shifts. During gestation, particularly in the first trimester, leiomyomas exhibit rapid expansion due to high circulating estrogen and progesterone levels; studies cited in the discussion indicate an average weekly growth rate of approximately 6.12% between the first and second trimesters. While growth continues into the third trimester, the rate slows significantly to roughly 3.10%. Conversely, the puerperium marks a period of substantial involution. As seen in our patient's residual pelvic mass, which regressed from 9 cm to 7.5 cm post-partum, myomas typically decrease in size by an average of 44% within six weeks after delivery due to the abrupt withdrawal of steroid hormones and resolution of uterine edema.

This predictable pattern directly informs the timing of definitive surgical management for asymptomatic residual masses. Because a significant proportion of fibroids will spontaneously regress following parturition, immediate postpartum intervention is generally contraindicated for asymptomatic patients. Aggressive surgery during the puerperium exposes the patient to unnecessary risks—such as hemorrhage from a highly vascularized involuting uterus and potential injury to adjacent structures—without guaranteed benefit if the mass shrinks naturally. Therefore, as illustrated by this patient's successful delay in further resection despite having a "huge obstacle" mass initially noted during pregnancy, the standard of care is expectant management with serial monitoring for at least several months post-delivery. Definitive surgical intervention should be reserved only for masses that remain symptomatic or fail to regress sufficiently after this period of physiological involution, thereby avoiding overtreatment and optimizing maternal safety.
