Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Surgical Decision-Making and Operative Management

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention during the second trimester of pregnancy represents a complex equilibrium between maternal symptomatology and fetal safety. In this case, the threshold for surgery was crossed not merely by the presence of large masses, but by the convergence of refractory clinical symptoms and specific mechanical risks. While conservative management is generally preferred for asymptomatic uterine fibroids due to the risks of hemorrhage, preterm labor, and spontaneous abortion associated with operative manipulation, this patient presented with severe abdominal pain (VAS score 5), dyspnea secondary to diaphragmatic compression, and a low-grade fever suggestive of tissue compromise. These factors indicated a high probability of acute complications such as torsion, rupture, or infection, which posed immediate threats to both maternal hemodynamic stability and fetal well-being. Furthermore, the anticipated continued growth of the gravid uterus combined with existing massive lesions threatened to exacerbate restrictive lung disease and precipitate necrotic events within the tumors themselves. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team concluded that the risks of expectant management outweighed the surgical hazards, necessitating an intervention aimed at resolving life-threatening complications while preserving the pregnancy.

Pre-operative planning relied heavily on a rigorous consensus among obstetricians, gynecologic surgeons, and anesthesiologists to define a strategy that prioritized fetal preservation alongside maternal resolution of symptoms. A critical component of this planning involved selecting the appropriate surgical approach. Although laparoscopic myomectomy is increasingly favored in early pregnancy for smaller, accessible masses due to reduced postoperative pain and shorter recovery times, it was deemed inappropriate in this scenario. The sheer size of the upper mass (18 cm), its complex vascularity, and its location extending from the subphrenic space made laparoscopic visualization and safe dissection technically prohibitive without risking excessive pneumoperitoneum pressure or prolonged operative time. Therefore, a longitudinal xiphopubic laparotomy was selected to ensure adequate exposure for rapid control of potential hemorrhage and precise handling of delicate tissues. Throughout this process, strict adherence to fetal monitoring protocols was maintained; continuous documentation of fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery is mandatory to detect early signs of distress attributable to maternal hypotension or uterine irritation.

Upon entering the abdominal cavity via laparotomy following informed consent and confirmation of ongoing fetal cardiac activity, intraoperative findings clarified the nature of the pathology previously obscured by imaging limitations caused by steric bulk. The superior mass was identified as a huge pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone twisting at its stalk (torsion), leading to ischemic necrosis and leakage—findings that directly correlated with the patient's fever and acute pain spectrum. This confirmed the suspicion that mechanical complications were driving the clinical deterioration rather than malignancy or simple mass effect alone. In contrast to standard oncologic resections where en bloc removal might be prioritized, the surgical objective here shifted toward "damage control" for pregnancy maintenance. The surgical team executed a targeted partial myomectomy on only this symptomatic pedunculated mass. By isolating the twisted stalk with clamps prior to transection and meticulously closing the uterine defect with absorbable sutures, they minimized trauma to the myometrium and avoided unnecessary entry into other parts of the uterus where additional masses resided.

Strategic restraint was exercised regarding the caudal pelvic mass; despite its significant size (9 cm) and proximity to previous imaging suggesting attachment to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration, it was deliberately left *in situ*. Excision of this lower lesion would have required extensive dissection near critical pelvic structures and carried a heightened risk of disrupting cervical integrity or inducing labor contractions due to manipulation near the lower uterine segment—a known risk factor for preterm delivery in pregnant patients undergoing myomectomy. Given that this mass did not exhibit signs of torsion or active infection at exploration, leaving it undisturbed aligned with principles of minimizing iatrogenic injury while addressing only those lesions causing immediate harm. To further safeguard against preterm labor triggered by surgical stress or prostaglandin release from tissue manipulation, aggressive pharmacological prophylaxis was instituted immediately intraoperatively. This included parenteral progesterone supplementation initiated during surgery and continued postoperatively via intramuscular injection (hydroxyprogesterone caproate) until delivery at term. Concurrently, broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage tailored to urine cultures (*Klebsiella pneumoniae*) addressed concurrent infectious risks without compromising fetal development.

The successful execution of this limited resection underscores several key principles in managing complicated abdominal masses during pregnancy: surgery should be reserved for clear indications such as torsion or uncontrollable symptoms rather than incidental findings; approaches must be tailored to tumor dimensions where laparotomy may offer superior safety over minimally invasive techniques for giant masses; and interventions should be as localized as possible to reduce uterine irritability. By removing only the necrotic pedunculated leiomyoma while preserving intact adjacent fibroids and maintaining robust hormonal support for pregnancy continuation until 37 weeks gestation via elective cesarean section (necessary due to obstruction by residual pelvic masses), clinicians achieved an optimal outcome characterized by excellent neonatal health and maternal recovery without long-term sequelae requiring immediate postpartum intervention for remaining fibroids.
