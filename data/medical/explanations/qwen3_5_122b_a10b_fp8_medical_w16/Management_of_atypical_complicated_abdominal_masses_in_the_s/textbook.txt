Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment in Pregnancy

The clinical scenario begins with a nulliparous woman in her forties, presenting a confluence of demographic risk factors including advanced maternal age and obesity, indicated by a Body Mass Index (BMI) of 33. These factors inherently elevate the baseline obstetric risk, complicating the physiological adaptations of pregnancy and narrowing the margin for error in diagnosing acute pathology. At 21 weeks of gestation, the patient presented to the emergency department with severe, acute abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and the pelvis, accompanied by mild dyspnea. While abdominal discomfort can be a common feature of normal pregnancy expansion or ligamentous stretching, the severity of this patient's pain—rated 5 on a Visual Analogue Scale—and its association with respiratory compromise necessitated an urgent distinction between benign obstetric phenomena and life-threatening surgical emergencies. The initial assessment focused heavily on ruling out primary cardiopulmonary etiologies; vital signs remained stable with normal oxygen saturation (97%), heart rate (74 bpm), and blood pressure (100/60 mm Hg), and electrocardiography was unremarkable. This stability effectively excluded acute cardiac decompensation or primary pulmonary embolism as the source of her dyspnea, redirecting the diagnostic focus toward extrinsic mechanical compression caused by intra-abdominal pathology.

Physical examination revealed findings that were disproportionately severe relative to the expected uterine size for 21 weeks of gestation. The abdomen was notably enlarged beyond gestational norms, characterized by a tense, elastic swelling occupying the entire left upper quadrant measuring approximately 18 cm. This finding immediately raised concern for a space-occupying lesion rather than simple polyhydramnios or macrosomia. Concurrently, a bimanual vaginal examination identified a distinct, fixed mass in the lesser pelvis measuring roughly 9 cm. This mass was described as rounded, hard, and protruding into the posterior fornix. The identification of a "fixed" pelvic mass is clinically significant because it suggests adherence to surrounding structures or deep infiltration, differentiating it from mobile ovarian cysts or normal fetal parts. The combination of an upper abdominal mass causing diaphragmatic splinting (manifesting as dyspnea) and a fixed pelvic mass created a clinical picture suggestive of large-volume neoplasms or degenerating fibroids exerting significant mass effect on adjacent organs.

Laboratory evaluation during this initial phase provided critical data points for refining the differential diagnosis while accounting for physiological changes unique to pregnancy. The patient exhibited iron deficiency anemia with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/dL and low ferritin, likely exacerbated by increased plasma volume demands in mid-pregnancy superimposed on chronic nutritional deficits or blood loss from tumor vascularity. An inflammatory response was evident through elevated C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL) and neutrophilia (neutrophils 9.27×10³/µL). Interpreting these white blood cell counts requires caution; pregnancy induces a state of physiologic leukocytosis where total white blood cell counts can naturally rise to 15–20×10³/µL without indicating infection. However, in this case, the specific elevation above baseline combined with clinical signs such as low-grade fever prompted an investigation for infectious sources versus sterile inflammation secondary to tissue necrosis or degeneration within a tumor. Urine cultures subsequently confirmed *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, explaining some systemic inflammatory markers but failing to account for the massive abdominal pathology itself. Furthermore, while serum tumor markers like CA-125 were noted to be elevated (41.8 U/mL), clinicians must recognize that CA-125 levels fluctuate physiologically during pregnancy due to serosal irritation from uterine expansion; thus, an isolated elevation is non-diagnostic for malignancy but warrants close monitoring alongside imaging findings to assess trends rather than relying on absolute thresholds used in non-pregnant populations.

## Diagnostic Imaging Strategy and Differential Diagnosis

The diagnostic evaluation of large abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy presents a formidable challenge due to the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus, which obscures standard anatomical landmarks and renders physical examination insufficient for precise characterization. In this case, despite a thorough bimanual examination revealing a fixed, hard mass in the pelvis and palpation of a taut-elastic mass in the upper left quadrant, the sheer volume of the lesions prevented clear delineation of their origin or relationship to surrounding structures. The patient's presentation of severe dyspnea and pain was primarily driven by mechanical compression; the upper mass extended from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus, physically displacing the diaphragm and compromising respiratory mechanics. Consequently, reliance on clinical findings alone was inadequate, necessitating a sophisticated multimodal imaging strategy to guide management while ensuring fetal safety.

Ultrasound with color Doppler serves as the primary screening modality due to its ubiquity and lack of ionizing radiation. In this scenario, ultrasonography successfully identified two distinct unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. The upper lesion measured 18 cm, while the lower lesion appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration. While ultrasound effectively characterized the cystic nature and vascularity of these masses, it struggled to definitively distinguish between an ovarian origin (such as an endometrioma) and a uterine origin (a degenerating leiomyoma), particularly given the inability to visualize a connecting stalk or feeding vessel for the massive upper lesion amidst the distorted anatomy. This limitation highlighted the need for advanced cross-sectional imaging when ultrasound results are inconclusive regarding tissue planes or mass origin.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without contrast emerged as the critical secondary tool in this diagnostic algorithm. Current guidelines advise against gadolinium enhancement during pregnancy due to potential teratogenic risks; therefore, non-contrast MRI is indicated when ultrasound fails to resolve complex anatomy or when malignancy cannot be excluded. In this case, non-contrast MRI provided superior spatial resolution, revealing thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and focal solid components that were less apparent on ultrasound. Crucially, MRI clarified that the caudal mass maintained continuity with a posterior uterine leiomyoma, suggesting a myomatous rather than purely ovarian origin for that specific lesion. This distinction was vital for surgical planning, as it influenced the decision regarding which masses could be safely resected versus those that should remain in situ to avoid catastrophic obstetric complications.

Interpretation of serum tumor markers requires nuanced understanding within the unique physiological milieu of pregnancy. While CA-125 is traditionally utilized as an oncological marker for epithelial ovarian cancer with a cutoff typically set at 35 U/mL in non-pregnant populations, its levels naturally fluctuate during gestation. Physiologic elevations occur in both the first and third trimesters but generally normalize or dip during the second trimester. In this patient, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL with an upward trend over two weeks, raising concern for malignancy or significant inflammation such as endometriosis or adenomyosis. However, other markers including CA 19-9, CA 15-3, CEA, $\beta_2$-microglobulin, and LDH remained within normal limits. The isolated elevation of CA-125 created diagnostic ambiguity; while it favored a diagnosis involving endometrial tissue (endometrioma), it did not rule out benign conditions like degenerating fibroids or decidual reactions associated with pregnancy itself. Thus, tumor markers served as supportive data points rather than definitive diagnostic tools in this context.

The final differential diagnosis hinged on synthesizing imaging features with serological trends to distinguish between decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas—a classic diagnostic dilemma in pregnant patients with large pelvic masses. Decidualized endometriomas typically mimic simple cysts but may show internal echoes due to hemorrhage; conversely., degenerating leiomyomas often present as heterogeneous masses with cystic spaces resulting from ischemia following rapid growth relative to their blood supply. The imaging profile here showed features overlapping both entities: the upper mass resembled an endometrioma based on its cystic appearance and CA-125 elevation, yet its massive size and lack of identifiable ovarian attachment suggested otherwise upon closer inspection during surgery (revealing it as a pedunculated leiomyoma). The caudal mass displayed clear adherence to uterine tissue on MRI, strongly supporting a myomatous origin despite its cystic degeneration characteristics recognized on ultrasound. Ultimately, recognizing that large pedunculated fibroids can undergo torsion leading to necrosis—which explains the patient's fever and rising inflammatory markers—was essential before proceeding to surgical intervention that balanced oncological exclusion with obstetric preservation.

## Surgical Decision-Making and Operative Management

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention during the second trimester of pregnancy represents a complex equilibrium between maternal symptomatology and fetal safety. In this case, the threshold for surgery was crossed not merely by the presence of large masses, but by the convergence of refractory clinical symptoms and specific mechanical risks. While conservative management is generally preferred for asymptomatic uterine fibroids due to the risks of hemorrhage, preterm labor, and spontaneous abortion associated with operative manipulation, this patient presented with severe abdominal pain (VAS score 5), dyspnea secondary to diaphragmatic compression, and a low-grade fever suggestive of tissue compromise. These factors indicated a high probability of acute complications such as torsion, rupture, or infection, which posed immediate threats to both maternal hemodynamic stability and fetal well-being. Furthermore, the anticipated continued growth of the gravid uterus combined with existing massive lesions threatened to exacerbate restrictive lung disease and precipitate necrotic events within the tumors themselves. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team concluded that the risks of expectant management outweighed the surgical hazards, necessitating an intervention aimed at resolving life-threatening complications while preserving the pregnancy.

Pre-operative planning relied heavily on a rigorous consensus among obstetricians, gynecologic surgeons, and anesthesiologists to define a strategy that prioritized fetal preservation alongside maternal resolution of symptoms. A critical component of this planning involved selecting the appropriate surgical approach. Although laparoscopic myomectomy is increasingly favored in early pregnancy for smaller, accessible masses due to reduced postoperative pain and shorter recovery times, it was deemed inappropriate in this scenario. The sheer size of the upper mass (18 cm), its complex vascularity, and its location extending from the subphrenic space made laparoscopic visualization and safe dissection technically prohibitive without risking excessive pneumoperitoneum pressure or prolonged operative time. Therefore, a longitudinal xiphopubic laparotomy was selected to ensure adequate exposure for rapid control of potential hemorrhage and precise handling of delicate tissues. Throughout this process, strict adherence to fetal monitoring protocols was maintained; continuous documentation of fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery is mandatory to detect early signs of distress attributable to maternal hypotension or uterine irritation.

Upon entering the abdominal cavity via laparotomy following informed consent and confirmation of ongoing fetal cardiac activity, intraoperative findings clarified the nature of the pathology previously obscured by imaging limitations caused by steric bulk. The superior mass was identified as a huge pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone twisting at its stalk (torsion), leading to ischemic necrosis and leakage—findings that directly correlated with the patient's fever and acute pain spectrum. This confirmed the suspicion that mechanical complications were driving the clinical deterioration rather than malignancy or simple mass effect alone. In contrast to standard oncologic resections where en bloc removal might be prioritized, the surgical objective here shifted toward "damage control" for pregnancy maintenance. The surgical team executed a targeted partial myomectomy on only this symptomatic pedunculated mass. By isolating the twisted stalk with clamps prior to transection and meticulously closing the uterine defect with absorbable sutures, they minimized trauma to the myometrium and avoided unnecessary entry into other parts of the uterus where additional masses resided.

Strategic restraint was exercised regarding the caudal pelvic mass; despite its significant size (9 cm) and proximity to previous imaging suggesting attachment to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration, it was deliberately left *in situ*. Excision of this lower lesion would have required extensive dissection near critical pelvic structures and carried a heightened risk of disrupting cervical integrity or inducing labor contractions due to manipulation near the lower uterine segment—a known risk factor for preterm delivery in pregnant patients undergoing myomectomy. Given that this mass did not exhibit signs of torsion or active infection at exploration, leaving it undisturbed aligned with principles of minimizing iatrogenic injury while addressing only those lesions causing immediate harm. To further safeguard against preterm labor triggered by surgical stress or prostaglandin release from tissue manipulation, aggressive pharmacological prophylaxis was instituted immediately intraoperatively. This included parenteral progesterone supplementation initiated during surgery and continued postoperatively via intramuscular injection (hydroxyprogesterone caproate) until delivery at term. Concurrently, broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage tailored to urine cultures (*Klebsiella pneumoniae*) addressed concurrent infectious risks without compromising fetal development.

The successful execution of this limited resection underscores several key principles in managing complicated abdominal masses during pregnancy: surgery should be reserved for clear indications such as torsion or uncontrollable symptoms rather than incidental findings; approaches must be tailored to tumor dimensions where laparotomy may offer superior safety over minimally invasive techniques for giant masses; and interventions should be as localized as possible to reduce uterine irritability. By removing only the necrotic pedunculated leiomyoma while preserving intact adjacent fibroids and maintaining robust hormonal support for pregnancy continuation until 37 weeks gestation via elective cesarean section (necessary due to obstruction by residual pelvic masses), clinicians achieved an optimal outcome characterized by excellent neonatal health and maternal recovery without long-term sequelae requiring immediate postpartum intervention for remaining fibroids.

## Postoperative Care, Obstetric Course, and Delivery Planning

The immediate postoperative period following myomectomy in pregnancy requires vigilant management of iatrogenic and disease-related complications, primarily anemia and infection, alongside rigorous hormonal support to maintain uterine quiescence. In this case, the patient experienced a precipitous drop in hemoglobin to 7.5 g/dL post-laparotomy, exacerbating her pre-existing iron deficiency anemia. This decline necessitated aggressive correction via intravenous iron supplementation (ferric carboxymaltose) and red blood cell transfusions to restore hematologic stability before discharge. Concurrently, the management of the concurrent *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection dictated a targeted antibiotic strategy. While standard surgical prophylaxis with cefazolin was initiated intraoperatively, the postoperative regimen was specifically tailored based on urine culture sensitivities, combining cefazolin with clarithromycin to ensure eradication of the pathogen while maintaining safety for the fetus. To mitigate the high risk of preterm labor triggered by surgical manipulation of the gravid uterus, a robust hormonal prophylaxis protocol was implemented immediately. This involved intraoperative and daily intramuscular progesterone (100 mg), transitioning later to depot hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg every three days). This regimen is critical in stabilizing the decidua-myometrial interface and suppressing oxytocin receptor expression during the vulnerable post-recovery window.

Obstetric surveillance following stabilization focused on confirming fetal viability and assessing growth trajectories against the backdrop of maternal physiological changes. Serial ultrasounds demonstrated reassuring fetal development, with growth tracking at the 86th percentile according to World Health Organization standards, indicating that the surgical intervention did not compromise placental perfusion or nutrient delivery. Amniotic fluid indices remained within normal limits throughout the follow-up period, which occurred every three weeks. Crucially, the obstetric course highlighted a dynamic relationship between maternal anatomy and fetal environment; while the resection of the massive upper leiomyoma relieved diaphragmatic compression and resolved acute symptoms like dyspnea and pain, a residual pelvic mass persisted. Pathological examination confirmed that both resected tissues were benign uterine leiomyomas exhibiting ischemic necrosis at the twisted pedicle, validating the diagnosis of torsion as the cause of acute decompensation rather than malignancy despite initial concerns raised by elevated CA-125 levels. The resolution of these acute symptoms allowed for a transition from crisis management to planned obstetric care.

The final phase of management involved strategic delivery planning dictated by the residual pelvic anatomy established during surgery. Although conservative management is often preferred for asymptomatic fibroids in late pregnancy, this case presented a distinct contraindication for vaginal delivery due to the location and size of the remaining caudal mass. The residual 7.5 cm leiomyoma was situated at the uterine torus in close proximity to the cervix and lower segment, creating a mechanical obstruction that would likely prevent fetal engagement or descent during labor attempts. Consequently, an elective cesarean section was scheduled at 37 weeks of gestation once fetal maturity was assured but prior to spontaneous labor onset or potential rupture risks associated with obstructed labor. The procedure utilized an incision aligned with previous surgical scars to minimize tissue trauma and reduce the risk of uterine dehiscence or rupture at old suture lines. This approach resulted in an excellent neonatal outcome and avoided catastrophic complications such as cervical laceration or arrest disorders that might have arisen from attempting a trial of labor in the setting of significant pelvic obstruction by fibroid tissue.
