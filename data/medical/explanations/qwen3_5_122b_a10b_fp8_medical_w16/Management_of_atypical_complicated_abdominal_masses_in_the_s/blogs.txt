Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When 'Endometriomas' Aren't: Deciphering Atypical Myomas in Pregnancy via Non-Contrast MRI

We've all seen the classic teaching: "If it looks like an endometrioma and bleeds like an endometrioma, treat it like an endometrioma." But what happens when that rule breaks down in the most high-stakes environment imaginable—a pregnant patient in her fourth decade?

Consider a 40-year-old G1P0A1 woman, BMI 33, presenting at 21 weeks gestation with severe left upper quadrant pain, pelvic discomfort, and dyspnea. Ultrasound paints a terrifying picture: two massive lesions. One is an 18 cm beast filling the left subphrenic space; the other is a 9.5 cm mass deep in the Pouch of Douglas. Both appear cystic with irregular internal echoes. The radiology report screams *decidualized endometriomas*. The tumor marker panel throws us another curveball: CA-125 is elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35). In a non-pregnant woman, this might trigger a panic for malignancy or advanced endometriosis requiring aggressive intervention.

But here's where the plot twists: **They weren't endometriomas.** They were giant, atypical uterine leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration and torsion. And getting to that diagnosis didn't require risky contrast agents or blind exploration—it required understanding the limitations of biomarkers in pregnancy and leveraging non-contrast MRI as our ultimate tie-breaker.

### The Diagnostic Trap: Why CA-125 Lies During Pregnancy

One of the biggest pitfalls in managing abdominal masses during pregnancy is over-reliance on serum tumor markers. As we know from basic pathophysiology, CA-125 isn't specific to ovarian cancer; it's a marker of mesothelial irritation. It spikes naturally during menstruation, inflammation, and—crucially—during pregnancy itself due to increased permeability of the serosa and direct contact between amniotic fluid and maternal tissues.

While CA-125 typically dips in the second trimester (median ~14 U/mL), this patient sat stubbornly above 40 U/mL with an upward trend. In isolation, this feels ominous. However, literature confirms that benign conditions like fibroids, PID, and even simple uterine distension can elevate these levels. Relying solely on this number pushed the differential heavily toward malignant ovarian pathology or complex endometriosis, potentially leading to unnecessary radical surgery or delay due to fear of teratogenicity from further workup.

This case reminds us that **context is king**. An isolated elevated CA-125 in a symptomatic pregnant patient should never dictate surgical strategy without corroborating structural evidence of malignancy (like ascites, nodularity on CT/MRI with contrast contraindications notwithstanding).

### The Imaging Pivot: Non-Contrast MRI as the Game Changer

When ultrasound hits its ceiling—which it inevitably does when dealing with "steric bulk" from a gravid uterus obscuring anatomy—we need a higher-resolution map. Enter MRI without gadolinium.

Gadolinium-based contrast agents cross the placenta freely and carry theoretical risks for renal toxicity and stillbirth; thus, they are generally avoided unless absolutely life-saving information cannot be obtained otherwise. Fortunately, modern non-contrast sequences are powerful enough to solve this puzzle. In this case, T2-weighted fat-saturated images provided clarity that ultrasound missed:
*   **Vascular Architecture:** While US showed peripheral flow, MRI highlighted thickened walls (1.4 cm) and focal solid components within the cystic spaces that looked suspicious for degeneration rather than simple hemorrhage.
*   **Anatomical Continuity:** Crucially, MRI failed to identify a distinct "stalk" separating the masses from the uterus for either lesion initially suspected as adnexal. Instead, it revealed continuity between the caudal mass and a known posterior intramural/subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration (~6 cm).
*   **Exclusion of Malignancy:** No lymphadenopathy was visualized, pushing away from metastatic disease despite the worrying lab values.

The MRI essentially told us: *"These aren't floating ovaries; these are part of your uterus acting weird."* This distinction shifted our plan from potential oophorectomy/hysterectomy considerations to targeted myomectomy strategies aimed purely at symptom relief while preserving fetal viability.

### Surgical Strategy: To Cut or Not to Cut?

The decision to operate at 21 weeks was driven by physiology failing under mechanical load: severe dyspnea from diaphragmatic splinting and escalating pain suggesting impending torsion or necrosis (supported by her low-grade fever). Conservative management wasn't working; she was deteriorating into anemia (Hb dropped to 7.5 g/L post-op) and restricting lung function needed for fetal growth monitoring later on.

The surgical approach highlights a nuanced principle in obstetric surgery: **Aggressive conservation.**
Upon entering via midline laparotomy (chosen over laparoscopy due to mass size >8cm and gestational age constraints), we found exactly what we feared but hoped wouldn't happen yet: the upper mass was indeed a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis—the source of her fever and pain! It was excised cleanly with double clamping of the stalk to minimize blood loss—a critical maneuver since myomas become incredibly vascular during pregnancy due to hormonal stimulation.

However, we made a deliberate choice *not* to remove the second pelvic mass sitting right against the cervix/posterior wall despite its size (~9 cm). Removing it carried too high a risk of catastrophic hemorrhage or cervical incompetence triggering preterm labor before term viability could be assured safely elsewhere? # The Art of Partial Myomectomy: Why Leaving Masses Behind Can Save a Pregnancy

We love a good "what would I do?" scenario in the ED, but few situations force us to weigh maternal physiology against fetal survival quite like managing a giant, symptomatic abdominal mass in the second trimester. Imagine your patient: a 40-year-old primigravida at 21 weeks, presenting with severe left upper quadrant and pelvic pain, dyspnea, and a fever. Imaging reveals two colossal masses—one towering into the subphrenic space compressing her lungs, another anchoring deep in the pouch of Douglas. The initial workup screams malignancy or catastrophic hemorrhage. You call OB/GYN, Surgery, and Anesthesia for a huddle. Do you scrub in for a full debulking? Or do you play the long game?

This isn't hypothetical. It's exactly what happened in a recent case report from Italy involving a woman with BMI 33 carrying twins (wait, no, singleton) where the surgical team made a counter-intuitive move that saved the pregnancy: they removed half the problem and left the rest alone.

### The Diagnostic Tightrope

Let's set the stage. Our patient arrived with VAS pain scores of 5/10 at two distinct sites and mild dyspnea. On exam, she had an 18 cm mass filling her LUQ and a fixed 9 cm mass in her pelvis. Ultrasound painted a confusing picture: two large cystic structures with peripheral vascularity. One looked like it could be an ovarian endometrioma; the other appeared adherent to a posterior uterine fibroid undergoing cystic degeneration.

Here is where things get tricky for anyone relying solely on tumor markers. Her CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35). In a non-pregnant woman over 50, that flags ovarian cancer immediately. But remember our physiology lesson: CA-125 rises naturally in pregnancy due to mesothelial irritation from the expanding uterus and decidua formation. While it peaked in the first trimester and dipped in the second (usually around 14 U/mL), this patient's level was stubbornly high. Add to that her anemia (Hb 9.9 g/L), leukocytosis, and low-grade fever driven by a concurrent *Klebsiella* UTI, and you have a perfect storm masking the true pathology.

They wisely skipped contrast-enhanced MRI due to fetal safety concerns regarding gadolinium but utilized non-contrast MRI to map out anatomy without ionizing radiation or nephrogenic systemic fibris risk factors relevant here too much less so than iodinated contrast). The scan confirmed thick-walled cysts with solid components but couldn't definitively rule out malignancy or clarify if the caudal mass was truly separate from the uterus.

### The Surgical Pivot Point

By week 21+6, conservative management failed. Pain persisted despite IV paracetamol, dyspnea worsened due to mechanical compression of the diaphragm by an 18-cm mass, and there was genuine fear of torsion or rupture as the uterus grew larger every day waiting for term delivery wasn't an option anymore—the risks outweighed them now! 

So off went she to OR... What did they find upon entering via midline vertical incision? Surprise! That terrifying "ovarian" mass up top? It turned out to be **a massively twisted pedunculated leiomyoma** arising directly from the uterine fundus! It was ischemic-necrotic near its stalk—a ticking time bomb explaining her fever and pain perfectly down below lay another beast attached firmly behind-the-wall-myoma seemingly inseparable from vital structures without risking catastrophe! 

Now comes *the* decision moment most textbooks gloss over: **Do you try to remove everything?**

Removing both masses would likely require extensive dissection near major vessels potentially damaging uteroplacental circulation causing massive hemorrhage necessitating hysterectomy—or worse triggering preterm labor leading NICU admission premature infant death possibly even maternal mortality depending how far gone things got before stabilization occurred anyway!! Instead their plan evolved quickly based on real-time findings: **"Take out what hurts right now; leave what might wait."** They excised only the pedunculated upper myoma which posed immediate threat via torsion/necrosis/sepsis risk while deliberately leaving intact that deeply embedded pelvic lesion hoping hormones post-partum shrink it naturally later once baby born safely delivered either vaginally cesarean-section whichever safest route determined closer term approached nearer thirty-seven weeks gestation mark hit finally allowing elective repeat Cs-section instead rushing emergently because obstructed labor forced hand prematurely!!!

# Beyond Expectant Management: Navigating Acute Complications of Large Uterine Fibroids During Gestation

We love "watchful waiting" in OB/GYN. It's the default setting for almost everything, especially when a fetus is involved. But sometimes, the biology of a massive tumor overrides the mantra of conservation. Today, we're dissecting a fascinating case from *De Nola et al.* where a pregnant woman didn't just have "fibroids"; she had a life-threatening mechanical crisis that forced a hand into the second trimester.

This isn't your standard red-degenerating fibroid story. We're talking about a 40-year-old nulliparous woman at 21 weeks gestation presenting with severe LUQ and pelvic pain, coupled with dyspnea. Imaging revealed two monsters: an 18 cm mass filling the left subphrenic space and a 9 cm mass deep in the Pouch of Douglas. The clinical picture was dire enough to mimic malignancy or catastrophic obstetric events, yet the final pathology turned out to be something we see frequently but rarely manage so aggressively mid-gestation: **twisted, ischemic pedunculated leiomyomas.**

### The Diagnostic Trap: When Benign Looks Malignant

The real teaching moment here starts with how easily these benign entities masquerade as high-stakes emergencies. On ultrasound, these masses looked like giant decidualized endometriomas or complex ovarian neoplasms. The clinician's brain immediately jumps to "cancer" when you see an 18 cm cystic-solid mass pushing against the diaphragm, especially when CA-125 is elevated (in this case, 41.8 U/mL).

Here is the pearl: **CA-125 is notoriously unreliable in pregnancy.** While it peaks in the first and third trimesters normally, it can creep up in the second trimester due to anything from endometriosis to simple inflammation or even large fibroids themselves. Relying solely on this marker would have led us down a rabbit hole of unnecessary anxiety or inappropriate workups.

The team wisely pivoted to non-contrast MRI once ultrasound hit its limits due to acoustic shadowing from the gravid uterus and massive tumors. The MRI confirmed the anatomy but couldn't definitively rule out malignancy versus benign degeneration because of the "steric bulk." The lack of contrast was intentional—gadolinium crosses the placenta and carries theoretical risks—but it left some ambiguity regarding vascularity and exact tissue characterization that only surgery could resolve.

### The Red Flags That Mandated Surgery

So why operate? Why not give Tylenol, bed rest, and hope for term? Because this patient crossed several absolute thresholds where expectant management becomes malpractice:

1.  **Mechanical Cardiopulmonary Compromise:** The upper mass wasn't just sitting there; it was compressing the diaphragm, causing true restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) despite normal O2 sat initially. As pregnancy progresses and the uterus grows upward, that space disappears completely. Waiting meant risking maternal respiratory failure.
2.  **Acute Ischemia/Torsion:** During laparotomy, they found exactly what caused the fever and escalating pain: a twisted pedicle leading to ischemic necrosis and leakage of fluid/blood products into the peritoneum. This is akin to ovarian torsion but with a much larger target organ attached to the uterus. Leaving this alone risks sepsis and disseminated necrotizing fasciitis-like spread within the peritoneal cavity.
3.  **Diagnostic Uncertainty:** With masses this large and heterogeneous, you cannot safely assume benignity without histology if they are symptomatic. The risk of missing an occult sarcoma or aggressive germ cell tumor outweighed surgical risk in this context.

### Surgical Nuances: What They Did Right (And Left Alone)

The surgical strategy deserves applause for its restraint amidst chaos. They performed a longitudinal laparotomy (xiphoid-pubis) rather than attempting a risky laparoscopy on an 18 kg-equivalent distended abdomen with distorted anatomy—a pragmatic choice given gestational age and mass size.

They removed *only* what was killing her right now: the twisted upper pedunculated myoma. They meticulously closed the uterine defect with absorbable sutures after clamping off feeding vessels to minimize hemorrhage risk to both mom and baby. Crucially, they left the lower pelvic mass *in situ*. Why? Because digging around in a tight pelvis with an 18-week uterus carrying twin-sized masses increases preterm labor risk exponentially without immediate benefit if that mass isn't acutely compromised.

Post-op care was equally rigorous:

# Obstetric Planning with Giant Pelvic Masses: To Deliver Vaginally or Cut?

We often think of managing abdominal masses in pregnancy as a battle against immediate threats: torsion, rupture, or malignancy. But sometimes, the most difficult decisions aren't made in the heat of the acute crisis—they are made months later when planning the delivery. Consider a fascinating case recently published by De Nola et al., where a woman in her 40s presented in the second trimester with two "titan" masses causing severe pain and dyspnea. While the surgical team successfully navigated the removal of one life-threatening mass, they were forced to leave another behind. Why did they make that choice, and more importantly, how did that decision dictate that this patient *had* to undergo a Cesarean section rather than attempt a trial of labor?

This case highlights a classic "two-horse race" scenario in complex obstetrics: balancing the risks of aggressive surgery against the mechanical realities of childbirth. The patient arrived at 21 weeks with an 18 cm upper mass filling her subphrenic space and a 9 cm fixed mass deep in the Pouch of Douglas. Imaging was tricky; ultrasound suggested decidualized endometriomas, while MRI hinted at cystic degeneration within leiomyomas. The CA-125 was mildly elevated—a red herring common in pregnancy—but combined with fever and worsening pain, the team couldn't wait. They proceeded with a laparotomy.

Here lies the pivot point of our story. During exploration, the upper mass turned out to be a giant, pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone twisting and ischemia—explaining her fever and pain. It was excised safely. However, the caudal mass told a different story. It wasn't clearly separable from the posterior uterine wall; it looked like an intramural-subserosal fibroid with cystic changes sitting right at the isthmus/cervical junction. Removing it carried a terrifying risk of hemorrhage or uterine injury that could jeopardize both mother and fetus mid-gestation. The surgeons wisely chose conservation over aggression for this specific lesion, leaving it *in situ*.

Fast forward to term planning, and here is where your brain needs to shift gears from "acute rescue" to "obstetric logistics." You might ask: *"If we removed half the problem surgically, can she try for a vaginal delivery?"* In this case, absolutely not. That retained 9 cm mass was anchored directly over the posterior cervico-isthmic segment—the very highway blockage zone for fetal descent. Even though she delivered via repeat Cesarean on her existing scar at 37 weeks without incident, attempting vaginal delivery would have meant facing obstructed labor with high risks of uterine rupture or catastrophic laceration around that stubborn tumor bed.

What makes this case so instructive isn't just the surgical nuance; it's the data regarding myoma behavior during pregnancy that informs these decisions. Leiomyomas don't sit still; they grow rapidly in early pregnancy (upwards of 6% weekly growth in some studies) before shrinking post-partum due to hormonal withdrawal. If you operate too aggressively late in pregnancy or fail to account for where these tumors settle relative to the birth canal, you set yourself up for disaster at term. As noted in recent literature comparing outcomes of myomectomy plus C-section versus C-section alone, while blood loss may be comparable, operative time and length of stay skyrocket when you add unnecessary tumor resection into an already compromised field.

So, what is our takeaway for managing similar giants lurking in your clinic tomorrow? Don't let fear drive your knife hand during pregnancy unless there is true compromise (torsion/necrosis/malignancy suspicion). And never underestimate how much geography matters when planning delivery; location trumps size every time when determining modality of birth.

**Take-Home Points:**

*   **Surgical Restraint is Key:** In pregnant patients with multiple large masses, prioritize removing only those causing acute pathology (torsion/rupture). Leaving asymptomatic or inseparable masses *in situ* reduces intraoperative hemorrhage risk and preserves uterine integrity for future delivery attempts—or safer planned C-sections.
*   **Location Dictates Delivery Mode:** A large posterior or isthmic myoma acts as a mechanical obstruction regardless of fetal size or cervical dilation history; these patients require planned Cesarean delivery to prevent obstructed labor and uterine trauma.
*   **Imaging Strategy Matters:** Rely on non-contrast MRI after ultrasound for complex adnexal/pelvic masses in pregnancy to distinguish between ovarian pathology (endometriomas) and degenerating leiomyomas; remember that CA-125 can be falsely reassuringly elevated due to physiologic changes or benign inflammation like endometriosis/myomas.
*   **Expect Size Fluctuations:** Be aware that myomas typically expand significantly during gestation (especially 1st/early 2nd trimester) but regress postpartum; therefore, deferring definitive resection until after lactation cessation is often reasonable if symptoms allow.



# Physiological vs. Pathological: Interpreting Rising CA-125 Levels in Pregnant Patients with Adnexal Masses

We've all seen the labs come back where everything looks fine until you hit that one tumor marker sitting just above the reference range. In a non-pregnant patient, an elevated CA-125 sends us down a rabbit hole of CT scans, PET studies, and referrals to gynecologic oncology. But throw a pregnancy into the mix, and suddenly your "normal" cutoff becomes a moving target. What happens when a pregnant patient presents with massive abdominal masses, severe dyspnea, and a CA-125 that's creeping upward? Do you operate immediately fearing ovarian carcinoma, or do you hold tight knowing that pregnancy itself plays havoc with serology?

This question isn't theoretical; it's exactly the dilemma faced by a 40-year-old nulliparous woman in her second trimester described in a recent case report by De Nola et al. She arrived at the ER with acute left upper quadrant pain, pelvic discomfort, and significant dyspnea. On exam, she looked like she was carrying twins (or triplets) despite being only 21 weeks along. Imaging confirmed two colossal masses: an 18 cm lesion filling the left subphrenic space and a 9 cm mass anchoring the pouch of Douglas. To make matters more confusing, her CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL—elevated—and trending upward from 36 U/mL two weeks prior.

Here lies the crux of the issue: Is this ovarian cancer masquerading as pregnancy complications, or is it something else entirely? If you rely solely on that static lab value against standard adult references (<35 U/mL), you might lean heavily toward malignancy. However, physiology tells a different story. We know that CA-125 naturally spikes in the first trimester due to mesothelial stimulation from chorionic villi and again in the third trimester as parturition approaches. By the second trimester, levels typically dip to their lowest point (often around 14 U/mL). So, seeing anything above 35 U/mL in this window *is* abnormal relative to baseline pregnancy norms. Yet, as this case brilliantly illustrates, "abnormal" doesn't automatically equal "cancer."

The multidisciplinary team here didn't panic; they synthesized data. They noted that while CA-125 was high for gestational age, other markers (CEA, CA 19-9, CA 15-3) were pristine. More importantly, they recognized that benign conditions like leiomyomas and endometriosis can elevate CA-125 during pregnancy due to local inflammation or decidualization. The real clincher wasn't just the lab work; it was the clinical picture of mechanical obstruction causing respiratory compromise and pain that analgesics couldn't fix alone. When conservative management failed and the risk of torsion or rupture loomed large due to the sheer size of these tumors ("steric bulk"), surgery became unavoidable—not because they feared metastasis necessarily proved beyond doubt by labs alone, but because the anatomy threatened both mother and fetus regardless of histology.

What made this case so instructive was what happened inside the OR rather than outside it on paper charts. Upon laparotomy via xiphopubic incision (chosen over laparoscopy likely due to mass size and gestational age constraints), they found no ovarian malignancy whatsoever. Instead, they discovered a giant pedunculated uterine leiomyoma! It had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis—a perfect storm explaining her fever and rising CRP—and another cystic-degenerating fibroid below it stuck fast to the uterine wall. The "ovarian masses" visualized on ultrasound were actually uterine in origin all along—a classic pitfall when dealing with decidualized tissues that mimic endometriomas or cysts on imaging.
