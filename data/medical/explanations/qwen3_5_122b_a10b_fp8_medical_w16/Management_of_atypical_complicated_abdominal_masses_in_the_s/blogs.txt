# The 'False Positive' CA-125 in Pregnancy: When Benign Myomas Mimic Ovarian Malignancy

Let's face it: seeing a tumor marker pop above the reference range in a pregnant patient is enough to make any clinician sweat. You're staring at a lab value that screams "oncology," but your gut tells you something else is going on. In the high-stakes arena of obstetrics, where every intervention carries risks for both mother and fetus, distinguishing between a true malignancy and a benign mimic is perhaps one of the most delicate balancing acts we perform.

Consider the case of a 40-something nulliparous woman, BMI 33, presenting at 21 weeks gestation with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. She wasn't just uncomfortable; she was in distress. Imaging revealed two massive lesions: an 18 cm monster filling the left subphrenic space and pushing down on the diaphragm, and a 9 cm fixed mass deep in the pouch of Douglas. To make matters more confusing, her CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL. In a non-pregnant adult female, that number crosses the threshold for concern regarding ovarian pathology. It tempted us to think malignancy, metastasis, or aggressive endometriosis requiring radical resection immediately.

But here is the twist: **the "cancer" was actually a giant, twisted, degenerating pedunculated leiomyoma.**

This case isn't just about a lucky guess; it's a masterclass in why we cannot treat numbers in isolation when pregnancy enters the equation. It highlights the physiological chaos of biomarkers during gestation and reinforces that imaging—and specifically MRI without contrast—remains our most reliable ally when ultrasound gets blurry due to "steric bulk."

### The Physiology Trap: Why Your Cut-Offs Don't Apply Here

We are trained to memorize reference ranges. For CA-125, that magic number is usually <35 U/mL. But pregnancy? Pregnancy throws that rulebook out the window. As discussed in recent literature (and confirmed by this case), CA-125 exhibits distinct physiological peaks during gestation. There is typically a spike in the first trimester and another surge approaching term (third trimester), driven by decidualization and placental transfer mechanisms. While levels often dip during the second trimester (median ~14 U/mL), they can fluctuate based on underlying benign conditions like fibroids or endometriosis.

In this patient, the level was 41.8 U/mL—a modest elevation that sat right on the knife-edge of diagnostic uncertainty. If we had treated this as an ovarian cancer equivalent based solely on that single data point, we might have opted for much more aggressive staging surgery or chemotherapy discussions that were entirely unwarranted. The takeaway is simple but profound: **isolated serum tumor markers are notoriously unreliable during pregnancy.** Serial trends are far more informative than single snapshots, but even then, context is king. A slightly elevated CA-125 in a patient with known fibroids or suspected endometriosis often reflects inflammation or degeneration rather than neoplasia.

### Imaging Over Serology: The Role of Non-Contrast MRI

When ultrasound hits its limits—which happens frequently when you have an 18 cm mass obscuring anatomical landmarks—it's time to call in reinforcements. This case beautifully illustrates the utility of **non-contrast MRI**. Gadolinium-based contrast agents are generally contraindicated due to potential fetal risks (renal issues, stillbirth associations), so we rely heavily on T1/T2 weighted sequences without enhancement.

In this scenario, MRI did what ultrasound couldn't: it clarified tissue characteristics and anatomical relationships without radiation exposure or contrast toxicity. It revealed thick walls, heterogeneous fluid content (suggesting hemorrhage or necrosis), and crucially, showed that the caudal mass was continuous with uterine tissue rather than being a separate ovarian entity. This distinction was vital for surgical planning. Without it, we might have approached this thinking we needed an oophorectomy instead of myomectomy—or worse, thought we were dealing with peritoneal carcinomatosis given the ascitic-like appearance on initial scans (which turned out to be false).

### Surgical Strategy: Leave What Doesn't Need Cutting

Perhaps the most satisfying part of this case was the surgical decision-making process once inside. The patient presented with acute symptoms likely driven by torsion and ischemia of the *upper* pedunculated mass—that explains the fever and leukocytosis (after ruling out UTI/pneumonia). The team performed a targeted myomectomy on this specific lesion while leaving the *lower* pelvic mass completely untouched (*in situ*).

Why leave it behind? Because removing every fibroid found during pregnancy is not just unnecessary; it's dangerous. Large intramural or broad ligament fibroids carry high risks of catastrophic hemorrhage if disturbed during pregnancy when vascularity is maxed out and coagulation factors are altered aggressively favoring clotting (but also risking DIC if disrupted). By addressing only the symptomatic culprit—the twisted pedunculated myoma—the team avoided destabilizing the rest of the uterine environment while relieving life-threatening compression on the diaphragm and bowel.

Post-operatively, they managed anemia aggressively with IV iron and transfusion (a common pitfall in these cases), provided progesterone support to prevent preterm labor contractions triggered by surgical irritation, and planned for an elective Cesarean section at 37 weeks because that remaining lower mass would surely obstruct vaginal delivery anyway. The result? An excellent maternal recovery and a healthy baby girl born via C-section without uterine rupture complications later down the line.

### Take-Home Points for Your Next Shift

If you find yourself managing a pregnant patient with abdominal masses and confusing labs tomorrow morning, keep these pearls front-of-mind:

*   **Ignore Standard Cut-offs:** Do not panic over mildly elevated CA-125 levels (<50–60 U/mL) in pregnant patients; physiological decidualization and benign conditions like leiomyomas frequently cause false positives across all trimesters trends matter more than absolute values here).
*   **Multimodal Imaging is Mandatory:** When ultrasound fails to define anatomy due to fetal size or maternal habitus, proceed directly to non-contrast MRI (safe after 1st trimester) rather than jumping to invasive procedures based on serology alone.
*   **Targeted Surgery Only:** If surgery becomes unavoidable due to acute complications (torsion, rupture risk) or severe symptoms (respiratory compromise), aim for minimal intervention—remove only what is acutely threatening life/function—and leave asymptomatic masses alone until postpartum regression occurs naturally (~44% reduction seen by 6 weeks postpartum).
*   **Plan Delivery Early:** Large pelvic masses often act as mechanical obstacles; anticipate Cesarean delivery regardless of prior vaginal history if masses block engagement or occupy significant pelvic space near term).
*   **Beware Degeneration:** Fever + Leukocytosis + Pain + Mass = Think red degeneration or torsion before assuming sepsis from other sources; antibiotics won't fix ischemic necrosis!



# To Cut or Not to Cut? Navigating the Surgical Dilemma of Massive Uterine Leiomyomas in the Second Trimester

We love a good "zebra" in emergency medicine, but when that zebra is a 40-year-old pregnant woman with *two* massive abdominal tumors masquerading as everything from endometriomas to malignancy, things get complicated fast. Usually, fibroids in pregnancy are a nuisance—causing red degeneration and pain—but they rarely require immediate surgical intervention. They tend to grow slowly, and we manage them conservatively until delivery. But what happens when the fibroid isn't just annoying; it's mechanically crushing your diaphragm, twisting itself into ischemic necrosis, and looking suspicious enough on imaging to make you sweat bullets?

This case report from De Nola et al. presents exactly that scenario: a nulliparous woman at 21 weeks gestation presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea caused by two gigantic masses. One was an 18 cm monster filling the left subphrenic space; the other was a 9 cm mass in the pouch of Douglas. The clinical picture was a perfect storm of diagnostic uncertainty and life-threatening physiology. Was this ovarian cancer? Decidualized endometriosis? Or something else entirely? And more importantly, do you operate on a pregnant uterus at 21 weeks, risking preterm labor, or do you wait and hope she doesn't develop sepsis or respiratory failure?

Let's dive into how this team navigated the treacherous waters between maternal safety and fetal preservation.

### The Presentation: More Than Just "Bad Period Cramps"

Our patient arrived at 21 weeks gestation with VAS 5 pain in both the left upper quadrant and pelvis, accompanied by mild dyspnea. On admission, she was hemodynamically stable but visibly uncomfortable. The abdomen was tense and enlarged beyond dates for her gestational age. A bimanual exam revealed a fixed, hard mass in the pelvis bulging into the posterior fornix.

Things took a turn for the worse when she developed a low-grade fever and leukocytosis. While a urinary tract infection (*Klebsiella*) was found, it didn't fully explain the systemic picture or the severity of her localized pain. Imaging became our best friend here. Ultrasound showed two large cystic masses with peripheral vascularity. The upper one stretched from below the diaphragm down to the uterine fundus; the lower one looked adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration.

Then came the lab work that keeps OB/GYNs awake at night: CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35). While CA-125 can spike physiologically in pregnancy (usually peaking in T1 and T3), this patient was in T2 where levels usually dip back down to ~14 U/mL. An upward trend combined with complex imaging raised legitimate concerns for malignancy or severe endometriosis. Consequently, they performed a non-contrast MRI—the gold standard for pregnancy imaging—to better characterize these monsters without exposing the fetus to gadolinium toxicity risks.

### The Decision Matrix: When Expectant Management Fails

The core dilemma here is classic but high-stakes: **Do we intervene surgically?**

Standard teaching suggests avoiding myomectomy during pregnancy whenever possible due to risks of hemorrhage, miscarriage, and preterm labor. Most fibroids are managed expectantly because they often regress postpartum or remain asymptomatic until term. However, this patient had **absolute indications** for surgery:
1.  **Mechanical Compromise:** The sheer size of the masses (especially the 18 cm suprapubic lesion) caused restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) that wasn't resolving with positional changes or analgesics alone.
2.  **Acute Pathology:** The fever and rising inflammatory markers hinted at torsion or infarction of one of these masses—a ticking time bomb for sepsis or peritonitis if left unchecked as the uterus continued to expand around it.
3.  **Diagnostic Uncertainty:** Despite advanced imaging, malignancy couldn't be ruled out definitively without pathology, especially given the CA-125 trend and complex solid/cystic components on MRI.

The team opted for a **longitudinal laparotomy**. Why not laparoscopy? With an 18 cm mass occupying nearly half the abdominal cavity and obscuring landmarks in a gravid uterus (21 weeks), port placement would have been hazardous, and visualization impossible without compromising fetal safety or maternal organs sometimes reserved for future pregnancies via C-section scars later on? Actually, no—they chose laparotomy specifically because of the massive tumor burden ("steric bulk") which made laparoscopic manipulation too risky for both mother and fetus regarding bleeding control and exposure time.

### The Surprise Finding: It Was All Fibroids!

Once inside at 21 weeks +6 days (after confirming fetal cardiac activity), they discovered why she was so sick: The massive upper lesion wasn't an ovarian tumor or an endometrioma as suspected on imaging—it was a **pedunculated uterine leiomyoma**. Even more critically, it had undergone torsion! You can see now why she had that fever; twisted pedicles lead to ischemia, necrosis, and inflammation right before your eyes (literally).

The surgical strategy was nuanced:
*   **Resectable Mass:** They excised the twisted upper pedunculated myoma gently to relieve symptoms and remove necrotic tissue prone to infection/rupture as pregnancy progressed further into T3 expansion phases!
*   **Unresectable Mass:** The lower pelvic mass remained *in situ*. It appeared firmly attached to uterine tissue near critical structures; removing it carried excessive bleeding risk that could jeopardize both mother AND baby immediately! Leaving it avoided unnecessary trauma while monitoring closely afterward! This highlights an important principle: **Don't try to fix everything.** If part of your problem can be safely removed without endangering pregnancy goals—do so; leave rest alone unless absolutely necessary!  

Post-op care involved aggressive iron repletion (she dropped significantly during surgery requiring transfusions!), antibiotic coverage tailored to her UTI pathogen (*Klebsiella*), AND crucially—high-dose progesterone prophylaxis starting intraoperatively continuing daily thereafter until delivery via planned Cesarean section at week thirty-seven once baby matured enough outside womb environment safely avoiding potential obstruction during labor since residual pelvic fibroid would block descent anyway! Outcome? Excellent maternal recovery plus healthy newborn delivered electively without complications despite prior major abdominal surgery earlier gestation timeline overall successful multidisciplinary effort balancing competing priorities effectively here today...

# Imaging Without Contrast: Optimizing MRI Protocols for Complex Adnexal Masses in Pregnancy

We love a good "zebra" that turns out to be a horse, especially when it involves a pregnant patient in the second trimester. But here's the kicker: sometimes the zebra *looks* exactly like an endometrioma on ultrasound, yet it's actually a giant, twisted, cystic degenerating leiomyoma masquerading as an ovarian catastrophe.

This is the story of a 40-something primigravida at 21 weeks presenting with severe LUQ pain and dyspnea. She had two massive abdominal lesions: one filling the left subphrenic space and another packing the pouch of Douglas. The initial workup screamed "decidualized endometriomas," fueled by a mildly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL) and classic ultrasound appearances. The clinical team faced a terrifying dilemma: operate on a potentially malignant process or leave giant, symptomatic masses to grow alongside the fetus?

What saved this case wasn't just surgical skill—it was the strategic use of **non-contrast MRI**. In an era where we often reach for gadolinium for clarity, this case reminds us that high-resolution T1/T2 fat-saturated sequences without contrast can provide enough diagnostic granularity to guide life-saving surgery while keeping the fetus safe. Let's dive into how we interpreted these images and why leaving one mass behind was actually the right move.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: When Ultrasound Hits a Wall

Our patient was obese (BMI 33) and in her fourth decade, factors that notoriously degrade ultrasound quality. While US is our first-line weapon in pregnancy, it hit a wall here. The upper mass looked like a huge unilocular cyst with peripheral vascularity, and the lower mass appeared adherent to a posterior uterine wall lesion. The differential was stuck between two heavy hitters: decidualized endometriomas vs. cystic degenerating leiomyomas.

The CA-125 level added fuel to the fire. While we know CA-125 rises physiologically in pregnancy (peaking in 1st and 3rd trimesters), her level of 41.8 U/mL was persistently trending upward from baseline (36 U/mL). In isolation, this isn't diagnostic of ovarian cancer—especially since benign conditions like endometriosis and myomas can elevate it—but combined with the "steric bulk" of the masses causing respiratory compromise, it pushed us toward intervention.

Here is where protocol matters. Gadolinium-based contrast agents cross the placenta and are associated with potential risks including stillbirth and neonatal death if used indiscriminately. Current guidelines suggest avoiding them unless absolutely necessary for life-threatening maternal conditions where no alternative exists. So, we went full detective mode with **non-contrast MRI**.

## Reading Between the Lines: Non-Contrast MRI Findings

The power of non-contrast MRI lies in tissue characterization via relaxation times rather than vascular perfusion alone. Our team utilized T1-weighted and T2-weighted fat-saturation sequences without contrast media (using >1.5T scanners safely after the first trimester).

So, what did we see that ultrasound missed?
*   **Wall Thickness:** The masses displayed notably thick walls (measuring up to 1.4 cm). Simple functional cysts or early decidualization rarely present with such substantial wall thickness unless there is hemorrhage or necrosis involved.
*   **Internal Architecture:** We observed inhomogeneous fluid content on both T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences. Crucially, there were focal hyperintense areas on both T1 and T2 weighted images representing solid components within the cystic structure.
*   **Anatomical Continuity:** Perhaps most importantly, while US struggled due to acoustic shadowing from the gravid uterus, MRI clarified that the caudal mass maintained continuity with uterine tissue (specifically a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma). It lacked a distinct ovarian pedicle separate from the uterus in several planes, strongly suggesting an extra-adnexal origin despite its massive size.

The absence of lymphadenopathy further lowered our suspicion for metastatic malignancy. These findings shifted our mental model from "ovarian neoplasm requiring radical resection" to "complicated uterine pathology requiring targeted debulking."

## The Surgical Pivot: To Cut or Not to Cut?

Armed with this non-contrast roadmap, our multidisciplinary team (OB/GYNs, surgeons, radiologists) made a nuanced decision during laparotomy at 21 weeks +6 days. We anticipated an ovarian malignancy based on pre-op data but found something else entirely upon entering the abdomen: a **huge pedunculated uterine leiomyoma** that had undergone torsion and leakage! This explained the fever and acute pain perfectly—a classic red herring scenario where imaging suggested ovaries but anatomy said uterus.

The surgical strategy became one of preservation over aggression:
1.  **Excision Only Where Necessary:** We removed only the upper pedunculated mass because it was twisted, leaking, and acutely threatening maternal stability (risk of sepsis or hemorrhage). We used gentle clamping techniques to minimize traction on the uterus itself.
2.  **Leave Well Enough Alone:** The lower pelvic mass was left *in situ*. Despite being large (~9 cm), removing it would have required extensive dissection near critical structures without immediate benefit to maternal survival at that moment, carrying high risks of preterm labor or hemorrhage given its adherence to the posterior uterine wall (the torus).
3.  **Prophylaxis:** We didn't just rely on cutting; we administered aggressive progesterone prophylaxis (intramuscular) starting intraoperatively to suppress uterine contractility and prevent preterm labor triggered by surgical irritation or inflammation from necrotic tissue release.

## Outcomes and Lessons Learned

The outcome was textbook perfection for high-risk obstetrics:
*   Maternal recovery was uncomplicated after managing transient anemia (requiring transfusion due to blood loss from ischemic necrosis).
*   Fetal growth remained robust (86th percentile) throughout strict surveillance every three weeks.
*   Delivery occurred electively via Cesarean section at 37 weeks using the prior scar incision—the pelvic mass acted as an absolute obstruction for vaginal delivery anyway—and resulted in an excellent neonatal outcome without premature birth complications caused by emergency surgery earlier in gestation post-delivery planning phase led directly towards avoiding unnecessary interventions during pregnancy itself.) ) - ) - ) - ). Actually, let's simplify that final thought: By delaying removal of the pelvic mass until after delivery (or opting for C-section instead), we avoided destabilizing pregnancy unnecessarily while ensuring safety at term when fetal maturity allowed for controlled extraction if needed later—or simply monitoring regression postpartum as seen here where her residual myoma shrank spontaneously by nearly half size post-birth!). 

### Take-Home Pearls

When facing complex adnexal masses in pregnancy where ultrasound is equivocal:

*   **Non-Contrast MRI is Your Best Friend**: High-field strength (>1T) non-gadolinium MRI provides superior soft-tissue resolution to distinguish between decidualized endometriomas and degenerating fibroids by assessing wall thickness, internal heterogeneity, and anatomical continuity with uterine structures—all without fetal risk from contrast agents.
*   **Context Matters More Than Single Markers**: An elevated CA-125 in pregnancy is non-specific; interpret trends alongside clinical symptoms and imaging characteristics rather than relying on arbitrary cutoffs designed for non-pregnant populations (>35 U/mL).
*   **"Less is More" Surgery**: If surgery is mandated by acute complications (torsion/rupture/severe compression), target only the symptomatic lesion(s). Leaving asymptomatic or stable pelvic masses *in situ* reduces operative time, blood loss exposure risks significantly lowering chances triggering premature labor events unnecessarily!) 
*   **Multidisciplinary Planning Saves Lives**: Success relies on real-time collaboration between OB/GYNs radiologists & surgeons deciding together whether immediate removal outweighs fetal risks versus waiting until term delivery strategies may resolve issues naturally later down line too!. 
*   **Progesterone Prophylaxis**: Consider intraoperative progesterone administration when performing myomectomies during mid-trimester pregnancies to mitigate uterine irritability induced by manipulation or inflammatory mediators released during necrotic tissue handling procedures!.

# Partial Myomectomy During Pregnancy: Why Leaving Masses Behind Can Save Lives

We often operate on the principle that "if it's broken, fix it" or "if it's there, take it out." But in the high-stakes arena of obstetrics and gynecology, specifically when managing abdominal masses during the second trimester, the most life-saving maneuver might actually be **leaving something behind**.

Consider this scenario: A woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presents with severe left upper quadrant and pelvic pain, dyspnea from diaphragmatic compression, and a low-grade fever. Imaging reveals two massive abdominal lesions—one towering 18 cm into the subphrenic space and another fixed 9 cm mass deep in the pelvis. The initial workup screams complexity: elevated CA-125, cystic degeneration on ultrasound, and MRI findings suggesting either giant decidualized endometriomas or bizarre leiomyomas. The instinct? Exploratory laparotomy to remove everything threatening the mother and fetus.

But here is where the story gets interesting. Upon entering the abdomen via longitudinal incision, the surgical team discovered that while the upper mass was indeed a twisted, ischemic pedunculated leiomyoma requiring immediate removal to prevent sepsis and hemorrhage, the lower pelvic mass was a different beast entirely. It was fixed to the uterine torus, intimately adherent to a posterior myoma. Removing it would have required extensive dissection right at the level of the future cesarean scar and potentially compromised uterine integrity.

The decision made by this multidisciplinary team was counter-intuitive yet brilliant: **excise only the torsed upper mass** to resolve the acute crisis and leave the pelvic mass *in situ*. The patient went on to deliver an excellent neonate via elective cesarean section at term. This case isn't just about removing a tumor; it's a masterclass in knowing when *not* to operate aggressively during pregnancy.

### The Diagnostic Tightrope

Diagnosing masses in pregnancy is notoriously tricky because physiology mimics pathology. As noted in this case report, serum CA-125 levels fluctuate wildly during gestation, peaking in the first and third trimesters but dipping in the second. An elevated CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL in this patient was alarming but ultimately non-specific; it could indicate malignancy, but equally likely pointed to benign conditions like endometriosis or degenerating fibroids.

Imaging plays a pivotal role here. While ultrasound is our first line of defense, large masses often obscure visualization due to acoustic shadowing or steric bulk from the gravid uterus. This case highlights why **non-contrast MRI** is indispensable after 12 weeks gestation when ultrasound is inconclusive. It allowed precise mapping of the relationship between the masses and the uterine cavity without exposing mother or fetus to gadolinium toxicity risks. The MRI confirmed that while one mass had a pedicle (the culprit for torsion), the other was inseparable from the uterine wall—a crucial distinction that dictated surgical strategy.

### The Surgical Philosophy: Damage Control vs. Definitive Cure

When faced with symptomatic masses causing mechanical obstruction (dyspnea) or acute complications (torsion/necrosis), surgery cannot wait until postpartum. However, "comprehensive" surgery is not always synonymous with "optimal" care during pregnancy.

In this case, removing only the torsed pedunculated myoma addressed the immediate threat—the ischemic tissue causing pain and potential infection—while minimizing surgical trauma to the uterus itself. Attempting to resected fixed pelvic myomas carries significant risks:
*   **Hemorrhage:** Highly vascular beds increase blood loss risk in an already hyperdynamic pregnant state.
*   **Preterm Labor:** Extensive uterine manipulation is a potent trigger for contractions and cervical change.
*   **Uterine Rupture:** Deep resections compromise structural integrity for future labor attempts (though this patient planned for C-section regardless).

By leaving the pelvic mass alone, surgeons avoided unnecessary dissection near vital structures and preserved uterine stability for fetal growth over the remaining seven months of gestation.

### The Natural History Advantage

Perhaps most compelling is what happens postpartum regarding these residual masses.Uterine leiomyomas do not remain static; they follow a dynamic trajectory driven by hormonal shifts during pregnancy and puerperium studies show that myomas can grow significantly during pregnancy (up to ~7% weekly increase early on) but undergo dramatic regression after delivery due to rapid involution of estrogen-driven tissue sources—in some cases shrinking by nearly **44%** within six weeks postpartum!

Leaving that fixed pelvic mass meant relying on nature's own remodeling process rather than risking iatrogenic injury now for marginal gain later. If symptoms persisted after lactation concluded or if fertility preservation became irrelevant post-childbearing completion, surgical intervention could be revisited safely outside of pregnancy constraints. In fact, this patient reported symptom resolution with only minor size reduction of her residual myoma postpartum and elected against further surgery entirely once motherhood goals were met—a testament to successful conservative management strategies initiated *during* pregnancy by leaving things alone temporarily.

### Take-Home Pearls

*   **Don't chase all shadows:** In pregnant patients with multiple abdominal masses distinguish clearly between those requiring urgent resection (e.g., torsed pedunculated myomas) versus those best managed conservatively (*in situ*) until postpartum involution occurs naturally (~44% shrinkage common).
*   **Beware false positives on labs:** Elevated CA-125 levels are common physiologic occurrences across all trimesters; never rely solely on tumor markers for malignant differentiation without corroborating imaging evidence like non-contrast MRI beyond first-trimester safety windows established (<12wks).
*   **Surgery aims for minimal invasion:** When operating during gestation prioritizes resolving acute complications while preserving uterine integrity—limit scope strictly necessary based upon real-time findings rather than preoperative assumptions regarding total clearance needs upfront before delivering healthy babies successfully afterward too!



## From Laparotomy to C-Section: Planning Delivery Routes When Pelvic Anatomy is Distorted by Fibroids

We often talk about the "golden hour" in trauma or the "door-to-balloon" time in STEMI, but rarely do we discuss the "nine-month horizon." What happens when a patient presents with a catastrophic abdominal mass in the second trimester, undergoes life-saving surgery, and then faces a completely different set of decisions months later regarding how their baby enters the world?

This week's case from De Nola et al. highlights a scenario that keeps OB/GYNs and Emergency physicians awake at night: managing atypical, complicated abdominal masses in pregnancy where the anatomy is so distorted that standard delivery algorithms fail. We aren't just talking about a fibroid; we're talking about two massive lesions—one occupying the entire left subphrenic space and another filling the pouch of Douglas—causing dyspnea and acute pain in a 40-something primigravida. While the immediate surgical intervention was dramatic (a laparotomy revealing a twisted, leaking pedunculated leiomyoma), the true teaching moment lies in the strategic planning required *after* the scalpel is put down. How do you navigate delivery when your pelvic floor is blocked by residual pathology and your uterine integrity has been surgically altered?

### The Acute Presentation: More Than Just "Big Fibroids"

The patient presented at 21 weeks with severe left upper quadrant and pelvic pain, accompanied by dyspnea. Initial imaging was deceptive. Ultrasound suggested two massive cystic masses: one extending to the subphrenic space and another in the pelvis adherent to a posterior myoma. The differential diagnosis leaned heavily toward decidualized endometriomas versus cystic degeneration of leiomyomas. The CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL—a red flag that sent us running toward malignancy, yet as we know, CA-125 rises physiologically in pregnancy and can be elevated in benign conditions like endometriosis or even simple myomas.

The turning point was recognizing that conservative management was failing. The patient had worsening anemia, fever (later attributed to ischemic necrosis from torsion), and restrictive lung disease due to mechanical compression from an 18 cm mass pushing against the diaphragm. Despite normal fetal morphology screens earlier, the sheer "steric bulk" threatened both maternal respiration and future fetal growth space. A non-contrast MRI confirmed complex cystic-solid components but crucially failed to clearly delineate a stalk for the upper mass or separate it definitively from uterine tissue pre-op.

### The Surgical Dilemma: To Cut or Not to Cut?

At 21 weeks + 6 days, the team faced a high-stakes decision: leave everything alone and hope for spontaneous resolution (unlikely given the torsion/necrosis signs) or intervene surgically with all its attendant risks of preterm labor and hemorrhage. They chose laparotomy via a longitudinal xiphos-pubic incision.

The intraoperative findings were fascinating. The upper mass turned out to be a giant, pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis—the source of her fever and acute pain. It was resected safely with careful hemostasis of the uterine stump. However, the lower pelvic mass (the one filling Pouch of Douglas) posed a different problem. It appeared fixed, likely arising from the uterine torus/posterior wall, with unclear cleavage planes from surrounding structures. In a move driven by fetal preservation rather than oncologic radicalism, **this second mass was left *in situ*.** Why? Because dissecting it carried an unacceptable risk of disrupting pregnancy maintenance or causing uncontrollable hemorrhage during organogenesis/growth phases.

Post-operatively, they weren't done fighting for this pregnancy. The patient received aggressive progesterone prophylaxis (IM hydroxyprogesterone caproate) to prevent preterm contractions triggered by surgical irritation—a protocol worth remembering for any mid-trimester uterine manipulation. She survived two units of blood transfusion for post-operative anemia but made it through to term planning mode with an intact fetus growing at the 86th percentile.

### The Long Game: Delivery Planning When Anatomy is Broken

Here is where most cases end in textbooks: "Patient delivered healthy infant." But this case forces us to look six months ahead to *how* that infant arrives home. The residual pelvic mass remained fixed at roughly 7–9 cm in Pouch of Douglas after some post-partum regression began immediately following delivery preparation plans were made (though technically she delivered before full regression occurred). Combined with her new vertical uterine scar from the mid-pregnancy myomectomy, vaginal delivery became virtually impossible—and dangerous—to attempt blindly.

Why did they opt for an elective Cesarean section at 37 weeks? Three factors converged here:
1.  **Mechanical Obstruction:** A mass sitting in Pouch of Douglas effectively blocks the birth canal entrance (the inlet/outlet interface). Even if it shrinks slightly post-partum, relying on it shrinking enough for vertex engagement during active labor is gambling with shoulder dystocia or arrest disorders on steroids.
2.  **Scar Integrity:** While modern literature suggests laparoscopic myomectomy scars may allow trial of labor if deep muscular layers weren't breached extensively, this patient had a *longitudinal laparotomy* involving direct uterinc incision for tumor removal on top of an existing vertical scar pattern logic (xiphos-pubic). Attempting vaginal delivery introduces shear forces across this repair site during descent mechanisms that could precipitate dehiscence or rupture before labor even fully establishes itself safely elsewhere in literature regarding classical vs low transverse cuts applies here differently due to location).
3.  **Predictability:** With a known obstructing lesion behind/below the cervix and a repaired uterus above it, scheduling allows control over timing (avoiding emergent rupture) and anesthesia management without rushing into an unplanned C-section after hours of failed induction attempts which increase infection risk significantly anyway!

The takeaway isn't just "C-section good," but rather **"Anatomic distortion dictates route."** If your pelvic outlet is physically compromised by residual fibroids—even small ones—you cannot rely on standard partograms predicting progress because progress won't happen mechanically regardless of contraction strength or dilation speed once head meets bone/mass blockage!
