Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 3: ## Management Strategies for Recurrent Episodes and Therapeutic Dilemmas

The clinical management of this patient was complicated by a multiphasic course characterized by four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock occurring within a 24-hour window. The initial stabilization in the emergency department utilized standard protocols, including a 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine dose delivered via auto-injector, alongside adjunctive therapies such as clemastine and fluid resuscitation. While the patient initially responded well, the subsequent recurrence of symptoms necessitated a critical re-evaluation of dosing strategies. A significant discrepancy emerged between the administered dose and guideline recommendations; current international guidelines suggest an adult epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, capped at 0.5 mg for individuals weighing over 50 kg. Given this patient's weight of 104 kg, the initial 0.3 mg auto-injector dose represented a substantial underdose. Recognizing that inadequate epinephrine dosing is a known predictor for treatment failure and the need for escalated care, the clinical team transitioned from auto-injectors to manually prepared intramuscular injections of 0.5 mg for the latter two episodes. This adjustment aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that higher initial doses in larger adults correlate with reduced rates of secondary interventions and improved hemodynamic stability during acute anaphylaxis.

As the patient experienced repeated relapses requiring admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), the role of corticosteroids became a focal point of therapeutic decision-making. Historically, corticosteroids were routinely administered with the intent of preventing biphasic or multiphasic reactions by suppressing late-phase inflammatory mediators. In this case, following the first recurrence, the team initiated intravenous prednisone and dexamethasone consistent with institutional protocols derived from older paradigms. However, this intervention highlights a growing controversy in anaphylaxis management; contemporary systematic reviews have failed to demonstrate that corticosteroids reduce the incidence of biphasic reactions or shorten recovery times. Conversely, recent data suggests potential harms associated with steroid use in this context, including increased risks of hyperglycemia and prolonged hospital stays. While the patient received these agents during her acute crisis, modern teaching emphasizes that corticosteroids should not be relied upon as a primary preventive measure against recurrence, nor should they replace prompt epinephrine administration as the cornerstone of therapy.

The severity and frequency of the relapses prompted an evaluation of advanced therapeutic modalities beyond intermittent bolus dosing. Two divergent strategies were considered: initiating a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to preemptively maintain hemodynamic stability versus adopting an expectant approach given the changing nature of subsequent attacks. The decision to avoid continuous infusion was influenced by both resource constraints and clinical judgment regarding risk-benefit ratios; while continuous infusion offers precise titration, it requires invasive central or large-bore venous access and carries risks such as arrhythmias and ischemia if not meticulously monitored. Conversely, close observation revealed a crucial pattern in the clinical course: symptom attenuation across episodes. The initial presentation involved profound hypoxia, cyanosis, and severe hypotension, whereas subsequent recurrences manifested with progressively milder symptoms, eventually resolving with only cutaneous signs and mild dyspnea without cardiovascular collapse. This trajectory suggested that despite the alarming frequency of relapses, the underlying mast cell activation was waning rather than escalating toward refractory shock.

Ultimately, the combination of correcting epinephrine dosing errors and recognizing the self-limiting nature of symptom severity allowed for successful conservative management without resorting to continuous infusions or invasive procedures other than standard monitoring lines established in the ICU. The patient stabilized after approximately 24 hours free from further recurrence, validating a strategy that prioritized accurate drug dosing over aggressive prophylactic measures like steroids or continuous vasopressors in this specific context. This case underscores that while multiphasic anaphylaxis presents unique challenges due to its unpredictability and lack of standardized guidelines for recurrent events, careful assessment of symptom evolution can guide clinicians away from overly aggressive interventions toward tailored support based on actual physiological needs rather than theoretical risks alone.
