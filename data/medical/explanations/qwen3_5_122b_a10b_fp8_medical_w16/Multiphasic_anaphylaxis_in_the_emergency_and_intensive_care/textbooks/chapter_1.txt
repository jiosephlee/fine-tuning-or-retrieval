Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Stabilization of Severe Anaphylaxis

The clinical narrative begins with a woman in her sixties presenting to the emergency department (ED) with a classic, albeit severe, manifestation of anaphylactic shock. Understanding this patient's background is essential for contextualizing the severity of her reaction and the complexity of her management. Her medical history is significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression, managed with a regimen including metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole, and sertraline. Notably, she carries a documented history of allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, having experienced facial swelling after amoxicillin use in adolescence without systemic compromise. This prior localized reaction serves as a crucial historical anchor, indicating a predisposition to hypersensitivity while distinguishing this current event as her first true episode of systemic anaphylaxis. The precipitating event occurred five days after a course of clindamycin for erysipelas; following the development of a rash attributed to clindamycin, her physician switched her therapy to clarithromycin. One hour after ingesting the first 500 mg dose of clarithromycin—a drug to which she had no prior exposure—the patient developed progressive symptoms that rapidly escalated from pruritus and flushing to agitation and a sensation of throat swelling.

Upon arrival at the ED, the patient exhibited signs consistent with Grade III or IV anaphylaxis according to standard classification systems, characterized by simultaneous involvement of the skin/mucosa and cardiovascular/respiratory systems. Physical examination revealed generalized erythema and urticaria covering the entire body alongside facial edema. More critically, her vital signs indicated profound hemodynamic instability and respiratory compromise: non-invasive blood pressure was recorded at 74/50 mm Hg (shock), peripheral oxygen saturation dropped to 88% on room air despite tachypnea at 22 breaths per minute, and she displayed diaphoresis and agitation. Although auscultation was initially normal regarding heart and lung sounds, the presence of hypoxia suggests significant ventilation-perfusion mismatch or upper airway edema not yet palpable as stridor or wheezing. The rapid progression from cutaneous symptoms to circulatory collapse within minutes underscores the fulminant nature of IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation triggered by clarithromycin in this sensitized individual.

Immediate stabilization followed established emergency protocols prioritizing airway management, breathing support, and circulatory restoration. The cornerstone of therapy was the prompt administration of epinephrine via intramuscular (IM) injection using an auto-injector delivering 0.3 mg. While guidelines recommend a dose of 0.5 mg for adults weighing over 50 kg—and this patient weighed 104 kg—the use of available auto-injectors in time-critical scenarios is often favored to minimize delays caused by drawing up medications or overcoming provider hesitation with vasoactive drugs in non-routine settings. This decision highlights a common tension in emergency medicine between ideal pharmacological dosing and practical accessibility during crisis intervention. Concurrently, aggressive supportive measures were initiated: supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min via a non-rebreather mask corrected hypoxia immediately upon administration, while a bolus of 500 mL normal saline addressed vasodilation-induced hypovolemia. To manage cutaneous symptoms specifically, intravenous clemastine (an antihistamine) was administered. Crucially, the offending agent was identified and discontinued immediately; no further doses of clarithromycin were given.

The efficacy of this multimodal approach was evident almost immediately following intervention. Within moments of receiving epinephrine and fluid resuscitation, the patient's blood pressure normalized, resolving the state of shock. Cutaneous manifestations began to recede as facial swelling diminished and generalized redness faded. Supplemental oxygen was successfully weaned off as saturation improved with hemodynamic stability restored by epinephrine's alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction and beta-adrenergic bronchodilation effects coupled with volume expansion from crystalloids. This rapid reversal confirmed the diagnosis of reversible anaphylactic shock responsive to standard first-line therapy rather than refractory shock requiring continuous infusion or advanced airway management at that stage. Following this initial stabilization and confirmation that symptoms had resolved completely upon arrival at the acute internal ward for monitoring, the team anticipated a straightforward recovery; however, this initial success masked the underlying trajectory toward multiphasic recurrence that would define the remainder of her hospital course.
