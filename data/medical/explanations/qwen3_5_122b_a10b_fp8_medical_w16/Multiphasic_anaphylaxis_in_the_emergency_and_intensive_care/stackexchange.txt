Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Based on the patient's presentation of recurrent anaphylactic shock separated by an asymptomatic interval without re-exposure to the trigger, how do you differentiate 'multiphasic anaphylaxis' from 'refractory anaphylaxis' and 'biphasic anaphylaxis'?

This is a classic distinction that hinges entirely on the temporal relationship between the resolution of symptoms and the need for therapeutic intervention. Let us look closely at the definitions as they apply to our patient's course with clarithromycin-induced anaphylaxis.

**Refractory anaphylaxis** describes a scenario where the initial reaction fails to respond adequately to appropriate therapy, specifically epinephrine. In other words, the patient remains in a state of shock or severe compromise *without* ever achieving clinical stability. Our patient did not fit this definition; she demonstrated a "good response" to the initial epinephrine dose, with normalization of blood pressure and resolution of cutaneous and respiratory symptoms. Because she achieved complete clinical remission between events, we cannot label her condition as refractory.

**Biphasic anaphylaxis** is defined by a recurrence of symptoms after an initial complete resolution, occurring without re-exposure to the allergen, typically within 24 hours but occasionally extending longer. Crucially, biphasic implies exactly *two* phases: the initial event and one subsequent recurrence. While our patient certainly experienced a recurrence, her clinical trajectory involved **four distinct episodes** of anaphylactic shock separated by asymptomatic intervals over a 12-hour period. Each episode resolved with epinephrine before recurring again. Therefore, limiting her diagnosis to "biphasic" would be clinically inaccurate and fail to capture the severity and complexity of her repeated decompensations.

We classify this case as **multiphasic anaphylaxis**. This term is reserved for patients who experience multiple recurrences (more than two) of anaphylactic symptoms following an initial event that had completely resolved, all in the absence of further antigen exposure. As highlighted in our discussion regarding this case, multiphasic courses present a unique management challenge because they defy standard observation windows and require repeated administrations of epinephrine despite adequate prior responses. The underlying pathophysiology likely involves a hyper-reactive mast cell population; indeed, our patient's elevated baseline tryptase and hepatomegaly raised suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, which may explain this propensity for recurrent mediator release even after clearance of the triggering drug.

In summary: If there is no response to epinephrine at all, it is refractory. If there is one recurrence after resolution, it is biphasic. When there are multiple cycles of resolution followed by recurrence—as seen in this woman's four distinct shocks—we must diagnose multiphasic anaphylaxis to ensure appropriate monitoring intensity and anticipate the need for repeated rescue therapy or potentially continuous infusion strategies in future similar cases.

### [Management] In this case, the patient received 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors for her first two episodes. Given her weight of 104 kg, what is the discrepancy between the administered dose and current guideline recommendations for adult dosing, and what impact might this underdosing have had on the clinical course?

In this case of multiphasic anaphylaxis, the administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine to a 104 kg patient represents a significant deviation from current guideline recommendations regarding weight-based dosing. Current international guidelines, including those from the World Allergy Organization and the Resuscitation Council UK, recommend an initial intramuscular epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, capped at a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults. For this patient weighing 104 kg, the calculated ideal dose would be 1.04 mg; however, adhering to the established ceiling, the correct therapeutic dose should have been **0.5 mg**. By utilizing a 0.3 mg auto-injector—a device often standardized for pediatric use or smaller adults—the patient received only 60% of the recommended maximum adult dose.

This underdosing likely contributed to the severity and complexity of her clinical course. As noted in the discussion of this case report, suboptimal initial dosing may result in incomplete reversal of vasodilation and bronchoconstriction, necessitating repeated administrations to achieve hemodynamic stability. In this patient's trajectory, the initial 0.3 mg doses failed to prevent immediate recurrence, leading to four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock within 12 hours. Only after escalating to appropriately sized manually prepared doses of **0.5 mg** did the frequency and intensity of subsequent relapses diminish. This aligns with emerging data suggesting that patients receiving inadequate initial epinephrine doses require more frequent rescue interventions and higher levels of care escalation compared to those treated with guideline-concordant dosing from the outset. While this patient's underlying susceptibility may have been influenced by undiagnosed systemic mastocytosis, ensuring adequate initial drug delivery remains a critical modifiable factor in preventing protracted or refractory anaphylactic courses in larger adults.

### [Clinical Pearls] While corticosteroids were administered in this case with the intent of preventing further relapses, what does current high-level evidence suggest regarding their efficacy in preventing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis?

Current high-level evidence indicates that corticosteroids do **not** effectively prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis. Although corticosteroids were traditionally administered for this purpose—as seen in our patient's case where prednisone and dexamethasone were initiated after the first recurrence to mitigate further relapses—recent systematic reviews and observational data have failed to demonstrate a protective effect.

Specifically, a landmark 2017 systematic review examining 22 studies found that only one study supported the use of corticosteroids, while 21 studies showed no evidence that they reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Conversely, emerging data suggests potential harm; a 2019 study noted an association between prehospital corticosteroid administration and an increased likelihood of subsequent hospital or ICU admission, possibly reflecting confounding by severity or steroid-related adverse events. Consequently, contemporary guidelines have shifted away from recommending routine corticosteroid use for the prevention of biphasic anaphylaxis.

In this case, despite the administration of intravenous corticosteroids, the patient experienced three additional episodes of anaphylaxis within 12 hours. While her symptoms eventually resolved spontaneously over 24 hours, the clinical course underscores that corticosteroids did not abort the multiphasic pattern. The cornerstone of managing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis remains prompt recognition and repeated administration of intramuscular epinephrine upon symptom recurrence, coupled with extended observation periods rather than reliance on anti-inflammatory prophylaxis.

### [Pathophysiology] The patient exhibited elevated baseline tryptase levels and hepatomegaly alongside severe anaphylaxis. What underlying condition should be strongly suspected in patients with a history of severe or recurrent anaphylaxis and elevated baseline tryptase, even if formal diagnostic criteria are not fully met?

In patients presenting with severe or recurrent anaphylaxis, particularly those exhibiting "multiphasic" courses unresponsive to standard observation windows, the combination of elevated baseline serum tryptase and organomegaly—specifically hepatomegaly—should immediately raise suspicion for **systemic mastocytosis**.

While the formal diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis requires meeting specific World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, such as multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone marrow biopsies or detection of the *KIT* D816V mutation, clinical vigilance must extend beyond strict criterion fulfillment. As illustrated in our case report, a patient with multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin demonstrated persistently elevated baseline tryptase levels and hepatomegaly on ultrasound. Although she possessed only two minor WHO criteria and lacked sufficient tissue for major criterion assessment, the constellation of severe mediator-release events, elevated baseline tryptase (indicating increased mast cell burden rather than just acute activation), and hepatic involvement strongly pointed toward an underlying myeloproliferative disorder of mast cells.

The pathophysiological rationale lies in the expanded pool of neoplastic mast cells characteristic of systemic mastocytosis. Unlike typical IgE-mediated allergies where mast cell numbers are normal but hyper-responsive, these patients possess a higher basal load of unstable cells prone to spontaneous or triggered degranulation. This explains why such patients are disproportionately susceptible to severe anaphylaxis and why they may experience recurrent episodes (biphasic or multiphasic) that defy standard prophylactic measures like corticosteroids. Therefore, in any adult with unexplained severe anaphylaxis and a baseline tryptase level exceeding 20 ng/mL—especially when accompanied by signs of extracutaneous organ infiltration like hepatosplenomegaly—a workup for systemic mastocytosis is mandatory, even if definitive histologic proof is initially elusive. Early recognition is crucial not only for prognostication but for guiding long-term management and avoiding unnecessary allergen challenges.

### [Workup] According to best practices for confirming anaphylaxis via serum biomarkers, describe the optimal timing for obtaining serial tryptase levels relative to symptom onset and resolution.

In the evaluation of anaphylaxis, serum tryptase serves as the gold-standard biomarker for confirming mast cell activation, yet its utility is entirely dependent on strict adherence to temporal sampling windows. As illustrated in our case of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where diagnostic clarity was complicated by missed opportunities for early sampling, the optimal strategy requires a multiphasic approach rather than a single measurement.

First, an initial sample must be drawn as soon as feasible after the onset of symptoms, prioritizing immediate stabilization with epinephrine over phlebotomy. Crucially, this should be followed by a second sample 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset—though never exceeding 4 hours—to capture the physiological peak of serum tryptase release. Finally, a third sample is mandatory at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish the patient's baseline tryptase level. The diagnostic criterion for confirming anaphylaxis relies on demonstrating that the acute level exceeds the baseline by either 20% plus 2 ng/mL or simply by a significant absolute elevation depending on the specific guideline interpretation; without the baseline comparison, distinguishing acute mast cell degranulation from chronic elevated states (such as in suspected systemic mastocytosis) becomes impossible.

In our patient's case, this protocol was unfortunately breached; tryptase levels were not obtained during the initial episodes but were only measured during the third recurrence and subsequently at follow-up. While these later measurements confirmed elevated levels consistent with underlying mastocytosis pathology, they failed to provide the definitive kinetic profile needed to confirm the diagnosis acutely or rule out other etiologies with certainty during the active crisis. Therefore, to maximize diagnostic yield in future presentations of biphasic or multiphasic reactions, clinicians must anticipate these recurrences and ensure serial sampling aligns with both the acute peaks and the post-resolution baseline window.

### [Management] When managing a patient with multiphasic anaphylaxis who has responded adequately to repeated intramuscular epinephrine but continues to recur every few hours, what are the two primary controversial management strategies discussed in expert literature regarding the transition from intermittent rescue therapy to continuous infusion versus expectant management?

In the management of multiphasic anaphylaxis where a patient responds adequately to repeated intramuscular epinephrine yet experiences recurrent episodes, the transition from intermittent rescue therapy to a definitive strategy presents a significant therapeutic dilemma. As illustrated in our case of the elderly woman with clarithromycin-induced multiphasic anaphylaxis and suspected systemic mastocytosis, the literature highlights two primary, albeit controversial, management pathways when standard bolus dosing fails to sustain remission.

The first strategy involves the initiation of a **continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion**. This approach shifts the paradigm from reactive rescue to proactive suppression, aiming to maintain steady-state plasma concentrations of catecholamines sufficient to stabilize mast cells and vascular tone preemptively. In our case, this was seriously considered as a means to "pre-emptively treat" rather than wait for hemodynamic collapse. The theoretical advantage is the avoidance of the peaks and troughs associated with intermittent dosing, which may allow for sub-therapeutic windows where mediator release can reignite symptoms. However, this carries inherent risks of tachyphylaxis, arrhythmias, and ischemia, particularly in older patients with comorbidities like hypertension and diabetes, all without robust evidence from randomized trials guiding its use in this specific context.

The contrasting strategy is **expectant management**, relying on close monitoring while anticipating spontaneous resolution. This approach is grounded in the observation that the intensity of recurrent episodes often wanes over time—a phenomenon noted in our patient whose final episode manifested only with cutaneous symptoms and mild dyspnea compared to the profound shock seen initially. Proponents argue that because multiphasic reactions are self-limiting and carry low mortality rates if monitored closely, exposing a stable patient to the toxicity of continuous vasopressors may constitute overtreatment. The decision rests on weighing the risk of a catastrophic breakthrough reaction against the iatrogenic burden of aggressive pharmacotherapy.

Ultimately, as demonstrated by our patient's outcome where neither intervention became necessary after 24 hours of stability, there is currently no consensus guideline favoring one approach over the other for multiphasic cases. The choice must be individualized based on the trajectory of symptom severity, the frequency of recurrences, and the clinician's assessment of whether the patient's condition is trending toward exhaustion or impending deterioration.

### [Clinical Reasoning] This patient had a history of a mild reaction to amoxicillin years ago and a local reaction to a wasp sting. How does a history of isolated cutaneous reactions or mild allergies influence the risk stratification for developing severe or multiphasic anaphylaxis upon subsequent exposure?

In this case, the patient's history of a mild, isolated cutaneous reaction to amoxicillin and a localized response to a wasp sting serves as a classic example of why past "mild" allergies cannot reliably predict the severity or multiphasic nature of future anaphylactic events. The clinical reasoning here hinges on the distinction between the *threshold* for sensitization and the *magnitude* of the mast cell response upon re-exposure.

While it is intuitive to assume that a history of only cutaneous symptoms implies a low-risk profile, this assumption is dangerously misleading. As demonstrated in our case, a patient with a remote history of limited facial swelling to beta-lactams and a local wasp sting reaction experienced her first-ever episode of life-threatening anaphylaxis with cardiovascular collapse upon exposure to clarithromycin. More critically, she exhibited a rare **multiphasic course**, suffering four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock within 12 hours despite initial stabilization. This underscores that prior mild reactions do not confer protection against severe systemic decompensation nor do they serve as negative predictors for biphasic or multiphasic recurrences.

Current literature and this case highlight that reliable predictors for biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis remain elusive. Factors such as delayed epinephrine administration, severity of the initial presentation (e.g., hypotension), and the need for multiple epinephrine doses are associated with higher risks, but these are features of the *acute* event rather than historical antecedents like mild hives or localized stings. The pathophysiology suggests that once mast cell activation reaches a critical threshold—potentially influenced by underlying conditions like undiagnosed systemic mastocytosis, which we suspected in this patient due to elevated baseline tryptase—the clinical trajectory becomes unpredictable regardless of prior allergy history.

Therefore, risk stratification must rely on the acuity and severity of the *current* presentation rather than historical "mildness." We must observe all patients presenting with significant anaphylaxis closely, particularly those with hypotension or respiratory compromise, because even those with benign allergy histories can progress to multiphasic shock. In this patient's scenario, assuming low risk based on her adolescent amoxicillin reaction would have been catastrophic; instead, her aggressive monitoring allowed us to manage four separate relapses successfully. Ultimately, every episode of anaphylaxis carries its own unique risk profile that supersedes past medical history regarding allergy severity.

### [Pathophysiology] Despite the theoretical rationale for using corticosteroids to blunt late-phase inflammatory responses, why have recent guidelines shifted away from recommending them routinely in acute anaphylaxis management?

Despite the historical rationale that corticosteroids might mitigate the late-phase inflammatory cascade and prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, contemporary guidelines have shifted away from recommending their routine use because robust evidence fails to demonstrate a reduction in recurrence rates. As illustrated in our case of a woman with multiphasic anaphylaxis secondary to clarithromycin, the administration of intravenous prednisone and dexamethasone occurred after the first recurrence, yet it did not halt the subsequent relapses; the patient continued to experience four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock despite steroid coverage. This clinical reality aligns with broader systematic reviews indicating that corticosteroids do not shorten the duration of acute symptoms nor prevent biphasic reactions. The pharmacokinetics of glucocorticoids simply cannot match the rapid onset required to arrest immediate hypersensitivity pathways, as their anti-inflammatory effects take hours to manifest—a timeline incompatible with the urgent stabilization needs of anaphylaxis. Furthermore, recent data suggests potential harms associated with routine steroid use, including increased rates of hospital admission and complications related to hyperglycemia or fluid retention, particularly in patients like ours with comorbidities such as diabetes. Therefore, the cornerstone of management remains prompt, appropriately dosed intramuscular epinephrine and vigilant observation, rather than reliance on adjunctive therapies that offer unproven benefits and carry avoidable risks.
