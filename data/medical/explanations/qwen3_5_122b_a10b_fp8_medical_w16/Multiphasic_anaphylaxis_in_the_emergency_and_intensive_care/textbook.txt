Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Stabilization of Severe Anaphylaxis

The clinical narrative begins with a woman in her sixties presenting to the emergency department (ED) with a classic, albeit severe, manifestation of anaphylactic shock. Understanding this patient's background is essential for contextualizing the severity of her reaction and the complexity of her management. Her medical history is significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression, managed with a regimen including metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole, and sertraline. Notably, she carries a documented history of allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, having experienced facial swelling after amoxicillin use in adolescence without systemic compromise. This prior localized reaction serves as a crucial historical anchor, indicating a predisposition to hypersensitivity while distinguishing this current event as her first true episode of systemic anaphylaxis. The precipitating event occurred five days after a course of clindamycin for erysipelas; following the development of a rash attributed to clindamycin, her physician switched her therapy to clarithromycin. One hour after ingesting the first 500 mg dose of clarithromycin—a drug to which she had no prior exposure—the patient developed progressive symptoms that rapidly escalated from pruritus and flushing to agitation and a sensation of throat swelling.

Upon arrival at the ED, the patient exhibited signs consistent with Grade III or IV anaphylaxis according to standard classification systems, characterized by simultaneous involvement of the skin/mucosa and cardiovascular/respiratory systems. Physical examination revealed generalized erythema and urticaria covering the entire body alongside facial edema. More critically, her vital signs indicated profound hemodynamic instability and respiratory compromise: non-invasive blood pressure was recorded at 74/50 mm Hg (shock), peripheral oxygen saturation dropped to 88% on room air despite tachypnea at 22 breaths per minute, and she displayed diaphoresis and agitation. Although auscultation was initially normal regarding heart and lung sounds, the presence of hypoxia suggests significant ventilation-perfusion mismatch or upper airway edema not yet palpable as stridor or wheezing. The rapid progression from cutaneous symptoms to circulatory collapse within minutes underscores the fulminant nature of IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation triggered by clarithromycin in this sensitized individual.

Immediate stabilization followed established emergency protocols prioritizing airway management, breathing support, and circulatory restoration. The cornerstone of therapy was the prompt administration of epinephrine via intramuscular (IM) injection using an auto-injector delivering 0.3 mg. While guidelines recommend a dose of 0.5 mg for adults weighing over 50 kg—and this patient weighed 104 kg—the use of available auto-injectors in time-critical scenarios is often favored to minimize delays caused by drawing up medications or overcoming provider hesitation with vasoactive drugs in non-routine settings. This decision highlights a common tension in emergency medicine between ideal pharmacological dosing and practical accessibility during crisis intervention. Concurrently, aggressive supportive measures were initiated: supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min via a non-rebreather mask corrected hypoxia immediately upon administration, while a bolus of 500 mL normal saline addressed vasodilation-induced hypovolemia. To manage cutaneous symptoms specifically, intravenous clemastine (an antihistamine) was administered. Crucially, the offending agent was identified and discontinued immediately; no further doses of clarithromycin were given.

The efficacy of this multimodal approach was evident almost immediately following intervention. Within moments of receiving epinephrine and fluid resuscitation, the patient's blood pressure normalized, resolving the state of shock. Cutaneous manifestations began to recede as facial swelling diminished and generalized redness faded. Supplemental oxygen was successfully weaned off as saturation improved with hemodynamic stability restored by epinephrine's alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction and beta-adrenergic bronchodilation effects coupled with volume expansion from crystalloids. This rapid reversal confirmed the diagnosis of reversible anaphylactic shock responsive to standard first-line therapy rather than refractory shock requiring continuous infusion or advanced airway management at that stage. Following this initial stabilization and confirmation that symptoms had resolved completely upon arrival at the acute internal ward for monitoring, the team anticipated a straightforward recovery; however, this initial success masked the underlying trajectory toward multiphasic recurrence that would define the remainder of her hospital course.

## The Challenge of Multiphasic Courses: Definition and Diagnostic Reasoning

The clinical trajectory of anaphylaxis is not invariably linear; while the classic presentation involves a single, self-limiting, or successfully treated episode, a distinct subset of patients experiences a recurrence of symptoms after an initial period of stability. Understanding the nuances between monophasic, biphasic, and multiphasic courses is fundamental to managing complex cases like that of the 60-year-old woman presenting with clarithromycin-induced anaphylaxis. Monophasic anaphylaxis describes a singular reaction that resolves completely without recurrence. In contrast, biphasic anaphylaxis is characterized by a recurrence of symptoms following complete resolution of the initial reaction and a symptom-free interval, occurring in the absence of re-exposure to the triggering allergen. This case report illustrates an even more complex variant: multiphasic anaphylaxis, defined by multiple distinct recurrences of anaphylactic shock separated by periods of clinical improvement. The distinction is critical because it dictates the duration and intensity of monitoring required; whereas a monophasic patient may require short-term observation, a patient exhibiting multiphasic behavior demands extended intensive care until the cycle of recurrence definitively ceases.

Differentiating true multiphasic relapse from other phenomena presents a significant diagnostic challenge for clinicians in the emergency and intensive care settings. The primary differential consideration is refractory anaphylaxis, where symptoms persist continuously despite adequate administration of epinephrine and supportive care, representing a failure to stabilize rather than a true "relapse" after stabilization. Another confounding factor is ongoing allergen absorption; in cases involving oral medications or ingested foods, residual antigen may continue to enter systemic circulation long after initial treatment, mimicking a biphasic pattern. In this specific case, careful timeline analysis ruled out these alternatives. The patient experienced four discrete episodes: the initial presentation in the Emergency Department (ED), followed by three subsequent relapses within 12 hours of admission to the acute ward and ICU. Crucially, between these events, the patient achieved full clinical resolution with normalized hemodynamics and clearing cutaneous symptoms before deteriorating again. Furthermore, clarithromycin was discontinued immediately upon suspicion of allergy after only a single 500 mg dose was consumed; thus, continued ingestion was excluded as a cause for the recurring shock states.

The epidemiology of these recurrent courses adds another layer of uncertainty to clinical decision-making. Reported incidence rates for biphasic anaphylaxis vary wildly across literature, ranging from as low as 0.4% to as high as 23.3%, with most data clustering between 4% and 6%. This wide disparity stems largely from inconsistent definitions among different guidelines regarding what constitutes "resolution" versus "transient response," as well as variations in observation durations studied. For instance, while some protocols define biphasic reactions based on recurrence within 24 hours others cite cases occurring up to 78 hours post-initial exposure. Multiphasic anaphylaxis represents an even rarer phenomenon within this spectrum, lacking robust statistical characterization due to its scarcity in large-scale datasets. Consequently, there are no universally accepted prediction models or biomarkers that reliably identify which patients will progress from a single episode to a multiphasic course at presentation.

This lack of predictive precision creates substantial difficulties in risk stratification and resource allocation. Clinicians must balance the need for aggressive monitoring against healthcare constraints without clear indicators of who requires prolonged ICU stays versus those safe for standard wards after brief observation. Current literature suggests potential risk factors such as delayed epinephrine administration during the initial event, severity of the first presentation (e.g., hypotension requiring vasopressors), need for multiple doses of epinephrine initially, or a history of prior biphasic reactions. In this case report's patient, several high-risk features were evident: she presented with profound hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxia requiring immediate resuscitation with epinephrine and fluids; she required repeated doses of epinephrine (four total) across distinct episodes; and she exhibited severe cardiovascular instability during each relapse despite initial responsiveness to therapy. These factors collectively signaled a high probability for complicated disease progression, justifying her transfer to the ICU after the first relapse rather than remaining on the general ward where telemetry was unavailable. Ultimately, recognizing that this patient's course deviated from standard recovery patterns allowed for escalated surveillance that likely prevented fatal outcomes during subsequent silent phases before symptom recurrence became apparent again.

## Management Strategies for Recurrent Episodes and Therapeutic Dilemmas

The clinical management of this patient was complicated by a multiphasic course characterized by four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock occurring within a 24-hour window. The initial stabilization in the emergency department utilized standard protocols, including a 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine dose delivered via auto-injector, alongside adjunctive therapies such as clemastine and fluid resuscitation. While the patient initially responded well, the subsequent recurrence of symptoms necessitated a critical re-evaluation of dosing strategies. A significant discrepancy emerged between the administered dose and guideline recommendations; current international guidelines suggest an adult epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, capped at 0.5 mg for individuals weighing over 50 kg. Given this patient's weight of 104 kg, the initial 0.3 mg auto-injector dose represented a substantial underdose. Recognizing that inadequate epinephrine dosing is a known predictor for treatment failure and the need for escalated care, the clinical team transitioned from auto-injectors to manually prepared intramuscular injections of 0.5 mg for the latter two episodes. This adjustment aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that higher initial doses in larger adults correlate with reduced rates of secondary interventions and improved hemodynamic stability during acute anaphylaxis.

As the patient experienced repeated relapses requiring admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), the role of corticosteroids became a focal point of therapeutic decision-making. Historically, corticosteroids were routinely administered with the intent of preventing biphasic or multiphasic reactions by suppressing late-phase inflammatory mediators. In this case, following the first recurrence, the team initiated intravenous prednisone and dexamethasone consistent with institutional protocols derived from older paradigms. However, this intervention highlights a growing controversy in anaphylaxis management; contemporary systematic reviews have failed to demonstrate that corticosteroids reduce the incidence of biphasic reactions or shorten recovery times. Conversely, recent data suggests potential harms associated with steroid use in this context, including increased risks of hyperglycemia and prolonged hospital stays. While the patient received these agents during her acute crisis, modern teaching emphasizes that corticosteroids should not be relied upon as a primary preventive measure against recurrence, nor should they replace prompt epinephrine administration as the cornerstone of therapy.

The severity and frequency of the relapses prompted an evaluation of advanced therapeutic modalities beyond intermittent bolus dosing. Two divergent strategies were considered: initiating a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to preemptively maintain hemodynamic stability versus adopting an expectant approach given the changing nature of subsequent attacks. The decision to avoid continuous infusion was influenced by both resource constraints and clinical judgment regarding risk-benefit ratios; while continuous infusion offers precise titration, it requires invasive central or large-bore venous access and carries risks such as arrhythmias and ischemia if not meticulously monitored. Conversely, close observation revealed a crucial pattern in the clinical course: symptom attenuation across episodes. The initial presentation involved profound hypoxia, cyanosis, and severe hypotension, whereas subsequent recurrences manifested with progressively milder symptoms, eventually resolving with only cutaneous signs and mild dyspnea without cardiovascular collapse. This trajectory suggested that despite the alarming frequency of relapses, the underlying mast cell activation was waning rather than escalating toward refractory shock.

Ultimately, the combination of correcting epinephrine dosing errors and recognizing the self-limiting nature of symptom severity allowed for successful conservative management without resorting to continuous infusions or invasive procedures other than standard monitoring lines established in the ICU. The patient stabilized after approximately 24 hours free from further recurrence, validating a strategy that prioritized accurate drug dosing over aggressive prophylactic measures like steroids or continuous vasopressors in this specific context. This case underscores that while multiphasic anaphylaxis presents unique challenges due to its unpredictability and lack of standardized guidelines for recurrent events, careful assessment of symptom evolution can guide clinicians away from overly aggressive interventions toward tailored support based on actual physiological needs rather than theoretical risks alone.
