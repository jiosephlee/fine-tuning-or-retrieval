Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The Underdose Trap: Why 0.3 mg Epinephrine May Be Failing Our Adult Patients

We love a good "zebra" in the ER. Usually, that means spotting a rare disease like Kounis syndrome or a bizarre toxin ingestion. But sometimes, the most dangerous thing isn't the obscure pathology hiding in the chart—it's the medication sitting right next to your hand that you've been using wrong for decades.

Consider this scenario: A woman in her 60s walks into your ED with classic anaphylactic shock after taking clarithromycin. Hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxic (SpO2 88%), with generalized urticaria and airway anxiety. You act fast. You grab an epinephrine auto-injector, slap it on her thigh, and give fluids and antihistamines. She responds beautifully. BP normalizes, lungs clear out, and she looks ready for discharge... until she doesn't.

Within 12 hours, she crashes *again*. And again. And again. Four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock in a single admission, requiring repeated epinephrine boluses and eventual ICU transfer. Was this a case of "multiphasic anaphylaxis" driven by a hidden mastocytosis? Perhaps. But look closer at the treatment algorithm, and you'll find a far more preventable culprit lurking in plain sight: **underdosing.**

This case report from Schneider et al. serves as a stark reminder of the disconnect between guideline-recommended epinephrine dosing and the reality of what we actually carry in our crash carts and auto-injector pens.

### The Math Doesn't Add Up

Let's talk numbers, because they don't lie. Current international guidelines (including WAO and Resuscitation Council UK) are remarkably consistent: for an adult weighing over 50 kg, the recommended intramuscular (IM) dose of epinephrine is **0.5 mg**.

Yet, walk into almost any pharmacy or check your stockroom auto-injectors, and you will predominantly find devices delivering **0.3 mg**. In many jurisdictions, 0.3 mg is the standard "adult" dose sold off the shelf, while 0.5 mg devices are either unavailable or reserved for specific markets (like Japan or parts of Europe).

In the case we're dissecting today, our patient weighed **104 kg**. She received two doses of 0.3 mg via auto-injector during her initial presentation and first relapse before finally receiving appropriately scaled 0.5 mg manual IM doses for her subsequent crises. While she eventually stabilized on higher doses, she endured three unnecessary cycles of shock solely because we defaulted to a device designed for smaller adults or children weighing <30 kg (depending on regional labeling).

Why does this matter? Because anaphylaxis is not just about stopping hives; it's about reversing profound vasodilation and bronchoconstriction immediately to prevent cardiac arrest or irreversible airway edema under-dosing delays that reversal. A recent retrospective study cited in the discussion highlights exactly this: patients who initially received 0.3 mg required significantly more rescue interventions (additional injections) compared to those who got the full 0.5 mg upfront. We aren't just giving them "less drug"; we are actively prolonging their time in extremis.

### The "Safe Enough" Fallacy

There is a pervasive cultural inertia around epinephrine dosing that suggests "some is better than none," or that 0.3 mg is "safe enough" for everyone to avoid tachyarrhythmias in healthy hearts while waiting for paramedics to arrive with bigger guns in pre-hospital settings might make sense—but inside the Emergency Department? It shouldn't be acceptable practice anymore if alternatives exist or can be drawn up manually without delay.

The authors of this case note that while auto-injectors are fantastic for reducing hesitation among staff who rarely handle vasoactive drugs (and they should be!), relying on them exclusively creates a ceiling effect on efficacy for larger patients or severe reactions where every milligram counts toward hemodynamic stability. When you have a patient crashing with hypoxia and hypotension, do you really want to wait another 15 minutes while they metabolize a sub-therapeutic dose before reaching therapeutic levels? Or do you draw up that extra syringe now?

Furthermore, this case highlights the difficulty distinguishing between true multiphasic/anaphylaxis recurrence versus simply inadequate initial resuscitation masquerading as recurrence due to waning drug effect from underdosing. If you never gave enough epi to begin with, how do you know if it wasn't just wearing off too quickly?

### The Mastocytosis Red Herring?

To be fair to our patient's complexity, she did turn out to have markers suggestive of systemic mastocytosis (elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly), which certainly predisposed her to severe reactions regardless of dosing nuances. However, even patients with mast cell disorders require adequate receptor occupancy by agonists like epinephrine to survive their own storms under-dosing leaves them vulnerable no matter how hyper-reactive their mast cells are trying to get!

It is tempting to blame the biology—the "rare mastocytosis"—for why she kept crashing four times over two days but let us not forget that every single time she crashed after stabilization involved a return to sub-optimal hemodynamics likely exacerbated by insufficient adrenergic support earlier in her course! Had she received robust initial dosing perhaps some of those secondary peaks could have been blunted entirely! Instead we watched her cycle through shock repeatedly because we stuck rigidly to whatever came out of the box rather than calculating based on weight!

### Take-Home Pearls: Stop Guessing Your Dose

So what should change tomorrow morning when your next allergic storm rolls through triage? Here is your checklist for breaking free from the underdose trap:

*   **Know Your Weight Threshold:** For any adult patient >50 kg (roughly anyone over ~110 lbs), aim for **0.5 mg** IM epinephrine as your first-line intervention unless contraindicated by specific cardiac history where benefits still outweigh risks! Do not default blindly to 03-mg pens unless absolutely nothing else is available!
*   **Manual Syringes Are Your Friend:** Don't fear drawing up epinephrine manually! It takes seconds longer than clicking open an injector but ensures precise dosing tailored to size severity! If you have access ampules vials mixers use them especially when dealing with large-bodied patients or refractory cases!
*   **Re-evaluate Auto-Injectors:** While convenient pre-hospital tools limit utility indoors where equipment flexibility exists consider keeping dedicated vial/syringe setups readily accessible alongside injectors specifically sized correctly!
*   **Don't Blame Biology Alone Before Checking Pharmacology:** Before attributing recurrent symptoms purely to complex pathologies like biphasic reactions mastocytosis ensure initial resuscitation was truly maximal first rule out iatrogenic undertreatment before diving deep into rare diagnoses!
*   **Advocate Locally:** Push your hospital pharmacy leadership formulary committees toward stocking appropriate concentrations volumes ensuring staff aren't forced into corners choosing between convenience correctness!
