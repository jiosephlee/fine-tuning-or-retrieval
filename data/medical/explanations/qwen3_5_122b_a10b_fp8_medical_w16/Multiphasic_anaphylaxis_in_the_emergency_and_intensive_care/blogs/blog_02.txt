Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Beyond Biphasic: Navigating the Diagnostic Black Hole of Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

We all know the drill for anaphylaxis: give epi, fix the airway, push fluids, and watch them for a few hours. If they stabilize, we send them home with an EpiPen and a stern warning about avoidance. But what happens when the "biphasic" rulebook falls apart? What if your patient doesn't just bounce back once after resolving their initial crash, but cycles through *four* distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock over 12 hours?

This isn't just a theoretical nightmare; it's exactly what happened to a woman in her 60s recently described in a fascinating case report from Schneider et al. She presented with classic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin, responded beautifully to epinephrine, and then promptly crashed again—not once, but three more times. This case of **multiphasic anaphylaxis** exposes a massive gap in our guidelines. While biphasic reactions (recurrence within 24 hours) get some attention, multiphasic courses remain a diagnostic black hole with zero clear management algorithms.

### The Case That Broke the Protocol

Let's walk through this rollercoaster. Our patient arrived hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxic (88%), and covered in urticaria shortly after taking her first dose of clarithromycin. Standard care kicked in immediately: IM epinephrine (0.3 mg via auto-injector), antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids. She stabilized rapidly. Vital signs normalized, swelling receded, and she was moved to the acute internal ward for observation.

Then came the twist. Within 12 hours of admission, she didn't just have a "relapse"; she plunged back into anaphylactic shock. And she did it again. And again. In total, she suffered **four separate episodes** requiring repeated IM epinephrine doses (escalating from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to manually drawn 0.5 mg doses).

Here is where it gets truly tricky: the team faced a genuine therapeutic dilemma with no playbook to consult. They debated two extreme paths:
1.  **Aggressive Preemption:** Start a continuous IV epinephrine infusion immediately to keep mast cells suppressed before the next crash hits.
2.  **Expectant Management:** Ride out the storm with repeat boluses only when symptoms return, noting that each successive episode seemed slightly less severe than the last (from cyanotic shock down to just dyspnea and rash).

They chose the latter—a "wait-and-see" approach—and luckily for the patient, she never crashed again after that fourth episode and was discharged three days later without further incident. But was that luck or good medicine? We don't actually know because there is no literature telling us which path saves lives in multiphasic scenarios.

### The Diagnosis Conundrum: Is It Mastocytosis?

Once the dust settled on this chaotic admission, the allergology team dug deeper into *why* this happened so violently and repeatedly. The suspicion landed heavily on **systemic mastocytosis**. While she didn't meet full WHO diagnostic criteria (likely due to sampling limitations in her bone marrow biopsy), she ticked enough boxes to raise serious alarms: elevated baseline tryptase levels that stayed high even between attacks, hepatomegaly on ultrasound, and a history suggestive of neuropsychiatric involvement (depression/memory issues).

This is a crucial teaching point regarding diagnostics in severe anaphylaxis: **Tryptase timing matters.** In this case, tryptase wasn't checked until hour 1 of the *third* episode because it wasn't part of their initial workup for the first crash—a missed opportunity by modern standards. To confirm mastocytosis or characterize anaphylaxis properly, you need serial draws: one ASAP upon presentation (without delaying treatment), one at peak levels (1–2 hours post-symptom onset), and one baseline draw >24 hours after full resolution. Missing these windows leaves you guessing whether you're dealing with simple IgE-mediated allergy or a hyper-reactive mast cell disorder lurking underneath.

### The Dosing Reality Check: Are Your Auto-Injectors Under-dosing Adults?

One glaring detail in this case screams out for discussion among emergency physicians globally: **epinephrine dosing.** The patient's first two rescue doses came from auto-injectors containing only **0.3 mg**. Yet, current guidelines recommend **0.5 mg** for adults weighing over 50 kg (which she did at 104 kg).

While auto-injectors are fantastic for reducing hesitation in pre-hospital settings or among staff unfamiliar with drawing up pressors, relying on them can lead to critical under-dosing in large adults during severe shock events like this one. Recent data suggests that starting with 0.3 mg increases the likelihood of needing escalation compared to hitting that target dose of 0 steroids upfront could be counterproductive; mounting evidence suggests they offer no protection against biphasic reactions and may even prolong hospital stays due to side effects like tachycardia or hyperglycemia—complications we definitely don't want in someone already crashing from shock! So why did they get steroids here? Tradition mixed with conflicting institutional protocols between wards and ICUs highlights how much practice still lags behind evidence-based guidelines regarding adjunctive therapies in anaphylaxis management today!

### Take-Home Points

*   **Multiphase vs Refractory:** True multiphasic anaphylysis involves distinct asymptomatic intervals between recurrent episodes without re-exposure; distinguish this carefully from refractory shock (persistent despite epi) or ongoing antigen absorption (e.g., slow-release meds).
*   **Dose Matters:** Do not rely solely on standard adult auto-injectors (often 0.3 mg) for obese or average-sized adults (>50 kg); aim for guideline-recommended doses of **0.5 mg IM** early to avoid suboptimal resuscitation attempts requiring escalation later on!
*   **Steroid Skepticism:** Be wary of reflexively administering corticosteroids; current evidence shows little benefit preventing biphasic/multiphase events while adding significant morbidity risks including prolonged ICU stays/hospitalizations overall!
*   **Think Mastocytosis:** Consider underlying mast cell disorders (like systemic mastocytosis) in patients with unusually severe/recurrent/anomalous presentations—even if formal criteria aren't fully met—to guide long-term management strategies appropriately moving forward!
*   **Data Gap Alert:** There is currently NO consensus algorithm managing multiphase cases specifically; consider enrolling such rare occurrences into registries rather than defaulting purely anecdotal decision-making processes alone going forward henceforth onwards indefinitely...
