Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Mast Cell Disorders Unmasked: When Recurrent Anaphylaxis Signals Systemic Mastocytosis

We've all been there. You treat a textbook case of anaphylaxis, the patient responds beautifully to epinephrine, fluids, and antihistamines, and you breathe a sigh of relief. Then, six hours later, they crash again. It's frustrating, scary for the family, and confusing for the team. We call it "biphasic" anaphylaxis and move them to the ICU. But what happens when they don't just bounce back once? What if they crash *four* times in 24 hours despite aggressive treatment?

That was the reality for a woman in her 60s recently detailed in a fascinating case report by Schneider et al. She presented with severe anaphylactic shock after taking a single dose of clarithromycin. She stabilized, then relapsed three more times over the next 12 hours. Each episode required repeat epinephrine. The severity waned slightly with each recurrence, but the pattern was undeniable: **multiphasic anaphylaxis**.

While biphasic reactions occur in roughly 4–6% of cases, true multiphasic courses are rare beasts that challenge our standard algorithms. In this specific case, the culprit wasn't just "bad luck" or incomplete clearance of the drug; it was likely an underlying **systemic mastocytosis (SM)** masquerading as simple drug allergy. Here is why this distinction matters for your next code blue or observation ward admission.

### The Clinical Trap: Why Standard Care Isn't Always Enough

This patient had a classic setup for confusion. She had a remote history of mild penicillin sensitivity and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, but never full-blown anaphylaxis until now. After developing a rash on clindamycin five days prior, she was switched to clarithromycin—a drug she had never touched—and promptly crashed within an hour.

In the ED, she received 0.3 mg IM epinephrine (via auto-injector), clemastine, oxygen, and fluids. She improved rapidly. But within hours on the ward, she deteriorated again with hypotension and hypoxia. This cycle repeated four times total.

Two things jumped out immediately upon reviewing this case that often get overlooked in the chaos of resuscitation:
1.  **The Epinephrine Dose:** The first two doses were 0.3 mg auto-injectors. For an adult weighing 104 kg (BMI ~33), this is suboptimal dosing compared to the guideline-recommended 0.5 mg (or 0.01 mg/kg). While she responded eventually to escalating doses (switching to manually drawn 0.5 mg IM), under-dosing can prolong instability and complicate the clinical picture of "refractory" vs "relapsing."
2.  **The Missing Baseline:** Crucially, tryptase levels were *not* checked during the initial surge or at peak symptoms (within 1–4 hours). They weren't drawn until after the third episode and then again at discharge (27+ hours later). By missing that acute window relative to baseline establishment during active shock, we lost some immediate diagnostic clarity right at the bedside.

### Unmasking Systemic Mastocytosis

So how do we differentiate a stubborn biphasic reaction from an underlying mast cell disorder? The answer lies in looking past the immediate crisis once the patient is stable enough to investigate.

In this case report, subsequent workup painted a compelling picture of systemic mastocytosis:
*   **Elevated Baseline Tryptase:** Even months after discharge, her tryptase remained chronically elevated (>20 ng/mL is often cited as suspicious for SM).
*   **Hepatomegaly:** Abdominal ultrasound revealed liver enlargement without other obvious causes like heart failure or viral hepatitis—a sign of organ infiltration by mast cells.
*   **"Idiopathic" Features:** Depression and memory issues were noted in her history; neuropsychiatric symptoms are increasingly recognized as part of the SM spectrum due to mediator release affecting CNS function.
*   **Refractoriness/Multiphasic Nature:** The sheer number of relapses without re-exposure suggests a hyper-reactive mast cell pool rather than simple pharmacokinetic redistribution of an allergen.

It is worth noting that while she fit many criteria for SM (high tryptase + organ involvement + symptoms), she technically didn't meet all WHO criteria because a definitive bone marrow biopsy couldn't confirm clonal mast cell markers due to limited sample material ("minor criteria only"). Yet clinically? The diagnosis was highly probable based on probability scores alone (Cleveland Clinic score). If you suspect SM but can't get a perfect biopsy result immediately, do not dismiss it if the clinical phenotype fits: recurrent unexplained anaphylaxis + elevated baseline tryptase + organomegaly = high index of suspicion for SM referral regardless of biopsy ambiguity at first glance! 

> 💡 **Pearl**: Don't let "inconclusive biopsy" stop you from pursuing SM diagnosis if baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL AND there are compatible clinical features like hepatosplenomegaly or recurrent idiopathic anaphylaxis!

### Take-Home Points for Your Next Shift

When faced with recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis—especially when triggers seem absent or reactions disproportionate—keep these pearls front-of-mind:

*   **Dose Aggressively**: Ensure adults receive at least **0.5 mg** IM epinephrine initially if weight >50 kg; relying solely on 0.3 mg auto-injectors risks under-treatment and potential escalation needs later!
*   **Time Your Tryptases Right**: Draw three samples if possible: 
    - Sample #1 ASAP after stabilization  
    - Sample #2 at peak effect (**1–4 hrs** post-onset)  
    - Sample #3 ≥**24 hrs** post-resolution for baseline comparison  
    *Missing these windows makes distinguishing acute spike from chronic elevation nearly impossible.*
    
*   **Suspect Underlying Pathology Early**: Consider systemic mastocytosis in any patient with:
    - Recurrent/anaphylaxis without clear allergen exposure  
    - Elevated baseline serum tryptase (>20 ng/mL)  
    - Associated findings like hepatosplenomegaly or unexplained GI/CNS symptoms
    
*   **Avoid Steroid Myths**: Remember that corticosteroids have *no proven benefit* in preventing biphasic/multiphasic reactions despite decades of tradition—they add side effects without changing outcomes according to modern meta-analyses! Focus on adequate epinephrine dosing instead!

*   **Refer Strategically**: If tryptase remains elevated long-term or episodes recur frequently despite avoidance measures → refer urgently to Allergy/Immunology for consideration of KIT mutation testing/bone marrow biopsy even if initial results are equivocal!  

Multiphasic anaphylaxis forces us out of autopilot mode—it demands we look deeper than just "allergic reaction." Could your next patient be harboring silent mastocytosis waiting to strike again? Keep your eyes open for those subtle clues hidden behind every crash cart moment! 🚑🔬
