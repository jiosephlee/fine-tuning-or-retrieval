Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Morning Report] In the context of acute neuro-Behçet's disease presenting with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, what is the expected frequency of parenchymal brain lesions visible on MRI compared to the frequency of aseptic meningitis findings in the cerebrospinal fluid?

In the classic presentation of acute neuro-Behçet's disease, one typically anticipates meningoencephalitis accompanied by parenchymal brain lesions, most frequently involving the brainstem. However, as illustrated in this case report, the radiological and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings can diverge significantly from this textbook expectation. Specifically, **parenchymal brain lesions are absent in a substantial subset of patients**, whereas **aseptic meningitis findings in the CSF are consistently present** even when imaging is unrevealing.

In this patient, who presented with fever, headache, and hyponatremia due to SIADH, the brain MRI was entirely normal, showing no evidence of the characteristic parenchymal inflammation or brainstem involvement often seen in neuro-Behçet's. Conversely, the CSF analysis definitively demonstrated aseptic meningitis, characterized by mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and elevated protein (0.97 g/L), after infectious and neoplastic etiologies were rigorously excluded. This discrepancy underscores a critical diagnostic principle: **a normal brain MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet's disease.** The discussion within the case report explicitly notes that acute neuro-Behçet's may manifest as "aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions," particularly in the early phases or specific phenotypes where meningeal inflammation predominates over parenchymal necrosis. Therefore, while parenchymal lesions are a hallmark of many cases, their absence should not deter the diagnosis if clinical features (such as concurrent mucocutaneous ulcers) and CSF findings support active CNS inflammation. In this specific instance, the diagnosis relied on the convergence of systemic Behçet's criteria and CSF evidence of sterile inflammation rather than radiological confirmation of parenchymal disease.
