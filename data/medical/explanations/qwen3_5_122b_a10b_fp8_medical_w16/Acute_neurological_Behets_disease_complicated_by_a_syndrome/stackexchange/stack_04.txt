Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating a patient with fever, headache, and hyponatremia where infectious etiologies like HSV encephalitis and tuberculous meningitis have been ruled out via CSF analysis, which specific clinical finding appearing later in the course would most strongly pivot the differential diagnosis toward Behçet's disease over neurosarcoidosis or primary CNS lymphoma?

In a patient presenting with fever, headache, and hyponatremia where infectious etiologies like HSV encephalitis and tuberculous meningitis have been definitively excluded, the clinical finding that most strongly pivots the differential diagnosis toward Behçet's disease (BD) over neurosarcoidosis or primary CNS lymphoma is the **development of painful genital ulcers** (genital aphthosis) in conjunction with existing oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes.

While neurosarcoidosis and CNS lymphoma can mimic the meningoencephalitic presentation and even cause SIADH through hypothalamic infiltration or inflammation, they lack the specific mucocutaneous signature of Behçet's. As illustrated in this case report, the patient initially presented with nonspecific systemic symptoms and hyponatremia, leading to a broad workup that included ruling out malignancy (via negative CSF cytology and imaging) and sarcoidosis (via normal ACE and lysozyme levels). The critical turning point occurred on day 5 with the appearance of a painful scrotal ulcer.

According to the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, the presence of recurrent oral ulcers combined with genital ulcers constitutes a highly specific diagnostic feature. In this case, the development of genital aphthosis allowed for a definitive diagnosis (scoring ≥4 points), distinguishing it from neurosarcoidosis or lymphoma, which would not manifest with this specific pattern of mucocutaneous lesions. Furthermore, the concurrent resolution of neurological symptoms, SIADH-related hyponatremia, and mucocutaneous lesions upon initiation of colchicine monotherapy supports that the neurological dysfunction was driven by acute neuro-Behçet's disease rather than a neoplastic or granulomatous process requiring different therapeutic modalities. Thus, in the setting of aseptic meningitis and SIADH, new-onset genital ulceration serves as the pathognomonic sign anchoring the diagnosis to Behçet's disease.
