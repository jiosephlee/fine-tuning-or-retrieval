Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The patient, an Asian male in his sixth decade of life, presented to the emergency department with a three-day history of constitutional and neurological symptoms that initially obscured the underlying etiology. His clinical picture began with fever, headache, general malaise, and arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. Notably, he reported persistent hiccups, a symptom that served as a critical, albeit easily overlooked, indicator of severe metabolic disturbance rather than a primary gastrointestinal issue. His medical history was significant for undiagnosed Type 2 diabetes mellitus; despite hyperglycaemia and an HbA1c of 8.2%, he had received no prior treatment due to limited health literacy regarding his condition. While this comorbidity complicated the metabolic landscape, it did not explain the acute inflammatory or neurological features of his presentation. Socially, he was a non-smoker with minimal alcohol use and no recent travel or animal exposure, making infectious zoonoses less likely. Upon arrival, he was febrile at 38.1°C and tachycardic at 119 beats per minute but remained alert, though visibly fatigued.

Physical examination revealed the first tangible clues pointing toward a systemic inflammatory disorder rather than a localized infection or simple metabolic derangement. The patient exhibited distinct mucocutaneous lesions: oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes distributed across the face and trunk. Crucially, cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable aside from tachycardia, and manual muscle testing showed no weakness. Volume assessment determined the patient to be clinically euvolemic with no peripheral edema. This euvolemic state is a pivotal distinction in the workup of hyponatremia; it helps rule out hypovolemic causes such as dehydration or third-spacing seen in pancreatitis or burns, as well as hypervolemic states like congestive heart failure or cirrhosis. The presence of these specific dermatological findings alongside fever immediately raised suspicion for Behçet's disease (BD), yet without genital ulcers—which developed later—the diagnosis remained presumptive at this stage.

Laboratory investigations unveiled a complex interplay of severe electrolyte imbalance and intense systemic inflammation that deepened the diagnostic complexity. The most striking anomaly was marked hyponatremia with a serum sodium level of 124 mmol/L. Concurrently, inflammatory markers were profoundly elevated, with a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 180 mg/L and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 70 mm/hour, signaling an aggressive inflammatory process far exceeding what would typically be expected from uncomplicated viral illness or minor trauma. Furthermore, initial labs indicated rhabdomyolysis through significantly elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L) alongside transaminitis (elevated AST and ALT). In the context of severe hyponatremia caused by Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH), rhabdomyolysis is a recognized but often underappreciated complication resulting from cellular swelling and membrane instability in skeletal muscle fibers caused by osmotic shifts. The combination of fever, headache, persistent hiccups (a hallmark sign of profound hyponatremia affecting the brainstem), and rhabdomyolysis created a clinical triad that could mimic sepsis or viral meningoencephalitis just as readily as it suggested an autoimmune flare.

Consequently, the initial diagnostic dilemma centered on differentiating between primary infectious etiologies—such as bacterial sepsis or viral meningoencephalitis—and systemic autoimmune or neoplastic processes. The persistence of hiccups specifically prompted consideration of central nervous system involvement near the medulla oblongata or severe metabolic derangement requiring urgent correction before irreversible neuronal injury occurred. While bacterial meningitis was considered due to fever and headache, the absence of nuchal rigidity initially tempered this suspicion until further lumbar puncture data became available. Similarly, while malignancy such as lymphoma could explain fever and elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptors (sIL-2R), the lack of lymphadenopathy on CT imaging made this less probable without histological confirmation. The presence of oral ulcers was suggestive but non-specific enough to warrant ruling out herpetic infections via PCR before committing to an immunosuppressive regimen for presumed Behçet's disease. Thus, the admitting team faced a "perfect storm" where non-specific symptoms masked a rare convergence of acute neuro-Behçet's disease and SIADH necessitating a rigorous exclusionary diagnostic strategy before definitive management could begin.

## Pathophysiological Framework: Neuro-Behçet's and SIADH Mechanisms

To fully appreciate the diagnostic challenges presented in this case, one must first establish the fundamental pathophysiology of Behçet's disease (BD) and its neurological variant. BD is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory disorder characterized primarily by recurrent oral and genital aphthous ulcers, uveitis, and dermatological lesions such as folliculitis-like rashes. The underlying pathology involves a panvasculitis affecting vessels of all sizes and types, driven by an aberrant immune response where genetic predispositions, notably HLA-B51 positivity in many populations, interact with environmental triggers. While the classic mucocutaneous manifestations are relatively straightforward to recognize, the neurological involvement—known as neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD)—presents a more complex clinical picture. Typically, neuro-BD manifests years after the initial diagnosis of systemic BD, often appearing as parenchymal brain lesions concentrated in the brainstem or diencephalon. Consequently, the simultaneous presentation of acute neuro-BD alongside new-onset systemic BD is a rare occurrence that complicates the diagnostic algorithm, requiring clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion even before the full spectrum of mucocutaneous symptoms has evolved.

The development of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) in this context represents a critical intersection between neuro-inflammation and endocrine dysfunction. SIADH is defined by the excessive release of arginine vasopressin (AVP), formerly known as antidiuretic hormone (ADH), leading to impaired water excretion and consequent dilutional hyponatremia. In cases where SIADH arises from central nervous system pathology rather than medication or malignancy, the mechanism is believed to be driven by local inflammation disrupting the normal regulatory feedback loops within the hypothalamus or pituitary gland. Specifically, proinflammatory cytokines play a pivotal role; interleukin-6 (IL-6) is frequently elevated in acute neuro-BD and has been shown to stimulate AVP secretion directly. In this patient, the intense systemic inflammation evidenced by markedly elevated C-reactive protein likely reflects an underlying surge in IL-6 within the cerebrospinal fluid environment. This cytokine-mediated stimulation overrides physiological osmoreceptors, resulting in sustained ADH release despite hypo-osmolarity, thereby precipitating severe hyponatremia.

Understanding why standard diagnostic imaging failed to reveal structural abnormalities requires an appreciation of the distinct phenotypes of acute neuro-BD. Unlike chronic or progressive forms that present with visible infarcts or mass-like lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), acute presentations often manifest as aseptic meningoencephalitis without parenchymal destruction. In these instances, the pathological process is confined to cellular infiltration and edema within the meninges or superficial cortical layers that may not generate sufficient signal change to be detected by conventional MRI sequences early in the disease course. Therefore, a "normal" MRI does not exclude active CNS inflammation; instead, it necessitates reliance on cerebrospinal fluid analysis for definitive evidence of aseptic meningitis, characterized here by lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein levels despite negative infectious workups.

Finally, the clinical sequelae of severe hyponatremia induced by SIADH extend beyond simple electrolyte imbalance to include specific neuromuscular phenomena observed in this case: persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis. Persistent hiccups serve as a crucial clinical clue pointing toward brainstem irritation or severe metabolic derangement; in this scenario, they likely resulted from direct irritation of the medullary hiccup reflex arc exacerbated by cerebral edema associated with rapid shifts in tonicity. Furthermore, profound hyponatremia can lead to cellular swelling across various tissues, including skeletal muscle membranes. This osmotic stress compromises membrane integrity and ion homeostasis within muscle fibers, potentially triggering rhabdomyolysis—a complication highlighted by this patient's significantly elevated creatine kinase levels. Recognizing these seemingly disparate symptoms as downstream effects of CNS-driven SIADH allows for a cohesive understanding of how localized neuro-inflammation can precipitate widespread systemic complications requiring targeted management strategies beyond simple fluid restriction.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Exclusionary Workup and Confirmatory Criteria

The diagnostic journey in this case began with a critical challenge: distinguishing between a primary infectious etiology, a neoplastic process, and an emerging autoimmune disorder in a patient presenting with fever, headache, and profound hyponatraemia. Initial clinical suspicion naturally leaned toward viral meningoencephalitis, specifically Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) or Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV), given the acute onset of neurological symptoms and fever. Consequently, empirical antiviral therapy was initiated while awaiting confirmatory data. However, the definitive exclusion of infectious causes relied heavily on comprehensive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis. The CSF profile demonstrated mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein but lacked the neutrophilic predominance typical of bacterial meningitis. Crucially, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays and antigen testing for HSV, VZV, and other common pathogens were negative, as were cultures for bacteria and mycobacteria. This effectively ruled out tuberculous meningitis and viral encephalitis, shifting the diagnostic focus away from an infectious agent toward a sterile inflammatory or neoplastic process.

With infection excluded, the differential diagnosis expanded to include primary central nervous system (CNS) malignancy and other systemic inflammatory diseases such as neurosarcoidosis. The presence of elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels raised concern for hematologic malignancy or lymphoma; however, this marker must always be interpreted within the clinical context. While sIL-2R is sensitive for lymphoproliferative disorders, mild elevations are non-specific and can occur in various active inflammatory states. In this case, the absence of mass lesions on brain MRI and the lack of malignant cells on CSF cytology rendered CNS lymphoma highly unlikely despite the biochemical anomaly. Similarly, neurosarcoidosis was considered due to its ability to mimic CNS inflammation with normal imaging in early stages. Yet, the normal serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme levels provided evidence against granulomatous disease, reinforcing that these systemic markers were likely reflecting non-specific immune activation rather than a specific sarcoidotic process.

The pivotal moment in the diagnostic algorithm occurred on day 5 with the emergence of a painful scrotal ulcer. Prior to this event, the diagnosis remained equivocal despite the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes; however, genital ulceration is a hallmark feature that significantly increases diagnostic specificity for Behçet's disease (BD). This new finding allowed clinicians to apply the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), which assigns points based on clinical manifestations: two points each for oral aphthosis and genital aphthosis, one point for folliculitis-like rash or papulopustular lesions. With a cumulative score of five—exceeding the threshold of four required for "definite" BD—the systemic nature of the disease was confirmed. Notably, while HLA-B51 positivity was noted later in the workup, it served merely as supportive evidence rather than a diagnostic requirement under current ICBD guidelines.

The final synthesis of the diagnosis integrated these systemic findings with the neurological presentation to establish acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). The patient exhibited classic signs of CNS inflammation—headache, vertigo, fever, and aseptic meningitis on lumbar puncture—in the setting of confirmed systemic BD. Importantly, this diagnosis was reached despite a normal brain MRI showing no parenchymal lesions in the brainstem or hypothalamus. This case illustrates that acute neuro-BD can manifest primarily as aseptic meningitis without visible structural abnormalities on imaging during early phases. Therefore, once infectious and neoplastic mimics were rigorously excluded through CSF analysis and advanced imaging, the presence of definitive mucocutaneous criteria combined with sterile meningeal inflammation provided sufficient grounds to diagnose acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH. This logical progression underscored that in patients with characteristic mucocutaneous lesions presenting with non-specific neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia，the exclusion of alternative causes followed by strict adherence to ICBD scoring is essential for timely identification of this rare but treatable condition.

## Therapeutic Strategy: Conservative Management and Monotherapy Success

The management of this complex case required a nuanced therapeutic strategy that diverged from standard protocols for acute neurological Behçet's disease (Neuro-BD) while rigorously addressing the concurrent Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH). Upon admission, the patient received empirical antimicrobial coverage for potential bacterial or viral etiologies, which was appropriately discontinued once infectious workups returned negative. The definitive treatment plan emerged on day 8, following the confirmation of systemic BD and acute Neuro-BD. Unlike the conventional first-line approach for acute Neuro-BD, which typically involves high-dose systemic corticosteroids often combined with cyclophosphamide or other potent immunosuppressants to rapidly suppress parenchymal inflammation, this case utilized colchicine monotherapy. This decision was driven by the patient's specific clinical trajectory and the desire to avoid the significant side effect profile of steroids, particularly given his undiagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus and poor glycemic control. While current literature suggests colchicine is primarily effective for mucocutaneous manifestations and often requires adjunctive steroids for neurological involvement, this case demonstrated that colchicine could successfully resolve both systemic and neurological symptoms as a standalone agent.

The pharmacological rationale for selecting colchicine rests on its ability to modulate the innate immune response through mechanisms distinct from glucocorticoids. Colchicine functions by binding to tubulin, thereby inhibiting microtubule polymerization and disrupting neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion, and degranulation. Furthermore, it interferes with inflammasome assembly, leading to the suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). In the context of this patient's presentation, where elevated C-reactive protein likely reflected an underlying IL-6-mediated inflammatory cascade contributing to both the aseptic meningitis and the dysregulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH), colchicine offered a targeted anti-inflammatory effect. By dampening this cytokine storm without inducing hyperglycemia—a critical consideration in a patient with uncontrolled diabetes—the therapy addressed the root inflammatory driver of both the neurological deficits and the endocrine disturbance simultaneously.

Concurrent with immunomodulatory therapy, the management of SIADH relied strictly on conservative measures rather than aggressive pharmacological intervention. The patient exhibited severe hyponatremia (serum sodium 124 mmol/L) with elevated urine osmolality and inappropriate ADH levels. Standard guidelines for symptomatic or severe hyponatremia often recommend hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans); however, in this instance, these interventions were deemed unnecessary due to the patient's stable hemodynamic status and euvolemic presentation. Instead, strict fluid restriction was instituted at less than 800 mL per day. This conservative approach allowed for a gradual correction of serum sodium levels, minimizing the risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome associated with overly rapid shifts in tonicity. The success of this strategy was evidenced by the normalization of serum sodium to 136 mmol/L within two weeks and a concomitant decrease in ADH levels to 1.8 pg/mL as systemic inflammation subsided.

Supportive care played a complementary role in managing the patient's mucocutaneous burden without escalating systemic toxicity. While topical corticosteroid ointments were applied to oral aphthae and silver sulfadiazine cream utilized for genital ulcers to promote local healing and prevent secondary infection, these measures were purely palliative compared to the systemic action required for Neuro-BD. The absence of uveitis further simplified ocular management requirements during this acute phase. Crucially, the resolution of these superficial lesions paralleled the improvement in deep-seated neurological symptoms, reinforcing the concept that controlling systemic inflammation via colchicine was sufficient to address multi-system involvement in this specific phenotype of Behçet's disease.

Continuous monitoring protocols were essential to validate treatment efficacy and ensure safety throughout hospitalization. Daily assessments tracked serum sodium concentrations closely to guide fluid restriction adjustments and confirm steady normalization without rebound hyponatremia or overcorrection. Simultaneously, clinical examinations focused on resolving neurological signs; notably, persistent hiccups—a recognized marker of severe hyponatremia or brainstem irritation—resolved alongside vertigo and headache within days of initiating colchicine. Myogenic enzymes (creatine kinase) also normalized as rhabdomyolysis secondary to electrolyte imbalance resolved. Over a one-month hospital stay followed by four years of outpatient maintenance on colchicine monotherapy, no recurrence of mucocutaneous lesions or neurological deficits occurred. This long-term stability supports the hypothesis that early identification of inflammatory drivers allows for effective management with less toxic regimens than traditionally prescribed for acute Neuro-BD when patients present with concurrent SIADH-like physiology responsive to anti-neutrophil therapy.
