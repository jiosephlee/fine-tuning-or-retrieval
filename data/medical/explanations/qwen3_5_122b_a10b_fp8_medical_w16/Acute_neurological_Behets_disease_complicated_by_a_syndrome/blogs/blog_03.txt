Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Beyond Steroids: Can Colchicine Monotherapy Treat Acute Neuro-Behçet's and Associated SIADH?

We usually think of Behçet's disease (BD) as a slow-burner. You get your oral ulcers, maybe some genital sores or uveitis, and if you're one of the unlucky 10–20% who develop neurological involvement (Neuro-BD), it tends to show up years down the line. But what happens when a patient walks into your ED with fever, headache, and severe hyponatremia, and you realize they have *all* three components—systemic BD, acute Neuro-BD, and Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH)—hitting simultaneously? And what if the standard "high-dose steroids" playbook wasn't actually needed to fix it?

That's exactly the puzzle presented in a fascinating case report by Kakehi et al., describing a man in his 60s who challenged our assumptions about how we treat acute neuro-inflammation in Behçet's. This isn't just another case of "rare disease"; it forces us to rethink the hierarchy of treatment options when the usual suspects don't quite fit or when we want to avoid heavy immunosuppression.

### The Presentation: More Than Just Headache

The patient arrived with a classic triad that often screams "meningitis": fever (38.1°C), severe headache, and altered mental status (fatigue/malaise). But look closer at the vitals: tachycardia at 119 bpm. Look at the labs: CRP was sky-high at 180 mg/L. And then there was the elephant in the room—the sodium was 124 mmol/L.

Initially, this looked like sepsis or viral encephalitis. They even got him on cefepime and acyclovir empirically while waiting for cultures and PCR panels, which all came back negative. The real clues were hiding in plain sight but easy to miss until Day 5: oral aphthae that had been present from the start, followed by a painful scrotal ulcer and folliculitis-like rashes on his trunk. Combined with a positive HLA-B51, he met the International Criteria for Definite BD.

But here is where things get spicy for us emergency physicians and neurologists alike: **the MRI was normal.** No brainstem lesions, no parenchymal enhancement, nothing in the hypothalamus where you'd expect pathology causing SIADH. Yet, he had clear aseptic meningitis on lumbar puncture (lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein) and biochemical proof of SIADH (low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, elevated ADH).

He also had persistent hiccups—a subtle but critical clue often missed as "just annoying," which turned out to be a sign of profound hyponatremia complicating his CNS inflammation. To top it off, he developed rhabdomyolysis (CK ~1350 U/L), likely secondary to the severe electrolyte disturbance itself.

### The Therapeutic Twist: Why Not Steroids?

So far, so standard for a workup of fever + headache + mucocutaneous ulcers = suspect Neuro-BD. Now comes the treatment decision matrix. Current guidelines scream for high-dose IV corticosteroids as first-line therapy for acute parenchymal Neuro-BD to prevent permanent disability. Cyclophosphamide or TNF inhibitors are often added for refractory cases or major vessel involvement.

However, this team took a different road. Recognizing that this patient presented with *acute meningoencephalitis-like symptoms* rather than frank parenchymal destruction on imaging—and wanting to spare an older diabetic patient from massive steroid spikes—they opted for **colchicine monotherapy**. Yes, just colchicine. Plus fluid restriction for the SIADH and topical care for the ulcers.

No dexamethasone pulse doses. No azathioprine loading dose. No biologics yet either.

And guess what happened? Within days, his fever broke, his appetite returned, and his neurological symptoms began to recede. By Day 16, his sodium normalized (from 124 to 136 mmol/L), ADH levels plummeted back to baseline, and his rhabdomyolysis resolved completely over four weeks of colchicine therapy alone. He stayed stable on this regimen for four years without recurrence or need for escalation to steroids or other immunosuppressants during that period.

### What Does This Mean For Us?

This case is provocative because it sits right at odds with established dogma regarding Neuro-BD management while offering a plausible biological mechanism for success elsewhere in medicine: colchicine isn't just an old-school gout drug; it's potent anti-neutrophil agent that suppresses IL-1β and IL-6 production—key drivers in Behçet's pathophysiology and potentially in SIADH-mediated ADH release via hypothalamic inflammation (even if invisible on MRI).

While we must acknowledge that spontaneous remission can occur in aseptic meningitis variants of Neuro-BD (which affects roughly 5% of patients), the concurrent resolution of *three* distinct systems—mucocutaneous lesions, CNS inflammation/SIADH, and rhabdomyolysis—suggests more than luck here. It raises the question: Could colchicine be sufficient as induction therapy for early-phase Neuro-BD presenting primarily as meningoencephalitis without large-vessel or brainstem infarction? Especially in patients where steroids pose significant risks?

It certainly doesn't mean we stop giving steroids tomorrow for everyone with Neuro-BD. If you see a brainstem lesion on MRI or significant mass effect, you still reach for those pulses immediately. But this case argues that we shouldn't reflexively assume every acute neuro-behcet presentation requires nuclear-grade immunosuppression before trying targeted anti-inflammatory strategies like colchicine in select phenotypes. Furthermore, it reminds us that SIADH isn't just a side note; it can be a direct consequence of CNS inflammation that resolves only when the underlying driver is treated effectively enough to calm the cytokine storm (likely IL-6 mediated).

### Take-Home Points

*   **Think Broadly:** In any patient with recurrent oral/genital ulcers presenting with fever and headache—even without prior BD history—consider **Acute Neuro-Behçet's** immediately; do not wait months or years after initial diagnosis rules this out prematurely because concurrent onset is possible though rare (<5%).
*   **Normal MRI Doesn't Rule Out Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis with normal neuroimaging; rely heavily on CSF analysis (lymphocytic pleocytosis) combined with clinical mucocutaneous criteria if infection is ruled out aggressively first.
*   **Hyponatremia & Hiccups Matter:** Persistent hiccups and unexplained hyponatremia/SIADH in this context may signal hypothalamic dysfunction driven by CNS inflammation; watch out for secondary complications like rhabdomyolysis from severe sodium shifts.
*   **Re-evaluate First-Line Therapy:** While corticosteroids remain standard for parenchymal lesions with structural damage on imaging (**brainstem involvement**), **colchicine monotherapy** warrants consideration as an effective initial strategy for acute meningoencephalitic presentations without major structural deficits, potentially sparing patients from steroid toxicity while resolving both neurological and endocrine sequelae simultaneously.
