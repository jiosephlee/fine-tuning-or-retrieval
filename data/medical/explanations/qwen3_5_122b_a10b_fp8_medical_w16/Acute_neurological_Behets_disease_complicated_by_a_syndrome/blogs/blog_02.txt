Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Normal MRI Trap: Diagnosing Acute Parenchemal Neuro-Behçet's Without Radiographic Evidence

We've all been there. You're staring at a brain MRI that looks pristine—no parenchymal enhancement, no signal changes in the brainstem, no hypothalamic lesions. Your internal alarm bells start ringing less loudly as you rule out the "big bad" strokes, tumors, and classic demyelinating events. But then you remember the patient sitting in your ED bed: a man in his 60s with fever, persistent hiccups, severe headache, and a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L. If you stop searching once the MRI comes back normal, you might miss a diagnosis that requires immediate intervention.

This week, we're dissecting a fascinating case report from *BMJ Case Reports* that serves as a stark reminder of why we must never let a clean scan override the clinical picture in inflammatory neurology. The authors describe a patient with **Acute Neurological Behçet's Disease (Neuro-BD)** complicated by **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH)**, all while sporting a completely normal brain MRI.

### The Presentation: When Hiccups Mean More Than Indigestion

The patient was an Asian male in his 60s presenting with a three-day history of fever (38.1°C), headache, general malaise, and arthralgia. But two symptoms stood out as red flags for deep-seated pathology: **persistent hiccups** and **hyponatremia**.

He had oral aphthae on exam—a classic sign—but nothing else pointed immediately to Behçet's until later developments occurred. Crucially, he was euvolemic despite having severe hyponatremia (Na+ 124 mmol/L). Endocrine workup ruled out adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism. Instead, labs screamed SIADH: low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L).

Here is where many clinicians would pause and look for pneumonia or medication side effects. But this patient also had worsening vertigo and headache by day 3. The team ordered an MRI of the brain to rule out meningoencephalitis or stroke. **It was normal.** No hypothalamic lesions, no brainstem involvement—the usual suspects for parenchymal Neuro-BD were absent on imaging.

### The Diagnostic Pivot: Trusting the Fluid Over the Film

If you relied solely on the MRI here, you might have dismissed Neuro-BD entirely. However, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) told a different story. Despite the lack of nuchal rigidity or cranial nerve palsies, the LP revealed:
*   Mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O)
*   Elevated protein (0.97 g/L)
*   Lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL)
*   Negative cultures for bacteria, TB, HSV, VZV, and Cryptococcus

This was **aseptic meningitis**. Combined with the systemic signs—fever, mucocutaneous lesions—and later appearing genital ulcers and folliculitis-like rashes on day 5, the puzzle pieces snapped together into **Definite Behçet's Disease** according to International Criteria. The neurological symptoms were attributed to acute Neuro-BD presenting as meningoencephalitis *without* visible parenchymal lesions on MRI at that stage.

### Why This Matters: The Pathophysiology of Silent Inflammation

This case challenges our heuristic that "Neuro-BD = Visible Lesions." While parenchymal involvement in Behçet's typically affects the brainstem and presents months or years after initial diagnosis (mean delay ~4 years), this patient presented with concurrent systemic and neurological disease—a rarity seen in fewer than 5% of cases at onset.

Furthermore, this case illuminates a terrifying but logical connection: **Neuro-BD causing SIADH**.
The mechanism likely involves CNS inflammation driving IL-6 production within the CSF (a known feature of acute Neuro-BD), which stimulates inappropriate ADH release from the posterior pituitary or hypothalamus—even if those structures don't light up on an MRI yet. The persistent hiccups weren't just annoying; they were a hallmark sign of severe hyponatremia driven by this inflammatory cascade. Additionally, the patient developed rhabdomyolysis (CK 1349 U/L), another under-recognized complication of profound hyponatremia that can masquerade as primary muscle pain or sepsis-associated myopathy.

### Treatment Surprise: Colchicine Monotherapy Wins Big?

Perhaps the most controversial yet exciting aspect of this case is the management strategy. Standard teaching dictates that acute Neuro-BD requires high-dose corticosteroids and often aggressive immunosuppression (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide). Systematic reviews generally label colchicine as ineffective for *acute* neurological involvement compared to mucocutaneous disease.

Yet here? They started **colchicine monotherapy**. No steroids were given initially; topical steroids covered oral ulcers locally; fluid restriction handled the SIADH conservatively without hypertonic saline or vaptans because Na+ was rising steadily but safely under close watch.

The result? Rapid resolution of fever within one day. Mucocutaneous lesions cleared over weeks. Most impressively, by Day 16, his sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L and ADH levels dropped precipitously from 22 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL—all without a single dose of IV methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide while he was still hospitalized for four weeks before discharge on maintenance colchicine alone for four years with zero recurrence.

While we must be cautious about drawing definitive conclusions from a single case report regarding steroid-sparing efficacy in acute neuro-inflammation, it forces us to reconsider whether every case needs an immediate steroid bomb if colchicine can modulate neutrophil migration and IL-6 sufficiently to halt this specific inflammatory storm. It certainly suggests that in patients where steroids are contraindicated or risky (like our patient with undiagnosed Type 2 Diabetes), alternatives deserve serious consideration earlier than we think.

### Take-Home Pearls for the Emergency Physician & Hospitalist

Don't let a normal MRI make you complacent when facing fever + headache + hyponatremia in young adults or those with suggestive skin findings:

*   **Normal MRI Does Not Rule Out Acute Neuro-BD:** Early-stage meningoencephalitic presentations can exist without parenchymal signal changes on structural imaging; rely heavily on CSF findings showing aseptic meningitis when clinical suspicion is high.
*   **Connect Hyponatremia to CNS Inflammation:** In patients with unexplained SIADH and non-focal CNS symptoms (headache, confusion), consider underlying inflammatory etiologies like neuro-Behçet's even before classic mucocutaneous signs appear fully formed; persistent hiccups are a specific warning sign for severe hyponatremia in this context.
*   **Look Beyond Steroids:** While corticosteroids remain first-line for acute Neuro-BD, this case highlights that colchicine monotherapy may successfully treat concurrent mucocutaneous disease AND acute neurological/SIADH manifestations in select cases; keep it in your toolkit for patients who cannot tolerate immunosuppressants immediately upon presentation until full workup is complete?
