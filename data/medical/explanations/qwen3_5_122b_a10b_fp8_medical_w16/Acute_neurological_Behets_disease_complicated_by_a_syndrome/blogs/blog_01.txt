Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When Hyponatremia Meets Headache: Unmasking Neuro-Behçet's via SIADH

We love a good "zebra" in the ER, but sometimes the zebras wear stethoscopes and show up with very subtle clues. Imagine a man in his 60s walks into your ED with a three-day history of fever, a pounding headache, and—here is the kicker—persistent hiccups. He looks tired, he's tachycardic, and he has some painful mouth sores. You run the labs, and while the fever points toward infection, the chemistry panel hits you with a hard stop: **severe hyponatremia** (124 mmol/L).

Your mind likely races through the usual suspects: sepsis, pneumonia, maybe subarachnoid hemorrhage? But then you notice something else on his exam: folliculitis-like rashes on his trunk and face. And later that day? A genital ulcer appears.

This isn't just a tricky electrolyte disturbance or a standard viral meningitis. This is a rare confluence of **acute neurological Behçet's disease (Neuro-BD)** manifesting simultaneously with systemic Behçet's, complicated by **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH)**. Even more striking? The brain MRI was completely normal.

Let's dive into how this case challenges our diagnostic algorithms and why SIADH might be the silent herald of CNS inflammation in Behçet's.

## The Presentation: More Than Just Mouth Sores

The patient arrived with fever (38.1°C), headache, malaise, arthralgia, and those persistent hiccups—a classic but often overlooked sign of severe hyponatremia or brainstem irritation. His vitals showed tachycardia, but he was hemodynamically stable otherwise. On physical exam, aside from the obvious oral aphthae and skin rashes, he was neurologically intact regarding strength and reflexes initially. No nuchal rigidity either.

But the lab work told a different story. Beyond the sodium crash to 124 mmol/L, he had sky-high inflammatory markers (CRP 180 mg/L) and significantly elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), hinting at rhabdomyolysis—a complication often missed until it's too late if you don't think about severe hyponatremia first.

The endocrine workup sealed the deal for SIADH: low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), high urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), and elevated ADH levels—all in a euvolemic patient with normal thyroid and adrenal function. Infectious workups for HSV, VZV, TB, and bacteria were all negative. Malignancy screens were unrevealing except for a non-specific bump in sIL-2R.

Then came Day 5: **Genital ulcers.** Combined with the oral aphthae and skin lesions, the diagnosis shifted from "mystery febrile illness" to definitive Behçet's Disease (International Criteria score ≥4). With CNS symptoms persisting despite negative infectious workup, we landed on **Acute Neuro-BD**.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Normal MRI Does Not Mean Nothing Happening

Here is where this case gets tricky for us clinicians. In many textbooks, Neuro-BD is synonymous with parenchymal lesions in the brainstem or basal ganglia visible on MRI. However, acute Neuro-BD can present as **aseptic meningitis** without any structural abnormalities on imaging.

This patient had worsening headache and vertigo on Day 3. We sent him for an MRI of the brain—specifically looking at the hypothalamus—and it was read as normal. If we had stopped there thinking "no lesion = no neuro involvement," we would have missed the diagnosis entirely. The CSF analysis was our saving grace here: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and elevated protein confirmed CNS inflammation even in the absence of radiological evidence.

Furthermore, SIADH is rarely discussed as a primary feature of Neuro-BD unless it's drug-induced (e.g., cyclophosphamide toxicity). This case suggests that **CNS inflammation itself**, potentially driven by IL-6 mediated pathways similar to other neuroinflammatory states, can disrupt ADH regulation directly from within the inflamed meninges or hypothalamus before frank lesions appear on scan.

## Treatment Surprises: Colchicine Monotherapy Works?

Standard teaching for acute Neuro-BD usually screams **"High-dose steroids!"** followed by aggressive immunosuppression like azathioprine or cyclophosphamide if refractory. Steroids are indeed the mainstay for most cases to prevent long-term disability from parenchymal damage.

However, this patient took a different path that offers fascinating food for thought for our practice guidelines in resource-limited settings or steroid-averse patients (like diabetics with poor glycemic control). Once Behçet's was confirmed via clinical criteria (oral + genital ulcers + rash + HLA-B51 positive), they started **Colchicine monotherapy**. No IV methylprednisolone bursts were needed initially; just topical care for ulcers and fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) for SIADH management.

The result? Rapid resolution of fever within 24 hours, gradual improvement of neurological symptoms over two weeks, normalization of sodium by Day 16, and discharge after one month without ever touching systemic steroids or cytotoxic agents at this stage! While literature reviews often cite colchicine as ineffective *alone* for parenchymal Neuro-BD compared to steroids, this case suggests it may hold unexpected efficacy in early-phase meningoencephalitic presentations or pure aseptic meningitis variants of Neuro-BD by dampening neutrophil migration and cytokine production (IL-1β/IL-6). Whether this represents spontaneous remission triggered by colchicine or true monotherapy success requires larger cohorts to confirm—but it definitely warrants keeping colchicine higher on our radar than we currently do for early neuro-involvement without massive mass effect or stroke-like deficits.

## Take-Home Pearls for Your Next Shift

Don't let this rare constellation become your next missed diagnosis when you see these patterns tomorrow morning:

*   **Hyponatremia + Fever + Ulcers = Think Neuro-Behçet's:** If a patient presents with mucocutaneous ulcers (oral/genital/skin) alongside unexplained hyponatremia and CNS symptoms (headache/confusion/hiccups), aggressively rule out SIADH secondary to acute neuro-inflammatory processes before settling on simple dehydration or psychogenic polydipsia.
*   **Normal MRI ≠ Absent CNS Pathology:** Acute Neuro-BD can present purely as aseptic meningitis with normal brain imaging early in the course. If clinical suspicion is high based on systemic signs (ulcers/rash) plus CSF findings (lymphocytic pleocytosis), do not dismiss Neuro-BD solely because the scan is clean.
*   **Persistent Hiccups are Red Flags:** Intractable hiccups can be an early warning sign of severe hyponatremia OR brainstem irritation/inflammation; always check electrolytes immediately when they accompany fever/neurologic complaints.
*   **Check CK Levels:** Severe hyponatremia can precipitate rhabdomyolysis; an elevated CK in this context might be underappreciated muscle injury rather than primary myopathy or trauma alone.
