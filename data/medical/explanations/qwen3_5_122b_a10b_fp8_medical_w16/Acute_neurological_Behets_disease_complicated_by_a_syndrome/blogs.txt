Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When Headache Meets Hyponatremia: Unmasking SIADH as a Hidden Complication of Acute Neuro-Behçet's

Let's talk about a case that challenges our usual timelines and forces us to look beyond the "classic" textbook presentation. We often teach that Behçet's disease (BD) is a slow-burner, where neurological involvement—Neuro-Behçet's (neuro-BD)—typically lags behind mucocutaneous symptoms by years. But what happens when the mouth ulcers, the genital sores, and the brain inflammation all show up at the same party? Even more intriguingly, what if the patient walks in with persistent hiccups and severe hyponatremia, pointing toward a hidden endocrine storm driven by CNS inflammation?

This week, we're diving into a fascinating case report from *Kakehi et al.* describing a man in his 60s who presented with fever, headache, oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and a sodium level of 124 mmol/L. The twist? He didn't just have acute neuro-BD; he had it complicated by Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH), and remarkably, he was cured with colchicine monotherapy. No steroids. No heavy-hitting immunosuppressants. Just one anti-inflammatory agent turning off the fire.

### The Presentation: More Than Just a Feverish Headache

Our patient arrived with three days of fever, headache, malaise, and those annoying persistent hiccups. On exam, he looked fatigued but alert. The vitals screamed inflammation: temp 38.1°C and tachycardia at 119 bpm. The physical exam gave us our first clues to the systemic nature of his illness: oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on his face and trunk. Crucially, his volume status was euvolemic—a key discriminator when you see hyponatremia in the ER.

The labs told a story of chaos. Marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), sky-high inflammatory markers (CRP 180 mg/L!), and an unexpected elevation in Creatine Kinase (CK) at nearly 1350 U/L. Endocrine workup ruled out adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism but confirmed SIADH: low serum osmolality paired with high urine osmolality and elevated ADH levels.

Here is where many clinicians might stop chasing infections or malignancies if they don't find an obvious source on CT or MRI. Our patient's brain MRI was completely normal regarding parenchymal lesions or hypothalamic involvement. Yet, his CSF told a different story: aseptic meningitis with lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. It wasn't until day 5 that the missing puzzle piece fell into place: a painful scrotal ulcer appeared. Combined with his oral ulcers and rash, he met the International Criteria for Definite Behçet's Disease right then and there.

### Why This Case Matters: Breaking the Timeline Rule

The most striking aspect here is the simultaneity. Usually, neuro-BD strikes roughly four years after the initial diagnosis of systemic BD. Here, we had acute meningoencephalitis meeting active mucocutaneous disease on day zero of presentation. This case shatters the assumption that you need a long history of oral/genital ulcers before worrying about brain involvement in Behçet's. If your patient has classic mucocutaneous findings *and* new-onset neurological symptoms (headache, vertigo, behavioral changes), you must suspect neuro-BD immediately—even if their MRI looks pristine in the early phases of meningoencephalitis-like presentations.

Furthermore, this case illuminates a dangerous but often missed link: **Acute neuro-BD can drive SIADH.** While SIADH is a known complication of CNS infections or tumors in BD patients treated with drugs like cyclophosphamide, this case suggests that CNS inflammation itself—potentially via an IL-6 mediated pathway—is sufficient to dysregulate ADH secretion in untreated patients. The "persistent hiccups" weren't just annoying; they were likely a sign of severe hyponatremia irritating the diaphragmatic center or phrenic nerve irritation secondary to electrolyte shifts. And let's not forget that elevated CK wasn't just muscle pain; it was likely rhabdomyolysis triggered by severe hyponatremia itself—a complication we too often overlook until it's too late.

### The Treatment Surprise: Colchicine Monotherapy

Now for the plot twist that every emergency physician loves to hear about: How did we fix him? Standard teaching for acute neuro-BD screams "High-dose IV Steroids!" followed by cyclophosphamide or infliximab if things get ugly. But this patient got neither corticosteroids nor traditional immunosuppressants for his neurological flare-up alone (he got topical steroids for his mouth). Instead, they started **colchicine**.

Colchicine works by inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6—the very cytokine implicated in driving ADH release in this scenario within hours of starting therapy? Yes! Within two weeks of colchicine initiation:
*   Fever resolved
*   Hiccups stopped
*   Mucocutaneous lesions healed
*   Sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L
*   ADH levels plummeted back to baseline

While spontaneous resolution is always a confounder in single-case reports, the temporal relationship here is compelling enough to make us reconsider our algorithms for mild-to-moderate acute neuro-BD presentations where steroid toxicity is a concern or contraindication exists (like his uncontrolled Type 2 Diabetes). It suggests colchicine isn't just for mucosal stuff; it might be potent enough to dampen the CNS inflammatory cascade responsible for both meningoencephalitis and secondary SIADH in select cases.

# The Normal MRI Trap: Diagnosing Acute Neuro-Behçet's Without Parenchymal Lesions

We love a classic teaching case where the MRI lights up like a Christmas tree, pointing us straight to the diagnosis. But what happens when your patient is crashing neurologically, their labs scream inflammation, and their brain scan looks perfectly normal? That was the reality for a man in his 60s who walked into the ED with fever, headache, persistent hiccups, and severe hyponatremia.

This isn't just another story about Behçet's disease (BD); it's a masterclass in resisting the "normal MRI" trap. While textbooks tell us that neurological involvement in Behçet's (Neuro-BD) typically manifests as brainstem parenchymal lesions appearing years after mucocutaneous symptoms, this case flipped the script entirely. Here, we saw **acute** Neuro-BD diagnosed concurrently with systemic disease, complicated by Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH), all while the brain MRI remained stubbornly unremarkable.

### The Presentation: More Than Just Headache
The patient arrived with a three-day history of fever, malaise, arthralgia, and those dreaded persistent hiccups—a subtle but critical clue often missed until it's too late. On exam, he was febrile (38.1°C) and tachycardic. The physical exam held the first keys to the puzzle: oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on his face and trunk. Crucially, he was euvolemic despite having a sodium level of 124 mmol/L.

Initial workup painted a picture of severe systemic inflammation: CRP was sky-high at 180 mg/L, and he had significant hyperglycemia from undiagnosed Type 2 diabetes. But the real curveball was the endocrine profile. He met every criterion for SIADH: low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), all driven by inappropriately high ADH levels.

### The Diagnostic Dilemma: When Imaging Lies Flat
By Day 3, his headache and vertigo worsened. The clinical team rightly suspected Central Nervous System (CNS) inflammation. They rushed him to get an MRI of the brain—the gold standard for ruling out structural causes like abscesses or demyelinating plaques—and got… nothing. No brainstem lesions, no hypothalamic enhancement, no edema. Just gray matter looking exactly like white matter should look on T1/T2 sequences.

In many settings, a normal MRI would push you away from Neuro-BD toward functional disorders or less urgent etiologies. But here lies the danger zone: **Acute Neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal involvement.** If you wait for a lesion to appear on an MRI before treating or even seriously considering Neuro-BD, you might miss your window for intervention or misdiagnose a treatable inflammatory condition as idiopathic meningitis or viral encephalitis.

The lumbar puncture told a different story than the scanner did. CSF analysis revealed aseptic meningitis with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and elevated protein (0.97 g/L). Extensive infectious workups—bacterial cultures, HSV/VZV PCR, fungal antigens—were all negative. Malignancy screens came back clean too. It wasn't until Day 5 that the missing piece fell into place: a painful scrotal ulcer appeared alongside his oral sores and skin rash. Suddenly, the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease clicked into focus (score ≥4), confirming definite BD with concurrent acute neurological involvement.

### Why Did He Have Hyponatremia?
The presence of SIADH in this case adds another layer of complexity to our understanding of Neuro-BD pathophysiology. While SIADH is well-documented in CNS infections or malignancies causing hypothalamic irritation, its appearance here suggests that acute neuro-inflammation itself can disrupt ADH regulation even without visible hypothalamic lesions on imaging.

There is emerging evidence linking Interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a cytokine known to be elevated in acute Neuro-BD—to increased ADH release. This patient's massive CRP elevation likely mirrored this cytokine storm driving both his neurological symptoms and his electrolyte derangement. Furthermore, don't overlook the "hiccups." Persistent hiccups are often dismissed as benign GI issues or anxiety; in this context, they were likely a direct manifestation of severe hyponatremia acting on the medullary centers or diaphragmatic irritation from electrolyte shifts—a warning sign that shouldn't be ignored in any febrile patient with altered mental status or headache.

### Treatment: Breaking Stereotypes
Perhaps most surprisingly for seasoned rheumatologists and intensivists alike, this patient did *not* receive corticosteroids or heavy-hitting immunosuppressants like cyclophosphamide or infliximab—the usual suspects for acute Neuro-BD management based on current guidelines which favor steroids for rapid control of parenchymal disease instead of colchicine monotherapy which is traditionally reserved for mucocutaneous flares.* Yet,* colchicine monotherapy led to complete resolution of his fever, neurological symptoms (including vertigo and hiccups), mucocutaneous ulcers*,and* normalization of his sodium levels within two weeks.* 

While spontaneous remission cannot be entirely ruled out given that some cases of meningoencephalitic Neuro-BD are self-limiting*,the* rapidity of response across three distinct systems (skin*, neuro*, endocrine*) following colchicine initiation suggests it may play a larger role than previously appreciated*.Colchicine works by inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis*and*suppressing IL-1β*and*IL-6 production*,potentially hitting*the same inflammatory pathways driving both his ulcers*and*his SIADH*.For patients where steroids are contraindicated*(like those with uncontrolled diabetes)*or where minimizing toxicity is paramount,this case offers compelling evidence to reconsider our therapeutic algorithm*.

### Take-Home Pearls
This case challenges several dogmas regarding Behçet's disease presentation and management:

*   **Don't let a normal MRI rule out Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-Behçet's can present purely as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions on imaging early in the course; rely heavily on clinical criteria (oral/genital ulcers + skin lesions) and CSF findings if infection/malignancy are excluded.
*   **Hyponatremia is a red flag:** In patients with BD presenting with fever/headache/SIADH-like physiology (especially with persistent hiccups), suspect acute CNS inflammation even without focal deficits or radiographic abnormalities; check ADH levels if available but diagnose based on electrolyte/osmolality profiles first.* 
*   **Persistent hiccups matter:** Unexplained hiccups in a febrile patient should trigger an immediate search for severe hyponatremia or brainstem pathology*;do not dismiss them as simple indigestion.* 
*   **Re-evaluate treatment paradigms:** While corticosteroids remain first-line for many forms of active Neuro-BD*,this case demonstrates that colchicine monotherapy may suffice for select presentations involving meningoencephalitis*-type syndromes combined with mucocutaneous flares*,particularly when steroid risks outweigh benefits.* 
*   **Think beyond infection:** When faced with "viral-like" meningitis plus systemic inflammatory markers plus hyponatremia*,always keep autoimmune vasculitides like Behçet's high on your differential even before genetic testing confirms HLA-B51 positivity.* 

In summary*,if you see an adult male with mouth sores*,genital ulcers*,fever*,headache*,hyponatremia*-even if their MRI looks perfect*-think Behçet's*.It could save you from unnecessary antivirals*-antibiotics*-or worse*-delayed treatment leading to permanent neurological sequelas*.Stay curious*:stay vigilant*:and remember that sometimes,the best diagnostic tool isn't inside your PACS workstation*:but right there under your stethoscope*.



# Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-Behçet's: A Paradigm Shift or a Fluke?

Let's talk about a case that just might make your standard treatment algorithms twitch. We are taught, almost as gospel, that acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) is a beast requiring aggressive immunosuppression—think high-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or TNF inhibitors. The logic is sound: it's a fulminant CNS inflammation driven by Th1/Th17 pathways, and you need heavy artillery to put it out.

But what happens when a patient walks into the ED with fever, headache, and severe hyponatremia, gets diagnosed with *both* systemic and acute neuro-BD simultaneously (a rarity in itself), and then recovers completely on **colchicine monotherapy**? No steroids. No cyclophosphamide. Just colchicine. And not only did the oral ulcers and genital sores vanish, but the neurological symptoms and the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) resolved right along with them.

This isn't just a "nice story"; it's a case report by Kakehi et al. that forces us to question whether we are over-treating certain phenotypes of neuro-BD or if we've missed a nuance in how colchicine works in meningoencephalitic presentations versus parenchymal disease. Let's dive into the details of this fascinating outlier.

## The Patient Who Had Everything (Except Parenchymal Lesions)

The protagonist of this story is an Asian man in his 60s who rolled into the ER with three days of fever, headache, malaise, and some very specific complaints: persistent hiccups and arthralgia. On top of that, he had oral aphthae. Vital signs showed he was febrile (38.1°C) and tachycardic (119 bpm). Physical exam revealed the classic triad for Behçet's suspicion: oral ulcers, folliculitis-like rashes on the face/trunk, and later in his course, a painful scrotal ulcer.

Here is where things get tricky for the emergency physician or hospitalist: he also had **severe hyponatremia** (Na+ 124 mmol/L). He was euvolemic on exam, but labs told a different story regarding water handling: low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), high urine sodium (>80 mmol/L), and elevated ADH levels. This was textbook SIADH.

The initial workup was a whirlwind of exclusion. They ruled out bacterial meningitis (negative cultures), viral encephalitis (negative HSV/VZV PCR), TB meningitis, malignancy (normal MRI head/chest/abdomen), and sarcoidosis. The CSF showed aseptic meningitis with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, but crucially, **the brain MRI was normal**. No brainstem lesions, no hypothalamic enhancement—the usual suspects for parenchymal neuro-BD were absent.

By Day 5, the appearance of the genital ulcer sealed the deal for Systemic Behçet's Disease based on International Criteria scoring ≥4 points (oral + genital ulcers + rash). With CNS symptoms in this context and infectious/malignant causes excluded, they landed on **Acute Neuro-BD**.

## The Therapeutic Twist

Standard protocol would dictate starting pulse dose steroids immediately to prevent permanent neurological sequelae or death from cerebral edema/inflammation. But here is where this case diverges from the script. Once the diagnosis was secured on Day 8, they started **colchicine monotherapy**. That's it. Topical steroids for the mouth were allowed locally, but no systemic corticosteroids were touched.

The result? Within 24 hours of starting colchicine, his fever broke and appetite returned. Over two weeks, his vertigo cleared up, his hiccups stopped (a huge relief for anyone who has suffered through them), his mucocutaneous lesions healed, and critically, his sodium normalized back to 136 mmol/L as ADH levels plummeted to near zero. He went home after one month on colchicine alone and stayed relapse-free for four years while maintaining good glycemic control for his previously undiagnosed Type 2 Diabetes.

## Why This Matters: Mechanisms vs. Dogma

So why did this work? For decades, systematic reviews have labeled colchicine as ineffective for *parenchymal* neuro-BD because it doesn't cross into deep brain tissue inflammation well enough compared to biologics or cytotoxic agents. However, this patient didn't have parenchymal disease; he had an aseptic meningoencephalitis picture with SIADH likely driven by hypothalamic irritation without frank structural damage visible on MRI.

Colchicine is primarily known for inhibiting microtubule polymerization in neutrophils and suppressing chemotaxis via NF-kB inhibition downregulation of IL-1β production. Recent evidence also suggests it suppresses IL-6 release from activated monocytes—a key player here given that IL-6 is known to stimulate ADH release directly or indirectly via CNS inflammation pathways. If you view SIADH in this context as a downstream effect of IL-6 mediated CNS inflammation rather than direct structural destruction of the posterior pituitary pathway or hypothalamus target neurons being destroyed by necrosis ("parenchymal" disease), then blocking that cytokine cascade becomes highly logical even without massive steroid doses.

It raises an uncomfortable question: Are we treating every case of acute neuro-BD like it's impending herniation when many might actually be self-limiting meningoencephalitides responsive to less toxic agents? Or was this simply spontaneous remission dressed up nicely? The authors argue against pure coincidence given the temporal relationship between drug initiation and rapid resolution across three distinct systems (skin/neuro/endocrine). Still，we must acknowledge that single-case reports are hypothesis-generating at best; they don't rewrite guidelines overnight without replication cohorts showing similar efficacy rates compared to steroid-sparing regimens containing biologics like infliximab or adalimumab which currently dominate modern management strategies especially severe forms involving brainstem structures specifically targeted heavily by those drugs themselves!

*   **Hyponatremia Is Not Always Renal/Diabetic/Medications:** In patients presenting with fever/headache/hyponatremia alongside oral/genital ulcers/rash patterns suggestive Behcet's syndrome consider ## Beyond the Pinprick: Navigating the Diagnostic Maze When Genital Ulcers Are Absent at Onset

Let's be honest: diagnosing Behçet's disease (BD) in the Emergency Department feels less like solving a puzzle and more like trying to assemble one while someone keeps changing the pieces. You see a young man with oral ulcers, fever, and a headache, and your mind races through the differential: viral meningoencephalitis? Sarcoidosis? Lymphoma? But then you hit the wall. The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) demands two points for oral aphthae, two for genital aphthosis, and one for skin lesions. If the patient walks in with just the mouth sores and a headache, you're stuck at three points—below the threshold for "definite" disease.

Enter a fascinating case from Japan that challenges our diagnostic dogma. An Asian man in his 60s rolled into the ED with fever, debilitating headaches, persistent hiccups, and oral aphthae. He looked sick. His labs screamed inflammation (CRP 180 mg/L!) and confusion (hyponatremia down to 124 mmol/L). But here's the kicker: **he didn't have genital ulcers yet.** They wouldn't appear until Day 5 of his hospitalization. Until then, he was a walking diagnostic dilemma with a "probable" Behçet's label hanging over him, complicated by a Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) that threatened to drown his brain in water.

### The Great Mimicry: When Neuro-BD Strikes Early

Typical teaching tells us that neurological involvement in Behçet's (Neuro-BD) is a latecomer, usually appearing years after mucocutaneous symptoms are established. This patient flipped that script entirely. He presented with *concurrent* systemic BD and acute Neuro-BD—a rarity seen in fewer than 5% of patients at initial presentation.

The clinical picture was dominated by meningoencephalitis-like symptoms: fever, headache, vertigo, and those nagging persistent hiccups (a classic red flag for severe hyponatremia or brainstem irritation). The workup was exhaustive. We ruled out HSV/VZV encephalitis (negative PCR), bacterial meningitis (negative culture), tuberculous meningitis (negative AFB/DNA), and even primary CNS lymphoma (despite a mildly elevated sIL-2R).

Here lies the first trap: **over-interpreting non-specific markers.** The team noted an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R). While often flagged for hematologic malignancies or sarcoidosis, this marker is notoriously non-specific in active inflammation. In this case, it was a distractor. Similarly, they checked HLA-B51 immediately upon suspecting BD. It came back positive—but as we know from current guidelines, HLA-B51 adds very little weight to the diagnosis if clinical criteria aren't met. Relying on genetics before clinical signs can lead you down a rabbit hole of false positives; remember, up to 20% of healthy populations in endemic areas carry HLA-B51 without ever developing BD.

### The Silent Hypothalamus: Normal MRI Doesn't Mean No Neuro-BD

Perhaps the most anxiety-inducing moment for any EM physician is seeing "normal" imaging on a patient who clearly has CNS pathology. This patient's brain MRI showed no parenchymal lesions in the brainstem or hypothalamus—the usual suspects for Neuro-BD. Yet, his lumbar puncture told a different story: aseptic meningitis with lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein.

This reinforces a critical pearl: **Acute Neuro-BD can present as pure aseptic meningitis without radiographic evidence.** Waiting for an MRI to light up before treating or seriously considering Neuro-BD could delay crucial management hours or days away from homeostasis restoration. The absence of hypothalamic lesions on MRI does not rule out hypothalamic dysfunction causing SIADH; functional disruption often precedes structural change visible on standard sequences.

### Managing SIADH Without Corticosteroids

The hyponatremia wasn't just an incidental finding; it was likely driven by CNS inflammation triggering ADH release via an IL-6 mediated pathway (a hypothesis supported by similar inflammatory states). The patient required fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) and vigilant monitoring to prevent rapid overcorrection and osmotic demyelination syndrome.

But what about treating the underlying fire? Standard protocol for acute Neuro-BD screams "High-dose IV steroids!" However, this patient took a detour onto Colchicine monotherapy once the diagnosis became clear after Day 5 when the genital ulcers finally manifested as "the missing piece." Surprisingly—and contrary to some literature suggesting colchicine is ineffective for parenchymal disease—he responded beautifully within days. Fever broke, mental status cleared, sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by Day 16, and he never needed steroids or cyclophosphamide during his acute phase.

While spontaneous remission cannot be entirely ruled out given that some cases of meningoencephalitic Neuro-BD are self-limiting, this case suggests that colchicine might be underutilized as an early intervention tool, particularly when steroid toxicity is a concern or when rapid mucocutaneous control is needed alongside neurological stabilization. It also highlights that SIADH secondary to Neuro-BD resolves when the underlying inflammation is quelled; aggressive hypertonic saline or vaptans weren't necessary once fluid restriction was instituted alongside targeted anti-inflammatory therapy.

### Take-Home Pearls for the Busy Clinician

*   **Don't wait for all symptoms:** Behçet's can present with sequential symptoms where genital ulcers lag behind oral ones by days or weeks. Do not dismiss "Probable Behçet's" if you have oral ulcers + systemic inflammation + compatible neuro symptoms; keep looking for the rest of the clues while working up mimics aggressively but efficiently.
*   **Normal MRI ≠ No Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-involvement can manifest purely as aseptic meningitis with normal brain imaging early in the course Rely heavily on CSF analysis (lymphocytic pleocytosis) when clinical suspicion is high despite negative scans.
*   **Beware of Distractors:** Elevated sIL-2R is common in active inflammation and does not automatically equal lymphoma or sarcoidosis unless supported by strong clinical/radiological correlation Avoid pan-screening autoimmune panels without pre-test probability; let specific clinical features guide your testing strategy to avoid false-positive traps like HLA-B51 positivity in isolation.
*   **Think beyond Steroids:** While corticosteroids remain first-line for acute Neuro-BD monotherapy with colchicine may suffice in select cases presenting primarily with meningoencephalitis/mucocutaneous flares especially if steroid-sparing is desired Monitor closely however as relapse rates vary widely compared to immunosuppressive regimens
*   **Hyponatremia Clues:** Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be subtle presentations of severe hyponatremia driven by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation Treat both ends simultaneously restrict fluids AND address underlying inflammatory drive Don't forget CK levels when evaluating unexplained fatigue/arthritis/fever syndromes!



# Persistent Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis: The Underappreciated Signs of Severe Hyponatremia in Neuro-Inflammation

We've all seen the classic textbook presentation of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH): a euvolemic patient with confusion, nausea, and a serum sodium that refuses to budge above 125 mmol/L. But what happens when the electrolyte disturbance masquerades as something entirely different? What if your primary complaint isn't lethargy, but intractable hiccups and muscle pain?

That is exactly the puzzle presented in a fascinating case report by Kakehi et al., detailing a man in his 60s who walked into the ED with fever, headache, and persistent hiccups. While the eventual diagnosis was acute neurological Behçet's disease (Neuro-BD) complicated by SIADH, the real takeaway for us in the emergency department lies in those two seemingly unrelated symptoms: **hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis**.

## The Case: More Than Just a Headache

This patient arrived with a three-day history of fever, malaise, arthralgia, and those nagging hiccups. On exam, he was febrile (38.1°C) and tachycardic, but notably alert. The physical exam revealed the "classic" mucocutaneous triad of Behçet's—oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes on the trunk, and later, a painful genital ulcer—but at presentation, these were just pieces of a confusing mosaic.

The lab work told a story of severe systemic inflammation (CRP 180 mg/L) and marked hyponatremia (Na+ 124 mmol/L). Crucially, he also had significantly elevated creatine kinase (CK) at 1349 U/L.

Here is where many clinicians might stop looking. They see hyponatremia and assume it's incidental or drug-related. They see elevated CK and worry about sepsis or trauma. But in this case, the hiccups were likely the first sign of hypothalamic irritation driven by severe hyponatremia or direct CNS inflammation from Neuro-BD. The rhabdomyolysis wasn't due to trauma or statins; it was a direct consequence of profound hyponatremia causing cellular swelling and membrane instability in skeletal muscle—a complication often overlooked until it becomes critical.

## The Diagnostic Tightrope: Why This Was Hard

Diagnosing Neuro-BD concurrently with systemic Behçet's is rare; usually, neurological involvement appears years after mucocutaneous symptoms begin. Here, everything happened at once.

The diagnostic journey was fraught with red herrings:
*   **Infection:** With fever and altered mental status (headache/vertigo), viral encephalitis (HSV/VZV) was top of mind. CSF showed lymphocytic pleocytosis and high protein but was negative for all standard pathogens.
*   **Malignancy:** An elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) raised flags for lymphoma, but cytology was negative.
*   **Sarcoidosis:** Normal ACE levels helped rule this out early on.
*   **Imaging Trap:** The brain MRI was completely normal regarding parenchymal lesions or hypothalamic abnormalities. This is a crucial teaching point: **Acute Neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without visible parenchymal lesions.** A normal MRI does *not* rule out CNS inflammation in this context.

The diagnosis eventually clicked when the full International Criteria for Behçet's Disease were met (oral ulcers + genital ulcers + skin lesions = definitive score). Once that label stuck, the SIADH became understandable as a complication of the CNS inflammation itself rather than an isolated endocrine glitch.

## The Treatment Surprise: Colchicine Monotherapy?

Perhaps the most controversial aspect of this case—and one that challenges our usual algorithms—is how it resolved. Standard teaching dictates that acute Neuro-BD requires high-dose corticosteroids or aggressive immunosuppression (like cyclophosphamide). Steroids are usually mandatory to prevent permanent brainstem damage.

Yet, this patient received **colchicine monotherapy**. No steroids were used initially; fluid restriction managed the SIADH conservatively without hypertonic saline or vaptans. Within days, his fever broke, hiccups stopped, mental status cleared, sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L within two weeks, and CK levels plummeted back to baseline over four years without relapse while on colchicine alone.

While we must be cautious about extrapolating one case report to general practice—especially given that spontaneous resolution is possible—the authors suggest that colchicine's ability to inhibit neutrophil migration and suppress cytokines like IL-6 may have targeted both the mucocutaneous flare *and* the CNS-driven SIADH effectively here. It serves as a potent reminder that sometimes less invasive therapy works when we least expect it in inflammatory conditions.

## Why You Should Care About Hiccups and CK Levels

This case drives home two specific clinical pearls that apply far beyond Behçet's disease:

1.  **Persistent Hiccups are a Red Flag:** In an adult presenting with unexplained hiccups alongside fever or headache, think "hypothalamic irritation." Whether due to stroke, tumor, meningitis, or severe electrolyte derangement like SIADH-hyponatremia, hiccups are often dismissed as benign GI issues until they become refractory. In this case, they were an early warning sign of significant CNS/endocrine pathology before frank seizures or coma set in.
2.  **Hyponatremia Can Cause Rhabdo:** We routinely check troponins for chest pain and lipase for abdominal pain when thinking about electrolyte imbalances affecting organs we don't immediately associate with sodium shifts (heart/bowel). Yet we forget skeletal muscle. Profound hyponatremia causes cellular edema that can rupture muscle membranes leading to rhabdomyolysis (as evidenced by this patient's CK >1300). If you see unexplained myalgias or weakness with hyponatremia *without* obvious trauma or statin use**, look closer at the sodium level.**

## Take-Home Points

*   **Don't ignore "benign" symptoms:** Persistent hiccups in a febrile patient with headache should trigger an immediate workup for CNS pathology or severe electrolyte disturbances like SIADH; they may be your earliest clue to hypothalamic dysfunction.
*   **Check CK in severe hyponatremia:** Unexplained rhabdomyolysis can be a direct complication of profound hyponatremia due to cellular swelling; do not attribute myopathy solely to sepsis or drugs without ruling out electrolyte causes first.
*   **Normal MRI $\neq$ No Neuro-BD:** Acute neurological Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis with normal neuroimaging; rely heavily on CSF analysis (lymphocytic pleocytosis) combined with systemic mucocutaneous findings for diagnosis early on before parenchymal lesions develop on MRI.
*   **Think Broadly before Steroids:** While steroids remain first-line for most acute neuro-inflammation cases involving Behçet's, consider alternative anti-inflammatory pathways (like colchicine) in select scenarios where steroid toxicity is high-risk or when mucocutaneous features dominate early presentation—though always weigh risks vs benefits carefully against current guidelines which favor aggressive immunosuppression for parenchymal disease.
