# When Hyponatremia Meets Headache: Unmasking Neuro-Behçet's via SIADH

We love a good "zebra" in the ER, but sometimes the zebras wear stethoscopes and show up with very subtle clues. Imagine a man in his 60s walks into your ED with a three-day history of fever, a pounding headache, and—here is the kicker—persistent hiccups. He looks tired, he's tachycardic, and he has some painful mouth sores. You run the labs, and while the fever points toward infection, the chemistry panel hits you with a hard stop: **severe hyponatremia** (124 mmol/L).

Your mind likely races through the usual suspects: sepsis, pneumonia, maybe subarachnoid hemorrhage? But then you notice something else on his exam: folliculitis-like rashes on his trunk and face. And later that day? A genital ulcer appears.

This isn't just a tricky electrolyte disturbance or a standard viral meningitis. This is a rare confluence of **acute neurological Behçet's disease (Neuro-BD)** manifesting simultaneously with systemic Behçet's, complicated by **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH)**. Even more striking? The brain MRI was completely normal.

Let's dive into how this case challenges our diagnostic algorithms and why SIADH might be the silent herald of CNS inflammation in Behçet's.

## The Presentation: More Than Just Mouth Sores

The patient arrived with fever (38.1°C), headache, malaise, arthralgia, and those persistent hiccups—a classic but often overlooked sign of severe hyponatremia or brainstem irritation. His vitals showed tachycardia, but he was hemodynamically stable otherwise. On physical exam, aside from the obvious oral aphthae and skin rashes, he was neurologically intact regarding strength and reflexes initially. No nuchal rigidity either.

But the lab work told a different story. Beyond the sodium crash to 124 mmol/L, he had sky-high inflammatory markers (CRP 180 mg/L) and significantly elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), hinting at rhabdomyolysis—a complication often missed until it's too late if you don't think about severe hyponatremia first.

The endocrine workup sealed the deal for SIADH: low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), high urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), and elevated ADH levels—all in a euvolemic patient with normal thyroid and adrenal function. Infectious workups for HSV, VZV, TB, and bacteria were all negative. Malignancy screens were unrevealing except for a non-specific bump in sIL-2R.

Then came Day 5: **Genital ulcers.** Combined with the oral aphthae and skin lesions, the diagnosis shifted from "mystery febrile illness" to definitive Behçet's Disease (International Criteria score ≥4). With CNS symptoms persisting despite negative infectious workup, we landed on **Acute Neuro-BD**.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Normal MRI Does Not Mean Nothing Happening

Here is where this case gets tricky for us clinicians. In many textbooks, Neuro-BD is synonymous with parenchymal lesions in the brainstem or basal ganglia visible on MRI. However, acute Neuro-BD can present as **aseptic meningitis** without any structural abnormalities on imaging.

This patient had worsening headache and vertigo on Day 3. We sent him for an MRI of the brain—specifically looking at the hypothalamus—and it was read as normal. If we had stopped there thinking "no lesion = no neuro involvement," we would have missed the diagnosis entirely. The CSF analysis was our saving grace here: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and elevated protein confirmed CNS inflammation even in the absence of radiological evidence.

Furthermore, SIADH is rarely discussed as a primary feature of Neuro-BD unless it's drug-induced (e.g., cyclophosphamide toxicity). This case suggests that **CNS inflammation itself**, potentially driven by IL-6 mediated pathways similar to other neuroinflammatory states, can disrupt ADH regulation directly from within the inflamed meninges or hypothalamus before frank lesions appear on scan.

## Treatment Surprises: Colchicine Monotherapy Works?

Standard teaching for acute Neuro-BD usually screams **"High-dose steroids!"** followed by aggressive immunosuppression like azathioprine or cyclophosphamide if refractory. Steroids are indeed the mainstay for most cases to prevent long-term disability from parenchymal damage.

However, this patient took a different path that offers fascinating food for thought for our practice guidelines in resource-limited settings or steroid-averse patients (like diabetics with poor glycemic control). Once Behçet's was confirmed via clinical criteria (oral + genital ulcers + rash + HLA-B51 positive), they started **Colchicine monotherapy**. No IV methylprednisolone bursts were needed initially; just topical care for ulcers and fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) for SIADH management.

The result? Rapid resolution of fever within 24 hours, gradual improvement of neurological symptoms over two weeks, normalization of sodium by Day 16, and discharge after one month without ever touching systemic steroids or cytotoxic agents at this stage! While literature reviews often cite colchicine as ineffective *alone* for parenchymal Neuro-BD compared to steroids, this case suggests it may hold unexpected efficacy in early-phase meningoencephalitic presentations or pure aseptic meningitis variants of Neuro-BD by dampening neutrophil migration and cytokine production (IL-1β/IL-6). Whether this represents spontaneous remission triggered by colchicine or true monotherapy success requires larger cohorts to confirm—but it definitely warrants keeping colchicine higher on our radar than we currently do for early neuro-involvement without massive mass effect or stroke-like deficits.

## Take-Home Pearls for Your Next Shift

Don't let this rare constellation become your next missed diagnosis when you see these patterns tomorrow morning:

*   **Hyponatremia + Fever + Ulcers = Think Neuro-Behçet's:** If a patient presents with mucocutaneous ulcers (oral/genital/skin) alongside unexplained hyponatremia and CNS symptoms (headache/confusion/hiccups), aggressively rule out SIADH secondary to acute neuro-inflammatory processes before settling on simple dehydration or psychogenic polydipsia.
*   **Normal MRI ≠ Absent CNS Pathology:** Acute Neuro-BD can present purely as aseptic meningitis with normal brain imaging early in the course. If clinical suspicion is high based on systemic signs (ulcers/rash) plus CSF findings (lymphocytic pleocytosis), do not dismiss Neuro-BD solely because the scan is clean.
*   **Persistent Hiccups are Red Flags:** Intractable hiccups can be an early warning sign of severe hyponatremia OR brainstem irritation/inflammation; always check electrolytes immediately when they accompany fever/neurologic complaints.
*   **Check CK Levels:** Severe hyponatremia can precipitate rhabdomyolysis; an elevated CK in this context might be underappreciated muscle injury rather than primary myopathy or trauma alone.

# The Normal MRI Trap: Diagnosing Acute Parenchemal Neuro-Behçet's Without Radiographic Evidence

We've all been there. You're staring at a brain MRI that looks pristine—no parenchymal enhancement, no signal changes in the brainstem, no hypothalamic lesions. Your internal alarm bells start ringing less loudly as you rule out the "big bad" strokes, tumors, and classic demyelinating events. But then you remember the patient sitting in your ED bed: a man in his 60s with fever, persistent hiccups, severe headache, and a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L. If you stop searching once the MRI comes back normal, you might miss a diagnosis that requires immediate intervention.

This week, we're dissecting a fascinating case report from *BMJ Case Reports* that serves as a stark reminder of why we must never let a clean scan override the clinical picture in inflammatory neurology. The authors describe a patient with **Acute Neurological Behçet's Disease (Neuro-BD)** complicated by **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH)**, all while sporting a completely normal brain MRI.

### The Presentation: When Hiccups Mean More Than Indigestion

The patient was an Asian male in his 60s presenting with a three-day history of fever (38.1°C), headache, general malaise, and arthralgia. But two symptoms stood out as red flags for deep-seated pathology: **persistent hiccups** and **hyponatremia**.

Here is where many clinicians would pause and look for pneumonia or medication side effects. But this patient also had worsening vertigo and headache by day 3. ### The Diagnostic Pivot: Trusting the Fluid Over the Film

If you relied solely on the MRI here, you might have dismissed Neuro-BD entirely. However, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) told a different story. Despite the lack of nuchal rigidity or cranial nerve palsies, the LP revealed:
*   Mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O)
*   Elevated protein (0.97 g/L)
*   Lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL)
*   Negative cultures for bacteria, TB, HSV, VZV, and Cryptococcus

### Why This Matters: The Pathophysiology of Silent Inflammation

This case challenges our heuristic that "Neuro-BD = Visible Lesions." While parenchymal involvement in Behçet's typically affects the brainstem and presents months or years after initial diagnosis (mean delay ~4 years), this patient presented with concurrent systemic and neurological disease—a rarity seen in fewer than 5% of cases at onset.

Furthermore, this case illuminates a terrifying but logical connection: **Neuro-BD causing SIADH**.
The mechanism likely involves CNS inflammation driving IL-6 production within the CSF (a known feature of acute Neuro-BD), which stimulates inappropriate ADH release from the posterior pituitary or hypothalamus—even if those structures don't light up on an MRI yet. The persistent hiccups weren't just annoying; they were a hallmark sign of severe hyponatremia driven by this inflammatory cascade. ### Treatment Surprise: Colchicine Monotherapy Wins Big?

Perhaps the most controversial yet exciting aspect of this case is the management strategy. Standard teaching dictates that acute Neuro-BD requires high-dose corticosteroids and often aggressive immunosuppression (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide). Systematic reviews generally label colchicine as ineffective for *acute* neurological involvement compared to mucocutaneous disease.

Yet here? They started **colchicine monotherapy**. No steroids were given initially; topical steroids covered oral ulcers locally; fluid restriction handled the SIADH conservatively without hypertonic saline or vaptans because Na+ was rising steadily but safely under close watch.

Most impressively, by Day 16, his sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L and ADH levels dropped precipitously from 22 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL—all without a single dose of IV methylprednisolone or cyclophosphamide while he was still hospitalized for four weeks before discharge on maintenance colchicine alone for four years with zero recurrence.

While we must be cautious about drawing definitive conclusions from a single case report regarding steroid-sparing efficacy in acute neuro-inflammation, it forces us to reconsider whether every case needs an immediate steroid bomb if colchicine can modulate neutrophil migration and IL-6 sufficiently to halt this specific inflammatory storm. It certainly suggests that in patients where steroids are contraindicated or risky (like our patient with undiagnosed Type 2 Diabetes), alternatives deserve serious consideration earlier than we think.

# Beyond Steroids: Can Colchicine Monotherapy Treat Acute Neuro-Behçet's and Associated SIADH?

We usually think of Behçet's disease (BD) as a slow-burner. You get your oral ulcers, maybe some genital sores or uveitis, and if you're one of the unlucky 10–20% who develop neurological involvement (Neuro-BD), it tends to show up years down the line. But what happens when a patient walks into your ED with fever, headache, and severe hyponatremia, and you realize they have *all* three components—systemic BD, acute Neuro-BD, and Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH)—hitting simultaneously? And what if the standard "high-dose steroids" playbook wasn't actually needed to fix it?

That's exactly the puzzle presented in a fascinating case report by Kakehi et al., describing a man in his 60s who challenged our assumptions about how we treat acute neuro-inflammation in Behçet's. This isn't just another case of "rare disease"; it forces us to rethink the hierarchy of treatment options when the usual suspects don't quite fit or when we want to avoid heavy immunosuppression.

### The Presentation: More Than Just Headache

The patient arrived with a classic triad that often screams "meningitis": fever (38.1°C), severe headache, and altered mental status (fatigue/malaise). But look closer at the vitals: tachycardia at 119 bpm. Look at the labs: CRP was sky-high at 180 mg/L. And then there was the elephant in the room—the sodium was 124 mmol/L.

Initially, this looked like sepsis or viral encephalitis. They even got him on cefepime and acyclovir empirically while waiting for cultures and PCR panels, which all came back negative. The real clues were hiding in plain sight but easy to miss until Day 5: oral aphthae that had been present from the start, followed by a painful scrotal ulcer and folliculitis-like rashes on his trunk. Combined with a positive HLA-B51, he met the International Criteria for Definite BD.

But here is where things get spicy for us emergency physicians and neurologists alike: **the MRI was normal.** No brainstem lesions, no parenchymal enhancement, nothing in the hypothalamus where you'd expect pathology causing SIADH. Yet, he had clear aseptic meningitis on lumbar puncture (lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein) and biochemical proof of SIADH (low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, elevated ADH).

He also had persistent hiccups—a subtle but critical clue often missed as "just annoying," which turned out to be a sign of profound hyponatremia complicating his CNS inflammation. To top it off, he developed rhabdomyolysis (CK ~1350 U/L), likely secondary to the severe electrolyte disturbance itself.

### The Therapeutic Twist: Why Not Steroids?

So far, so standard for a workup of fever + headache + mucocutaneous ulcers = suspect Neuro-BD. Now comes the treatment decision matrix. Current guidelines scream for high-dose IV corticosteroids as first-line therapy for acute parenchymal Neuro-BD to prevent permanent disability. Cyclophosphamide or TNF inhibitors are often added for refractory cases or major vessel involvement.

However, this team took a different road. Recognizing that this patient presented with *acute meningoencephalitis-like symptoms* rather than frank parenchymal destruction on imaging—and wanting to spare an older diabetic patient from massive steroid spikes—they opted for **colchicine monotherapy**. Yes, just colchicine. Plus fluid restriction for the SIADH and topical care for the ulcers.

No dexamethasone pulse doses. No azathioprine loading dose. No biologics yet either.

And guess what happened? Within days, his fever broke, his appetite returned, and his neurological symptoms began to recede. By Day 16, his sodium normalized (from 124 to 136 mmol/L), ADH levels plummeted back to baseline, and his rhabdomyolysis resolved completely over four weeks of colchicine therapy alone. He stayed stable on this regimen for four years without recurrence or need for escalation to steroids or other immunosuppressants during that period.

### What Does This Mean For Us?

This case is provocative because it sits right at odds with established dogma regarding Neuro-BD management while offering a plausible biological mechanism for success elsewhere in medicine: colchicine isn't just an old-school gout drug; it's potent anti-neutrophil agent that suppresses IL-1β and IL-6 production—key drivers in Behçet's pathophysiology and potentially in SIADH-mediated ADH release via hypothalamic inflammation (even if invisible on MRI).

While we must acknowledge that spontaneous remission can occur in aseptic meningitis variants of Neuro-BD (which affects roughly 5% of patients), the concurrent resolution of *three* distinct systems—mucocutaneous lesions, CNS inflammation/SIADH, and rhabdomyolysis—suggests more than luck here. It raises the question: Could colchicine be sufficient as induction therapy for early-phase Neuro-BD presenting primarily as meningoencephalitis without large-vessel or brainstem infarction? Especially in patients where steroids pose significant risks?

It certainly doesn't mean we stop giving steroids tomorrow for everyone with Neuro-BD. If you see a brainstem lesion on MRI or significant mass effect, you still reach for those pulses immediately. But this case argues that we shouldn't reflexively assume every acute neuro-behcet presentation requires nuclear-grade immunosuppression before trying targeted anti-inflammatory strategies like colchicine in select phenotypes. Furthermore, it reminds us that SIADH isn't just a side note; it can be a direct consequence of CNS inflammation that resolves only when the underlying driver is treated effectively enough to calm the cytokine storm (likely IL-6 mediated).

### Take-Home Points

*   **Think Broadly:** In any patient with recurrent oral/genital ulcers presenting with fever and headache—even without prior BD history—consider **Acute Neuro-Behçet's** immediately; do not wait months or years after initial diagnosis rules this out prematurely because concurrent onset is possible though rare (<5%).
*   **Normal MRI Doesn't Rule Out Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis with normal neuroimaging; rely heavily on CSF analysis (lymphocytic pleocytosis) combined with clinical mucocutaneous criteria if infection is ruled out aggressively first.
*   **Hyponatremia & Hiccups Matter:** Persistent hiccups and unexplained hyponatremia/SIADH in this context may signal hypothalamic dysfunction driven by CNS inflammation; watch out for secondary complications like rhabdomyolysis from severe sodium shifts.
*   **Re-evaluate First-Line Therapy:** While corticosteroids remain standard for parenchymal lesions with structural damage on imaging (**brainstem involvement**), **colchicine monotherapy** warrants consideration as an effective initial strategy for acute meningoencephalitic presentations without major structural deficits, potentially sparing patients from steroid toxicity while resolving both neurological and endocrine sequelae simultaneously.



# Persistent Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis: The Underappreciated Clues of Severe Hyponatremia

We tend to think of hyponatremia as a quiet killer—a slow drift in sodium levels that manifests primarily as confusion, lethargy, or seizures once things go south. But what if the warning lights were flashing long before the patient became obtunded? What if they were manifesting as relentless, soul-crushing hiccups and unexplained muscle breakdown?

I recently reviewed a fascinating case report from *BMJ Case Reports* by Kakehi et al. that challenges our standard mental models of how severe hyponatremia presents. It involves a man in his 60s who walked into the ED with fever, headache, and "general malaise," but the real story lies in two specific clues that were almost missed: **persistent hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis**.

This wasn't just simple SIADH. This was a perfect storm where acute neurological Behçet's disease (Neuro-BD) triggered Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH), creating a cascade of electrolyte chaos that masqueraded as a complex infectious or autoimmune workup. Here is why this case demands we re-evaluate our approach to the febrile, confused patient with electrolyte abnormalities.

## The Presentation: More Than Just Confusion

The patient presented with a classic triad of fever, headache, and malaise. On top of that, he had oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes—clues pointing toward an underlying systemic inflammatory process. But the vitals told a different story. He was tachycardic (HR 119), febrile (38.1°C), and critically, he had **marked hyponatremia** at 124 mmol/L.

In many EDs, a sodium of 124 might trigger a fluid restriction order and a "wait-and-see" attitude while you hunt for pneumonia or meningitis. However, this patient had something else going on: **persistent hiccups**. While hiccups are often dismissed as benign irritation from reflux or stress, in the context of CNS inflammation and hyponatremia, they are a red flag for severe electrolyte derangement affecting the phrenic nerve or brainstem centers regulating respiration.

Even more striking was the lab workup for his "malaise." His Creatine Kinase (CK) was sky-high at 1349 U/L. Rhabdomyolysis is rarely discussed as a primary feature of hyponatremia unless we see frank seizure activity or prolonged immobilization. Yet here it was, likely driven by severe hypotonicity causing cellular swelling and membrane instability in skeletal muscle.

## The Diagnostic Maze: When MRI is Normal

The team rightly suspected Central Nervous System (CNS) pathology given the fever, headache, and SIADH profile (low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, elevated ADH). They threw everything at the wall: infectious panels for HSV/VZV/TB were negative; tumor markers were inconclusive; sarcoidosis labs didn't fit perfectly.

Then came the brain MRI. And guess what? **It was normal.** No parenchymal lesions in the brainstem or hypothalamus—the usual suspects for Neuro-BD. This is where many clinicians would throw up their hands and call it "viral prodrome" or "functional." Combined with aseptic meningitis on lumbar puncture (lymphocytic pleocytosis), this was **Acute Neuro-BD**, even without radiographic evidence of parenchymal disease.

The takeaway here is brutal but necessary: A normal brain MRI does not rule out acute neuro-inflammation causing SIADH. If your patient has mucocutaneous signs of BD and CNS symptoms with SIADH, don't let a clean scan stop you from looking deeper into inflammatory etiologies.

## The Twist: Colchicine Monotherapy Wins

Perhaps the most controversial aspect of this case—and the one that generated buzz—is how they treated it. Standard teaching for acute Neuro-BD screams **"High-dose Corticosteroids!"** Often followed by cyclophosphamide or infliximab if refractory. Steroids are potent anti-inflammatories capable of quelling CNS edema rapidly.

Yet, this patient received **colchicine monotherapy**. No steroids. No heavy immunosuppression beyond topical creams for ulcers. They managed his SIADH conservatively with strict fluid restriction (<800 mL/day).

And it worked spectacularly within days. His fever broke, hiccups stopped, mental status cleared, sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16, and CK levels plummeted back to baseline over four weeks without recurrence over four years of follow-up while on colchicine alone. While guidelines usually reserve colchicine for mucocutaneous BD rather than neurological involvement, this case suggests that in select presentations—perhaps those driven heavily by IL-6 mediated inflammation—colchicine's ability to inhibit neutrophil migration and cytokine production might be sufficient to break the cycle of CNS inflammation driving SIADH. Whether this represents true efficacy or spontaneous resolution remains debatable given literature stating colchicine is often ineffective for parenchymal Neuro-BD alone—but it certainly warrants discussion in steroid-sparing scenarios or when contraindications exist.

## Why This Matters for Your Next Shift

This case isn't just about Behçet's disease; it's about pattern recognition in metabolic emergencies driven by hidden inflammation. We often treat numbers (Sodium = 124) rather than syndromes (Hyponatremia + Hiccups + Fever = ?). When you see persistent hiccups in an altered patient with hyponatremia, suspect CNS irritation driving ADH release far earlier than you expect rhabdo to appear—or perhaps recognize that rhabdo is already happening silently until you check that CK panel on every confused febrile patient with low sodium. Don't dismiss these symptoms as incidental; they are often the canary in the coal mine for profound cellular dysfunction waiting to tip into irreversible injury if not corrected gently but urgently against its root cause—inflammation itself rather than just salt deficit alone!

Let's talk about a case that throws a wrench into the standard textbook timeline of Behçet's disease (BD). We're taught that BD is a chronic, relapsing-remitting vasculitis where neurological involvement—Neuro-Behçet's (neuro-BD)—is a late-stage event, typically appearing years after the initial diagnosis of mucocutaneous symptoms. But what happens when the "late" stuff shows up right alongside the "early" stuff? And worse, what if it comes wrapped in a bundle of hyponatremia so severe it mimics a primary endocrine crisis?

That's exactly the scenario presented by Kakehi et al., featuring a man in his 60s who walked into the ED with a classic triad of confusion: fever, headache, and persistent hiccups. Add to that oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L, and you've got a diagnostic maze that could easily lead you down the wrong path toward infection or malignancy.

This case isn't just about checking boxes for the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease; it's a masterclass in recognizing that **acute neuro-BD can present de novo**, simultaneously with systemic signs, and can trigger Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) purely through CNS inflammation.

## When "Classic" Presentations Break the Rules

The patient arrived with fever (38.1°C), tachycardia, and significant malaise. The physical exam was telling: oral aphthae and folliculitis on the trunk and face. Crucially, he didn't develop his genital ulcer until day 5 of admission—a delay that nearly derailed the diagnosis early on. Without that specific lesion immediately visible, he looked like someone with viral encephalitis or perhaps an occult malignancy.

The real curveball was the lab work. He had marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) with low serum osmolality but high urine osmolality and elevated urinary sodium. Endocrine workup ruled out adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism, confirming SIADH. Simultaneously, he had elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 180 mg/L) and even developed rhabdomyolysis (CK 1349 U/L), likely secondary to the profound hyponatremia—a detail often missed unless you think beyond simple electrolytes.

Here is where many clinicians might get stuck: *Is this HSV encephalitis?* *Is this tuberculous meningitis?* Or is it neurosarcoidosis?

The brain MRI was surprisingly quiet. No parenchymal lesions in the brainstem or hypothalamus—the usual suspects for neuro-BD. The CSF showed aseptic meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein) but was negative for bacteria, viruses (HSV/VZV), TB, and malignancy via cytology. Even HLA-B51 positivity wasn't enough to seal the deal on its own; as we know from current criteria, genetics are supportive but not diagnostic without clinical evidence.

## Navigating the Differential Without Going Down Rabbit Holes

The brilliance of this case lies in how they navigated the differential diagnosis without falling into the trap of "fishing expeditions." Initially, they rightly targeted infections with cefepime and acyclovir while waiting for cultures. Once those came back negative, they shifted gears to systemic inflammatory causes.

They screened for sarcoidosis (normal ACE/lysozyme) and hematologic malignancy (mildly elevated sIL-2R). They wisely noted that an isolated elevation in sIL-2R is non-specific in an inflammatory state and shouldn't trigger an immediate lymphoma hunt without other red flags like lymphadenopathy or mass effect on imaging. The absence of neck stiffness made bacterial meningitis less likely despite the fever, but they kept it on the radar until culture results returned negative.

The turning point was clinical patience combined with pattern recognition:
*   **Oral + Genital Ulcers:** The appearance of the scrotal ulcer on day 5 pushed him over the threshold for "Definite BD" according to ICBD scoring (>4 points).
*   **Concurrent Neuro Symptoms:** Fever + Headache + Aseptic Meningitis = Neuro-BD until proven otherwise in this context.
*   **Hyponatremia:** Recognizing SIADH as a complication of CNS inflammation rather than a separate endocrine pathology tied to medication toxicity or pituitary mass effect (which weren't seen on CT/MRI).

It is worth noting that while SIADH is documented in BD patients *on* immunosuppressants like cyclophosphamide, this case suggests SIADH can arise directly from the cytokine storm of acute neuro-inflammation itself—potentially driven by IL-6 pathways common in both BD flare-ups and SIADH pathophysiology.

## Why This Matters: Treatment Implications

Perhaps the most surprising aspect of this report is how it challenges our therapeutic dogma regarding acute neuro-BD. Standard teaching dictates that corticosteroids are mandatory for acute neurological flares to prevent permanent damage or death from brainstem edema/vasculitis. Immunosuppressants like azathioprine or cyclophosphamide are often added for refractory cases or parenchymal involvement.

Yet, this patient received **colchicine monotherapy**. No steroids. No heavy-hitting immunosuppression outside of colchicine plus local wound care for ulcers. Within days of starting colchicine, his fever broke, his mental status improved, his hiccups ceased, his mucocutaneous lesions healed, and critically—his sodium normalized as ADH levels dropped back to baseline by day 16. He went home stable after one month without ever touching prednisone or IVIG/IV steroids again during his hospitalization. Follow-up at four years showed no recurrence while staying on colchicine alone.
