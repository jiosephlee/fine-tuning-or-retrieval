Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiological Framework: Neuro-Behçet's and SIADH Mechanisms

To fully appreciate the diagnostic challenges presented in this case, one must first establish the fundamental pathophysiology of Behçet's disease (BD) and its neurological variant. BD is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory disorder characterized primarily by recurrent oral and genital aphthous ulcers, uveitis, and dermatological lesions such as folliculitis-like rashes. The underlying pathology involves a panvasculitis affecting vessels of all sizes and types, driven by an aberrant immune response where genetic predispositions, notably HLA-B51 positivity in many populations, interact with environmental triggers. While the classic mucocutaneous manifestations are relatively straightforward to recognize, the neurological involvement—known as neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD)—presents a more complex clinical picture. Typically, neuro-BD manifests years after the initial diagnosis of systemic BD, often appearing as parenchymal brain lesions concentrated in the brainstem or diencephalon. Consequently, the simultaneous presentation of acute neuro-BD alongside new-onset systemic BD is a rare occurrence that complicates the diagnostic algorithm, requiring clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion even before the full spectrum of mucocutaneous symptoms has evolved.

The development of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) in this context represents a critical intersection between neuro-inflammation and endocrine dysfunction. SIADH is defined by the excessive release of arginine vasopressin (AVP), formerly known as antidiuretic hormone (ADH), leading to impaired water excretion and consequent dilutional hyponatremia. In cases where SIADH arises from central nervous system pathology rather than medication or malignancy, the mechanism is believed to be driven by local inflammation disrupting the normal regulatory feedback loops within the hypothalamus or pituitary gland. Specifically, proinflammatory cytokines play a pivotal role; interleukin-6 (IL-6) is frequently elevated in acute neuro-BD and has been shown to stimulate AVP secretion directly. In this patient, the intense systemic inflammation evidenced by markedly elevated C-reactive protein likely reflects an underlying surge in IL-6 within the cerebrospinal fluid environment. This cytokine-mediated stimulation overrides physiological osmoreceptors, resulting in sustained ADH release despite hypo-osmolarity, thereby precipitating severe hyponatremia.

Understanding why standard diagnostic imaging failed to reveal structural abnormalities requires an appreciation of the distinct phenotypes of acute neuro-BD. Unlike chronic or progressive forms that present with visible infarcts or mass-like lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), acute presentations often manifest as aseptic meningoencephalitis without parenchymal destruction. In these instances, the pathological process is confined to cellular infiltration and edema within the meninges or superficial cortical layers that may not generate sufficient signal change to be detected by conventional MRI sequences early in the disease course. Therefore, a "normal" MRI does not exclude active CNS inflammation; instead, it necessitates reliance on cerebrospinal fluid analysis for definitive evidence of aseptic meningitis, characterized here by lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein levels despite negative infectious workups.

Finally, the clinical sequelae of severe hyponatremia induced by SIADH extend beyond simple electrolyte imbalance to include specific neuromuscular phenomena observed in this case: persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis. Persistent hiccups serve as a crucial clinical clue pointing toward brainstem irritation or severe metabolic derangement; in this scenario, they likely resulted from direct irritation of the medullary hiccup reflex arc exacerbated by cerebral edema associated with rapid shifts in tonicity. Furthermore, profound hyponatremia can lead to cellular swelling across various tissues, including skeletal muscle membranes. This osmotic stress compromises membrane integrity and ion homeostasis within muscle fibers, potentially triggering rhabdomyolysis—a complication highlighted by this patient's significantly elevated creatine kinase levels. Recognizing these seemingly disparate symptoms as downstream effects of CNS-driven SIADH allows for a cohesive understanding of how localized neuro-inflammation can precipitate widespread systemic complications requiring targeted management strategies beyond simple fluid restriction.
