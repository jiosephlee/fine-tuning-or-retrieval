Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The clinical narrative begins with a woman in her late twenties presenting to a tertiary care center with a five-year history of progressively worsening generalized weakness and early fatigability. Unlike typical presentations of acquired fatigue where symptoms might fluctuate or respond rapidly to rest, this patient experienced a relentless decline in functional capacity that eventually impaired her ability to perform basic activities of daily living. Over the preceding three months, the trajectory accelerated, necessitating assistance with self-care and accompanied by exertional dyspnea, intermittent chest discomfort, significant weight loss, and anorexia. This temporal pattern of insidious onset followed by rapid decompensation in a young adult immediately raises suspicion for a systemic process rather than an acute infectious or localized etiology. The sheer duration of symptoms combined with their progressive nature suggests a chronic, underlying pathology that has been slowly eroding physiological reserves across multiple organ systems.

Physical examination provided critical clues that began to shift the diagnostic focus away from simple nutritional deficiency toward a syndromic or genetic disorder. Beyond the expected pallor associated with anemia, the patient exhibited stigmata of premature aging that are discordant with her chronological age. Key findings included premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with atrophy of the lingual papillae. Musculoskeletal evaluation revealed thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, while abdominal palpation confirmed significant hepatomegaly extending 5 cm below the right costal margin. Respiratory auscultation detected reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scapular regions, hinting at underlying parenchymal lung disease. The convergence of dermatological signs suggestive of accelerated aging (hyperpigmentation, greying), skeletal abnormalities (scoliosis), visceral enlargement (hepatosplenomegaly), and respiratory compromise creates a complex clinical picture that defies explanation by a single common acquired condition.

Laboratory investigations initially reinforced the complexity of the case by revealing pancytopenia characterized by a profoundly low reticulocyte count of 0.39%. The peripheral blood smear displayed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—morphological hallmarks classically associated with megaloblastic anemia due to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. Serum studies confirmed severe deficiencies in both vitamins B12 and folate. However, the clinical reasoning must extend beyond confirming these deficiencies; one must interrogate why such profound systemic collapse occurred in a young patient solely based on nutritional status. While the bone marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis driven by nutrient lack, the persistence of multisystem involvement—including liver fibrosis and pulmonary changes—despite these findings suggested that nutritional deficiency might be either a comorbidity or an exacerbating factor rather than the sole driver of the disease process.

The central diagnostic dilemma lies in distinguishing between an isolated acquired nutritional deficit and an underlying genetic telomere biology disorder (TBD) masquerading as such. In typical scenarios, correcting severe vitamin B12 deficiency leads to rapid hematological recovery and resolution of neurological symptoms within weeks. In this case, however, the constellation of cytopenia coupled with organ fibrosis (hepatic and pulmonary) and premature aging features created a "red flag" scenario where the severity of organ damage appeared disproportionate to the degree of malnutrition alone. The presence of hepatosplenomegaly and pulmonary fibrosis in a patient under thirty is exceptionally rare for pure nutritional anemia but aligns perfectly with the phenotype of TBDs, where defective telomere maintenance leads to accelerated cellular senescence in high-turnover tissues like bone marrow, liver epithelium, and lung alveoli. Recognizing this discrepancy is pivotal; it forces the clinician to look past the obvious metabolic abnormality and consider rare monogenic disorders that present with overlapping features of marrow failure and organ fibrosis before irreversible damage ensues.

## Pathophysiological Framework: Telomere Biology Disorders

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a spectrum of premature aging syndromes rooted in the inability of cells to maintain chromosomal integrity during replication. At the core of this pathophysiology lies the telomere, a repetitive nucleotide sequence capping the ends of linear chromosomes that protects them from degradation and fusion. In normal physiology, telomeres shorten with every cell division; once they reach a critically short length, cells enter a state of irreversible growth arrest known as senescence or undergo apoptosis. To counteract this attrition, actively dividing tissues rely on the enzyme telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein complex composed primarily of the catalytic subunit telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the RNA template component (TERC). Telomerase functions by adding telomeric repeats to chromosome ends, thereby preserving replicative potential. When genetic defects impair the synthesis, assembly, or function of telomerase components or their accessory proteins, telomeres shorten prematurely. This "telomere crisis" disproportionately affects tissues with high turnover rates—specifically hematopoietic stem cells, epithelial linings of the skin and gut, and hepatocytes—leading to the classic manifestations of bone marrow failure, mucocutaneous abnormalities, and organ fibrosis.

The clinical presentation of TBDs varies widely depending on the specific gene involved, the mode of inheritance, and the age at onset. Historically, dyskeratosis congenita (DC) served as the prototype for these disorders, characterized by a classic mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and abnormal skin pigmentation. However, modern understanding recognizes that many TBDs present later in life without these hallmark dermatological features. Instead, adult-onset presentations often manifest as isolated idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), cryptorchidism-associated liver disease (cirrhosis/fibrosis), or unexplained bone marrow failure. This phenotypic heterogeneity complicates diagnosis, particularly when patients lack the traditional triad but exhibit multisystem involvement suggestive of accelerated aging. The underlying mechanism remains consistent across these varied presentations: insufficient telomere maintenance leads to replicative exhaustion in stem cell compartments across multiple organ systems.

In the specific context of this case report involving *PARN* mutations, it is essential to understand that *PARN* encodes poly(A)-specific ribonuclease A homologue 1. Unlike structural components like TERT or TERC directly incorporated into the holoenzyme, *PARN* plays a critical regulatory role in telomerase biogenesis. Specifically, *PARN* is responsible for trimming post-transcriptionally added oligo(A) tails from precursor TERC transcripts during their nuclear maturation process. Without functional *PARN*, TERC fails to mature correctly and is targeted for degradation rather than being assembled into active telomerase complexes. Consequently, even if TERT levels are normal, the lack of sufficient mature TERC template results in inadequate telomere elongation. This molecular bottleneck explains why *PARN* deficiency leads to short telomeres and subsequent tissue failure despite not being a direct catalytic component of the enzyme itself.

The distinction between biallelic and monoallelic *PARN* variants dictates both inheritance patterns and clinical severity within this spectrum. Biallelic loss-of-function mutations cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6), often presenting in childhood with severe phenotypes resembling Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome or Revesz syndrome, characterized by profound immunodeficiency and cerebellar hypoplasia alongside bone marrow failure. In contrast, monoallelic germline variants are increasingly recognized as causes of adult-onset TBDs, specifically categorized under pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure type 4 (PFBMFT4). Patients with monoallelic mutations typically present with less severe but progressive organ fibrosis—most notably pulmonary and hepatic—and cytopenias later in life. As illustrated by this case report's patient who carried a monoallelic missense variant (*c.*613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), these individuals may lack classical cutaneous signs entirely yet still suffer from significant multisystem morbidity driven by haploinsufficiency or dominant-negative effects on telomere maintenance machinery. Recognizing this genotype-phenotype correlation is vital for clinicians evaluating young adults with unexplained fibrosis or cytopenia where conventional etiologies have been excluded.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Nutritional Deficiency to Genetic Etiology

The diagnostic journey in this case began with a classic presentation of severe vitamin B12 deficiency, characterized by pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, and neurological symptoms. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed a low reticulocyte count alongside peripheral smear findings of hypersegmented neutrophils and basophilic stippling, which are hallmarks of megaloblastic anemia driven by ineffective erythropoiesis. Bone marrow examination corroborated this with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, effectively ruling out primary marrow aplasia or infiltration at that stage. However, the clinical reasoning process encountered a critical fork when the patient failed to exhibit the expected robust hematologic recovery after aggressive repletion of B12 and folate. Furthermore, the persistence of systemic symptoms—specifically the rapid progression of hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary compromise, and premature aging signs like greying hair and osteoporosis—was disproportionately severe for a nutritional deficit alone in a woman in her late twenties. This discrepancy forced clinicians to look beyond the metabolic correction and consider an underlying structural or genetic vulnerability that rendered the bone marrow unable to sustain regeneration even when substrates were available.

Simultaneously, the presence of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG initially raised suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). A rigorous exclusionary workup was therefore undertaken to differentiate between peripheral destruction and central production failure. While markers of hemolysis such as elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin were present, the combination of a low reticulocyte response and bone marrow findings indicated that red cell destruction was not the primary driver; rather, the marrow was failing to produce sufficient cells to compensate for turnover. The negative autoimmune serologies ruled out connective tissue diseases like lupus or autoimmune hepatitis, while extensive infectious screening excluded viral etiologies such as HIV or active hepatitis viruses. Malignancy was similarly scrutinized; despite weight loss and constitutional symptoms, biopsies and cytogenetics showed no evidence of leukemia or lymphoma. The inability to attribute the multisystem collapse to these common acquired causes necessitated a shift toward evaluating rare syndromic disorders.

Advanced imaging played a pivotal role in visualizing the extent of this multisystem involvement and guiding the differential diagnosis away from localized pathology. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed inhomogeneous fluorodeoxydeoxyglucose uptake across both axial and appendicular bone marrow, suggesting a global disturbance in hematopoietic activity rather than focal metastasis or infection. More critically, the scan highlighted diffuse ground-glass opacities with subpleural fibrotic changes in the lungs alongside significant hepatic stiffness on elastography confirming cirrhosis. This pattern of simultaneous pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and bone marrow dysfunction created a specific clinical signature: a "telomere biology disorder" (TBD). In typical presentations of TBDs like dyskeratosis congenita, one might expect the classic mucocutaneous triad; however, this case illustrated that adult-onset TBDs can present primarily with organ fibrosis and cytopenias without overt skin or nail changes initially. The imaging confirmed that the pathology was systemic and degenerative rather than inflammatory or neoplastic.

The definitive pivot point in this case occurred upon recognizing that this constellation of findings represented "accelerated aging" in a young adult where standard acquired causes had been exhausted. The coexistence of pancytopenia resistant to nutritional therapy, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), liver cirrhosis without viral etiology, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and premature greying formed a cohesive syndromic picture pointing toward defective telomere maintenance. Telomeres protect chromosomal ends during replication; their premature shortening leads to cellular senescence in high-turnover tissues like bone marrow skin and epithelia, as well as stromal tissues like lung parenchyma and liver sinusoids. Consequently, clinical exome sequencing was pursued not merely as an exploratory tool but as a targeted investigation based on this strong phenotypic correlation. The identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant provided the unifying genetic explanation for why this patient's organ systems failed prematurely despite her young age, transforming the diagnosis from an isolated nutritional deficiency into a complex genetic telomere biology disorder requiring lifelong surveillance for malignancy and organ failure progression.

## Genetic Confirmation and Variant Interpretation Challenges

The definitive diagnostic pivot in this case occurred upon clinical exome sequencing, which identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While the identification of a genetic alteration provided a molecular anchor for the patient's multisystem presentation, the immediate challenge lay in interpreting a finding classified as a "Variant of Uncertain Significance" (VUS). In the context of telomere biology disorders (TBDs), where phenotypic expressivity is highly variable and many pathogenic variants are private or ultra-rare, distinguishing a causative mutation from a benign polymorphism requires rigorous application of structural biology knowledge and standardized classification frameworks. The specific variant affects codon 205 within exon 8, altering a cysteine residue to arginine. This amino acid substitution occurs within the R3H domain (residues 178–245), a highly conserved region critical for the protein's function as an exoribonuclease involved in the maturation of telomerase RNA component (TERC). The high degree of evolutionary conservation at this locus suggests that any disruption here is likely detrimental to protein stability or enzymatic activity, serving as an initial red flag for pathogenicity despite the lack of prior reports for this exact change.

To move beyond heuristic assessment, the variant was subjected to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) classification guidelines. Several lines of evidence supported upgrading the likelihood of pathogenicity despite the initial VUS label. First, the variant met criterion PM1 because it resides in a mutational hotspot or critical functional domain where no benign variation has been observed; specifically, no benign missense variants are recorded in population databases for this segment of *PARN*. Second, criterion PM2 was satisfied as the variant is absent or extremely rare in large-scale population datasets like gnomAD, indicating it is not a common polymorphism. Furthermore, multiple independent *in silico* prediction algorithms—including LRT, MutationAssessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT—converged on predicting a deleterious effect on protein function (PP3). Finally, given that *PARN* is a gene intolerant to missense variation and where missense mutations are a known mechanism of disease (PP2), the aggregate data strongly suggested that this specific nucleotide change disrupts normal gene function. However, without segregation data from family members or functional assays demonstrating reduced telomerase activity directly attributable to this specific allele, strict adherence to guidelines necessitated retaining the VUS designation until further validation could be achieved.

A central complexity in reconciling this genetic finding with the patient's severe clinical phenotype involves understanding the inheritance pattern and dosage sensitivity of *PARN*. Classically, biallelic loss-of-function variants in *PARN* cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6), often presenting with severe childhood-onset features like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Conversely, monoallelic variants are increasingly recognized as causes of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4), typically with later onset and variable penetrance. The presence of significant organ fibrosis and pancytopenia in this young adult with only one mutated allele raises questions regarding haploinsufficiency versus dominant-negative mechanisms. Current literature supports haploinsufficiency as a plausible driver; *PARN* is among the genes least tolerant to heterozygous loss-of-function variants, suggesting that having only one functional copy reduces total cellular PARN levels below a threshold required for efficient TERC maturation. This reduction leads to accelerated telomere shortening across actively dividing tissues. In this patient's case, it is hypothesized that while the monoallelic mutation created a baseline susceptibility by compromising telomere maintenance in hematopoietic stem cells and hepatocytes, severe nutritional stressors—specifically profound vitamin B12 deficiency—acted as precipitating factors that overwhelmed these already compromised regenerative capacities, unmasking frank bone marrow failure and accelerating fibrotic processes earlier than might occur with age-matched controls carrying similar genotypes but better nutritional status.

Synthesizing available literature reveals that while monoallelic *PARN* mutations are most frequently associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in older adults without overt bone marrow failure, they represent an expanding spectrum capable of causing multiorgan involvement including hepatic fibrosis and cytopenias. Reports indicate that approximately 2% of sporadic IPF cases harbor rare monoallelic *PARN* variants, yet cases presenting with concurrent liver disease are less common but documented. For instance, comparative cases have shown patients with dual heterozygous mutations (*PARN* combined with *TERT*) developing both pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis; however, this patient demonstrates that monogenic heterozygosity can suffice for broad multisystem involvement when compounded by environmental or metabolic stressors. The discrepancy between typical presentations (isolated lung disease) and this case's severity underscores the concept of incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity inherent to TBDs. Consequently, identifying this VUS allowed clinicians to reframe the diagnosis from disparate acquired conditions to a unified genetic etiology: a telomere biology disorder driven by *PARN* insufficiency. This distinction is clinically vital not only for prognosis but also for guiding surveillance strategies for malignancy risks and informing reproductive counseling for family members who may carry similar asymptomatic alleles predisposing them to future organ failure under specific stress conditions.
