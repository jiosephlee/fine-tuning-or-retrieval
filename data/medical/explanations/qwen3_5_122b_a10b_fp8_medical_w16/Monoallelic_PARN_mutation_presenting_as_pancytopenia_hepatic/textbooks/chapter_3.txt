Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Nutritional Deficiency to Genetic Etiology

The diagnostic journey in this case began with a classic presentation of severe vitamin B12 deficiency, characterized by pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, and neurological symptoms. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed a low reticulocyte count alongside peripheral smear findings of hypersegmented neutrophils and basophilic stippling, which are hallmarks of megaloblastic anemia driven by ineffective erythropoiesis. Bone marrow examination corroborated this with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, effectively ruling out primary marrow aplasia or infiltration at that stage. However, the clinical reasoning process encountered a critical fork when the patient failed to exhibit the expected robust hematologic recovery after aggressive repletion of B12 and folate. Furthermore, the persistence of systemic symptoms—specifically the rapid progression of hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary compromise, and premature aging signs like greying hair and osteoporosis—was disproportionately severe for a nutritional deficit alone in a woman in her late twenties. This discrepancy forced clinicians to look beyond the metabolic correction and consider an underlying structural or genetic vulnerability that rendered the bone marrow unable to sustain regeneration even when substrates were available.

Simultaneously, the presence of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG initially raised suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). A rigorous exclusionary workup was therefore undertaken to differentiate between peripheral destruction and central production failure. While markers of hemolysis such as elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin were present, the combination of a low reticulocyte response and bone marrow findings indicated that red cell destruction was not the primary driver; rather, the marrow was failing to produce sufficient cells to compensate for turnover. The negative autoimmune serologies ruled out connective tissue diseases like lupus or autoimmune hepatitis, while extensive infectious screening excluded viral etiologies such as HIV or active hepatitis viruses. Malignancy was similarly scrutinized; despite weight loss and constitutional symptoms, biopsies and cytogenetics showed no evidence of leukemia or lymphoma. The inability to attribute the multisystem collapse to these common acquired causes necessitated a shift toward evaluating rare syndromic disorders.

Advanced imaging played a pivotal role in visualizing the extent of this multisystem involvement and guiding the differential diagnosis away from localized pathology. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed inhomogeneous fluorodeoxydeoxyglucose uptake across both axial and appendicular bone marrow, suggesting a global disturbance in hematopoietic activity rather than focal metastasis or infection. More critically, the scan highlighted diffuse ground-glass opacities with subpleural fibrotic changes in the lungs alongside significant hepatic stiffness on elastography confirming cirrhosis. This pattern of simultaneous pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and bone marrow dysfunction created a specific clinical signature: a "telomere biology disorder" (TBD). In typical presentations of TBDs like dyskeratosis congenita, one might expect the classic mucocutaneous triad; however, this case illustrated that adult-onset TBDs can present primarily with organ fibrosis and cytopenias without overt skin or nail changes initially. The imaging confirmed that the pathology was systemic and degenerative rather than inflammatory or neoplastic.

The definitive pivot point in this case occurred upon recognizing that this constellation of findings represented "accelerated aging" in a young adult where standard acquired causes had been exhausted. The coexistence of pancytopenia resistant to nutritional therapy, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), liver cirrhosis without viral etiology, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and premature greying formed a cohesive syndromic picture pointing toward defective telomere maintenance. Telomeres protect chromosomal ends during replication; their premature shortening leads to cellular senescence in high-turnover tissues like bone marrow skin and epithelia, as well as stromal tissues like lung parenchyma and liver sinusoids. Consequently, clinical exome sequencing was pursued not merely as an exploratory tool but as a targeted investigation based on this strong phenotypic correlation. The identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant provided the unifying genetic explanation for why this patient's organ systems failed prematurely despite her young age, transforming the diagnosis from an isolated nutritional deficiency into a complex genetic telomere biology disorder requiring lifelong surveillance for malignancy and organ failure progression.
