Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 4: ## Genetic Confirmation and Variant Interpretation Challenges

The definitive diagnostic pivot in this case occurred upon clinical exome sequencing, which identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While the identification of a genetic alteration provided a molecular anchor for the patient's multisystem presentation, the immediate challenge lay in interpreting a finding classified as a "Variant of Uncertain Significance" (VUS). In the context of telomere biology disorders (TBDs), where phenotypic expressivity is highly variable and many pathogenic variants are private or ultra-rare, distinguishing a causative mutation from a benign polymorphism requires rigorous application of structural biology knowledge and standardized classification frameworks. The specific variant affects codon 205 within exon 8, altering a cysteine residue to arginine. This amino acid substitution occurs within the R3H domain (residues 178–245), a highly conserved region critical for the protein's function as an exoribonuclease involved in the maturation of telomerase RNA component (TERC). The high degree of evolutionary conservation at this locus suggests that any disruption here is likely detrimental to protein stability or enzymatic activity, serving as an initial red flag for pathogenicity despite the lack of prior reports for this exact change.

To move beyond heuristic assessment, the variant was subjected to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) classification guidelines. Several lines of evidence supported upgrading the likelihood of pathogenicity despite the initial VUS label. First, the variant met criterion PM1 because it resides in a mutational hotspot or critical functional domain where no benign variation has been observed; specifically, no benign missense variants are recorded in population databases for this segment of *PARN*. Second, criterion PM2 was satisfied as the variant is absent or extremely rare in large-scale population datasets like gnomAD, indicating it is not a common polymorphism. Furthermore, multiple independent *in silico* prediction algorithms—including LRT, MutationAssessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT—converged on predicting a deleterious effect on protein function (PP3). Finally, given that *PARN* is a gene intolerant to missense variation and where missense mutations are a known mechanism of disease (PP2), the aggregate data strongly suggested that this specific nucleotide change disrupts normal gene function. However, without segregation data from family members or functional assays demonstrating reduced telomerase activity directly attributable to this specific allele, strict adherence to guidelines necessitated retaining the VUS designation until further validation could be achieved.

A central complexity in reconciling this genetic finding with the patient's severe clinical phenotype involves understanding the inheritance pattern and dosage sensitivity of *PARN*. Classically, biallelic loss-of-function variants in *PARN* cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6), often presenting with severe childhood-onset features like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Conversely, monoallelic variants are increasingly recognized as causes of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4), typically with later onset and variable penetrance. The presence of significant organ fibrosis and pancytopenia in this young adult with only one mutated allele raises questions regarding haploinsufficiency versus dominant-negative mechanisms. Current literature supports haploinsufficiency as a plausible driver; *PARN* is among the genes least tolerant to heterozygous loss-of-function variants, suggesting that having only one functional copy reduces total cellular PARN levels below a threshold required for efficient TERC maturation. This reduction leads to accelerated telomere shortening across actively dividing tissues. In this patient's case, it is hypothesized that while the monoallelic mutation created a baseline susceptibility by compromising telomere maintenance in hematopoietic stem cells and hepatocytes, severe nutritional stressors—specifically profound vitamin B12 deficiency—acted as precipitating factors that overwhelmed these already compromised regenerative capacities, unmasking frank bone marrow failure and accelerating fibrotic processes earlier than might occur with age-matched controls carrying similar genotypes but better nutritional status.

Synthesizing available literature reveals that while monoallelic *PARN* mutations are most frequently associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in older adults without overt bone marrow failure, they represent an expanding spectrum capable of causing multiorgan involvement including hepatic fibrosis and cytopenias. Reports indicate that approximately 2% of sporadic IPF cases harbor rare monoallelic *PARN* variants, yet cases presenting with concurrent liver disease are less common but documented. For instance, comparative cases have shown patients with dual heterozygous mutations (*PARN* combined with *TERT*) developing both pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis; however, this patient demonstrates that monogenic heterozygosity can suffice for broad multisystem involvement when compounded by environmental or metabolic stressors. The discrepancy between typical presentations (isolated lung disease) and this case's severity underscores the concept of incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity inherent to TBDs. Consequently, identifying this VUS allowed clinicians to reframe the diagnosis from disparate acquired conditions to a unified genetic etiology: a telomere biology disorder driven by *PARN* insufficiency. This distinction is clinically vital not only for prognosis but also for guiding surveillance strategies for malignancy risks and informing reproductive counseling for family members who may carry similar asymptomatic alleles predisposing them to future organ failure under specific stress conditions.
