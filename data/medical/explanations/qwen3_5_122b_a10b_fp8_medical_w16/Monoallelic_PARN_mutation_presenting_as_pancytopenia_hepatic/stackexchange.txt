Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult presenting with pancytopenia, premature greying, and pulmonary fibrosis, why might the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation) be absent, and what alternative clinical clues should raise suspicion for a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD)?

In a young adult presenting with pancytopenia, premature greying, and pulmonary fibrosis, the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation) does not rule out a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD). As illustrated in this case of a woman in her late 20s with a monoallelic *PARN* mutation, TBDs exhibit significant phenotypic heterogeneity and variable expressivity. The classic triad represents the prototypical presentation of severe, often childhood-onset forms (such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome); however, adult-onset TBDs frequently manifest with "cryptic" presentations where organ-specific failure precedes or entirely supersedes cutaneous findings.

The pathophysiological basis for this discrepancy lies in the differential sensitivity of tissues to telomere attrition. While rapidly renewing epithelial tissues (mucosa, nails, skin) are hallmark targets, hematopoietic stem cells, pulmonary alveolar type II cells, and hepatic stellate cells are equally dependent on telomerase activity for replication. In cases involving monoallelic variants in genes like *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC*, the cumulative effect of shortened telomeres may reach a critical threshold in high-turnover internal organs before manifesting externally. Consequently, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for TBD when encountering unexplained multisystem fibrosis or bone marrow failure in young patients, particularly when accompanied by subtle stigmata of accelerated aging such as premature greying (as seen in our patient), osteoporosis, or neuropathy.

Alternative clinical clues that should raise suspicion for a TBD in the absence of the classic triad include:
1.  **Unexplained Organ Fibrosis:** The concurrent presence of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis in a patient under age 50 is highly suggestive. Our patient's combination of interstitial lung disease and liver stiffness (cirrhosis) without traditional etiologies was pivotal in shifting the differential away from nutritional or autoimmune causes.
2.  **Disproportionate Marrow Failure:** Bone marrow failure that persists despite correction of reversible causes (e.g., severe Vitamin B12 deficiency) or presents with features of ineffective erythropoiesis should prompt genetic evaluation.
3.  **Family History Nuances:** A lack of family history does not exclude TBD; de novo mutations occur, and autosomal dominant inheritance patterns can show reduced penetrance or variable onset ages across generations.
4.  **Extra-hematologic Features:** Premature greying, skeletal abnormalities (osteoporosis/scoliosis), and cryptorchidism or gonadal failure serve as important "soft signs" pointing toward a systemic telomeropathy.

Ultimately, this case underscores that relying solely on mucocutaneous findings leads to missed diagnoses. The diagnosis rests on recognizing the pattern of multisystem organ failure driven by replicative senescence and confirming it via flow-FISH for telomere length and targeted next-generation sequencing panels for telomere-related genes. Early recognition is critical for appropriate surveillance for malignancy and timely consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation before irreversible organ damage ensues.

### [Pathophysiology] How do biallelic versus monoallelic pathogenic variants in the PARN gene typically correlate with distinct clinical phenotypes within the spectrum of telomere biology disorders?

In the spectrum of telomere biology disorders (TBDs), the zygosity of *PARN* pathogenic variants serves as a critical discriminator for clinical phenotype and inheritance pattern. **Biallelic** (autosomal recessive) loss-of-function variants in *PARN* typically manifest as Dyskeratosis Congenita, Autosomal Recessive Type 6 (DKCB6). This form often presents earlier in life with severe, classic features such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, characterized by profound bone marrow failure, cerebellar hypoplasia, and immunodeficiency.

Conversely, **monoallelic** (heterozygous) variants in *PARN* are increasingly recognized as a cause of dominant telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (designated PFBMFT4). As illustrated in our case report, patients with monoallelic *PARN* mutations frequently present in adulthood with a "cryptic" phenotype that lacks the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. Instead, they exhibit multisystem organ fibrosis—specifically idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and hepatic fibrosis—and variable degrees of bone marrow failure or cytopenias.

The pathophysiological basis for this distinction lies in the haploinsufficiency of *PARN*, an exoribonuclease essential for the 3'-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (*TERC*). Even a single defective allele impairs telomerase activity enough to accelerate telomere attrition in high-turnover tissues. However, unlike the catastrophic failure seen in biallelic loss, monoallelic defects often allow for survival into adulthood until cumulative replicative stress precipitates organ-specific failure. Our patient's presentation of pancytopenia, hepatic cirrhosis, and pulmonary fibrosis in her late 20s, driven by a monoallelic missense variant (c.613T>C), exemplifies this expanded phenotype where liver and lung fibrosis dominate over cutaneous stigmata. Consequently, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for TBDs in young adults with unexplained multiorgan fibrosis or cytopenias, even in the absence of family history or classic skin findings.

### [Clinical Pearls] Despite identifying a 'Variant of Uncertain Significance' (VUS) in the PARN gene, what specific clinical-genetic correlations in this case justified classifying the patient's condition as a probable Telomere Biology Disorder?

In this case, the classification of a probable Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD) despite a "Variant of Uncertain Significance" (VUS) in *PARN* rests on the power of **phenotype-driven genetic interpretation**. While the specific missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) lacks definitive population-level evidence for pathogenicity, the clinical reasoning hinges on three converging pillars: the biological plausibility of the gene, the location of the mutation, and the specificity of the multisystem phenotype.

First, we must recognize that *PARN* is a highly constrained gene where haploinsufficiency is a known disease mechanism. The identified variant affects a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain, a region critical for telomerase RNA component (TERC) maturation. Computational models strongly predict a deleterious effect, and similar variants in this domain have been classified as likely pathogenic elsewhere. Therefore, biologically, this variant fits the profile of a damaging allele capable of disrupting telomere maintenance.

Second, and most critically, the patient's clinical presentation aligns perfectly with the expanding spectrum of *monoallelic* *PARN*-associated disease. Unlike biallelic *PARN* mutations which cause classic autosomal recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita (often with severe mucocutaneous features), monoallelic variants typically present in adulthood with pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. This patient exhibited exactly that constellation: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, and pancytopenia accompanied by premature aging stigmata (premature greying, osteoporosis). The presence of hepatic fibrosis alongside pulmonary involvement in a young adult with cytopenias is highly specific for a telomereopathy when common etiologies like viral hepatitis or autoimmunity are excluded.

Finally, we apply the principle that in rare monogenic disorders, a strong genotype-phenotype correlation can upgrade a VUS to "pathogenic" or "probable" status for clinical decision-making. The patient's symptoms were disproportionate to her concurrent vitamin B12 deficiency; even after metabolic correction, the multisystem fibrotic burden persisted. This persistence confirms an underlying structural/genetic defect rather than an acquired nutritional one. Thus, treating this as a TBD allows us to guide appropriate surveillance for malignancy and consider hematopoietic stem cell transplantation if marrow failure progresses, acknowledging that the genetic finding explains the unique convergence of lung, liver, and marrow failure in this young woman.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why is hepatic fibrosis increasingly recognized as a significant manifestation of telomere biology disorders, and how does its prevalence compare to that of pulmonary fibrosis in patients with TERT or TERC mutations?

Hepatic fibrosis is increasingly recognized as a significant manifestation of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) because the liver, much like the bone marrow and lungs, relies heavily on rapid cellular turnover; consequently, it is uniquely vulnerable to the premature senescence driven by critically short telomeres. Historically, the focus in TBDs has centered on the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita or isolated pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. However, recent registry data and case series reveal that hepatic involvement is far more common than previously appreciated and often precedes overt liver failure.

Regarding prevalence, hepatic fibrosis appears to occur at rates comparable to, if not exceeding, those of pulmonary fibrosis in patients with *TERT* or *TERC* mutations. As highlighted in the discussion of our case report, a retrospective analysis from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography demonstrated that **88.8%** of adult patients with pathogenic *TERT* or *TERC* mutations exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, with one-third progressing to frank cirrhosis. This stands in stark contrast to earlier assumptions that liver disease was a rare complication.

In the specific context of our patient's monoallelic *PARN* mutation—which typically presents with pulmonary fibrosis—we observe an expansion of the phenotype where hepatic fibrosis manifests alongside pulmonary disease. This reinforces the principle that TBDs represent a systemic disorder of regenerative failure rather than an isolated organ pathology. Therefore, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for liver disease in any young adult presenting with unexplained cytopenias or interstitial lung disease, regardless of whether classical dermatologic stigmata are present, as silent hepatic fibrosis may be a dominant feature of their genetic burden.

### [Pathophysiology] Given that this patient had severe Vitamin B12 deficiency and effective erythropoiesis markers suggestive of megaloblastic anemia, what is the proposed mechanistic interaction between acute nutritional stressors (like B12 deficiency) and an underlying genetic predisposition to bone marrow failure in TBDs?

In this case, the interplay between severe Vitamin B12 deficiency and the underlying *PARN* mutation illustrates how an acute metabolic insult can unmask a latent genetic predisposition to bone marrow failure. The proposed mechanistic interaction centers on the concept of **replicative reserve exhaustion** in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs).

Patients with Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs), such as those caused by *PARN* variants, harbor HSPCs with inherently shortened telomeres due to defective telomerase RNA component (TERC) maturation. These cells possess a critically reduced "replicative reserve," meaning they have fewer divisions available before triggering senescence or apoptosis compared to wild-type cells. Under baseline conditions, this deficit may remain subclinical or compensated. However, severe Vitamin B12 deficiency induces a state of profound **ineffective erythropoiesis**. As evidenced by this patient's dimorphic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, and elevated LDH with a paradoxically low reticulocyte count, the marrow attempts high-output proliferation that fails due to impaired DNA synthesis (the "megaloblast" block).

This creates a "perfect storm": the genetic defect limits the maximum proliferative capacity of the stem cell pool, while the nutritional stressor demands rapid expansion that exceeds this compromised threshold. Consequently, the stressed HSPCs undergo accelerated senescence and depletion much faster than they would in isolation. While B12 replacement can reverse the megaloblastic changes temporarily, it does not restore telomere length or the fundamental replicative limit imposed by the *PARN* mutation. Therefore, in TBDs, acute nutritional stressors act as precipitants that push already fragile hematopoietic compartments into frank bone marrow failure earlier than anticipated, highlighting why clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for underlying genetic etiologies even when reversible nutritional causes are present.

### [Management] When evaluating a young patient with unexplained multisystem fibrosis (lung and liver) and cytopenias where autoimmune and infectious etiologies are excluded, what is the recommended diagnostic algorithm to confirm a suspected Telomere Biology Disorder beyond standard genetic panels?

In a young patient presenting with unexplained multisystem fibrosis involving the lung and liver alongside cytopenias, where common autoimmune and infectious etiologies have been rigorously excluded, the diagnostic algorithm must pivot immediately toward Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs), even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. As illustrated in the case of *PARN*-associated TBD, relying solely on standard genetic panels can be insufficient if they yield Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS) or fail to capture monoallelic pathogenic mechanisms that drive disease.

The definitive confirmation requires a multimodal approach combining advanced molecular genetics with functional telomere length assessment. First, while Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) is the entry point—as seen when our patient's clinical exome identified a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant—it must be interpreted through the lens of genotype-phenotype correlation. In TBDs, particularly those involving *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC*, monoallelic variants can cause significant disease through haploinsufficiency, mimicking sporadic presentations like idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure without a strong family history. If NGS reveals a VUS in a telomere-related gene, one cannot dismiss it based on classification alone; instead, one must correlate the variant's location (e.g., critical domains like the R3H domain in *PARN*) and computational predictions with the patient's "premature aging" phenotype (early greying, osteoporosis, multiorgan fibrosis).

Crucially, to move beyond genetic uncertainty and confirm the diagnosis functionally, **flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization (Flow-FISH)** for quantitative measurement of leukocyte telomere length is mandatory. This serves as the physiologic gold standard: demonstrating significantly shortened telomeres (typically <1st percentile for age) provides functional proof that the genetic variant is pathogenic and driving cellular senescence. In our case report, this correlation between the monoallelic *PARN* finding and the clinical picture of pancytopenia combined with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis validated the diagnosis despite the initial VUS classification. Therefore, the recommended algorithm excludes reversible causes (like severe B12 deficiency, which may exacerbate but not fully explain the syndrome), proceeds to broad NGS focusing on telomere maintenance genes, and culminates in Flow-FISH to establish biological causality before initiating long-term surveillance or considering hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

### [Management] Considering the diagnosis of a monoallelic PARN variant associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4), what are the current therapeutic limitations regarding curative interventions versus supportive management strategies for hematologic versus pulmonary manifestations?

In managing a patient with a *PARN* variant-associated telomere biology disorder (TBD), specifically presenting with the PFBMFT4 phenotype of pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, we must distinguish sharply between reversible contributors to cytopenia and the irreversible trajectory of organ fibrosis. As illustrated in this case, the immediate therapeutic priority is aggressive supportive management of comorbidities that exacerbate the underlying telomeric defect. Our patient's profound pancytopenia was driven significantly by severe vitamin B12 deficiency; correcting this nutritional deficit stabilized her blood counts, demonstrating that hematologic parameters can improve with supportive care even in the setting of genetic susceptibility. However, it is critical to recognize that while nutritional correction addresses the acute insult, it does not alter the fundamental pathophysiology of telomere shortening.

Regarding curative intervention, the landscape differs fundamentally between hematologic and pulmonary manifestations. For progressive bone marrow failure, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only potentially curative therapy capable of replacing defective hematopoietic stem cells. Yet, HSCT carries significant risks in TBD patients due to heightened sensitivity to conditioning regimens and a propensity for non-relapse mortality from multiorgan toxicity. Conversely, there is currently no curative intervention for pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis once established. Lung transplantation is an option for end-stage respiratory failure but is complicated by high perioperative morbidity and poor long-term survival in TBD cohorts due to extrapulmonary disease progression and graft-versus-host-like complications. Similarly, hepatic fibrosis in this context lacks targeted pharmacotherapy; management relies entirely on surveillance for portal hypertension and variceal bleeding.

Emerging data suggest that androgen therapies, such as danazol or nandrolone, may promote telomere elongation and stabilize blood counts in some TBD patients. However, these agents are not curative for existing fibrotic burden in the lungs or liver, nor are their long-term safety profiles fully established. Therefore, our approach remains anchored in early recognition to facilitate genetic counseling and timely referral for transplant evaluation before irreversible organ damage ensues, while relying on vigilant supportive care—including infection prophylaxis, transfusion support when necessary, and avoidance of unnecessary myelotoxic exposures—to bridge patients until definitive decisions regarding transplantation can be made based on organ-specific decline.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the specific molecular role of the PARN protein in telomere maintenance, particularly regarding its interaction with TERC RNA maturation?

In this case of a young woman presenting with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, the identification of a monoallelic *PARN* mutation anchors the diagnosis to a telomere biology disorder (TBD). To understand the pathophysiology here, we must look beyond the telomerase complex itself (*TERT* and *TERC*) to the upstream processing machinery.

The *PARN* gene encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease (exoribonuclease) that plays a non-redundant, critical role in the biogenesis of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Specifically, newly transcribed TERC precursors acquire aberrant oligo(A) tails at their 3' ends. The specific molecular function of PARN is to catalyze the **deadenylation** of these tails. By removing these post-transcriptionally added adenylates, PARN facilitates the proper 3'-end maturation and stabilization of mature TERC.

Without functional PARN activity—as seen in our patient's variant within the R3H domain—this maturation step fails. Consequently, unstable or immature TRNA accumulates and is targeted for degradation rather than assembly into the active telomerase holoenzyme. This results in insufficient levels of functional telomerase, leading to accelerated telomere shortening across rapidly dividing tissues like hematopoietic stem cells and epithelial linings. This explains our patient's "premature aging" phenotype: bone marrow failure (pancytopenia), pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic cirrhosis are all sequelae of replicative senescence driven by critically short telomeres due to defective TERC maturation.
