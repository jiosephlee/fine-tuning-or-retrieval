Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Monoallelic vs. Biallelic: Decoding Variable Penetrance in *PARN* Mutations and Adult-Onset Fibrosis

We often think of genetic syndromes as having a rigid script: a specific mutation leads to a specific set of symptoms appearing at a predictable age. But in the world of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs), the script is anything but linear. Consider the case of a woman in her late 20s who spent five years battling progressive fatigue, only to reveal a constellation of findings that defied simple categorization: pancytopenia, severe vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic cirrhosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), premature greying, and osteoporosis.

At first glance, this looked like a classic nutritional disaster compounded by an autoimmune storm. Her labs screamed "megaloblastic anemia" with hypersegmented neutrophils and basophilic stippling, and she had profound B12 and folate deficits. But here's the kicker: even after aggressive repletion of vitamins and correction of her nutritional status, her systemic decline didn't stop. The liver stiffness persisted at cirrhotic levels; the lung fibrosis remained; and the bone marrow continued to falter. Why? Because we were treating the symptom, not the root cause—a fragile genome unable to keep up with cellular replication demands.

This case brings us face-to-face with the nuanced genetics of the *PARN* gene and the critical distinction between biallelic and monoallelic mutations. Understanding this difference isn't just academic; it determines whether you're looking at a catastrophic pediatric syndrome or a stealthy adult-onset fibrotic disorder.

### The Two Faces of *PARN*: A Tale of Two Genotypes

The *PARN* gene encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease essential for the maturation of telomerase RNA (TERC). Without functional PARN, telomeres shorten prematurely, leading to cellular senescence in high-turnover tissues like bone marrow, skin, gut mucosa, liver, and lungs. However, the dosage matters immensely.

When **biallelic** (autosomal recessive) loss-of-function mutations occur in *PARN*, we see **Dyskeratosis Congenita Autosomal Recessive Type 6 (DKCB6)**. This presents early in life with severe phenotypes resembling Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome: cerebellar hypoplasia, immunodeficiency, growth retardation, and rapid bone marrow failure. These patients rarely survive into adulthood without intervention because their telomeres are critically short from birth.

In contrast, our patient carried a **monoallelic** missense variant (*c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg*). This fits the profile of **Telomere-Related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure Type 4 (PFBMFT4)**. Monoallelic variants typically exhibit haploinsufficiency—one good copy isn't enough to maintain telomere homeostasis under stress—but they allow for survival into adulthood. The phenotype is often dominated by organ fibrosis (lungs and liver) rather than early childhood developmental catastrophe or fulminant marrow failure. Our patient's presentation is textbook for this subset: she had no family history (suggesting either a de novo event or incomplete penetrance in relatives), no classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (oral leukoplakia/nail dystrophy/skin pigmentation), but rather "cryptic" multi-system aging manifesting as fibrosis and cytopenias in her third decade.

### The VUS Trap: Context is King

One of the most challenging aspects of this case was the genetic result itself: a **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)**. The variant affects a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain—a region critical for RNA binding—and multiple *in silico* predictors flagged it as damaging. Yet, without prior reports linking this exact change to disease, strict adherence to ACMG guidelines leaves it hanging in limbo as "uncertain."

However, clinical medicine requires synthesis beyond raw data points. When you pair a VUS located in a critical functional domain with a phenotype that perfectly mirrors known *PARN*-associated disorders (pulmonary + hepatic fibrosis + marrow dysfunction), the probability shifts dramatically toward pathogenicity. As discussed in recent literature regarding *PARN*, monogenic causes of IPF are frequently missed because clinicians don't suspect them until other causes are ruled out. This case reinforces that we must interpret genetic results through the lens of clinical correlation. If your patient has unexplained fibrosis across three organ systems and "nutritional" anemia that won't resolve fully with supplements, do not dismiss a VUS too quickly—it might be the smoking gun you need to guide surveillance for malignancy or transplant eligibility later on.

### Why Liver AND Lungs? Expanding the Phenotype

Historically, monoallelic *PARN* variants were primarily linked to Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF). Hepatic involvement was thought to be more characteristic of *TERT* or *TERC* mutations or biallelic states where telomere erosion is more profound globally. This case challenges that boundary map entirely here is evidence that monoallelic *PARN* insufficiency can drive significant hepatic fibrosis alongside pulmonary disease without early childhood onset.

The mechanism likely involves cumulative oxidative stress on rapidly dividing hepatocytes combined with impaired regenerative capacity due to shortened telomeres. In fact, registry data suggests that hepatic fibrosis may be vastly under-diagnosed in TBD populations because transient elastography isn't routinely ordered unless there are obvious stigmata of liver disease or abnormal LFTs—findings that can be subtle until cirrhosis sets in. Recognizing this expanded phenotype prevents us from missing liver complications until they become irreversible complications like portal hypertension or variceal bleeding.

### Take-Home Pearls for the Frontline Clinician

So how does this translate to your next shift? Here are the critical lessons from this complex case:

*   **Suspect TBDs Beyond Mucocutaneous Triads:** Do not rule out Telomere Biology Disorders just because your young adult lacks nail dystrophy or oral leukoplakia; many present solely with unexplained organ fibrosis (lung/liver) and cytopenias before classic skin signs appear—or ever appear at all.*
*   **"Nutritional" Anemia That Doesn't Fix:** If you treat severe B12/folate deficiency aggressively but pancytopenia persists or returns rapidly despite normalization of levels, consider an underlying bone marrow failure syndrome exacerbated by metabolic stress.*
*   **Monoallelic vs. Biallelic Matters:** Remember that monoallelic *PARN* mutations typically present as adult-onset IPF/BMF (PFBMFT4) rather than severe pediatric syndromes like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson; this dictates prognosis and screening timelines.*
*   **Liver Fibrosis is Part of the Spectrum:** Unexplained hepatic stiffness/cirrhosis in young adults should trigger thoughts about telomere biology disorders alongside standard autoimmune/infectious workups; consider transient elastography if clinical suspicion arises.*
*   **Interpret VUS Clinically:** A Variant of Uncertain Significance becomes actionable when paired with a highly specific phenotypic match; use clinical context to justify genetic counseling referrals and longitudinal surveillance plans even before definitive pathogenicity is proven.*
*   **Surveillance Saves Lives:** Once diagnosed (even provisionally), these patients require multidisciplinary monitoring for hematologic transformation (MDS/AML), progression of fibrosis requiring transplant evaluation, and solid tumor risks associated with accelerated aging.*
