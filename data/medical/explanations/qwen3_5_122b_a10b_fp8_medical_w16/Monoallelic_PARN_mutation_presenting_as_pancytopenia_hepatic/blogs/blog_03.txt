Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## The Cirrhosis Conundrum: Why Liver Stiffness Matters in Hematology Workups

We often think of the bone marrow as the star of the show in hematologic workups. When a young patient presents with pancytopenia, our eyes immediately dart to the peripheral smear, the reticulocyte count, and the bone marrow aspirate. We hunt for leukemia, myelodysplasia, or perhaps a nutritional deficit like B12 deficiency. But what happens when we treat the "deficit," watch the numbers stabilize slightly, yet the patient remains a wreck? What if their liver is screaming for help before their spleen even enlarges?

Today's case report from *Son et al.* forces us to confront a uncomfortable truth: **liver fibrosis might be the silent sentinel of telomere biology disorders (TBDs)**, often masquerading as simple nutritional anemia or idiopathic cirrhosis in young adults.

### The Patient Who Wasn't Just Deficient
Consider this scenario: A woman in her late 20s walks into your clinic with a 5-year history of crushing fatigue. She looks older than her years—premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue suggesting atrophic glossitis. Her labs scream "megaloblastic anemia": severe B12 deficiency, hypersegmented neutrophils, and dimorphic red cells.

It's an easy trap to fall into. You start aggressive B12 replacement, give folate, and wait for recovery. And sure enough, her counts tick up. But here's where the plot thickens: despite correcting the nutrients, she still has hepatosplenomegaly, significant weight loss, exertional dyspnea, and osteoporotic fractures. She has neuropathy and thoracic scoliosis. If you stop looking after fixing the B12 level, you miss the forest for the trees.

In this case, a routine abdominal ultrasound didn't just show fatty liver; it revealed **cirrhosis**. A transient elastography (FibroScan) kicked back a liver stiffness of **18.1 kPa**—a value indicative of advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis—in a woman barely out of her twenties with no history of alcohol abuse or viral hepatitis. Autoimmune panels were negative. Malignancy was ruled out by PET-CT (which did show diffuse marrow uptake and lung fibrosis).

The final piece of the puzzle wasn't found in the marrow; it was found in her genome: a monoallelic *PARN* mutation (c.613T>C).

### The Hidden Link Between Marrow Failure and Fibrosis
This case illustrates a classic but frequently missed presentation of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs). While Dyskeratosis Congenita (the prototype TBD) is famous for its mucocutaneous triad (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation), many patients—especially those with monoallelic variants like *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC*—present with **"occult" TBDs**. They lack the classic skin findings but harbor catastrophic telomere shortening that attacks rapidly dividing tissues first: bone marrow and liver.

Here is why this matters for every hematologist:
*   **Liver fibrosis is common:** Emerging registry data suggests that up to 88% of adults with pathogenic *TERC* or *TERT* mutations have at least moderate hepatic fibrosis. Cirrhosis is not rare; it is under-diagnosed because we aren't looking for it until jaundice sets in too late.
*   **Nutritional deficits are red herrings:** As seen in this patient, severe B12 deficiency can coexist with TBDs due to gut mucosal turnover issues caused by telomere attrition. Correcting the vitamin improves blood counts temporarily but does nothing to stop the progressive organ failure driven by genetic senescence.
*   **The "VUS" Trap:** Our patient carried a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in *PARN*. In isolation, genetics can be confusing. But when you pair a VUS with **pancytopenia + pulmonary fibrosis + hepatic cirrhosis** in a young person without toxic exposures, the clinical gestalt overrides statistical uncertainty. This is likely pathogenic haploinsufficiency in action.

### Why Your Next FibroScan Might Save Lives
If you see a patient under 40 with unexplained cytopenias—even if they look like aplastic anemia—and they have any hint of liver enzyme elevation or splenomegaly, **do not ignore the liver.**

Transient elastography (FibroScan) should move from being an infectious disease tool to a core part of your hematology workup for unexplained marrow failure syndromes. It is non-invasive, rapid, and sensitive enough to detect early-stage fibrosis that standard LFTs might miss entirely until irreversible damage occurs. Identifying this early changes everything: it shifts management from generic supportive care to targeted surveillance for malignancy (hepatocellular carcinoma risk is sky-high in these patients), genetic counseling for family members (who may carry silent mutations), and discussions about bone marrow transplantation before irreversible multi-system collapse occurs.

### Take-Home Points
*   **Think Beyond Nutrition:** In young patients with pancytopenia and "megaloblastic" features who fail to fully recover after vitamin repletion suspect an underlying Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD).
*   **Screen for Liver Fibrosis Early:** Unexplained hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis in a young adult with cytopenias is highly suggestive of TBD until proven otherwise; utilize transient elastography routinely in these cases even if LFTs are normal or mildly elevated.
*   **Look for Systemic Clues:** Premature greying, osteoporosis/fractures atypical age onset, pulmonary symptoms (dyspnea/fibrosis), and nail/skin changes are vital clues that point away from isolated acquired marrow failure toward a systemic genetic etiology.
*   **Contextualize Genetic Results:** A monoallelic variant classified as "Uncertain Significance" can be diagnostic when paired with a compatible multisystem phenotype involving bone marrow failure and organ fibrosis (*PARN*, *TERT*, *TERC*).
*   **Surveillance is Key:** Early recognition allows for proactive monitoring for hepatocellular carcinoma and pulmonary hypertension, and informs critical decisions regarding hematopoietic stem cell transplantation timing before multiorgan failure ensues.
