Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Beyond Leukoplakia: The Expanding Phenotype of PARN Mutations in Young Adults

If you're a clinician who sees a young adult with pancytopenia, premature grey hair, and liver scarring, your mind might immediately jump to autoimmune hepatitis, myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), or perhaps a nutritional catastrophe. But what if none of those fit? What if the "nutritional" cause doesn't fully explain why their lungs are fibrosing and their bones are crumbling?

That's exactly where we landed with a recent case that serves as a stark reminder: **Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) don't always wear their classic face.**

For decades, Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) has been taught as the holy grail of mucocutaneous triads: nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation, and oral leukoplakia. If you see two of three + bone marrow failure, you call it DC. But the reality of modern genetics is far messier—and far more dangerous for those we fail to recognize early enough. This week, we're diving into a fascinating case of a woman in her late 20s whose presentation defied the textbook definition, driven by a *monoallelic* **PARN** mutation.

### The Case: A Multisystem Puzzle

Our patient walked in with a five-year history of crushing fatigue and progressive weakness that had recently escalated to impairing her basic self-care. On exam, she didn't look like the classic DC patient. No obvious nail dystrophy or mucosal plaques were noted initially. Instead, she presented with **premature greying**, knuckle hyperpigmentation, a bald tongue with atrophic papillae, and significant hepatosplenomegaly.

The labs told a chaotic story:
*   **Pancytopenia** with a reticulocyte count of 0.39% (pointing squarely at ineffective erythropoiesis).
*   **Severe Vitamin B12 deficiency** confirmed on serum studies.
*   **Dimorphic anemia** on smear with basophilic stippling and hypersegmented neutrophils.
*   **Liver stiffness** suggesting cirrhosis (18.1 kPa on elastography) despite negative autoimmune and viral workups.
*   **Osteoporosis** so severe it explained her recurrent fractures and bone pain.
*   **Pulmonary fibrosis** seen on CT as diffuse ground-glass opacities and subpleural atelectasis.

Here was the trap: The team correctly identified severe B12 deficiency and started aggressive replacement therapy. The patient improved symptomatically, but the *constellation* remained suspicious. Why would B12 deficiency cause hepatic cirrhosis? Why did she have such profound pulmonary fibrosis at age 28? And why did she have sensorimotor neuropathy alongside all this?

When nutritional correction failed to resolve the systemic decline, they turned to genetics. The answer wasn't just one missing vitamin; it was a broken clockwork mechanism for aging cells themselves. Exome sequencing revealed a **monoallelic missense variant in *PARN*** (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While technically labeled a "Variant of Uncertain Significance" (VUS), the clinical-genetic correlation screamed Telomere Biology Disorder.

### Unpacking *PARN*: More Than Just Lung Disease

Let's pause on the gene itself because this is where things get tricky for clinicians used to thinking in binary terms (*pathogenic* vs. *benign*).

Historically, *biallelic* (autosomal recessive) mutations in *PARN* were linked to severe childhood-onset Dyskeratosis Congenita Type 6 (DKCB6), often resembling Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome with cerebellar hypoplasia and immunodeficiency. However, *monoallelic* variants tell a different story: they are increasingly associated with adult-onset **telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure**.

In fact, recent data suggests that monoallelic *PARN* variants are found in roughly 2% of sporadic idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis cases—far higher than previously thought for such rare genetic events. Our patient fits this emerging profile perfectly: young adult onset, multisystem involvement (lung + liver + marrow), but lacking the classic skin/nail triad that usually tips off the diagnosis earlier in life.

The *PARN* gene encodes an exoribonuclease critical for processing TERC RNA (the RNA component of telomerase). Without proper TERC maturation, telomeres shorten prematurely during cell division. Since rapidly dividing tissues need long telomeres most desperately—bone marrow stem cells, hepatocytes, alveolar epithelial cells—they are the first to hit the wall and undergo senescence or apoptosis, leading to fibrosis and failure.

### The Liver Connection: An Under-Recognized Feature?

One of the most striking aspects of this case is the hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis occurring without viral or autoimmune etiology. For years, liver disease was considered a minor player in TBDs compared to marrow failure or lung disease. However, emerging registry data challenges this hierarchy. Studies from telomere registries indicate that up to 88% of adults with pathogenic telomere mutations show some degree of hepatic fibrosis on elastography, with nearly one-third progressing to frank cirrhosis—even before hematologic crises occur!

Monoallelic *PARN*, traditionally linked more strongly to pulmonary issues than marrow failure in isolation here presents as both liver AND lung involvement alongside cytopenias suggests we must broaden our differential for "cryptic cirrhosis" in young adults to include TBDs regardless of family history or skin findings.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

So how do we apply this tomorrow morning when seeing that tired 30-year-old with unexplained cytopenias? Don't wait for leukoplakia to appear—it might never come until it's too late for intervention planning like transplant eligibility or family counseling.* Consider screening for TBDs if you see:*

> - **"Cryptic Cirrhosis":** Unexplained liver stiffness/fibrosis in young patients (<45 y/o) without metabolic/viral/autoimmune causes should trigger consideration of telomere disorders even without skin signs.*  
> - **"Disproportionate" Nutritional Deficiencies:** When B12/folate/iron deficiencies seem severe enough to cause massive organ damage beyond expected hematology norms—think about whether an underlying genetic fragility exacerbated them.*  
> - **"Premature Aging" Clusters:** Combine unrelated findings like osteoporosis/fractures + sensorimotor neuropathy + early greying + cytopenias = high suspicion for accelerated aging syndromes.*  
> - **"Negative Family History"?**: De novo mutations happen! Lack thereof does NOT rule out dominant-inherited telomeropathies due to variable penetrance across generations.*  

Early recognition isn't just academic curiosity; it changes management trajectories profoundly—from avoiding unnecessary immunosuppression trials toward targeted surveillance strategies including serial echocardiograms/pulmonary function tests/liver elastography scans—and crucially opens doors for genetic counseling regarding reproductive risks within families affected by these silent killers lurking behind vague symptoms today!*
