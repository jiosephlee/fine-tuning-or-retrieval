Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Silent Cirrhosis: Why Hepatic Fibrosis Must Be Screened in Patients with Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis and Cytopenias

Let's talk about the "silent" killer hiding in plain sight within the world of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). We are all familiar with the classic triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita: nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin pigmentation. But here is the hard truth: relying on these mucocutaneous stigmata to diagnose a TBD is like trying to find a needle in a haystack while wearing blindfolds. Many patients present with organ-specific failure long before they develop the textbook physical exam findings. And perhaps the most dangerously overlooked organ system in this spectrum? The liver.

We recently reviewed a fascinating case that drives this point home. A woman in her late 20s arrived at our doors with a five-year history of crushing fatigue, progressive weakness, and exertional dyspnea. On the surface, she looked like a textbook case of severe nutritional anemia. She had profound vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis (think hypersegmented neutrophils and basophilic stippling), and even a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test that initially threw us off the trail regarding hemolysis versus marrow failure.

But something didn't add up. Even after aggressive repletion of vitamins, her clinical picture remained disproportionate to simple malnutrition. She wasn't just anemic; she was aging prematurely. We saw premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, osteoporosis with fractures, and neuropathy. Then came the bombshell from her abdominal workup: she had significant hepatosplenomegaly and, upon transient elastography (FibroScan), **grade II hepatic steatosis and frank cirrhosis** (liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa).

In a young adult without alcohol abuse or viral hepatitis, finding cirrhosis alongside idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (ground-glass opacities on CT) should immediately trigger a genetic alarm bell. This constellation—pancytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, and unexplained hepatic fibrosis—is the hallmark of a Telomere Biology Disorder. In this specific case, clinical exome sequencing revealed a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the location within the critical R3H domain and the perfect phenotypic match strongly supported a diagnosis of *PARN*-associated TBD.

Why does this matter for your practice today? Because we are missing these diagnoses every day by assuming "idiopathic" means "unknown cause" rather than "undiscovered genetic etiology." Historically, we thought liver disease was rare in TBDs unless it was part of Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome or specific pediatric presentations. That paradigm is shifting rapidly. Recent data from registries like Aachen indicate that nearly 90% of adult patients with *TERC* or *TERT* mutations have at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, with one-third progressing to cirrhosis. If we extend this logic to *PARN* mutations—which are increasingly linked to pulmonary fibrosis—we realize that liver screening isn't optional; it's mandatory for anyone presenting with unexplained multi-system decline in their 20s or 30s.

The mechanism is elegant in its cruelty: defective telomerase leads to shortened telomeres in rapidly dividing cells. While we traditionally focus on hematopoietic stem cells (leading to bone marrow failure) or alveolar epithelial cells (leading to pulmonary fibrosis), hepatocytes are equally vulnerable to replicative senescence when telomeres run too short too fast. The result is cryptic cirrhosis that can be missed on standard ultrasound if you aren't specifically looking for stiffness or if you attribute elevated enzymes solely to fatty liver disease or drug toxicity until it's too late for transplant listing considerations without genetic counseling first.

This case also highlights the nuance of monoallelic vs. biallelic mutations. While biallelic *PARN* variants cause severe recessive Dyskeratosis Congenita type 6 (DKCB6) often presenting in childhood, monoallelic variants are increasingly recognized as drivers of adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). Our patient had only one hit, yet manifested severe multisystem disease including liver cirrhosis—a phenotype usually reserved for *TERT/TERC*. This expands our differential diagnosis significantly: if you see IPF + Cytopenia + Liver issues in a young person, check their telomeres regardless of whether they have white nails or grey hair yet.

Management remains supportive but requires high-level vigilance once the diagnosis is suspected. We corrected her B12 deficiency and managed her osteoporosis and neuropathy supportively, but the key takeaway is long-term surveillance for those who carry these variants—or even those who don't have them yet but fit the phenotype—to catch progression early enough for interventions like Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT) before irreversible organ damage sets in. Danazol has shown promise in telomere elongation trials, though long-term data is still maturing; knowing your patient has a TBD opens doors to clinical trials and specialized care pathways that standard "idiopathic" management misses entirely.

Don't let another young patient slip through the cracks because their liver looked "normal" on basic labs or their skin didn't show classic DC signs yet. If you see pulmonary fibrosis paired with cytopenias in someone under 40, reach for the FibroScan order form before you discharge them home with a label of "nutritional deficiency." The liver might be silent now, but it won't stay that way forever without intervention based on accurate genetics-driven risk stratification.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Look Beyond Mucocutaneous Signs:** Do not rule out Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) based on the absence of nail dystrophy or oral leukoplakia; many adults present primarily with organ fibrosis and bone marrow failure ("cryptic" TBD).
*   **Screen Young Adults Rigorously:** In any patient <40 years old presenting with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis AND cytopenias OR unexplained hepatic dysfunction/splenomegaly, suspect an underlying TBD immediately.
*   **Utilize Transient Elastography:** Standard imaging often misses early-stage fibrosis; use FibroScan (transient elastography) routinely to detect hepatic stiffness in patients with suspected telomere defects, as cirrhosis prevalence is vastly underestimated in this population (>80% may have some degree of fibrosis).
*   **Monoallelic Variants Matter:** Remember that heterozygous (*monoallelic*) mutations in genes like *PARN*, *TERT*, and *TERC* can drive severe adult-onset phenotypes including pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure; do not dismiss VUS results if they align perfectly with the clinical picture involving multiple organs prone to rapid turnover/fibrosis.
*   **Genetics Guide Prognosis:** Identifying a pathogenic variant changes management from generic supportive care to targeted surveillance for malignancy risks (hepatocellular carcinoma), eligibility for HSCT discussion prior to decompensation, and family genetic counseling regarding reproductive planning and sibling screening
