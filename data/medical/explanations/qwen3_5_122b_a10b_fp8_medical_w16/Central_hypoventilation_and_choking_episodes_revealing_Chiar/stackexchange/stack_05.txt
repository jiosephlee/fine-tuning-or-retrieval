Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating an adult with unexplained central sleep apnea or 'Ondine's curse,' what specific neurological signs or historical features (such as headache characteristics) should trigger an urgent MRI of the brain and cervical spine to rule out Chiari Malformation Type I?

When evaluating an adult with unexplained central sleep apnea or "Ondine's curse," the clinician must maintain a high index of suspicion for Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI), particularly when respiratory symptoms are accompanied by specific cranial neuropathies and headache phenotypes. As illustrated in the recent case report of a woman presenting with nocturnal choking and asymptomatic desaturation, the triad warranting urgent MRI of the brain and cervical spine includes: **(1) chronic occipital headaches**, **(2) bulbar dysfunction manifesting as dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally**, and **(3) paradoxical central hypoventilation**.

While classic teaching emphasizes that CMI-associated headaches are typically precipitated by Valsalva maneuvers, this case underscores that the clinical presentation can be more subtle or atypical. Our patient suffered from chronic occipital and vertex headaches for 14 years, characterized by marked **scalp allodynia** where even the weight of hair provoked pain—a feature suggestive of occipital neuralgia or "burning vertex syndrome" secondary to brainstem compression. Although her headaches were not explicitly exacerbated by coughing, their chronic nature combined with unexplained hypoxia served as a critical red flag. Furthermore, the presence of **neurogenic dysphagia**—defined here as choking on both solids and liquids with normal laryngoscopy—should immediately shift the differential away from gastroesophageal pathology toward lower cranial nerve involvement (CN IX, X), which occurs in up to 26% of symptomatic CMI cases.

Most critically, the hallmark of CMI-related respiratory failure is the dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing. Patients often retain full voluntary control while awake but lose automatic drive during sleep or distraction, leading to severe desaturation (as seen in our patient dropping to 70%–80% saturation) without the subjective sensation of dyspnea ("air hunger"). This lack of awareness results from disrupted afferent pathways in the compressed brainstem preventing cortical integration of respiratory distress signals. Therefore, any adult presenting with "silent" central apnea, nocturnal choking episodes unresponsive to ENT/GI workup, or chronic suboccipital pain—even if atypical—requires immediate cross-sectional imaging to evaluate for tonsillar herniation and brainstem compression, as posterior fossa decompression can potentially reverse these life-threatening respiratory deficits.
