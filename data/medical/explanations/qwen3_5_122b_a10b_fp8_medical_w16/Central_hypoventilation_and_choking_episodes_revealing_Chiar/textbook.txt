Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Odyssey

The clinical trajectory of this patient underscores the complexity of diagnosing Chiari malformation type I (CMI) when it presents with a triad of seemingly disparate symptoms: chronic cephalalgia, recurrent oropharyngeal dysfunction, and paradoxical respiratory compromise. The patient's history spans 14 years, initiating shortly after a minor motor vehicle accident that resulted in brief loss of consciousness. In the immediate aftermath, she developed persistent occipital and vertex headaches characterized by a constant, pressure-like quality. Notably, these headaches did not exhibit the classic exacerbation with Valsalva maneuvers often seen in CMI; instead, they manifested as localized allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her hair induced significant discomfort. This unique sensory profile led to an initial misdiagnosis of nummular headache and ineffective treatments involving local scalp injections. The temporal proximity to trauma raises the question of whether the event precipitated tonsillar herniation or simply unmasked a pre-existing anatomical vulnerability, illustrating the spectrum between congenital and acquired presentations of CMI.

Seven years later, the clinical picture evolved to include recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids equally, occurring unpredictably during meals and frequently disturbing sleep with violent coughing and gasping. The nocturnal frequency of these events—three to four times weekly—significantly impacted her quality of life, inducing profound anxiety and social withdrawal as she avoided public dining due to fear of embarrassment. Despite the severity of these symptoms, extensive workups by gastroenterologists and otolaryngologists yielded normal results. Three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies failed to identify structural lesions such as strictures or masses, effectively ruling out common causes like gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) or mechanical obstruction. This diagnostic stalemate is characteristic of neurogenic dysphagia in CMI, where lower cranial nerve dysfunction mimics peripheral pathology but lacks visible mucosal abnormalities on standard endoscopic evaluation.

Perhaps the most perplexing aspect of this case was the discovery of "silent" hypoxia during the COVID-19 pandemic era home monitoring. The patient documented repeated episodes of severe oxygen desaturation (dropping to 70%–80%) without accompanying subjective dyspnea or respiratory distress. This phenomenon highlights a critical pathophysiological concept in brainstem compression: the dissociation between automatic respiratory control and voluntary respiratory drive. While cortical pathways maintaining voluntary breathing remain intact, compression of medullary respiratory centers disrupts automatic breathing regulation during sleep or distraction. Consequently, patients may experience life-threatening hypoventilation without the conscious sensation of air hunger because afferent pathways transmitting chemoreceptor signals to the cortex are interrupted at the brainstem level. A pivotal incident at an airport confirmed this disconnect; despite an oxygen saturation of 78%, she exhibited no overt signs of distress until supplemental oxygen resolved her dizziness rapidly. Standard pulmonary evaluations, including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, were normal at rest, further obscuring the central etiology until dynamic sleep physiology was assessed.

The psychosocial burden imposed by this diagnostic odyssey was substantial. Beyond physical suffering, the patient endured emotional distress stemming from cultural conflicts regarding her coping strategy: cutting her hair short to alleviate vertex allodynia conflicted with societal expectations for women in her community, adding a layer of identity crisis to her medical plight. The persistence of symptoms despite negative workups fostered a sense of invalidation among treating physicians who initially attributed her hypoxia to anxiety disorders. This case serves as a stark reminder that functional psychiatric diagnoses should be reserved only after organic causes have been rigorously excluded through advanced imaging and specialized testing.

The definitive breakthrough in this case occurred not through new technology but through a meticulous re-examination of historical data fourteen years prior to presentation. Upon reviewing an original non-contrast CT scan obtained immediately after her initial trauma—a scan previously reported as normal—the clinical team identified a crowded foramen magnum with loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction and cerebellar tissue extending toward the opisthion. This retrospective identification transformed an obscure collection of symptoms into a coherent neurological diagnosis: CMI causing brainstem compression and secondary central hypoventilation syndrome (often referred to as acquired Ondine's curse). This diagnostic pivot emphasizes that in patients with unexplained central respiratory dysfunction combined with craniovertebral junction symptoms, historical imaging must be scrutinized for subtle anatomical crowding that may have been overlooked during acute trauma evaluations focused solely on hemorrhage or fracture detection.

## Pathophysiology of Chiari Malformation Type I and Brainstem Compression

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is anatomically defined by the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). While contemporary population studies indicate a prevalence of up to 1% in adults, the majority of these individuals remain asymptomatic, representing benign anatomical variants rather than pathological entities. The transition from an incidental finding to "Chiari syndrome" occurs when this volumetric mismatch between the posterior fossa contents and the bony enclosure results in clinical symptoms. In the case presented, the patient's 14-year-old CT scan revealed a crowded foramen magnum that was initially dismissed, illustrating how subtle structural anomalies can persist undiagnosed until they manifest with significant morbidity. The pathophysiology centers on the compression of neural structures at the craniocervical junction, which disrupts cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics and impinges upon critical brainstem nuclei. This compression creates a bottleneck effect, obstructing normal CSF outflow and generating localized pressure gradients that damage adjacent neural tissue.

The unique respiratory profile observed in this patient—characterized by severe oxygen desaturation without subjective dyspnea—stems from a fundamental dissociation in respiratory control systems. Breathing is regulated by two parallel pathways: voluntary control mediated by cortical inputs via the corticospinal tracts, and automatic control governed by rhythm-generating centers in the medulla oblongata and pons. In CMI, the descending cerebellar tonsils physically compress the ventral medulla, specifically targeting the dorsal and ventral respiratory groups responsible for automatic breathing regulation. Consequently, while cortical pathways often remain intact enough to sustain voluntary ventilation during wakefulness or focused attention, the automatic drive fails during states of reduced consciousness, such as sleep or distraction. This mechanism explains why the patient could maintain adequate oxygenation while awake but suffered profound desaturation during sleep or moments of mental diversion at an airport, effectively "forgetting to breathe."

This specific pathophysiological pattern manifests clinically as an acquired form of central hypoventilation syndrome, historically referred to as "Ondine's curse," distinct from its congenital counterpart associated with *PHOX2B* mutations. In congenital cases, genetic defects impair autonomic signaling from birth; in CMI-associated cases, structural brainstem compression acquires this phenotype later in life. The absence of dyspnea during hypoxic episodes is a hallmark of this condition. Breathlessness is an interoceptive sensation generated when afferent signals regarding CO2 retention or O2 deprivation ascend through the brainstem to reach cortical processing areas like the anterior insula. When tonsillar herniation disrupts these ascending afferent pathways within the compressed medulla, the cortex receives no signal of physiological distress despite systemic hypoxia. Thus, patients may exhibit life-threatening desaturation (e.g., SpO2 dropping to 70%) while remaining completely unaware of their respiratory failure until secondary signs appear or external monitoring detects it.

The etiology of CMI in this case raises important questions regarding trauma versus congenital predisposition. While CMI is traditionally viewed as a developmental anomaly arising from a small posterior fossa during embryogenesis, there is growing recognition of trauma-induced or trauma-exacerbated presentations. The temporal relationship between this patient's motor vehicle accident and her symptom onset suggests two possibilities: either traumatic forces induced acute tonsillar herniation into an already crowded space, or more likely, trauma unmasked a pre-existing asymptomatic anatomical variant by causing edema or altering CSF dynamics at a vulnerable craniocervical junction. The immediate onset of occipital headaches post-accident supports an acute insult that destabilized a precarious equilibrium between neural volume and skull capacity. Regardless of whether the malformation was purely congenital or secondarily acquired, once symptomatic brainstem compression establishes itself—particularly involving lower cranial nerve nuclei and respiratory centers—the clinical trajectory involves progressive bulbar dysfunction (choking) and autonomic respiratory instability. Recognizing that structural brain abnormalities can mimic primary pulmonary or psychiatric disorders is therefore essential; in cases where standard workups for dysphagia and hypoxia are unrevealing, high-resolution imaging focusing on CSF flow dynamics at the foramen magnum becomes indispensable for identifying this treatable cause of central hypoventilation.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis Strategy

The diagnostic journey in this case report serves as a critical lesson in synthesizing disparate clinical features to identify a unifying neurological etiology behind what initially appeared to be isolated or functional disorders. The patient's presentation was characterized by a triad of chronic occipital headaches, recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids, and paradoxical oxygen desaturation without subjective dyspnea. The initial clinical approach correctly prioritized ruling out common peripheral causes, yet the persistence of symptoms despite negative workups necessitated a shift toward evaluating central nervous system pathology. A fundamental pivot point in this reasoning process was the differentiation between neurogenic and mechanical dysphagia. Mechanical obstructions, such as strictures or tumors, typically present with difficulty swallowing solids first, progressing to liquids only as the lumen narrows further. In contrast, this patient exhibited equal difficulty with both consistencies from the onset, a hallmark of neuromuscular coordination failure rather than physical blockage. This "consistency-independent" choking pattern, combined with normal findings on three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, effectively excluded structural esophageal or pharyngeal lesions and directed attention toward the neural control of swallowing.

With structural causes excluded, the differential diagnosis expanded to include systemic neuromuscular disorders and primary pulmonary pathologies. Myasthenia gravis was a logical consideration given its propensity to affect bulbar muscles; however, it was reasonably ruled out based on the absence of characteristic fatigability throughout the day and negative serologic testing for acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibodies. Similarly, primary pulmonary etiologies were systematically eliminated through comprehensive evaluation. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests (with preserved forced expiratory volume in 1 second and forced vital capacity) indicated that the lungs themselves were structurally sound and capable of adequate gas exchange during voluntary exertion. Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) with laryngopharyngeal reflux was also considered given the choking symptoms but was dismissed due to normal pH studies and a lack of response to maximal acid suppression therapy. These exclusions left a narrow window for diagnosis: a central process affecting the brainstem's respiratory centers or cranial nerve nuclei while sparing peripheral nerves and muscle fibers.

The interpretation of respiratory data provided further evidence pointing toward a central neurological defect rather than an obstructive sleep apnea or parenchymal lung disease. The patient's sleep study revealed an Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) of 6.0, which technically falls within the range of mild sleep apnea; however, the qualitative composition of these events was diagnostically significant. Seventy-two percent of the recorded events were central in nature, indicating a failure of respiratory drive rather than airway collapse. This high proportion of central events amidst a relatively low overall AHI is unusual for typical obstructive syndromes but aligns perfectly with brainstem compression disrupting automatic breathing rhythms. Furthermore, the clinical observation that the patient experienced severe desaturation (dropping to 70%–80%) without any sensation of breathlessness or respiratory distress highlighted a dissociation between physiological status and conscious perception—a phenomenon known as "silent hypoxia." This specific lack of dyspnea during profound hypoxia suggests an interruption in afferent pathways traveling through the brainstem to the cortex, preventing the generation of air hunger despite severe physiological compromise.

It is essential to recognize that while these findings strongly supported central hypoventilation secondary to Chiari malformation type I (CMI), there are inherent limitations in the diagnostic data obtained from this specific case that complicate definitive confirmation. The sleep study utilized was a Type III device, which monitors respiratory effort, airflow, oxygen saturation, and heart rate but lacks electroencephalogram (EEG) channels required to distinguish sleep stages accurately from wakefulness. Consequently, the reported AHI may significantly underestimate the true severity of sleep-disordered breathing because it divides event counts by total recording time rather than actual sleep time. Had Type I polysomnography been performed—the gold standard involving full EEG monitoring—sleep architecture could have been analyzed precisely, potentially revealing a much higher burden of central apneas occurring specifically during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep or deep non-REM stages where automatic control is most vulnerable. Despite this methodological constraint, the convergence of clinical history (trauma-onset headaches), physical exam findings (allodynia suggesting occipital neuralgia), imaging results (crowded foramen magnum on historical CT confirmed by MRI showing 11 mm tonsillar descent), and physiologic data (central-predominant apnea with silent hypoxia) created a compelling narrative for CMI as the root cause.

Ultimately, this case underscores the necessity of viewing seemingly unrelated symptoms through an integrative lens when common explanations fail to account for clinical reality. The delay in diagnosis over fourteen years illustrates how easily subtle signs like vertex allodynia or asymptomatic nocturnal desaturation can be misattributed to anxiety or psychogenic causes if clinicians do not consider structural brain abnormalities in patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction or bulbar symptoms. By recognizing that voluntary breathing remained intact while automatic control failed—a signature pattern of medullary compression—and correlating this with radiological evidence of hindbrain herniation, clinicians can arrive at a unifying diagnosis that bridges neurology, pulmonology, and gastroenterology considerations into a single management pathway focused on posterior fossa decompression.

## Management Decisions and Therapeutic Considerations

The therapeutic approach to Chiari malformation type I (CMI) complicated by respiratory dysfunction requires a nuanced balance between the potential for symptom reversal through surgical decompression and the risks inherent to neurosurgical intervention. Unlike many structural lesions where observation is purely palliative, posterior fossa decompression (PFD) offers the possibility of restoring normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics and relieving brainstem compression, thereby addressing the root cause of acquired central hypoventilation. In cases like this patient's, where brainstem compression manifests as life-threatening hypoxia and bulbar dysfunction, surgical intervention is generally considered the definitive treatment. The primary goal of PFD is to expand the posterior fossa volume, allowing for the upward retraction of the cerebellar tonsils and restoration of CSF flow across the craniocervical junction. Literature supports that successful decompression can lead to significant improvement or complete resolution of respiratory symptoms, particularly when the pathophysiology involves direct mechanical compression of the medullary respiratory centers. Consequently, for patients with documented central sleep apnea or acute respiratory failure attributable to CMI, surgery is often prioritized over long-term conservative measures.

However, the decision-making process must account for individual patient factors, comorbidities, and personal preferences regarding risk tolerance. Conservative management serves as a viable interim strategy or a permanent choice for select patients who decline surgery or whose symptoms are deemed too mild to justify operative risks. In this specific case, the patient elected to pursue conservative management while deliberating on surgical options. This approach does not imply passivity; rather, it necessitates active supportive care and lifestyle adaptations to mitigate immediate dangers. For instance, dietary modifications were implemented to reduce aspiration risk during choking episodes, and behavioral strategies were employed to manage headache triggers, such as maintaining shorter hairstyles to alleviate scalp allodynia. While these measures address symptom burden, they do not alter the underlying anatomical defect or prevent progression of brainstem compression. Therefore, conservative management is best viewed as a temporizing measure that requires vigilant oversight rather than a curative solution for significant respiratory compromise.

For patients opting for watchful waiting or those awaiting definitive surgical planning, a rigorous monitoring protocol is essential to detect early signs of clinical deterioration that might mandate urgent intervention. Given the unpredictable nature of central hypoventilation and its potential for sudden decompensation—exemplified by this patient's airport incident where desaturation occurred without subjective dyspnea—reliance on subjective reporting is insufficient. A structured surveillance plan should include regular clinical assessments focused on new neurological deficits or worsening bulbar function. Crucially, objective physiologic monitoring is required; in this case report, a regimen involving monthly clinical reviews combined with quarterly overnight oximetry was instituted to track oxygen saturation trends during sleep. Annual magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) surveillance allows for the assessment of tonsillar position changes and CSF flow dynamics over time. If serial monitoring reveals declining oxygen saturations, increasing frequency of central apneic events on polysomnography (once fully characterized with Type I studies), or new neurological deficits indicating progressive myelopathy or brainstem injury, the threshold for surgical intervention lowers significantly. This proactive monitoring framework ensures that patients remain safe while preserving their autonomy in choosing the timing of definitive treatment.
