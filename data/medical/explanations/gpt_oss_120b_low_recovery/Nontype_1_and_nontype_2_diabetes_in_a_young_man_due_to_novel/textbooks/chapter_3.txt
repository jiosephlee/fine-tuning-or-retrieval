Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 3: ## 3. Genetic Testing and Pathophysiology of HNF1A‑MODY  

Maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes that follows an **autosomal‑dominant** inheritance pattern. A single pathogenic variant in any one of >15 genes is sufficient to cause disease, and each gene confers a characteristic biochemical and therapeutic profile. Among these, **HNF1A** (hepatocyte nuclear factor‑1α) is the most frequently implicated, accounting for roughly one‑third to two‑thirds of all MODY cases worldwide. In populations that have been systematically screened, pathogenic HNF1A variants are identified in 1–2 % of young adults with diabetes who lack autoantibodies and retain measurable C‑peptide. The high penetrance of the mutation (≈70 % of carriers develop diabetes by age 25) makes a careful genetic work‑up essential for these patients, especially when the clinical picture overlaps with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

### Structure and function of the HNF1A transcription factor  

The HNF1A protein is a 631‑amino‑acid transcription factor expressed in pancreatic β‑cells, liver, kidney, and intestine. It consists of three functional regions:

| Domain | Approximate residues | Key features |
|--------|---------------------|--------------|
| **Dimerisation domain** | 1‑32 | Mediates homo‑ and heterodimer formation, necessary for DNA binding. |
| **DNA‑binding domain (DBD)** | 33‑280 (winged‑helix motif) | Contains two sub‑domains (P‑box and D‑box) that recognize specific promoter sequences. |
| **Transactivation domain (TAD)** | 281‑631 | Interacts with co‑activators and the basal transcriptional machinery to up‑regulate target genes. |

Mutations clustered in the DBD tend to reduce the ability of HNF1A to bind its cognate DNA response elements, whereas TAD mutations usually diminish the recruitment of transcriptional co‑factors. Both classes ultimately lower transcription of several downstream genes that are critical for glucose sensing and handling.

### How HNF1A loss translates into β‑cell dysfunction  

In pancreatic β‑cells, HNF1A directly regulates transcription of multiple genes that together define the cell’s glucose‑responsive phenotype:

1. **Insulin gene (INS)** – HNF1A enhances insulin mRNA synthesis; reduced activity blunts insulin production.  
2. **GLUT2 (SLC2A2)** – Encodes the low‑affinity, high‑capacity glucose transporter that supplies β‑cells with glucose for metabolism‑stimulated insulin release. Lower GLUT2 expression limits glucose entry and diminishes the secretory trigger.  
3. **Pyruvate kinase (PKLR)** – Controls glycolytic flux; decreased PK activity impairs ATP generation, further weakening the ATP‑dependent closure of K_ATP channels that drives insulin exocytosis.  
4. **SGLT2 (SLC5A2)** – Expressed in the renal proximal tubule; HNF1A drives its transcription. When HNF1A is deficient, SGLT2 expression falls, lowering the renal glucose re‑absorption threshold and producing the characteristic **glycosuria** seen even at modest plasma glucose levels in HNF1A‑MODY.

Collectively, these molecular derangements produce a phenotype of **progressive β‑cell insufficiency** with relative preservation of insulin secretion early in the disease, post‑prandial hyperglycaemia, and low renal glucose thresholds. The phenotype is distinct from type 1 diabetes (autoimmune β‑cell loss) and type 2 diabetes (insulin resistance with variable β‑cell decline).

### Next‑generation sequencing workflow for MODY panels  

Because dozens of genes can cause MODY, most centres now employ a **targeted NGS panel** rather than sequential single‑gene testing. The typical workflow is:

1. **Pre‑analytical selection** – Patients meeting clinical criteria (early‑onset diabetes, autosomal‑dominant family history, negative autoimmune antibodies, preserved C‑peptide) are consented and a peripheral blood sample is drawn.  
2. **Library preparation** – Genomic DNA is fragmented, adapters are ligated, and a capture probe set specific for the MODY gene list (including HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.) enriches the regions of interest.  
3. **Sequencing** – Enriched libraries are sequenced on a platform such as Illumina MiSeq or NextSeq, generating 100–150 bp paired‑end reads with >100× average depth across target exons.  
4. **Bioinformatic pipeline** – Reads are aligned to the reference genome (GRCh38), duplicate reads are marked, and variant calling identifies single‑nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions/deletions, and splice‑site changes. Quality filters (read depth ≥ 20, allele frequency ≥ 20 % for heterozygotes) are applied.  
5. **Annotation and filtering** – Detected variants are annotated for gene, transcript, predicted protein change, population allele frequencies (gnomAD), and in‑silico deleteriousness scores (e.g., REVEL, CADD, AlphaMissense). Variants common in the general population (MAF > 0.01) are typically excluded as benign.  
6. **Interpretation** – The filtered list is reviewed by a molecular geneticist and an endocrinologist, who apply the **American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria** to classify each variant as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, or benign.  
7. **Reporting** – A concise clinical report states the classification, supporting evidence, and recommended follow‑up (e.g., cascade testing of relatives).

### ACMG classification of the novel c.809A>G (p.N270S) HNF1A variant  

The patient’s NGS panel identified a heterozygous **c.809A>G** change, translating to a **p.Asn270Ser (N270S)** substitution in exon 4, within the DNA‑binding domain. Applying the ACMG/AMP framework, the variant meets several criteria:

| ACMG Criterion | Evidence in this case |
|----------------|-----------------------|
| **PM2** (moderate) – absent from population databases (gnomAD, 1000 Genomes) | Variant not reported in any curated frequency dataset (MAF = 0). |
| **PM1** (moderate) – located in a mutational hot‑spot or critical functional domain | N270 lies in the highly conserved DBD; >90 % of pathogenic HNF1A variants cluster here. |
| **PP3** (supporting) – multiple computational tools predict deleterious effect | AlphaMissense score = 0.995 (near‑maximum), REVEL = 0.88, CADD = 28. |
| **PP1** (supporting) – cosegregation with disease in family | The variant is present in the mother and younger brother, both with early‑onset diabetes; absent in an unaffected aunt (if tested). |
| **PS4** (strong) – prevalence of the variant is significantly increased in affected individuals versus controls | Not applicable because the variant is novel, but the phenotype‑genotype match (early‑onset, glycosuria, negative antibodies) strongly supports pathogenicity. |

Collectively, the combination **PM2 + PM1 + PP3 + PP1** yields a **likely pathogenic** classification per ACMG rules (≥2 moderate + ≥2 supporting). The authors of the case report interpreted the variant as **pathogenic**, citing the very high AlphaMissense score and the functional relevance of the altered residue (adjacent to known pathogenic Asn268/Asn271 mutations). In clinical practice, a likely pathogenic result in a gene with a well‑established disease association (HNF1A) is sufficient to make a molecular diagnosis of MODY, particularly when the clinical picture aligns.

### AlphaMissense scoring and its role in variant interpretation  

AlphaMissense is a deep‑learning model that predicts the impact of missense changes on protein function based on structure‑aware embeddings. Scores range from 0 (benign) to 1 (highly deleterious). A threshold of **≥0.8** is commonly used to support a damaging prediction. In this case, the N270S substitution received a **0.995** score, placing it among the top 0.5 % of predicted deleterious missense variants across the human proteome. When combined with other lines of evidence (absence from population data, domain location, segregation), AlphaMissense provides a strong computational argument for pathogenicity, satisfying the ACMG **PP3** criterion.

### Clinical impact of DNA‑binding vs transactivation domain mutations  

Large genotype‑phenotype series of HNF1A‑MODY have shown that **DNA‑binding domain (DBD) mutations** are generally associated with:

* **Earlier age of diabetes onset** (median ≈ 18 years) because the ability of the transcription factor to bind DNA is severely compromised.  
* **More pronounced glycosuria** due to stronger down‑regulation of renal SGLT2.  
* **Greater sensitivity to low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists**, as some residual β‑cell function remains but insulin secretion is glucose‑dependent.

In contrast, **transactivation domain (TAD) mutations** often produce a milder phenotype, with later onset (median ≈ 25 years), less frequent glycosuria, and a slower rate of β‑cell decline. The current case, harbouring a DBD mutation (p.N270S), fits these patterns: the patient presented in his early twenties, exhibited marked glycosuria despite modest hyperglycaemia, and responded well to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist after insulin discontinuation.

Understanding whether a mutation resides in the DBD or TAD can therefore inform **prognostic counselling** (expected disease trajectory) and **therapeutic selection** (e.g., early use of agents that augment insulin secretion without causing hypoglycaemia). It also underscores why **genetic confirmation** is more than a diagnostic label—it tailors management to the underlying molecular defect.  

---  

In summary, the identification of the novel **c.809A>G (p.N270S)** HNF1A variant illustrates the full continuum from **clinical suspicion** of MODY, through **targeted NGS**, to **rigorous ACMG‐based interpretation** and **mechanistic insight** into β‑cell dysfunction. The variant’s location in the DNA‑binding domain, its predicted deleterious effect (high AlphaMissense score), and its segregation with disease collectively explain the patient’s early‑onset hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, and preserved yet insufficient insulin secretion, guiding the shift from insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist‑based regimen.
