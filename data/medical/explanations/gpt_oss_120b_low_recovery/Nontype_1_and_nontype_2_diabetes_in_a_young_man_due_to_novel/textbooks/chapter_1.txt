Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment  

The patient is a young adult male who was referred for assessment after routine health screening revealed persistent hyperglycaemia and the presence of glucose in the urine.  He is 22 years old, weighs 72 kg, and has a body‑mass index of 24.6 kg/m²—within the upper range of normal but not frankly obese.  Vital signs are unremarkable: blood pressure 112/70 mm Hg and a regular pulse of 78 beats per minute, indicating he is hemodynamically stable and not hypertensive.  

### Symptoms  
At presentation the patient reports classic osmotic symptoms of diabetes: increased thirst (polydipsia) and increased hunger (polyphagia) accompanied by a recent, involuntary weight loss of about 4 kg over several weeks.  He also notes frequent urination (polyuria) that prompted the detection of glycosuria on a dip‑stick test during his annual check‑up.  He denies any history of ketoacidosis, visual changes, or neuropathic symptoms.  

### Family History  
The pedigree is striking for early‑onset diabetes affecting three consecutive generations on the maternal side: the maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her early‑30s, the mother was diagnosed in her late‑20s, and the patient’s younger brother was found to be diabetic at age 20.  No relatives have reported premature cardiovascular disease, retinopathy, or nephropathy, suggesting that the familial diabetes phenotype has been relatively uncomplicated thus far.  

### Physical Examination  
A focused examination corroborates the absence of insulin‑resistance stigmata.  The skin is free of acanthosis nigricans, and there are no lipodystrophic changes.  Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations are normal, with no hepatomegaly or splenomegaly.  The absence of acanthosis nigricans—a cutaneous marker of chronic hyperinsulinaemia—makes severe insulin resistance less likely, steering the clinician away from a typical type 2 diabetes picture.  

### Initial Biochemical Profile  

| Parameter | Result | Interpretation |
|-----------|--------|----------------|
| HbA1c | 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol) | Marked chronic hyperglycaemia |
| Fasting plasma glucose | 12.4 mmol/L | Diagnostic of diabetes (≥7.0 mmol/L) |
| 2‑hour OGTT (75 g) | 19.8 mmol/L | Severe post‑prandial hyperglycaemia (≥11.1 mmol/L) |
| Lipid panel | HDL‑C 1.01 mmol/L (≈39 mg/dL) ; Triglycerides 4.31 mmol/L (≈382 mg/dL) | Low HDL and markedly elevated triglycerides, features that can accompany metabolic syndrome but may also be secondary to uncontrolled diabetes |
| Serum ketones, arterial pH | Not elevated, normal pH | No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) |

The markedly elevated HbA1c together with fasting and 2‑hour OGTT values confirm the presence of diabetes mellitus.  The OGTT pattern—disproportionately high post‑prandial glucose relative to fasting glucose—is characteristic of β‑cell dysfunction rather than pure insulin resistance.  

### Assessment for Acute Complications  
Because the patient presented with hyperglycaemia but no symptoms of nausea, abdominal pain, or altered mental status, bedside assessment for DKA was performed.  Serum bicarbonate, anion gap, and venous blood gases were all within normal limits, and urine ketones were absent.  Thus, there were no acute metabolic complications requiring emergent metabolic correction.  

In summary, the initial assessment highlights a young, non‑obese adult with classic diabetes symptoms, a strong multigenerational family history suggestive of an autosomal‑dominant pattern, and laboratory evidence of significant hyperglycaemia without ketosis.  The lack of insulin‑resistance stigmata, together with preserved (though not normal) β‑cell function on subsequent testing, raises suspicion for a monogenic form of diabetes such as HNF1A‑MODY, prompting further targeted investigations.
