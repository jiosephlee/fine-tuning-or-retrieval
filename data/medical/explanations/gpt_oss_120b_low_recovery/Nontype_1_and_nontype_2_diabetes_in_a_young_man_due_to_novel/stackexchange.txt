- Negative islet autoantibodies and *preserved stimulated C‑peptide* argue against type 1.

**Bottom line:** The minimal panel required to separate HNF1A‑MODY from autoimmune type 1 diabetes consists of (1) a full islet‑autoantibody panel (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8‑Ab, IAA when appropriate) and (2) a C‑peptide assessment (fasting and/or stimulated). In this case, the autoantibodies were negative and the glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide rose to 0.83 nmol/L, confirming residual β‑cell function and supporting the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY rather than type 1 diabetes.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanism by which HNF1A mutations lead to both impaired insulin secretion and low renal glucose threshold (glycosuria).

**Pathophysiology of HNF‑1α (HNF1A) MODY – why patients have both an insulin‑secretory defect and a low renal glucose threshold**

1. **HNF‑1α is a master transcription factor**  
   - Expressed in pancreatic β‑cells, liver, and the proximal tubule of the kidney.  
   - In β‑cells it drives expression of *INS* (insulin), *GLUT2* (SLC2A2), and enzymes of glycolysis (e.g., pyruvate kinase).  
   - In the renal proximal tubule it controls the *SLC5A2* gene that encodes sodium‑glucose cotransporter‑2 (SGLT2), the principal transporter responsible for re‑absorbing >90 % of filtered glucose.

2. **Heterozygous loss‑of‑function mutations (such as the novel p.N270S variant in exon 4)** produce a dose‑dependent reduction of HNF‑1α activity.  
   - Because the protein functions as a dimer, a single defective allele markedly diminishes transcription of its target genes (haploinsufficiency).

3. **Impaired insulin secretion**  
   - ↓ GLUT2 reduces glucose entry into β‑cells, blunting the glucose‑stimulated rise in intracellular ATP.  
   - ↓ pyruvate kinase and other glycolytic enzymes further limit ATP generation.  
   - The ATP‑sensitive K⁺ channel (K_ATP) therefore remains more open, β‑cell depolarisation is attenuated, and calcium‑mediated exocytosis of insulin granules is reduced.  
   - The net effect is a progressive, quantitative defect in insulin release that first manifests as post‑prandial hyperglycaemia and later as fasting hyperglycaemia, as seen in the case (stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L, far below the normal response).

4. **Reduced renal glucose threshold (glycosuria)**  
   - HNF‑1α directly activates *SLC5A2* transcription. Haploinsufficiency leads to ↓ SGLT2 protein in the proximal tubule.  
   - With fewer functional SGLT2 transporters, the maximal re‑absorption capacity for glucose falls (normally ~350 mg/min).  
   - Consequently, the plasma glucose concentration at which glucose begins to appear in urine drops from the usual ≈10–11 mmol/L to ≈5–7 mmol/L.  
   - Clinically this manifests as “glycosuria out of proportion to the plasma glucose,” a classic bedside clue to HNF1A‑MODY and exactly what the young man exhibited.

5. **Why both phenomena coexist**  
   - The same transcription factor regulates genes in two distinct organs.  
   - A single heterozygous loss‑of‑function mutation therefore simultaneously compromises β‑cell glucose sensing/insulin release and tubular glucose re‑absorption.  
   - The degree of each defect depends on the residual activity of the mutant protein; in most HNF1A mutations the β‑cell defect is more clinically apparent because β‑cells have a limited reserve, whereas the renal phenotype is evident early as low‑threshold glycosuria.

**Take‑home point for trainees:**  
When a young adult presents with hyperglycaemia, modestly preserved C‑peptide, negative autoantibodies, and especially “unexpected” glycosuria, think of HNF1A‑MODY. The dual pathophysiology—β‑cell transcriptional insufficiency and SGLT2 down‑regulation—explains the characteristic combination of impaired insulin secretion and a low renal glucose threshold described in the case report. Recognizing this pattern directs you to genetic testing and toward sulfonylurea or GLP‑1‑RA therapy rather than unnecessary insulin or SGLT2‑inhibitors.

### [Morning Report] How does the glucagon stimulation test aid in the assessment of β‑cell function in suspected MODY, and what does the patient’s result indicate about his disease stage?

**Answer – How the glucagon stimulation test fits into the work‑up of MODY and what the patient’s result tells us about his disease stage**

The glucagon stimulation test (GST) is a bedside‐level dynamic assessment of residual β‑cell mass. After an intravenous bolus of 1 mg glucagon, insulin (and therefore C‑peptide) secretion from pancreatic β‑cells is acutely amplified via the cAMP‑mediated pathway. The magnitude of the rise in C‑peptide therefore reflects how much functional β‑cell tissue remains and is useful when the differential diagnosis includes type 1 diabetes (autoimmune β‑cell loss), type 2 diabetes (relative insulin insufficiency with insulin resistance), or a β‑cell–specific genetic defect such as MODY.

| **Interpretation of GST (stimulated C‑peptide)** | **Implication** |
|---------------------------------------------------|-----------------|
| **>0.60 nmol L⁻¹ (or >1.8 ng mL⁻¹)**               | Preserved endogenous secretion → argues against absolute β‑cell destruction (i.e., classic type 1). |
| **0.30–0.60 nmol L⁻¹**                           | Moderately reduced secretion → indicates β‑cell dysfunction but not complete loss; seen in early‑stage MODY or advanced type 2. |
| **<0.30 nmol L⁻¹**                               | Severely impaired → compatible with longstanding type 1 or end‑stage β‑cell failure. |

In the reported case the GST showed:

- **Fasting C‑peptide 0.39 nmol L⁻¹** (baseline, low but detectable)  
- **6‑minute stimulated C‑pep 0.83 nmol L⁻¹** (increase of ≈ 0.44 nmol L⁻¹, still below the 0.60 nmol L⁻¹ “preserved” threshold)

**What this means for the patient**

1. **β‑cell function is still present but markedly diminished.**  
   The rise after glucagon is real (≈ 110 % increase) yet the absolute value remains below the level that would be considered “preserved.” This pattern is classic for **HNF1A‑MODY in the “moderate” stage**, where the transcription‑factor defect has already produced a progressive loss of insulin secretory capacity but has not yet resulted in complete β‑cell exhaustion.

2. **Helps exclude classic type 1 diabetes.**  
   In autoimmune diabetes the C‑peptide response to glucagon is usually <0.30 nmol L⁻¹ even early after diagnosis because the autoimmune attack rapidly depletes β‑cell mass. The patient’s modest but measurable response, together with negative GADA/IA‑2A, makes type 1 unlikely.

3. **Distinguishes from pure insulin‑resistant type 2 diabetes.**  
   In early‑stage type 2 patients, fasting C‑peptide is often >0.6 nmol L⁻¹ and the glucagon‑stimulated value exceeds 1 nmol L⁻¹, reflecting a relatively robust β‑cell reserve that is “over‑working.” Our patient’s low baseline and blunted peak point toward an intrinsic secretory defect rather than simple insulin resistance.

4. **Provides a baseline for therapeutic planning and follow‑up.**  
   Because HNF1A‑MODY patients retain some β‑cell capacity, they respond well to agents that amplify insulin secretion (low‑dose sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 receptor agonists). The GST result reassured the team that withdrawing insulin was safe once glycaemia was controlled, and it gave a quantitative reference to monitor future decline (e.g., repeat GST after several years).

**Take‑home teaching point**

The glucagon stimulation test is not a diagnostic test for MODY per se, but it is an inexpensive, physiologically meaningful adjunct that:

- **Demonstrates preserved but impaired β‑cell reserve** (the hallmark of many MODY subtypes, especially HNF1A),  
- **Helps rule out autoimmune β‑cell loss**, and  
- **Informs choice and timing of β‑cell–targeted therapy**.

In this young man, a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol L⁻¹ signals **moderate β‑cell dysfunction**, consistent with an intermediate disease stage of HNF1A‑MODY—enough residual function to allow safe transition off insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist–based regimen, yet insufficient to rely on diet alone. Continual monitoring of C‑peptide (fasting or GST) can track disease progression and guide future therapeutic adjustments.

### [Management] According to current guidelines, what is the first‑line pharmacologic therapy for HNF1A‑MODY and why was a GLP‑1 receptor agonist chosen instead in this patient?

**First‑line pharmacologic therapy for HNF1A‑MODY**

Guidelines from the American Diabetes Association, the European Society of Diabetes, and the International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes agree that **low‑dose sulfonylureas** (most often gliclazide, glimepiride or tolbutamide) are the preferred initial agents for patients with HNF1A‑MODY who have a HbA1c > 7 % and retained β‑cell function.  

*Why sulfonylureas?*  
- HNF1A mutations impair the transcription of the β‑cell glucose‑sensing apparatus (GLUT2, glucokinase) and reduce insulin secretion in a glucose‑dependent manner. Sulfonylureas close the ATP‑sensitive K⁺ channel downstream of this defect, bypassing the impaired glucose‑sensing pathway and producing a robust, dose‑responsive rise in insulin.  
- Clinical series show that a daily dose of 0.1–0.3 mg/kg of a second‑generation sulfonylurea normalises fasting and post‑prandial glucose in >90 % of HNF1A‑MODY patients, often allowing complete insulin discontinuation.  
- Compared with insulin, sulfonylureas are inexpensive, oral, and avoid the need for injections or frequent glucose monitoring in a young, otherwise healthy individual.  

**Why a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (GLP‑1 RA) was chosen in this patient**

1. **β‑cell reserve was already modest** – The glucagon stimulation test showed a stimulated C‑peptide of only 0.83 nmol/L, indicating that endogenous insulin secretion was declining. Sulfonylureas rely on the presence of functional β‑cells; as reserve wanes, the risk of inadequate response and subsequent hyperglycaemia increases.  

2. **High risk of hypoglycaemia with sulfonylureas** – HNF1A‑MODY patients have a markedly reduced renal threshold for glucose, leading to prominent glycosuria even at modest glucose levels. When a sulfonylurea‑induced insulin surge meets this low threshold, mild to moderate hypoglycaemia occurs in up to 30 % of treated patients. The case report notes the authors’ concern for “hypoglycaemia‑prone” individuals and cites a crossover trial showing fewer hypoglycaemic episodes with liraglutide versus glimepiride.  

3. **Weight considerations and metabolic profile** – The patient’s BMI (24.6 kg/m²) and triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) suggested early metabolic syndrome. GLP‑1 RAs promote weight loss and improve post‑prandial lipemia, providing ancillary benefit not offered by sulfonylureas.  

4. **Preservation of β‑cell function** – GLP‑1 RAs augment glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon, and have been shown in short‑term studies to slow the decline of C‑peptide in HNF1A‑MODY. In the reported case, switching to dulaglutide after cessation of insulin led to a rapid fall in HbA1c from 9.1 % to 6.0 % within three months, and the regimen was later simplified to metformin + saxagliptin for maintenance—demonstrating that GLP‑1 RA therapy achieved durable control without sulfonylurea‑related hypoglycaemia.  

5. **Patient‑centered factors** – The initial insulin requirement (degludec 8 U/day) was modest, reflecting limited insulin resistance. The treating team therefore felt comfortable transitioning to an injectable GLP‑1 RA rather than adding or maintaining a sulfonylurea, which would have increased the pill burden and hypoglycaemia risk.  

**Bottom line for the morning report**

- **Standard first‑line**: low‑dose sulfonylurea for HNF1A‑MODY with preserved β‑cell reserve.  
- **When to consider alternatives**: (a) evidence of declining β‑cell function, (b) prior hypoglycaemia or high hypoglycaemia risk (e.g., pronounced glycosuria), (c) need for weight loss or cardiometabolic benefit, or (d) patient preference for an injectable agent with a lower hypoglycaemia profile.  

In this young man, the combination of modest C‑peptide, the desire to avoid hypoglycaemia, and the added metabolic advantages justified the switch to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, which proved effective and allowed simplification of his regimen after glycaemic targets were achieved.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why are SGLT‑2 inhibitors contraindicated or used with caution in patients with HNF1A‑MODY?

**Why SGLT‑2 inhibitors are contraindicated or must be used with great caution in HNF1A‑MODY**

HNF1A‑MODY is caused by loss‑of‑function mutations in the transcription factor **HNF‑1α**, which controls expression of several renal tubular proteins, most importantly the **sodium‑glucose co‑transporter‑2 (SGLT2)**. In normal kidney proximal tubules HNF‑1α drives synthesis of SGLT2, the carrier that reabsorbs ~90 % of filtered glucose. When HNF1A is defective, SGLT2 expression is intrinsically reduced, so the renal glucose‑threshold is already low and patients frequently present with **glycosuria despite modest plasma glucose levels** (the case report notes “glycosuria…attribut­ed to reduced expression of SGLT2”).

SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) act by *further* blocking the residual transport capacity. In HNF1A‑MODY this produces three inter‑related hazards:

1. **Exaggerated osmotic diuresis → volume depletion**  
   The kidney is already “leaking” glucose and water. Adding an SGLT2 blocker can precipitate marked dehydration, orthostatic hypotension, and in extreme cases vascular collapse. The case discussion explicitly states that “HNF1A‑MODY patients may experience severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to high inhibition of residual SGLT‑2 activity.”

2. **Euglycaemic (or normoglycaemic) diabetic keto‑acidosis (DKA)**  
   With less insulin secretion (the patient’s stimulated C‑peptide was only 0.83 nmol/L), the counter‑regulatory milieu favours lipolysis. The additional glucosuria removes a substrate for glycolysis, shifts metabolism toward ketogenesis, and can trigger DKA even when blood glucose is not markedly elevated. This phenomenon is well‑described for SGLT2 inhibitors and is amplified when baseline SGLT2 activity is already low.

3. **Increased risk of genital and urinary infections**  
   Chronic glucosuria creates a nutrient‑rich environment for fungi and bacteria. The case report lists “genital infections” among the adverse events that may outweigh any potential cardiovascular benefit of the drug class in this population.

Because the therapeutic goal in HNF1A‑MODY is to **preserve residual β‑cell function and avoid hypoglycaemia**, the standard first‑line agents are low‑dose sulfonylureas or, as illustrated in this patient, GLP‑1 receptor agonists plus metformin. Both improve glycaemia without further impairing renal glucose reabsorption.

**Bottom line for the bedside:**  
In HNF1A‑MODY the kidney already has a reduced SGLT2‑mediated glucose‑reabsorption threshold, so pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition adds little glycaemic benefit while markedly raising the risk of dehydration, euglycaemic DKA, and infections. Consequently, SGLT‑2 inhibitors are either contraindicated or should be used only after careful risk‑benefit assessment, with strict monitoring of hydration status, ketones, and renal function.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how the ACMG criteria were applied to classify the novel HNF1A p.N270S variant as pathogenic/likely pathogenic in this report.

**How the ACMG/AMP criteria were used to classify the novel HNF1A p.N270S variant as pathogenic/likely‑pathogenic**

The authors applied the 2015 ACMG/AMP framework (Richards et al., *Genet Med* 2015) by gathering **independent lines of evidence** that fall into the three strength categories (very strong, strong, moderate, supporting). For a single heterozygous miss‑missense change in an autosomal‑dominant disease such as HNF1A‑MODY, the combination **PVS1** (null‑variant) is not applicable; instead the classification rested on **PS**, **PM**, and **PP** criteria as follows:

| ACMG code | Requirement | Evidence supplied in the report | Strength |
|-----------|-------------|--------------------------------|----------|
| **PM2**   | Variant absent from population databases (gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, ExAC) | The authors state that p.N270S “was not reported in the literature, VarSome or ClinVar” and is not present in population frequency repositories. | Moderate |
| **PM5**   | Novel miss‑missense change at an amino‑acid residue where a different pathogenic miss‑missense change has been established | Asparagine 270 lies next to residues (e.g., N266K, N271S) that are already classified as pathogenic in HNF1A. The authors note that the variant alters an amino‑acid **adjacent to known pathogenic residues** in the DNA‑binding domain. | Moderate |
| **PM1**   | Located in a mutational hot‑spot or critical functional domain without benign variation | p.N270S resides in exon 4, within the well‑characterised DNA‑binding domain (aa 150‑274) where >70 % of HNF1A pathogenic variants cluster. No benign variants are reported in this sub‑region. | Moderate |
| **PP3**   | Multiple in‑silico tools predict a deleterious effect | The AlphaMissense deep‑learning score for p.N270S is 0.995 (near‑maximal), supporting a damaging impact. The authors cite this high score as computational evidence. | Supporting |
| **PP4**   | Patient’s phenotype is highly specific for a disease with a single gene etiology | The proband has classic HNF1A‑MODY features: early‑onset non‑ketoacytic hyperglycaemia, autosomal‑dominant family history, negative pancreatic auto‑antibodies, modestly preserved C‑peptide, and glycosuria (reflecting renal SGLT2 down‑regulation). This phenotype matches the known HNF1A‑MODY spectrum. | Supporting |
| **PP1 (co‑segregation)** | Variant segregates with disease in multiple affected family members | The report indicates that the same heterozygous p.N270S mutation is present in the mother and brother, both with early‑onset diabetes, establishing co‑segregation across ≥ 2 affected relatives. | Supporting |
| **PP5** (optional) | Previously reported as pathogenic in a reputable source | Not applicable because the variant is novel; the authors explicitly state it is absent from ClinVar/VarSome. | – |

**Summation of criteria**

- **Three moderate** criteria (PM2, PM5, PM1)  
- **Three supporting** criteria (PP3, PP4, PP1)

According to the ACMG combinatorial rules, **≥ 2 moderate + ≥ 1 supporting** is sufficient for a **“likely pathogenic”** classification. The presence of **three supporting** lines pushes the interpretation toward the upper end of the “likely pathogenic” range; some laboratories would upgrade to **“pathogenic”** when strong functional data were available, but in this case the authors conservatively reported the variant as **pathogenic/likely‑pathogenic (P/LP)**, reflecting the weight of the collective evidence.

**Key teaching points**

1. **Population absence (PM2)** is critical for novel variants; a low allele frequency (<0.001) in large reference cohorts argues against a benign polymorphism.  
2. **Domain‐specific clustering (PM1)** and **adjacent known pathogenic residues (PM5)** provide disease‑specific context that is especially powerful for transcription factors like HNF1A, where the DNA‑binding domain is a mutational hotspot.  
3. **Co‑segregation (PP1)**, even with only two additional affected relatives, adds strong phenotypic concordance, reinforcing the genetic link.  
4. **High‑confidence in‑silico prediction (PP3)** (e.g., AlphaMissense > 0.9) contributes supporting evidence but never substitutes for functional data.  
5. **Phenotype specificity (PP4)** is a reminder that molecular classification must be anchored to the clinical picture; the classic HNF1A‑MODY traits in this patient make the genetic finding highly plausible.

By systematically applying these ACMG criteria, the authors demonstrated that the novel HNF1A p.N270S variant fulfills the evidence thresholds required for a likely‑pathogenic classification, justifying its use in guiding precision therapy for the patient and his affected relatives.

### [Management] What are the implications of identifying a pathogenic HNF1A mutation for the patient’s family members, and what cascade testing strategy would you recommend?

**Answer – Implications of a pathogenic HNF1A mutation for relatives and recommended cascade testing**

The identification of a heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S) in this young man confirms autosomal‑dominant HNF1A‑MODY. Because the mutation is transmitted in a 50 % manner to each first‑degree relative, any biologically related adult who carries the same allele is at high risk of developing progressive β‑cell failure and will usually manifest diabetes before the third decade of life.  

**Key implications for family members**

| Issue | Clinical relevance |
|------|---------------------|
| **Risk of diabetes** | ≈ 50 % chance of inheriting the mutation; carriers typically develop diabetes by age 25‑35, often with mild‑to‑moderate hyperglycaemia, post‑prandial excursions, and a low renal glucose threshold (glycosuria). |
| **Therapeutic advantage** | Early molecular diagnosis permits use of low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1‑RA ± metformin rather than lifelong insulin, dramatically reducing hypoglycaemia risk and improving quality of life (as illustrated by the proband’s HbA1c fall from 10.2 % to 6.0 %). |
| **Surveillance** | For carriers without overt diabetes, yearly fasting glucose or HbA1c beginning at age 10 – 12, with a low‑threshold oral glucose tolerance test (2‑h ≥ 11.1 mmol/L) and periodic C‑peptide assessment to gauge β‑cell reserve. |
| **Reproductive counselling** | The mutation confers a 50 % transmission risk to each offspring; pre‑conception or prenatal genetic counselling is appropriate for couples who wish to know the fetal genotype. |
| **Psychosocial impact** | Knowledge of carrier status may relieve uncertainty, but counseling should address anxiety and the need for lifelong monitoring. |

**Cascade testing strategy**

1. **Identify the pedigree** – Draw a three‑generation family tree, marking all individuals with documented diabetes (maternal grandmother, mother, brother) and those with unknown status.

2. **Test all first‑degree relatives** (parents, siblings, children). In this family:
   * **Mother** – already diabetic; predictive testing will confirm whether her disease is mutation‑linked (informative for future children).
   * **Younger brother** – diabetic; testing clarifies whether his phenotype is HNF1A‑MODY (guides therapy).
   * **Father** – clinically unaffected; a negative test excludes him as a carrier and reduces anxiety for his side of the family.

3. **If a first‑degree relative is positive**, extend testing to their first‑degree relatives (second‑degree relatives of the proband). This includes:
   * Maternal aunt/uncle and their children (maternal cousins).
   * The proband’s own children (once they are ≥ 5 years old, or earlier if there is unexplained hyperglycaemia).

4. **Testing methodology** – Targeted Sanger sequencing or a focused NGS panel for the known HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S) variant is sufficient; full‑gene sequencing is not required once the pathogenic allele is defined.

5. **Timing** – Offer testing promptly after the proband’s result is communicated. For asymptomatic minors, testing is justified because early detection changes management (choice of oral agents, avoidance of unnecessary insulin, and lifestyle planning).

6. **Interpretation and follow‑up** –  
   * **Positive result** – initiate MODY surveillance protocol (annual fasting glucose/HbA1c, education on low‑threshold glycosuria, and consider early low‑dose sulfonylurea/GLP‑1‑RA if hyperglycaemia emerges).  
   * **Negative result** – reassure that the risk of MODY is not increased; routine diabetes screening according to population guidelines applies.

7. **Documentation** – Record the variant in the patient’s electronic health record and request inclusion in any national rare‑disease registry to facilitate future genotype‑phenotype studies.

**Bottom line:** Because HNF1A‑MODY is an autosomal‑dominant disease with high penetrance, cascade genetic testing of all first‑degree relatives (and subsequently their first‑degree relatives if positive) is essential. Early identification allows genotype‑directed therapy, prevents unnecessary insulin exposure, and provides accurate prognostic information for the entire extended family.
