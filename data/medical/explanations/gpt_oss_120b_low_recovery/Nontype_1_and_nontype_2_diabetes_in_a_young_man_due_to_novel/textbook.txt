Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment  

The patient is a young adult male who was referred for assessment after routine health screening revealed persistent hyperglycaemia and the presence of glucose in the urine.  He is 22 years old, weighs 72 kg, and has a body‑mass index of 24.6 kg/m²—within the upper range of normal but not frankly obese.  Vital signs are unremarkable: blood pressure 112/70 mm Hg and a regular pulse of 78 beats per minute, indicating he is hemodynamically stable and not hypertensive.  

### Symptoms  
At presentation the patient reports classic osmotic symptoms of diabetes: increased thirst (polydipsia) and increased hunger (polyphagia) accompanied by a recent, involuntary weight loss of about 4 kg over several weeks.  He also notes frequent urination (polyuria) that prompted the detection of glycosuria on a dip‑stick test during his annual check‑up.  He denies any history of ketoacidosis, visual changes, or neuropathic symptoms.  

### Family History  
The pedigree is striking for early‑onset diabetes affecting three consecutive generations on the maternal side: the maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her early‑30s, the mother was diagnosed in her late‑20s, and the patient’s younger brother was found to be diabetic at age 20.  No relatives have reported premature cardiovascular disease, retinopathy, or nephropathy, suggesting that the familial diabetes phenotype has been relatively uncomplicated thus far.  

### Physical Examination  
A focused examination corroborates the absence of insulin‑resistance stigmata.  The skin is free of acanthosis nigricans, and there are no lipodystrophic changes.  Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations are normal, with no hepatomegaly or splenomegaly.  The absence of acanthosis nigricans—a cutaneous marker of chronic hyperinsulinaemia—makes severe insulin resistance less likely, steering the clinician away from a typical type 2 diabetes picture.  

### Initial Biochemical Profile  

| Parameter | Result | Interpretation |
|-----------|--------|----------------|
| HbA1c | 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol) | Marked chronic hyperglycaemia |
| Fasting plasma glucose | 12.4 mmol/L | Diagnostic of diabetes (≥7.0 mmol/L) |
| 2‑hour OGTT (75 g) | 19.8 mmol/L | Severe post‑prandial hyperglycaemia (≥11.1 mmol/L) |
| Lipid panel | HDL‑C 1.01 mmol/L (≈39 mg/dL) ; Triglycerides 4.31 mmol/L (≈382 mg/dL) | Low HDL and markedly elevated triglycerides, features that can accompany metabolic syndrome but may also be secondary to uncontrolled diabetes |
| Serum ketones, arterial pH | Not elevated, normal pH | No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) |

The markedly elevated HbA1c together with fasting and 2‑hour OGTT values confirm the presence of diabetes mellitus.  The OGTT pattern—disproportionately high post‑prandial glucose relative to fasting glucose—is characteristic of β‑cell dysfunction rather than pure insulin resistance.  

### Assessment for Acute Complications  
Because the patient presented with hyperglycaemia but no symptoms of nausea, abdominal pain, or altered mental status, bedside assessment for DKA was performed.  Serum bicarbonate, anion gap, and venous blood gases were all within normal limits, and urine ketones were absent.  Thus, there were no acute metabolic complications requiring emergent metabolic correction.  

In summary, the initial assessment highlights a young, non‑obese adult with classic diabetes symptoms, a strong multigenerational family history suggestive of an autosomal‑dominant pattern, and laboratory evidence of significant hyperglycaemia without ketosis.  The lack of insulin‑resistance stigmata, together with preserved (though not normal) β‑cell function on subsequent testing, raises suspicion for a monogenic form of diabetes such as HNF1A‑MODY, prompting further targeted investigations.

## 2. Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning  

Distinguishing maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY) from type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) requires the integration of clinical clues, targeted laboratory tests, and, when available, probabilistic tools that estimate the likelihood of a monogenic etiology. In the present case the physician proceeded through this algorithmic pathway, allowing an early, though provisional, diagnosis of MODY that was later confirmed by genetic testing.

### Key distinguishing features of type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and MODY  

| Feature | Type 1 diabetes | Type 2 diabetes | HNF1A‑MODY |
|---------|-----------------|----------------|-----------|
| Age at onset | Usually <30 yr, often in childhood | >40 yr (but increasingly early) | <25 yr (classically) but can present in young adults |
| Body habitus | Often lean | Overweight/obese, metabolic syndrome | Usually normal BMI; can be modestly overweight (as in this patient) |
| Autoimmunity | Positive pancreatic‑autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8) | Negative antibodies | Negative antibodies (≈90 %); occasional seropositivity |
| C‑peptide (fasting or stimulated) | Low/undetectable early; declines rapidly | Preserved for many years; may be modestly reduced | Initially preserved, then gradually declines; often >0.6 nmol/L early |
| Insulin requirement | Mandatory at diagnosis | May be absent initially | May be required temporarily; often can be withdrawn |
| Family history | Usually sporadic (autoimmune) | May be present, but often polygenic | Autosomal dominant pattern—multiple first‑degree relatives with early-onset diabetes |
| Renal glucose threshold | Normal | Normal | Lowered → glycosuria at modest plasma glucose |

In this patient, the age (early 20 s), modest BMI (24.6 kg/m²), absence of acanthosis nigricans, a striking multigenerational pattern of diabetes diagnosed before age 30, and the presence of glycosuria despite a fasting glucose only modestly above the renal threshold raised suspicion for MODY rather than classic T1D or T2D.

### Interpretation of negative GADA and IA‑2A autoantibodies  

The detection of pancreatic autoantibodies is the cornerstone of T1D diagnosis. In the case, both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen‑2 antibodies (IA‑2A) were negative. A negative autoantibody panel strongly argues against autoimmune β‑cell destruction, especially when combined with a clinical picture lacking rapid progression to absolute insulin deficiency. While rare cases of seronegative T1D exist, the pre‑test probability of T1D in a non‑obese young adult with a strong family history of early‑onset diabetes is low; thus, the negative result shifted the diagnostic balance toward a non‑autoimmune etiology such as MODY.

### Glucagon stimulation test: methodology, results, and implications for β‑cell reserve  

The glucagon stimulation test evaluates residual β‑cell function by measuring the rise in C‑peptide after an intravenous bolus of 1 mg glucagon. C‑peptide is a reliable surrogate for endogenous insulin because it is secreted in equimolar amounts with insulin and is not extracted by the liver.  

- **Procedure**: Baseline (0 min) C‑peptide is drawn, followed by glucagon injection; a second sample is taken at 6 min.  
- **Interpretation**: A stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L is generally considered evidence of preserved β‑cell reserve; values <0.30 nmol/L suggest severe dysfunction.  

In this case, the baseline C‑peptide was 0.39 nmol/L and rose to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 min. The increase crossed the 0.60 nmol/L threshold, indicating that the pancreas retained some capacity to secrete insulin. Importantly, the absolute value remained modest, reflecting the progressive β‑cell impairment typical of HNF1A‑MODY, which often presents with a “partial” secretory defect rather than the near‑absent secretion seen in classic T1D.

### C‑peptide thresholds for preserved insulin secretion  

Different guidelines use slightly varying cut‑offs, but in practice:

- **Fasting C‑peptide >0.20 nmol/L (≈0.6 ng/mL)** suggests the presence of endogenous insulin.  
- **Stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L** (after glucagon or mixed‑meal) is taken as evidence of clinically meaningful reserve.  

Values in the range of 0.6–1.0 nmol/L, as observed here, are compatible with HNF1A‑MODY, especially when autoantibodies are absent. They also argue against initiating lifelong intensive insulin therapy, a key therapeutic implication.

### Use of the Exeter MODY probability calculator and its limitations in non‑White populations  

The Exeter MODY probability calculator incorporates six variables (age at diagnosis, BMI, gender, family history, HbA1c, and treatment) to generate a percentage likelihood of MODY. When the patient’s data were entered, the algorithm returned a 62 % probability (≈1 in 1.6).  

**Strengths**  
- Provides an objective, rapid screen that can prioritize patients for genetic testing.  
- Helps avoid missed MODY diagnoses in busy clinics.

**Limitations**  
- Derived from predominantly White European cohorts; thus, the baseline prevalence used in the model may not reflect Asian, African, or mixed‑ethnicity populations.  
- Certain features (e.g., BMI, presence of metabolic syndrome) have shifted in many ethnic groups due to global obesity trends, reducing the discriminatory power of those inputs.  
- The calculator does not incorporate autoantibody status or detailed C‑peptide data, which can be decisive in borderline cases.  

Therefore, while the Exeter tool supported the clinician’s suspicion, the final diagnosis still required confirmatory genetic testing.

### Role of sulfonylurea responsiveness and renal glucose threshold in clinical suspicion  

Two physiologic hallmarks are particularly suggestive of HNF1A‑MODY:

1. **Sulfonylurea responsiveness** – HNF1A mutations increase β‑cell sensitivity to ATP‑dependent potassium channel blockers. A dramatic fall in glucose after a low‑dose sulfonylurea trial is classic for MODY, often allowing discontinuation of insulin. Although a sulfonylurea challenge was not performed in this case (the patient was already on insulin), the knowledge that HNF1A‑MODY patients usually respond well to these agents reinforced the decision to switch to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist after genetic confirmation.

2. **Reduced renal glucose reabsorption** – HNF1A regulates the transcription of SGLT2 in the proximal tubule. Loss‑of‑function mutations lower the renal glucose threshold, leading to glycosuria even at modest plasma glucose concentrations. The patient’s persistent glycosuria despite a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L was an early clue pointing toward HNF1A‑MODY.

### Why initial management with insulin, metformin, and a DPP‑4 inhibitor was reasonable pending genetic results  

At presentation the patient had a markedly elevated HbA1c (10.2 %) and fasting glucose (>12 mmol/L), which constitute a hyperglycaemic emergency zone. Until the genetic result was available, the treating team needed a regimen that would:

- **Rapidly lower glucose** to avoid acute complications (e.g., osmotic diuresis, dehydration).  
- **Address possible insulin resistance**, suggested by the modestly elevated triglycerides and BMI.  
- **Preserve β‑cell function** by avoiding high insulin doses that could precipitate hypoglycaemia, especially given the suspicion of a partial secretory defect.

Basal insulin (degludec) provided a safe, titratable bridge to achieve target glucose while the oral agents (metformin and saxagliptin) tackled hepatic glucose output and incretin‑mediated pathways, respectively. This “triple‑therapy” approach is widely accepted when the underlying diabetes subtype is uncertain but hyperglycaemia is severe. Importantly, it did not preclude later de‑escalation; once the HNF1A mutation was confirmed, insulin could be stopped, and therapy was streamlined to agents (GLP‑1 agonist, metformin, DPP‑4 inhibitor) that are known to be effective and low‑risk in HNF1A‑MODY.

---  

Through systematic evaluation—recognizing a strong autosomal‑dominant family pattern, noting glycosuria and a modestly preserved C‑peptide response, ruling out autoimmunity, and applying a probability calculator—the clinicians moved from a broad differential (T1D vs. T2D) to a focused suspicion of MODY. Prompt initiation of insulin ensured safety while awaiting definitive genetic confirmation, after which therapy could be rationally tailored to the molecular diagnosis. This step‑wise reasoning exemplifies how precision medicine can be operationalized in everyday endocrine practice.

## 3. Genetic Testing and Pathophysiology of HNF1A‑MODY  

Maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes that follows an **autosomal‑dominant** inheritance pattern. A single pathogenic variant in any one of >15 genes is sufficient to cause disease, and each gene confers a characteristic biochemical and therapeutic profile. Among these, **HNF1A** (hepatocyte nuclear factor‑1α) is the most frequently implicated, accounting for roughly one‑third to two‑thirds of all MODY cases worldwide. In populations that have been systematically screened, pathogenic HNF1A variants are identified in 1–2 % of young adults with diabetes who lack autoantibodies and retain measurable C‑peptide. The high penetrance of the mutation (≈70 % of carriers develop diabetes by age 25) makes a careful genetic work‑up essential for these patients, especially when the clinical picture overlaps with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

### Structure and function of the HNF1A transcription factor  

The HNF1A protein is a 631‑amino‑acid transcription factor expressed in pancreatic β‑cells, liver, kidney, and intestine. It consists of three functional regions:

| Domain | Approximate residues | Key features |
|--------|---------------------|--------------|
| **Dimerisation domain** | 1‑32 | Mediates homo‑ and heterodimer formation, necessary for DNA binding. |
| **DNA‑binding domain (DBD)** | 33‑280 (winged‑helix motif) | Contains two sub‑domains (P‑box and D‑box) that recognize specific promoter sequences. |
| **Transactivation domain (TAD)** | 281‑631 | Interacts with co‑activators and the basal transcriptional machinery to up‑regulate target genes. |

Mutations clustered in the DBD tend to reduce the ability of HNF1A to bind its cognate DNA response elements, whereas TAD mutations usually diminish the recruitment of transcriptional co‑factors. Both classes ultimately lower transcription of several downstream genes that are critical for glucose sensing and handling.

### How HNF1A loss translates into β‑cell dysfunction  

In pancreatic β‑cells, HNF1A directly regulates transcription of multiple genes that together define the cell’s glucose‑responsive phenotype:

1. **Insulin gene (INS)** – HNF1A enhances insulin mRNA synthesis; reduced activity blunts insulin production.  
2. **GLUT2 (SLC2A2)** – Encodes the low‑affinity, high‑capacity glucose transporter that supplies β‑cells with glucose for metabolism‑stimulated insulin release. Lower GLUT2 expression limits glucose entry and diminishes the secretory trigger.  
3. **Pyruvate kinase (PKLR)** – Controls glycolytic flux; decreased PK activity impairs ATP generation, further weakening the ATP‑dependent closure of K_ATP channels that drives insulin exocytosis.  
4. **SGLT2 (SLC5A2)** – Expressed in the renal proximal tubule; HNF1A drives its transcription. When HNF1A is deficient, SGLT2 expression falls, lowering the renal glucose re‑absorption threshold and producing the characteristic **glycosuria** seen even at modest plasma glucose levels in HNF1A‑MODY.

Collectively, these molecular derangements produce a phenotype of **progressive β‑cell insufficiency** with relative preservation of insulin secretion early in the disease, post‑prandial hyperglycaemia, and low renal glucose thresholds. The phenotype is distinct from type 1 diabetes (autoimmune β‑cell loss) and type 2 diabetes (insulin resistance with variable β‑cell decline).

### Next‑generation sequencing workflow for MODY panels  

Because dozens of genes can cause MODY, most centres now employ a **targeted NGS panel** rather than sequential single‑gene testing. The typical workflow is:

1. **Pre‑analytical selection** – Patients meeting clinical criteria (early‑onset diabetes, autosomal‑dominant family history, negative autoimmune antibodies, preserved C‑peptide) are consented and a peripheral blood sample is drawn.  
2. **Library preparation** – Genomic DNA is fragmented, adapters are ligated, and a capture probe set specific for the MODY gene list (including HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.) enriches the regions of interest.  
3. **Sequencing** – Enriched libraries are sequenced on a platform such as Illumina MiSeq or NextSeq, generating 100–150 bp paired‑end reads with >100× average depth across target exons.  
4. **Bioinformatic pipeline** – Reads are aligned to the reference genome (GRCh38), duplicate reads are marked, and variant calling identifies single‑nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions/deletions, and splice‑site changes. Quality filters (read depth ≥ 20, allele frequency ≥ 20 % for heterozygotes) are applied.  
5. **Annotation and filtering** – Detected variants are annotated for gene, transcript, predicted protein change, population allele frequencies (gnomAD), and in‑silico deleteriousness scores (e.g., REVEL, CADD, AlphaMissense). Variants common in the general population (MAF > 0.01) are typically excluded as benign.  
6. **Interpretation** – The filtered list is reviewed by a molecular geneticist and an endocrinologist, who apply the **American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria** to classify each variant as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, or benign.  
7. **Reporting** – A concise clinical report states the classification, supporting evidence, and recommended follow‑up (e.g., cascade testing of relatives).

### ACMG classification of the novel c.809A>G (p.N270S) HNF1A variant  

The patient’s NGS panel identified a heterozygous **c.809A>G** change, translating to a **p.Asn270Ser (N270S)** substitution in exon 4, within the DNA‑binding domain. Applying the ACMG/AMP framework, the variant meets several criteria:

| ACMG Criterion | Evidence in this case |
|----------------|-----------------------|
| **PM2** (moderate) – absent from population databases (gnomAD, 1000 Genomes) | Variant not reported in any curated frequency dataset (MAF = 0). |
| **PM1** (moderate) – located in a mutational hot‑spot or critical functional domain | N270 lies in the highly conserved DBD; >90 % of pathogenic HNF1A variants cluster here. |
| **PP3** (supporting) – multiple computational tools predict deleterious effect | AlphaMissense score = 0.995 (near‑maximum), REVEL = 0.88, CADD = 28. |
| **PP1** (supporting) – cosegregation with disease in family | The variant is present in the mother and younger brother, both with early‑onset diabetes; absent in an unaffected aunt (if tested). |
| **PS4** (strong) – prevalence of the variant is significantly increased in affected individuals versus controls | Not applicable because the variant is novel, but the phenotype‑genotype match (early‑onset, glycosuria, negative antibodies) strongly supports pathogenicity. |

Collectively, the combination **PM2 + PM1 + PP3 + PP1** yields a **likely pathogenic** classification per ACMG rules (≥2 moderate + ≥2 supporting). The authors of the case report interpreted the variant as **pathogenic**, citing the very high AlphaMissense score and the functional relevance of the altered residue (adjacent to known pathogenic Asn268/Asn271 mutations). In clinical practice, a likely pathogenic result in a gene with a well‑established disease association (HNF1A) is sufficient to make a molecular diagnosis of MODY, particularly when the clinical picture aligns.

### AlphaMissense scoring and its role in variant interpretation  

AlphaMissense is a deep‑learning model that predicts the impact of missense changes on protein function based on structure‑aware embeddings. Scores range from 0 (benign) to 1 (highly deleterious). A threshold of **≥0.8** is commonly used to support a damaging prediction. In this case, the N270S substitution received a **0.995** score, placing it among the top 0.5 % of predicted deleterious missense variants across the human proteome. When combined with other lines of evidence (absence from population data, domain location, segregation), AlphaMissense provides a strong computational argument for pathogenicity, satisfying the ACMG **PP3** criterion.

### Clinical impact of DNA‑binding vs transactivation domain mutations  

Large genotype‑phenotype series of HNF1A‑MODY have shown that **DNA‑binding domain (DBD) mutations** are generally associated with:

* **Earlier age of diabetes onset** (median ≈ 18 years) because the ability of the transcription factor to bind DNA is severely compromised.  
* **More pronounced glycosuria** due to stronger down‑regulation of renal SGLT2.  
* **Greater sensitivity to low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists**, as some residual β‑cell function remains but insulin secretion is glucose‑dependent.

In contrast, **transactivation domain (TAD) mutations** often produce a milder phenotype, with later onset (median ≈ 25 years), less frequent glycosuria, and a slower rate of β‑cell decline. The current case, harbouring a DBD mutation (p.N270S), fits these patterns: the patient presented in his early twenties, exhibited marked glycosuria despite modest hyperglycaemia, and responded well to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist after insulin discontinuation.

Understanding whether a mutation resides in the DBD or TAD can therefore inform **prognostic counselling** (expected disease trajectory) and **therapeutic selection** (e.g., early use of agents that augment insulin secretion without causing hypoglycaemia). It also underscores why **genetic confirmation** is more than a diagnostic label—it tailors management to the underlying molecular defect.  

---  

In summary, the identification of the novel **c.809A>G (p.N270S)** HNF1A variant illustrates the full continuum from **clinical suspicion** of MODY, through **targeted NGS**, to **rigorous ACMG‐based interpretation** and **mechanistic insight** into β‑cell dysfunction. The variant’s location in the DNA‑binding domain, its predicted deleterious effect (high AlphaMissense score), and its segregation with disease collectively explain the patient’s early‑onset hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, and preserved yet insufficient insulin secretion, guiding the shift from insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist‑based regimen.

## 4. Management Tailored to the Genetic Diagnosis  

The confirmation of a heterozygous **HNF1A** pathogenic variant reclassifies the patient’s diabetes from a presumed mixed type‑1/type‑2 phenotype to **HNF1A‑MODY**. This shift has immediate therapeutic implications because the underlying pathophysiology—impaired β‑cell insulin secretion with preserved insulin sensitivity and a reduced renal glucose threshold—responds predictably to specific drug classes. The management plan therefore now centres on agents that maximise residual β‑cell output, minimise hypoglycaemia, and avoid medications that exacerbate the intrinsic glycosuric tendency.

### First‑line pharmacotherapy for HNF1A‑MODY: low‑dose sulfonylureas versus GLP‑1 receptor agonists  

Historically, **low‑dose sulfonylureas (SUs)** have been the cornerstone of HNF1A‑MODY treatment. SUs close the ATP‑sensitive potassium channel on β‑cells, causing depolarisation and a rapid rise in insulin release. Because patients with HNF1A mutations retain the downstream insulin secretory machinery, even a modest SU dose (e.g., gliclazide 30–60 mg daily or glimepiride 1–2 mg daily) can bring HbA1c into target range. However, the efficacy of SUs wanes as β‑cell reserve progressively declines, and the risk of **hypoglycaemia** rises, particularly in younger, more active individuals.

**Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1RAs)** such as dulaglutide or liraglutide augment glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon, slow gastric emptying, and promote satiety. In HNF1A‑MODY, GLP‑1RAs provide a physiologically aligned stimulus—insulin release only when glucose is elevated—thereby lowering hypoglycaemia risk while still achieving potent glucose lowering. Randomised crossover data in HNF1A‑MODY patients have shown that dulaglutide and glimepiride produce comparable reductions in fasting and post‑prandial glucose, but the GLP‑1RA arm experiences fewer symptomatic hypoglycaemic episodes. Consequently, GLP‑1RAs are increasingly considered first‑line, especially when patients have borderline β‑cell function, weight concerns, or prior hypoglycaemia with SUs.

### Rationale for switching from basal insulin to dulaglutide after genetic confirmation  

At presentation the patient was started on basal insulin degludec to achieve rapid glucose control while awaiting genetic results. Once the **HNF1A variant** was identified, two key observations prompted insulin discontinuation:  

1. **Preserved, albeit reduced, β‑cell function** (stimulated C‑peptide ≈ 0.8 nmol/L) indicated that endogenous insulin secretion could be pharmacologically amplified.  
2. **Absence of autoimmunity** and a strong autosomal‑dominant family pattern made a chronic insulin requirement unlikely.  

Switching to **dulaglutide (1.5 mg weekly)** provided several advantages:  
- It restored endogenous insulin release in a glucose‑dependent manner, aligning with the patient’s residual β‑cell capacity.  
- It eliminated the daily injection burden of basal insulin and the associated risk of overtreatment.  
- It offered modest weight loss and improved satiety, beneficial given the patient’s borderline BMI and triglyceride elevation.  

Within one month after the transition, HbA1c fell from 9.1 % to 7.4 %, and after six months reached 6.0 %, confirming the appropriateness of the switch.

### Evidence base for GLP‑1RA efficacy and hypoglycaemia risk in HNF1A‑MODY  

- **Efficacy**: A double‑blind, crossover trial (n ≈ 30 HNF1A‑MODY patients) compared liraglutide 1.8 mg daily with glimepiride 2 mg daily. Both agents reduced fasting plasma glucose by ~1.5 mmol/L and post‑prandial excursions by ~2.0 mmol/L, with no statistically significant difference in HbA1c reduction after 12 weeks.  
- **Hypoglycaemia**: The same study recorded symptomatic hypoglycaemia in 16 % of the glimepiride arm versus 2 % of the liraglutide arm. Real‑world registries corroborate these findings, noting that GLP‑1RAs rarely cause hypoglycaemia unless combined with SUs or insulin.  
- **Safety**: GLP‑1RAs are well tolerated; the most common adverse events are transient gastrointestinal symptoms, which typically resolve within weeks.

These data support the selection of dulaglutide as a safe, effective alternative to both basal insulin and sulfonylureas for patients with moderate β‑cell dysfunction.

### Role of metformin and DPP‑4 inhibitors as adjuncts or maintenance therapy  

**Metformin** acts primarily by reducing hepatic gluconeogenesis and modestly improving peripheral insulin sensitivity. In HNF1A‑MODY, insulin sensitivity is usually preserved, so metformin’s glucose‑lowering contribution is modest (≈0.3–0.5 % HbA1c reduction). Nonetheless, it is retained because:  

- It addresses the patient’s **elevated triglycerides** and may favorably influence the lipid profile.  
- It lowers the insulin dose required from the GLP‑1RA, reducing potential gastrointestinal side effects.  

**DPP‑4 inhibitors** (e.g., saxagliptin 5 mg daily) increase endogenous GLP‑1 levels by preventing its degradation. In isolation they produce limited HbA1c reduction, but when combined with metformin they provide an additive effect without increasing hypoglycaemia risk. In this case, saxagliptin was continued after the GLP‑1RA was tapered to a maintenance regimen, offering a convenient oral backbone while preserving glycaemic control.

### Contraindications: SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY and risk of dehydration/ketoacidosis  

HNF1A directly regulates **SGLT2 transcription** in the proximal tubule. Loss‑of‑function mutations diminish SGLT2 expression, resulting in a **lower renal glucose threshold** and baseline glycosuria. Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor (e.g., empagliflozin) could precipitate **excessive glucosuria**, leading to intravascular volume depletion, electrolyte imbalance, and, in the setting of reduced insulin secretion, an increased propensity for **euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)**. Case series have documented severe dehydration and DKA in HNF1A‑MODY patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors, especially during intercurrent illness or reduced oral intake. Consequently, SGLT2 inhibitors are **contraindicated** in this genetic subtype, and patients should be educated to report any signs of dehydration or nausea promptly.

### Lifestyle recommendations: diet, exercise, monitoring of glycosuria  

Although pharmacotherapy is central, **lifestyle measures** reinforce metabolic control and mitigate complications:  

- **Diet**: Emphasise a **moderately carbohydrate‑controlled diet** (45–50 % of total calories) with high‑fiber, low‑glycaemic‑index foods to blunt post‑prandial spikes. Since glycosuria can cause caloric loss, ensure adequate **hydration** and **electrolyte balance**.  
- **Exercise**: Encourage **150 min/week of moderate aerobic activity** (e.g., brisk walking) plus two sessions of resistance training. Physical activity improves insulin sensitivity and assists triglyceride reduction.  
- **Glycosuria monitoring**: Periodic urine dipstick testing can serve as a bedside indicator of glucose excursions. Persistent high‑grade glycosuria despite good HbA1c may suggest overtreatment with agents that raise the renal glucose load (e.g., unrecognized SGLT2 inhibition) and should prompt medication review.  

### Monitoring plan: HbA1c targets, C‑peptide trends, renal function, and microvascular screening  

1. **HbA1c**: Aim for **≤ 7 % (53 mmol/mol)**; given the patient’s age and low hypoglycaemia risk, a tighter target of **≤ 6.5 %** is reasonable if achievable without adverse effects. Test every 3 months until stable, then every 6 months.  
2. **C‑peptide**: Repeat **fasting and stimulated C‑peptide** annually to document β‑cell trajectory. A declining trend may herald the need to re‑introduce low‑dose sulfonylureas or consider early combination therapy.  
3. **Renal function**: Baseline eGFR and urine albumin‑to‑creatinine ratio (UACR) are required; repeat **annually** (more frequently if eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²). Metformin is withheld if eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m².  
4. **Microvascular screening**:  
   - **Retinopathy**: Dilated retinal exam at diagnosis, then **every 2 years** if no abnormalities.  
   - **Nephropathy**: As above, with UACR and eGFR.  
   - **Neuropathy**: Annual clinical foot exam; consider **Sudoscan** or quantitative sensory testing if symptoms arise.  
5. **Cardiovascular risk**: Lipid panel every 6 months; address triglyceride elevation with diet, omega‑3 fatty acids, or fibrates if > 5 mmol/L.  

By integrating **genotype‑guided pharmacology** with vigilant monitoring and lifestyle optimisation, the patient’s glucose homeostasis is maintained, hypoglycaemia avoided, and long‑term organ protection maximised—exemplifying precision medicine in HNF1A‑MODY.

## 5. Outcomes, Follow‑up, and Clinical Lessons  

### Glycaemic trajectory after targeted therapy  
The most striking clinical datum in this case is the speed and magnitude of glycaemic improvement once a MODY‑directed regimen was instituted. At diagnosis the patient’s HbA1c was 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol), reflecting chronic hyperglycaemia despite a relatively short symptom duration. After one month of combined basal insulin, metformin and saxagliptin the HbA1c fell modestly to 9.1 %. The pivotal change occurred when the HNF1A mutation was identified and the insulin was stopped in favour of a once‑weekly GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide 1.5 mg) added to metformin. Within six months the HbA1c reached 6.0 % (42 mmol/mol) and has remained below 6.5 % on a simplified regimen of metformin 1500 mg day⁻¹ plus saxagliptin 5 mg day⁻¹. This trajectory mirrors the expected pharmacodynamics of GLP‑1 agonists in HNF1A‑MODY: by enhancing glucose‑dependent insulin secretion they compensate for the β‑cell secretory defect without provoking hypoglycaemia, allowing rapid normalisation of fasting and post‑prandial glucose concentrations.

### Rational de‑escalation of therapy  
The decision to withdraw insulin was supported by three objective findings: (1) a robust fall in HbA1c after only a brief insulin course, (2) a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol L⁻¹ (well above the 0.60 nmol L⁻¹ threshold that suggests clinically meaningful endogenous insulin secretion), and (3) the genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY, a condition that classically responds to secretagogue‑type agents rather than insulin. By eliminating insulin the patient avoided the burden of injections, the risk of weight gain and the costs associated with long‑acting basal analogues. The subsequent simplification from three agents (insulin, metformin, saxagliptin) to a dual oral regimen further reduced pill burden and out‑of‑pocket expenses while preserving glycaemic control.

### Autonomic nervous system monitoring  
Sudomotor testing performed at presentation revealed severely reduced electrochemical skin conductance in the hands, indicating small‑fiber autonomic dysfunction. Autonomic neuropathy can precede overt microvascular complications and is not captured by routine HbA1c or lipid monitoring. In this patient, serial sudomotor assessments at three and twelve months showed a gradual return toward normal conductance values (improvement of >30 % from baseline). This partial reversal likely reflects two mechanisms: (i) mitigation of glucotoxicity after rapid glycaemic control, and (ii) the neuroprotective actions of GLP‑1 agonists, which have been shown experimentally to improve endothelial function and small‑fiber integrity. Ongoing evaluation every six months is recommended, with the understanding that autonomic recovery may plateau and that persistent abnormalities warrant intensified cardiovascular risk management.

### Microvascular surveillance – renal and retinal outcomes  
At one year the patient’s urine albumin‑to‑creatinine ratio remained in the normal range (<30 mg g⁻¹) and retinal examination showed no diabetic lesions. These findings are reassuring, especially given the prior HbA1c >10 % and the known propensity for HNF1A‑MODY patients to develop microvascular disease when hyperglycaemia is prolonged. Nonetheless, guidelines for MODY advise annual screening for albuminuria and biennial dilated fundus examination, paralleling recommendations for type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Early detection remains essential because the natural history of HNF1A‑MODY can include progressive β‑cell loss, which may eventually raise the risk for complications despite good current control.

### Family counselling and cascade testing  
Because HNF1A‑MODY follows an autosomal‑dominant inheritance pattern, each first‑degree relative carries a 50 % chance of harbouring the pathogenic variant. After the index case’s diagnosis, genetic counselling was provided to discuss implications for screening, reproductive planning, and therapeutic optimisation of affected relatives. Cascade testing was offered to the mother, younger brother and maternal grandmother; the mother and brother were found to carry the p.N270S variant and were already on metformin with adequate control, whereas the grandmother (diagnosed in her thirties) was switched from a sulfonylurea regimen to a low‑dose GLP‑1 agonist, resulting in an HbA1c reduction from 8.1 % to 6.8 % over three months. This exemplifies how a single molecular diagnosis can streamline care for multiple family members and prevent unnecessary insulin or high‑dose sulfonylurea use.

### Economic and quality‑of‑life impact of a precise MODY diagnosis  
From a health‑system perspective the initial work‑up (including autoantibody panels, glucagon stimulation test, OGTT and NGS) incurs a modest cost (≈US $1 500 in most settings). However, the downstream savings are considerable: avoidance of lifelong basal insulin (>US $5 000 yr⁻¹), reduced frequency of severe hypoglycaemia‑related emergency visits, and the possibility of using lower‑cost oral agents. Moreover, the patient reported marked improvements in daily functioning—no more injection‑related anxiety, fewer dietary restrictions, and restored confidence in physical activity. Quality‑adjusted life‑year (QALY) modelling in similar MODY cohorts estimates a gain of 0.3–0.5 QALYs over a decade when a correct molecular diagnosis guides therapy, underscoring both clinical and economic value.

### Looking ahead – expanding precision tools for MODY  
The case highlights several unmet needs that will shape future practice:

1. **Broader NGS implementation** – Current testing is often limited to individuals meeting stringent clinical criteria, which under‑detects MODY, particularly in non‑European populations. Universal or population‑based NGS panels for early‑onset diabetes could capture atypical presentations such as mild obesity or metabolic‑syndrome features, as seen in this patient.

2. **Ethnicity‑adjusted probability calculators** – The Exeter MODY probability tool performed well here (62 % estimated probability) but was derived from predominantly White cohorts. Incorporating data from Asian, African and Hispanic registries would improve specificity and avoid over‑ or under‑triage in diverse groups.

3. **Biomarker development** – No single laboratory test reliably differentiates HNF1A‑MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Emerging candidates (e.g., urinary C‑peptide/creatinine ratios, serum microRNA signatures, or metabolomic patterns reflecting impaired SGLT2 expression) merit prospective validation. A rapid bedside assay would dramatically accelerate case identification and reduce unnecessary testing.

4. **Therapeutic algorithms tailored to genotype** – While sulfonylureas remain first‑line in many HNF1A‑MODY patients, this case illustrates that GLP‑1 receptor agonists can be equally effective with lower hypoglycaemia risk. Future randomised trials should stratify participants by specific HNF1A domain mutations (DNA‑binding vs. transactivation) to determine whether genotype predicts response to sulfonylurea versus incretin‑based therapy.

In summary, the patient’s rapid HbA1c reduction, safe discontinuation of insulin, recovery of autonomic function, and lack of microvascular disease at one year collectively validate the clinical advantage of a precision‑medicine approach. By confirming a pathogenic HNF1A variant, the care team was able to tailor therapy, provide targeted family counselling, and avoid the long‑term costs and complications associated with misdiagnosed diabetes. Ongoing surveillance will focus on preserving β‑cell reserve, monitoring autonomic status, and ensuring early detection of any microvascular injury, while broader efforts aim to make such individualized diagnoses routine rather than exceptional.
