Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanism by which HNF1A mutations lead to both impaired insulin secretion and low renal glucose threshold (glycosuria).

**Pathophysiology of HNF‑1α (HNF1A) MODY – why patients have both an insulin‑secretory defect and a low renal glucose threshold**

1. **HNF‑1α is a master transcription factor**  
   - Expressed in pancreatic β‑cells, liver, and the proximal tubule of the kidney.  
   - In β‑cells it drives expression of *INS* (insulin), *GLUT2* (SLC2A2), and enzymes of glycolysis (e.g., pyruvate kinase).  
   - In the renal proximal tubule it controls the *SLC5A2* gene that encodes sodium‑glucose cotransporter‑2 (SGLT2), the principal transporter responsible for re‑absorbing >90 % of filtered glucose.

2. **Heterozygous loss‑of‑function mutations (such as the novel p.N270S variant in exon 4)** produce a dose‑dependent reduction of HNF‑1α activity.  
   - Because the protein functions as a dimer, a single defective allele markedly diminishes transcription of its target genes (haploinsufficiency).

3. **Impaired insulin secretion**  
   - ↓ GLUT2 reduces glucose entry into β‑cells, blunting the glucose‑stimulated rise in intracellular ATP.  
   - ↓ pyruvate kinase and other glycolytic enzymes further limit ATP generation.  
   - The ATP‑sensitive K⁺ channel (K_ATP) therefore remains more open, β‑cell depolarisation is attenuated, and calcium‑mediated exocytosis of insulin granules is reduced.  
   - The net effect is a progressive, quantitative defect in insulin release that first manifests as post‑prandial hyperglycaemia and later as fasting hyperglycaemia, as seen in the case (stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L, far below the normal response).

4. **Reduced renal glucose threshold (glycosuria)**  
   - HNF‑1α directly activates *SLC5A2* transcription. Haploinsufficiency leads to ↓ SGLT2 protein in the proximal tubule.  
   - With fewer functional SGLT2 transporters, the maximal re‑absorption capacity for glucose falls (normally ~350 mg/min).  
   - Consequently, the plasma glucose concentration at which glucose begins to appear in urine drops from the usual ≈10–11 mmol/L to ≈5–7 mmol/L.  
   - Clinically this manifests as “glycosuria out of proportion to the plasma glucose,” a classic bedside clue to HNF1A‑MODY and exactly what the young man exhibited.

5. **Why both phenomena coexist**  
   - The same transcription factor regulates genes in two distinct organs.  
   - A single heterozygous loss‑of‑function mutation therefore simultaneously compromises β‑cell glucose sensing/insulin release and tubular glucose re‑absorption.  
   - The degree of each defect depends on the residual activity of the mutant protein; in most HNF1A mutations the β‑cell defect is more clinically apparent because β‑cells have a limited reserve, whereas the renal phenotype is evident early as low‑threshold glycosuria.

**Take‑home point for trainees:**  
When a young adult presents with hyperglycaemia, modestly preserved C‑peptide, negative autoantibodies, and especially “unexpected” glycosuria, think of HNF1A‑MODY. The dual pathophysiology—β‑cell transcriptional insufficiency and SGLT2 down‑regulation—explains the characteristic combination of impaired insulin secretion and a low renal glucose threshold described in the case report. Recognizing this pattern directs you to genetic testing and toward sulfonylurea or GLP‑1‑RA therapy rather than unnecessary insulin or SGLT2‑inhibitors.
