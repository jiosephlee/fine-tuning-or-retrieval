Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which laboratory tests are essential to differentiate HNF1A‑MODY from autoimmune type 1 diabetes, and what are the expected results in this case?

**Essential laboratory tests to separate HNF1A‑MODY from autoimmune type 1 diabetes**

| Test | Rationale | Expected result in this patient (HNF1A‑MODY) |
|------|-----------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Pan‑creatic‑islet autoantibodies** (GAD‑65 = GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8‑Ab, (occasionally IAA)) | Presence of any of these antibodies is the hallmark of autoimmune β‑cell destruction (type 1 diabetes). Their absence makes an autoimmune etiology unlikely, although a small minority of MODY patients can be seropositive. | **Negative** for GADA and IA‑2A (the case reported both antibodies as negative; ZnT8 was not measured but would be expected negative). |
| **Fasting and stimulated C‑peptide** (or alternatively fasting insulin) | C‑peptide reflects endogenous insulin secretion. In early type 1 diabetes C‑peptide may still be ≥0.6 nmol/L, but it falls rapidly; in MODY it is usually preserved or only modestly reduced, because β‑cell loss is progressive rather than immune‑mediated. | **Fasting C‑peptide 0.39 nmol/L** (low) and **stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L** after glucagon – a modest rise that indicates residual β‑cell function, compatible with HNF1A‑MODY and inconsistent with established type 1 diabetes (where stimulated C‑peptide would be <0.2 nmol/L). |
| **HbA₁c and glucose profile** (fasting, OGTT) | Both disorders can have markedly elevated glucose, but MODY often shows a pronounced post‑prandial excursion with relatively preserved fasting glucose early in the disease. | **HbA₁c = 10.2 %**, fasting glucose = 12.4 mmol/L, 2‑h OGTT = 19.8 mmol/L – severe hyperglycaemia, but the presence of a robust post‑prandial rise supports a β‑cell secretory defect rather than absolute insulin deficiency. |
| **Renal threshold for glucose / urinary glucose** (optional) | HNF1A‑MODY is characterised by a lowered renal glucose‑reabsorption threshold, producing glycosuria at modest plasma glucose levels. This feature is absent in type 1 diabetes. | **Glycosuria** noted on routine screening despite plasma glucose that is not extremely high, consistent with the reduced SGLT2 expression seen in HNF1A‑MODY. |
| **HLA high‑risk typing** (optional) | Certain HLA haplotypes (DR3‑DQ2, DR4‑DQ8) confer strong susceptibility to type 1 diabetes. Absence of these haplotypes lowers the pre‑test probability of autoimmunity. | Not performed in the case, but a negative high‑risk HLA would further favour MODY. |

**How the results distinguish the two entities**

1. **Autoantibodies** – The cornerstone of type 1 diabetes diagnosis. Their negativity in this patient makes autoimmune diabetes unlikely.
2. **C‑peptide** – Demonstrates that the pancreas still produces insulin (stimulated C‑peptide > 0.6 nmol/L). In classic type 1 diabetes, especially after a few months of hyperglycaemia, the stimulated C‑peptide is usually <0.2 nmol/L.
3. **Glycosuria with a relatively modest renal threshold** – A characteristic of HNF1A‑MODY that is not seen in type 1 diabetes.
4. **Family history and age of onset** – Although not a laboratory test, they provide context; the autosomal‑dominant pattern strongly points toward MODY when combined with the laboratory picture.

**Bottom line:** The minimal panel required to separate HNF1A‑MODY from autoimmune type 1 diabetes consists of (1) a full islet‑autoantibody panel (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8‑Ab, IAA when appropriate) and (2) a C‑peptide assessment (fasting and/or stimulated). In this case, the autoantibodies were negative and the glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide rose to 0.83 nmol/L, confirming residual β‑cell function and supporting the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY rather than type 1 diabetes.
