## Why This Case Stands Out  

A 24‑year‑old Asian man walks into the clinic for his routine health screen and is found to have a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, an HbA1c of 10.2 % and glycosuria – but no ketoacidosis, no autoantibodies, and a BMI that hovers just below the obesity threshold. His family tree looks like a classic autosomal‑dominant pedigree: mother, maternal grandmother and younger brother all diagnosed with “young‑onset diabetes.”  

The clinicians start him on basal insulin, metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor, only to later discover a **novel heterozygous HNF1A mutation (p.N270S)**. Within three months of switching to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist plus metformin, his HbA1c plummets to 6 %.  

What makes this story compelling isn’t just the dramatic glucose drop—it’s the reminder that **monogenic diabetes (MODY) is often masquerading as type 1 or type 2**, especially in non‑European populations where awareness is low and genetic panels are rarely ordered.  

---

## The Clinical Puzzle: When to Think MODY  

MODY sits at the crossroads of type 1 (autoimmune β‑cell destruction) and type 2 (insulin resistance). The clinical clues are subtle, but they tend to cluster. Below is a bedside checklist that can tip you off:

1. **Age of onset ≤ 25 years** (often discovered incidentally).  
2. **Strong autosomal‑dominant family history** – at least two first‑degree relatives with diabetes diagnosed before age 30, and the pattern spans at least two generations.  
3. **Negative diabetes‑related autoantibodies** (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8).  
4. **Preserved or only modestly reduced C‑peptide** – fasting >0.6 ng/mL (or stimulated >0.6 nmol/L).  
5. **Absence of marked insulin resistance** – BMI usually <30 kg/m², minimal acanthosis nigricans, lipid profile not overtly dyslipidemic (though our case had high triglycerides).  
6. **Glycosuria out of proportion to glucose level** – a low renal threshold caused by impaired SGLT2 transcription in HNF1A‑MODY.  
7. **Lack of ketosis or DKA** despite high glucose.  

If **three or more** of these features are present, MODY should move from “rare” to “probable” on your differential.

## The Exeter MODY Probability Calculator – Use with Caution  

The Exeter group built an online tool that plugs in age, BMI, family history, autoantibodies, C‑peptide, and treatment response to output a probability of MODY. In the reported case, the calculator gave a **62 % probability** (≈1 in 1.6).  

**Key limitations**:  

- **Derivation cohort was predominantly White European**. Validation in Asian, African, or Hispanic groups is sparse, so the algorithm can over‑ or underestimate true risk.  
- **It doesn’t weight glycosuria or sudomotor dysfunction**, both of which are clues in HNF1A‑MODY.  
- **The tool assumes physicians have already ruled out secondary causes** (e.g., steroid‑induced hyperglycaemia).  

**Practical tip**: Use the calculator as a *conversation starter* with genetics services, not as a definitive gatekeeper. When the output is >20 % in a young adult with a classic family history, push for sequencing even if the number seems modest.

## When to Order Genetic Testing  

**Indications** (based on consensus statements and real‑world experience):  

- **Young‑onset diabetes (<25 y) with a >=2‑generation autosomal‑dominant pedigree**.  
- **Negative autoantibodies** and **preserved C‑peptide** beyond the first year of disease.  
- **Unexplained glycosuria** at relatively low plasma glucose.  
- **Unusual response to therapy** – for example, rapid decompensation on low‑dose insulin or need for high‑dose sulfonylureas.  
- **Patient interest in family planning** – a confirmed monogenic diagnosis informs cascade testing and reproductive counseling.  

**What test?**  

- **Targeted NGS panel for MODY genes** (including HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.) is cost‑effective and captures >95 % of known pathogenic variants.  
- **Whole‑exome sequencing** may be considered if the panel is negative but suspicion remains high.  

**Turn‑around time** usually 2–4 weeks at a reference lab; plan interim management based on clinical phenotype, not awaiting results.

---

## Therapeutic Implications of an HNF1A Diagnosis  

The genotype directly informs drug choice:  

- **First‑line**: Low‑dose sulfonylureas (e.g., glimepiride 1–2 mg) exploit the residual β‑cell sensitivity to ATP‑channel closure.  
- **Alternative/adjunct**: GLP‑1 receptor agonists (liraglutide, dulaglutide) are increasingly favored, especially when hypoglycaemia risk or β‑cell exhaustion is a concern. In the case report, dulaglutide plus metformin drove HbA1c from 10 % to 6 % while allowing insulin discontinuation.  
- **Metformin** remains useful for modest insulin resistance, but it is not disease‑modifying for MODY.  
- **SGLT‑2 inhibitors** should be used **cautiously**; HNF1A‑MODY already reduces SGLT2 expression, predisposing to dehydration and euglycaemic DKA.  

**What to avoid:**  
- Unnecessary high‑dose insulin (risk of weight gain, hypoglycaemia).  
- Long‑acting sulfonylureas at high doses (increased hypoglycaemia).  

**Monitoring:** Continue periodic C‑peptide and HbA1c checks, and screen for microvascular complications as per standard diabetes guidelines—MODY does not eliminate long‑term risk.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Don’t let age bias you** – early‑onset diabetes is not automatically type 1; a strong autosomal‑dominant pedigree should raise red flags.  
- **Negative autoantibodies plus preserved C‑peptide = think MODY**; add glycosuria and low renal threshold to the suspicion list.  
- **The Exeter calculator is a helpful adjunct but not definitive**—especially in non‑European populations.  
- **Order a MODY gene panel when ≥3 clinical criteria are met**; the result can dramatically reshape treatment (e.g., swapping insulin for a sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 agonist).  
- **Treatment is genotype‑driven**: HNF1A‑MODY patients respond superbly to low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists and can often discontinue insulin.  
- **Family cascade testing** is essential—each affected relative can avoid years of misdiagnosis and inappropriate therapy.  

---

### Bottom Line  

A seemingly straightforward case of “type 2 diabetes in a young adult” can hide a **precision‑medicine opportunity**. By recognizing the constellation of early onset, autosomal‑dominant inheritance, negative autoimmunity, and quirky labs like glycosuria, we can identify HNF1A‑MODY, unlock targeted therapy, and spare patients—and their families—from months or years of unnecessary insulin, hypoglycaemia, and poor glycaemic control.  

Stay curious, keep the MODY checklist at the bedside, and when in doubt, **genotype first**. Your patients will thank you.
