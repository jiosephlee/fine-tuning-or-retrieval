Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Beyond the Calculator: Challenges of MODY Screening in Asian and Multiracial Populations  

## Why this case caught my eye  

When a 22‑year‑old Asian man walks into clinic with a fasting glucose of 12 mmol/L, an HbA1c of 10.2 % and a family tree dotted with early‑onset diabetes, the reflex is often “type 2 diabetes with a family‑history component.” Yet his lack of obesity, negative autoantibodies, and a modestly preserved C‑peptide should have raised a red flag for monogenic diabetes.  

What makes the story compelling isn’t just the discovery of a *novel* HNF1A mutation (p.N270S) – it’s the way this patient’s journey exposes a blind spot in our current screening tools. The Exeter MODY probability calculator, the most widely used algorithm, was built on data from predominantly White European cohorts. When you plug his numbers into the calculator you get a 62 % probability, which sounds convincing, but that figure is anchored to a population in which the prevalence, BMI distribution, and background risk of type 2 diabetes are very different from those of East Asian or mixed‑ethnicity patients.  

In practice, the calculator can both over‑ and under‑estimate risk, leading to missed diagnoses or unnecessary genetic testing. This case reminded me that precision medicine starts with the right *pre‑test* probability, and that probability must be calibrated to the patient’s ancestry and local epidemiology.  

## The epidemiologic reality in non‑European cohorts  

| Population | Study | % of early‑onset diabetes that is MODY* | Common MODY genes identified |
|------------|-------|----------------------------------------|------------------------------|
| Chinese adults (young‑onset T2D) | Chen et al., 2023 (n = 2429) | 1.5 % (0.74 % pathogenic/likely‑pathogenic) | HNF1A predominates |
| Danish Caucasians (type 1‑like phenotype) | Møller et al., 2022 (n = 39) | 10 % of antibody‑negative cases | HNF1A, GCK |
| Russian multiracial cohort (clinical suspicion, negative antibodies) | Russian Diabetes Study, 2024 (n = 506) | 46.4 % with pathogenic/likely‑pathogenic variants | HNF1A, HNF4A, GCK |

\*“Early‑onset” generally defined as diagnosis before age 25.  

A few take‑home points from these data:  

* The overall proportion of MODY among young diabetic patients is highly variable—tiny in some East Asian series, but approaching half of carefully selected Russian cases.  
* Even within the same gene (HNF1A), mutation spectra differ: many Asian reports describe novel variants in the DNA‑binding domain, exactly where our patient’s p.N270S sits.  
* The presence of metabolic‑syndrome features (elevated triglycerides, modestly high BMI) is no longer a reliable exclusion criterion, especially as obesity prevalence rises across all ethnicities.  

## How obesity and the metabolic syndrome muddy the waters  

Historically, clinicians have leaned on “lean, non‑obese” as a quick heuristic for MODY. That worked reasonably well when the background prevalence of obesity was low. Today, the WHO reports adult overweight/obesity rates of 30‑40 % in many Asian countries and even higher in mixed‑ethnicity populations.  

Consequences for MODY screening:  

* **Phenotypic overlap** – A patient with HNF1A‑MODY may now have a BMI of 24‑26 kg/m² and triglycerides in the borderline‑high range, blurring the line with classic type 2 diabetes.  
* **Calculator bias** – The Exeter model heavily weights BMI and lipid profile, so an “overweight” Asian patient may be assigned a lower MODY probability than a similarly profiled White patient, despite similar genetic risk.  
* **Clinical inertia** – Providers may default to standard T2D algorithms (metformin → sulfonylurea) and miss the chance to use low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists that preserve β‑cell function in HNF1A‑MODY.  

Our case illustrates this perfectly: the patient’s BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L would have nudged the calculator toward “type 2” in a European‑centric model, yet the family history and autoantibody negativity tipped the scale toward MODY.  

## Practical adjustments for clinicians in diverse settings  

1. **Don’t rely on a single numeric cutoff** – Use the calculator as a *guide*, not a gatekeeper. If the result is borderline (e.g., 40‑70 % probability) and the patient is of Asian, Hispanic, or mixed ancestry, consider a lower threshold for testing.  

2. **Prioritise family history** – A clear autosomal dominant pattern (≥2 first‑degree relatives with diabetes before 35 y) should outweigh BMI or lipid values. In the Russian cohort, a strong family history was the strongest predictor of a pathogenic variant.  

3. **Add ethnicity‑specific modifiers** – When documenting the case, note the patient’s self‑identified ethnicity and apply a correction factor (e.g., +10 % MODY probability for East Asian descent, –5 % for South Asian if obesity is present). These numbers are not evidence‑based yet, but they help clinicians consciously adjust for known biases.  

4. **Screen for autoantibodies and C‑peptide early** – A negative GADA/IA‑2A panel combined with a stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (or >0.2 ng/mL fasting) should prompt a MODY work‑up, regardless of BMI.  

5. **Leverage NGS panels that include the full MODY spectrum** – Targeted sequencing of the 15+ known MODY genes is now affordable and can be ordered reflexively once clinical suspicion is raised. Avoid “stepwise” single‑gene testing, which delays diagnosis.  

6. **Educate the multidisciplinary team** – Nurses, dietitians, and pharmacists often see the patient’s weight trends and medication tolerability first. Teaching them the red‑flag features (early‑onset, family clustering, absence of autoimmunity) expands the net for appropriate referrals.  

7. **Consider local prevalence data** – If your institution serves a high proportion of Asian patients, develop an internal audit of MODY testing yield and adjust your institutional protocol accordingly.  

## Take‑home pearls (bullet‑point style for quick recall)  

- **MODY calculators are Euro‑centric** – they can mis‑estimate risk in Asian, Hispanic, and multiracial patients.  
- **Family history trumps BMI** – a clear autosomal dominant pattern should prompt genetic testing even with overweight or metabolic‑syndrome features.  
- **Negative autoantibodies + preserved C‑peptide = red flag** – especially when combined with early‑onset hyperglycaemia.  
- **Novel HNF1A variants are common in non‑White cohorts** – don’t dismiss a pathogenic interpretation because the variant isn’t in ClinVar.  
- **GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an excellent alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A‑MODY** when β‑cell reserve is waning, as demonstrated by the rapid HbA1c drop from 10.2 % to 6.0 % in the case report.  
- **Avoid SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY** – reduced SGLT2 expression increases risk of dehydration and euglycaemic ketoacidosis.  

## Bottom line  

Precision diabetes care isn’t just about sequencing the genome; it starts with **accurate pre‑test probability** that reflects the patient’s ancestry, family history, and the evolving epidemiology of obesity. The Exeter MODY calculator is a valuable tool, but it’s not a universal one. By understanding its limitations and supplementing it with ethnicity‑aware clinical judgment, we can uncover hidden cases of MODY—like the young Asian man with a novel HNF1A mutation—allowing us to tailor therapy, prevent unnecessary insulin, and ultimately improve long‑term outcomes.  

Stay curious, keep the family tree handy, and remember: when the calculator feels “off,” it probably is.
