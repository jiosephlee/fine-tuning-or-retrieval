Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When Sepsis Masks a TMA: Unraveling Thrombotic Microangiopathy in the ICU  

*Based on the “dog bite → dialysis” case of complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.*

---  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A previously healthy 40‑something woman went from a dog‑bite‑induced septic shock to dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and, after a brief improvement, she suddenly developed a classic picture of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). The twist? The underlying driver was not persistent sepsis or DIC, but a complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) that only revealed itself once the overt infection was under control.  

The clinical journey forces us to ask: **When should we stop treating “just sepsis” and start looking for a superimposed TMA?** The answer lies in a few red‑flag labs, timing of the haematologic changes, and a systematic diagnostic algorithm.

---

## The Clinical Timeline – A Quick Recap  

| Day | What Happened | Key Labs / Findings |
|-----|---------------|----------------------|
| 0   | Dog bite → no antibiotics | │ |
| 4   | Fever, hypoglycaemia, mottled feet → ED → septic shock picture | ↑WBC, ↑CRP, coagulopathy, AKI, lactate 5.7 mmol/L |
| 1‑3 ICU | Broad‑spectrum antibiotics, fluids, CRRT | DIC‑like labs (low fibrinogen, prolonged PT/aPTT), pancytopenia |
| 8   | Transfer to nephrology, anuric, dialysis‑dependent | Platelets improving, coagulation normalising |
| 10‑15| Sudden Hb drop (111 → 68 g/L), persistent thrombocytopenia, LDH surge, undetectable haptoglobin | *Schistocytes 15‑20 %* on smear, DAT false‑positive, ADAMTS13 43 % |
| 16  | Kidney biopsy: ATN + fibrin thrombi → TMA | Complement tests normal (C3/C4) |
| 17‑20| Plasma exchange (PEX) → modest haematologic response | Diuresis returns |
| 21  | Eculizumab induction (after negative cultures, meningococcal vaccination) | Haematologic remission, partial renal recovery |

**Take‑home:** The “second hit” (MAHA, worsening thrombocytopenia, kidney injury) appeared **after** the initial septic storm had begun to settle. This temporal separation is often the clue that a second process is brewing.

## Red Flags that Separate DIC from a Superimposed TMA  

| Feature | Typical in DIC | Typical in TMA (CM‑HUS/aHUS) |
|---------|----------------|------------------------------|
| **Platelet trend** | Drops early, recovers with infection control | Persistent low platelets despite resolving infection |
| **Coagulation tests (PT/aPTT, fibrinogen)** | Prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen throughout | Normalising quickly once sepsis improves |
| **Schistocytes** | May be present early, usually <5 % | >5‑10 % and increase over time |
| **LDH** | Mild‑moderate rise in early sepsis | Sharp surge contemporaneous with haemoglobin fall |
| **Haptoglobin** | Often low in both, but rebounds in DIC as hemolysis abates | Remains undetectable until the TMA is treated |
| **ADAMTS13 activity** | Usually >10 % (often >30 %) | Severely reduced (<10 %) in TTP; >10 % in CM‑HUS |
| **Renal profile** | AKI can be multifactorial, often improves with fluids | Persistent/ worsening AKI despite fluid/vasopressor optimisation |

**Key point:** In a septic patient who *initially* has DIC‑type labs, the emergence of *isolated* haemolysis (low Hb, high LDH, schistocytes) **with normalising coagulation** should trigger an urgent TMA work‑up.

---

## Step‑by‑Step Diagnostic Algorithm (ICU Focus)

1. **Recognise the shift** – Look for falling Hb out of proportion to blood loss, new schistocytes, and a rising LDH while PT/aPTT and fibrinogen trend toward normal.
2. **Run a quick haematology panel** – CBC, peripheral smear, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, direct Coombs.  
   *If schistocytes ≥5 % and haptoglobin undetectable → suspect TMA.*
3. **Calculate PLASMIC score** (if thrombocytopenia <30 ×10⁹/L, hemolysis, no active cancer, etc.) to gauge TTP likelihood.  
   *In our case: PLASMIC 4/7 → low TTP probability.*
4. **Send ADAMTS13** (turn‑around 12‑24 h in most centres). While awaiting, **initiate plasma exchange** if PLASMIC ≥6 *or* if the patient is deteriorating rapidly.  
   *In sepsis‑related TMA, early PEX provides a bridge and removes inflammatory mediators.*
5. **Screen for infection‑related triggers** – Blood cultures (already positive for *C. canimorsus*), stool PCR for Shiga toxin, viral serologies as indicated.
6. **Complement evaluation** – C3/C4 are often normal; request functional assays (AH50) and factor H/I/B levels if available.  
   *Remember: normal C3/C4 does **not** exclude CM‑HUS.*
7. **Kidney biopsy** (if renal function not improving after 48‑72 h of supportive care) – Useful to histologically confirm TMA and to rule out other glomerulopathies.
8. **Decide on definitive therapy** –  
   *If ADAMTS13 < 10 % → TTP → continue PEX + caplacizumab.*  
   *If ADAMTS13 > 10 % and complement assays suggest activation → start eculizumab (or ravulizumab) after ensuring meningococcal vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis.*

---

## Therapeutic Nuances in the Overlap Setting  

### Plasma Exchange (PEX)  
- **Why we used it:** Rapid removal of circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators while awaiting ADAMTS13 & complement results.  
- **Limitations:** In CM‑HUS, PEX only gives a transient drop in haemolysis; the underlying alternative pathway dysregulation persists.  

### Eculizumab (C5 inhibitor)  
- **Timing is everything:** Initiate *as soon as infection is controlled* (negative cultures, hemodynamic stability). Delayed therapy correlates with poorer renal recovery.  
- **Infection safety:** Terminal complement blockade predisposes to encapsulated organisms (Neisseria, *Capnocytophaga*). Vaccinate at least 2 weeks prior, give prophylactic antibiotics (penicillin or alternatives) if vaccination cannot be timed.  
- **Monitoring:** CBC, LDH, renal function weekly for the first month, then every 2‑4 weeks.  

### Adjuncts  
- **Steroids:** May blunt autoimmune haemolysis but have limited impact on complement‑mediated TMA.  
- **Renal replacement therapy:** Continue as needed; early diuresis after PEX can be a sign of partial response, but sustained renal improvement hinges on complement blockade.  

---

## Take‑Home Pearls (Bullet Summary)

- **Red flag timing:** New isolated haemolysis (falling Hb, ↑LDH, schistocytes) **after** sepsis and DIC have begun to improve = think TMA.  
- **Schistocyte count matters:** ≥5 % on peripheral smear should prompt immediate TMA work‑up.  
- **PLASMIC score is a bedside triage:** Low score → TTP unlikely; focus on complement‑mediated HUS.  
- **Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out CM‑HUS** – functional assays and clinical picture are more important.  
- **Start plasma exchange early** when TTP cannot be ruled out; it serves as a bridge while awaiting definitive labs.  
- **Eculizumab (or ravulizumab) should be started promptly** once infection control is ensured; every day of delay reduces the chance of renal recovery.  
- **Vaccination & prophylaxis are mandatory** before complement blockade, especially with a gram‑negative, encapsulated pathogen like *C. canimorsus*.  
- **Kidney biopsy can clinch the diagnosis** when the clinical picture is ambiguous and renal recovery stalls.  

---

## Bottom Line  

Sepsis is a great imitator. In the ICU, a **second hit** of TMA can lurk behind improving septic parameters. By staying alert to the evolution of haemolysis markers, applying a rapid PLASMIC‑based algorithm, and moving swiftly from PEX to complement inhibition, we can rescue patients from irreversible renal failure and improve survival. The dog‑bite case teaches us that even the most “ordinary” zoonotic infection can precipitate a life‑threatening complement‑mediated crisis—so keep your differential list as open as your lab panel.
