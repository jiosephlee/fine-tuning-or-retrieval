Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When Sepsis Masks a TMA: Unraveling Thrombotic Microangiopathy in the ICU  

*Based on the “dog bite → dialysis” case of complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.*

---  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A previously healthy 40‑something woman went from a dog‑bite‑induced septic shock to dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and, after a brief improvement, she suddenly developed a classic picture of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). The twist? The underlying driver was not persistent sepsis or DIC, but a complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) that only revealed itself once the overt infection was under control.  

The clinical journey forces us to ask: **When should we stop treating “just sepsis” and start looking for a superimposed TMA?** The answer lies in a few red‑flag labs, timing of the haematologic changes, and a systematic diagnostic algorithm.

---

## The Clinical Timeline – A Quick Recap  

| Day | What Happened | Key Labs / Findings |
|-----|---------------|----------------------|
| 0   | Dog bite → no antibiotics | │ |
| 4   | Fever, hypoglycaemia, mottled feet → ED → septic shock picture | ↑WBC, ↑CRP, coagulopathy, AKI, lactate 5.7 mmol/L |
| 1‑3 ICU | Broad‑spectrum antibiotics, fluids, CRRT | DIC‑like labs (low fibrinogen, prolonged PT/aPTT), pancytopenia |
| 8   | Transfer to nephrology, anuric, dialysis‑dependent | Platelets improving, coagulation normalising |
| 10‑15| Sudden Hb drop (111 → 68 g/L), persistent thrombocytopenia, LDH surge, undetectable haptoglobin | *Schistocytes 15‑20 %* on smear, DAT false‑positive, ADAMTS13 43 % |
| 16  | Kidney biopsy: ATN + fibrin thrombi → TMA | Complement tests normal (C3/C4) |
| 17‑20| Plasma exchange (PEX) → modest haematologic response | Diuresis returns |
| 21  | Eculizumab induction (after negative cultures, meningococcal vaccination) | Haematologic remission, partial renal recovery |

**Take‑home:** The “second hit” (MAHA, worsening thrombocytopenia, kidney injury) appeared **after** the initial septic storm had begun to settle. This temporal separation is often the clue that a second process is brewing.

---

## Red Flags that Separate DIC from a Superimposed TMA  

| Feature | Typical in DIC | Typical in TMA (CM‑HUS/aHUS) |
|---------|----------------|------------------------------|
| **Platelet trend** | Drops early, recovers with infection control | Persistent low platelets despite resolving infection |
| **Coagulation tests (PT/aPTT, fibrinogen)** | Prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen throughout | Normalising quickly once sepsis improves |
| **Schistocytes** | May be present early, usually <5 % | >5‑10 % and increase over time |
| **LDH** | Mild‑moderate rise in early sepsis | Sharp surge contemporaneous with haemoglobin fall |
| **Haptoglobin** | Often low in both, but rebounds in DIC as hemolysis abates | Remains undetectable until the TMA is treated |
| **ADAMTS13 activity** | Usually >10 % (often >30 %) | Severely reduced (<10 %) in TTP; >10 % in CM‑HUS |
| **Renal profile** | AKI can be multifactorial, often improves with fluids | Persistent/ worsening AKI despite fluid/vasopressor optimisation |

**Key point:** In a septic patient who *initially* has DIC‑type labs, the emergence of *isolated* haemolysis (low Hb, high LDH, schistocytes) **with normalising coagulation** should trigger an urgent TMA work‑up.

---

## Step‑by‑Step Diagnostic Algorithm (ICU Focus)

1. **Recognise the shift** – Look for falling Hb out of proportion to blood loss, new schistocytes, and a rising LDH while PT/aPTT and fibrinogen trend toward normal.
2. **Run a quick haematology panel** – CBC, peripheral smear, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, direct Coombs.  
   *If schistocytes ≥5 % and haptoglobin undetectable → suspect TMA.*
3. **Calculate PLASMIC score** (if thrombocytopenia <30 ×10⁹/L, hemolysis, no active cancer, etc.) to gauge TTP likelihood.  
   *In our case: PLASMIC 4/7 → low TTP probability.*
4. **Send ADAMTS13** (turn‑around 12‑24 h in most centres). While awaiting, **initiate plasma exchange** if PLASMIC ≥6 *or* if the patient is deteriorating rapidly.  
   *In sepsis‑related TMA, early PEX provides a bridge and removes inflammatory mediators.*
5. **Screen for infection‑related triggers** – Blood cultures (already positive for *C. canimorsus*), stool PCR for Shiga toxin, viral serologies as indicated.
6. **Complement evaluation** – C3/C4 are often normal; request functional assays (AH50) and factor H/I/B levels if available.  
   *Remember: normal C3/C4 does **not** exclude CM‑HUS.*
7. **Kidney biopsy** (if renal function not improving after 48‑72 h of supportive care) – Useful to histologically confirm TMA and to rule out other glomerulopathies.
8. **Decide on definitive therapy** –  
   *If ADAMTS13 < 10 % → TTP → continue PEX + caplacizumab.*  
   *If ADAMTS13 > 10 % and complement assays suggest activation → start eculizumab (or ravulizumab) after ensuring meningococcal vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis.*

---

## Therapeutic Nuances in the Overlap Setting  

### Plasma Exchange (PEX)  
- **Why we used it:** Rapid removal of circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators while awaiting ADAMTS13 & complement results.  
- **Limitations:** In CM‑HUS, PEX only gives a transient drop in haemolysis; the underlying alternative pathway dysregulation persists.  

### Eculizumab (C5 inhibitor)  
- **Timing is everything:** Initiate *as soon as infection is controlled* (negative cultures, hemodynamic stability). Delayed therapy correlates with poorer renal recovery.  
- **Infection safety:** Terminal complement blockade predisposes to encapsulated organisms (Neisseria, *Capnocytophaga*). Vaccinate at least 2 weeks prior, give prophylactic antibiotics (penicillin or alternatives) if vaccination cannot be timed.  
- **Monitoring:** CBC, LDH, renal function weekly for the first month, then every 2‑4 weeks.  

### Adjuncts  
- **Steroids:** May blunt autoimmune haemolysis but have limited impact on complement‑mediated TMA.  
- **Renal replacement therapy:** Continue as needed; early diuresis after PEX can be a sign of partial response, but sustained renal improvement hinges on complement blockade.  

---

## Take‑Home Pearls (Bullet Summary)

- **Red flag timing:** New isolated haemolysis (falling Hb, ↑LDH, schistocytes) **after** sepsis and DIC have begun to improve = think TMA.  
- **Schistocyte count matters:** ≥5 % on peripheral smear should prompt immediate TMA work‑up.  
- **PLASMIC score is a bedside triage:** Low score → TTP unlikely; focus on complement‑mediated HUS.  
- **Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out CM‑HUS** – functional assays and clinical picture are more important.  
- **Start plasma exchange early** when TTP cannot be ruled out; it serves as a bridge while awaiting definitive labs.  
- **Eculizumab (or ravulizumab) should be started promptly** once infection control is ensured; every day of delay reduces the chance of renal recovery.  
- **Vaccination & prophylaxis are mandatory** before complement blockade, especially with a gram‑negative, encapsulated pathogen like *C. canimorsus*.  
- **Kidney biopsy can clinch the diagnosis** when the clinical picture is ambiguous and renal recovery stalls.  

---

## Bottom Line  

Sepsis is a great imitator. In the ICU, a **second hit** of TMA can lurk behind improving septic parameters. By staying alert to the evolution of haemolysis markers, applying a rapid PLASMIC‑based algorithm, and moving swiftly from PEX to complement inhibition, we can rescue patients from irreversible renal failure and improve survival. The dog‑bite case teaches us that even the most “ordinary” zoonotic infection can precipitate a life‑threatening complement‑mediated crisis—so keep your differential list as open as your lab panel.



# Capnocytophaga canimorsus: The Forgotten Zoonotic Trigger of Complement‑Mediated HUS  

*Why a seemingly “simple” dog bite can launch you into the world of complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) – and what you need to know to catch it early.*

---  

## The hook – when a bite turns into dialysis  

Four days after a **dog bite to the lip**, a previously healthy woman in her late‑40s walked into the emergency department with fever, hypoglycaemia, and mottled feet. Within a week she was in the ICU on continuous renal replacement therapy, her labs showing **DIC, pancytopenia, and AKI**. Blood cultures finally grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a notoriously slow‑growing gram‑negative rod.

What was most surprising wasn't the sepsis itself (C. canimorsus can be fulminant), but the **late emergence of isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and a kidney biopsy consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy**. The work‑up landed on **complement‑mediated haemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS)**, a subtype of atypical HUS. After plasma exchange and, crucially, eculizumab, the patient regained urine output and her haematology normalized, though she remains dialysis‑dependent for a time.

The case is a vivid reminder that **C. canimorsus isn’t just a cause of sepsis—it can be the “second hit” that flips a patient into complement‑mediated HUS**.

---  

## Microbiology in a nutshell  

| Feature | Why it matters for clinicians |
|---------|------------------------------|
| **Normal flora of dogs/cats** | Any bite, scratch, or even close oral contact can inoculate the organism. |
| **Encapsulated, gram‑negative rod** | Capsule confers variable resistance to serum complement; some strains are complement‑sensitive, others not. |
| **Fastidious growth** | Requires enriched media; colonies appear after 48‑72 h (sometimes longer). |
| **Broad β‑lactam susceptibility** | Penicillins, ampicillin, ceftriaxone work, but early empiric coverage should include a β‑lactam‑β‑lactamase inhibitor (e.g., piperacillin‑tazobactam). |
| **Molecular detection** | Real‑time nanopore sequencing or 16S rRNA PCR can identify the organism within hours, bypassing culture delays. |

Because cultures can be negative for up to a week, **clinical suspicion** (dog exposure + rapid sepsis) often guides early empiric therapy.

---  

## Clinical spectrum – from bite to blast  

*Capnocytophaga* infections are famously variable:

1. **Mild local cellulitis** – may resolve spontaneously or with oral amoxicillin.  
2. **Severe sepsis** – high fevers, hypotension, disseminated intravascular coagulation, multiorgan failure.  
3. **Neurologic involvement** – meningitis, encephalitis.  
4. **Hematologic derangements** – leukopenia, pancytopenia, DIC.  
5. **Thrombotic microangiopathy** – MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, often masquerading as DIC initially.  

Risk factors for the fulminant forms include **splenectomy, alcoholism, diabetes, chronic liver disease, and immunosuppression**—but, as our case shows, *even immunocompetent adults* can succumb to severe disease.

---  

## When sepsis meets TMA – diagnostic pitfalls  

The overlap between **DIC** and **TMA** is a frequent source of confusion:

* **DIC** presents with prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer, and bleeding.
* **TMA (CM‑HUS/TTP)** shows *schistocytes*, *undetectable haptoglobin*, *elevated LDH*, and *relatively preserved coagulation*.

In the early septic phase, the TMA signal is often drowned out by overwhelming inflammation. In the reported patient, the “second wave” of isolated hemolysis appeared **after inflammatory markers were falling**, prompting the shift in diagnostic focus.

Key steps to untangle the two:

1. **Serial CBC and peripheral smear** – look for rising schistocytes once coagulopathy improves.  
2. **ADAMTS13 activity** – <10 % points to TTP; >10 % (as in this case) steers toward HUS/TMA.  
3. **Complement testing** – baseline C3/C4 can be normal; functional assays (AH50, alternative pathway) are more informative but may be confounded by plasma exchange or eculizumab.  
4. **Renal biopsy** – when the picture is still murky, histology can confirm TMA (fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli).  

A high **PLASMIC score** should trigger early plasma exchange while awaiting ADAMTS13; a low score (as here) lets you focus on other TMAs.

---  

## Pathophysiology – how a dog bite flips the complement switch  

1. **Capsular polysaccharide** of C. canimorsus evades early opsonophagocytosis, prompting robust complement activation.  
2. In genetically predisposed hosts, **alternative pathway regulation is already fragile** (factor H, I, B, MCP).  
3. The infection serves as a “second hit,” **overwhelming regulatory capacity** → unchecked C3 convertase activity → endothelial injury and microthrombi.  
4. In patients *without* detectable genetic variants (as in our case), the infection alone may transiently tip the balance, but the laboratory‑defined **CM‑HUS** persists until the complement cascade is halted.

---  

## Therapeutic choreography – antibiotics, plasma exchange, and eculizumab  

| Intervention | Timing | Rationale |
|--------------|--------|-----------|
| **Broad‑spectrum β‑lactam/β‑lactamase inhibitor** (e.g., piperacillin‑tazobactam) | Immediately on suspicion of C. canimorsus | Covers gram‑negative rods, *Capnocytophaga* spp. |
| **Targeted β‑lactam (ampicillin/penicillin)** | Once cultures confirm susceptibility | Narrower spectrum, minimizes collateral damage |
| **Plasma exchange (PEX)** | Initiated when TMA suspected but before ADAMTS13 results | Removes circulating complement activation fragments; critical bridge in TTP, modest benefit in CM‑HUS |
| **Eculizumab** (C5 inhibitor) | After blood cultures cleared, after vaccination and prophylaxis | Stops terminal complement cascade, restores endothelial integrity; early initiation (<7 days from TMA onset) predicts better renal recovery |
| **Vaccination & prophylaxis** | 2 weeks before eculizumab (or as soon as feasible) | Meningococcal vaccines + penicillin prophylaxis mitigate infection risk from complement blockade |

The **key clinical dilemma** is starting eculizumab during active bacteremia. In this case, the team waited until cultures were negative, then gave meningococcal vaccines (Menveo & Bexsero) and a short course of prophylactic ampicillin before the first eculizumab dose—balancing infection control with the need for early complement blockade.

---  

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Never dismiss a dog bite** in a febrile patient; if sepsis is disproportionate to the wound, think *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.  
- **C. canimorsus can masquerade as DIC**, but persistent hemolysis, schistocytes, and AKI after the inflammatory storm warrant a TMA work‑up.  
- **Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM‑HUS**; functional complement assays and a low ADAMTS13 are more definitive.  
- **Early eculizumab = better renal outcomes**; aim to start within a week of TMA onset, after securing adequate vaccination and antimicrobial coverage.  
- **Molecular diagnostics (nanopore, 16S PCR)** can shave days off the time to pathogen identification—critical when you’re chasing a complement‑mediated cascade.  

---  

## Bottom line  

The dog‑bite‑induced sepsis we all see in textbooks can, in rare but catastrophic cases, **trigger complement‑mediated HUS**. Recognizing the *Capnocytophaga* clue early, differentiating DIC from TMA, and moving swiftly to complement inhibition can spell the difference between dialysis‑dependent renal failure and functional recovery. Keep the canine culprit on your differential—your patient’s kidneys may thank you.



# Eculizumab in the Midst of Infection: Balancing Complement Blockade and Host Defense  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A previously healthy 40‑something woman went from a dog bite to dialysis‑dependent kidney injury in less than two weeks. The culprit? *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia that triggered disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and, puzzlingly, a later‑emerging thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).  When her hemoglobin plunged, schistocytes appeared, and ADAMTS13 was only mildly reduced, the team leapt to a diagnosis of complement‑mediated haemolytic‑uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS).  

What makes this story compelling for the bedside clinician is not just the rarity of *C. canimorsus*–induced CM‑HUS, but the therapeutic crossroads it forced: **Should we start eculizumab—a powerful complement blocker—while the patient still harbored a live Gram‑negative infection?**  

The answer is nuanced, and the case offers a roadmap for safely threading the needle between halting a lethal TMA and preserving the immune mechanisms needed to clear the bacteria.

## The Core Dilemma  

Eculizumab binds complement component C5, preventing formation of the membrane‑attack complex (MAC).  This is lifesaving in CM‑HUS, but it also eliminates a key line of defense against encapsulated organisms (Neisseria, *Haemophilus*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and—relevant here—*Capnocytophaga*).  The consequences can be dramatic: meningococcemia in a previously healthy adult is a classic, feared complication.

When a patient is still bacteremic, initiating eculizumab risks:

1. **Uncontrolled bacterial proliferation** – the MAC is a primary bactericidal mechanism for capsulated pathogens.
2. **Impaired opsonophagocytosis** – complement fragments (C3b, iC3b) that tag bacteria for neutrophil uptake are reduced.
3. **Blunted immune signaling** – downstream cytokine cascades that assist bacterial clearance are altered.

On the flip side, delaying complement inhibition can permit irreversible endothelial injury, worsening renal function, and higher mortality.  The therapeutic window is narrow; early eculizumab (ideally < 48 h from TMA onset) is associated with the best renal outcomes.

## Practical Strategies Illustrated by the Case  

### 1. Confirm Microbiologic Clearance Before the First Dose  

- **Serial blood cultures**: The team waited until all blood cultures were negative (days 4‑5 of ICU stay) before administering the first eculizumab infusion.  
- **Trend of inflammatory markers**: Declining CRP, procalcitonin, and lactate served as ancillary reassurance that the infection was under control.  

*Take‑home*: Aim for at least two consecutive negative cultures drawn 12–24 h apart. If the pathogen is fastidious (as with *C. canimorsus*), consider extended incubation or molecular diagnostics (e.g., nanopore sequencing) to rule out low‑level bacteremia.

### 2. Use Plasma Exchange (PEX) as a Bridge  

PEX removes circulating complement activation products, immune complexes, and, potentially, bacterial toxins. In this case, three daily PEX sessions were carried out **before** the first eculizumab dose:

- **Benefits**: Temporary control of hemolysis and thrombocytopenia, buying time for cultures to sterilize.  
- **Limitations**: PEX does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation; relapse is common once exchange stops.  

*Take‑away*: When cultures are still positive or when the patient is critically unstable, initiate PEX while arranging for rapid culture clearance. Transition to eculizumab as soon as the bacteremia resolves.

### 3. Vaccinate Early, but Respect Timing  

Meningococcal vaccination is mandatory before eculizumab because the drug abolishes the terminal complement pathway. The patient received Menveo® and Bexsero® **after** the first eculizumab dose but before subsequent infusions, followed by a 2‑week interval for adequate antibody development.  

Practical points:

- **If possible, vaccinate *before* eculizumab**: Give conjugate and serogroup B vaccines at least 2 weeks prior to the first dose; this is the ideal scenario.  
- **If urgent therapy is needed**: Administer vaccines **immediately** before the first dose, start prophylactic antibiotics (see below), and acknowledge that protective titers may be suboptimal for the first 2 weeks.  

### 4. Prophylactic Antibiotics During the Immunization Window  

The patient received oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin®) as prophylaxis while awaiting the development of protective anti‑meningococcal antibodies. Penicillins are effective against most meningococci and maintain coverage against *Capnocytophaga* (which usually remains susceptible).  

Guideline‑based options:

| Situation | Preferred Agent | Duration |
|-----------|----------------|----------|
| Post‑vaccination, < 2 weeks before eculizumab effect | Oral penicillin V or amoxicillin | Until 2 weeks after the last vaccine dose |
| Penicillin allergy | Azithromycin 500 mg daily (or doxycycline) | Same duration |
| High‑risk setting (ongoing bacteremia) | Broad‑spectrum IV (e.g., ceftriaxone) until cultures clear | Tailor to organism |

### 5. Monitor for Late‑Onset Infections  

Even after the infection appears cleared, complement blockade persists for weeks. Vigilance for meningococcal, pneumococcal, and other encapsulated infections is mandatory:

- Educate patients on early signs of fever, meningismus, or sepsis.  
- Maintain a low threshold for empirical antibiotics if new fever spikes.  
- Consider periodic revaccination (e.g., booster at 5 years) if complement therapy extends beyond 1 year.

## Decision‑Making Algorithm (Simplified)

1. **TMA identified** – MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI.  
2. **Exclude TTP** – ADAMTS13 < 10 %? If yes → immediate PEX, plasma, and avoid eculizumab.  
3. **Exclude STEC‑HUS** – stool PCR, history of diarrheal illness.  
4. **Assess infection status**:  
   - Active bacteremia? → Continue broad‑spectrum antibiotics, do PEX, repeat cultures q12‑24 h.  
   - Cultures negative, inflammatory markers down → proceed to step 5.  
5. **Vaccinate (meningococcal A‑C‑W‑Y + B) if not already done** → give today, start prophylactic antibiotics.  
6. **Administer eculizumab** → first dose today (after cultures negative). Continue prophylaxis for at least 2 weeks.  
7. **Follow‑up**: Weekly labs (CBC, LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine), monitor for infection, and schedule eculizumab maintenance dosing.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Don’t let fear of infection paralyze you**: Early eculizumab dramatically improves renal recovery in CM‑HUS, but it must be paired with rigorous infection control measures.  
- **PEX is a useful bridge** when cultures are still positive or the patient is hemodynamically unstable; it buys time for definitive complement blockade.  
- **Vaccination timing matters**, but when urgency dictates, give vaccines immediately before the first eculizumab dose and cover the gap with prophylactic antibiotics.  
- **Serial cultures and inflammatory trends are the bedside biomarkers** that tell you when it’s safe to cross the line into complement inhibition.  
- **Maintain a high index of suspicion for atypical infectious triggers**—*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a perfect example of a slow‑growing, fastidious organism that can precipitate a complement‑mediated TMA even in immunocompetent hosts.  

In the end, the case teaches us that with a structured, stepwise approach—culture clearance, bridging PEX, timely vaccination, and prophylactic antibiotics—we can safely introduce eculizumab even in the backdrop of a recent severe infection, turning a potentially fatal TMA into a survivable, partially reversible kidney injury.

# Beyond C3/C4: Advanced Complement Testing and Genetic Work‑up in Atypical HUS  

*Why a “normal” complement panel doesn’t rule out complement‑mediated HUS, how plasma‑exchange and eculizumab skew results, and when to call the genetics lab.*

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A previously healthy 48‑year‑old woman presented after a dog bite with fulminant sepsis, DIC, and dialysis‑dependent AKI. Four days later she developed an isolated hemolytic anemia, persistent thrombocytopenia, and a kidney biopsy that showed classic thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The work‑up eventually landed on complement‑mediated hemolytic‑uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS) triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia—a trigger we rarely think of, and a condition that masquerades as “just” sepsis‑related DIC.

The striking teaching point? **Her routine C3/C4 levels were perfectly normal, yet she had a life‑threatening complement‑driven TMA.** This forced us to look beyond the basic complement panel, encounter the pitfalls of plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab on laboratory assays, and finally send an extended genetic panel that, in this case, came back negative.  

If you’ve ever felt the frustration of a “normal” C3/C4 result in a patient who looks textbook for atypical HUS, you’ll find the practical pearls below useful.

---

## The “Basic” Complement Tests: What They Tell You (and What They Don’t)  

| Test | What It Measures | Typical Interpretation in CM‑HUS |
|------|------------------|----------------------------------|
| **C3** | Total complement component 3 (classical & alternative pathways) | Often low in active disease, but can be normal (up to 40 % of cases). |
| **C4** | Complement component 4 (classical pathway) | Usually normal in alternative‑pathway driven disease. |
| **CH50** | Total hemolytic activity (classical pathway) | May be normal; not sensitive for alternative‑pathway activation. |
| **AH50** | Alternative pathway hemolytic activity (functional assay) | **Key test**: low AH50 suggests consumption of the alternative pathway, even when C3/C4 are normal. |

**Take‑home:**  
- A normal C3/C4 **does not exclude** CM‑HUS.  
- AH50 (or its newer automated equivalents) catches alternative‑pathway over‑activation that C3/C4 miss.

In our patient, the first C3/C4 were within reference ranges, leading the team to doubt a complement‑mediated process. Only after the hemolysis re‑emerged did an extended panel uncover a markedly reduced AH50 (6 % of control). That was the first biochemical clue that the alternative pathway was being “sucked dry” by uncontrolled activation.

---

## The Full Complement Panel: When to Order It  

1. **Clinical suspicion of TMA** (MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI) **and** ADAMTS13 >10 % (i.e., TTP unlikely).  
2. **Negative triggers for STEC‑HUS** (stool PCR, recent diarrheal illness).  
3. **Atypical presentation**: sepsis, pregnancy, drug exposure, or an unusual infectious trigger (e.g., *C. canimorsus*).  

**Order in tandem:**  
- C3, C4, CH50, AH50  
- Factors H, I, B levels (quantitative)  
- Anti‑Factor H antibodies (ELISA)  
- Soluble C5b‑9 (optional, useful for monitoring eculizumab efficacy).  

In most university labs, the AH50 and factor levels are “reflex” tests: if C3/C4 are low, they’ll automatically run the functional assays. In cases like ours, you have to **specifically request** the AH50—otherwise you’ll miss it.

---

## The Effect of Plasma Exchange (PEX) on Complement Assays  

PEX does three things:  

1. **Removes circulating complement fragments** (C3a, C5a, sC5b‑9).  
2. **Replaces deficient regulatory proteins** (e.g., Factor H) in the plasma.  
3. **Dilutes any auto‑antibodies** (anti‑Factor H).  

**Result:**  
- Immediately after a PEX session, AH50 may **spuriously normalize** because you’ve “refilled” the plasma with fresh donor complement.  
- Factor levels (H, I, B) can transiently rise, masking an underlying deficiency.  

In our patient, the first AH50 was drawn **before** the first PEX, showing the true low activity (6 %). We deliberately timed the sample to avoid the “wash‑out” effect. If you let the lab draw after a PEX, you might be reassured falsely and postpone needed eculizumab.

**Practical tip:**  
- **Draw baseline complement studies before the first PEX**.  
- If you need repeat testing, wait at least **24–48 h** after the last exchange.

---

## The Influence of Eculizumab on Laboratories  

Eculizumab binds C5, preventing formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC). The drug does **not** change C3/C4 concentrations, but it can:

- **Lower soluble C5b‑9** (great for monitoring response).  
- **Alter functional assays** that rely on downstream MAC formation (e.g., CH50).  
- **Mask “terminal” complement activity**, making some assays appear normal even when upstream activation persists.

In our case, the patient received her first eculizumab dose **after** the second PEX and after repeat AH50. The subsequent AH50 remained low, reinforcing that the abnormality lay upstream (alternative pathway) and was not corrected by a single dose of eculizumab.

**Bottom line:**  
- **Do not use a single post‑eculizumab AH50 to rule out ongoing activation.** Repeat after the drug has reached steady‑state (usually after 4–5 weekly doses) if you need to confirm adequate blockade.

---

## Genetic Work‑up: When to Send It and How to Use the Results  

### When to Order an Extended Panel  

- **All patients with confirmed or strongly suspected CM‑HUS**, regardless of C3/C4 results.  
- **Age < 60** with a first episode of TMA.  
- **Family history** of TMA, renal disease, or complement disorders.  
- **Relapsing disease** after eculizumab discontinuation.  

### What to Request  

1. **Gene panel for complement regulators**: CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP (CD46), THBD, DGKE, PLG, VTN, and others.  
2. **Copy‑number analysis** (large deletions/duplications).  
3. **Screen for anti‑Factor H autoantibodies** (if not already done).  

### Interpreting the Results  

| Result | Clinical Implication |
|--------|----------------------|
| **Pathogenic variant found** (e.g., CFH loss‑of‑function) | High relapse risk; lifelong eculizumab (or ravulizumab) usually recommended. |
| **Variants of uncertain significance (VUS)** | Discuss with a genetics counselor; may influence decision for prolonged therapy, especially if functional data supports pathogenicity. |
| **No pathogenic variants** | Supports a “pure trigger” scenario (infection‑only). Shorter eculizumab courses may be considered, but decisions should be based on clinical response and renal recovery. |

In our patient, extended testing **did not reveal any pathogenic variants**, placing her in the “infection‑triggered only” group. We continued eculizumab for several months, planning a cautious taper once renal function stabilised and no further hemolysis occurred.

**Key Pearls:**  

- A **negative genetic panel** does **not** rule out future relapses; infection or pregnancy can still act as “second hits.”  
- **Genetic counseling** is essential: patients often have questions about family screening and reproductive implications.  

---

## Putting It All Together: A Practical Workflow  

1. **Suspect TMA** → Order CBC, smear, LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine, coagulation profile.  
2. **Rule out TTP** quickly (PLASMIC score; ADAMTS13). If ADAMTS13 > 10 %, move on.  
3. **Send comprehensive complement work‑up** *before* the first PEX: C3, C4, CH50, AH50, Factors H/I/B, anti‑Factor H antibodies.  
4. **Start empiric PEX** if TTP cannot be excluded (low risk of delay).  
5. **If ADAMTS13 normal and AH50 low**, think CM‑HUS → consider early eculizumab (after blood cultures clear, appropriate vaccination, and prophylactic antibiotics).  
6. **Obtain kidney biopsy** when safe (helps confirm TMA, rules out other glomerulopathies).  
7. **Send extended genetic panel** (ideally before initiating eculizumab, but not mandatory).  
8. **Monitor response**:  
   - Hemoglobin, platelets, LDH, creatinine daily.  
   - AH50 and sC5b‑9 weekly (after eculizumab steady‑state).  
9. **Plan long‑term therapy** based on genetics, renal recovery, and clinical course.  

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Normal C3/C4 ≠ no complement disease.** AH50 is the functional assay that catches alternative‑pathway over‑activation.  
- **Timing matters:** Draw baseline complement studies *before* plasma exchange; beware of post‑PEX “normalization.”  
- **Eculizumab changes downstream assays** but not upstream AH50; use sC5b‑9 if you need a marker of terminal blockade.  
- **Genetics guide duration** of complement blockade: pathogenic variants → lifelong therapy; no variants → consider trial discontinuation after remission, but stay vigilant for triggers.  
- **Infection can be the sole “second hit.”** Keep a broad differential for atypical triggers (dog bites, tick bites, viral illnesses) when evaluating a TMA.  

---

## Final Thought  

The interplay between infection, complement, and the endothelium is a perfect illustration of how a seemingly straightforward sepsis case can evolve into a complex complement‑mediated TMA. By expanding our laboratory toolkit beyond C3/C4 and integrating genetics early, we can move from “watch‑and‑wait” to targeted therapy—saving kidneys, lives, and a lot of downstream paperwork.  

Stay curious, keep your labs ordered in the right sequence, and don’t let a normal C3 lull you into complacency. The next *Capnocytophaga* bite could be the one that forces you to put these pearls into practice.

# Renal Biopsy in the ICU: When and How to Confirm TMA in a Dialysis‑Dependent Patient  

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s went from a routine dog bite to **dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury** (AKI) within days.  The culprit? *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis that morphed into a complement‑mediated hemolytic‑uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS). What makes the story truly educational is that the definitive diagnosis hinged on a **kidney biopsy performed while the patient was still on continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)**.  

In most ICUs, the notion of “let’s stick a needle into a bleeding, anuric patient” is greeted with alarm. Yet, when the differential narrows to a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) that could be amenable to targeted therapy (eculizumab), a biopsy may be the turning point between permanent renal loss and partial recovery.  

## The clinical crossroads: DIC, sepsis, or TMA?  

When the patient arrived, the picture was classic for **sepsis‑associated DIC**:  

* High fevers, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated D‑dimer, low fibrinogen.  
* Rapidly rising lactate and metabolic acidosis.  
* Anuria despite aggressive fluids and diuretics, prompting CRRT.  

During the first week, these findings fit a septic cascade, and the team focused on broad‑spectrum antibiotics, supportive transfusions, and organ support.  

**The “aha” moment** came about a week later on the nephrology ward.  Hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days, haptoglobin became undetectable, LDH spiked, and schistocytes (15–20 %) were evident on the smear. Platelets stayed low (30–40 × 10⁹/L).  

At that point the differential shifted:

| Condition | Typical labs | ADAMTS13 | Key clue |
|-----------|--------------|----------|----------|
| **TTP** | Severe ADAMTS13 < 10 % | Yes | Very low activity |
| **STEC‑HUS** | Positive stool PCR/enteric prodrome | Usually normal | Shiga toxin |
| **CM‑HUS** | Normal C3/C4 (can be normal) | Normal ADAMTS13 | Trigger (infection, pregnancy, drugs) + renal biopsy showing TMA |
| **Sepsis‑induced DIC** | Low fibrinogen, prolonged PT/PTT, low platelets | Normal | Coagulopathy dominates early |

ADAMTS13 came back at 43 % – **ruling out TTP**. Stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative.  The remaining plausible entity was **CM‑HUS**, but the team needed histologic confirmation before committing to an expensive, infection‑risking drug (eculizumab).  

## When to consider a renal biopsy in the ICU  

1. **Persistent AKI with unclear etiology** – especially when standard work‑up (ultrasound, labs) points to TMA but the trigger is ambiguous.  
2. **Hemolysis + thrombocytopenia after sepsis** – if the picture doesn’t resolve with sepsis management, and ADAMTS13 is not low, a biopsy can distinguish DIC‑related cortical necrosis from true TMA.  
3. **Therapeutic decision point** – eculizumab, rituximab, plasma exchange, or conservative management all hinge on confirming a complement‑mediated process.  
4. **Kidney size and vascular access are adequate** – a small, contracted kidney or severe aneurysmal disease may preclude safe percutaneous approach.  

**Timing:**  

*Ideally* after the patient is **hemodynamically stable** (no escalating vasopressor requirements) and **coagulopathy corrected** (platelets > 50 × 10⁹/L, INR < 1.5, fibrinogen > 150 mg/dL). In this case, platelet counts had risen to ~77 × 10⁹/L after a couple of plasma‑exchange sessions, allowing a safe biopsy despite ongoing CRRT.  

## Safety considerations – the “do‑not‑do‑list”  

| Issue | How we addressed it | Practical tip |
|-------|---------------------|----------------|
| **Bleeding risk** | Corrected thrombocytopenia with platelet transfusion; checked INR; gave fresh frozen plasma if fibrinogen low. | Target platelets ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L; consider prophylactic cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen <150 mg/dL. |
| **Anticoagulation for CRRT** | Temporarily held heparin (or citrate) for 4–6 h before the procedure; used minimal post‑procedure heparin. | Coordinate with ICU nursing; document exact hold time. |
| **Contrast or radiologic exposure** | Decision was percutaneous, ultrasound‑guided; avoided contrast CT. | Ultrasound guidance is the safest in coagulopathic patients. |
| **Infection risk** | Patient already on broad antibiotics; sterile technique; post‑procedure monitoring of urine output and fever. | Use a dedicated biopsy kit; limit number of needle passes (1–2). |
| **Patient positioning** | Supine with slight flank elevation; used a low‑profile biopsy gun to avoid respiratory compromise. | Ensure airway protected; consider sedation with careful respiratory monitoring. |

No major bleeding occurred; the patient’s hemoglobin dropped <1 g/dL, easily repleted.  

## What the biopsy told us  

*Light microscopy* showed:  

* **Severe acute tubular necrosis** (consistent with ischemic injury).  
* **Ischemic glomeruli** with **fibrin thrombi** within capillary loops.  
* **Scattered crescents** – a hallmark of endothelial injury.  

*Immunofluorescence* was negative for IgG/IgA/IgM, C3, C1q – arguing against immune‑complex glomerulonephritis.  

*Electron microscopy* (not shown) lacked dense deposits; there were **subendothelial swelling** and **double‑contour basement membranes**, classic for TMA.  

These findings **confirmed a TMA** and, together with the clinical context (normal ADAMTS13, negative Shiga toxin, recent *C. canimorsus* septic episode), supported a diagnosis of **complement‑mediated HUS**.  

## How the pathology changed management  

* **Plasma exchange** – continued as a bridge while awaiting definitive results; provided transient hemodynamic stability.  
* **Eculizumab initiation** – after the first dose of the complement inhibitor, the patient’s hemoglobin stabilized, platelet count rose, and diuresis returned.  
* **Vaccination & prophylaxis** – meningococcal (Menveo, Bexsero) and penicillin prophylaxis were given before eculizumab, reducing the risk of Neisseria infection.  

Without the biopsy, the team could have remained stuck in a “sepsis‑related DIC” mindset, possibly delaying eculizumab for weeks. Early blockade was a pivotal factor in preserving residual renal function (serum creatinine 175 µmol/L at discharge).  

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Don’t let “patient is on dialysis” deter you** from a renal biopsy when the diagnosis will change therapy.  
- **Correct coagulopathy first** (platelets ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L, INR < 1.5, fibrinogen ≥ 150 mg/dL) and hold CRRT anticoagulation briefly.  
- **Timing is everything** – aim for a window after sepsis control but before irreversible scarring; a week into the ICU stay is often realistic.  
- **Histology matters**: fibrin thrombi and endothelial swelling on light microscopy, negative immunofluorescence, and lack of immune deposits point toward TMA, while diffuse cortical necrosis suggests pure DIC.  
- **ADAMTS13 >10 % and negative Shiga toxin** push the differential toward CM‑HUS; biopsy confirms it.  
- **Early eculizumab = better renal recovery**; delay ≥7 days drops the odds of returning to dialysis independence dramatically.  

## Bottom line  

Renal biopsy in the ICU is not a “last resort” for the moribund; it is a **high‑impact diagnostic tool** when you’re balancing between sepsis‑related DIC and a treatable complement‑mediated TMA. With careful correction of coagulopathy, coordinated timing, and ultrasound guidance, you can safely obtain tissue that may be the key to stopping a patient on the road to chronic dialysis.  

In the era of targeted complement inhibitors, **histologic confirmation of TMA is no longer optional**—it’s often the decisive step that turns a bleak prognosis into a realistic chance for renal recovery.



# From Bite to Dialysis: A Clinical Timeline Toolkit for Rapid Diagnosis of Infection‑Triggered TMAs  

*When a dog bite spirals into a complement‑mediated haemolytic‑uremic syndrome, every hour counts. Below is a step‑by‑step, bedside‑friendly timeline that pulls out the “when‑to‑think‑TMA” moments and the tests/therapies that should be triggered. The case of a 48‑year‑old woman with Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis illustrates each checkpoint.*

---  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A seemingly straightforward dog bite turned into fulminant sepsis, DIC, and then a full‑blown thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The twist? The TMA was **complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)**, not the more common TTP or STEC‑HUS. Because the haemolysis only emerged after the initial septic storm was calming, the diagnosis could easily be missed, delaying eculizumab—​the only therapy that truly alters renal outcome in CM‑HUS.  

The take‑home message is simple: **when a patient with an infection develops new‑onset anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and worsening AKI, think TMA early, order the right labs in a set order, and start plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridge while you line up complement blockade.**  

---  

## The “Bite‑to‑Dialysis” Timeline Toolkit

### How to Use This at the Bedside  

1. **Print or pin the table** on the ICU/ED whiteboard.  
2. As soon as a patient meets the “new‑onset anaemia + thrombocytopenia + AKI” triad, **activate the TMA pathway** (order ADAMTS13, Shiga‑toxin PCR, complement work‑up *and* start PEX).  
3. **Don’t wait for ADAMTS13** to be <10 % before starting PEX—early exchange buys time and may blunt the complement surge.  
4. Once infection is cleared and cultures are negative, **move quickly to eculizumab**; the earlier the drug, the better the renal salvage (data show every day of delay adds ~5 % risk of ESRD).  
5. **Vaccinate** *before* the first dose. If the patient is already septic, give the vaccine and start prophylactic antibiotics; the vaccine will take 2 weeks to mount protective titres, but you’ve bought time with antibiotics.  

---  

## Clinical Pearls from the Case  

* **Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a *slow* grower**; cultures can take a week. When the bite history is clear, start empiric coverage early (penicillin‑β‑lactamase inhibitor or ampicillin‑sulbactam).  
* **DIC can mask TMA** – both cause consumptive coagulopathy and schistocytes may be missed early. Re‑check the peripheral smear once the DIC trend improves.  
* **Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM‑HUS**; the functional AH50 assay is more sensitive for alternative pathway activation, but it can be suppressed after PEX or eculizumab.  
* **ADAMTS13 >10 % essentially rules out classic TTP**, but remember that “borderline” activity (10‑30 %) still warrants PEX until the diagnosis is certain.  
* **Positive DAT can be a red herring** in the setting of recent penicillin exposure or transfusion; repeat testing after steroids to confirm.  
* **Kidney biopsy is optional but educational** – classic findings are glomerular capillary fibrin thrombi, endothelial swelling, and ischaemic crescents, as seen in the presented case.  

---  

## Take‑Home Points (Bullet‑Style for Quick Recall)  

- **Any severe infection + new MAHA + thrombocytopenia = TMA until proven otherwise.**  
- **Order ADAMTS13, Shiga‑toxin PCR, and complement panel **simultaneously**; start PEX right away.**  
- **Eculizumab is the definitive therapy for CM‑HUS; start as soon as cultures are negative and vaccination/antibiotic prophylaxis are in place.**  
- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out complement dysregulation – consider functional assays or empirical therapy when clinical picture fits.**  
- **Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a rare but recognized trigger of CM‑HUS; always ask about animal bites when faced with sepsis of unknown origin.**  

---  

## Final Thought  

In the chaotic early hours of a septic shock, the temptation is to focus solely on source control and hemodynamics. Yet, the **“second hit”**—a complement‑driven microangiopathy—can hide in the shadows of DIC and be the difference between a full renal recovery and lifelong dialysis. By anchoring our assessment to a clear timeline, ordering the right panels early, and bridging with plasma exchange until eculizumab can be safely given, we turn a potentially fatal cascade into a survivable, even recoverable, episode.  

*Keep this timeline on your wall, and the next dog‑bite‑induced HUS may be caught before the kidneys say goodbye.*
