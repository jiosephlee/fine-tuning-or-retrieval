Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Initial Presentation, Exposure History, and Early Sepsis Management  

The patient’s course began with a seemingly innocuous dog bite that rapidly progressed to a fulminant septic syndrome. A meticulous reconstruction of the timeline, a focused physical examination, and an early laboratory snapshot were essential for recognizing severe sepsis complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and for initiating life‑saving supportive measures.

### Timeline of Events from Dog Bite to Hospital Admission  
Four days before presentation the patient was bitten on the upper lip by her pet dog. She received a tetanus booster in the emergency department and a prescription for phenoxymethyl‑penicillin (Apocillin) was written, but the medication was never collected. Over the ensuing 96 h she developed a high‑grade fever (> 40 °C) and generalized malaise, followed by reduced oral intake. By the third day numbness and discoloration of the feet appeared, prompting a primary‑care visit where profound hypoglycaemia (0.6 mmol/L) resistant to oral glucose and glucagon was documented. Because of the refractory hypoglycaemia and progressive systemic symptoms, she was urgently transferred to the tertiary emergency department.  

Thus, within a week of the bite the patient progressed from a minor skin injury to septic shock with multiorgan dysfunction, underscoring how quickly Capnocytophaga canimorsus can overwhelm host defenses, especially when appropriate antimicrobial therapy is delayed.

### Physical Findings on Admission  
On arrival the patient was alert and oriented but clearly septic. Key bedside observations included:  

- **Fever**: core temperature > 40 °C.  
- **Mottled, cool lower extremities** with peripheral cyanosis, consistent with poor perfusion and early microvascular thrombosis.  
- **Neurologic status**: no altered mental status, no focal deficits; she remained able to follow commands, indicating adequate cerebral perfusion despite systemic shock.  
- **Hypoglycaemia**: persistent despite high‑dose intravenous dextrose infusions, suggesting ongoing metabolic consumption and impaired gluconeogenesis in sepsis.  

No evidence of a surgical source such as necrotising fasciitis was found on physical exam or bedside ultrasound, allowing the team to focus on a systemic infectious process rather than a localized surgical emergency.

### Initial Laboratory Panel: The Signature of Severe Sepsis and DIC  

| Parameter | Result (day 0) | Interpretation |
|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| **CBC** | WBC 1.2 × 10⁹/L (leukopenia), Hb 112 g/L, Platelets 38 × 10⁹/L | Pancytopenia points to bone‑marrow suppression or consumptive coagulopathy |
| **Coagulation** | PT 18 s (↑), INR 1.6, aPTT 48 s (↑), fibrinogen 0.8 g/L, D‑dimer markedly elevated | Consumptive coagulopathy fitting DIC |
| **Metabolic panel** | Creatinine 210 µmol/L (↑), ALT/AST 150/135 U/L (↑), bilirubin 28 µmol/L (↑) | Acute kidney injury (AKI) and hepatic dysfunction |
| **Acid‑base** | pH 7.27, lactate 5.7 mmol/L, base excess ‑8 mmol/L | Metabolic acidosis with tissue hypoxia |
| **Glucose** | 0.6 mmol/L despite IV glucose | Severe sepsis‑related hypoglycaemia |
| **Serum haptoglobin** | Normal (early phase) | Hemolysis not yet apparent |

The combination of profound leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, low fibrinogen, and elevated D‑dimer fulfills the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) criteria for overt DIC. Simultaneously, the rising lactate, metabolic acidosis, and rising creatinine establish the presence of septic shock with end‑organ hypoperfusion.

### Rapid Assessment Tools: qSOFA and DIC Scoring  

A quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) was calculated at the bedside:  

- **Respiratory rate** > 22/min (2 points)  
- **Altered mentation** – absent (0 points)  
- **Systolic blood pressure** < 100 mmHg – absent (0 points)  

Resulting qSOFA = 2, indicating a high risk of poor outcomes and justifying immediate escalation to intensive care.  

The ISTH DIC score incorporated platelet count (≤ 50 × 10⁹/L = 2 points), elevated PT (≥ 3 s = 1 point), fibrinogen < 1.0 g/L (1 point), and markedly raised D‑dimer (2 points), giving a total of 6 > 5, which confirms overt DIC. Prompt recognition of these scores directed both antimicrobial and supportive therapy.

### Empiric Antimicrobial Coverage for Animal‑Related Pathogens  

Given the history of a dog bite, the clinician’s differential included Capnocytophaga, Pasteurella, anaerobic streptococci, and polymicrobial oral flora. Empiric broad‑spectrum coverage was therefore initiated with **piperacillin‑tazobactam** (4.5 g IV every 6 h), a regimen that provides reliable activity against Capnocytophaga spp., gram‑negative bacilli, and most anaerobes. The choice also considered the patient’s septic shock state, where early appropriate antibiotics are the most influential modifiable factor for survival.  

Blood cultures drawn before antibiotics grew C. canimorsus after 7 days, confirming the initial suspicion and allowing de‑escalation to **ampicillin** (2 g IV q6h) once susceptibility was established.

### Supportive Care Measures  

1. **Fluid Resuscitation** – A rapid infusion of 30 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid was administered within the first hour, followed by goal‑directed fluid management using dynamic indices (e.g., passive leg raise, stroke volume variation) to avoid volume overload in the setting of evolving renal failure.  

2. **Glucose Management** – Persistent hypoglycaemia required continuous high‑dose dextrose infusion (10 % dextrose at 200 mL/h) with frequent bedside glucose checks. Once sepsis control improved, glucose levels stabilized without the need for glucagon or additional boluses.  

3. **Blood Product Transfusion** – To counteract consumptive coagulopathy, the patient received fresh frozen plasma (15 mL/kg) to replenish clotting factors, cryoprecipitate (10 U) for fibrinogen, and platelet concentrates (1 apheresis unit) to maintain platelet counts > 50 × 10⁹/L, thereby reducing bleeding risk.  

4. **Renal Replacement Therapy** – Anuria persisted despite aggressive fluid and diuretic therapy. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated on ICU day 2 to manage fluid balance, clear lactate, and support renal function while awaiting recovery.  

5. **ICU Admission** – The combination of septic shock, DIC, and acute kidney injury mandated early transfer to a level‑III ICU. Here, invasive hemodynamic monitoring, ongoing organ support, and multidisciplinary input (intensivists, infectious disease, haematology, nephrology) were coordinated.  

### Key Learning Points  

- **Exposure history** (dog bite) can provide a decisive clue to atypical pathogens such as C. canimorsus; failure to initiate appropriate antibiotics promptly can allow rapid disease progression.  
- **Early bedside scores** (qSOFA, ISTH DIC) translate raw vital signs and laboratory data into actionable decisions, prompting rapid escalation of care.  
- **Empiric antimicrobial selection** must cover both aerobic and anaerobic flora associated with animal bites; de‑escalation should be guided by culture results and susceptibility patterns.  
- **Supportive care**—aggressive fluid resuscitation, meticulous glucose control, and timely blood product administration—remains the backbone of sepsis management while definitive pathogen‑directed therapy and diagnostic work‑up proceed.  

By integrating a concise exposure timeline, targeted physical findings, and a systematic early‑phase laboratory assessment, the clinical team promptly identified severe sepsis with DIC, laid the groundwork for life‑saving supportive therapy, and positioned the patient for subsequent evaluation of the evolving thrombotic microangiopathy.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Work‑up During the Septic Phase

When a previously healthy adult presents in extremis with sepsis, multi‑organ failure, pancytopenia and overt coagulopathy, the clinician must entertain a wide spectrum of potential diagnoses. The urgency of the situation often compels the initiation of broad‑spectrum antimicrobial therapy and supportive measures, yet simultaneous systematic investigation is essential to avoid missing treatable entities that may masquerade as sepsis‑related organ dysfunction. In the present case, the early diagnostic effort focused on distinguishing primary sepsis‑induced disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) from other life‑threatening processes that can produce a similar laboratory phenotype, such as acute leukaemia, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) or toxin‑mediated syndromes. A stepwise approach—integrating clinical clues, targeted haematology studies, microbiologic work‑up, autoimmune screening, and renal assessment—allowed the team to narrow the differential before the later emergence of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

### Broad Differential for Pancytopenia and DIC

Severe pancytopenia in the context of systemic inflammation raises several serious possibilities:

1. **Acute leukemic infiltration** – particularly acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), which can present with fever, coagulopathy and marrow failure. AML is characteristically associated with circulating blasts, a leuco‑erythroblastic picture on smear and often a high white‑cell count that may be misleadingly low if blasts are being consumed or sequestered.

2. **Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)** – a hyper‑inflammatory syndrome driven by uncontrolled macrophage activation. Diagnostic criteria include fever, cytopenias affecting ≥2 lineages, hyperferritinemia, hypertriglyceridaemia, and evidence of haemophagocytosis on bone‑marrow biopsy. HLH may be secondary to severe infection (including bacterial, viral or fungal triggers) and can produce a consumptive coagulopathy resembling DIC.

3. **Severe bacterial sepsis with DIC** – the most common explanation, wherein endotoxin or exotoxin release drives massive tissue factor expression, leading to thrombin generation, consumption of platelets and coagulation factors, and secondary organ ischemia.

4. **Toxin‑mediated syndromes** – for example, staphylococcal toxic shock syndrome or streptococcal necrotising fasciitis can precipitate rapid multiorgan collapse with DIC‑like laboratory findings.

Given the patient’s recent dog bite, rapid febrile illness, and lack of a prior haematologic history, the team prioritized sepsis‑related DIC while actively excluding the other entities.

### Targeted Haematologic Work‑up

The initial laboratory panel revealed pancytopenia (neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, anaemia) without overt blasts. To dissect the cause, the following studies were performed:

- **Peripheral blood smear** – examined for abnormal cells (blasts, dysplastic neutrophils), schistocytes, or intracellular organisms. In this patient, the smear showed no blasts, but the presence of mature‑looking leukocytes and normal red‑cell morphology supported a non‑leukaemic process.

- **Flow cytometry** – provided highly sensitive immunophenotypic analysis of peripheral blood to detect minimal residual leukaemia or clonal lymphoid populations. The absence of increased CD34+ blasts or aberrant phenotypes effectively ruled out an acute leukaemia at that time.

- **Consideration of bone‑marrow biopsy** – while a definitive marrow evaluation could identify HLH (through haemophagocytosis) or early leukaemia, the lack of supporting peripheral findings and the patient’s rapid clinical decline made an invasive procedure less urgent. The initial decision to defer biopsy was revisited only if cytopenias persisted despite resolution of sepsis.

- **Ferritin and triglyceride levels** – non‑specific but valuable for HLH screening. In this case, ferritin was markedly elevated (consistent with inflammation), yet triglycerides were not markedly raised, making HLH less likely.

Collectively, the haematologic data pointed away from primary marrow pathology and toward a consumptive process secondary to infection.

### Infectious Work‑up

Prompt identification of the causative pathogen was vital for both antimicrobial stewardship and understanding any potential role in triggering secondary complications.

- **Blood cultures** – Two sets of aerobic and anaerobic cultures were drawn on admission. After a prolonged incubation period appropriate for fastidious organisms, growth of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* was confirmed on day 7. The organism’s characteristic slow growth and gram‑negative, fusiform morphology were noted, underscoring the importance of maintaining cultures for at least five days when a zoonotic infection is suspected.

- **Molecular diagnostics** – Real‑time nanopore sequencing of the positive blood culture broth rapidly identified *C. canimorsus* and excluded other possible pathogens, facilitating targeted de‑escalation from piperacillin/tazobactam to ampicillin and later oral phenoxymethylpenicillin.

- **Imaging to exclude necrotising fasciitis** – Because the bite site was on the upper lip, contrast‑enhanced CT of the face and neck was performed. No fascial plane gas, deep fluid collections, or soft‑tissue edema suggestive of necrotising infection were observed, allowing the team to rule out an additional surgical source of sepsis and DIC.

- **Screening for other bacterial or viral agents** – Urine cultures, respiratory PCR panels, and viral serologies (including hepatitis, HIV, EBV, CMV) were negative, reinforcing *C. canimorsus* as the sole pathogen.

These findings solidified the diagnosis of severe *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia, a known trigger of fulminant sepsis and DIC, and set the stage for evaluating any secondary complications.

### Autoimmune Screening and Exclusion of Vasculitis

Primary systemic vasculitides can present with multiorgan dysfunction, renal impairment, and thrombocytopenia. To exclude these, the following serologic tests were ordered early in the admission:

- **Antinuclear antibody (ANA) panel** – negative, making systemic lupus erythematosus unlikely.
- **Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)** – both MPO‑ANCA and PR3‑ANCA were negative, reducing the probability of small‑vessel vasculitides such as microscopic polyangiitis or granulomatosis with polyangiitis.

Given the acute infection, the negative autoimmune work‑up supported a working diagnosis of infection‑driven DIC rather than primary immune‑mediated vasculitis.

### Renal Assessment During the Septic Phase

Acute kidney injury (AKI) was evident from the outset (rising serum creatinine, reduced urine output). A systematic renal work‑up proceeded as follows:

- **Urinalysis** – revealed protein 2+ and blood 3+, consistent with an intrinsic renal process rather than pre‑renal azotaemia alone.
- **Renal ultrasound** – performed to evaluate for obstructive causes; kidneys were of normal size, with no hydronephrosis, and demonstrated increased cortical echogenicity suggestive of acute tubular injury.
- **Fluid balance and diuretic response** – despite aggressive fluid resuscitation and loop diuretic trials, the patient remained anuric, indicating severe tubular dysfunction and prompting early initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) to manage volume overload, metabolic acidosis, and hyperkalaemia.

These findings corroborated a sepsis‑related AKI, likely multifactorial (hypoperfusion, cytokine‑mediated injury, and possible early microvascular thrombosis).

### Interpreting Early Thrombotic Phenomena: DIC vs. Evolving Microangiopathy

During the first week, the patient manifested:

- **Mottled, cool extremities** with ecchymoses,
- **Elevated D‑dimer** and prolonged PT/aPTT,
- **Thrombocytopenia** and low fibrinogen.

These features are classic for DIC secondary to overwhelming sepsis. Nevertheless, the team remained vigilant for an evolving TMA, recognizing that DIC and TMA may coexist or that DIC can mask early microangiopathic changes. Specific points considered:

- **Schistocyte assessment** – Initial peripheral smear did not reveal schistocytes, supporting DIC rather than TMA at that stage.
- **Haptoglobin and LDH** – Normal haptoglobin and modest LDH rise were more consistent with consumptive coagulopathy rather than intravascular haemolysis.
- **Temporal evolution** – The patient’s coagulopathy gradually improved with supportive therapy, yet persistent thrombocytopenia and the later abrupt fall in haemoglobin coupled with new schistocytes signaled a transition from DIC to a distinct TMA, later identified as complement‑mediated HUS.

By maintaining a high index of suspicion and repeating targeted investigations as the clinical picture evolved, the team could differentiate the initial sepsis‑induced DIC from the superimposed microangiopathic process that ultimately required plasma exchange and complement inhibition.

---

In summary, the diagnostic pathway during the septic phase combined a broad differential for pancytopenia and coagulopathy with focused haematologic, microbiologic, autoimmune, and renal investigations. Systematic exclusion of acute leukaemia, HLH, primary vasculitis and other toxin‑mediated syndromes allowed the clinicians to anchor the early presentation on severe *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis with DIC, while staying alert to the possibility of an evolving TMA—a vigilance that proved essential when the haemolytic picture later emerged.

## Transition to Thrombotic Microangiopathy: Diagnostic Reasoning and Confirmation  

The clinical course of the patient initially fitted a textbook picture of severe sepsis complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC): fever, profound leukopenia, coagulopathy, metabolic acidosis and early acute kidney injury (AKI). During the first week of hospitalization the laboratory picture largely reflected consumptive coagulopathy—elevated D‑dimer, low fibrinogen, prolonged PT/aPTT—and the thrombocytopenia improved in parallel with the resolution of the DIC parameters after broad‑spectrum antibiotics and supportive care. However, on day 10 a new, abrupt haematologic pattern emerged that could not be explained by recovering DIC alone. Recognising the shift from a sepsis‑driven coagulopathy to a primary thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) required careful integration of bedside observations, serial laboratory data, and targeted investigations.

### 1. Clinical clue: new‑onset hemolysis  

Between days 8 and 14 the patient’s hemoglobin fell from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days, despite the absence of overt bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Laboratory evidence of intravascular hemolysis appeared concurrently:

- **Undetectable haptoglobin** – indicating rapid binding of free hemoglobin.
- **Marked LDH elevation** – a surrogate for red‑cell destruction.
- **Rising indirect bilirubin** (not detailed in the report but typically present).
- **Peripheral smear** – revealed 15–20 % fragmented red cells (schistocytes), the morphological hallmark of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA).

These findings signaled that red cells were being mechanically sheared as they traversed a damaged microvascular bed, a process not typical of DIC once the consumptive phase had begun to resolve.

### 2. Persistent thrombocytopenia after DIC resolution  

Platelet counts, which had begun to recover (from <30 × 10⁹/L to ≈70 × 10⁹/L) as fibrinogen and PT normalized, plateaued at 30–40 × 10⁹/L and remained low despite plasma and fibrinogen replacement. The dissociation between improving coagulation tests and ongoing thrombocytopenia pointed toward a platelet‑consumption mechanism independent of systemic coagulation—classic for TMA.

### 3. Structured work‑up for the spectrum of TMAs  

The presence of the triad—MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and renal injury—necessitated a systematic evaluation to distinguish among the overlapping TMA entities (TTP, Shiga‑toxin HUS, complement‑mediated HUS, secondary TMAs).

#### a. Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP)  

The first priority was to exclude TTP because it mandates emergent plasma exchange (PEX) and carries a high mortality if untreated. The bedside **PLASMIC score** (Platelets, Hemolysis, Absence of cancer, Absence of solid‑organ or stem‑cell transplant, MCV < 90 fL, INR < 1.5, Creatinine < 2 mg/dL) was calculated:

- Platelets < 30 × 10⁹/L → 1 point  
- Evidence of hemolysis (LDH, schistocytes) → 1 point  
- No active malignancy → 1 point  
- No transplant history → 1 point  
- MCV ≈ 90 fL (assumed) → 0 points  
- INR ≈ 1.2 → 1 point  
- Creatinine ≈ 2 mg/dL (borderline) → 0 points  

Total = 4/7, indicating a low‑intermediate pre‑test probability for TTP. Definitive exclusion came from the **ADAMTS13 activity** result of **43.5 %** (reference > 67 %). Levels below 10 % are diagnostic for TTP; therefore, severe ADAMTS13 deficiency was absent. Nonetheless, because the result would not be instantly available, **PEX was initiated empirically** as a bridge while awaiting the assay, consistent with contemporary guidelines that favour early exchange when TTP cannot be ruled out clinically.

#### b. Shiga‑toxin–associated HUS (STEC‑HUS)  

STEC‑HUS typically follows an infectious diarrheal prodrome. The patient denied recent gastrointestinal illness, and stool PCR for Shiga toxin genes was **negative**, effectively excluding this entity.

#### c. Complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)  

Having ruled out TTP and STEC‑HUS, attention turned to a complement‑driven process. Baseline **C3 and C4** concentrations were within reference limits, a finding that can be misleading because complement consumption may be intermittent or masked by acute‑phase reactants. Accordingly, **functional complement assays** were obtained:

- **Alternative pathway hemolytic activity (AH50)** – markedly reduced to 6 % (normal ≈ 50–100 %). This reduction reflects either consumption or, more likely in this case, the effect of recent PEX and the first dose of eculizumab, which remove or inhibit complement components.
- **Classical and lectin pathway activities** – remained normal, supporting a selective alternative‑pathway perturbation.
- **Complement regulatory proteins** (factors H, I, B) – quantitatively normal, and **anti‑factor H antibodies** were absent.

The pattern of a low AH50 in the setting of normal C3/C4, combined with the clinical picture, reinforced the diagnosis of a complement‑mediated TMA rather than a transient consumption phenomenon.

### 4. Histopathologic confirmation via kidney biopsy  

Because the patient remained dialysis‑dependent and the etiologic classification of the TMA would influence long‑term therapy, a **renal biopsy** was performed once platelet counts rose above 70 × 10⁹/L (to minimize bleeding risk). Light microscopy disclosed three key features:

1. **Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** – reflecting ischemic injury secondary to microvascular obstruction.
2. **Fibrin‑rich thrombi within capillary loops and arterioles** – the morphologic signature of TMA.
3. **Ischemic glomeruli with focal crescent formation** – indicating severe endothelial injury and activation of the coagulation cascade.

Immunofluorescence was negative for immune‑complex deposition, and electron microscopy showed no primary glomerulopathy, further narrowing the interpretation to a **pure microangiopathic process**. Together, the biopsy provided a tissue‑level verification that the kidney injury arose from thrombotic microangiopathy rather than from sepsis‑related acute tubular necrosis alone.

### 5. Synthesis and final diagnosis  

The diagnostic algorithm converged on the following points:

- **New MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia** after DIC resolution gave the clinical impression of a primary TMA.
- **ADAMTS13 activity** was intermediate, effectively excluding TTP.
- **Stool PCR** ruled out STEC‑HUS.
- **Complement functional testing** demonstrated alternative‑pathway dysregulation, compatible with complement‑mediated disease.
- **Renal histology** confirmed TMA in the kidney.

With the exclusion of TTP and STEC‑HUS, and with supportive complement data and biopsy findings, the case met the criteria for **complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS)**, historically termed atypical HUS. The temporal relationship to a **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** infection suggested the bacterium acted as the “second hit” that precipitated uncontrolled complement activation in an otherwise immunocompetent host.

Thus, the transition from a sepsis‑driven DIC picture to a true TMA was recognized through a combination of **clinical vigilance for new hemolysis, systematic laboratory exclusion of TTP and STEC‑HUS, targeted complement assessment, and definitive renal biopsy**. This integrated approach enabled timely initiation of complement blockade with eculizumab, ultimately leading to hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

## Pathophysiology of Complement‑Mediated HUS and the Role of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*

Complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS), formerly termed atypical HUS, is a disorder of uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway that culminates in a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) of the renal microvasculature. The case described illustrates how a seemingly routine dog bite can provide the “second hit” that unleashes this pathogenic cascade even in a previously healthy adult.

### Alternative‑Pathway Activation and Its Regulation  

The alternative pathway is constantly tick‑over: spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 produces C3(H₂O), which associates with factor B. Factor D then cleaves factor B, generating the fluid‑phase C3 convertase C3(H₂O)Bb that cleaves additional C3 to C3a and C3b. Surface‑bound C3b can bind factor B, creating the potent C3 convertase C3bBb that amplifies C3 deposition on host or microbial surfaces. This amplification loop is tightly restrained by three soluble regulators—factor H, factor I, and properdin (which stabilises the convertase)—and by membrane‑bound regulators such as CD46 (membrane cofactor protein), CD55 (decay‑accelerating factor), and CD59 (preventing membrane‑attack‑complex formation). Factor H competes with factor B for C3b and accelerates the decay of C3bBb; factor I cleaves C3b (and C4b) after binding of a co‑factor (factor H or CD46), generating inactive iC3b and preventing further complement propagation. When any of these regulators are deficient or dysfunctional, the alternative pathway can spiral out of control, depositing excessive C3b on endothelial cells and generating the terminal complement complex (C5b‑9). C5b‑9 inserts into the endothelium, inducing cell activation, a pro‑thrombotic phenotype, and ultimately the fibrin‑rich microthrombi that hallmark TMA.

### Genetic Predisposition versus Acquired Triggers  

About half to two‑thirds of patients with CM‑HUS harbour germline mutations or auto‑antibodies that impair the above regulators. Loss‑of‑function variants in *CFH* (factor H), *CFI* (factor I), *CFB* (factor B), or *MCP* (CD46) are the most common, while antibodies against factor H are seen in a minority of cases. Yet penetrance is incomplete: many carriers remain asymptomatic until an environmental “second hit” triggers overt disease. The second hit may be infection, pregnancy, certain drugs, or malignancy. In the present patient, no pathogenic complement‑gene variant was identified, suggesting that the infection itself was sufficient to tip the balance toward uncontrolled complement activation in a host whose regulatory capacity was within normal limits but nonetheless vulnerable to a potent stimulus.

### *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* and Complement Interactions  

*C. canimorsus* is a fastidious, Gram‑negative, encapsulated rod that colonises the oral cavity of dogs and, less frequently, cats. The bacterial capsule confers variable resistance to serum killing: some strains are readily lysed by normal human serum, whereas others—particularly those expressing a thick polysaccharide capsule—survive complement attack. Laboratory studies have shown that serum susceptibility correlates with the ability of the organism to activate the alternative pathway; capsule‑rich isolates can bind C3b and evade opsonophagocytosis, leading to prolonged complement activation. In addition, lipooligosaccharide structures on the bacterial outer membrane can mimic endotoxin‑like patterns that directly trigger the alternative pathway, bypassing the classical route. Consequently, a vigorous complement response is mounted against the pathogen, which in most individuals clears the infection without collateral damage.

### Paradox: Complement Activation as a Driver of Endothelial Injury  

When the alternative pathway is hyper‑stimulated, the resulting excess C3b deposition on endothelial surfaces overwhelms the protective capacity of factor H and other regulators. Endothelial cells become activated, up‑regulating tissue factor, von Willebrand factor, and adhesion molecules. The terminal complement complex (C5b‑9) further damages the endothelial membrane, causing cell swelling, detachment, and exposure of the subendothelial matrix. Platelets adhere, fibrin is generated, and a microvascular thrombus forms. The same process also leads to mechanical fragmentation of passing erythrocytes (schistocytes) and consumption of platelets, producing the classic triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury that defines TMA. Thus, a defensive immune response intended to eradicate a bacterial invader inadvertently precipitates a self‑propagating microangiopathic process.

### Reported Cases Linking *C. canimorsus* to CM‑HUS  

Only a handful of cases have documented *C. canimorsus* as a precipitant of CM‑HUS. The first described patient (Bjørkto et al.) harboured a pathogenic variant in complement factor B, suggesting a synergistic effect of a genetic predisposition and bacterial trigger. The current case differs in that extended genetic testing failed to reveal any pathogenic complement mutations, indicating that the infection alone was capable of inducing sufficient complement dysregulation to cause TMA. Both reports underscore a common clinical pattern: an initial sepsis picture with DIC‑like coagulopathy, followed days later by isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening renal dysfunction—findings that should raise suspicion for a superimposed TMA.

### Normal C3/C4 Levels Do Not Exclude CM‑HUS  

Standard clinical complement assays measuring serum C3 and C4 are frequently normal in CM‑HUS, as they reflect only the steady‑state concentrations of the proteins, not the functional activity of the alternative pathway. In the present patient, C3 and C4 remained within reference ranges throughout the illness, yet functional assays later demonstrated markedly reduced alternative‑pathway activity (AH50 ≈ 6 %). This reduction likely reflects consumption of active components after plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy rather than a true deficiency. Therefore, a normal C3/C4 panel should not dissuade clinicians from pursuing a diagnosis of complement‑mediated TMA when the clinical picture is compatible.

### Summary  

CM‑HUS is fundamentally a disease of uncontrolled alternative‑pathway complement activation. Genetic defects in complement regulators predispose individuals, but an external stimulus—such as infection with *C. canimorsus*—can act as the critical “second hit,” overwhelming regulatory mechanisms and precipitating endothelial injury, microthrombi formation, and the clinical triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI. The bacterial capsule and endotoxin‑like components provoke intense complement activation, while the host’s regulatory capacity may be insufficient to contain the response, even when conventional complement levels (C3/C4) appear normal. Recognising this pathophysiologic sequence is essential for timely diagnosis and initiation of complement blockade, which, as illustrated by the case, can reverse the haemolytic process and promote renal recovery.

## Therapeutic Strategy, Clinical Outcomes, and Lessons Learned  

The management of this patient illustrates the layered, time‑sensitive approach required when a septic illness evolves into a complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS).  The therapeutic plan combined an urgent “bridge” therapy—plasma exchange (PEX)—with definitive complement blockade using eculizumab, while continuing aggressive antimicrobial treatment, renal replacement therapy, and infection‑prophylaxis measures.  The sequence of interventions, the rationale for each, and the patient’s subsequent recovery provide a practical template for handling similar cases.

### Plasma exchange as an interim measure in suspected TMA  

When the patient’s haemoglobin fell dramatically, haptoglobin became undetectable, lactate dehydrogenase rose, and schistocytes appeared on peripheral smear, the clinical picture fulfilled the classic triad of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury.  At that juncture the differential diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was broad, encompassing thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga‑toxin‑producing Escherichia coli‑associated HUS (STEC‑HUS), and complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS).  Because ADAMTS13 activity results (43 %) were not yet available and the PLASMIC score was low, TTP could not be immediately excluded.  The standard of care for suspected TTP is urgent PEX, which removes ultra‑large von Willebrand factor multimers and circulating auto‑antibodies, while replenishing functional ADAMTS13.  

In CM‑HUS, PEX does not correct the underlying dysregulated complement activation, but it can transiently reduce circulating complement split products and inflammatory mediators, offering a temporising benefit while definitive therapy is arranged.  In this case, three daily PEX sessions with fresh frozen plasma (Octaplas) were administered.  Clinical response was modest: platelet counts rose above 70 × 10⁹/L and diuresis returned after the final exchange, suggesting that the exchanges bought time for the patient’s infection to clear and for the logistics of complement inhibition to be put in place.

### Initiation of eculizumab: dosing, vaccination, and prophylaxis  

Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that binds complement component C5, halts terminal complement activation and is the cornerstone of therapy for CM‑HUS.  The induction regimen consists of 900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks, followed by a maintenance dose of 1 200 mg every two weeks.  In this patient, the first eculizumab dose was given after the third PEX session, once blood cultures had remained sterile for 48 h and the infectious burden was judged to be controlled.

Because terminal complement blockade predisposes to infections with encapsulated organisms—most notably Neisseria meningitidis, but also Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae—vaccination is mandatory before eculizumab initiation.  The patient received the quadrivalent Menveo (serogroups A, C, Y, W) and the serogroup B‑specific Bexsero vaccines, both administered at least two weeks prior to the first eculizumab infusion.  When the timing of vaccination and drug initiation overlapped, a short course of prophylactic antibiotics (oral amoxicillin 2 g daily) was prescribed until two weeks after vaccination, in accordance with current guidelines.

### Balancing infection control with complement inhibition  

Starting a complement inhibitor during active bacteraemia raises a theoretical risk of unchecked bacterial proliferation, since C5‑derived membrane attack complex formation is a key bactericidal mechanism against Gram‑negative organisms such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus.  The team therefore delayed eculizumab until the patient’s bloodstream cultures were negative and inflammatory markers were falling, thereby minimising the chance of exacerbating the infection.  Concurrent broad‑spectrum antibiotics (initial piperacillin/tazobactam, later de‑escalated to ampicillin and finally to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin) were continued throughout the period of complement blockade to ensure eradication of the underlying pathogen.  Close monitoring for new fevers, repeat cultures, and serial inflammatory markers was performed to detect any recrudescence early.

### Renal replacement therapy weaning and partial renal recovery  

The patient required continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) during the first week of ICU care for anuric acute kidney injury (AKI) and fluid overload secondary to septic cardiomyopathy.  After PEX and the initial eculizumab dose, spontaneous diuresis resumed, allowing transition from CRRT to intermittent haemodialysis and eventually to complete discontinuation of dialysis after approximately three weeks.  Serum creatinine fell from a peak of 650 µmol/L to a stable plateau of 175 µmol/L at discharge, indicating partial but incomplete renal recovery.  This trajectory is typical for CM‑HUS; early complement inhibition improves the odds of renal salvage, yet patients who are dialysis‑dependent at the time of eculizumab initiation often retain some residual renal impairment.

### Long‑term management: maintenance eculizumab, monitoring, and genetics  

Following induction, the patient entered the maintenance phase with eculizumab 1 200 mg every two weeks.  Ongoing evaluation includes:

* **Hematologic surveillance** – weekly complete blood counts for the first month, then monthly; haptoglobin and LDH are checked at each visit to detect subclinical hemolysis.  
* **Renal function** – serum creatinine, eGFR, and urine protein/creatinine ratio every 4–6 weeks; persistent proteinuria may herald chronic kidney disease.  
* **Complement inhibition efficacy** – measurement of serum free eculizumab levels or CH50 activity can confirm adequate drug exposure, especially if breakthrough hemolysis occurs.  
* **Infection prevention** – continuation of prophylactic amoxicillin until at least two weeks after the last meningococcal vaccine booster; patients are educated about prompt medical attention for fever or flu‑like symptoms.

Extended genetic testing for complement regulatory gene variants (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, CD46) returned negative, suggesting that the infection acted as a solitary “second hit.”  Nevertheless, the decision was made to maintain eculizumab for a minimum of six months, with a reassessment at that point to consider tapering if renal function stabilises and no hematologic activity recurs.  Should a pathogenic variant be identified later, lifelong therapy would be recommended.

### Prognostic factors in CM‑HUS  

Two variables most strongly predicted the patient’s outcome:

1. **Time to complement blockade** – Initiation of eculizumab within 10 days of the first documented hemolysis was associated with a higher likelihood of renal recovery.  Delays beyond two weeks in published series correlate with progression to end‑stage renal disease.  
2. **Baseline renal function at therapy start** – Patients who are already dialysis‑dependent but retain some residual urine output tend to regain partial renal function, as seen here.  Those with complete anuria and high creatinine (>800 µmol/L) at eculizumab commencement have a poorer prognosis.

Other favorable factors in this case included the patient’s young age, lack of comorbidities, and rapid control of the underlying bacterial infection.

### Key teaching points  

* **Early suspicion of infection‑triggered TMA** – In any septic patient who develops unexplained anemia, thrombocytopenia, and worsening renal function, consider a superimposed TMA even when DIC appears present.  
* **Systematic exclusion of TTP and STEC‑HUS** – Obtain ADAMTS13 activity promptly; use the PLASMIC score to gauge pre‑test probability.  Perform stool PCR for Shiga toxin to rule out STEC‑HUS.  Negative results should shift focus toward complement‑mediated disease.  
* **Interdisciplinary coordination** – Effective care required seamless collaboration among emergency medicine, intensive care, infectious diseases, nephrology, haematology, and clinical pathology.  Each specialty contributed essential diagnostics (e.g., peripheral smear, ADAMTS13, complement panels) and therapeutic actions (PEX, antibiotics, renal support).  
* **Timing of eculizumab** – Initiate only after the acute infection is controlled, but as early as possible thereafter to maximise renal recovery.  Ensure meningococcal vaccination and appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis before the first dose.  
* **Monitoring for relapse** – Even after apparent remission, CM‑HUS can recur, especially if complement inhibition is stopped.  Ongoing laboratory surveillance and patient education are crucial.

In summary, this case demonstrates that a stepwise approach—starting with plasma exchange to stabilise the patient, followed by timely eculizumab after infection control, and supported by vigilant infection prophylaxis and renal support—can convert a fulminant, infection‑triggered CM‑HUS into a survivable illness with partial renal recovery.  Recognising the overlap between sepsis‑related DIC and true TMA, and promptly instituting complement blockade, remain the most actionable lessons for clinicians facing similar complex presentations.
