Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the presence and location of large uterine leiomyomas influence the choice of mode of delivery, and why was elective cesarean delivery favored in this patient?

**Clinical Pearl – Large uterine leiomyomas and the choice of delivery mode**

**Key principle**  
When a pregnant woman harbors one or more sizable leiomyomas, the decision between vaginal birth and elective cesarean delivery rests on three anatomic variables:

1. **Size** – Leiomyomas > 5 cm, especially > 8 cm, are more likely to impede the descent of the fetal head, restrict the lower uterine segment, or cause mal‑presentation.  
2. **Location** –  
   * **Submucosal or lower‑segment (isthmic) myomas** directly interfere with cervical dilatation and fetal head engagement.  
   * **Posterior‑uterine or fundal myomas** can distort the uterine cavity and reduce uterine contractility, raising the risk of arrest of labour or uterine rupture.  
   * **Pedunculated or cervical‑adjacent myomas** may obstruct the vaginal canal or become injured during traction.  
3. **Number** – Multiple co‑existent fibroids compound the above problems and increase the likelihood of intra‑operative hemorrhage if a myomectomy is attempted at delivery.

When any of these factors predict a mechanical obstacle to the expulsive phase, or when the uterine wall is compromised (e.g., a large intramural myoma abutting the incision line), an elective cesarean section (CS) is the safest route.

**Application to the presented case**

- **Size** – The patient had two leiomyomas: an 18 × 12 × 7 cm pedunculated mass occupying the left sub‑phrenic space and a second 9.5 cm intramural‑subserosal mass in the posterior uterine wall (later left in situ). Both exceed the 5‑cm threshold that predicts difficulty with labour.
- **Location** –  
  * The **caudal myoma** was attached to the posterior uterine wall near the isthmus (“uterine torus”). At term, this region becomes the lower uterine segment; a bulky lesion here would prevent adequate thinning and dilatation, acting as a “hard stop” for the presenting part.  
  * The **cranial pedunculated leiomyoma** had already been removed, but its former vascular pedicle and the residual scar placed the uterus at higher risk for dehiscence if subjected to the strong traction forces of vaginal delivery.
- **Obstructive potential** – The residual 7.5‑cm posterior myoma would have acted as a fixed barrier to fetal head passage, raising the chance of prolonged labour, mal‑presentation, or uterine rupture. Imaging showed it was fixed to the posterior wall and not mobile enough to be displaced during descent.

Because the mechanical impediment could not be reliably overcome by a trial of labour, the multidisciplinary team elected an **elective cesarean delivery** on the same low‑transverse (longitudinal) incision used for the myomectomy. This choice:

1. **Avoided labour‑related uterine stress** on a scarred and partially myomectomized uterus.  
2. **Reduced the risk of intra‑operative hemorrhage** that can occur when a large posterior fibroid is compressed or torn during fetal descent.  
3. **Ensured a predictable operative field**; the existing laparotomy scar provided immediate access, minimizing operative time and preserving fetal wellbeing.

**Bottom line for trainees** – In pregnant patients with large (≥ 5 cm) leiomyomas, especially when the lesions are situated in the lower uterine segment, posterior wall, or near the cervical os, plan delivery by elective CS. The goal is to prevent obstructed labour, uterine rupture, and catastrophic hemorrhage, while providing a safe environment for both mother and neonate.
