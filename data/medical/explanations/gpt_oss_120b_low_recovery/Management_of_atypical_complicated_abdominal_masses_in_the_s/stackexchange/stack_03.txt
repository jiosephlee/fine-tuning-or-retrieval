Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] List the key sonographic and MRI features that help distinguish a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma from a decidualised endometrioma in a pregnant woman.

**Pedunculated cystic leiomyoma vs decidualised endometrioma in pregnancy – key imaging discriminators**

| Modality | Feature | Pedunculated cystic leiomyoma (case) | Decidualised endometrioma (typical) |
|----------|---------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Ultrasound (gray‑scale & colour Doppler)** | **Morphology** – shape, borders, internal architecture | • Large, unilocular‑cystic‑appearing mass with *regular* smooth margins but *heterogeneous* internal echoes because of cystic degeneration. <br>• Presence of a **pedicle** (when visible) connecting the cyst to the uterine serosa (often seen as a thin, hypoechoic band). | • True endometrioma is “chocolate cyst” – homogeneous low‑level internal echoes, posterior acoustic shadowing, **no stalk**. |
| | **Vascular pattern** – Doppler flow | • Prominent peripheral vascularity and **central feeding vessels** coursing through the stalk; vessels may be hypertrophied (as described for both masses in the case). <br>• Pulsatile flow often seen at the junction with uterine wall. | • Decidualised endometrioma shows **peripheral rim‑type flow** only; absent or minimal central feeding vessels; Doppler is usually low‑grade. |
| | **Relation to uterus** | • “Continuity” with myometrial tissue on sagittal/transverse sweep; the cyst drapes over the uterine surface and may appear to **compress** rather than displace the uterine wall. In the case the lower mass was adherent to a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma; the upper mass, once examined surgically, was found to arise from a uterine pedicle. | • Ovarian or ad‑nexal location, separate from the uterine serosa; a clear *cleavage plane* between ovary and uterus. |
| | **Mobility & “sliding sign”** | • Limited mobility because the stalk anchors the lesion; on gentle probe pressure the lesion **does not glide** away from the uterus. | • Endometriomas are usually mobile relative to the uterus (positive sliding sign). |
| **MRI (non‑contrast, T1/T2 fat‑sat)** | **Wall thickness & signal** | • **Thickened wall** (≈1–1.5 cm) that is *heterogeneous* on both T1‑ and T2‑weighted images because of fibrous tissue and cystic degeneration. <br>• The wall may show low‑signal intensity on T2 (fibrous component) interspersed with high‑signal cystic areas. | • Endometrioma wall is thin (< 5 mm) and the cyst content is **high T1‑signal** (blood products) with **low T2 “shading”**; wall remains thin and smooth. |
| | **Internal contents** | • Mixed fluid signal: predominantly *hyper‑intense* on T2 (simple fluid) with *focal hyper‑intense* solid nodules on T1‑fat‑sat representing hemorrhagic or necrotic foci; may be inhomogeneous because of cystic degeneration. | • Classic “chocolate” cyst: **uniformly high T1‑signal** (blood‑laden) and **markedly low T2‑signal** (shading). No solid nodular components. |
| | **Presence of a stalk** | • On sagittal or coronal planes, a **linear low‑signal band** extending from the cyst to the uterine serosa, often tapering – this is the pedicle. In the case the MRI showed continuity of the caudal cyst with a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma, and the upper mass was later proven to have a pedicle. | • No low‑signal stalk; the lesion is confined to the ovary or adnexa. |
| | **Adjacency to myometrium** | • “Uterine‑contiguity sign”: the mass abuts the myometrium with loss of an intervening fat plane and may cause a subtle bulge of the uterine serosa; the interface appears **isointense to myometrium** on T1/T2, indicating true attachment. | • A clear fat plane separates the ovarian cyst from the uterus; uterine contour is normal. |
| | **Vascular “flow voids”** (seen even without contrast) | • Prominent flow voids within the pedicle or at the periphery of the lesion, reflecting the hypertrophied vessels described on Doppler. | • Little or no flow voids; only faint peripheral vessels may be seen. |
| | **Diffusion/ADC** (if performed) | • May show **restricted diffusion** in solid nodular components (fibrous tissue, necrotic foci). | • Endometrioma fluid typically **does not restrict** (high ADC), whereas solid implants (if any) would. |

### How the case fits the pattern
* **US** showed two large unilocular cystic masses with *peripheral vascularisation* and, for the lower lesion, an *adherent intramural‑subserosal myoma*—the classic clue that the cystic component is part of a degenerating leiomyoma rather than an ovarian cyst.  
* **MRI** demonstrated **thick (1.4 cm) walls**, heterogeneous fluid, and **focal hyper‑intense solid components** on T1/T2 fat‑sat, all aligning with cystic degeneration of a leiomyoma. The “continuity” with the posterior uterine myoma and lack of a clean cleavage plane further supported a myomatous origin.  
* The **absence of a thin, uniform low‑signal rim** and *shading* on T2 made a decidualised endometrioma less likely, despite the mildly elevated CA‑125.

### Bottom line for the bedside learner
When confronted with a cystic pelvic mass in pregnancy:

1. **Look for a stalk or continuity with the uterus** on both US (hypoechoic band, limited mobility) and MRI (low‑signal linear connection).  
2. **Assess wall thickness and internal signal heterogeneity** – thick, fibrous walls with mixed fluid favour a degenerated leiomyoma; thin walls with uniform high T1/low T2 (“shading”) favour an endometrioma.  
3. **Evaluate Doppler/flow voids** – prominent feeding vessels through a pedicle point to a pedunculated myoma; peripheral rim flow only suggests an endometrioma.  

These imaging hallmarks allow a confident distinction, guiding timely surgical decision‑making while preserving the pregnancy.
