Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] What imaging modalities are considered safe and most informative for evaluating large abdominal or pelvic masses in the second trimester of pregnancy, and why is contrast‑enhanced MRI generally avoided?

**Imaging large abdominal or pelvic masses in the 2nd trimester – what we can use safely and what we avoid**

In pregnancy the radiologic work‑up must obey two rules: (1) avoid ionising radiation to the fetus, and (2) avoid agents that cross the placenta in amounts that could be toxic.  For a large intra‑abdominal or pelvic mass the two modalities that meet these criteria are **(i) gray‑scale and colour‑Doppler ultrasonography** and **(ii) non‑contrast magnetic‑resonance imaging (MRI) performed on a 1.5‑T scanner**.  Both were employed in the case reported by De Nola et al.

| Modality | Safety in pregnancy | What it adds |
|----------|--------------------|--------------|
| **Transabdominal / trans‑vaginal US with colour Doppler** | No ionising radiation; equipment is universally available; can be repeated without risk. | Determines size, cystic vs solid composition, vascular pedicles, relationship to uterus/ovaries, and guides bimanual findings. In the case, US identified two unilocular cystic lesions with peripheral flow and suggested a possible cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma. |
| **Non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T, no gadolinium)** | No ionising radiation; static magnetic fields up to 1.5 T are considered safe after the first trimester.  Gadolinium‑based contrast agents are the only MRI component that is contraindicated (see below). | Provides superior soft‑tissue contrast, delineates the exact anatomic origin of a mass (uterine vs adnexal), defines wall thickness, internal septations and any solid enhancing components that cannot be seen on US.  In the case, non‑contrast MRI clarified that the cranial mass was a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma and showed the continuity of the caudal lesion with a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma. |
| **CT or plain radiography** | Uses ionising radiation; fetal dose, even when low, is avoided unless the benefit is overwhelming (e.g., acute trauma). | Not used for elective evaluation of pelvic masses in pregnancy. |
| **Contrast‑enhanced MRI (gadolinium‑based agents)** | Gadolinium chelates cross the placental barrier and enter the fetal circulation.  Animal data show fetal toxicity and human registries have linked first‑trimester exposure to rheumatologic, inflammatory, and dermal malformations, as well as a small but real increase in stillbirth/neonatal death.  Consequently, ACR, ACOG and ESMO guidelines advise **against gadolinium use in pregnancy unless the diagnostic benefit is absolutely crucial and cannot be obtained by other means**. | In the reported case the team deliberately omitted gadolinium; the non‑contrast study already gave sufficient anatomic detail to plan surgery, so the risks of contrast outweighed any incremental information. |

### Why contrast‑enhanced MRI is avoided
1. **Placental transfer** – Gadolinium freely crosses the placenta and the fetus lacks mature renal clearance, leading to prolonged exposure.
2. **Potential fetal toxicity** – Reports of fetal growth restriction, skeletal abnormalities, and stillbirth have been associated with gadolinium exposure, though causality is not definitive; the precautionary principle prevails.
3. **Lack of added value for most obstetric indications** – For characterising leiomyomas, endometriomas, or simple cystic masses, the intrinsic tissue contrast of T1/T2 and fat‑sat sequences is usually adequate.  The case demonstrated that non‑contrast MRI successfully differentiated a pedunculated leiomyoma from an ovarian cystic lesion.
4. **Guideline consensus** – ACR and ACOG state that gadolinium‑enhanced studies should only be performed when absolutely necessary (e.g., suspected malignant infiltration where treatment decisions hinge on contrast enhancement).

### Practical take‑home points for the bedside clinician
- **First‑line**: high‑resolution transabdominal (and when needed, transvaginal) ultrasound with colour Doppler.
- **If ultrasound is equivocal** (large size, unclear uterine‑adnexal origin, need to assess relationship to placenta), order a **non‑contrast MRI** on a 1.5‑T system after 13 weeks’ gestation.
- **Never** use CT, plain radiographs, or gadolinium‑enhanced MRI for routine evaluation of pelvic masses in pregnancy unless a life‑threatening condition mandates it and no safer alternative exists.
- **Document** fetal heart rate before and after the MRI (as per institutional protocol) to reassure both patient and team.

In the case of the 21‑week‑pregnant woman with two massive abdominal lesions, the combination of ultrasound and a non‑contrast MRI provided sufficient diagnostic confidence to proceed with a targeted laparotomy while preserving fetal safety—illustrating the optimal, evidence‑based imaging pathway for large abdominal/pelvic masses in the second trimester.
