Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## 3. Therapeutic Decision‑Making and Surgical Management  

The presence of two voluminous abdominal masses in a woman who was already 21 weeks pregnant created a therapeutic dilemma that required a coordinated, multidisciplinary approach.  The team—comprising obstetricians, maternal‑fetal medicine specialists, gynecologic oncologists, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists—first clarified **why surgery was unavoidable**, then weighed the **maternal‑fetal risks and benefits**, selected the **most appropriate operative route**, and finally detailed the intra‑operative and postoperative plan that would protect both the pregnancy and the mother.

### Indications for surgery during pregnancy  

In the obstetric literature, operative intervention for uterine or adnexal masses is reserved for a limited set of circumstances because of the inherent threats to fetal viability.  In this case, three major criteria were fulfilled:  

1. **Severe, refractory pain** – the patient’s visual‑analogue scale remained ≥5 despite optimal analgesia, positioning, and supportive care, indicating that the mass was producing ongoing visceral irritation.  
2. **Respiratory compromise** – the large left‑subphrenic lesion displaced the diaphragm, producing dyspnoea that threatened maternal oxygenation and, consequently, fetal oxygen delivery.  
3. **Evidence of evolving necrosis** – a low‑grade fever appeared after admission, and intra‑operative findings later confirmed ischaemic necrosis at the twisted pedicle of the cranial mass.  Necrotic tissue can precipitate infection, peritonitis, or abrupt hemorrhage, all of which carry high maternal and fetal morbidity.  

Secondary considerations—rapid enlargement on serial ultrasound, a modest rise in CA‑125 suggesting possible malignant potential, and the limited capacity of the growing uterus to accommodate further mass growth—reinforced the decision to intervene rather than continue expectant management.

### Risk–benefit analysis  

Once surgical necessity was established, the team performed a structured risk–benefit assessment centred on **fetal viability** and **maternal safety**:  

| Parameter | Maternal considerations | Fetal considerations |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------------|
| **Gestational age** | At 21 w + 6 d the uterus is still largely intra‑abdominal, permitting a midline laparotomy without excessive uterine manipulation. | Viability is assured; fetal monitoring is feasible throughout the operation. |
| **Estimated blood loss** | Anticipated moderate loss (pedunculated leiomyoma highly vascular) → need for pre‑operative type‑and‑cross and intra‑operative cell‑saver if available. | Maternal hypovolemia could precipitate uterine hypoperfusion; careful fluid and blood management protects fetal oxygen delivery. |
| **Uterine preservation** | A limited myomectomy (single pedunculated lesion) minimizes disruption of the myometrial envelope, reducing the risk of uterine dehiscence and future scar complications. | Preserving uterine integrity maintains the capacity for term continuation and lowers the chance of preterm labour triggered by uterine irritation. |
| **Anesthetic risk** | General anesthesia with a short operative time (<3 h) is considered safe in the second trimester; regional techniques are less practical for a large midline incision. | Inhalational agents cross the placenta but at the doses required for a brief surgery have not been shown to cause teratogenicity after the first trimester. |
| **Potential benefits** | Immediate relief of pain, removal of a necrotic source of infection, prevention of torsion or rupture of the cranial mass, and avoidance of future obstructive labour. | Eliminates a compressive element that could worsen maternal pulmonary mechanics, thereby improving fetal oxygenation; removes a lesion that might otherwise generate obstetric complications at term. |

The net balance favoured operative management, provided the procedure could be performed swiftly, with meticulous haemostasis, and with continuous fetal surveillance.

### Choice of surgical approach  

Both laparoscopy and laparotomy are technically feasible for myomectomy in pregnancy, yet **patient‑specific factors dictated an open approach**:  

* **Mass size and location** – the cranial lesion extended from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus (≈18 × 12 × 7 cm) and was highly vascularised; a pedicle could not be visualised on imaging, raising the possibility of a broad‑based attachment that would be difficult to control laparoscopically.  
* **Gestational uterus size** – at 21 weeks the uterus occupies the pelvis and lower abdomen, limiting the operative working space required for safe trocar placement and manipulation of large specimens.  
* **Urgency of decompression** – the open longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision permits rapid entry, direct tactile assessment of the pedicle, and swift en‑bloc removal of the mass, thereby minimizing operative time and maternal‑fetal exposure.  

Consequently, the team elected a **midline laparotomy** extending from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis, providing excellent exposure of the upper abdomen, the diaphragmatic dome, and the pelvis in a single incision.

### Pre‑operative preparation  

1. **Informed consent** – a detailed discussion with the patient covered the specific maternal risks (hemorrhage, infection, uterine rupture, anesthesia complications) and fetal risks (preterm labor, fetal distress, potential loss). Written consent was obtained after confirming that the patient understood the necessity of surgery, the possibility of conversion to cesarean delivery, and the plan to leave the caudal mass in situ.  
2. **Antibiotic prophylaxis** – because a urine culture had identified **Klebsiella pneumoniae** highly sensitive to cephalosporins, the protocol comprised:  
   * **Cefazolin 2 g IV** administered 30 minutes before skin incision, followed by **cefazolin 1 g IV every 8 h** post‑operatively;  
   * **Clarithromycin 500 mg PO twice daily** added to the post‑operative regimen to ensure coverage of the isolated organism, as per the antibiogram.  
3. **Progesterone supplementation** – to mitigate the risk of uterine irritability and support early gestational maintenance, the patient received:  
   * **Progesterone 100 mg IM** intra‑operatively (after induction of anesthesia) and then **100 mg IM daily** until discharge;  
   * **Hydroxy‑progesterone caproate 341 mg IM** on day 3 post‑op and subsequently every three days until elective delivery at 37 weeks, following protocols used for threatened preterm labour.  
4. **Maternal‑fetal monitoring preparation** – a portable fetal Doppler was readied for intra‑operative verification of fetal heart activity before incision, during critical steps (e.g., uterine manipulation), and after closure.

### Intra‑operative sequence  

1. **Peritoneal fluid sampling** – after entry into the peritoneal cavity, two separate specimens of free fluid were aspirated for **culture** (to rule out bacterial peritonitis) and **cytology** (to exclude occult malignancy).  
2. **Fetal heart confirmation** – a trans‑abdominal Doppler probe confirmed a baseline fetal heart rate of 140 bpm with reassuring variability before proceeding.  
3. **Identification of the cranial mass** – the upper lesion was found to be a pedunculated, cystic‑appearing leiomyoma attached to the uterine fundus by a narrow stalk that was twisted on itself. The pedicle was markedly vascular, accounting for the patient’s febrile episode (likely low‑grade septic necrosis).  
4. **Excision of the pedunculated leiomyoma** – the surgeon applied a **double‑clamp technique**: two atraumatic clamps were placed distal to the twisted portion of the stalk, the vascular supply was occluded, and the stalk was sharply transected between the clamps. Immediate haemostasis was achieved by **electrocautery** of the residual myometrial edges, followed by a **single–layer absorbable (0 Vicryl) suture** to re‑approximate the uterine wall, preserving myometrial integrity and preventing a future dehiscence site.  
5. **Assessment of the caudal mass** – the lower lesion, adherent to the posterior uterine serosa, measured ≈9 cm and was smooth, cystic, and lacked a discernible stalk. Given its deep pelvic location, the risk of extensive dissection, hemorrhage, and uterine destabilisation outweighed any immediate benefit of removal. The team therefore **deferred excision**, electing to monitor the mass throughout the remainder of gestation.  
6. **Operative metrics** – the total skin‑to‑skin time was **2 hours 25 minutes**; estimated blood loss was **≈400 mL**, managed with crystalloid infusion and one unit of packed red cells prepared in the OR. Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring was performed with a handheld Doppler every 15 minutes; no decelerations occurred.  

### Post‑operative immediate care  

* **Analgesia** – the pain regimen continued with **paracetamol 1 g IV every 6 h** and, when needed, **short‑acting opioids** (e.g., morphine 2 mg IV) titrated to maintain a VAS ≤ 2.  
* **Uterine activity surveillance** – uterine tone was assessed every 4 hours with bedside palpation and a tocodynamometer; no excessive contractions were observed. The prophylactic **progesterone 100 mg IM** was administered daily for the first three postoperative days, then transitioned to the caproate schedule.  
* **Antibiotic continuation** – cefazolin (1 g IV q8h) and clarithromycin (500 mg PO BID) were maintained for **5 days**, after which the patient completed a **7‑day oral course** of amoxicillin‑clavulanate to cover peripartum urinary flora.  
* **Laboratory follow‑up** – a postoperative CBC showed a transient drop in haemoglobin to 7.5 g/dL, prompting **intravenous ferric carboxymaltose 1 g** and **two units of packed red blood cells**; subsequent labs demonstrated haemoglobin rise to 10 g/dL.  
* **Obstetric monitoring** – fetal wellbeing was reassessed with a **biophysical profile** 24 hours after surgery; the amniotic fluid index and growth parameters remained within normal limits. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10 after an uneventful recovery, with instructions for **weekly obstetric visits** and a **planned elective cesarean at 37 weeks** to avoid the residual pelvic myoma obstructing vaginal delivery.

In summary, the therapeutic plan was built on a clear, evidence‑based framework: surgical indication was driven by symptomatic, potentially necrotic, and compressive disease; a careful risk–benefit calculus justified operation at 21 weeks; an open midline laparotomy offered the safest and most efficient route to excise the pedunculated leiomyoma; and meticulous peri‑operative preparation—including informed consent, targeted antibiotics, and progesterone support—enabled a successful outcome with preservation of both maternal health and fetal viability.
