Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 5: ## 5. Clinical Pearls, Evidence Synthesis, and Guideline Implications  

Uterine leiomyomas are the most frequent benign tumours encountered in pregnant women, yet the coexistence of large, cystic‑appearing pelvic masses creates a diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma. The present case illustrates how a systematic, evidence‑based approach can resolve uncertainty, prevent adverse obstetric events, and preserve both maternal and fetal health. The following pearls condense the lessons learned and translate them into practical algorithms that can be applied to similar scenarios.

### Epidemiology of uterine leiomyomas and adnexal masses in pregnancy  
- **Leiomyomas** affect 0.3–10 % of pregnancies, with prevalence rising with maternal age and obesity. Their growth is driven by progesterone and estrogen, resulting in a non‑linear size increase of ≈ 7 % per week in the first half of gestation.  
- **Adnexal cystic lesions** (functional ovarian cysts, endometriomas, para‑ovarian cysts) are encountered in 1–6 % of pregnancies. Because they share sonographic features with degenerated myomas, misdiagnosis occurs in up to 30 % of cases when imaging is limited to ultrasound alone.  

Understanding these baseline frequencies alerts the clinician to the possibility that a “large ovarian cyst” may in fact be a pedunculated leiomyoma, as occurred in the present patient.

### Diagnostic algorithm for large pelvic masses in pregnant patients  

1. **History and physical examination** – Focus on pain pattern, dyspnoea, obstetric milestones, and a thorough bimanual exam to assess mobility and relationship to the uterus.  
2. **Transabdominal/Transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler** – First‑line modality; evaluate size, wall thickness, internal septations, vascular pattern, and continuity with uterine tissue. Presence of a pedicle, a broad base attached to the myometrium, or marked peripheral vascularity favours a myoma.  
3. **Serum tumour markers** – CA‑125, CA‑19‑9, CEA, β‑2‑microglobulin, LDH. In pregnancy, CA‑125 may be modestly elevated (median 14 U/mL in the second trimester) and is not diagnostic; serial trends are more informative than a single value.  
4. **Non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging** – Indicated when ultrasound is indeterminate or when a large mass threatens maternal or fetal physiology. Use a ≤1.5 T scanner, omit gadolinium, and obtain T1/T2 fat‑sat sequences. Look for:  
   - **Myometrial continuity** (low‑signal uterine wall surrounding the lesion)  
   - **Signal heterogeneity** (cystic degeneration → high T2, low T1; hemorrhagic content → high T1)  
   - **Absence of solid enhancing nodules** (would raise suspicion for malignancy)  
5. **Multidisciplinary review** – Radiology, maternal‑fetal medicine, and surgical teams jointly interpret findings and discuss risks versus benefits of operative intervention.  

Applying this algorithm to the current case clarified that the upper mass originated from the uterus (pedunculated leiomyoma) and that the lower mass represented a cystically degenerated intramural‑subserosal myoma rather than an ovarian tumour.

### Interpretation of CA‑125 trends in pregnancy  
- **Physiological peaks** occur in the first (≈ 23 U/mL) and third trimesters (≈ 21 U/mL). Values > 35 U/mL in the second trimester, as seen in this patient (41.8 U/mL), are atypical but still lack specificity.  
- **Sources of elevation** include decidualised endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammation, and leiomyoma degeneration. Hence, CA‑125 should be regarded as an adjunct, not a decisive marker, and only significant, progressive rises should prompt further imaging or operative planning.  

### Safety profile of non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester  
- **Magnetic field strength** ≤ 1.5 T is considered safe; no teratogenic effects have been documented.  
- **Gadolinium** is contraindicated unless the benefit decisively outweighs the potential fetal toxicity (risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, unknown long‑term effects).  
- **Acoustic noise** can be mitigated with ear protection; fetal monitoring is not required for non‑contrast scans but may be prudent when the gestational age exceeds 24 weeks.  

### When to consider myomectomy during pregnancy  
Surgical removal of a leiomyoma is reserved for **complicated** lesions. Indications include:  

| Indication | Typical Threshold |
|------------|-------------------|
| **Size** > 10 cm (or rapid growth causing compression) | > 10 cm or > 5 cm increase within 4 weeks |
| **Pedunculated with torsion** (pain, necrosis, fever) | Any size when torsion is evident |
| **Refractory symptoms** (uncontrolled pain, dyspnoea, obstructive urinary symptoms) despite optimal analgesia and positioning | Persistent VAS ≥ 4 |
| **Imminent obstetric compromise** (mass limiting uterine expansion, threatening fetal head descent) | Mass impinging on uterine cavity or lower segment |

In the presented patient, the combination of a > 10 cm pedunculated leiomyoma, signs of torsion (pain, low‑grade fever, necrotic tissue at surgery), and restrictive lung mechanics satisfied three of the four criteria, justifying operative intervention.

### Laparotomy versus laparoscopic myomectomy in the second trimester  

| Parameter | Laparotomy (open) | Laparoscopic (Hasson) |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------------|
| **Maternal morbidity** | Higher postoperative pain, longer hospital stay (average 5–7 days) | Lower pain scores, shorter stay (2–3 days) |
| **Operative time** | 120‑150 min (as in case) | 90‑110 min (when feasible) |
| **Blood loss** | 300‑800 mL (higher transfusion risk) | 150‑400 mL (often transfusion‑free) |
| **Fetal safety** | Comparable fetal heart‑rate stability when surgery ≤ 2 h | Similar when CO₂ pressure ≤ 12 mm Hg |
| **Mass characteristics** | Preferred for very large (> 12 cm), multiple, or posterior/intramural lesions | Feasible for ≤ 10 cm, pedunculated, anterior lesions, early second trimester |

The decision to perform a laparotomy in this case was driven by the massive size, posterior location, and suspicion of torsion—factors that would have limited the visual field and maneuverability of laparoscopic instruments.

### Progesterone prophylaxis to reduce pre‑term labour during intra‑operative fetal monitoring  
- **Rationale**: Surgical manipulation and anaesthesia can trigger uterine irritability. A single intramuscular dose of 100 mg 17‑hydroxyprogesterone caproate intra‑operatively, followed by daily dosing, has been shown in small series to lower the incidence of postoperative preterm contractions (relative risk ≈ 0.6).  
- **Practical tip**: Administer the first dose immediately after uterine closure, then continue until delivery or at least 4 weeks postpartum. This regimen was employed successfully in the present case, with no subsequent preterm labour.

### Algorithm for delivery planning in the presence of residual uterine masses  

1. **Assess residual mass size and location** (ultrasound at 34‑36 weeks).  
2. **Determine likelihood of obstructed labour**:  
   - *Posterior or isthmic mass > 8 cm* → high risk of impaction → elective CS.  
   - *Anterior, lateral, or subserosal mass < 5 cm* → consider trial of labour.  
3. **Evaluate uterine scar integrity** (post‑myomectomy). If a full‑thickness myometrial repair was performed, schedule CS at 37‑38 weeks to reduce rupture risk.  
4. **Counsel the patient** on neonatal outcomes, maternal recovery, and future fertility.  

In the reported patient, a 9 cm posterior torus myoma was deemed an obstetric obstacle, prompting an elective repeat laparotomy‑CS at 37 weeks, which resulted in an uncomplicated delivery.

### Future research gaps  

- **Randomised controlled trials on timing of myomectomy** (first vs second trimester) to delineate optimal gestational windows that balance maternal symptom relief against fetal risk.  
- **Standardised MRI protocols** (e.g., diffusion‑weighted imaging without contrast) to improve differentiation between degenerated leiomyoma and ovarian pathology.  
- **Long‑term reproductive outcomes** after intra‑pregnancy myomectomy, particularly regarding subsequent fertility, uterine rupture rates, and placental implantation abnormalities.  

Addressing these gaps will refine guidelines, reduce practice variability, and improve counselling for pregnant patients faced with large pelvic masses.  

---

**Take‑home message:** Large, symptomatic uterine masses in pregnancy demand a step‑wise imaging work‑up (US → non‑contrast MRI), careful interpretation of tumour markers, and a multidisciplinary decision‑making process. Myomectomy should be reserved for lesions that are large, pedunculated with torsion, or threatening maternal‑fetal physiology, with laparotomy preferred for very large or posterior lesions. Progesterone prophylaxis and vigilant intra‑operative fetal monitoring enhance safety, while delivery planning must incorporate residual mass characteristics to decide between vaginal birth and elective caesarean section.
