Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 4: ## 4. Obstetric Follow‑up, Delivery Planning, and Post‑operative Outcomes  

After the laparotomy at 21 weeks + 6 days, the clinical focus shifted from urgent surgical care to safeguarding the ongoing pregnancy while monitoring the sequelae of the operation. The multidisciplinary team (obstetricians, maternal‑fetal medicine specialists, anesthesiologists, and hematologists) implemented a structured follow‑up protocol that addressed the hematologic recovery, fetal well‑being, the residual leiomyoma, and the timing and mode of delivery.

### Management of postoperative anaemia  

The patient entered the postoperative period with a hemoglobin level of 7.5 g/dL, well below the trimester‑specific threshold for symptomatic anemia and far beneath the WHO recommendation of 11 g/dL for pregnant women. In pregnancy, transfusion decisions balance maternal oxygen‑carrying capacity, fetal demands, and the risk of alloimmunization. The team adopted a two‑step approach:

1. **Intravenous iron repletion** – a single dose of ferric carboxymaltose 1 g was administered, providing an immediate iron load of ~1000 mg, which is safe in the second trimester and avoids the gastrointestinal side effects of oral preparations.  
2. **Red cell transfusion** – two units of cross‑matched packed red blood cells were given intra‑operatively and post‑operatively to raise the hemoglobin above 9 g/dL, the level at which tissue oxygenation and uteroplacental perfusion are reliably maintained. The transfusion threshold of 8 g/dL, recommended by obstetric guidelines for pregnant patients with symptomatic anemia, was therefore exceeded, and the patient’s hemoglobin stabilized at 10 g/dL without further need for transfusion.

Iron studies after the infusion showed ferritin >100 µg/L, confirming adequate repletion. Subsequent oral iron was prescribed for maintenance until delivery.

### Serial obstetric ultrasounds  

From discharge, the patient attended obstetric visits every three weeks. Each encounter included:

* **Fetal biometry** – longitudinal measurements demonstrated growth tracking at the 86th percentile on the WHO fetal growth chart, indicating appropriate growth despite the maternal surgical stress.  
* **Amniotic fluid index (AFI)** – serial AFI values remained within the normal range (8–24 cm), ruling out oligohydramnios which could have been precipitated by uterine compression or placental insufficiency.  
* **Myoma surveillance** – transabdominal and transvaginal sonography measured the residual posterior myoma. At 28 weeks the lesion measured 8.2 cm; by 36 weeks it had regressed to 7.5 cm, an expected postpartum involution pattern accelerated by the cessation of hormonal stimulation after delivery. No increase in vascularity or signs of degeneration were observed, decreasing the risk of acute complications (torsion, rupture).

These imaging checkpoints also allowed continuous assessment of placental location relative to the myoma, ensuring that the scarred uterine segment would not compromise placental implantation.

### Resolution of the caudal myoma postpartum  

Post‑delivery ultrasound confirmed a further reduction of the caudal myoma to 7.5 cm, with a homogeneous hypoechoic texture and a thin peripheral rim, consistent with involution. The size reduction underscores the physiological shrinkage of leiomyomas after the abrupt drop in estrogen and progesterone levels that follows delivery. Although the lesion remained asymptomatic, its persistence warranted a plan for future evaluation.

### Decision for elective caesarean section at 37 weeks  

Several factors converged on the choice of an elective caesarean delivery on the existing laparotomy scar:

1. **Mechanical obstruction** – the posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma occupied the lower pelvis, extending into the pouch of Douglas. Its size and fixed nature would likely impede fetal head descent during the expulsive phase, raising the risk of prolonged labour, uterine rupture, or severe perineal trauma.  
2. **Scar integrity** – the myomectomy had been performed via a longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision with a single‑layer absorbable uterine closure. Although the incision healed uneventfully, the presence of a uterine scar combined with a large residual mass increased the theoretical risk of dehiscence under the stress of labour. Elective cesarean delivery thus offered a controlled environment to avoid uterine rupture.  
3. **Gestational timing** – delivering at 37 weeks balances fetal maturity (average birth weight >3000 g) with the avoidance of further myoma‑related complications that could arise with advancing gestation (e.g., increased uterine irritability, preterm labor).  

The operation was performed through the previous laparotomy incision, minimizing additional abdominal trauma. A low transverse uterine incision was not feasible because of the location of the residual myoma; the existing longitudinal scar was therefore utilized, and meticulous hemostasis was achieved.

### Neonatal outcomes  

The neonate weighed 3 120 g (approximately the 78th percentile for gestational age) and had Apgar scores of 9 and 10 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively. The infant required only routine temperature regulation and glucose monitoring; no respiratory support was needed, despite the maternal pre‑operative dyspnoea. Umbilical arterial blood gases were within normal limits (pH 7.30, base excess −2 mmol/L), confirming adequate fetal oxygenation throughout delivery. The newborn was breast‑fed successfully from the first day, and the mother reported no post‑partum complications.

### Long‑term follow‑up plan  

Given the residual leiomyoma’s size and asymptomatic nature, the following strategy was adopted:

* **Imaging** – a transvaginal ultrasound at 6 weeks postpartum and again at 12 months will document further involution. If the lesion exceeds 5 cm after one year or becomes symptomatic, magnetic resonance imaging (without contrast) will be considered for detailed mapping.  
* **Symptom monitoring** – the patient was educated on signs of myoma‑related problems (pelvic pain, heavy menstrual bleeding, pressure symptoms) and instructed to seek care promptly.  
* **Fertility counseling** – the residual myoma is unlikely to impair future fertility, but the patient was advised that a large posterior lesion can interfere with embryo implantation or increase the risk of obstetric complications in subsequent pregnancies. Should she desire another child, pre‑conception counseling will include a discussion of elective myomectomy versus expectant management, weighing the risks of surgery against the likelihood of spontaneous regression.  
* **Surgical options** – if the myoma persists or enlarges, minimally invasive myomectomy (laparoscopic or hysteroscopic, depending on location) or uterine‑sparing ablative techniques (e.g., MRI‑guided focused ultrasound) may be offered. All plans would incorporate a multidisciplinary review with a maternal‑fetal medicine specialist.

### Pathology report  

The excised cranial mass was submitted for histopathology. Microscopic examination revealed bundles of smooth‑muscle cells with whorled architecture, consistent with a benign uterine leiomyoma. Areas of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle were identified, explaining the intra‑operative finding of a leaking, inflamed peduncle and the transient fever. No atypia, mitotic activity, or cellular pleomorphism was seen; immunohistochemistry for desmin and smooth‑muscle actin was positive, confirming smooth‑muscle origin. Margins were free of tumor, and cytology of peritoneal fluid was negative for malignant cells. The absence of malignancy corroborated the decision to limit surgical excision to the pedunculated mass and forego a more extensive uterine resection.

Overall, the coordinated postoperative care—prompt correction of anemia, vigilant obstetric surveillance, a well‑justified elective cesarean delivery, and an evidence‑based long‑term plan—enabled an optimal maternal and neonatal outcome while preserving the option for future fertility and definitive management of the residual leiomyoma.
