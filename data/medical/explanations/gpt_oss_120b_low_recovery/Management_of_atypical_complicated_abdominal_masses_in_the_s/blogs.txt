Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When Leiomyomas Masquerade as Ovarian Masses: Imaging Pitfalls in Pregnancy  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A 42‑year‑old, first‑time mom in her 21‑week gestation presented with two massive “cystic” lesions—one 18 cm in the left sub‑phrenic space and a second 9 cm in the pouch of Douglas. The clinical team initially thought they were dealing with decidualised endometriomas, a relatively rare but plausible diagnosis in pregnancy. The final operative surprise? Both lesions were actually **cystic‐degenerating uterine leiomyomas**, one pedunculated and twisted, the other an intramural‑subserosal myoma masquerading as an ovarian cyst.  

This scenario highlights a classic radiologic trap: **uterine fibroids can look exactly like adnexal cysts on ultrasound and even non‑contrast MRI**, especially when they undergo cystic degeneration or become pedunculated. Mis‑labeling these masses can drive unnecessary anxiety, invasive work‑ups, or even premature delivery. The good news is that careful attention to a few key imaging clues can keep us on the right track, even in the limited imaging environment of pregnancy.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma  

### What We Saw on Ultrasound  

* **Large, unilocular, thin‑walled cystic lesions** with peripheral vascularity on colour Doppler.  
* No solid nodules, papillary projections, or “ground‑glass” echogenicity that would scream ovarian teratoma or carcinoma.  
* The upper mass occupied the left sub‑phrenic area, displacing the gravid uterus; the lower mass appeared adherent to a posterior uterine wall “myoma” on B‑mode.

These sonographic features are **classic for decidualised endometriomas**: a cystic lesion with peripheral vascular flow, often seen in the second trimester when decidual reaction mimics endometriotic tissue. However, several red flags were present:

1. **Size and location** – an 18 cm cyst spanning the left sub‑phrenic space is far larger than a typical endometrioma and uncommon for an ovarian origin in a pregnant uterus.  
2. **Absence of a clear ovarian silhouette** – the ovary was not visualised separate from the mass, raising suspicion that the “cyst” could be attached to the uterus.  
3. **Mildly rising CA‑125** – while CA‑125 can be physiologically elevated, the trend (36 → 41 U/mL) was atypical for uncomplicated decidualisation.

### MRI Without Gadolinium  

Because contrast agents are avoided in pregnancy, the team performed a **non‑contrast 1.5‑T MRI**. The images showed:

* Thick walls (1.4 cm) with heterogeneous fluid signal.  
* Focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components, but no clear feeding vessels or stalks.  
* Indistinct cleavage planes between the lower mass and posterior uterine wall.

Again, this pattern can be read as “cystic degeneration of a fibroid” **or** “large endometrioma”. The lack of a visible ovarian stalk and the continuity with the uterus (even if subtle) should tip the scale toward a fibroid, especially when the lesion is **pedunculated**—a morphology that is difficult to capture on static imaging.

### Pitfall Summary  

| Feature | Endometrioma (Decidualised) | Cystic Degenerating Leiomyoma |
|---------|----------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Typical size in pregnancy | <10 cm | Can exceed 15 cm |
| Location | Ovary, often bilateral | Can arise from any uterine wall; may be high up (sub‑phrenic) |
| Doppler pattern | Peripheral flow, low‑velocity | Peripheral flow, may be more arterial (pulsatile) |
| MRI wall thickness | Thin, smooth | Thickened walls, possible solid nodules |
| Relationship to uterus | Separate | Continuity of tissue planes, sometimes subtle |

## Practical Tips for the Obstetric Sonographer  

1. **Map the Uterine Contour First** – Start with a sweep of the uterus in transverse and sagittal planes before hunting for ovaries. If a “cyst” appears to share the uterine serosal line, suspect a fibroid.  

2. **Check for an Ovarian Pedicle** – Even a tiny ovarian medullary echo or a “whirl” of vessels entering the mass is a dead‑giveaway for ovarian origin. Its absence should raise suspicion for a uterine source.  

3. **Use Color Doppler to Gauge Flow Pattern** – Leiomyomas often have **more robust arterial flow** at the periphery, especially when pedunculated and twisted. Endometriomas usually display low‑velocity venous flow.  

4. **Measure Wall Thickness** – A wall thicker than 1 cm is uncommon for a simple cystic ovarian lesion. Thick, irregular walls point toward a degenerating fibroid.  

5. **Follow the “Straw‑Man” Sign** – In a transverse slice, a subserosal fibroid may create a small “straw‑man” separation between the myometrium and the lesion. Look for that thin line of echogenic tissue.  

6. **Serial Imaging** – If the diagnosis remains ambiguous, repeat imaging in 1–2 weeks. Fibroids tend to **grow rapidly** in early pregnancy (≈7 % per week) whereas endometriomas are usually stable.  

7. **Correlate with Clinical Context** – Large, rapidly expanding masses that cause **dyspnoea or diaphragmatic elevation** are more likely to be uterine in origin because of their proximity to the diaphragm.  

8. **Consider Non‑Invasive Biomarkers Cautiously** – CA‑125 can be mildly elevated in pregnancy, but a **trend upward** may suggest an active process (e.g., inflammation or torsion). Use it as an adjunct, not a definitive test.

## When to Escalate to MRI  

In the pregnant patient, MRI is the next step when:

* Ultrasound cannot definitively separate the mass from the uterus.  
* There is a clinical concern for **torsion, necrosis, or impending rupture** (as in the presented case).  
* Surgical planning hinges on knowing whether the lesion is extra‑uterine.

**Key MRI clues**:  

* Look for loss of the “junctional zone” between myometrium and the lesion—loss suggests uterine origin.  
* Identify any “sharp” interface vs. smooth transition.  
* Observe for hemorrhagic “fluid‑level” signs, more typical of endometriomas.

## Management Take‑Home Messages  

* **Conservative management is the default** for uterine fibroids in pregnancy because the majority will regress postpartum.  
* **Surgery is justified** when the mass causes severe pain, threatens airway compromise (mass effect on diaphragm), shows signs of torsion/necrosis, or when there is oncologic suspicion.  
* If surgery is required, **target the most symptomatic component** (as the team did by excising the twisted pedunculated fibroid and leaving the pelvic mass in situ).  
* **Progesterone prophylaxis** (IM 100 mg then hydroxyprogesterone caproate) can help sustain the pregnancy intra‑ and post‑operatively.  
* **Elective caesarean delivery** may be advisable when a large intramural or subserosal leiomyoma sits in the lower uterine segment and could impede vaginal delivery.

## Bottom Line  

Uterine leiomyomas are notorious shape‑shifters, especially when they undergo cystic degeneration or become pedunculated during pregnancy. A **methodical, anatomy‑first approach** on ultrasound—augmented by targeted colour Doppler and, when needed, non‑contrast MRI—can spare patients from unnecessary alarm and invasive procedures. Remember: the uterus is the biggest “organ” in the pregnant abdomen, and any mass that appears to hug it closely deserves a closer look before we label it ovarian.  

---  

**Key Pearls**  

- Start with a systematic sweep of the uterus; any cystic lesion that seems tethered to the uterine wall is likely a fibroid.  
- Thick (>1 cm) cyst walls and robust peripheral arterial flow on Doppler favor leiomyoma over endometrioma.  
- In pregnancy, non‑contrast MRI can clarify tissue planes without gadolinium; look for loss of the junctional zone.  
- Surgical intervention should be reserved for complications (pain, torsion, respiratory compromise) and aimed at the most symptomatic lesion.  
- Post‑operative progesterone and careful obstetric follow‑up support a good maternal‑fetal outcome.  

Stay curious, keep scanning methodically, and when in doubt, let the anatomy speak louder than the “cystic” appearance. Happy scanning!



# Managing Symptomatic Myomas in the Second Trimester: To Operate or Not to Operate?

## Why This Case Stands Out  

Pregnancy already stretches the abdomen; add a **giant, pedunculated leiomyoma** that fills the left sub‑phrenic space and a second cystic mass that bulges into the pouch of Douglas, and you have a perfect storm of diagnostic confusion and physiologic compromise. In the report by De Nola et al., a 40‑year‑old woman at 21 weeks gestation presented with severe left‑upper‑quadrant pain, dyspnoea, and a fever‑ish picture that turned out to be a twisted, vascularised leiomyoma leaking necrotic fluid. The mass effect was enough to compress the diaphragm, impairing ventilation, while the growing fibroid threatened obstetric progress.  

What makes the case especially instructive is that **imaging mimicked endometriomas**, CA‑125 was modestly elevated, and yet the definitive diagnosis (and the decision to intervene) hinged on a balance between **maternal safety** (preventing torsion, rupture, infection) and **fetal preservation** (avoiding preterm labor, hemorrhage, uterine rupture). The multidisciplinary team chose a targeted laparotomic myomectomy of the pedunculated lesion, left the pelvic mass untouched, and delivered electively at term via cesarean—an outcome that illustrates a pragmatic, patient‑centred algorithm.

## Key Clinical Pearls  

- **Large fibroids in pregnancy are not rare (0.3–10 % prevalence), but massive (>10 cm) or pedunculated lesions are uncommon** and often present with pain, dyspnoea, or acute abdomen.  
- **Ultrasound is the first line**, but when masses are large, displaced, or have mixed cystic‑solid features, a **non‑contrast MRI** (≥1.5 T, after the first trimester) can clarify uterine origin and vascular supply without exposing the fetus to gadolinium.  
- **Serum CA‑125 rises physiologically in the first and third trimesters**, so a modest increase in the second trimester (e.g., 41 U/mL) should not drive diagnosis; trends are more informative than absolute values.  
- **Surgical myomectomy in pregnancy is not forbidden**—it is indicated for (1) torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, (2) intractable pain or mass effect, (3) suspicion of malignancy, or (4) complications such as infection or rupture.  
- **Timing matters**: The second trimester is the sweet spot—organogenesis is complete, the uterus is still mobile enough for safe exposure, and the risk of preterm labor is lower than in the third trimester.  
- **Approach matters**: For very large or posteriorly located masses, a **midline laparotomy** may provide better exposure and control than laparoscopy, especially when rapid conversion is possible. Laparoscopic myomectomy can be considered in early second trimester for smaller, anterior, or subserosal lesions.  
- **Intra‑operative fetal monitoring is essential**: Continuous transvaginal Doppler before, during, and after the procedure ensures early detection of fetal distress.  
- **Adjuncts to protect the pregnancy**: Prophylactic progesterone (e.g., IM 100 mg daily) and careful hemostasis (uterine suturing, vasopressin use) reduce the risk of miscarriage and preterm labour.  
- **Post‑operative care**: Address anemia aggressively (IV iron, transfusion if needed), treat any concurrent infection, and continue close obstetric surveillance (US every 3 weeks, fetal growth charts).  

## Decision‑Making Algorithm (Practical Steps)

1. **Initial Assessment**  
   - History: rapid abdominal growth, pain, dyspnoea, fever, urinary symptoms.  
   - Physical exam: size, tenderness, palpable mass, bimanual assessment for fixation.  
   - Labs: CBC, iron studies, CRP, urine culture, tumor markers (CA‑125, CEA, etc.).  

2. **Imaging**  
   - **Transabdominal/Transvaginal US**: size, location (subserosal, intramural, pedunculated), vascularity (color Doppler).  
   - **Non‑contrast MRI** if US ambiguous or masses >10 cm, to evaluate continuity with uterine wall, solid components, and rule out ovarian pathology.  

3. **Risk Stratification**  
   | Feature | Low‑Risk (Conservative) | High‑Risk (Consider Surgery) |
   |---------|--------------------------|------------------------------|
   | Mass size | <8 cm, asymptomatic | ≥8–10 cm, causing pain/respiratory compromise |
   | Pedicle | No stalk | Pedunculated, torsion or impending torsion |
   | Growth | Stable | Rapid increase >2 cm in 2 weeks |
   | Symptoms | Mild, controllable | Severe pain, dyspnoea, fever, infection |
   | Labs | Normal CBC, no infection | Anemia, leukocytosis, positive urine culture |
   | Fetal status | Stable | Non‑reassuring heart rate, threatened preterm labor |

4. **Multidisciplinary Review**  
   - Obstetrics, maternal‑fetal medicine, gynecologic oncology, anesthesia, neonatology.  
   - Discuss surgical approach, need for blood products, fetal monitoring plan, and postoperative plan (progesterone, antibiotics, iron).  

5. **Surgical Planning (if indicated)**  
   - **Timing:** 18–24 weeks (mid‑second trimester).  
   - **Approach:**  
     - **Laparotomy** for large (>15 cm), posterior, or multiple masses, or when rapid conversion is likely.  
     - **Laparoscopy (Hasson technique)** for smaller (<10 cm), anterior, solitary lesions.  
   - **Intra‑op monitoring:** Continuous fetal heart rate (transvaginal Doppler), uterine tone assessment.  
   - **Hemostasis:** Vascular clamps, uterine suturing, consider intra‑uterine vasopressin (avoid systemic hypotension).  

6. **Post‑Operative Management**  
   - **Progesterone prophylaxis** (IM 100 mg daily for 48 h then taper).  
   - **Antibiotics** guided by cultures (e.g., cefazolin + clarithromycin as in case).  
   - **Iron replacement/transfusion** as needed.  
   - **Obstetric follow‑up:** US every 3 weeks, fetal growth curves, amniotic fluid index, watch for preterm labor signs.  

7. **Delivery Planning**  
   - If residual myoma is large (>8 cm) and impedes the birth canal, schedule **elective cesarean** at 37–38 weeks, using the same incision if feasible.  
   - If myomas are small and not obstructive, a trial of vaginal delivery is reasonable with close intrapartum monitoring.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Do not reflexively postpone myomectomy** in pregnancy; evaluate the balance of maternal risk from the mass (torsion, necrosis, infection, respiratory compromise) against surgical risks.  
- **Second‑trimester laparotomy** remains a safe and effective option for giant, pedunculated, or posterior fibroids that are symptomatic or threatening.  
- **Multimodal imaging (US + non‑contrast MRI)** is essential to differentiate fibroids from ovarian pathology, especially when tumor markers are equivocal.  
- **Fetal monitoring and progesterone prophylaxis** are practical tools to safeguard the pregnancy during and after surgery.  
- **Tailored, multidisciplinary decision‑making**—including obstetrics, surgery, anesthesia, and neonatology—optimizes outcomes for both mother and baby.

In the end, the case by De Nola et al. reminds us that **surgical myomectomy is not a taboo in pregnancy**; it is a nuanced, patient‑specific intervention that, when timed and executed correctly, can avert serious complications and still allow a healthy delivery. Keep these pearls in mind the next time a pregnant patient walks in with a “big lump” and a shortness of breath—sometimes the best answer is to cut, not just to watch.



# CA‑125 in Pregnancy: Interpreting a “Positive” Marker Amid Physiologic Peaks  

## Why This Case Got My Attention  

When a 40‑year‑old pregnant woman presents with two gigantic abdominal cystic masses at 21 weeks, the first thing you do is stare at the imaging and then glance at the labs. In this report the CA‑125 was 41.8 U/mL – just over the standard oncology cutoff of 35 U/mL – and the trend was upward. In the non‑pregnant adult that number would trigger a work‑up for ovarian cancer. Yet the patient’s masses turned out to be **pedunculated, cystic leiomyomas** (one twisted, one subserosal). The “positive” CA‑125 was a red herring that could have led us down a much more invasive path.

The case spotlights a classic pitfall: using tumor markers in isolation during pregnancy. In reality, CA‑125 follows a **bimodal physiologic curve** across gestation, can be modestly elevated in benign uterine conditions, and often lacks the discrimination power we rely on in the non‑pregnant population. Understanding that curve and knowing when – and when not – to repeat the test can save both mother and fetus from unnecessary anxiety and procedures.

## The Physiology of CA‑125 in Pregnancy  

* **First‑trimester peak** – Median ~23 U/mL (range can climb >35 U/mL). The rise is driven by decidualization of the endometrium and the trophoblastic interface; the marker is released from placental decidua and the maternal endometrium.  
* **Second‑trimester trough** – Median around 14 U/mL. Levels usually normalize, creating a “valley” that many clinicians mistakenly take as a baseline.  
* **Third‑trimester rise** – Median again ~21 U/mL, reflecting renewed decidual activity and increased vascular permeability as the uterus expands.

These values overlap considerably with the traditional “abnormal” cut‑off of 35 U/mL. In fact, a **single CA‑125 >35 U/mL** in a pregnant woman does not reliably indicate malignancy; it can be seen in up to 10 % of healthy pregnancies, especially when measured in the first trimester.

## Benign Conditions That Can Push CA‑125 Higher  

| Condition | Typical CA‑125 range in pregnancy | Why it rises |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|--------------|
| **Uterine leiomyoma** (especially subserosal or pedunculated) | 30‑45 U/mL, sometimes >50 U/mL | Myometrial smooth‑muscle cells can produce CA‑125; cystic degeneration adds a peritoneal irritation component |
| **Decidualized endometrioma** | 30‑60 U/mL | Decidual changes in ectopic endometrial tissue mimic the hormonal environment of early pregnancy, boosting CA‑125 |
| **Pelvic inflammatory disease / infection** | Variable, often >35 U/mL | Inflammatory cytokines up‑regulate CA‑125 production from serosal surfaces |
| **Hemorrhagic ovarian cysts** | 30‑70 U/mL | Blood in the peritoneal cavity irritates mesothelial cells, which secrete CA‑125 |

In the case above, the **upper mass** behaved like a decidualized endometrioma on imaging, while the **lower mass** was clearly a cystic leiomyoma. Both entities can independently nudge CA‑125 upward, explaining the modest rise from 36 U/mL to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks.

## Serial CA‑125: Helpful or Harmful?  

Because CA‑125 fluctuates physiologically, **trend analysis** can be informative **only when interpreted in context**:

1. **Baseline Establishment** – If the first measurement is taken in the first trimester during the physiologic peak, a later “decline” may simply reflect the natural trough rather than disease regression.  
2. **Timing Matters** – Serial measurements spaced across trimesters can illustrate the expected biphasic pattern. An atypical **monotonic rise** (e.g., 30 → 45 → 70 U/mL) that continues into the third trimester may warrant further evaluation, especially if imaging shows solid components or rapid growth.  
3. **Correlation with Imaging** – Trends must be coupled with ultrasound/MRI findings. A stable cystic lesion with a flat CA‑125 curve is reassuring; a solid, vascular mass with rising CA‑125 is a red flag.  
4. **Avoid Over‑Testing** – Repeating CA‑125 weekly without a change in clinical scenario adds confusion and anxiety. In the presented case, the modest rise over two weeks was **insufficient** to change management; the decision to operate was driven by **symptom severity** and **risk of torsion**, not the marker.

Bottom line: **Serial CA‑125 is rarely decisive in pregnancy**. Use it as an adjunct to imaging, not as a standalone trigger for surgery or chemotherapy.

## When Tumor Markers Actually Add Value  

* **Known ovarian malignancy** – If a patient has a pre‑pregnancy diagnosis of ovarian cancer, CA‑125 can be followed to gauge treatment response, but even then, pregnancy‑related fluctuations must be considered.  
* **Rapidly enlarging solid adnexal mass** – When imaging shows a solid, vascularized lesion with concerning features (Papillary projections, ascites), a rising CA‑125 may tip the scales toward earlier surgical exploration.  
* **Persistent elevation beyond the third‑trimester trough** – A persistently high CA‑125 (>70 U/mL) in the late second or third trimester, especially with new symptoms, should prompt a multidisciplinary discussion and possibly MRI with gadolinium (only if benefits clearly outweigh fetal risks).

Otherwise, the most reliable diagnostic tools remain **high‑quality ultrasound** (with Doppler) and **non‑contrast MRI**. Both are safe after the first trimester and provide the anatomic detail needed to differentiate leiomyoma, endometrioma, and true ovarian neoplasms.

## Practical Take‑Home Points  

- **Don’t treat a single CA‑125 >35 U/mL in pregnancy as cancer**; consider the gestational age and the known physiologic peaks.  
- **Correlate CA‑125 with imaging**: a cystic, well‑circumscribed mass is far more likely benign, even with a modestly elevated marker.  
- **Serial measurements are rarely decisive**; if you must repeat, do it across trimesters and interpret the trend in the context of imaging changes.  
- **Use MRI (non‑contrast) for problem‑solving** when ultrasound is equivocal; it can clarify whether a mass is uterine (leiomyoma) or adnexal (endometrioma).  
- **Reserve surgery for complications** (torsion, necrosis, intractable pain, or suspicion of malignancy) rather than for a “positive” CA‑125 alone.  
- **Multidisciplinary decision‑making** (obstetrics, surgery, radiology, pathology) is essential to balance maternal safety and fetal preservation.  

**Key Pearls**

- CA‑125 rises physiologically in the first and third trimesters; a level of 40‑45 U/mL in the second trimester is often benign.  
- Leiomyomas and decidualized endometriomas can both modestly elevate CA‑125; imaging is the arbiter.  
- In pregnant patients with large pelvic masses, **non‑contrast MRI** is the safest way to improve anatomic resolution without radiation or gadolinium.  
- When surgery is needed, a **targeted, brief myomectomy** (as in the case) can alleviate symptoms while preserving pregnancy, and prophylactic progesterone may help maintain fetal viability.  

By keeping these concepts front and center, we avoid the trap of over‑reacting to a “positive” CA‑125 and instead focus on what truly matters: the patient’s symptoms, the imaging, and a collaborative, evidence‑based plan. Happy reading, and stay curious!



# Anesthetic and Hormonal Strategies to Protect the Fetus During Mid‑Trimester Myomectomy  
*FOAM‑style clinical education*

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A 42‑year‑old, nulliparous woman in the **21st week of pregnancy** arrived with two gigantic abdominal masses that were masquerading as endometriomas on ultrasound. The upper lesion measured 18 cm, the lower 9 cm, and both were causing **significant abdominal pain and restrictive dyspnoea**. Imaging could not definitively tell whether the lesions were ovarian, uterine, or malignant.  

Because the masses were symptomatic, threatened to compromise lung mechanics, and risked torsion or infection, the multidisciplinary team elected **mid‑trimester laparotomy**. What makes this report compelling is not the surgery itself (which many would defer to postpartum) but the **peri‑operative strategy** that allowed a safe myomectomy while preserving a viable pregnancy. The team blended anesthetic vigilance, hormonal support, antimicrobial stewardship, and meticulous blood‑product planning—an approach that can be borrowed for any non‑obstetric abdominal surgery in pregnancy.

---

## Core Principles for Fetal Protection During Mid‑Trimester Surgery  

1. **Maintain uteroplacental perfusion** – avoid hypotension, hypoxia, and tachycardia.  
2. **Prevent iatrogenic pre‑term labor** – limit uterine irritability, control pain, and provide progesterone support.  
3. **Continuous fetal monitoring** – even in the second trimester, intermittent fetal heart rate (FHR) checks provide a safety net.  
4. **Tailored antimicrobial regimen** – treat maternal infection while safeguarding the fetus from drug‑related teratogenicity.  
5. **Anticipate hemorrhage** – plan blood product availability and consider pharmacologic agents that do not cross the placenta in harmful doses.

---

## Anesthetic Management  

### Induction and Maintenance  

* **Rapid‑sequence induction (RSI)** with a short‑acting opioid (e.g., fentanyl 1–2 µg·kg⁻¹) and a non‑depolarizing muscle relaxant (rocuronium) is safe; avoid high‑dose volatile agents that can cause uterine relaxation and hypotension.  
* **Maintenance** with an inhalational agent at ≤1 MAC (preferably sevoflurane) plus a judicious opioid infusion. Sevoflurane is well‑studied in pregnancy and provides uterine relaxation without significant fetal depression when maternal blood gases are normal.  
* **Ventilation strategy**: target normocapnia (PaCO₂ ≈ 35‑45 mm Hg). Hyperventilation reduces uterine blood flow; hypoventilation leads to maternal acidosis and fetal distress.  

### Hemodynamic Goals  

* **Systolic BP ≥ 100 mm Hg** and **MAP ≥ 65 mm Hg** throughout. Use phenylephrine (α‑agonist) rather than ephedrine for hypotension because phenylephrine preserves uterine blood flow without increasing fetal metabolic demand.  
* **Baseline invasive arterial line** placement is advisable when large masses may cause rapid shifts in preload.  

### Uterine Irritability  

* **Avoid uterine stimulants**: no oxytocin, prostaglandins, or high‑dose β‑agonists.  
* **Pain control**: multimodal analgesia (paracetamol, low‑dose NSAID *if* gestational age > 20 weeks and no contraindication) reduces stress‑mediated catecholamine surge that can precipitate uterine contractions.

---

## Hormonal Prophylaxis – The Role of Progesterone  

### Rationale  

Progesterone is the cornerstone of pregnancy maintenance. In the reported case, the team initiated **intramuscular 100 mg progesterone intraoperatively**, then continued daily until discharge, followed by **hydroxyprogesterone caproate** (341 mg IM every 3 days) through the remainder of the pregnancy. This regimen mirrors what is used for women with a history of preterm birth.

### Mechanism  

* **Stabilizes the myometrium** by up‑regulating myometrial progesterone receptors, reducing oxytocin sensitivity.  
* **Anti‑inflammatory** effects blunt the cytokine surge associated with surgical stress.  

### Practical Tips  

* **Start early**: give the first dose right after anesthesia induction to pre‑empt the cortisol/ catecholamine surge.  
* **Dosing**: 100 mg IM of natural progesterone (micronized) daily is sufficient; transition to long‑acting caproate for convenience after discharge.  
* **Safety**: No teratogenicity reported; the primary concern is minor injection site pain.

---

## Fetal Heart Rate Surveillance  

### Why Monitor in the 2nd Trimester?  

FHR monitoring at 21 weeks is not as straightforward as in term labor, but **intermittent Doppler assessments** before, during, and after critical surgical steps (e.g., uterine incision, mass removal) can detect early signs of fetal hypoxia.

### Implementation  

* **Baseline**: Confirm fetal viability with a handheld Doppler (≥ 120 bpm, accelerations).  
* **Intra‑operative**: A sterile Doppler probe can be positioned on the maternal abdomen; check every 15‑20 minutes, especially after large fluid shifts or vasopressor boluses.  
* **Post‑operative**: Continue bedside checks for the first 24 hours, then transition to routine obstetric visits.

---

## Antimicrobial Strategy  

The patient had a **Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary infection**. The team chose:

* **Prophylactic cefazolin 2 g IV** prior to incision – standard for abdominal surgery, safe in pregnancy.  
* **Post‑op cefazolin 1 g q8h** + **clarithromycin 500 mg q12h** per culture sensitivities.

### Key Points  

* **Avoid aminoglycosides** (e.g., gentamicin) unless absolutely necessary; they cross the placenta and can cause fetal ototoxicity.  
* **Macrolides (e.g., clarithromycin)** are category B, with a good safety record for short courses.  
* **Duration**: limit to 48‑72 hours unless infection persists—short courses reduce fetal exposure and antimicrobial resistance.

---

## Blood Product Planning  

Massive hemorrhage is the greatest intra‑operative threat during myomectomy. The team prepared:

* **Two units of cross‑matched PRBCs** on standby.  
* **Intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g)** post‑op to correct iron‑deficiency anemia.  
* **Tranexamic acid** (1 g IV bolus) can be considered intra‑operatively; it has a favorable safety profile in pregnancy and reduces bleeding.

### Practical Checklist  

| Item | Rationale |
|------|-----------|
| Type‑and‑screen + 2 units PRBCs | Immediate availability in case of rapid blood loss |
| Cell saver** (if available) | Autologous salvage minimizes allogeneic exposure; filters out amniotic debris |
| Tranexamic acid | Antifibrinolytic reduces operative blood loss without fetal toxicity |
| IV iron post‑op | Restores iron stores, avoids delayed anemia, improves postpartum recovery |

\*Cell saver should be used only when amniotic fluid contamination is unlikely; otherwise, discard salvaged blood.

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Mid‑trimester abdominal surgery is feasible** when a structured peri‑operative plan safeguards uteroplacental flow and minimizes uterine irritability.  
- **Progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg IM daily → caproate)** is a simple, low‑risk maneuver that can blunt surgery‑induced pre‑term labor.  
- **Goal‑directed anesthesia** – maintain MAP ≥ 65 mm Hg, normocapnia, use phenylephrine for hypotension, and avoid uterine stimulants.  
- **Intermittent fetal heart monitoring** in the second trimester provides an early warning system for fetal distress.  
- **Targeted antibiotics** based on culture and susceptibility treat maternal infection without compromising fetal safety.  
- **Blood product readiness** (cross‑matched PRBCs, IV iron, optional tranexamic acid) prepares the team for the most dreaded complication—hemorrhage—while preserving maternal and fetal outcomes.

In the reported case, these strategies converged to allow a successful **mid‑trimester myomectomy**, an uncomplicated pregnancy thereafter, and a healthy neonate delivered by elective cesarean at term. The case underscores that, with a multidisciplinary mindset and evidence‑based peri‑operative protocols, we can push the boundaries of what is surgically possible during pregnancy—without sacrificing the baby.



# Delivery Planning After Massive Myomectomy: Cesarean vs. Vaginal Birth  

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

When a 40‑year‑old, gravida 1 at 21 weeks suddenly presents with two giant abdominal masses—one 18 cm sub‑phrenic cystic lesion and another 9‑cm pelvic cystic mass—the first thought is “endometrioma” or “ovarian tumour”. The reality? Both turned out to be **uterine leiomyomas** (a massive pedunculated mass and a large intramural‑subserosal myoma). The surgical team excised the cranial mass, left the lower one in situ, and later delivered the baby by elective Cesarean section on the same scar.  

What makes this story a teaching gold‑mine is the **decision‑making crossroads**: after a massive myomectomy in pregnancy, do we aim for a vaginal delivery (risking uterine rupture or obstructed labor) or schedule a Cesarean (potentially adding surgical morbidity)? This scenario forces us to weigh **myoma size**, **location**, **scar integrity**, and **future reproductive plans**—topics that remain hotly debated in the obstetric literature.

## The Anatomical and Physiological Stakes  

- **Size matters**: Leiomyomas can grow ~7 % per week in early pregnancy, often reaching 8–10 cm by the third trimester. In this case, the residual posterior myoma measured >7 cm after delivery.  
- **Location is destiny**: Posterior or lower‑segment myomas sit right in the path of the fetal head. They can act as a mechanical “doorstop” during the expulsive phase, increasing the risk of **prolonged second stage**, **uterine rupture**, or **uterine dehiscence**.  
- **Scar anatomy after myomectomy**: A myomectomy performed via a longitudinal xipho‑pubic laparotomy leaves a transverse uterine incision that is **not a classical low transverse cesarean**. Scar strength is unknown, and the uterus has already endured a “double hit” (pregnancy + surgery).  

All these variables tip the scales toward a **Cesarean delivery** in many high‑risk cases, but the literature is not unanimous.

## What the Evidence Says  

### Concurrent Cesarean‑Myomectomy  
- **Zhao et al., multicenter cohort (2022)**: No difference in postpartum hemoglobin or transfusion rates between women undergoing Cesarean + myomectomy vs. Cesarean alone, but operative time and length of stay were higher in the combined group.  
- **Sakinci et al., retrospective (2021)**: Myoma size correlated positively with hospital stay (p = 0.01) and inversely with gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035). Mean myoma size 8.3 ± 4.1 cm. No increase in maternal morbidity when myomectomy was done at the time of Cesarean.  

### Vaginal Delivery after Myomectomy  
- **Systematic review (2020, 12 studies, 1,043 pregnancies)**: Vaginal delivery was achieved in 61 % of cases where the largest myoma was <5 cm and not located in the lower uterine segment. Complication rates (uterine rupture, postpartum hemorrhage) rose sharply when myomas >8 cm or were posterior/inferior.  
- **Uterine rupture risk**: Reported rates after ante‑partum myomectomy range from 0.5 % to 3 %—higher than the 0.2 % baseline for classical Cesarean scars, but still low enough that many providers consider vaginal delivery safe if the myoma is small and anterior.  

### Imaging and Scar Assessment  
- **Transvaginal sonography with color Doppler** can estimate scar thickness >2 mm, but its predictive value for rupture after myomectomy is limited.  
- **MRI without contrast** (as used in the case) is safe after the first trimester and helps map the residual myoma’s relationship to the cervical canal and lower uterine segment—critical for labor planning.  

## Decision‑Tree in Practice  

Below is a practical algorithm—feel free to adapt it to your unit’s resources and patient preferences.

1. **Assess residual myoma burden**  
   - **Largest dimension ≤5 cm** AND **anterior wall** → Consider trial of labor (TOL).  
   - **5–8 cm** OR **posterior/lower‑segment** → High‑risk TOL; discuss Cesarean.  
   - **>8 cm** → Recommend Cesarean; uterine rupture risk outweighs benefits of vaginal birth.  

2. **Scar evaluation**  
   - **Scar thickness ≥2 mm** on TV‑US + **no dehiscence** → Cesarean possible, but still counsel on rupture risk.  
   - **Scar <2 mm** or **inconclusive** → Strongly favor Cesarean.  

3. **Maternal factors**  
   - **Obesity (BMI > 30)**, **anemia**, **previous uterine surgery** → Lower threshold for Cesarean.  
   - **Desire for future fertility**, **preference for vaginal birth**, **stable hemodynamics** → May tilt toward TOL if size/location is favorable.  

4. **Fetal considerations**  
   - **Non‑reassuring tracing**, **growth restriction**, **malpresentation** → Cesarean regardless of myoma.  

5. **Shared decision‑making**  
   - Explain absolute risks (uterine rupture 0.5–3 % vs. Cesarean‑related morbidity), benefits (shorter recovery, avoiding operative delivery), and uncertainties (scar strength after myomectomy).  

## Take‑Home Pearls  

- **Size >8 cm or posterior/low‑segment location = Cesarean** unless patient strongly prefers vaginal delivery and consents to the increased rupture risk.  
- **Non‑contrast MRI** is the safest way to map residual myomas after 12 weeks gestation; it guides both obstetric and surgical planning.  
- **Progesterone support** (IM 100 mg daily intra‑operatively, then weekly depot) can help maintain pregnancy after myomectomy, as demonstrated in the case.  
- **Iron deficiency anemia** is common with large myomas; correct it early (IV iron, transfusion) to avoid peri‑operative decompensation.  
- **Multidisciplinary care** (obstetrics, maternal‑fetal medicine, surgical oncology, anesthesiology) is essential for optimal outcomes.  

## Bottom Line  

Massive myomectomy in pregnancy forces a **personalized delivery plan**. The combination of **massive size**, **posterior location**, and **concern for uterine scar integrity** in the presented case justified an **elective Cesarean** on the same incision—a decision that yielded an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.  

When you encounter a pregnant patient with a residual myoma after myomectomy, run the numbers: **how big is it, where is it, what does the scar look like, and what does the patient want?** Let those answers drive a clear, evidence‑based recommendation—whether that lands you in the operating theatre for a planned C‑section or walking the patient down the labor ward with a well‑fitted birth plan.



# Pregnancy‑Associated Respiratory Compromise from Abdominal Mass Effect: Recognizing and Managing Dyspnea  

## Why This Case Stood Out  

Most of us think of dyspnea in pregnancy as a cardio‑pulmonary problem—pulmonary embolism, asthma, anemia, or even COVID‑19. Rarely do we pause to consider that a gigantic intra‑abdominal mass can mechanically “steal” diaphragmatic excursion and push the lungs into a restrictive pattern. In the case reported by De Nola et al., a 42‑year‑old primigravida in her 21st week of gestation presented with left‑upper‑quadrant pain **and** shortness of breath that dramatically improved when she was placed semi‑upright. Imaging revealed two massive uterine leiomyomas, one 18 cm in the left sub‑phrenic space that was directly compressing the diaphragm. The patient ultimately required a laparotomy to excise the pedunculated, twisted leiomyoma, after which her respiratory mechanics normalized.  

This scenario forces us to remember that large abdominal masses can cause a **mechanical restrictive lung physiology** that is entirely reversible with appropriate obstetric‑surgical management. Recognizing the pattern early can prevent unnecessary work‑ups, avoid fetal compromise, and guide timely surgical referral.

## Pathophysiology: How a Growing Belly “Squeezes” the Lungs  

1. **Diaphragmatic Elevation** – An intra‑abdominal mass occupies the sub‑phrenic space, pushing the diaphragm cephalad. The functional residual capacity (FRC) drops, and tidal volumes fall.  
2. **Reduced Chest Wall Compliance** – The expanding uterus and any additional pelvic mass restrict rib cage movement, compounding the restrictive effect.  
3. **Ventilation‑Perfusion Mismatch** – Basal alveoli become under‑ventilated, leading to mild hypoxemia that can be mistaken for pulmonary disease.  
4. **Altered Respiratory Drive** – Pregnancy already raises minute ventilation; any further reduction in lung volumes can trigger a subjective sense of “air hunger.”  

In the reported case, the patient’s oxygen saturations remained > 96 % on room air, yet she reported a VAS dyspnea score of 5 that dropped to 1 once she was semi‑upright, highlighting the mechanical nature of her symptoms.

## Clinical Assessment: Spotting the Mechanical Component  

When a pregnant patient presents with dyspnea, follow the usual “ABCDE” algorithm, but add a focused **mechanical screen**:

- **History**: Ask about the **timing** of symptom onset relative to abdominal growth, positional relief (sitting up, semi‑recumbent), and any associated pain or fullness.  
- **Physical Exam**:  
  - Look for **abdominal distension** disproportionate to gestational age.  
  - Palpate for **large, firm masses** in the upper abdomen or pelvis.  
  - Listen for **reduced breath sounds** at the bases and assess diaphragmatic excursion (use percussion or bedside ultrasound if available).  
- **Imaging**:  
  - **Point‑of‑care ultrasound** can quickly identify cystic or solid masses and estimate size.  
  - **Non‑contrast MRI** (1.5 T after first trimester) provides superior soft tissue delineation without ionizing radiation.  
- **Laboratory**: Routine labs (CBC, BMP) to rule out anemia or infection; tumor markers (CA‑125) are rarely decisive but may help with differential diagnosis.  

In the case, the ultrasound showed two unilocular cystic masses (18 × 12 × 7 cm and 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm) with peripheral vascularity. MRI confirmed the upper mass occupied the left sub‑phrenic space and was pedunculated, explaining the diaphragmatic compression.

## Positioning Strategies: A Simple, Yet Powerful, Intervention  

Before jumping to invasive diagnostics or surgery, try **positional therapy**:

- **Semi‑upright or sitting position**: Elevates the diaphragm slightly, reduces intra‑abdominal pressure, and often provides immediate symptom relief.  
- **Left lateral decubitus**: Shifts the gravid uterus away from the inferior vena cava and may improve venous return and lung volumes.  
- **Avoid supine positioning**: In late second/third trimester, supine can exacerbate aortocaval compression and diaphragmatic splinting.  

In the reported patient, a semi‑orthostatic stance reduced her VAS dyspnea from 5 to 1, reinforcing the mechanical nature of her respiratory limitation.

## When to Escalate: Indications for Surgical Intervention  

Most uterine leiomyomas are managed expectantly, but certain red‑flags tip the balance toward surgery even in pregnancy:

| Indication | Rationale |
|------------|-----------|
| **Severe, refractory dyspnea** despite positioning and analgesia | Ongoing restrictive physiology can compromise maternal oxygenation and fetal growth. |
| **Rapid mass growth** on serial ultrasounds | Suggests torsion, necrosis, or impending rupture. |
| **Pain unresponsive to medical management** | May indicate ischemia of the mass (e.g., twisted pedicle) or infection. |
| **Evidence of infection** (fever, leukocytosis, positive cultures) | As in this case, a twisted, leaking leiomyoma can seed infection. |
| **Imminent airway compromise** (e.g., inability to maintain SpO₂ > 94 % on RA) | Maternal hypoxia directly threatens the fetus. |

In the De Nola case, the combination of debilitating pain, worsening dyspnea, low‑grade fever, and imaging evidence of a large, pedunculated, potentially torsed leiomyoma met several of these criteria, prompting a **planned laparotomy at 21 weeks + 6 days**.

## Surgical Pearls for the Pregnant Patient  

1. **Multidisciplinary Planning** – Involve obstetrics, maternal‑fetal medicine, anesthesia, and surgery from day 1.  
2. **Gestational Age Consideration** – After the first trimester, the risk of spontaneous abortion drops, making surgery safer.  
3. **Approach Choice** –  
   - **Laparotomy** offers rapid access for massive or pedunculated lesions, especially when the mass is high in the abdomen.  
   - **Laparoscopy** (Hasson technique) can be considered for smaller, anterior lesions in the early second trimester, but beware of limited working space and CO₂ insufflation effects.  
4. **Fetal Monitoring** – Continuous intra‑operative fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring is mandatory once fetal viability is reached (≥ 23 weeks).  
5. **Hemostatic Strategies** – Use of uterine artery clamps, careful pedicle ligation, and prophylactic progesterone (as done in the case) may reduce postoperative bleeding and support the luteal phase.  
6. **Post‑operative Care** – Monitor maternal oxygenation, correct anemia aggressively (IV iron, PRBC transfusion if needed), and maintain a low‑threshold for fetal growth surveillance.  

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Mechanical dyspnea** from large intra‑abdominal masses in pregnancy is a real, reversible entity; always assess the abdomen when a pregnant patient complains of shortness of breath.  
- **Positional relief** (semi‑upright or left lateral) is a quick bedside test that can differentiate mechanical from cardiopulmonary causes.  
- **Imaging**—point‑of‑care US followed by non‑contrast MRI—provides the necessary anatomic detail without radiation.  
- **Surgical intervention** should be considered when dyspnea is severe, mass growth is rapid, pain persists, or infection is suspected, even in the second trimester.  
- **Multidisciplinary care** and intra‑operative fetal monitoring are essential to preserve both maternal and fetal outcomes.  

By keeping the “big mass = less room for the lungs” equation in mind, we can prevent unnecessary maternal anxiety, avoid extensive cardiac work‑ups, and, when indicated, intervene surgically to restore comfortable breathing for both mother and baby.
