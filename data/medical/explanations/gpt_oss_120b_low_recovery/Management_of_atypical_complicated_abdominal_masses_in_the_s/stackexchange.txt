Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] What imaging modalities are considered safe and most informative for evaluating large abdominal or pelvic masses in the second trimester of pregnancy, and why is contrast‑enhanced MRI generally avoided?

**Imaging large abdominal or pelvic masses in the 2nd trimester – what we can use safely and what we avoid**

In pregnancy the radiologic work‑up must obey two rules: (1) avoid ionising radiation to the fetus, and (2) avoid agents that cross the placenta in amounts that could be toxic.  For a large intra‑abdominal or pelvic mass the two modalities that meet these criteria are **(i) gray‑scale and colour‑Doppler ultrasonography** and **(ii) non‑contrast magnetic‑resonance imaging (MRI) performed on a 1.5‑T scanner**.  Both were employed in the case reported by De Nola et al.

| Modality | Safety in pregnancy | What it adds |
|----------|--------------------|--------------|
| **Transabdominal / trans‑vaginal US with colour Doppler** | No ionising radiation; equipment is universally available; can be repeated without risk. | Determines size, cystic vs solid composition, vascular pedicles, relationship to uterus/ovaries, and guides bimanual findings. In the case, US identified two unilocular cystic lesions with peripheral flow and suggested a possible cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma. |
| **Non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T, no gadolinium)** | No ionising radiation; static magnetic fields up to 1.5 T are considered safe after the first trimester.  Gadolinium‑based contrast agents are the only MRI component that is contraindicated (see below). | Provides superior soft‑tissue contrast, delineates the exact anatomic origin of a mass (uterine vs adnexal), defines wall thickness, internal septations and any solid enhancing components that cannot be seen on US.  In the case, non‑contrast MRI clarified that the cranial mass was a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma and showed the continuity of the caudal lesion with a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma. |
| **CT or plain radiography** | Uses ionising radiation; fetal dose, even when low, is avoided unless the benefit is overwhelming (e.g., acute trauma). | Not used for elective evaluation of pelvic masses in pregnancy. |
| **Contrast‑enhanced MRI (gadolinium‑based agents)** | Gadolinium chelates cross the placental barrier and enter the fetal circulation.  Animal data show fetal toxicity and human registries have linked first‑trimester exposure to rheumatologic, inflammatory, and dermal malformations, as well as a small but real increase in stillbirth/neonatal death.  Consequently, ACR, ACOG and ESMO guidelines advise **against gadolinium use in pregnancy unless the diagnostic benefit is absolutely crucial and cannot be obtained by other means**. | In the reported case the team deliberately omitted gadolinium; the non‑contrast study already gave sufficient anatomic detail to plan surgery, so the risks of contrast outweighed any incremental information. |

### Why contrast‑enhanced MRI is avoided
1. **Placental transfer** – Gadolinium freely crosses the placenta and the fetus lacks mature renal clearance, leading to prolonged exposure.
2. **Potential fetal toxicity** – Reports of fetal growth restriction, skeletal abnormalities, and stillbirth have been associated with gadolinium exposure, though causality is not definitive; the precautionary principle prevails.
3. **Lack of added value for most obstetric indications** – For characterising leiomyomas, endometriomas, or simple cystic masses, the intrinsic tissue contrast of T1/T2 and fat‑sat sequences is usually adequate.  The case demonstrated that non‑contrast MRI successfully differentiated a pedunculated leiomyoma from an ovarian cystic lesion.
4. **Guideline consensus** – ACR and ACOG state that gadolinium‑enhanced studies should only be performed when absolutely necessary (e.g., suspected malignant infiltration where treatment decisions hinge on contrast enhancement).

### Practical take‑home points for the bedside clinician
- **First‑line**: high‑resolution transabdominal (and when needed, transvaginal) ultrasound with colour Doppler.
- **If ultrasound is equivocal** (large size, unclear uterine‑adnexal origin, need to assess relationship to placenta), order a **non‑contrast MRI** on a 1.5‑T system after 13 weeks’ gestation.
- **Never** use CT, plain radiographs, or gadolinium‑enhanced MRI for routine evaluation of pelvic masses in pregnancy unless a life‑threatening condition mandates it and no safer alternative exists.
- **Document** fetal heart rate before and after the MRI (as per institutional protocol) to reassure both patient and team.

In the case of the 21‑week‑pregnant woman with two massive abdominal lesions, the combination of ultrasound and a non‑contrast MRI provided sufficient diagnostic confidence to proceed with a targeted laparotomy while preserving fetal safety—illustrating the optimal, evidence‑based imaging pathway for large abdominal/pelvic masses in the second trimester.

### [Pathophysiology] In a pregnant patient with an elevated CA‑125 but normal levels of other tumor markers, how should the clinician interpret this finding, and what benign conditions commonly cause modest CA‑125 elevations during pregnancy?

**Answer – Interpreting an Isolated CA‑125 Rise in Pregnancy and the Benign Causes to Consider**

In a pregnant woman who has a modestly elevated CA‑125 (e.g., 41 U/mL) while all other tumor markers are normal, the result should be viewed as **nonspecific and physiologically plausible**, not as evidence of malignancy.  The key points are:

1. **Physiologic variation of CA‑125 in pregnancy**  
   - CA‑125 rises in the **first trimester** (median ≈ 23 U/mL) and again in the **third trimester** (median ≈ 21 U/mL).  Values in the **second trimester are normally lower** (median ≈ 14 U/mL).  A level of 41 U/mL, although above the usual 35‑U/mL cut‑off used for ovarian cancer, is only modestly elevated and can be seen in a subset of normal second‑trimester pregnancies, as illustrated in the case report where the patient’s CA‑125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks without any malignant pathology.

2. **Benign conditions that raise CA‑125 during pregnancy**  
   The marker is secreted by mesothelial cells and by tissues that undergo inflammation or rapid growth.  The most common **non‑malignant** sources of a modest CA‑125 elevation in pregnancy are:
   - **Decidualised endometriomas** – endometriotic cysts that undergo hormonal transformation in pregnancy; they may produce CA‑125 levels in the 30–50 U/mL range.  
   - **Uterine leiomyomas (fibroids)** – especially those undergoing **cystic degeneration, necrosis, or torsion** (as in the presented case). Degenerating myomas can release CA‑125 into the circulation.  
   - **Physiologic decidual reaction of the peritoneum** – the “pregnancy‑associated peritoneal inflammation” that accompanies a large uterine size can modestly raise CA‑125.  
   - **Other benign pelvic inflammation** (pelvic inflammatory disease, tubo‑ovarian abscess, or even a urinary tract infection) can contribute, though these are less common in the setting of an otherwise sterile peritoneal cavity.

3. **Clinical implication**  
   - **Do not use an isolated CA‑125 value to trigger immediate oncologic surgery** in pregnancy.  The case illustrates that, despite the slight rise, the definitive diagnosis was a **pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma** with necrosis, not an ovarian malignancy.  
   - **Serial measurements** are more informative than a single value.  A stable or slowly rising CA‑125 without accompanying imaging features of malignancy (solid‑cystic masses with papillary projections, thick irregular septa, ascites, lymphadenopathy) points toward a benign process.  
   - **Imaging** (high‑resolution ultrasound with color Doppler, followed by non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester) remains the cornerstone for distinguishing degenerated fibroids from true adnexal tumors.  In the discussed patient, the imaging showed unilocular cystic lesions with peripheral vascularity and thick walls, typical of degenerating leiomyomas rather than ovarian cancer.

4. **Management strategy**  
   - **Observe** with repeat US/MRI and CA‑125 if the patient is clinically stable.  
   - **Proceed to surgery only** if there are obstetric complications (pain, torsion, risk of rupture, progressive respiratory compromise) or if imaging suggests a malignant pattern.  In the case, the decision for laparotomy was driven by pain, dyspnoea, and suspicion of torsion, not by the CA‑125 alone.

**Take‑home message for trainees:**  
A modest, isolated elevation of CA‑125 in the second trimester is **most often benign**, reflecting either decidualised endometriosis or degenerating uterine fibroids—both common in this age group.  Interpretation must be integrated with a careful physical exam and pregnancy‑safe imaging; tumor markers alone do not dictate operative intervention.  Serial trends, not a single cut‑off, and the overall clinical picture guide the decision‑making process.

### [Differential Diagnosis] List the key sonographic and MRI features that help distinguish a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma from a decidualised endometrioma in a pregnant woman.

**Pedunculated cystic leiomyoma vs decidualised endometrioma in pregnancy – key imaging discriminators**

| Modality | Feature | Pedunculated cystic leiomyoma (case) | Decidualised endometrioma (typical) |
|----------|---------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Ultrasound (gray‑scale & colour Doppler)** | **Morphology** – shape, borders, internal architecture | • Large, unilocular‑cystic‑appearing mass with *regular* smooth margins but *heterogeneous* internal echoes because of cystic degeneration. <br>• Presence of a **pedicle** (when visible) connecting the cyst to the uterine serosa (often seen as a thin, hypoechoic band). | • True endometrioma is “chocolate cyst” – homogeneous low‑level internal echoes, posterior acoustic shadowing, **no stalk**. |
| | **Vascular pattern** – Doppler flow | • Prominent peripheral vascularity and **central feeding vessels** coursing through the stalk; vessels may be hypertrophied (as described for both masses in the case). <br>• Pulsatile flow often seen at the junction with uterine wall. | • Decidualised endometrioma shows **peripheral rim‑type flow** only; absent or minimal central feeding vessels; Doppler is usually low‑grade. |
| | **Relation to uterus** | • “Continuity” with myometrial tissue on sagittal/transverse sweep; the cyst drapes over the uterine surface and may appear to **compress** rather than displace the uterine wall. In the case the lower mass was adherent to a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma; the upper mass, once examined surgically, was found to arise from a uterine pedicle. | • Ovarian or ad‑nexal location, separate from the uterine serosa; a clear *cleavage plane* between ovary and uterus. |
| | **Mobility & “sliding sign”** | • Limited mobility because the stalk anchors the lesion; on gentle probe pressure the lesion **does not glide** away from the uterus. | • Endometriomas are usually mobile relative to the uterus (positive sliding sign). |
| **MRI (non‑contrast, T1/T2 fat‑sat)** | **Wall thickness & signal** | • **Thickened wall** (≈1–1.5 cm) that is *heterogeneous* on both T1‑ and T2‑weighted images because of fibrous tissue and cystic degeneration. <br>• The wall may show low‑signal intensity on T2 (fibrous component) interspersed with high‑signal cystic areas. | • Endometrioma wall is thin (< 5 mm) and the cyst content is **high T1‑signal** (blood products) with **low T2 “shading”**; wall remains thin and smooth. |
| | **Internal contents** | • Mixed fluid signal: predominantly *hyper‑intense* on T2 (simple fluid) with *focal hyper‑intense* solid nodules on T1‑fat‑sat representing hemorrhagic or necrotic foci; may be inhomogeneous because of cystic degeneration. | • Classic “chocolate” cyst: **uniformly high T1‑signal** (blood‑laden) and **markedly low T2‑signal** (shading). No solid nodular components. |
| | **Presence of a stalk** | • On sagittal or coronal planes, a **linear low‑signal band** extending from the cyst to the uterine serosa, often tapering – this is the pedicle. In the case the MRI showed continuity of the caudal cyst with a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma, and the upper mass was later proven to have a pedicle. | • No low‑signal stalk; the lesion is confined to the ovary or adnexa. |
| | **Adjacency to myometrium** | • “Uterine‑contiguity sign”: the mass abuts the myometrium with loss of an intervening fat plane and may cause a subtle bulge of the uterine serosa; the interface appears **isointense to myometrium** on T1/T2, indicating true attachment. | • A clear fat plane separates the ovarian cyst from the uterus; uterine contour is normal. |
| | **Vascular “flow voids”** (seen even without contrast) | • Prominent flow voids within the pedicle or at the periphery of the lesion, reflecting the hypertrophied vessels described on Doppler. | • Little or no flow voids; only faint peripheral vessels may be seen. |
| | **Diffusion/ADC** (if performed) | • May show **restricted diffusion** in solid nodular components (fibrous tissue, necrotic foci). | • Endometrioma fluid typically **does not restrict** (high ADC), whereas solid implants (if any) would. |

### How the case fits the pattern
* **US** showed two large unilocular cystic masses with *peripheral vascularisation* and, for the lower lesion, an *adherent intramural‑subserosal myoma*—the classic clue that the cystic component is part of a degenerating leiomyoma rather than an ovarian cyst.  
* **MRI** demonstrated **thick (1.4 cm) walls**, heterogeneous fluid, and **focal hyper‑intense solid components** on T1/T2 fat‑sat, all aligning with cystic degeneration of a leiomyoma. The “continuity” with the posterior uterine myoma and lack of a clean cleavage plane further supported a myomatous origin.  
* The **absence of a thin, uniform low‑signal rim** and *shading* on T2 made a decidualised endometrioma less likely, despite the mildly elevated CA‑125.

### Bottom line for the bedside learner
When confronted with a cystic pelvic mass in pregnancy:

1. **Look for a stalk or continuity with the uterus** on both US (hypoechoic band, limited mobility) and MRI (low‑signal linear connection).  
2. **Assess wall thickness and internal signal heterogeneity** – thick, fibrous walls with mixed fluid favour a degenerated leiomyoma; thin walls with uniform high T1/low T2 (“shading”) favour an endometrioma.  
3. **Evaluate Doppler/flow voids** – prominent feeding vessels through a pedicle point to a pedunculated myoma; peripheral rim flow only suggests an endometrioma.  

These imaging hallmarks allow a confident distinction, guiding timely surgical decision‑making while preserving the pregnancy.

### [Management] What are the principal indications for surgical intervention (myomectomy) of uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy, and how do these indications apply to the presented case?

**Principal indications for myomectomy during pregnancy**  
(NEJM Clinical Pearl – Management of Leiomyoma in the Pregnant Patient)

| Indication | Rationale | How it fits into the case |
|------------|-----------|---------------------------|
| **1. Acute obstetric or gynecologic complication** – torsion, red‑degeneration with severe pain, necrosis, rupture, infection, or uncontrolled bleeding. | Pedunculated or subserosal leiomyomas that twist on their stalk become ischemic; the resulting necrosis can precipitate fever, peritonitis, and hemorrhage. Prompt removal eliminates the source of inflammation and prevents maternal hemodynamic compromise. | The upper left‑sided mass was a **pedunculated leiomyoma with a twisted stalk** that was leaking and had caused fever, leukocytosis and pain. The intra‑operative finding of ischaemic necrosis confirmed the complication, satisfying this indication. |
| **2. Symptomatic mass that is refractory to conservative therapy** – disabling pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, or rapid growth that threatens uterine expansion or fetal compromise. | When analgesia, positioning, or medical therapy cannot control symptoms, the risk of progressive compression (e.g., restrictive lung physiology, impaired uterine‑placental blood flow) outweighs the surgical risk. | The patient had **severe, persistent abdominal pain (VAS 5 → 1 only after limited relief) and restrictive‑type dyspnoea** caused by the 18‑cm mass occupying the left sub‑phrenic space and compressing the diaphragm. Conservative measures (paracetamol, semi‑orthostatic positioning) were insufficient, meeting this criterion. |
| **3. Suspicion of malignancy or atypical radiologic features** – rapid increase in size, solid‑cystic components, hyper‑vascularity, or rising tumor markers that cannot be excluded without tissue. | Definitive histology is required to rule out sarcoma or ovarian malignancy; delaying surgery could permit tumor progression and worsen obstetric outcomes. | Imaging showed a **large, highly vascularised, mixed solid‑cystic lesion** with thick walls and an increasing CA‑125 (41.8 U/mL). Although the marker is non‑specific in pregnancy, the combination of size, vascularity and atypical appearance justified operative exploration. |
| **4. Mass that will obstruct delivery** – large intramural/subserosal leiomyoma in the lower uterine segment or isthmus that would preclude vaginal birth or increase risk of uterine rupture. | Elective removal (or, more commonly, planned cesarean delivery) prevents intra‑partum obstruction and postpartum hemorrhage. | The caudal mass (≈9 cm) was adherent to a posterior intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma located near the uterine torus. Although it was left in situ during pregnancy to avoid uterine manipulation, its **size and position predicted obstruction of the fetal head**, leading the team to schedule an elective CS at term. |

### Application to the presented patient

- **Acute complication**: The **pedunculated, twisted leiomyoma** fulfilled the first indication; removal eliminated the source of necrosis, fever, and pain.
- **Refractory symptoms**: Persistent pain and dyspnoea despite optimal medical therapy satisfied the second indication.
- **Oncologic concern**: The size (> 15 cm), heterogeneous MRI appearance, and rising CA‑125 met the third indication, prompting operative histologic confirmation.
- **Obstructive potential**: The decision to leave the lower pelvic myoma in situ but plan a **cesarean delivery** reflected the fourth indication—recognition that the residual mass would impede a vaginal birth.

Thus, the surgery performed—**selective myomectomy of the pedunculated, symptomatic, and potentially necrotic upper mass**—was precisely aligned with established criteria for operative intervention on uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy. The conservative approach to the lower myoma (observation until delivery) further illustrates adherence to the principle of **minimizing uterine trauma** while still addressing the obstetric implications of a large residual leiomyoma. This balanced strategy resulted in an uneventful continuation of pregnancy, successful term cesarean delivery, and a favorable maternal‑neonatal outcome.

### [Morning Report] During intra‑operative management of a pregnant patient undergoing myomectomy, which fetal monitoring and maternal pharmacologic strategies are recommended to minimize the risk of preterm labor and fetal compromise?

**Intra‑operative fetal monitoring and maternal pharmacologic prophylaxis to limit pre‑term labor in a second‑trimester myomectomy**

1. **Fetal monitoring**  
   * **Baseline assessment** – Before the incision the obstetric team must obtain a **baseline fetal heart rate (FHR) tracing** by trans‑vaginal or trans‑abdominal Doppler ultrasound. In the case report the surgeons “checked fetal cardiac activity” before beginning the laparotomy, consistent with current recommendations that a reliable baseline is essential for intra‑operative comparison.  
   * **Continuous or intermittent monitoring** – Because the uterus is still relatively small at 21 weeks, a **continuous external Doppler** probe can be placed and recorded throughout the procedure. If positioning or surgical access makes continuous monitoring unsafe, the standard is **intermittent monitoring every 15‑20 min** (or after any major manipulation of the uterus) with a handheld Doppler. The goal is to detect bradycardia or decelerations promptly; any sustained abnormality should prompt immediate obstetric evaluation and consideration of emergent delivery if gestational age permits.  
   * **Documentation** – All pre‑, intra‑, and post‑operative FHR recordings must be saved in the operative note, as emphasized by the multicenter series cited in the discussion (Zhao et al.).

2. **Maternal pharmacologic strategies**  

   | Goal | Recommended agent (dose & timing) | Rationale in this case |
   |------|-----------------------------------|------------------------|
   | **Uterine quiescence (prevent pre‑term labor)** | **Progesterone** – 100 mg IM immediately after skin incision (as done in the case) and then 100 mg IM daily until discharge; thereafter depot hydroxy‑progesterone caproate 341 mg IM every 3 days until 37 wks. | Progesterone supports the luteal phase, decreases myometrial contractility, and has a well‑established safety profile in the second trimester. The case report used exactly this regimen and reported no episodes of intra‑operative uterine activity. |
   | **Rescue tocolysis (if uterine contractions develop)** | **Nifedipine** 10 mg PO loading, then 20 mg q8h (or IV nicardipine infusion if oral route unavailable). | Calcium‑channel blockers are first‑line for acute uterine contractions; they are safe for the fetus and do not interfere with surgical hemostasis. |
   | **Antibiotic prophylaxis** | **Cefazolin** 2 g IV 30 min before incision (as performed), then 1 g IV q8h for 24‑48 h; continue culture‑directed therapy (e.g., clarithromycin) if infection is present. | Reduces the risk of postoperative infection, which could precipitate uterine irritability and pre‑term labor. |
   | **Anemia correction** (indirectly helps uterine oxygenation) | **IV iron** (e.g., ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and blood transfusion if Hb < 8 g/dL. | Improves maternal oxygen‑carrying capacity, decreasing fetal hypoxia‑driven uterine activity. |
   | **Avoidance of uterine irritants** | No oxytocin, avoid prostaglandins, NSAIDs, or high‑dose beta‑agonists intra‑operatively. | These agents can trigger contractions; the case report deliberately omitted them. |

3. **Practical intra‑operative workflow (derived from the case)**  

   1. **Pre‑operative** – Review obstetric ultrasound, confirm fetal viability, start prophylactic 100 mg IM progesterone.  
   2. **Induction of anesthesia** – Use agents with minimal uterine effect (e.g., propofol, short‑acting opioids). Maintain normocapnia (PaCO₂ ≈ 35‑45 mm Hg) to avoid fetal acidosis.  
   3. **Positioning** – Semi‑recumbent with left uterine displacement to prevent aortocaval compression.  
   4. **Monitoring** – Baseline FHR, then continuous Doppler if feasible; otherwise intermittent checks every 15 min and after any uterine manipulation.  
   5. **Pharmacology** – Administer the second dose of IM progesterone intra‑operatively (as in the report) and have nifedipine ready for any uterine activity.  
   6. **Post‑operative** – Continue daily IM progesterone until discharge, then switch to depot caproate until 37 weeks; monitor FHR daily with obstetric ultrasound; watch for signs of pre‑term labor (uterine tenderness, cervical change).  

**Bottom line:** In a second‑trimester myomectomy, fetal well‑being is best safeguarded by **baseline and frequent Doppler FHR assessment** throughout the case, while maternal uterine quiescence is achieved with **immediate and ongoing progesterone supplementation** (100 mg IM daily, followed by depot caproate) and **readily available rescue tocolysis** (nifedipine). Antibiotic prophylaxis, correction of anemia, and avoidance of uterine irritants round out a strategy that, as illustrated in the presented case, can preserve pregnancy and yield an excellent neonatal outcome.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the physiologic changes in uterine leiomyoma size throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period, including the typical weekly growth rates and the expected regression after delivery.

**Physiologic growth and regression of uterine leiomyomas in pregnancy**

Uterine leiomyomas are hormonally responsive smooth‑muscle tumors that expand markedly when exposed to the high‐estrogen, high‑progesterone milieu of pregnancy. The case of a 40‑year‑old gravida at 21 weeks illustrates the typical kinetic pattern that has been documented in prospective ultrasound series.

| Pregnancy interval | Mean weekly increase in leiomyoma volume* | Comment |
|--------------------|--------------------------------------------|---------|
| 1st trimester → 2nd trimester | **≈ 6 % per week** (6.90 % overall; 6.12 % p < 0.001) | Growth is driven primarily by estrogen‑mediated smooth‑muscle hypertrophy and extracellular matrix deposition. |
| 2nd trimester → 3rd trimester | **≈ 3 % per week** (3.10 % ± 0.39 % p < 0.05) | Progesterone predominates; the slower rate reflects a plateau as the uterus reaches its maximal size and blood flow is redistributed to the placenta. |

*The percentages refer to *volume* change, not linear diameter, but for roughly spherical nodules the linear dimension rises by ~2 % per week in the first half of pregnancy and ~1 % per week thereafter.

#### Mechanisms

1. **Estrogen surge (weeks 4‑14).** Estrogen up‑regulates insulin‑like growth factor‑1 (IGF‑1) and promotes smooth‑muscle cell proliferation, accounting for the brisk early expansion.
2. **Progesterone dominance (mid‑gestation).** Progesterone promotes collagen synthesis and edema within the tumor stroma, leading to the classic “cystic degeneration” seen on ultrasound/MRI (as in the present case). This effect is less proliferative, hence the slower growth rate.
3. **Mechanical stretch.** Uterine enlargement itself stretches the myometrium, adding a component of passive enlargement.

#### Post‑partum involution

After delivery the hormone milieu reverses abruptly:

- **Estrogen and progesterone fall** within days.
- **Oxytocin‑mediated uterine contractions** compress the myoma, enhancing ischemia.
- **Matrix remodeling** (increased metalloproteinase activity) leads to rapid loss of the edema component.

The case report cites a prospective cohort that measured a **mean reduction of 44 % ± 36 % by 6 weeks postpartum** (p < 0.05). In practice:

- **Linear shrinkage** of 10‑15 % per week is common in the first month.
- **Residual size** after 6 weeks often mirrors the pre‑pregnancy baseline, especially for intramural or subserosal lesions that were ≤ 8 cm before conception (as in the residual 7.5‑cm myoma of the patient).

#### Clinical implications

- **Expectant management** is appropriate for most asymptomatic leiomyomas because they will tend to regress after delivery.  
- **Surgical intervention** during pregnancy is reserved for complications (torsion, necrosis, severe pain, obstruction) because the expected physiological growth (≈ 6 %/wk early, ≈ 3 %/wk later) can be anticipated and monitored.  
- **Post‑partum follow‑up** (US at 6 weeks) is essential to document involution and decide whether definitive surgery is needed.

In summary, uterine leiomyomas enlarge rapidly in the first half of pregnancy (≈ 6 % weekly), slow down in the later half (≈ 3 % weekly), and then regress dramatically after delivery, with a mean 44 % volume loss by 6 weeks postpartum. This kinetic profile underlies the conservative approach taken in the presented case, and it guides timing of any necessary surgical intervention.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the presence and location of large uterine leiomyomas influence the choice of mode of delivery, and why was elective cesarean delivery favored in this patient?

**Clinical Pearl – Large uterine leiomyomas and the choice of delivery mode**

**Key principle**  
When a pregnant woman harbors one or more sizable leiomyomas, the decision between vaginal birth and elective cesarean delivery rests on three anatomic variables:

1. **Size** – Leiomyomas > 5 cm, especially > 8 cm, are more likely to impede the descent of the fetal head, restrict the lower uterine segment, or cause mal‑presentation.  
2. **Location** –  
   * **Submucosal or lower‑segment (isthmic) myomas** directly interfere with cervical dilatation and fetal head engagement.  
   * **Posterior‑uterine or fundal myomas** can distort the uterine cavity and reduce uterine contractility, raising the risk of arrest of labour or uterine rupture.  
   * **Pedunculated or cervical‑adjacent myomas** may obstruct the vaginal canal or become injured during traction.  
3. **Number** – Multiple co‑existent fibroids compound the above problems and increase the likelihood of intra‑operative hemorrhage if a myomectomy is attempted at delivery.

When any of these factors predict a mechanical obstacle to the expulsive phase, or when the uterine wall is compromised (e.g., a large intramural myoma abutting the incision line), an elective cesarean section (CS) is the safest route.

**Application to the presented case**

- **Size** – The patient had two leiomyomas: an 18 × 12 × 7 cm pedunculated mass occupying the left sub‑phrenic space and a second 9.5 cm intramural‑subserosal mass in the posterior uterine wall (later left in situ). Both exceed the 5‑cm threshold that predicts difficulty with labour.
- **Location** –  
  * The **caudal myoma** was attached to the posterior uterine wall near the isthmus (“uterine torus”). At term, this region becomes the lower uterine segment; a bulky lesion here would prevent adequate thinning and dilatation, acting as a “hard stop” for the presenting part.  
  * The **cranial pedunculated leiomyoma** had already been removed, but its former vascular pedicle and the residual scar placed the uterus at higher risk for dehiscence if subjected to the strong traction forces of vaginal delivery.
- **Obstructive potential** – The residual 7.5‑cm posterior myoma would have acted as a fixed barrier to fetal head passage, raising the chance of prolonged labour, mal‑presentation, or uterine rupture. Imaging showed it was fixed to the posterior wall and not mobile enough to be displaced during descent.

Because the mechanical impediment could not be reliably overcome by a trial of labour, the multidisciplinary team elected an **elective cesarean delivery** on the same low‑transverse (longitudinal) incision used for the myomectomy. This choice:

1. **Avoided labour‑related uterine stress** on a scarred and partially myomectomized uterus.  
2. **Reduced the risk of intra‑operative hemorrhage** that can occur when a large posterior fibroid is compressed or torn during fetal descent.  
3. **Ensured a predictable operative field**; the existing laparotomy scar provided immediate access, minimizing operative time and preserving fetal wellbeing.

**Bottom line for trainees** – In pregnant patients with large (≥ 5 cm) leiomyomas, especially when the lesions are situated in the lower uterine segment, posterior wall, or near the cervical os, plan delivery by elective CS. The goal is to prevent obstructed labour, uterine rupture, and catastrophic hemorrhage, while providing a safe environment for both mother and neonate.
