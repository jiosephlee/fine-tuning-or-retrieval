Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the significance of allodynia localized to the scalp (vertex) in this case, and which headache syndrome does it most closely resemble?

**Answer – Why the vertex‑localized allodynia matters, and which headache entity it mimics**

The patient’s “coin‑sized” pain at the vertex that is provoked by the lightest touch (hair, a comb, even the weight of her own hair) is classic **cutaneous allodynia**. In the context of Chiari I malformation (CMI) this finding is not incidental; it reflects irritation of the **upper cervical dorsal root fibers and the termination of the greater occipital nerve (C2–C3)** that are being stretched or compressed by the descended cerebellar tonsils at the foramen magnum. The resulting sensitisation of these small‑fiber pathways produces a **localised scalp allodynia** that is often confined to a small area—exactly the pattern described in the case.

Why is this important for the clinician?

1. **Diagnostic clue to a brain‑stem/ posterior‑fossa process** – Scalp allodynia limited to the vertex is unusual for primary migraine or tension‑type headache, where the pain is more diffuse. When the allodynia is highly focal and reproducible with light touch, it points toward a **peripheral nerve irritation secondary to central compression**. In CMI, the tonsillar descent can tug on the C2 dorsal root entry zone, generating the “burning” vertex sensation.

2. **Helps distinguish CMI‑related headache from primary headache disorders** – The patient had previously been labelled with **nummular headache**, a primary headache that also produces a coin‑shaped painful area on the scalp. However, nummular headache is a diagnosis of exclusion; it lacks an anatomic lesion and typically does **not** improve with mechanical decompression (e.g., cutting hair short). In our case, the pain diminished when mechanical traction on the scalp was reduced, supporting a **secondary, compressive etiology** rather than a primary headache.

3. **Guides therapeutic direction** – Recognising that the scalp allodynia is a manifestation of CMI redirects treatment from repeated local scalp injections (which only give transient relief) toward addressing the underlying posterior‑fossa crowding—most commonly **posterior fossa decompression**. It also alerts the clinician to look for accompanying brain‑stem signs (e.g., dysphagia, central hypoventilation), which were indeed present.

**Which headache syndrome does it most closely resemble?**

The pattern of a **persistent, pressure‑like, sharply demarcated pain** at a single scalp area with **allodynia to light touch** is most reminiscent of **nummular headache** (also called “coin‑shaped headache”). Nummular headache is defined by:

- A single, round/oval painful area ≤ 4 cm in diameter.
- Pain that is constant or intermittent, often pressure‑like.
- Presence of cutaneous allodynia within the lesion.
- Absence of associated neurological deficits.

In the case report, the authors explicitly note that several physicians diagnosed her with nummular headache and treated her with local scalp injections. The key differentiator here is **the underlying cause**: in true nummular headache the scalp lesion is idiopathic, whereas in this patient the focal allodynia is a **secondary phenomenon** due to Chiari‑related compression of the C2/C3 dorsal root entry zone. Thus, while the clinical presentation **mirrors nummular headache**, the correct nosology is **“Chiari‑related vertex allodynia”**, a secondary headache that should prompt neuro‑imaging.

**Take‑home point for trainees**

When you encounter a patient with a tiny, well‑demarcated area of scalp pain that becomes painful to the slightest touch, think first of **primary nummular headache** but keep a low threshold to **search for secondary causes**—especially posterior‑fossa pathology—if any of the following are present:

- Associated brain‑stem symptoms (dysphagia, choking, unexplained hypoxia).
- Pain modulation by mechanical factors (e.g., relief after cutting hair short).
- Lack of durable response to local anesthetic injections.

In such scenarios, ordering a **MRI of the brain‑spine** can uncover Chiari I malformation, and early neurosurgical referral may prevent progression of both the headache and the more dangerous respiratory manifestations.
