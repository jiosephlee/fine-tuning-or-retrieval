## Why This Case Stands Out  

When a 40‑year‑old woman shows up at the clinic with “just” choking while eating and nightly “air‑hunger” episodes that she never feels, most physicians will chase a gastro‑oesophageal or ENT problem. Yet in this case the pulse‑oximeter at home was spitting out 70 % saturations while she was perfectly calm. The diagnostic needle finally landed on a **Chiari I malformation (CMI)** with an 11 mm tonsillar descent—a structural brainstem compression that masqueraded as an acquired “Ondine’s curse.”  

The lesson? CMI can present *almost exclusively* with sleep‑related respiratory failure, and standard outpatient work‑ups can miss it outright, especially when we rely on type III (home) sleep studies.  

---

## The Epidemiology of Central Sleep Apnoea in Chiari I  

*Chiari I* is not a rare anatomical variant—up to **1 % of adults** show >5 mm tonsillar descent on MRI. Only a fraction become symptomatic, but among those who do, **sleep‑disordered breathing is common**:  

* In a prospective series of 90 adult CMI patients, **≈50 %** had a sleep‑related breathing disorder.  
* Central apnoea index (CAI) ≥5 was documented in **≈7 %**, but pure central apnoea (no obstructive component) was seen in **only one patient**—highlighting how brainstem compression can produce a spectrum from mixed to pure central events.  

The case we’re dissecting had a **type III AHI of 6** (mostly central events). That number looks “mild” on paper, yet the patient experienced **recurrent desaturations to 70 %** without dyspnoea. The disconnect between the AHI and clinical severity is a hallmark of CMI‑related hypoventilation.

## Type III vs. Full Polysomnography: Where the Under‑estimation Happens  

### What Type III Gives You  

* Respiratory effort (chest/abdominal bands)  
* Airflow (nasal pressure or thermistor)  
* Pulse oximetry  
* Heart rate  

It **does not** record:

* EEG (sleep staging)  
* End‑tidal CO₂ or transcutaneous CO₂ (ventilatory drive)  
* Video (to see choking, arousal)  

Because AHI is calculated using **total recording time**, any wakeful minutes are counted as “sleep.” If a patient spends half the night awake (common in CMI with brainstem pain or dysphagia), the AHI is artificially lowered. Moreover, without CO₂ monitoring we miss **hypoventilation episodes** where airflow may be present but ventilation is inadequate—a classic feature of central hypoventilation syndromes.

### Why This Matters in Chiari  

* **Brainstem compression** can blunt the automatic respiratory drive while preserving voluntary breathing. During wakefulness the cortex compensates; during sleep the automatic drive fails, leading to long central apnoeas and profound desaturation.  
* Type III devices may register the **airflow interruption** but will under‑report its duration because the algorithm is tuned for obstructive events, not the prolonged “no‑effort” periods of central failure.  
* Absence of capnography means we cannot differentiate **hypoventilation** (elevated CO₂, normal airflow) from true apnoea, further clouding the picture.  

In our patient, a **type III AHI of 6** belied the reality of **repeated 70 % desats** and a central event proportion of **72 %**—a clinical scenario that would trigger urgent intervention if captured on a full PSG with capnography.

## Practical Screening for Chiara‑Related Central Apnoea  

1. **High‑yield history** – ask every CMI patient (or anyone with unexplained chronic occipital headache, dysphagia, or bulbar symptoms) about:  

   * Nighttime choking or coughing without feeling breathless.  
   * Episodes of “air‑hunger” that resolve with a sip of water or repositioning.  
   * Unexplained daytime fatigue disproportionate to AHI on prior home study.  

2. **Pulse‑ox overnight monitoring** – a simple finger‑ox for 2–3 nights can expose **asymptomatic desaturations**. A nadir <85 % should raise a red flag, regardless of reported AHI.  

3. **Early referral for full PSG** – if any of the above are positive, schedule an **in‑lab polysomnography** with:  

   * EEG (to accurately calculate sleep time).  
   * End‑tidal CO₂ or transcutaneous CO₂ (to detect hypoventilation).  
   * Video/audio (to capture choking, arousals).  

4. **Imaging correlation** – always review recent brain/neck MRI for the degree of tonsillar descent, crowding at the foramen magnum, or associated syrinx. A descent **>8 mm** and evident brainstem compression markedly increase the pre‑test probability of central events.

---

## Interpreting AHI in the Context of Brain‑Stem Compression  

* **Raw AHI is not enough** – look at the event composition. A high **central apnoea index (CAI)** (≥5) or a predominance of central events (>50 % of total) should tip you toward a neurological cause.  
* **Desaturation depth and duration** matter more than frequency. Two long central apnoeas with 30 % desats are clinically more ominous than ten brief obstructive events with 5 % desats.  
* **CO₂ trends** on capnography are the ultimate “wake‑up call.” A rising end‑tidal CO₂ plateau despite ongoing ventilation indicates loss of automatic drive.  

When the AHI looks modest but the **clinical picture is severe**, treat the patient, not the number.

## Peri‑Operative Considerations for Posterior Fossa Decompression  

If surgical decompression is on the table, anaesthetic and postoperative planning must account for the underlying respiratory instability.

| Aspect | Key Points |
|--------|------------|
| **Pre‑op assessment** | Full PSG with capnography; baseline arterial blood gas; document any nocturnal desaturations and CAI. |
| **Intra‑op monitoring** | Use capnography and BIS; avoid deep sedation that could further depress the already compromised automatic drive. |
| **Ventilation strategy** | Consider pressure‑support or volume‑assist modes that allow the patient to initiate breaths; avoid excessive tidal volumes that could raise intracranial pressure. |
| **Post‑op monitoring** | Overnight oximetry+CO₂ monitoring in a step‑down unit for at least 24 h; be ready to re‑intubate if central hypoventilation recurs. |
| **Long‑term follow‑up** | Repeat PSG at 3–6 months; assess for residual central events; track symptom resolution (headache, dysphagia, choking). |

Successful decompression often **normalises CSF flow** and can reverse central hypoventilation, but the improvement may be delayed. Close monitoring through the immediate postoperative period is non‑negotiable.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Chiari I malformation is an under‑appreciated cause of central sleep apnoea**; look for bulbar symptoms, chronic occipital headache, and unexplained nocturnal choking.  
- **Type III home sleep studies systematically underestimate the severity** of central hypoventilation in CMI because they lack EEG‑based sleep staging and CO₂ monitoring.  
- **A low AHI does not equal low risk**; focus on central event proportion, desaturation depth, and CO₂ trends.  
- **Full polysomnography with capnography should be the default** for any CMI patient with suspected sleep‑related breathing disorder.  
- **Peri‑operative planning for posterior fossa decompression requires meticulous respiratory surveillance**—both intra‑operatively and in the early postoperative window.  

When you encounter a patient who “chokes” in their sleep but never feels short of breath, remember: the brainstem might be “forgetting” to tell the cortex it needs air. A simple review of old head CTs, a pulse‑ox night, and a full PSG can unmask an occult Chiari‑related central hypoventilation—potentially steering the patient away from endless ENT work‑ups toward a definitive neurosurgical solution.  

Stay curious, keep your oximeter handy, and don’t let a “mild” AHI lull you into complacency.
