Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 3: ## 3. Diagnostic Reasoning and Advanced Neuro‑Imaging  

The diagnostic odyssey in this patient illustrates how a systematic re‑assessment of “normal” investigations can uncover a unifying structural cause. The key steps were (1) meticulous re‑evaluation of an old head CT, (2) targeted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the craniocervical junction, (3) careful interpretation of sleep‑monitoring data, and (4) exclusion of common mimics through focused laboratory and functional studies. Together these findings converged on a diagnosis of Chiari‑type I malformation (CMI) with acquired central hypoventilation (an Ondine‑type syndrome).

### Re‑evaluation of the historical non‑contrast CT head  

When the patient’s chronic choking, nocturnal desaturations, and long‑standing occipital headache remained unexplained, the care team retrieved a non‑contrast computed tomography (CT) scan performed 14 years earlier after a motor‑vehicle accident. The original radiology report had described the study as “normal,” yet a deliberate, senior‑radiologist review identified two subtle but pathognomonic features of CMI:  

1. **Crowded foramen magnum** – the posterior fossa appeared shallow, with the opisthion and foramen magnum almost in direct contact. The distance between the inner table of the occipital bone and the dorsal surface of the cerebellar tonsils was markedly reduced.  
2. **Loss of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction** – instead of the expected CSF cuff separating the cerebellar tonsils from the spinal canal, the tonsils were abutting the cervical spinal cord, suggestive of early tonsillar herniation.  

Because CT lacks the soft‑tissue contrast of MRI, these findings are often overlooked, especially when the study is performed for trauma rather than for posterior fossa assessment. Recognizing the crowded foramen magnum prompted dedicated MRI to quantify the degree of tonsillar descent and to search for associated pathology.

### MRI protocol for Chiari assessment  

A high‑resolution MRI of the brain and upper cervical spine was performed using a 1.5‑T (or 3‑T) scanner with the following sequences:  

| Sequence | Orientation | Relevance |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Sagittal T1‑weighted | Mid‑sagittal | Provides anatomic overview; delineates the extent of tonsillar descent relative to the opisthion. |
| Sagittal T2‑weighted (fast spin‑echo) | Mid‑sagittal | Highlights CSF signal; facilitates measurement of the tonsillar protrusion and identification of CSF flow obstruction. |
| Axial T2‑weighted | Through foramen magnum and C1–C2 | Demonstrates the transverse dimensions of the posterior fossa, the “crowding” of neural structures, and any cord compression. |
| 3‑D CISS or FIESTA (steady‑state) | High‑resolution cubic voxels | Enables precise visualization of the brainstem, cranial nerve roots, and the subarachnoid space. |
| Phase‑contrast CSF flow study (cine MRI) | Sagittal plane | Assesses the dynamics of CSF pulsation across the foramen magnum; abnormal flow patterns support a functional component of obstruction. |

The sagittal T2 images showed an **11 mm caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils** below the level of the foramen magnum (measured from the tip of the tonsils to the basion–opisthion line). This exceeds the conventional 5 mm threshold for CMI and correlates with the degree of brainstem compression observed on axial views. No syringomyelia or intramedullary signal change was seen throughout the cervical cord, effectively excluding a “Chiari‑associated syrinx” that could otherwise explain additional symptoms.

Phase‑contrast imaging demonstrated markedly reduced caudal pulsatile CSF flow during systole, confirming a mechanical obstruction at the craniocervical junction. The combination of an 11 mm tonsillar descent, a crowded posterior fossa, and impaired CSF dynamics satisfied radiologic criteria for symptomatic Chiari I malformation.

### Role of polysomnography versus type‑III sleep study  

The patient’s nocturnal desaturations were first captured by a home‑based type‑III sleep monitor, which records respiratory effort (chest and abdominal bands), airflow (nasal pressure), pulse oximetry, and heart rate. While convenient, this device lacks electroencephalography (EEG) and therefore cannot distinguish sleep from wakefulness. Consequently, the **apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI)** of 6.0 events per hour was calculated over the total recording time, not true sleep time, leading to an underestimation of respiratory event frequency.

Despite the modest AHI, two critical observations emerged:  

1. **Predominance of central events** – 72 % of the recorded apnoeas were classified as central (absence of respiratory effort), a pattern uncommon in primary obstructive sleep apnoea and highly suggestive of brainstem dysregulation.  
2. **Severe oxygen desaturations without arousal** – SpO₂ fell to 70 %–80 % during many central events, yet the patient reported no awakenings or dyspnoea, consistent with loss of automatic ventilatory drive.

These findings highlighted the need for a full **type‑I polysomnography** with EEG, capnography, and video monitoring to accurately quantify sleep time, evaluate arousal thresholds, and differentiate true central hypoventilation from mixed sleep‑disordered breathing. The planned full‑night study will likely reveal a higher true AHI and may capture prolonged periods of hypoventilation during non‑rapid‑eye‑movement (NREM) sleep, the stage most dependent on brainstem automatic control.

### Interpretation of arterial blood gas and spirometry  

Arterial blood gas (ABG) performed on room air showed a **normal PaCO₂ of 42 mm Hg** and a slightly reduced PaO₂ of 88 mm Hg. In classic central hypoventilation, hypercapnia is the hallmark because the medullary chemosensitive nuclei fail to increase ventilation in response to rising CO₂. The normal PaCO₂ in this patient reflects two important points:  

* **Preserved voluntary ventilation** – When the patient is awake and consciously breathing, cortical drive to the diaphragm and accessory muscles maintains appropriate minute ventilation, preventing CO₂ accumulation.  
* **Intermittent nature of the defect** – The central drive is selectively impaired during sleep or distraction, periods when automatic control dominates. Thus ABG taken while the patient is alert may appear normal, even though nocturnal hypoventilation is profound.

Spirometry revealed near‑normal flow volumes (FEV₁ 98 % predicted, FVC 96 % predicted) with modest reductions in maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (80 % and 75 % predicted, respectively). These findings exclude restrictive or obstructive pulmonary disease and demonstrate that the respiratory musculature is intact, reinforcing the conclusion that the primary pathology resides centrally.

### Videofluoroscopic swallow study  

A modified barium swallow (videofluoroscopic swallowing study) was performed to explore the patient’s choking episodes. The study demonstrated **penetration of both thin liquids and thin solids into the airway without any structural obstruction**, and the timing of the swallowing reflex was delayed relative to the bolus arrival. These patterns are characteristic of **neurogenic dysphagia**: the equal impairment of solids and liquids, the absence of mechanical obstruction on endoscopy, and the presence of aspiration despite intact pharyngeal musculature suggest a cranial‑nerve or brainstem etiology. In CMI, compression of the nucleus ambiguus and adjacent medullary pathways can produce precisely this type of dysphagia.

### Systematic exclusion of alternative diagnoses  

A disciplined differential diagnosis was pursued to ensure that the central hypoventilation was not attributable to more common conditions:

| Condition | Reason for exclusion |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **Gastro‑oesophageal reflux disease / laryngopharyngeal reflux** | 24‑h pH monitoring normal; symptom‑relief with proton‑pump inhibitors absent; endoscopies normal. |
| **Myasthenia gravis** | Negative acetylcholine‑receptor and anti‑MuSK antibodies; absence of fatigable weakness; normal repetitive nerve stimulation. |
| **Primary pulmonary disease** | Chest X‑ray and CT unremarkable; spirometry normal; no diffusion abnormality. |
| **Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CHHS)** | No neonatal history of hypoventilation; PHOX2B testing not indicated; symptom onset clearly post‑trauma. |
| **Obstructive sleep apnea** | Predominantly central events on type‑III study; lack of snoring or positional dependence; BMI not in obesity range. |

By sequentially ruling out these entities, the team reinforced the likelihood that the respiratory and swallowing findings were neurologically mediated.

### Integration into a unified diagnosis  

The convergence of imaging, functional, and clinical data allowed the clinicians to synthesize a **unified diagnosis of Chiari‑related acquired central hypoventilation syndrome** (an “acquired Ondine’s curse”). The diagnostic logic can be summarized:

1. **Structural evidence** – MRI confirmed substantial tonsillar descent (11 mm) and CSF flow obstruction, establishing the presence of a pathologic CMI.  
2. **Physiologic evidence** – Type‑III sleep monitoring showed a preponderance of central apnoeas with severe desaturation, and ABG demonstrated normal PaCO₂ only when the patient was awake, indicating selective loss of automatic drive.  
3. **Functional correlation** – Videofluoroscopic swallow study revealed neurogenic dysphagia compatible with brainstem cranial‑nerve involvement.  
4. **Exclusion of mimics** – Systematic laboratory and functional testing eliminated GERD, myasthenia, primary lung disease, and congenital central hypoventilation.  

Together, these elements fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for **acquired central hypoventilation secondary to Chiari I malformation**: (a) documented central apnoeic events causing clinically significant hypoxaemia, (b) radiologic confirmation of brainstem compression at the foramen magnum, and (c) absence of alternative explanations. The diagnosis not only explained the choking episodes and chronic occipital headache (via occipital neuralgia from cranial‑nerve irritation) but also provided a clear therapeutic target—posterior fossa decompression—to relieve brainstem compression and restore normal ventilatory control.
