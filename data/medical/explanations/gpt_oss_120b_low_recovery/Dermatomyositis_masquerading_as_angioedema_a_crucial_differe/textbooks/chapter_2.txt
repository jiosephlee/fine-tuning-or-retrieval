Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## Dermatomyositis: Pathophysiology, Clinical Spectrum, and the Role of Anti‑SAE1 Antibodies  

Dermatomyositis (DM) belongs to the group of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs), a heterogeneous family of autoimmune disorders that primarily target skeletal muscle but often involve skin, lung, heart and other organs. Within the IIM classification, DM is distinguished from polymyositis, inclusion‑body myositis, immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy and overlap myositis by a characteristic combination of cutaneous manifestations, a specific microvascular pathology, and a predilection for humoral immune mechanisms.

### Definition and Classification  
DM is defined as an acquired autoimmune disease characterized by (1) chronic inflammation of skeletal muscle with a predominantly **proximal, symmetric weakness**, (2) **typical dermatoses** that involve the hands, face, neck and trunk, and (3) a **histopathologic pattern of complement‑mediated microangiopathy**. The International Myositis Classification Criteria (2022) assign a high diagnostic weight to the presence of the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules or a biopsy showing perifascicular atrophy with C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) deposition. Patients are further stratified by myositis‑specific antibodies (MSAs); the anti‑small ubiquitin‑like modifier‑activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti‑SAE1) antibody defines a distinct serologic subgroup that carries particular clinical nuances.

### Key Pathogenic Mechanisms  

#### Complement‑Mediated Microangiopathy  
The hallmark lesion in DM is a **microvascular injury** of the capillaries that perfuse the perifascicular region of skeletal muscle. Autoantibodies and immune complexes activate the classical complement cascade, culminating in **C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) deposition** on endothelial membranes. This leads to capillary necrosis, hypoperfusion, and secondary perifascicular muscle fibre atrophy. The same process underlies the characteristic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash, where dermal vessels are similarly targeted.

#### Autoantibody‑Driven Immune Dysregulation  
Beyond complement activation, a spectrum of **myositis‑specific antibodies** (MSAs) exerts pathogenic influence by shaping the adaptive immune response. MSAs can be directed against cytoplasmic enzymes (e.g., Jo‑1), nuclear helicases (Mi‑2), transcriptional regulators (TIF1‑γ) or, in the case of SAE1, components of the **SUMO (small ubiquitin‑like modifier) pathway**. The presence of a particular MSA correlates with distinct clinical phenotypes, disease severity, and extramuscular involvement, informing both diagnostic suspicion and prognostic counseling.

#### Role of Sumoylation and the SAE Complex  
The **SAE (SUMO‑activating enzyme) heterodimer** comprises SAE1 and SAE2, which together catalyze the first step of sumoylation—a reversible post‑translational modification that modulates protein stability, nuclear‑cytoplasmic transport, and transcriptional activity. Anti‑SAE1 antibodies recognize the catalytic subunit, potentially interfering with normal sumoylation of proteins involved in immune regulation, muscle repair and endothelial integrity. Although the precise mechanistic link remains incompletely elucidated, experimental data suggest that dysregulated sumoylation may augment inflammatory cytokine production and promote the vascular injury characteristic of DM. Consequently, anti‑SAE1 antibodies serve as both a **biomarker of disease** and a **potential contributor to pathogenesis**.

### Clinical Hallmarks  

#### Cutaneous Signs  
Skin disease is often the first clue to DM and follows a characteristic distribution:  

* **Gottron’s papules** – raised violaceous papules over the extensor surfaces of the MCP and PIP joints.  
* **Heliotrope rash** – dusky periorbital erythema with edema, sometimes accompanied by a “shawl” of erythema over the neck and shoulders.  
* **V‑neck/holster sign** – erythematous, edematous rash of the anterior neck and upper chest, mirroring the shape of a V or a holster.  
* **Peri‑ungual telangiectasia** and nailfold capillary changes (microhemorrhages, avascular zones) detectable on capillaroscopy.  

These lesions arise from the same complement‑mediated microangiopathy that harms muscle capillaries.

#### Muscle Involvement  
Patients develop **proximal muscle weakness** (hip flexors, shoulder girdle) that progresses over weeks to months. Laboratory testing typically reveals **marked elevation of serum creatine kinase (CK)**—often >5 × upper limit of normal—as well as raised transaminases and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), reflecting muscle breakdown. Imaging (MRI, FDG‑PET) shows increased signal or metabolic activity in affected muscles, while biopsy confirms perifascicular atrophy, perimysial inflammation, and C5b‑9 deposition.

#### Extramuscular Manifestations  
* **Interstitial lung disease (ILD):** Occurs in ~30‑40 % of adults, presenting as ground‑glass opacities or traction bronchiectasis on CT.  
* **Dysphagia:** Involvement of oropharyngeal and esophageal musculature leads to aspiration, weight loss, and, as illustrated in the case, severe neck swelling that mimics angioedema.  
* **Cardiac involvement:** Arrhythmias, myocarditis or conduction disease may be present but are less common.  
* **Malignancy risk:** Adult DM confers a 15‑20 % cumulative risk of an associated cancer, especially within the first three years after diagnosis. Screening is guided by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) recommendations.

### Epidemiology and Age Distribution  
The overall incidence of DM is approximately **10 cases per million person‑years**. There is a bimodal age pattern: a peak in **children aged 5–14 years** and a second peak in **adults 45–64 years**. The disease is slightly more prevalent in females, but serologic subgroups such as anti‑SAE1 do not show a strong gender bias.

### Anti‑SAE1 Antibody Phenotype  

#### Frequency and Core Clinical Features  
Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are identified in **~5‑10 %** of adult DM cohorts, making them an uncommon but clinically important MSA. The serologic profile is associated with:

* **Prominent cutaneous disease** – often an early, extensive V‑neck/holster rash and peri‑ungual erythema.  
* **Dysphagia** – reported in ~45‑50 % of SAE1‑positive patients, reflecting early involvement of pharyngeal musculature.  
* **Interstitial lung disease** – present in ~40 % of cases, though typically milder than in anti‑MDA5 disease.  
* **Edema/angioedema‑like swelling** – observed in a minority (≈6‑8 % of all DM patients) but more frequently reported among SAE1‑positive individuals compared with other MSAs; however, the prevalence remains low (≈15‑20 % of SAE1‑positive cases).  

#### Edema versus True Angioedema  
In DM, **inflammatory edema** arises from capillary leakage secondary to complement‑mediated endothelial injury and cytokine‑driven increased vascular permeability. The resulting soft‑tissue swelling can be **localized (e.g., neck, face)** and clinically indistinguishable from **histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema**. Key discriminators include:

* **Absence of urticaria, pruritus, or rapid onset after an allergen exposure.**  
* **Persistence despite antihistamines or C1‑esterase inhibitor therapy.**  
* **Concurrent muscle weakness, elevated CK, and cutaneous signs of DM.**  

Understanding this distinction is essential because management diverges dramatically—DM requires high‑dose glucocorticoids and immunosuppression, whereas true angioedema is treated with airway‑protective measures, antihistamines, bradykinin‑targeted agents, or C1‑esterase replacement.

### Integrating Pathophysiology, Clinical Spectrum, and Anti‑SAE1 Antibodies in the Present Case  
The reported patient exemplifies how **anti‑SAE1‑driven dermatomyositis** can manifest with **neck soft‑tissue swelling** that mimics idiopathic angioedema. The underlying complement‑mediated microangiopathy produced capillary leak in the neck strap muscles, generating **inflammatory edema** rather than a histamine‑mediated reaction. Subtle dermatoses (peri‑ungual telangiectasia, holster sign) and a striking CK rise prompted the clinician to consider DM, leading to confirmatory testing for anti‑SAE1 antibodies and a muscle biopsy that demonstrated classic C5b‑9 deposition. Recognizing this serologic subtype informed both prognostication (higher risk of dysphagia and ILD) and therapeutic decisions (early aggressive immunosuppression with steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab).  

In sum, dermatomyositis is an immune‑mediated vasculopathy of muscle and skin, with anti‑SAE1 antibodies marking a subgroup prone to prominent cutaneous disease, dysphagia, and occasional inflammatory edema that can be mistaken for angioedema. Awareness of these pathogenetic and phenotypic nuances enables timely diagnosis and appropriate immunomodulatory treatment, as illustrated by the successful outcome of the case.
