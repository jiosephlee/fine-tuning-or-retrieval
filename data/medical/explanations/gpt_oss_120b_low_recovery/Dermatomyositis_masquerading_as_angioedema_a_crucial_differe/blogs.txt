# When Neck Swelling Isn’t Ludwig’s Angina: Dermatomyositis in the Differential  

*By [Your Name], MD – EM & Rheumatology Enthusiast*  

---  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A healthy 40‑year‑old walks into the ED with a three‑week history of a rapidly expanding “neck lump.” The initial instinct of most ENT and emergency physicians is Ludwig’s angina or a deep‑neck‑space infection—especially when the CT shows diffuse strap‑muscle edema and retropharyngeal inflammation. But the patient is afebrile, not toxic, and the swelling is **non‑tender**. He also has vague proximal weakness and nail‑fold micro‑haemorrhages that don’t fit a classic infectious picture.  

When a comprehensive work‑up finally revealed a CK of > 16,000 U/L and a positive anti‑SAE1 myositis antibody, the diagnosis shifted from “idiopathic angioedema” to **dermatomyositis (DM) masquerading as angioedema**. This isn’t just an academic curiosity; delayed recognition can lead to airway compromise, irreversible dysphagia, and missed malignancy screening.  

## The Clinical Puzzle: Angioedema vs. Inflammatory Myopathy  

| Feature | Typical Angioedema (Histamine/ Bradykinin) | Dermatomyositis‑Related Swelling |
|---------|--------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Pain** | Often painful or burning | Usually painless, firm |
| **Fever & Systemic Inflammation** | Rare | May have modest CRP elevation (as in our case) |
| **Skin** | Urticarial wheals, flushing | Subtle heliotrope‑like erythema, peri‑ungual telangiectasia, nail‑fold hemorrhages |
| **Muscle Weakness** | Absent | Proximal weakness, dysphagia, elevated CK |
| **Imaging** | Edema limited to soft tissue, possible fluid collections | Diffuse myofascial edema, FDG‑PET shows multifocal FDG uptake in muscles |
| **Response to Steroids/Antihistamines** | Antihistamines/IC2 blockers modest; steroids variable | Dramatic response to high‑dose steroids and immunosuppression |

The key red flag in our patient was **non‑painful, progressive neck swelling without infectious signs**, coupled with **muscle weakness** that was not initially obvious but became apparent on focused exam.  

## Step‑Wise Diagnostic Algorithm for the “Neck Swelling” Patient  

1. **Initial ABCs & Airway Assessment** – Always secure the airway first. A swollen neck can herald impending obstruction.  
2. **Focused History**  
   * Onset/duration (acute vs sub‑acute)  
   * Fever, recent dental work, recent infections, medication exposure (ACEi, DPP‑4 inhibitors)  
   * Constitutional symptoms (weight loss, night sweats) – raise malignancy suspicion.  
3. **Targeted Physical Exam**  
   * Evaluate for tenderness, erythema, fluctuance (infection)  
   * Look for **proximal muscle weakness** (hip flexors, shoulder abductors)  
   * Examine skin: heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V‑/shawl signs, peri‑ungual changes, nail‑fold capillaroscopy.  
4. **Basic Labs** – CBC, CRP/ESR, **CK**, LFTs, renal panel. An isolated CK > 5,000 U/L should prompt rheumatology input.  
5. **Imaging**  
   * **CT Neck** – If shows diffuse soft tissue edema without abscess, consider non‑infectious causes.  
   * **MRI** of neck or involved muscles – better delineates inflammatory edema.  
   * **FDG‑PET** – Helpful when malignancy or systemic myositis is suspected.  
6. **Specific Autoimmune Work‑up** (order early if CK is high or skin clues present)  
   * ANA, ENA, myositis panel (anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑MDA‑5, anti‑SAE1, etc.)  
   * Complement levels, C1‑esterase inhibitor (to exclude hereditary/ acquired angioedema).  
7. **Consults** – ENT for airway evaluation, Immunology/Rheumatology for myositis work‑up, and Critical Care if airway compromise looms.  

If the work‑up points toward an inflammatory myopathy, initiate **high‑dose IV methylprednisolone** (1 g daily for 3 days) while awaiting serology and muscle biopsy. Early immunosuppression can reverse edema and prevent permanent dysphagia.  

## What the Literature Tells Us About “Pseudo‑Angioedema” in DM  

* Only **6–7 %** of dermatomyositis patients develop clinically significant edema.  
* Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are linked to a higher incidence of cutaneous involvement (holster sign, V‑neck) and dysphagia, but overt neck swelling remains rare (≈3 % in SAE1‑positive cohorts).  
* Angioedema‑like presentations have been reported more often with anti‑MDA‑5 and anti‑TIF1‑γ antibodies, underscoring that any myositis antibody can manifest atypically.  
* Early identification is crucial because **airway obstruction** can be the first life‑threatening manifestation—something not often highlighted in standard DM teaching.  

## Treatment Nuances Highlighted by the Case  

* **Steroid Pulse + Rapid Taper** – The “three‑day 1 g methylprednisolone” regimen jumps the inflammatory cascade down and buys time for other agents to work.  
* **IVIG** – Frequently used when dysphagia is severe or when there’s rapid muscle breakdown; in this case, a 2 g/kg loading dose followed by 5 monthly cycles led to swift CK normalization.  
* **Steroid‑Sparing Agents** – Mycophenolate mofetil was favored over methotrexate because of the patient’s early lung involvement (avoid MTX‑induced pulmonary toxicity).  
* **Rituximab** – Added for B‑cell depletion; emerging data suggest it improves muscle strength and may reduce relapse in refractory DM.  
* **PJP Prophylaxis** – Start TMP‑SMX unless contraindicated (as happened here); switch to nebulized pentamidine if hemolysis or G6PD deficiency appears.  

## Malignancy Screening – Not Optional  

Dermatomyositis confers a **2‑3 ×** increased risk of occult cancer, especially within the first three years. The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) recommends:  

* Baseline CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, age‑appropriate cancer screening (colonoscopy, mammography, PSA), and PET‑CT if any suspicious lesions.  
* Repeat surveillance every 6–12 months for at least three years if the initial work‑up is negative.  

Our patient’s initial PET‑CT was negative, but he continues with scheduled GI endoscopy and periodic imaging.  

## Clinical Pearls & Take‑Home Messages  

- **Never assume all neck edema is infection.** A painless, slowly progressive swelling without fever or leukocytosis should trigger a broader differential.  
- **Look beyond the neck.** Subtle proximal weakness or dysphagia, even if the patient downplays it, is a red flag for myositis.  
- **Skin is the clue.** Peri‑ungual erythema, nail‑fold capillary changes, or a faint heliotrope hue may be the only dermatologic hint.  
- **CK is your friend.** A markedly elevated creatine kinase (> 5,000 U/L) in the setting of neck swelling is practically diagnostic of an inflammatory myopathy until proven otherwise.  
- **Imaging can mislead.** CT may show edema that looks infectious; MRI or FDG‑PET better characterizes inflammatory muscle involvement.  
- **Early steroids save airways.** High‑dose IV methylprednisolone should be started promptly once infection is reasonably excluded.  
- **Screen for malignancy early and often.** Dermatomyositis is a paraneoplastic sentinel in many cases.  

---  

### Bottom Line  

When a neck mass doesn’t behave like Ludwig’s angina—no pain, no fever, a slow rise, and the patient quietly complains of “weak hips” or “trouble swallowing”—consider **dermatomyositis**. A quick CK, a careful skin exam, and an early liaison with rheumatology can turn a potentially catastrophic airway emergency into a treatable autoimmune disorder.  

---  

**Key Take‑Home Points**  

- Painless, non‑febrile neck swelling → broaden beyond infection → think inflammatory myopathy.  
- Always obtain CK early; > 5,000 U/L = strong indicator of myositis.  
- Look for subtle cutaneous signs (peri‑ungual changes, nail‑fold hemorrhages, heliotrope‑like erythema).  
- Initiate high‑dose steroids once infection is ruled out; add IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab as needed.  
- Perform comprehensive malignancy screening per IMACS guidelines; continue surveillance for at least 3 years.  

Stay curious, keep the differential wide, and remember: **the neck can hide a myositis storm, not just a bacterial one.**



# Pseudo‑Angioedema in Inflammatory Myopathy: Pathophysiology and Practical Work‑up  

*Why a “swollen neck” in a previously healthy 40‑year‑old turned out to be dermatomyositis, not angioedema.*

---  

## Why this case is worth a blog post  

When a patient walks into the ED with rapidly expanding neck swelling, most of us run through the classic checklist: Ludwig’s angina, deep‑neck space infection, superior vena cava syndrome, or—if the swelling is non‑tender and non‑erythematous—histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.  

In the report by Chan et al., a previously well man in his 40s was **mis‑labelled** as having idiopathic angioedema for three weeks. The “angioedema” didn’t respond to standard therapies, there was no trigger, and the labs for C1‑INH dysfunction were perfectly normal. What finally set him apart was a **high CK (16 000 U/L)**, subtle **peri‑ungual telangiectasias**, and a **SAE1‑positive myositis antibody panel**—the hallmarks of dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema.  

That’s a teaching moment: not every “soft‑tissue edema” is histamine‑driven. Inflammatory myopathies can cause **pseudo‑angioedema** via muscle inflammation and capillary leakage, and recognizing it early can prevent weeks of unnecessary ENT work‑up and allow prompt immunosuppression.  

---

## Quick refresher: true angioedema vs pseudo‑angioedema  

| Feature | Histamine‑mediated (allergic) | Bradykinin‑mediated (C1‑INH deficiency) | Myositis‑related pseudo‑angioedema |
|---------|------------------------------|------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Onset** | Minutes–hours after exposure | Hours–days, often no trigger | Subacute (days–weeks) |
| **Pain/itch** | Often painful, pruritic | Usually painless | Usually painless, “floppy” feel |
| **Associated rash** | Urticaria, erythema | None | May have heliotrope, Gottron’s, peri‑ungual changes |
| **Lab clues** | Normal CK, normal complement | Low C4, low C1‑INH level or function | Elevated CK, ANA ±, myositis‑specific antibodies |
| **Response to epinephrine/antihistamines** | Good | Poor | No effect |
| **Imaging** | May show soft‑tissue swelling only | Same | Often shows inflamed strap muscles, retropharyngeal edema on CT/MRI |

The *key* differentiator in the ED is **CK**—a markedly raised CK should make you think of muscle injury rather than a vascular leak.

---

## Pathophysiology: how does dermatomyositis make your neck puff up?  

1. **Perifascicular microangiopathy** – Classic dermatomyositis has complement‑mediated damage (C5b‑9 deposition) in the capillaries supplying the perifascicular muscle fibers. The resulting endothelial leak allows protein‑rich fluid to seep into the interstitial space.  

2. **Cytokine storm** – Elevated type‑I interferons (driven by auto‑antibodies such as anti‑SAE, anti‑MDA5, anti‑TIF1‑γ) up‑regulate VEGF and other permeability factors, worsening edema.  

3. **Direct muscle edema** – Inflamed muscle fibers swell, and the strap muscles of the neck are relatively superficial, so the edema is readily palpable and can mimic angioedema.  

4. **Dysphagia & aspiration** – The same inflammatory process often involves the oropharyngeal musculature, leading to dysphagia, pooling of secretions, and secondary aspiration pneumonitis (as seen in the case).  

5. **Auto‑antibody phenotype** – Certain myositis antibodies appear to predispose to edema:  
   * **Anti‑SAE1** – Often presents with pronounced cutaneous findings (holster sign, peri‑ungual erythema) and dysphagia; edema is less common but documented.  
   * **Anti‑MDA5** – Strong association with amyopathic dermatomyositis and rapid‐progressive interstitial lung disease; edema reported in up to 30 % of cases.  
   * **Anti‑TIF1‑γ** – Frequently linked to malignancy‑associated dermatomyositis; facial swelling reported in several series.  

Understanding these mechanistic links helps the clinician appreciate why a **muscle‑focused work‑up** is warranted when the phenotype is atypical for classic angioedema.

---

## Practical bedside work‑up: “When to suspect myositis”  

1. **History clues**  
   * Subacute (≥ 48 h) swelling with **no obvious trigger**.  
   * Associated **proximal muscle weakness**, **myalgia**, or **dysphagia**.  
   * Skin findings: heliotrope rash, shawl sign, V‑neck, Gottron’s papules, peri‑ungual telangiectasias, holster sign.  
   * Absence of ACE‑I, DPP‑4 inhibitors, or known hereditary angioedema family history.  

2. **Focused exam**  
   * Look for **nailfold capillary changes** (micro‑haemorrhages, avascular areas).  
   * Assess **proximal strength** (hip flexors, deltoids).  
   * Inspect **face/neck** for violaceous erythema, “puffy” appearance without tenderness.  

3. **First‑line labs (order at bedside)**  

| Test | Expected result in true angioedema | Expected result in myositis‑related edema |
|------|-----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **CK** | Normal | Often > 5 000 U/L (dramatically raised) |
| **CBC / CRP** | May be normal or mild leukocytosis | Often mildly elevated CRP; WBC usually normal |
| **C4** | Low in hereditary/acquired C1‑INH deficiency | Normal |
| **C1‑INH level & function** | Low in C1‑INH deficiency | Normal |
| **ANA** | Usually negative | Positive (often speckled, 1:1280+ in case) |
| **Myositis antibody panel** (e.g., Euroline) | Negative | May show anti‑SAE1, anti‑MDA5, anti‑TIF1‑γ, etc. |

**Tip:** If CK is > 1 000 U/L, put the “angioedema” label aside and jump to a **muscle‑focused imaging** (MRI neck or CT neck showing strap‑muscle inflammation).  

4. **Imaging**  
   * **CT neck** – May already show diffuse strap‑muscle swelling; helps rule out abscess.  
   * **MRI muscle** – T2 hyperintensity in involved muscles is classic for inflammatory myositis.  

5. **Additional work‑up** (once myositis is on the radar)  
   * **Pulmonary function tests** – Screen for ILD.  
   * **Chest CT** – Look for ground‑glass opacities or nodules.  
   * **Cancer screening** – As per IMACS guidelines; high‑risk antibodies (TIF1‑γ, SAE) and dysphagia raise suspicion.  

---

## Management snapshot (from the case)  

* **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone** (1 g × 3 days) – rapid control of muscle inflammation and edema.  
* **IVIG** (2 g/kg over 5 days, then monthly) – especially useful when dysphagia is severe.  
* **Steroid‑sparing agents** – Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen here; alternatives include methotrexate, azathioprine, rituximab.  
* **Rituximab** – Consider in refractory disease or anti‑MDA5‑positive cases with ILD.  
* **Supportive care** – Nasogastric feeding for dysphagia, pneumocystis prophylaxis (pentamidine when sulfa intolerant).  

The patient’s CK normalized within weeks, neck swelling resolved over a few months, and dysphagia cleared by 7 months—classic of responsive dermatomyositis with prompt immunosuppression.

---

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Don’t let “angioedema” blind you**: a subacute, painless neck swelling with any muscle weakness or skin changes should trigger a CK check.  
- **CK > 5 000 U/L** in the context of edema is practically pathognomonic for an inflammatory myopathy.  
- **Anti‑SAE1, anti‑MDA5, anti‑TIF1‑γ** are the antibodies most associated with pseudo‑angioedema; knowing the antibody profile can hint at lung disease risk or malignancy risk.  
- **Complement studies (C4, C1‑INH)** are still essential to rule out true bradykinin‑mediated angioedema, but a normal result with high CK pushes you toward myositis.  
- **Early muscle‑focused imaging** (CT/MRI of neck) can spare the patient a prolonged ENT work‑up and get immunosuppression started sooner.  
- **Multidisciplinary care**—ENT, rheumatology/clinical immunology, radiology, and speech‑language pathology—is key for managing airway, dysphagia, and systemic disease.  

---

## Bottom line  

Pseudo‑angioedema is a rare but real presentation of dermatomyositis and other inflammatory myopathies. By keeping a **high index of suspicion**, ordering a **quick CK** (and if available, a myositis antibody panel), and interpreting the results in the context of the clinical picture, you can catch these masqueraders early, avoid unnecessary antibiotics and surgeries, and get patients on life‑saving immunotherapy before irreversible muscle or lung damage occurs.  

*Next time you see a swollen neck that isn’t behaving like an infection or classic allergic reaction—remember the “muscle” in “pseudo‑angioedema.”*



# Anti‑SAE1 Dermatomyositis: Clinical Phenotype, Cancer Risk, and Practical Screening Strategies  

## Why This Case Stood Out  

When a previously healthy man in his 40s walks into the ED with a “swollen neck” that looks like Ludwig’s angina, the reflex is to call a surgeon, start broad‑spectrum antibiotics, and think about an airway emergency. In this case, however, the swelling was *not* an infection, nor a classic histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema. Instead, it was the first clue of an underlying systemic autoimmune disease—anti‑SAE1 (small ubiquitin‑like modifier‑activating enzyme) dermatomyositis.  

The lesson? Subtle skin and nail findings, a disproportionately high creatine kinase (CK), and a “pseudo‑angioedema” should prompt you to think beyond ENT and consider inflammatory myopathies, even when the presentation mimics a pure ENT problem.  

## A Quick Recap of the Case  

- **Presentation:** 3‑week progressive neck swelling, proximal hip weakness, dysphagia, low‑grade inflammation (CRP ≈ 55 mg/L).  
- **Red flags that tipped the differential:**  
  - Neck edema not typical for infection (no fever, afebrile, normal WBC, CT showed diffuse soft‑tissue edema without abscess).  
  - Nailfold capillary changes (micro‑haemorrhages, avascular areas).  
  - Erythema over nasolabial folds and a right thigh rash.  
- **Key labs:** CK = 16 184 U/L, transaminases and LDH markedly elevated, ANA 1:1280 speckled, **anti‑SAE1 antibody strongly positive**.  
- **Imaging/biopsy:** MRI & FDG‑PET showed widespread myositis; muscle biopsy demonstrated perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition—classic dermatomyositis.  
- **Management:** Pulse IV methylprednisolone → oral prednisone taper + IVIG (2 g/kg × 5 cycles) + mycophenolate mofetil + rituximab. Airway and dysphagia improved; CK normalized; neck swelling resolved over weeks.  

## Anti‑SAE1 Dermatomyositis: What Do We Know?  

| Feature | Frequency / Typical Findings |
|---|---|
| **Epidemiology** | Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are identified in ~5‑10 % of adult dermatomyositis cohorts. Female predominance overall in DM, but anti‑SAE1 does not show a strong sex bias. |
| **Cutaneous phenotype** | Classic DM signs (heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules) *may* be absent. Instead, patients often have a “holster” sign (V‑neck), shawl sign, or peri‑ungual erythema/telangiectasia. Facial/neck edema is reported in ≤ 7 % of anti‑SAE1 cases. |
| **Muscle involvement** | Proximal weakness is universal; CK levels are often > 5 000 U/L (median ~ 9 000 U/L). Rapid CK rise should prompt serology even when skin signs are subtle. |
| **Dysphagia** | Occurs in ~ 45‑50 % of anti‑SAE1 patients—usually due to pharyngeal and esophageal muscle inflammation. May be the first overt symptom and can be severe, requiring NG‑tube feeding. |
| **Pulmonary disease** | Interstitial lung disease (ILD) present in ~ 40 % (often non‑fibrotic, ground‑glass pattern). Aspiration pneumonitis can coexist when dysphagia is severe, as in our patient. |
| **Malignancy risk** | Overall DM carries a 15‑20 % cancer association. For anti‑SAE1, malignancy has been reported in ~ 15‑20 % of cases, with lung, colorectal, and ovarian primaries being the most common. In the IMACS risk model, anti‑SAE1 is considered an **intermediate‑risk antibody**. |
| **Prognosis** | With early aggressive immunotherapy (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents), most patients achieve CK normalization and functional recovery. Persistent edema is a poor prognostic sign, but our case shows it can resolve with appropriate treatment. |

## Cancer Screening: From Guidelines to a Pragmatic Plan  

The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) issued a consensus algorithm for malignancy screening in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM). The algorithm stratifies patients based on **clinical phenotype**, **serology**, and **demographic risk factors**.  

### IMACS Risk Matrix (simplified)  

| Risk Factor | Weight | Points |
|---|---|---|
| Dermatomyositis (vs polymyositis) | High | +2 |
| Dysphagia | Medium | +1 |
| Anti‑SAE1 antibody | Medium | +1 |
| Male sex | Low | +0.5 |
| Age > 60 y | Low | +0.5 |
| **Total ≥ 3** → **High‑risk** (recommend extensive screening) |

Our patient scored **4.5** (DM + 2, dysphagia + 1, anti‑SAE1 + 1, male + 0.5) → **high‑risk**.  

### What “extensive screening” Means in Practice  

| Modality | Rationale | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Whole‑body contrast CT (chest/abdomen/pelvis)** | Detects solid organ malignancies, nodal disease. | Baseline, then at 6 mo, 12 mo, and annually for 3 years (or sooner if new symptoms). |
| **FDG‑PET/CT** | Higher sensitivity for occult lymphomas and small solid tumors; also maps myositis activity. | Baseline (often done anyway for disease staging). |
| **Age‑appropriate cancer screens** (colonoscopy, mammography, PSA, cervical cytology) | Standard population screening. | Align with national guidelines; colonoscopy at baseline for > 50 y or earlier if risk factors. |
| **Tumor markers** (CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA) | Low sensitivity but cheap; helps when imaging is equivocal. | Baseline, repeat if clinical suspicion rises. |
| **Serial CK and clinical review** | Rising CK or new systemic symptoms may herald malignancy. | Every 3‑6 mo for first 2 years, then annually. |
| **Patient‑driven symptom surveillance** | Weight loss, night sweats, unexplained pain. | Ongoing; reinforce at each visit. |

**Key practical tip:** If you already have a FDG‑PET for myositis assessment, incorporate the report into your cancer work‑up—no extra radiation, high yield.  

## Putting It All Together: A Step‑by‑Step Approach for the Front‑Line Clinician  

1. **When you see unexplained neck/face/limb edema** – think beyond allergic angioedema. Check CK, ESR/CRP, and do a quick skin/nail exam.  
2. **If CK > 1 000 U/L** or there is proximal weakness, order a myositis antibody panel (including SAE1).  
3. **Positive anti‑SAE1** → Initiate high‑dose steroids **immediately** (e.g., methylpred 1 g IV daily × 3 d).  
4. **Add early steroid‑sparing agents** (mycophenolate, azathioprine, or rituximab) and consider IVIG especially if dysphagia or severe edema is present.  
5. **Screen for malignancy** using the IMACS risk matrix; for anti‑SAE1 with dysphagia, default to “high‑risk” pathway.  
6. **Schedule imaging** (CT + PET) within the first two weeks of diagnosis, then repeat as outlined above.  
7. **Monitor** CK, muscle strength, and dysphagia weekly initially; taper steroids once CK is ≤ 2 × ULN and clinical exam is stable.  
8. **Long‑term follow‑up** – maintain mycophenolate (or alternative) and IVIG for at least 12 months; continue cancer surveillance for a minimum of 3 years, extending to 5 years if any risk factor persists.  

## Take‑Home Pearls  

- **Neck swelling ≠ infection** – in the setting of systemic inflammation, consider inflammatory myopathy.  
- **Anti‑SAE1 is a red flag**: frequent dysphagia, lung involvement, and a non‑negligible cancer risk.  
- **CK is your first lab**; a value > 5 000 U/L should trigger a myositis work‑up even without classic rashes.  
- **Screen aggressively**: combine baseline whole‑body CT and FDG‑PET, then follow the IMACS risk‑based schedule.  
- **Early combination therapy** (high‑dose steroids + IVIG + steroid‑sparing agent) improves outcomes and may reduce the duration of dysphagia and edema.  

---

**Bottom line:**  

Anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema, especially when cervical edema is the dominant feature. Recognizing the serologic profile, assessing dysphagia and pulmonary involvement, and applying a structured cancer‑screening protocol are essential to avoid missed diagnoses and to improve long‑term survival. Keep a low threshold to send CK and a myositis panel—your next patient’s airway may depend on it.



# Aggressive Immunosuppression for Severe Myositis with Airway Involvement: Lessons from a Real‑World Case  

## Why this case matters  

When a 40‑year‑old man walks into the ED with a three‑week history of “neck swelling” most clinicians will reach for the usual suspects: Ludwig’s angina, deep‑neck space infection, or—if the swelling is non‑tender—idiopathic angioedema. What makes this story stand out is that the swelling turned out to be **dermatomyositis‑related pseudo‑angioedema**, a rare manifestation of an already uncommon disease. The “angioedema” was actually muscle inflammation extending into the strap muscles, threatening the airway and causing dysphagia. The lesson? When neck edema is discordant with the classic features of histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema, think myositis and order a CK early.

## Quick snapshot of the presentation  

* **Chief complaint:** Progressive, non‑tender neck swelling + proximal hip weakness (3 weeks).  
* **Vitals:** Afebrile, sat 99 % on room air, no tachypnoea.  
* **Key physical clues:** Nailfold capillary micro‑haemorrhages, subtle peri‑ungual erythema, a “holster‑sign” rash on the thigh, hip flexor strength 4/5.  
* **Lab highlight:** CK 16 184 U/L (massive), ALT/AST/LDH modestly elevated, ANA 1:1280 speckled, anti‑SAE1 strongly positive.  
* **Imaging:** CT neck – diffuse soft‑tissue edema; MRI thigh – active myositis; FDG‑PET – widespread muscular uptake, no malignancy.  

The diagnostic tide turned when CK was drawn – a simple test that saved weeks of unnecessary ENT work‑up.

## The therapeutic dilemma: airway‑threatening myositis  

Once the diagnosis of anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with severe neck muscle edema was locked in, the team faced a **life‑threatening** scenario: airway compromise from inflamed strap muscles and dysphagia from pharyngeal involvement. Conventional dermatomyositis regimens (prednisone + a steroid‑sparing agent) would be inadequate on their own; a more **aggressive, multimodal** approach was required.

### What we did  

| Agent | Dose & Schedule | Rationale |
|------|----------------|-----------|
| **IV methylprednisolone** | 1 g daily × 3 days | Rapid‐acting “pulse” to quench cytokine storm, reduce edema, buy time for other agents to kick in. |
| **Oral prednisolone (taper)** | Start 60 mg daily, reduce 5 mg every 2‑3 weeks | Allows gradual weaning while maintaining anti‑inflammatory pressure; taper guided by CK trend and clinical exam. |
| **IVIG** | 2 g/kg total over 5 days (≈35 g/day), then 5 additional cycles 4 weekly (2 g/kg each) | Provides immunomodulation independent of B‑cell depletion; useful in refractory or severe myositis and improves dysphagia. |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)** | 1 g BID (target 2 g/day) | First‑line steroid‑sparing agent in DM; good pulmonary safety profile, oral daily dosing simplifies long‑term maintenance. |
| **Rituximab** | 1 g IV × 2 doses, 2 weeks apart | Targets CD20+ B cells; evidence supports benefit in refractory anti‑SAE1 disease and may reduce long‑term steroid burden. |

#### Why methotrexate was *not* used  

* **Pulmonary comorbidity:** The patient already had aspiration‑related infiltrates and a restrictive pattern on PFTs. MTX carries a known risk of pulmonary fibrosis, especially in the setting of pre‑existing lung disease.  
* **Patient preference & tolerability:** MMF is oral, once‑ or twice‑daily, and generally better tolerated than weekly MTX injections.  
* **Drug‑drug interactions:** The planned high‑dose steroids and potential future use of rituximab make MMF a safer concurrent partner.

## Infection prophylaxis – a must, not an afterthought  

High‑dose steroids, rituximab, and MMF all increase opportunistic infection risk. The team instituted:

* **PJP prophylaxis:** Started TMP‑SMX (800/160 mg) daily; switched to nebulised pentamidine 300 mg q4 weeks after the patient developed hemolytic anemia (no G6PD deficiency).  
* **Vaccinations:** Prior to immunosuppression, ensured pneumococcal (PCV20), influenza, and COVID‑19 boosters. Post‑rituximab, the live vaccines were deferred for 6 months.  
* **Monitoring:** Weekly CBC, LFTs, CK; monthly CMV PCR for high‑risk periods (especially after rituximab).

## Steroid tapering – the art of balance  

* The initial **pulse** provides a rapid “reset” of the inflammatory milieu.  
* Following the pulse, **prednisone 60 mg** is modest for dermatomyositis but allows a smoother taper.  
* The taper rate (5 mg every 2‑3 weeks) was adjusted based on CK, clinical swelling, and dysphagia.  
* By **month 7** the patient was off steroids entirely, underscoring that an early high‑intensity burst can permit an earlier steroid‑free state.

## Monitoring for relapse  

* **CK trend:** The most sensitive early marker; a rise > 2‑fold from baseline warrants a prompt reassessment.  
* **Clinical exam:** Return of neck swelling, new dysphagia, or re‑appearance of peri‑ungual capillary changes should trigger imaging.  
* **Imaging:** Repeat MRI or PET only if CK or exam suggest flare; avoids unnecessary radiation.  
* **Pulmonary function:** Serial PFTs every 6 months to catch early ILD progression.  

In this case, CK normalized (37 U/L) by week 3, neck edema resolved by month 3, and dysphagia cleared by month 7. Ongoing MMF and IVIG maintenance kept the disease quiescent.

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Never dismiss neck edema as “just angioedema”** when it’s non‑tender, progressive, and associated with muscle weakness – get a CK.  
- **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone** (1 g × 3) is life‑saving in airway‑compromising myositis; follow with a structured oral taper.  
- **IVIG** should be considered early for severe dysphagia or refractory muscle edema; it works synergistically with steroids and B‑cell depletion.  
- **Mycophenolate** is preferred over methotrexate when lung disease is present or when a patient needs a steroid‑sparing oral agent.  
- **Rituximab** adds durability, especially in anti‑SAE1 cases with extensive muscle involvement.  
- **Prophylaxis for PJP** and vigilant infection monitoring are essential when combining high‑dose steroids, MMF, and rituximab.  
- **Serial CK and targeted imaging** are the cornerstones of relapse surveillance; keep the data trending, not a single snapshot.  

## Bottom line  

Severe dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema, and when the airway is at stake, rapid, aggressive immunosuppression—pulse steroids, IVIG, MMF, and rituximab—can reverse life‑threatening edema while sparing the patient long‑term steroid toxicity. The case reinforces that a **high index of suspicion, early CK measurement, and a thoughtfully stacked therapeutic regimen** are the keys to turning a potential airway disaster into a story of remission.



# Dysphagia in Dermatomyositis: From Subclinical to Airway Threat  

---  

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When a 40‑something man walks into the ED with a “bloated” neck and no fever, the reflex is to think Ludwig’s angina, a dental infection, or—if the CT looks “fluffy”—idiopathic angioedema.  In the case report by Chan et al., the neck swelling turned out to be a **pseudo‑angioedema** caused by **dermatomyositis (DM)**.  What tipped the team off was *not* the imaging but the **combination of subtle skin clues (peri‑ungual telangiectasias, a “holster” rash) and a massive CK of >16 000 U/L**.  

The downstream consequence? A **severe dysphagia** that threatened the airway, required nasogastric feeding, and delayed discharge for weeks.  This illustrates a broader teaching point: **myositis‑related swallowing dysfunction can evolve silently and then become an emergent airway problem if missed**.  

Below we’ll walk through how to spot the early red flags, what investigations are most useful, and how to manage the swallowing crisis while your immunosuppression kicks in.  

---

## The pathophysiology in a nutshell  

Dermatomyositis is a complement‑mediated microangiopathy that targets the capillaries of skin and muscle.  In the **pharyngeal and laryngeal musculature**, the same perimysial inflammation leads to:

* **Fiber necrosis & regeneration** → loss of contractile strength.  
* **Peri‑vascular complement deposition (C5b‑9)** → edema of the sub‑muscular tissue.  
* **Reduced coordination** of the swallow because the intrinsic muscles of the pharynx are disproportionately affected.  

When the inflammation is intense (as in anti‑SAE1 positive disease), you can get **subcutaneous edema** that mimics angioedema and **massive swelling** that narrows the airway.  

---

## Practical bedside approach – “Swallow Screen in the ED”

1. **Ask the right questions** – “Do you feel food ‘stuck’ in your throat?”, “Do you cough during meals?”, “Any change in voice after eating?”  
2. **Quick physical exam** – look for:  
   * Peri‑ungual erythema or nailfold capillary changes.  
   * Rash over the face, chest, or proximal limbs (even subtle heliotrope or shawl‑sign variants).  
   * Neck swelling that is **soft, non‑tender, non‑fluctuant** – think edema rather than cellulitis.  
   * **Weakness of hip flexors or other proximal muscles** (strength 4/5 or less).  

3. **Rapid CK check** – a CK >1 000 U/L in the setting of neck swelling should prompt myositis work‑up.  

If any of the above are positive, **don’t settle for “idiopathic angioedema”**—activate the rheumatology/immunology consult early.

---

## Imaging and functional studies – what to order and when  

| Modality | What it tells you | Timing/Indication |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------|
| **CT neck (contrast‑free)** | Excludes abscess, shows diffuse strap‑muscle edema. | First‑line when airway compromise feared. |
| **Videofluoroscopic Swallow Study (VFSS)** | Characterises pharyngeal residue, penetration, aspiration; quantifies severity. | As soon as the patient can tolerate oral contrast (usually within 24‑48 h). |
| **Flexible Nasendoscopy** | Direct view of secretions, vocal cord motion, supraglottic edema. | Bedside, especially useful for ENT/ICU teams. |
| **MRI of the neck / torso** | Shows active myositis (STIR/FS hyperintensity) – helps with disease activity monitoring. | If diagnosis still uncertain or to guide biopsies. |
| **FDG‑PET** | Detects systemic muscle inflammation, screens for occult malignancy (important in DM). | After initial work‑up, before long‑term immunosuppression. |
| **Pulmonary function tests** | Baseline for ILD, restrictive pattern from neck/respiratory muscle weakness. | Routine in DM, especially anti‑SAE1 positivity. |

The case example used **VFSS**, which revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossa pooling and penetration of thin liquids—classic for myositis‑related swallowing impairment.  

---

## The role of the speech‑language pathologist (SLP) – don’t wait  

* **Early assessment (within 24 h of admission)** can identify safe diet modifications and teach compensatory maneuvers (chin‑tuck, supraglottic swallow).  
* **Serial VFSS** tracks recovery as immunotherapy takes effect; it also guides the timing of oral diet re‑introduction.  
* **Swallow‑related quality‑of‑life tools** (e.g., MDADI) help quantify impact and need for rehabilitation.  

In the reported case, the patient required an NG tube for **2 months** while the steroids, IVIG, rituximab, and mycophenolate were doing their work.  Prompt SLP involvement meant that once muscle strength returned, the transition to oral intake was smooth and without aspiration events.

---

## Nutritional support – NG tube vs PEG  

| Situation | Preferred route | Rationale |
|-----------|----------------|-----------|
| Acute, reversible dysphagia (<6 weeks) with expected rapid improvement after immunotherapy | **Nasogastric (NG) tube** | Less invasive, easy to place, can be removed once swallowing recovers. |
| Persistent dysphagia >6–8 weeks, poor oral intake, or high aspiration risk despite therapy | **Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG)** | Provides long‑term enteral nutrition, reduces risk of tube dislodgement, better patient comfort. |
| Contraindications to NG (e.g., severe facial edema, esophageal varices) | **PEG** or **jejunostomy** | Bypass obstructed or high‑risk anatomy. |

The case illustrates that **NG feeding can bridge the gap** while aggressive immunosuppression works, but a low threshold for PEG is warranted if the dysphagia does not improve after 6–8 weeks.  

---

## Immunotherapy – the driver of swallow recovery  

The **key to reversing dysphagia** is rapid control of the underlying inflammatory process:

1. **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone** (1 g × 3 days) – hits the muscle inflammation hard.  
2. **IVIG** – especially useful in anti‑SAE1 disease, where complement-mediated attack is prominent.  
3. **Steroid‑sparing agents** – mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, or methotrexate (avoid methotrexate if ILD risk).  
4. **Early taper** – once CK normalises and strength returns, start tapering steroids to avoid long‑term toxicity.  

In the case, CK fell from 16 184 U/L to 37 U/L within 3 weeks, and hip flexor strength returned to normal.  Swallowing function lagged behind muscle strength, underscoring that **functional recovery can trail biochemical improvement**.  

---

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Never accept “idiopathic angioedema”** in a patient with neck swelling and any hint of proximal muscle weakness or skin changes.  
- **CK >1 000 U/L** in this context is a red flag for inflammatory myopathy; order it early.  
- **VFSS is the gold‑standard** for quantifying dysphagia severity and guiding diet progression.  
- **Early SLP involvement** reduces aspiration risk and expedites safe oral intake.  
- **NG tube is usually sufficient** for the short‑term nutritional bridge; move to PEG if dysphagia persists beyond 6 weeks.  
- **Aggressive immunosuppression** (steroids + IVIG ± rituximab/MMF) can reverse muscle inflammation and, consequently, swallowing dysfunction—often within weeks.  
- **Screen for malignancy** in all DM patients, especially those with anti‑SAE1 antibodies, dysphagia, and male sex.  

---

## Bottom line  

Dermatomyositis is a master of disguise; it can masquerade as angioedema, deep neck infection, or a simple “muscle ache.”  The **airway‑threatening dysphagia** seen in the Chan et al. case is a stark reminder that **myositis‑related swallowing dysfunction is a medical emergency** when it progresses.  A systematic bedside screen, rapid CK measurement, early VFSS, and prompt immunotherapy—paired with skilled speech‑language pathology—turn a potential airway catastrophe into a recoverable episode.  

**Next time you see a neck “swelling” with subtle skin signs, think DM. Your early suspicion could spare a patient weeks of NG feeding and the looming risk of aspiration.**



# Imaging the Inflamed Muscles: When FDG‑PET, MRI, and CT Complement Each Other in Myositis  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

When a 40‑year‑old man walks into the ED with a three‑week history of a “bloated” neck, most clinicians first think infection (Ludwig’s angina), airway obstruction, or a classic histamine‑mediated angioedema. The CT looks like diffuse soft‑tissue edema, the ENT team finds no abscess, and the immunology consult is called for “idiopathic angioedema.” Only after a careful skin exam, nail‑fold capillaroscopy, and a **CK‑19,000 U/L** does the diagnostic net swing toward an inflammatory myopathy.  

What makes this story especially teachable is the **triad of cross‑sectional imaging** that turned a diagnostic nightmare into a clear, treatable pathology:  

* **CT of the neck** – ruled out a purulent deep‑neck space infection and showed the distribution of edema.  
* **MRI of the thigh/neck** – pinpointed active myositis in deep strap muscles and hip girdle, confirming that the swelling was muscular, not fascial or vascular.  
* **FDG‑PET/CT** – demonstrated diffuse, high‑grade glucose uptake throughout the musculature (head‑to‑toe), clinching the diagnosis of a systemic inflammatory process and simultaneously excluding hidden malignancy.  

In the era of rapid point‑of‑care labs, it’s easy to overlook how these imaging modalities can complement each other, especially when the clinical picture is atypical. Below is a practical, step‑by‑step guide for the frontline clinician dealing with “pseudo‑angioedema” or any unexplained soft‑tissue swelling that may hide a myositis.

## 1. When to Call for Imaging – The Clinical Trigger  

| Clinical clue | Suggested first test | Rationale |
|---------------|---------------------|-----------|
| Rapidly progressive neck/facial swelling **without** obvious infection, urticaria, or drug exposure | **Contrast‑enhanced CT neck** | Fast, widely available; rules out abscess, airway compromise, vascular lesions; gives a map of the edema (muscle vs. fascia vs. subcutaneous). |
| Presence of **muscle weakness, elevated CK, or subtle dermatomyositis stigmata** (peri‑ungual telangiectasia, heliotrope rash, shawl sign) | **MRI of the symptomatic region** (neck, thigh, shoulder) | MRI is the gold standard for detecting edema, inflammation, and atrophy in muscle tissue; can differentiate myositis from myopathic edema. |
| **Unexplained systemic inflammation** (CRP > 30 mg/L) **or concern for paraneoplastic myositis** | **Whole‑body FDG‑PET/CT** | Highlights metabolically active muscle inflammation and simultaneously screens for occult malignancy (high yield in dermatomyositis). |

The key is **sequential escalation**: start with the quickest rule‑out (CT), then move to tissue characterization (MRI), and finally to systemic disease mapping (FDG‑PET) when a myositis is likely or when cancer must be excluded.

## 2. CT Neck – What to Look For  

* **Distribution of edema** – In true infection, you’ll see fluid collections, rim enhancement, or gas. In inflammatory myositis, the edema is *diffuse* and *symmetric* within the strap muscles and often extends into the retropharyngeal space without a defined abscess wall.  
* **Airway patency** – Critical for immediate ENT decision‑making. A mildly narrowed airway from edema may still be managed conservatively; an obstructive abscess needs urgent drainage.  
* **Lymphadenopathy / mass lesion** – Enlarged nodes or a solid mass would tip the differential toward malignancy or lymphoma, prompting early tissue biopsy.  

In our case, CT showed **uniform low‑attenuation swelling of the strap muscles** and **no rim‑enhancing collection**, essentially “negative for infection” but “positive for muscle edema.” That finding alone should have raised the suspicion of a myopathic process.

## 3. MRI – The Myositis Workhorse  

### Sequences that matter  
| Sequence | What it shows |
|----------|----------------|
| T1‑weighted axial & coronal | Muscle bulk, fatty infiltration (chronic change) |
| STIR or fat‑suppressed T2 | **Active edema** – bright signal within affected muscle fibers (the hallmark of acute myositis). |
| Post‑gadolinium T1 with fat‑sat | **Enhancement pattern** – diffuse or perifascicular enhancement typical of dermatomyositis; helps differentiate from necrotizing myopathy (more focal). |
| Diffusion‑weighted imaging (optional) | Can help differentiate cellulitis (restricted diffusion) from pure edema. |

### Interpreting the pattern  

* **Diffuse, symmetric STIR hyperintensity** of the **deep neck strap muscles** plus the **obturator internus/externus** groups, as seen in the case, points to **systemic inflammatory myositis** rather than a localized infection.  
* **Perifascicular or perimysial enhancement** after contrast is strongly associated with dermatomyositis (C5b‑9 deposition in capillaries).  
* **Absence of fluid collections** rules out abscess; **no mass effect** argues against a neoplastic process compressing the airway.  

MRI also gives a **baseline for treatment response** – repeat MRI after 3–6 months can demonstrate resolution of STIR signal correlating with CK trends.

## 4. FDG‑PET/CT – When Myositis Meets Oncology  

FDG‑PET is often reserved for cancer staging, yet its role in inflammatory myopathies is expanding:

* **Diffuse, high‑grade FDG uptake** in skeletal muscle (SUVmax > 2.5–3 in several muscle groups) is a **signature of active inflammatory myositis**.  
* **Patchy or focal uptake** could represent **malignancy** (paraneoplastic myositis often co‑exists with a primary tumor). The whole‑body scan can simultaneously hunt for occult neoplasms in lung, breast, GI, or ovary – a critical step given the 15–20 % malignancy risk in adult dermatomyositis.  

In the presented case, FDG‑PET revealed **uniform muscle uptake from head to toe**, with the **greatest uptake in the neck and deltoids**, reinforcing the systemic nature of the disease, while **no hypermetabolic lymph nodes or masses** were identified. This allowed the team to **skip invasive biopsies of neck tissue** and focus on immunosuppression.

## 5. Putting It All Together – A Practical Algorithm  

```
Patient with unexplained soft‑tissue swelling ± weakness
│
├─► Immediate airway assessment (ENT)
│   └─► CT neck (contrast) → rule out abscess/obstruction
│
├─► Lab clues: CK ↑, inflammatory markers ↑, ANA +/‑ myositis panel
│   └─► MRI (regional) → confirm myositis, assess extent
│
└─► High suspicion for systemic disease or malignancy?
        └─► FDG‑PET/CT → map inflammation, screen for cancer
```

**Tip:** If MRI already shows classic dermatomyositis changes (perifascicular STIR edema + contrast enhancement) and CK is > 5,000 U/L, you can often **defer PET** unless there are red‑flag features (rapid weight loss, unexplained anemia, smoking history).  

## 6. Take‑Home Messages  

* **CT is the first‑line rule‑out** for deep‑neck infections; look for *diffuse muscular edema* without abscess formation.  
* **MRI is the definitive myositis modality**—STIR hyperintensity and contrast enhancement pinpoint active inflammation and guide muscle biopsy sites.  
* **FDG‑PET/CT provides a whole‑body snapshot**—it confirms systemic inflammatory activity and screens for occult malignancy, a must‑do in adult dermatomyositis.  
* A **multimodal imaging approach** shortens time to diagnosis, prevents unnecessary surgeries, and aligns treatment (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) with the true pathology.  
* Always **correlate imaging findings with clinical clues** (peri‑ungual telangiectasia, nailfold hemorrhages, dysphagia) and laboratory data (CK, autoantibodies).  

In the end, this case reminds us that **“angioedema” isn’t always allergic or bradykinin‑driven**—sometimes it’s inflamed muscle hiding behind a swollen neck. Leveraging CT, MRI, and FDG‑PET together turns a baffling presentation into a treatable disease, and most importantly, spares the patient from unnecessary antibiotics, surgeries, or delayed immunosuppression.
