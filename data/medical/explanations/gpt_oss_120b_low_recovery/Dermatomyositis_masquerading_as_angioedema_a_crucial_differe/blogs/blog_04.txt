Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Aggressive Immunosuppression for Severe Myositis with Airway Involvement: Lessons from a Real‑World Case  

## Why this case matters  

When a 40‑year‑old man walks into the ED with a three‑week history of “neck swelling” most clinicians will reach for the usual suspects: Ludwig’s angina, deep‑neck space infection, or—if the swelling is non‑tender—idiopathic angioedema. What makes this story stand out is that the swelling turned out to be **dermatomyositis‑related pseudo‑angioedema**, a rare manifestation of an already uncommon disease. The “angioedema” was actually muscle inflammation extending into the strap muscles, threatening the airway and causing dysphagia. The lesson? When neck edema is discordant with the classic features of histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema, think myositis and order a CK early.

## Quick snapshot of the presentation  

* **Chief complaint:** Progressive, non‑tender neck swelling + proximal hip weakness (3 weeks).  
* **Vitals:** Afebrile, sat 99 % on room air, no tachypnoea.  
* **Key physical clues:** Nailfold capillary micro‑haemorrhages, subtle peri‑ungual erythema, a “holster‑sign” rash on the thigh, hip flexor strength 4/5.  
* **Lab highlight:** CK 16 184 U/L (massive), ALT/AST/LDH modestly elevated, ANA 1:1280 speckled, anti‑SAE1 strongly positive.  
* **Imaging:** CT neck – diffuse soft‑tissue edema; MRI thigh – active myositis; FDG‑PET – widespread muscular uptake, no malignancy.  

The diagnostic tide turned when CK was drawn – a simple test that saved weeks of unnecessary ENT work‑up.

## The therapeutic dilemma: airway‑threatening myositis  

Once the diagnosis of anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with severe neck muscle edema was locked in, the team faced a **life‑threatening** scenario: airway compromise from inflamed strap muscles and dysphagia from pharyngeal involvement. Conventional dermatomyositis regimens (prednisone + a steroid‑sparing agent) would be inadequate on their own; a more **aggressive, multimodal** approach was required.

### What we did  

| Agent | Dose & Schedule | Rationale |
|------|----------------|-----------|
| **IV methylprednisolone** | 1 g daily × 3 days | Rapid‐acting “pulse” to quench cytokine storm, reduce edema, buy time for other agents to kick in. |
| **Oral prednisolone (taper)** | Start 60 mg daily, reduce 5 mg every 2‑3 weeks | Allows gradual weaning while maintaining anti‑inflammatory pressure; taper guided by CK trend and clinical exam. |
| **IVIG** | 2 g/kg total over 5 days (≈35 g/day), then 5 additional cycles 4 weekly (2 g/kg each) | Provides immunomodulation independent of B‑cell depletion; useful in refractory or severe myositis and improves dysphagia. |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)** | 1 g BID (target 2 g/day) | First‑line steroid‑sparing agent in DM; good pulmonary safety profile, oral daily dosing simplifies long‑term maintenance. |
| **Rituximab** | 1 g IV × 2 doses, 2 weeks apart | Targets CD20+ B cells; evidence supports benefit in refractory anti‑SAE1 disease and may reduce long‑term steroid burden. |

#### Why methotrexate was *not* used  

* **Pulmonary comorbidity:** The patient already had aspiration‑related infiltrates and a restrictive pattern on PFTs. MTX carries a known risk of pulmonary fibrosis, especially in the setting of pre‑existing lung disease.  
* **Patient preference & tolerability:** MMF is oral, once‑ or twice‑daily, and generally better tolerated than weekly MTX injections.  
* **Drug‑drug interactions:** The planned high‑dose steroids and potential future use of rituximab make MMF a safer concurrent partner.

## Infection prophylaxis – a must, not an afterthought  

High‑dose steroids, rituximab, and MMF all increase opportunistic infection risk. The team instituted:

* **PJP prophylaxis:** Started TMP‑SMX (800/160 mg) daily; switched to nebulised pentamidine 300 mg q4 weeks after the patient developed hemolytic anemia (no G6PD deficiency).  
* **Vaccinations:** Prior to immunosuppression, ensured pneumococcal (PCV20), influenza, and COVID‑19 boosters. Post‑rituximab, the live vaccines were deferred for 6 months.  
* **Monitoring:** Weekly CBC, LFTs, CK; monthly CMV PCR for high‑risk periods (especially after rituximab).

## Steroid tapering – the art of balance  

* The initial **pulse** provides a rapid “reset” of the inflammatory milieu.  
* Following the pulse, **prednisone 60 mg** is modest for dermatomyositis but allows a smoother taper.  
* The taper rate (5 mg every 2‑3 weeks) was adjusted based on CK, clinical swelling, and dysphagia.  
* By **month 7** the patient was off steroids entirely, underscoring that an early high‑intensity burst can permit an earlier steroid‑free state.

## Monitoring for relapse  

* **CK trend:** The most sensitive early marker; a rise > 2‑fold from baseline warrants a prompt reassessment.  
* **Clinical exam:** Return of neck swelling, new dysphagia, or re‑appearance of peri‑ungual capillary changes should trigger imaging.  
* **Imaging:** Repeat MRI or PET only if CK or exam suggest flare; avoids unnecessary radiation.  
* **Pulmonary function:** Serial PFTs every 6 months to catch early ILD progression.  

In this case, CK normalized (37 U/L) by week 3, neck edema resolved by month 3, and dysphagia cleared by month 7. Ongoing MMF and IVIG maintenance kept the disease quiescent.

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Never dismiss neck edema as “just angioedema”** when it’s non‑tender, progressive, and associated with muscle weakness – get a CK.  
- **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone** (1 g × 3) is life‑saving in airway‑compromising myositis; follow with a structured oral taper.  
- **IVIG** should be considered early for severe dysphagia or refractory muscle edema; it works synergistically with steroids and B‑cell depletion.  
- **Mycophenolate** is preferred over methotrexate when lung disease is present or when a patient needs a steroid‑sparing oral agent.  
- **Rituximab** adds durability, especially in anti‑SAE1 cases with extensive muscle involvement.  
- **Prophylaxis for PJP** and vigilant infection monitoring are essential when combining high‑dose steroids, MMF, and rituximab.  
- **Serial CK and targeted imaging** are the cornerstones of relapse surveillance; keep the data trending, not a single snapshot.  

## Bottom line  

Severe dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema, and when the airway is at stake, rapid, aggressive immunosuppression—pulse steroids, IVIG, MMF, and rituximab—can reverse life‑threatening edema while sparing the patient long‑term steroid toxicity. The case reinforces that a **high index of suspicion, early CK measurement, and a thoughtfully stacked therapeutic regimen** are the keys to turning a potential airway disaster into a story of remission.
