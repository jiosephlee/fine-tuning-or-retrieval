# When Neck Swelling Isn’t Ludwig’s Angina: Dermatomyositis in the Differential

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A healthy 40‑year‑old walks into the ED with a three‑week history of a rapidly expanding “neck lump.” The initial instinct of most ENT and emergency physicians is Ludwig’s angina or a deep‑neck‑space infection—especially when the CT shows diffuse strap‑muscle edema and retropharyngeal inflammation. But the patient is afebrile, not toxic, and the swelling is **non‑tender**. He also has vague proximal weakness and nail‑fold micro‑haemorrhages that don’t fit a classic infectious picture.  

When a comprehensive work‑up finally revealed a CK of > 16,000 U/L and a positive anti‑SAE1 myositis antibody, the diagnosis shifted from “idiopathic angioedema” to **dermatomyositis (DM) masquerading as angioedema**. This isn’t just an academic curiosity; delayed recognition can lead to airway compromise, irreversible dysphagia, and missed malignancy screening.  

## The Clinical Puzzle: Angioedema vs. Inflammatory Myopathy  

| Feature | Typical Angioedema (Histamine/ Bradykinin) | Dermatomyositis‑Related Swelling |
|---------|--------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Pain** | Often painful or burning | Usually painless, firm |
| **Fever & Systemic Inflammation** | Rare | May have modest CRP elevation (as in our case) |
| **Skin** | Urticarial wheals, flushing | Subtle heliotrope‑like erythema, peri‑ungual telangiectasia, nail‑fold hemorrhages |
| **Muscle Weakness** | Absent | Proximal weakness, dysphagia, elevated CK |
| **Imaging** | Edema limited to soft tissue, possible fluid collections | Diffuse myofascial edema, FDG‑PET shows multifocal FDG uptake in muscles |
| **Response to Steroids/Antihistamines** | Antihistamines/IC2 blockers modest; steroids variable | Dramatic response to high‑dose steroids and immunosuppression |

The key red flag in our patient was **non‑painful, progressive neck swelling without infectious signs**, coupled with **muscle weakness** that was not initially obvious but became apparent on focused exam.  

## Step‑Wise Diagnostic Algorithm for the “Neck Swelling” Patient  

1. **Initial ABCs & Airway Assessment** – Always secure the airway first. A swollen neck can herald impending obstruction.  
2. **Focused History**  
   * Onset/duration (acute vs sub‑acute)  
   * Fever, recent dental work, recent infections, medication exposure (ACEi, DPP‑4 inhibitors)  
   * Constitutional symptoms (weight loss, night sweats) – raise malignancy suspicion.  
3. **Targeted Physical Exam**  
   * Evaluate for tenderness, erythema, fluctuance (infection)  
   * Look for **proximal muscle weakness** (hip flexors, shoulder abductors)  
   * Examine skin: heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V‑/shawl signs, peri‑ungual changes, nail‑fold capillaroscopy.  
4. **Basic Labs** – CBC, CRP/ESR, **CK**, LFTs, renal panel. An isolated CK > 5,000 U/L should prompt rheumatology input.  
5. **Imaging**  
   * **CT Neck** – If shows diffuse soft tissue edema without abscess, consider non‑infectious causes.  
   * **MRI** of neck or involved muscles – better delineates inflammatory edema.  
   * **FDG‑PET** – Helpful when malignancy or systemic myositis is suspected.  
6. **Specific Autoimmune Work‑up** (order early if CK is high or skin clues present)  
   * ANA, ENA, myositis panel (anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑MDA‑5, anti‑SAE1, etc.)  
   * Complement levels, C1‑esterase inhibitor (to exclude hereditary/ acquired angioedema).  
7. **Consults** – ENT for airway evaluation, Immunology/Rheumatology for myositis work‑up, and Critical Care if airway compromise looms.  

If the work‑up points toward an inflammatory myopathy, initiate **high‑dose IV methylprednisolone** (1 g daily for 3 days) while awaiting serology and muscle biopsy. Early immunosuppression can reverse edema and prevent permanent dysphagia.  

## What the Literature Tells Us About “Pseudo‑Angioedema” in DM  

* Only **6–7 %** of dermatomyositis patients develop clinically significant edema.  
* Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are linked to a higher incidence of cutaneous involvement (holster sign, V‑neck) and dysphagia, but overt neck swelling remains rare (≈3 % in SAE1‑positive cohorts).  
* Angioedema‑like presentations have been reported more often with anti‑MDA‑5 and anti‑TIF1‑γ antibodies, underscoring that any myositis antibody can manifest atypically.  
* Early identification is crucial because **airway obstruction** can be the first life‑threatening manifestation—something not often highlighted in standard DM teaching.  

## Treatment Nuances Highlighted by the Case  

* **Steroid Pulse + Rapid Taper** – The “three‑day 1 g methylprednisolone” regimen jumps the inflammatory cascade down and buys time for other agents to work.  
* **IVIG** – Frequently used when dysphagia is severe or when there’s rapid muscle breakdown; in this case, a 2 g/kg loading dose followed by 5 monthly cycles led to swift CK normalization.  
* **Steroid‑Sparing Agents** – Mycophenolate mofetil was favored over methotrexate because of the patient’s early lung involvement (avoid MTX‑induced pulmonary toxicity).  
* **Rituximab** – Added for B‑cell depletion; emerging data suggest it improves muscle strength and may reduce relapse in refractory DM.  
* **PJP Prophylaxis** – Start TMP‑SMX unless contraindicated (as happened here); switch to nebulized pentamidine if hemolysis or G6PD deficiency appears.  

## Malignancy Screening – Not Optional  

Dermatomyositis confers a **2‑3 ×** increased risk of occult cancer, especially within the first three years. The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) recommends:  

* Baseline CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, age‑appropriate cancer screening (colonoscopy, mammography, PSA), and PET‑CT if any suspicious lesions.  
* Repeat surveillance every 6–12 months for at least three years if the initial work‑up is negative.  

Our patient’s initial PET‑CT was negative, but he continues with scheduled GI endoscopy and periodic imaging.  

## Clinical Pearls & Take‑Home Messages  

- **Never assume all neck edema is infection.** A painless, slowly progressive swelling without fever or leukocytosis should trigger a broader differential.  
- **Look beyond the neck.** Subtle proximal weakness or dysphagia, even if the patient downplays it, is a red flag for myositis.  
- **Skin is the clue.** Peri‑ungual erythema, nail‑fold capillary changes, or a faint heliotrope hue may be the only dermatologic hint.  
- **CK is your friend.** A markedly elevated creatine kinase (> 5,000 U/L) in the setting of neck swelling is practically diagnostic of an inflammatory myopathy until proven otherwise.  
- **Imaging can mislead.** CT may show edema that looks infectious; MRI or FDG‑PET better characterizes inflammatory muscle involvement.  
- **Early steroids save airways.** High‑dose IV methylprednisolone should be started promptly once infection is reasonably excluded.  
- **Screen for malignancy early and often.** Dermatomyositis is a paraneoplastic sentinel in many cases.  

---  

### Bottom Line  

When a neck mass doesn’t behave like Ludwig’s angina—no pain, no fever, a slow rise, and the patient quietly complains of “weak hips” or “trouble swallowing”—consider **dermatomyositis**. A quick CK, a careful skin exam, and an early liaison with rheumatology can turn a potentially catastrophic airway emergency into a treatable autoimmune disorder.  

---  

**Key Take‑Home Points**  

- Painless, non‑febrile neck swelling → broaden beyond infection → think inflammatory myopathy.  
- Always obtain CK early; > 5,000 U/L = strong indicator of myositis.  
- Look for subtle cutaneous signs (peri‑ungual changes, nail‑fold hemorrhages, heliotrope‑like erythema).  
- Initiate high‑dose steroids once infection is ruled out; add IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab as needed.  
- Perform comprehensive malignancy screening per IMACS guidelines; continue surveillance for at least 3 years.  

Stay curious, keep the differential wide, and remember: **the neck can hide a myositis storm, not just a bacterial one.**
